The seed microbiomes of staple food crops
Authors: Zhongke Sun, Bartholomew Saanu Adeleke, Yini Shi, Chengwei Li
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe scientific community increasingly recognized that seed microbiomes are important for plant growth and nutrition. The versatile roles and modulating properties that microbiomes hold in the context of seeds seem to be an inherited approach to avert adverse conditions. These discoveries attracted extensive interest, especially in staple food crops (SFCs) where grain was consumed as food. Along with the rapid expansion of population and industrialization that posed a severe challenge to the yield of SFCs, microbiologists and botanists began to explore and engineer seed microbiomes, for safer and more fruitful grain production. To utilize seed microbiomes, we present an overall review of the most updated scientific literature on three representative SFCs (wheat, rice and maize) using the 5W1H (Which, Where, What, Why, When and How) method that provides a comprehensive understanding of the issue. These include which factors determine the composition of seed microbiomes? Where do seed microbiomes come from? What are these seed microbes? Why do these microbes choose seeds as their destination and when do microbes settle down and become seed communists? In addition, how do seed microbiomes work and can be manipulated effectively? Therefore, answering the aforementioned questions regarding SFCs seed microbiomes remain fundamental in bridging endophytic research gaps and harnessing their ecological services.

STAPLE FOOD CROPS AND SEED MICROBIOMES

Human beings depend on staple food crops (SFCs) like wheat, rice, maize and other grain crops due to their nutritional benefits and energy reserves (Msungu et al., 2022). The seeds of SFCs contribute greatly to the agricultural enterprise with potentiality to supply nutritional requirements, ensure zero hunger and fight malnutrition (Kiean et al., 2022). Hence, preservation of seeds to ensure food safety and quality became important. The seeds' outer surfaces and internal tissues harbour diverse microbes (epiphytic and endophytic) with dynamic attributes, both beneficial and pathogenic (Pal et al., 2021). These seed‐associated microbes, collectively termed seed microbiomes, often shape disease resistance in plants and are unique among other microbial assemblies in plant environments (Matsumoto et al., 2021). Seed microbiomes have received intense attention because of their functional roles during seed germination and seedling growth and development (Abdelfattah et al., 2023). As primary food materials, the importance of seed microbiomes for grain quality and food security is also recognized, for example acidifying microorganisms are present in almost all wheat kernels during storage (Gaglio et al., 2020).

Of most importance, seed‐borne endophytes can regulate overall plant fitness through versatile plant growth‐promoting traits, such as nitrogen fixation, producing phytohormones, secretion of siderophores and enzymes and alleviating plant stress responses (Tsalgatidou et al., 2023). Some seed endophytic bacteria, such as Bacillus spp., Lysinibacillus spp. and Paenibacillus spp., with the ability to use host machinery in the synthesis of biocontrol metabolites, such as antibiotics, organic acids and lipopeptides, are already used as alternatives to chemical pesticides (Thomloudi et al., 2021). The close interactions of endophytes with plants during seed embryonic development make them unique compared with the rhizosphere colonizers (Chen et al., 2022). In fact, by soil inoculation and foliar spray of beneficial microbes at different growth stages, seed endophytes are being explored for their ability to enhance agricultural crop productivity (Mukherjee et al., 2022; Taheri et al., 2022).

As acknowledged, the vertical transmission of seed microbiomes is critical from one generation of plants to another, as they can help establish microbial assemblage and defence mechanisms against phytopathogens (Shade et al., 2017). The co‐evolution of endosymbionts with seed crops can be attained at different survival rates, levels of integration and transmission across maternal lineage (Abdelfattah et al., 2021). To establish a stable equilibrium interface of plant–soil‐microbe interactions, factors such as the transmission mode (vertical or horizontal) of specific microbes need to be considered on a case by case basis. Highlighting the factors mediating microbial assemblage in SFCs seeds remains important for providing information on their transmission, co‐evolution and functional attributes (Lamichhane et al., 2018). With current omic approaches, unravelling diverse seed microbiomes and their functional metabolites and metabolic pathways can be made easy.

Generally, a lot of plant microbiome research has been documented with the intent of ensuring safe ecosystems and food security. Nevertheless, information gaps still exist on SFCs seed microbiomes. Hence, this review addresses this concept critically and unravels the prospects of seed microbiomes in enhancing SFCs production, using the 5W1H (Which, Where, What, Why, When and How) method. It is important to shed more light on seed endophytic microbiomes due to their importance in seed germination, seedling growth, survival and establishment and ecological services (e.g. disease suppression and crop improvement). This review discloses the compositions, sources, functions, mechanisms and manipulations of seed microbiomes, focusing on three representative SFCs, namely wheat, rice and maize.

WHICH FACTORS DETERMINE THE COMPOSITION OF SEED MICROBIOMES?

Seed microbiomes differ from one plant to another due to the quality of the seeds, viability, cultivars and energy reserves needed for microbial metabolism (Roy et al., 2021). The host, microorganisms and environment are the major factors influencing microbial compositions in the plant‐seed endosphere (Figure 1). For example, the effects of host factors (such as genotype, immunity, growth stage, domestication), microorganisms and environment (including soil, farming practices, temperature, humidity, precipitation) on the composition of seed microbiomes has been revealed (Bintarti et al., 2022; Latz et al., 2021; Ren et al., 2023). Furthermore, the interlinkage of these factors may cause myriad shifts in seed microbial compositions and their possible ecological services.

The host factors

The microbiomes in SFCs seed can be influenced by the different plant cultivars and genotypes (Kuźniar et al., 2020; Rana et al., 2020). Study of the seeds of eight native plant species growing together for centuries under the same environmental conditions in Alpine meadows (Austria) by different molecular methods clearly identified plant genotype (species) as the main driver of microbiome composition, including bacteria, archaea and fungi (Wassermann et al., 2019). Host specificity and selectivity of seed microbiomes in wheat and other plants have been reported as well (Morales Moreira et al., 2021; Özkurt et al., 2020). In particular, fungal diversity analysis from seed to plant to seeds over one growth‐season in two environments (indoors and outdoors) has indicated that host genotype, tissue type and abiotic factors influenced fungal communities significantly, although the effect of host genotype was mostly limited to leaves and roots in wheat and was location‐independent (Latz et al., 2021). The pivotal roles of host genotype on the richness and diversity of endophytic bacteria in rice seeds have also been reported (Walitang et al., 2018; Zhang et al., 2019).

Plant immunity is another major factor influencing the microbiomes in seeds (Malacrinò et al., 2023; Teixeira et al., 2019). Beyond pathogen recognition, the plant immune system also plays an important role in the community assembly of the associated microorganism (Hassani et al., 2019). On one hand, plants protect themselves from pathogenic infection with a two‐layered immune system, namely pattern‐triggered immunity (PTI) that is activated upon recognition of microbe‐associated molecular patterns (MAMPs) and effector‐triggered immunity (ETI) recognized by resistance (R) proteins (Pedersen & Marzano, 2023). Also, contributing to host protection, the general non‐self response (GNSR) has been revealed as a part of plant immunity that is consistently elicited by diverse strains from various phyla (Maier et al., 2021). On the other hand, through detection of microbe‐associated molecular patterns (MAMPs) by specific cell surface receptors, a plant initiates an array of biochemical responses collectively known as MAMP‐triggered immunity (MTI). In particular, undergoing coadaptation, commensals can inhabit, persist and propagate within plants by suppressing or evading host immune responses (Hassani et al., 2019).

The plant growth stage also impacts microbiome composition. Microbiomes often have different compositions during seed germination, kernel development, post‐harvest maturation and storage. Many studies have revealed microbial compositions in SFCs seeds, with maize, rice and wheat being development stage dependent (Chen et al., 2019; Dai et al., 2021; Dutta et al., 2022). At seedling stage, the exudates produced from the plant roots at different concentrations can help in shaping seed microbiomes (Fiorilli et al., 2021; Rochefort et al., 2021). During kernel development, it has been found that bacterial and fungal members transmitted across generations are late colonizers dominating each community at the ripening stage in rice (Kim et al., 2022). After harvest, different distributions of bacterial taxa per time of storage has been revealed in seeds as well. For example, it has been found that lactobacilli and enterococci are able to overcome the stressing conditions during wheat kernel storage (e.g. from threshing to conservation for 1–3 years) better than other common lactic acid bacteria genera associated to harvested cereals (Gaglio et al., 2020). In addition, it has been demonstrated that plant domestication leads to bacterial diversification, as the diversity of seed microbiomes is generally higher in cultivated cereals than in wild ancestors (Abdullaeva et al., 2020). Nevertheless, there is a need to investigate the effect of plant secondary metabolite secretions at seed germination and seedling growth, which could form the basis of microbial proliferation and metabolism.

The microbial factors

How microbial factors influence seed microbiomes remains controversial as limited information is available. Generally, the interactions between microbes can be both direct or indirect. Directly, some microbes colonize or infect seeds. Indirectly, some microbes antagonize others by their secondary metabolites or by inducing plant system resistance. Both pathogenic and non‐pathogenic microbes inhabit the seeds' endosphere, but the latter can trigger plant immunity. Pathogenic microbes usually affect plant health status and compete with the beneficial counterparts in the same econiches (Sultana et al., 2022). Conversely, beneficial microbes may impact positively on the plants and their absence can cause a change in seed microbial diversities and functions (Adeleke et al., 2022). It has been found that taxonomically diverse MTI suppressor strains that are efficient root colonizers can enhance the colonization capacity of other commensal bacteria (Teixeira et al., 2021). The antibiosis activities of beneficial seed microbiomes through the synthesis of certain biologically active metabolic compounds can help in the maintenance of plant health by suppressing pathogen invasion of plants, which in turn can influence plant microbial populations (Segaran & Sathiavelu, 2019).

The varied effects of microbial infection/inoculation on the microbial community structure of plant seeds has been reported (Gadhave et al., 2018). Rapid colonization of Fusarium pathogens in wheat spikes appears to exclude other fungi from floral tissues as they are associated with a reduction in community diversity (Rojas et al., 2020). Furthermore, inoculation of endophytic fungi Penicillium olsonii ML37 reduces the abundance of Fusarium graminearum in wheat spikes due to transient activation of plant defence mechanisms (Rojas et al., 2022). Inoculation of wheat with other plant growth‐promoting endophytes might alter dominant microbes in wheat seeds, such as Bacillus spp. (Sun et al., 2021). It is apparent that the diverse mechanisms employed by seed‐associated endophytes in enhancing plant growth can in turn influence the seed microbiomes.

The secondary metabolite production potential of seed endophytes may cause a dynamic shift in the seeds, thus influencing their microbial abundance, diversity and functions (Hill et al., 2021). It has been found that some endophytic microbes have the ability to produce secondary metabolites, which suppress the growth of seed‐borne pathogenic microbes (Xu et al., 2021). For instance, the antagonistic activities of wheat seeds actinobacteria Streptomyces sampsonii WSA20 are due to synthesis of bioproducts against fungal pathogen infection in the seed, including by Alternaria sp., Bipolaris sorokiniana, Fusarium graminearum and Tilletia indica (Sharma et al., 2023).

Environmental factors

Different environmental factors contribute to the diversity and functions of seed microbiomes (Adeleke & Babalola, 2021). Some of these factors include soils, farming practices, temperature, salinity, humidity, ultraviolet (UV) light and toxic compounds. Shifts of diversity and evenness of the plant microbiomes are also commonly linked to different human activities, characterized by a decrease of host specificity and an increase of specific microbes, pathogens and hypermutators (Berg & Cernava, 2022). Hydrodynamics and flooding effects may also affect the composition and distribution of seed microbiomes (Francioli et al., 2021, 2022). Sprinkling irrigation or flooding of polluted water may introduce new microbes from other niches and contaminate seeds as well.

Plants grown in distinct soils typically harbour distinct microbial communities (Bahram et al., 2021). Differences of soil chemical and physical properties, as well as nutritional profiling can cause shifts in the microbial structures in SFCs seeds, especially during seed germination (Abdelfattah et al., 2023). Metagenomic sequencing has revealed the effect of different soil types on the composition of seedling microbiomes (Walsh et al., 2021). For example, soil pH and organic matter have been identified as the primary environmental drivers in shaping the microbial communities of seedlings in wheat and rice and the rhizosphere in Oplopanax elatus (Li et al., 2022; Nahar et al., 2022). In fact, it has been demonstrated that total carbon, available potassium, available phosphorus, aluminium, pH and total nitrogen might all affect the rice seed microbiome due to significant impact on the rhizosphere microbiome (Zhou et al., 2020). Nevertheless, understanding the actual mechanisms of soil properties on the diversity of seed microbiomes can help in understanding microbial communication that exists at the plant–soil interface.

Comparison of microbial communities in SFCs seeds suggests that the abundance of certain microbial species may be due to agricultural practices, for example farming‐specific OTUs and higher numbers of OTUs when grown conventionally rather than organically (Tkalec et al., 2022). As a common agricultural practice, seed coating with fungicide‐based matrices can negatively affect up to 50% of naturally occurring microorganisms (Wang & Cernava, 2020). For example, 36.8% of bacterial features are uniquely presented in uncoated seeds and 14.8% of features show a significantly lower abundance in coated seeds (Chen et al., 2020). Moreover, moderate nitrogen fertilization can enhance bacterial and fungal community colonization in rice seeds and improve grain eating and cooking qualities (Liu et al., 2023).

Distribution and biodiversity analysis of seed‐borne fungi of maize in Egypt has shown that relative humidity, temperature and wind speed, respectively, are the most influential weather variables (Shabana et al., 2022). At high temperatures, the physiology and metabolism of seed endophytes may be hampered (Aswini et al., 2023). Drought dampens microbiome development by disrupting the temporal dynamics of late‐colonizing microorganisms, permanently altering the normal successions of rice root microbiome (Santos‐Medellín et al., 2021). Similar to drought, wheat plants growing in fields with contrasting soil water stress history (WSH) are colonized by different microbial communities throughout their compartments, including seeds. Particularly, seeds harvested from the field with WSH had a higher relative abundance of Firmicutes but less epiphytic Ascomycota than seeds harvested from a normal field (Azarbad et al., 2022). It was found that seed moisture and winter mean annual temperature had significant impacts on rice seed microbiomes (Zhou et al., 2020). In addition, sudden ecological changes in the environment may affect the physiological and biochemical components of plants and also the microbes inhabiting them (Enebe & Babalola, 2018).

The recent understanding of the progressive implications of seed maturation and storage remains important in evaluating the microbial status in seeds. For example, a two‐fold decrease in the number of bacterial OTUs was observed in stored wheat seeds after harvest for 6 months, following phosphine treatment (Solanki et al., 2019). Interestingly, it is reported that bag type is the most important factor influencing changes in fungal microbiomes during hermetic storage of maize seeds (Lane et al., 2018). Therefore, the conditions in which seeds are stored can influence total seed microbiomes. Sometimes, microbes like mycotoxin‐producing fungi can be expanded in SFCs stored under normal conditions rather than temperature‐controlled conditions (Bertuzzi et al., 2019; Kobayashi et al., 2019). Hence, proper management of seedling growth under suitable conditions until maturation, harvesting and novel storage approaches of seeds remain critical pointers to unravel seed microbial dynamics and structures, as well as to preserve stored cereal crops and prevent the growth of spoilage organisms (Schmidt et al., 2019).

WHERE DO SEED MICROBIOMES COME FROM?

Whether they are largely acquired or inherited is still a question around seed microbiomes. Summarily, microbes can enter seeds from different sources, such as soil, plant parts, precipitation and air, during seed germination, development, maturation, transportation and storage (Figure 2). The primary source of seed endophytes can be the rhizosphere environment or the seeds themselves (Abdelfattah et al., 2023). Rhizodeposition establishes nutrient transfer across the soil gradients, stimulates signalling networking at the soil‐root interface and mediates microbial recruitment (Preece & Penuelas, 2016).

Soil harbours diverse microbial species, which serve as a primary starting point for microbial recruitment during seed germination (Tsolakidou et al., 2019). The secretion of root exudates contributing to the microbial population and colonization below‐ground can create a chance for microbial infiltration from the soil environment into the seeds during germination (Adeleke & Babalola, 2021). Upon planting, soil microbes and those found on the seed surface can infiltrate seeds and these microbes may exhibit similar or dissimilar key complementary ecological services. The intrusion of soil microbes into seeds has been recognized as the major source of seed microbiomes in Brassica napus (Rochefort et al., 2021). Monitoring and evaluation of microbes from the planting to the development of seeds will shed light on their origin.

Aside from soils, seed microbes can be inherited by vertical transmission, through gametes, embryo colonization and endosperm (Chesneau et al., 2022). Contrary to common sense, a higher level of bacterial vertical transmission (from parent seed to offspring) than acquisition from the rhizosphere (25.5% vs. 10.7%) has been reported in rice seeds (Zhou et al., 2020). Interestingly, a recent study using source‐sink modelling further revealed that parental seeds and the stem endosphere are major origins of progeny seed microbial communities in rice (Kim et al., 2022).

Seed microbiomes can also be traced to water and air during dispersal (Shade et al., 2017). The flow of water which serves as a means of transportation of seeds from one point to another can determine the nature and types of microbes on the seed surface (Hassani et al., 2018). The seeds of SFCs can be dispersed by water during rainfall or movement by surface runoff (De Jager et al., 2019). Air microbes can be directly attached to the seed surface and their movement from one geographical location to another with dissimilar conditions may influence their population and activity. For example, it has been found that airborne fungi is the primary source for fungal communities in aerial plant parts (Latz et al., 2021). In all, microbes can be easily recruited from the soil, plant parts, pollinating agents (insects) and water (Martínez‐Diz et al., 2019). However, the main sources of seed microbiomes in many SFCs remain elusive, due to the complex microbial composition and multiple routes involved.

WHAT ARE THESE SEED MICROBES?

As addressed above, the compositions of seed microbiomes are affected by many variables. The seeds can change their quality and forms during development and storage. Therefore, microbes within them attempt to adapt to the environmental conditions (Dutta et al., 2022). It is difficult to conclude what the seed microbes are, also partially due to incomparable study designs in the literature and different conditions. To simplify the question, we here focus on mature seeds of the three SFCs using a few landmarks as representatives. From different aspects, we extract data of dominant seed endophytes among the three selected SFCs from some key reports (Johnston‐Monje et al., 2021; Ren et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2022).

Based on an amplicon sequencing, it was found that seeds of three SFCs (Tritucum aestivum var. Prospect, Oryza sativa ssp. japonica var. Nipponbare and Zea mays ssp. mays var. B73) were all dominated by Proteobacteria and Ascomycetes, at phylum level (Johnston‐Monje et al., 2021). At genus level, Pantoea is the most abundant bacteria in wheat and rice (>50%), while Pseudomonas (~31%) is the most abundant in maize (Figure 3A–C). All SFCs harbour Pseudomonas and Bacillus, though only the former lists on the top 10 abundant genera. Alternaria is the most abundant fungi in wheat and rice, while Sarocladium is the most abundant in maize (Figure 3D–F). All seeds are enriched by Alternaria and Fusarium, indicating their prevalence in SFCs.

Due to the importance of seed endophytes, 1392 bacterial and 636 fungal isolates were identified from wheat seeds (Ren et al., 2023). As shown in Figure 4A,B, Curtobacterium, Bacillus and Pantoea are the most abundant bacterial genera, while Aureobasidium, Fusarium and Alternaria are the richest in fungal genera. However, a bit different from the previous reports, it was found that wheat and maize are mainly composed of three bacterial genera (Bacillus, Paenibacillus and Curtobactreium) and one fungal, Aspergillus, based on both sequencing and culturing techniques (Tkalec et al., 2022). In addition, wheat and maize share only less than 10% microbial population under both conventional and organic farming systems.

Vertical transmission of seed microbiomes plays an important role in nutrient uptake and stress attenuation for plants. A large‐scale analysis of the microbiomes of two generations of six different rice varieties from four geographic locations provided valuable insights into the vertical transmission of seed bacterial endophytes (Zhang et al., 2022). As shown in Figure 4C,D, the top genera, Pantoea and Pseudomonas are presented in both parent and progeny seeds of rice. Among these amplicon sequence variants (ASVs), ASV_2 (Pantoea) and ASV_48 (Xanthomonas) were identified as core potentially vertically transmitted taxa due to their existence across generations, frequent isolation from seeds, and high average nucleotide and core protein identities when isolated from the parental and offspring seeds. Although plenty of seed endophytes have been quantified and characterized, investigating and determining the ecological importance of seed microbiomes is still necessary. Using genomic techniques to fully know what seed endophytic microbes are and their abundance can help in engineering their bioproducts to maximally ensure higher and safer production.

WHY DO MICROBES CHOOSE SEEDS AS THEIR DESTINATION AND WHEN DO THEY SETTLE DOWN?

The choice of seeds for microbes may be due to their impacts on seed conservation, their growth potential and growth enhancement in different soils (Wallace, 2023). On one hand, microbes associated or within seeds might have higher survival chances under harsh conditions, considering the unique physical attributes of the seed (Bergmann & Leveau, 2022). To favourably enhance plant growth, seed microbes need good environmental conditions to stably proliferate and metabolize. The selection of seeds as a destination can be either selective or random for different microbes, because of the higher bioavailability of diverse organic and inorganic substrates for microbes in seed than in other niches. It has been found that beneficial endophytes prefer to transmit in seeds due to microbe‐plant mutualism (Bergna et al., 2018). On the other hand, various factors influencing the growth of plants may exert effects on the seed microbes and their host specificity (Li et al., 2020). The host specificity of certain groups of microbes may vary since most of them colonize seeds internally, while others through external sources. In fact, seed microbiomes could impact transgenerational effects on plants via parental modification of symbiont presence/absence, load, products, genotype or species composition or symbiont priming (Gundel et al., 2017). The changes that are usually observed at the seeds' maturity stage with an increase in the seeds' osmotic pressure may cause a greater shift in the seed microbiomes (Abdelfattah et al., 2023). For example, it has been found that barely bacterial community compositions are associated with the total and reducing sugar contents and the viscosity of the seeds, thus possibly connected to the osmotic pressure tolerance of the endophytes (Chen et al., 2022). Therefore, it seems most microbes find seeds as their destinations based on their nature, compartments, maturity and cultivars. Hence, obtaining data through comparative studies of these factors can provide information to possibly address this issue.

The settlement of microbes in seeds may rely on the possession of cellular organelles, such as pili, flagella and fimbriae, which mediate their attachment to the surfaces through entry sites and internal tissue colonization (Ulrich et al., 2021). The natural tendency of microbes to settle in seeds can be continuous or discontinuous. The modification of the plant's internal environment through the secretion of hydrolytic enzymes can facilitate microbial intrusion and dissemination in the seed endosphere. Naturally, seed cracks or seed germination may provide an environment for microbes to settle through entry points of root emergence (Banhara et al., 2015).

To precisely determine when microbes colonize or settle in the seeds to become communists, it is required to perform an inoculation with particular strains and monitor their efficacy. Inoculation of certain bacterial genera to form communities has been reported. However, whether the presence of these microbes is persistent or transient in seeds is undetermined. Along with planted seed germination, the microbial interactions at the soil‐seed interface begin. Although diverse microbes colonize the seed and settle there, only a few become communists through vertical transmission (Zhang et al., 2022). Furthermore, microbes access the seed endosphere by increasing the chance of colonization pattern after inoculation (Li et al., 2023). The colonization of microorganisms in plants involves recruitment, attachment, entry (mainly through natural discontinuities like root hairs, stomata and wounds) and occupation (Kandel et al., 2017). It has been reported that bacterial communities in rice seeds are introduced at the milky and dough stages, and they persist during seed maturation (Dutta et al., 2022). Moreover, the culturable bacterial population gradually increases during the ripening stage, whereas it decreases during storage. Therefore, it seems the time points for microbes to settle down depend on seed states. Research so far on how seed microbiomes settle and become communists is scare, and understanding this concept will aid stable and practical seed microbiome engineering.

HOW DO SEED MICROBIOMES WORK AND CAN BE MANIPULATED?

How seed microbiomes work and can be manipulated for more benefits deserves more attention in order to disclose their profound roles in sustaining ecosystems (Walitang et al., 2017). Some seed‐borne endophytes in SFCs with plant growth attributes have been presented (Table 1), although how to harness them maximally requires further studies. Seed microbiomes confer competitive ecological services by promoting plant growth, sustaining soil health against pathogens and mitigating oxidative stress, thus improving crop yield. The functional traits of seed microbiomes can be linked to their genes involved in plant responsiveness to diverse metabolic processes.

Effects of seed microbiomes on SFCs

Host‐associated microbiomes are central to food production systems and human nutrition and health (Jurburg et al., 2022). Seed microbiomes influence plant performance in several ways. For instance, they improve plant growth and health, enhance resistance against pathogens and boost plant responses to environmental stresses. The effects of seed endophytes, such as bioremediation, disease suppression and modulation of nutrients are evident (Pal et al., 2021; Samreen et al., 2021). Boosting of plant growth can be achieved by improving the bioavailability of nutrients (both macro‐ and micro‐), secreting hormones and enhancing plant photosynthetic rate (Liu et al., 2023). Stress tolerance can be increased by degradation of toxic and recalcitrant organic compounds and antagonism to phytopathogens due to production of different enzymes and metabolites (Sharma et al., 2023; Singh et al., 2018). The level of competition for space and nutrients in the seed endosphere is usually minimal compared with the external surfaces or rhizosphere due to the enclosed environment. Considering that some seed microbial inhabitants can be vertically transmitted from one plant generation to another, they are full of ecological promise to contribute to the plant microbial assemblage and the establishment of plant‐microbe interactions.

Application of SFCs seed endophytes

In contrast to microbiomes associated with the roots and leaves of SFCs, less information is available on the application and ecological services of seed endophytes.

As demonstrated, one approach to improving crop yield and microbial functions is the biofortification of seeds through inoculation with plant growth‐promoting endophytes (Trivedi et al., 2020). Inoculation of rice with seed endophytes not only enhances seedling germination but also enhances the functions of other plant microbes (Walitang et al., 2017). We have shown that endophytic Bacillus sp. improve wheat yield and grain quality, suggesting their potential in sustainable agriculture (Sun et al., 2021). Under normal or extreme conditions, seed endophytes can contribute immensely to plant growth and crop yield in the field. For instance, the increase in the yield of maize when inoculated with seed endophytic bacterial strains has been attributed to their ability to produce certain plant growth stimulators (e.g. hormones, siderophores and elicitors) and improve plant tolerance to stress (Siddique et al., 2022). Seed‐borne endophytes with antifungal activities have been employed in managing pathogenic infestations on plants (Newcombe et al., 2023; Sandhya et al., 2017; Yang et al., 2020). The characterization of seed‐borne endophytes to determine their functions and application on crops could greatly enrich nutrient‐limited environments and sustain plant health and growth in terms of yield enhancement, pathogen suppression and plant resilience to stresses (Gerna et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2023). These concepts can bring novel opportunities to help achieve food security. Thus, the incorporation of seed microbiomes as core biological tools will help advance and transform the agricultural system.

Manipulation of seed microbiomes

To enhance crop performance and sustainability, strategies for microbiome manipulation have been developed (French et al., 2021). Five different strategies, including host‐mediated and multi‐generation selection, inoculation into soil and rhizosphere, inoculation into seeds or seedlings, tissue atomization and direct injection into tissues or wounds, have been proposed for bioengineering of the endophyte microbiome (Orozco‐Mosqueda et al., 2018). To be specific, SFCs seed microbiomes can be intentionally manipulated by direct inoculation with exogenous microbes, environmental factor stimulation and plant host‐mediated engineering/selection. The aforementioned can result in the alteration of seed cultivars, seed microbial community structures and functions. Although their establishment, persistence and viability under field applications are challenging, identifying the strategies for the effective use of seed microbes in formulating bioinoculants under optimum conditions can be one of the approaches to maximally explore their potentialities (Mukherjee et al., 2021).

Microbial inoculation is a widely applied method for microbiome engineering. Introducing beneficial bacteria at flowering into progeny seeds has provided a new approach to seed microbiomes manipulation in wheat (Mitter et al., 2017). Seed biopriming by microbial coating on seeds can facilitate beneficial microbes refine plant and soil microbiomes and maintain plant health (Singh et al., 2023). Also, the adjustment of environmental factors using biological and chemical substances (like chemical and fertilizer inputs, soil amendments), singly or in combination, can change the composition of seed microbiomes (Albright et al., 2022). For instance, virulence effector VdAve1 facilitates fungal pathogen colonization through the manipulation of plant microbiomes by suppressing antagonistic bacteria, demonstrating that effector proteins can be used for modulating microbiome compositions inside and outside the plant host (Snelders et al., 2020).

In addition, seed microbiome engineering can be achieved through host‐mediated selection, because microbiome assembly along the soil–plant continuum is shaped predominantly by compartment niche and host species (Trivedi et al., 2020; Xiong et al., 2021; Zeng et al., 2017). Host‐mediated microbiome selection is a strategy that utilizes the host genotype/phenotype to indirectly select microbiomes though cyclic differentiation and propagation (Jochum et al., 2019). For example, host phenotype of delayed onset of seedling water deficit stress symptoms was used to select a microbial community that mediated a 5‐day delay in the onset of drought symptoms in wheat seedlings (Jochum et al., 2019). Moreover, plants with host‐mediated selected microbiomes had 55%–205% greater seed production than plants with unselected control microbiomes under sodium salt stress or aluminium salt stress (Mueller et al., 2021). The identification of diverse seed‐borne pathogenic and toxigenic fungi in maize plants is attracting great attention, considering potential issues around food safety and plant health (Shabana et al., 2022). Hence, efficient engineering strategies may accelerate the exploration of seed microbiomes as a promising direction for averting agricultural problems.

CONCLUDING REMARKS

Exploring seed microbiomes, especially endophytes of SFCs, is an emerging area of interest in agricultural biotechnology. Employing community profiling approaches has unravelled the total seed microbiomes to ascertain the core microbial genera based on the host specificity, genotypes and microbial and environmental interactions. With research advancement, the diverse environmental factors that influence seed microbial diversity and functions are now being studied. Understanding the mechanisms employed by seed endophytes remains important, and they should be further investigated in future studies. Due to the nature, diversity and multifaceted ecological services of plant microbes in improving SFCs, the emphasis on seed endophytes aims at providing answers to the yet unanswered questions relating to the science and critical components of seed microbiomes. The application of seed endophytic bioproducts for higher crop productivity will better inform researchers on their maximum deployment towards sustainable food production. Studies on the seed microbiomes of SFCs will ultimately help in providing information on the 5W1H concepts to comprehensively understand the issue at hand. Diverse use of seed endophytes through strategic conservative measures with multifaceted opportunities could help achieve food security.
