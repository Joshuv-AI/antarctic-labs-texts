A rapid review of digital health applications supporting self-care during pregnancy and postpartum period
Authors: Yasaman Jafari, Elham Shakibazadeh, Reza Rabiei, Hossein Sadr, Seyed Hamid Hosseini, Rezvan Rajabzadeh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWith the growing adoption of mobile technologies, mHealth applications have emerged as valuable tools for enhancing maternal healthcare by providing accessible and flexible support throughout pregnancy and postpartum period. Despite their widespread use, limited evidence exists on how these apps are designed and evaluated for self-care, highlighting the need for a structured review.MethodsA rapid review was conducted using a structured search in PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science to identify studies focusing on digital applications delivered through mobile or web-based platforms that support self-care during pregnancy and postpartum. Eligible studies included randomized controlled trials, study protocols, and development papers published from 2015 to 2025. Data were extracted and narratively synthesized to examine app features, theoretical foundations, and evaluation strategies.ResultsA total of 13 studies were included, primarily evaluating mobile health (mHealth) interventions during pregnancy and postpartum period. The applications predominantly targeted mental health, lifestyle behaviors, and health literacy. Randomized controlled trials were the most common study design. Although several studies applied behavioral theories such as Cognitive Behavioral Therapy and Social Cognitive Theory, many lacked explicit theoretical frameworks.ConclusionsmHealth applications show promise in supporting maternal self-care, particularly for postpartum mental health. However, many interventions lack theoretical grounding and long-term evidence. Future efforts should prioritize scalable, theory-based, and culturally tailored designs to enhance effectiveness and sustainability.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12884-026-09677-y.

Introduction

With the growing integration of technology into everyday life, mobile health (mHealth) applications have become influential tools in modern healthcare. These applications designed for smartphones and other mobile devices offer users convenient access to a wide range of health-related information, resources, and services, thereby improving patient engagement and health outcomes [1–3].

In recent years, the field of maternal health has particularly benefited from this digital transformation. As medical technologies related to pregnancy and childbirth have advanced, mobile apps and websites have increasingly entered this domain, offering innovative support for women during the prenatal and postnatal periods [4, 5]. Several widely used apps, such as What to Expect, Ovia Pregnancy, and Pregnancy+, provide features like personalized fetal development tracking, symptom monitoring, educational content, and virtual communities. Others like Flo, which evolved from a menstrual tracker to a comprehensive reproductive health tool, and Bonzun, which offers a virtual midwife interface, demonstrate the diverse capabilities of mobile platforms in supporting maternal well-being across different contexts [6].

One of the key advantages of mHealth apps is their flexibility. Unlike traditional, in-person care models, these applications can be accessed anytime and anywhere, which is especially valuable for pregnant women and new mothers who may face time constraints or logistical barriers. Home-based health behavior interventions delivered through mobile platforms are gaining popularity for precisely this reason they address many of the unique challenges mothers encounter in engaging with conventional programs [7, 8].

Moreover, mobile technology plays a vital role in promoting behavior change through information dissemination. During pregnancy and postpartum periods, women increasingly turn to digital media such as websites, blogs, discussion forums, and social media to seek health-related knowledge and peer support [9]. Among these, mobile apps stand out as particularly accessible and user-friendly tools. Studies show that over half of pregnant women use at least one app related to pregnancy, childbirth, or childcare [10, 11]. This widespread adoption reflects a global trend, as mobile health apps have become one of the most accessible sources of information and support for expectant mothers [12, 13]. According to a systematic review by Ameyaw et al. [14], the use of mHealth apps for maternal health care has increased globally, particularly in high-income countries, where these interventions have shown effectiveness in areas such as maternal anxiety and depression, gestational weight management, and behavioral modifications during pregnancy.

The growing reliance on digital health tools highlights their potential as scalable interventions to enhance maternal and fetal health outcomes worldwide.

Despite the increasing integration of mobile health (mHealth) technologies into maternal care, there remains a limited consolidated understanding of how these applications are conceptualized, developed, and evaluated particularly those aimed at promoting self-care during pregnancy and the postpartum period.

To address this gap, the present study conducts a rapid review to systematically identify and analyze existing digital applications (mobile or web-based) designed to support maternal self-care. The review focuses on the characteristics of these interventions including their goals, functionalities, theoretical foundations, and evaluation designs in order to extract critical insights that can inform the future design and development of an evidence-based, user-centered mHealth application tailored to the needs of women in this period.

Methods

A rapid review was undertaken to provide a timely synthesis of the existing literature about the design and development of a mobile application for maternal self-care due to the time-constrained nature of the development phase. This approach provided a streamlined yet systematic synthesis of evidence. Compared with a scoping review, which broadly maps available evidence, a rapid review is more focused on addressing specific research questions while applying time-efficient methods [15]. The review included both interventions that had already been developed and evaluated, as well as protocols and app design studies.

Search strategy

All searches were conducted between April 1 and May 20, 2025. A focused search strategy was employed to retrieve relevant literature on digital health applications (mobile or web‑based) for self-care in pregnancy and postpartum. Searches were conducted in PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science using combinations of keywords and MeSH terms such as “Mobile Applications,” “Self-Care,” “Pregnancy,” and “Postpartum Period.” Boolean operators (e.g., “Pregnancy AND Mobile Applications”) were used to refine the search. In the initial search strategy, an additional concept related to engagement features (e.g., “gamification,” “interactive, or “behavioral intervention”) was included to identify applications incorporating behavior‑change or interactive components. This was intended to capture apps designed to actively support self-care behaviors. However, we acknowledge that such terminology may not be consistently used in the indexing or description of maternal self‑care applications. The search was limited to peer-reviewed articles published in English between January 2015 and May 2025. Studies specifically targeting women with chronic medical conditions (e.g., diabetes, hypertension) were excluded.

Truncation was not applied in the search strings, which may have limited retrieval of term variations. This was noted as a methodological limitation.

Screening and selection process

Titles and abstracts retrieved from the database searches were screened for relevance based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Initial screening was conducted by one reviewer. Full texts of potentially relevant articles were then assessed independently by two reviewers to confirm eligibility. Disagreements were resolved through discussion and consensus. Reasons for exclusion at the full-text stage were documented.

Owing to the rapid review methodology, title and abstract screening was initially performed by one reviewer. To mitigate the potential risk of selection bias, all potentially eligible full-text articles were independently assessed by two reviewers, and any disagreements were resolved through discussion until consensus was reached.

Data extraction

A structured data extraction form was developed in Microsoft Excel and piloted prior to use. The form was initially tested on a small subset of included studies to assess clarity, completeness, and consistency of the predefined extraction fields. Based on this pilot phase, minor refinements were made to variable definitions and data categories before full data extraction was undertaken. Data extraction was primarily conducted by the first author using a standardized extraction form. To minimize potential extraction errors and reviewer bias, extracted data were independently checked by a second reviewer, and any uncertainties or discrepancies were resolved through discussion with the research team.

Quality assessment

Although the same JBI tool was applied across different RCT designs, this approach is consistent with rapid review guidance where methodological streamlining is recommended. One methodological study aimed at developing and validating a mobile application was identified but not included in the quality appraisal table, as it did not report intervention outcomes. Intervention characteristics

A total of 13 studies were included, focusing on digital health interventions during pregnancy and the postpartum period. The majority of interventions (n = 12) were delivered through mobile applications, while one study utilized a web‑based platform. Some interventions were implemented as standalone digital tools, whereas others were combined with additional components such as social media platforms (e.g., Facebook) or coaching sessions (Table 2). Nine records could not be retrieved because the full texts were not accessible through institutional subscriptions or publicly available sources. Several of the included trials were conducted as pilot or preliminary studies aimed at evaluating feasibility and initial effectiveness. All descriptions of intervention features were directly extracted from the included studies (Table 3), and citations have been added accordingly.

Targeted outcomes and populations

Most interventions aimed to improve mental health outcomes, particularly the prevention or management of postpartum depression, perinatal depression, or maternal selfcare. Others addressed dietary behaviors, physical activity, gestational weight gain, or health literacy. The timing of interventions varied, with many starting during pregnancy and continuing into the postpartum period, while others focused exclusively on the postpartum phase.

Study designs

The 13 included studies comprised different research designs. Most were completed intervention trials (n = 11), including standard randomized controlled trials, a small RCT, and a randomized pilot study. In addition, one paper was a protocol for a planned cluster randomized controlled trial, and one was a methodological study describing the development and content validation of a mobile application. The protocol and the development study were included to illustrate emerging intervention designs and app characteristics but were not considered as evidence of intervention effectiveness.

Theoretical foundations

Only a minority of studies explicitly reported a guiding theoretical framework. When stated by the study authors, the most frequently mentioned frameworks included Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT), Social Cognitive Theory, and the Transtheoretical Model. However, several studies did not explicitly report a theoretical basis for the intervention design. Therefore, only explicitly reported theories were considered when summarizing the theoretical grounding of the interventions.

Domain-specific interventions patterns

The included interventions could be broadly categorized into three main domains: mental health, lifestyle modification, and health literacy/self-care education.

Five interventions primarily targeted maternal mental health during pregnancy or the postpartum period [17, 22, 27]. Although the specific intervention strategies differed, these applications generally combined educational content with psychological support components such as psychoeducation, relaxation exercises, emotional regulation, self-monitoring, and motivational messages. Some interventions additionally incorporated professional consultation or lifestyle coaching, whereas others were designed as entirely self-guided mobile applications [17–19, 22].

Another five interventions focused on promoting healthy lifestyle behaviors during pregnancy, particularly nutrition, physical activity, gestational weight gain, and dietary self-management [21, 25, 26, 28]. Across these studies, the most frequently reported intervention components included food or activity tracking, goal setting, personalized feedback, educational materials, and progress monitoring. Most of these interventions were delivered through mobile applications, while one study employed a web-based platform to facilitate dietary monitoring and nutritional counseling [21].

Three interventions primarily addressed maternal health literacy and self-care education [4, 20, 24]. These applications mainly provided structured educational resources covering topics such as prenatal care, nutrition, physical activity, stress management, breastfeeding, newborn care, appointment reminders, and communication with healthcare professionals. Compared with mental health and lifestyle interventions, these studies placed greater emphasis on information delivery and educational support rather than intensive behavior-change strategies.

Across all included studies, mobile applications were the predominant delivery platform (12 of 13 studies), with only one intervention using a web-based application [21]. Self-monitoring was the most frequently implemented feature (11 studies), followed by reminders or notifications (9 studies) and feedback or progress reports (6 studies). Direct or indirect communication with healthcare professionals was comparatively uncommon, being reported in only three interventions. Overall, the reviewed applications most commonly targeted postpartum mental health (n = 5) and healthy lifestyle behaviors during pregnancy (n = 5), whereas relatively few focused primarily on maternal health literacy or comprehensive mother–infant care (Table 4).

Discussion

This rapid review included studies that used digital applications delivered through mobile or web‑based platforms as the primary intervention tool. These findings indicate the growing use of digital technologies in maternal health as multifunctional tools to support various aspects of self-care and health promotion.

Although this review identified several completed intervention studies evaluating digital applications for maternal self‑care, two included papers did not report effectiveness outcomes: one was a study protocol and another described the development and validation of an application. Additionally, Brunelli et al. [23] was retained because, despite addressing a broader age window, the intervention met the core definition of a maternal self‑care digital tool. These studies were included to provide insight into emerging intervention designs but were not considered as evidence of effectiveness.

The majority of studies employed randomized controlled trial (RCT) designs, reflecting an acceptable level of scientific rigor in assessing intervention effectiveness. However, many of these trials were conducted on a pilot or experimental scale, highlighting the need for larger, multicenter studies. A notable variation was observed in the type, duration, and structure of the interventions. Some apps, such as LoVE4MUM [17] and Smart Mama [18], were specifically focused on mental health, while others like HealthyMoms [25] and BeFAB [27] adopted more multidimensional approaches.

Across studies, interventions most frequently combined education, self‑monitoring, and reminder functions, whereas behavior‑change mechanisms such as goal‑setting, feedback loops, or social support were inconsistently applied. This pattern may partly explain the mixed outcomes reported in the included trials and reflects the early developmental stage of mHealth interventions for maternal self‑care.

Although a few interventions were grounded in behavioral theories such as Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) [17, 22], Social Cognitive Theory (SCT), or the Transtheoretical Model (TTM), in several studies, the intervention design or the application itself was not explicitly grounded in a behavioral theory. This is a significant limitation, as interventions designed based on theory tend to be more effective and allow for greater replicability and generalizability. Concerningly, many mHealth apps are not based on any behavior change theory, and in many cases, their effectiveness has not been adequately evaluated [30, 31]. This is particularly relevant considering the growing recognition of mobile apps’ potential to induce behavior change in digital health interventions [32]. Without a solid theoretical foundation, the long-term and sustained impact of behavior change promoted by these applications remains questionable.

Various features were employed to enhance user engagement, including reminders, notifications, progress dashboards, self-monitoring tools, gamification elements, and opportunities for communication with counselors or support groups. Apps that incorporated multimedia content, interactivity, and professional consultation were more likely to achieve higher levels of user interaction and effectiveness.

Cultural and geographic diversity was considered in some interventions for example, BeFAB [27] targeted African American women, and PurUmeed Aaghaz [26] was developed in an Asian context. This highlights the importance of culturally tailored content, as adapting the language, format, and content of mHealth interventions to local norms and conditions can positively affect their uptake and effectiveness. If an mHealth app lacks contextual or cultural alignment with its target audience, there is a risk of low adoption or misinterpretation. For instance, an app designed for prenatal care in European countries may not be effectively translated to sub-Saharan African contexts due to differences in language, beliefs, and healthcare infrastructure.

Despite notable progress in the field of maternal digital health, several important research and practical gaps persist. Many studies lack a clearly defined theoretical framework to guide intervention design and interpretation of outcomes. There is also limited evidence on the long-term sustainability of intervention effects and the persistence of behavioral changes over time. Furthermore, few studies have comprehensively evaluated how various app components interact to influence maternal health outcomes. Another key challenge lies in sustaining user engagement throughout the intervention period, which remains a critical factor for achieving meaningful and lasting health improvements.

Based on the findings of this review, several implications can be drawn for both future research and practical application. Future studies should employ well-defined theoretical frameworks to guide the design and implementation of interventions, ensuring that the underlying behavioral mechanisms are clearly articulated. Incorporating interactive, multimedia, and personalized consultation features may help enhance user engagement and adherence, although these aspects were not directly evaluated in the present review. Moreover, interventions should be tailored to the local cultural, linguistic, and social contexts to improve relevance and accessibility for target populations. Longitudinal evaluations are also needed to assess the long-term sustainability of health behaviors and outcomes during the postpartum period. Finally, future research should include cost-effectiveness analyses to determine the feasibility of integrating such digital health interventions into national maternal health programs.

Limitations

A rapid review approach was adopted to provide timely evidence to inform the design of a maternal self-care mobile application within the constraints of the project timeline and available resources.

This review has several limitations. First, methodological adaptations inherent to rapid reviews including time-limited searching, single-reviewer title and abstract screening, primary data extraction by one reviewer with independent verification by a second reviewer, and exclusion of grey literature may have reduced the comprehensiveness of the evidence and introduced selection or extraction bias compared with a full systematic review. However, full-text screening was conducted independently by two reviewers, and discrepancies were resolved through discussion. Restricting the review to English-language publications may also have introduced language bias.

Second, the review was limited to interactive mobile or web-based applications, excluding SMS-only interventions and passive monitoring systems, which may have omitted relevant evidence from other digital health modalities. In addition, the search strategy included engagement-related terms (e.g., gamification, interactive, and behavioral intervention), which may have unintentionally reduced retrieval because such terminology is not consistently used in maternal health app studies.

Third, only studies involving generally healthy pregnant or postpartum women were included, limiting the generalizability of the findings to women with specific medical conditions. Finally, limited access to some commercial or proprietary applications may have prevented comprehensive assessment of all relevant intervention features, and end users were not directly involved in shaping the review questions or interpreting the findings.

Future research should include multilingual databases, broader maternal populations, greater end-user involvement, and longitudinal implementation studies to better evaluate the real-world effectiveness and sustainability of maternal mHealth interventions.

Conclusion

This rapid review provides an overview of the growing role of mobile health (mHealth) applications as potentially valuable tools for supporting maternal self-care, with a predominant focus on mental health during the postpartum period. The findings highlight both the potential and limitations of current app-based interventions. While several studies demonstrated acceptable methodological rigor, often using randomized controlled designs, many were pilot studies with limited evidence regarding scalability and sustainability. Furthermore, the absence of clearly defined theoretical frameworks in many interventions raises concerns about their effectiveness and replicability.

Engagement features such as gamification, multimedia content, and access to professional support were associated with increased user interaction, suggesting that design elements may play an important role in user engagement and potentially in the success of digital interventions. Additionally, culturally tailored apps appeared more likely to be accepted by diverse populations, emphasizing the importance of contextual relevance in mHealth design.

Despite encouraging trends, significant research gaps remain, including limited long-term outcome data, insufficient exploration of how specific app components influence behavior change, and challenges in sustaining user engagement. While several digital interventions have been developed to support maternal self-care during pregnancy and the postpartum period, the number of rigorously evaluated interventions remains limited, highlighting the need for further high-quality randomized controlled trials.

Overall, this rapid review primarily provides a descriptive overview of the current landscape of mHealth applications for maternal self-care and aims to inform future intervention design. Although these tools show potential, stronger and more consistent evidence will be needed to determine their effectiveness and long-term impact on maternal health outcomes.
