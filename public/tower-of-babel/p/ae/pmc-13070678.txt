Effects of fixed and changing forage NDF levels on intake, growth performance, and carcass traits of Nellore bulls in the feedlot
Authors: Mailza Gonçalves de Souza, Filipe Araújo Canêdo Mendonça, João Pedro Azevedo Ventorin, Guilherme Ortega Zulim, Matheus Copeli Garcia, Rafael Rodrigues Afonso, Daniel Júdice Gonçalves, Dante Pazzanese Duarte Lanna
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGreater concentrate and reduced fiber intake may induce metabolic disorders, such as acidosis, laminitis, and gastrointestinal inflammation, thereby impairing growth performance. This study evaluated the effects of fixed and changing fNDF inclusion on growth performance, intake, digestion, and carcass traits of Nellore bulls during the feedlot. After a 21-day adaptation, 56 intact males (initial body weight (BW) = 460 ± 38 kg; 20 months) were fed for 100 days in a completely randomized design. Data were analyzed across three periods. During the first period (0–50 days), animals received diets containing either 12% or 7% fNDF. In the second period (51–100 days), one pen per treatment remained on the same diet, whereas the others switched fNDF levels (12→7% or 7→12% fNDF). Over the 100-day trial, animals were evaluated individually. Corn silage was the sole forage source, and data were analyzed using SAS. During the first 50 days, bulls fed 12% fNDF exhibited greater ADG (1.71 vs 1.43 kg/d; P = 0.01), DMI (10.97 vs 10.00 kg/d; P = 0.01), feed efficiency (0.154 vs 0.138; P = 0.04), Net energy for gain (NEg) (9.96 vs 7.93 Mcal/d; P = 0.01), and dry matter intake for gain (DMIg) (7.12 vs 6.01 kg/d; P = 0.01) compared with those fed 7% fNDF. From 51 to 100 days, DMI was greater for 12% fNDF than for 7% fNDF, whereas diets that switched fNDF levels did not differ from each other. Across the 100-day period, 12% and 12→7% fNDF resulted in greater DMI and DMIg than 7% and 7→12% fNDF (P = 0.01). Carcass traits and water intake were unaffected by treatment (P > 0.05). Fecal pH was greater in animals fed 12% fNDF during the first period, whereas after dietary changes, fecal pH of the 12→7% fNDF treatment was similar to that of 7% and 7→12% fNDF. Over the entire trial, fecal starch concentration and starch digestibility differed between the 12→7% and 7→12% fNDF treatments (P < 0.01), with greater fecal starch concentration and reduced starch digestibility observed in the 12→7% fNDF treatment. The hypothesis that increased dietary fiber would improve growth performance over the entire feedlot was not confirmed. Overall, diets containing 12% fNDF enhanced ADG, DMI, and feed efficiency during the first 50 days of the feedlot, but these advantages were not maintained throughout the 100-day trial.

Introduction

Forage intake is pivotal in stimulating chewing activity and energy intake in feedlot systems (Llonch et al. 2020). Increased mastication enhances salivary secretion, thereby contributing to the regulation of ruminal pH. Fiber also affects gastrointestinal motility, osmolarity, and ruminal passage rate. Maintaining a stable ruminal pH is crucial in mitigating the risk of metabolic disorders, such as acidosis, and its potential secondary consequences, including laminitis, liver abscesses, and systemic inflammation (NASEM 2016).

Zebu cattle (Bos indicus) have been reported to perform better on greater fiber diets than taurine breeds (Bos taurus), as they differ in both anatomical and physiological traits (Moore et al. 1975; Olbrich 1996; Chizzotti et al. 2009; Goulart et al. 2020). However, previous studies have demonstrated that purebred Zebus face greater challenges when fed greater concentrate diets than taurine and crossbred cattle (Olbrich 1996; Caetano et al. 2015).

Zinn et al. (2004) reported that forage inclusion in finishing diets has a biphasic effect on intake and weight gain. Adding fiber to all-concentrate diets increases energy intake up to an optimal level of about 11% fNDF. Beyond this point, more forage decreases total energy intake. Caetano et al. (2015) observed a similar pattern but found a slightly greater optimal fiber level for purebred Zebu cattle at 12.8% fNDF. Previous studies have demonstrated that in Nellore cattle, there was a much more pronounced drop in dry matter and energy intake when diets contained less than 8% forage (Caetano et al. 2015).

A recent Brazilian feedlot survey by Monsalve and Millen (2025) reported an average forage inclusion of 15.7% in the dry matter, with corn silage being the main forage source. These commercial fNDF levels (around 7.5% fNDF/dry matter (DM)) are significantly smaller than the 11% neutral detergent fiber (NDF) proposed by Zinn et al. (2004) and even smaller than the 12.8% fNDF/DM by Caetano et al. (2015).

Castillo-Lopez et al. (2014) demonstrated that reduced-fiber diets decrease rumination and are associated with increased risk of metabolic disorders. The incidence of these disorders increased with longer exposure to reduced fiber diets. These findings led to the hypothesis tested in this study, which suggests that increasing fiber levels may reduce such problems while improving health, growth performance, and feed efficiency.

The objective of this study was to evaluate the inclusion of two different levels of fNDF in the diets of feedlot-finished Nellore cattle. Considering the current average forage inclusion of 15.7% in Brazilian feedlots (Monsalve and Millen 2025) and an average NDF concentration of 50% in corn silage, as reported in the literature (Oliveira et al. 2017), the estimated forage NDF (fNDF) content of the reduced fiber treatment in the present study was approximately 7%. The 12% fNDF treatment represents the experimentally optimal level reported by Caetano et al. (2015).

Additionally, temporal variation in fNDF levels tested the hypothesis that the optimal levels are not constant throughout the feeding program. This study evaluated the standard practice in Brazilian feedlots of reducing fiber content and increasing the energy density of the final feedlot diet and investigated whether the subsequent increase in fiber could improve rumen health and animal performance after a period of feeding on a reduced-fiber diet.

Material and methods

All procedures adhered to CONCEA guidelines and received approval from the ESALQ/USP Animal Ethics Committee (protocol 2833020223) and were conducted at the Nutrigenomics Center in Piracicaba, Brazil.

Fifty-six Nellore bulls had an initial body weight of 460 ± 38 kg and were 20 months old. At the start of the feedlot, the animals underwent a health protocol that included deworming (Ivomec), vaccination to prevent respiratory diseases (Draxxin, Zoetis, Campinas, Brazil), vaccination against clostridial diseases (Poli Star© MSD, São Paulo, Brazil), and administration of a vitamin complex containing vitamins A, D, and E (Vigantol© Bayer, São Paulo, Brazil).

Analysis of diet components

Feed analyses (Table 1) quantified starch, NDF, acid detergent fiber (ADF), ash, fatty acids, and crude protein (CP) using a Leco FP-200 analyzer (Leco Corp©., St. Joseph, MI). Chemical analyses were conducted according to the guidelines of the Association of Official Analytical Chemists (AOAC 1997), while NDF and ADF were determined as described by Van Soest et al. (1991). Dry matter was measured in a forced-air oven at 105°C (AOAC Method 934.01). Additionally, daily silage samples were analyzed for dry matter to enable daily diet adjustments.

Diet management

The diets were mixed using an American Calan mixer wagon with a 5-minute mixing time (Northwood, New Hampshire, USA). The particle size distribution of the corn silage and diets (Table 2) was determined using a sieve box (Santa Fé Agroinstituto, PR, Brazil) following the method described by Lammers et al. (1996). Physically effective neutral detergent fiber (peNDF) was analyzed according to Mertens (1997). The diet was offered twice a day, 50% at 0700 h. and 50% at 1600 h, and prepared just before feeding. Leftovers were weighed daily to adjust the amount provided. Diets were formulated using RLM software (Lanna et al. 1999; Ração de Lucro Máximo, 2021; www.rlm.app.br) to meet the requirements for protein, degradable protein, minerals, and vitamins.

Experimental design

The study was conducted from May to September, lasting 121 days, including 21 days of adaptation with a gradual reduction in fiber (Table 3). The remaining 100 days were split into three experimental periods. During the first period (days 0 to 50), two treatments were tested: greater concentrate diets containing 12% or 7% fNDF on a DM basis (28 animals per treatment). The animals were subjected to four treatments in the second period (days 51–100) (14 animals per treatment). Two pens maintained the original treatments from the first period (12% or 7% fNDF), while one pen that initially received 12% was switched to a reduced 7% fNDF level, and another pen that started with 7% was changed to an increased 12% fNDF level (Table 4; Fig. 1).

Experimental design and timeline of the feeding trial.

The 100-day experiment was divided into three periods: the first (0–50), the second (51–100), and the overall (0–100). During the first period, animals were assigned to diets containing either 12% or 7% forage neutral detergent fiber (12% fNDF or 7% fNDF; DM basis), with 28 animals per treatment distributed across four pens, two per treatment. On day 50, two pens were redistributed to the diet, and two continued with the identical diets, resulting in four treatments in the second period (14 animals per treatment; one pen each): 12% fNDF, 7% fNDF, 12→7% fNDF, and 7→12% fNDF. This design allowed the evaluation of constant versus changing fNDF strategies and the effects of increasing or decreasing dietary fiber levels during the feedlot trial.

The experiment was carried out using a completely randomized design. Each animal was tagged with two different micro-identification devices, each operating at specific frequencies read by the Intergado (Betim—MG, BR) (body weight and water intake stations) and GrowSafe (Lenexa, KS, USA) (feed intake bunk) systems. The animals were randomly assigned to four collective pens and rotated among them every 14 days to nullify physical environmental effects of the pens, with 14 animals in each pen, using the individual animal as the experimental unit.

Measurement of live weight, water intake, and dry matter intake

Body weight and water intake were measured using four Intergado® VW 1000 automatic weighing platforms, each equipped with a water trough. Each pen was fitted with one unit, serving 14 animals. Feed intake data were recorded using eight GrowSafe® systems equipped with integrated sensors and load cells. Each pen was equipped with two GrowSafe® units, with seven animals monitored per unit. The rate of gain was estimated as the linear regression of the weights without fasting over time, as described by Ferreiro and Preston (1976). This method reduces variation caused by changes in gastrointestinal tract filling, since animals are weighed multiple times throughout the day.

Dietary net energy estimates

Estimates of energy requirements for gain (NEg) and maintenance (NEm) were calculated using daily weight gain and metabolic live weight (Lofgreen and Garrett 1968; NRC 1984), and with these parameters, the net energy values of the diet (Mcal/kg DM) for gain (NElg) and maintenance (NElm) were estimated (Zinn and Shen 1998). The daily dry matter intake required for maintenance (DMIm, kg/d) was calculated by the ratio between the daily maintenance requirement (NEm) and the net energy content for maintenance of the diet (NElm), while the dry matter intake for growth (DMIg, kg/d) was obtained by the difference between the total dry matter intake and the intake for maintenance (Silva 2001).

Fecal starch and pH analysis

Fecal samples were collected from the rectum on days 25 and 75 for analyzing fecal starch and estimating starch digestibility, and on days 21, 42, 71, and 92 for pH analysis. Samples were homogenized, dried at 65°C for 48 h, and ground in a Wiley-type mill with a 1 mm sieve. Fecal starch content was estimated using NIR spectroscopy (Foss NIRSystems Inc., Silver Spring, MD, USA) according to the equation of Caetano et al. (2009); pH was measured with a pH meter after collection, and apparent starch digestibility was calculated using the equation of Zinn et al. (2007). The proposed equation was used to estimate starch digestibility; however, it has not been validated in Zebu cattle.

Carcass evaluations

On the last day of the experimental trial, carcasses were evaluated by ultrasound using IMV/Exago equipment, with a 3.5 MHz linear transducer measuring 180 mm in length and an acoustic guide to capture images of the Ribeye area (RA), subcutaneous fat thickness (SF) between the 12th and 13th ribs, (Longissimus dorsi), and Rump Fat Thickness (FTR) under the (Biceps femoris muscle) (FTR in the rump or P8 site). The best-quality image was stored for later analysis and interpretation using Lince software. The hot carcass yield was calculated based on the live weight without fasting of the pens, applying a 4% deduction to account for gastrointestinal fill to determine fasting weight (NRC 1996).

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using SAS University Edition (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC). Data were initially tested for normality of residuals using the Shapiro-Wilk test, and potential outliers were identified using the UNIVARIATE procedure. The study followed a completely randomized design with the individual animal as the experimental unit (n = 56 in the first period and n = 14 in the second period and in period overall (100 days). This definition was supported by two critical methodological features: 1) the use of automated monitoring systems (GrowSafe® and Intergado®) which provided precise individual intake and performance data free from competition bias; and 2) the bi-weekly rotation of experimental groups among physical pens, which randomized the physical environmental effects across all dietary treatments, thereby nullifying the fixed effect of the pen.

The statistical model was implemented using the MIXED procedure. The model included dietary treatment as a fixed effect. To account for the rotation strategy, physical pen was included as a random effect, capturing the environmental variance distributed across treatments over time. An animal nested within treatment was included in the random statement to account for individual variability. For time-based analyses (0–50, 51–100, and 0–100 days), each period was analyzed separately using the same model structure. Results are presented as least square means (LSMeans), and treatment differences were considered statistically significant at P ≤ 0.05.

Results

Growth performance, DMI, and feed efficiency for the first (0–50 days), second (51–100 days), and overall (0–100 days) periods are presented in Table 5. There was no difference between treatments in either initial or final body weight. In the first period, animals fed the 12% fNDF diet exhibited greater DMI (P = 0.01), ADG (P = 0.01), and feed efficiency (P = 0.04) when compared to the 7% fNDF treatment. After the diet change, during the second period, animals fed the 7% fNDF diet continued to exhibit smaller DMI than those fed 12% fNDF or 12→7% fNDF; however, animals fed 7→12% fNDF differed from those continuously fed 12% fNDF (P = 0.01). There was no difference in ADG (P = 0.76) or feed efficiency (P = 0.85) during this period. Considering the entire 100-day period, treatments with 12% fNDF and 12→7% fNDF resulted in greater DMIg than the 7% fNDF diet and 7→12% fNDF (P = 0.01), and there was no difference in ADG (P = 0.54) and feed efficiency (P = 0.81) during this period.

Discussion

Several studies have indicated that Zebu cattle are more sensitive to greater-energy diets than taurine cattle (Moore et al. 1975; Olbrich 1996). Diets greater in rapidly fermentable carbohydrates and reduced NDF mainly threaten ruminal health because they create a more acidic environment, which can lead to metabolic disorders that decrease animal performance in the feedlot (Allen 1997; Llonch et al. 2020; Pereira et al. 2021).

Caetano et al. (2015) observed that increasing forage from 8% to 13% of fNDF/DM resulted in a 12% increase in daily gain in Zebu cattle. Other studies with Zebu have confirmed these results (Carvalho et al. 2016; Marques et al. 2016; Santos et al. 2017; De Melo et al. 2019; Rodrigues et al. 2022). Caetano et al. (2015) evaluated four forage levels, and the performance of Nellore bulls was optimized at 12.8% fNDF, whereas diets containing only 3% fNDF resulted in very reduced daily gains. Because these diets included whole cottonseed, the authors estimated that optimal performance would occur at approximately 14.5% (fNDF) in the absence of this ingredient.

Data from the first period (days 0–50) of the present study corroborate these findings, as animals receiving greater forage inclusion exhibited better performance. However, during the 100-day feedlot period, our results differ from those reported by Caetano et al. (2015), as no differences in animal performance were observed throughout the experimental period.

Brazilian feedlots have been decreasing the forage content of their diets; over the past 14 years (2009–2023), there has been a 45.5% reduction in average fiber inclusion in commercial feedlots (Monsalve and Millen 2025). These decreases can jeopardize the well-being and performance of the animals. Fiber content directly affects rumination and chewing times, which are linked to bicarbonate ion (HCO3-) production. This production acts as a buffer in the ruminal environment, helping to maintain a greater pH and prevent metabolic disorders (Owens et al. 1998; Nagaraja and Titgemeyer 2007).

In a study by Castillo-Lopez et al. (2014), animals on reduced-fiber diets for prolonged periods exhibited an increased incidence of metabolic disorders. This led to the idea that changing the fiber content during the feeding period could help prevent or reduce possible metabolic problems. This idea was tested in the second period of this study (51–100 days), when animals on the reduced fiber diet (7% fNDF) were transferred to a greater fiber diet (12% fNDF); however, we did not observe any improvement in daily gain or efficiency.

After diet change, the DMI of the switched groups (12→7% fNDF and 7→12% fNDF) did not differ from each other during the last 50 days. This result contradicts what Galyean and Defoor (2003) reported, suggesting that increasing forage inclusion from a diet with less fiber to one with a greater percentage of fiber would lead to increased intake to maintain energy levels.

Galyean and Defoor (2003) stated that greater forage inclusion reduces energy density and increases DMI. In the present study, this statement was confirmed: over the entire 100-day feedlot period, the diet containing 12% fNDF resulted in greater DMI. A similar pattern was observed by Goulart et al. (2020), who reported that animals fed 20% fNDF from corn silage had a 16% greater intake compared to those receiving a diet with 10% fNDF.

In a feedlot study, Pierce et al. (2022) subjected steers to a uniform pre-experimental diet, and during the last 58 days, three forage levels (6%, 12%, and 18% fNDF) were used. These authors found no significant differences in weight gain or final body weight among the three treatments evaluated. Our results appear to be similar, as we were unable to determine an effect of final fiber inclusion.

Our carcass results align with the performance data, showing no significant differences among treatments. Our findings are also similar to those reported by Pierce et al. (2022), who observed no changes in carcass weight and fat thickness parameters in feedlot cattle fed diets with varying forage contents.

Animals receiving 12% fNDF exhibited greater DMI, which resulted in increased net energy intake for gain during the initial phase of the trial. Galyean and Abney (2006) explored the relationships between diet NDF and DMI, and between diet NDF and net energy intake for gain. Their findings indicated that both DMI and NEg increased linearly with greater forage inclusion in the diet. The total NDF content of the diet explained 93.7% of the variation in DMI (R2 = 0.937), while the NDF content from forage accounted for 92.0% of this variation (R2 = 0.920).

In the second experimental period, swapping the fNDF levels had no effect on the gain intake data throughout the entire period, with animals in the 12→7% fNDF treatment maintaining their greater intake from the first phase, which may be associated with differences in rumen development or gastrointestinal tissue mass.

On the other hand, animals that started on a reduced fiber diet continued to have a reduced DMI over the next 50 days, despite an increase in forage content of their diets, contrary to Galyean and Abney (2006) suggestion. A reduced energy requirement may explain this, due to reduced gastrointestinal tissue mass and the energy needed to maintain these tissues.

It is also possible that differences in the animals’ health influenced the results. The 12% fNDF treatment, which resulted in greater intakes during the initial phase, may have challenged them due to increased starch intake and increased production of short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs). The reduced fiber group (7% fNDF) appeared to face a different challenge because of the lack of forage. Future studies should adopt more robust methods to evaluate both the rumen and the animals overall health.

The fecal starch content observed in this study aligns with values reported in the literature for animals fed finely ground flint corn grain (Nuñez et al. 2013; Caetano et al. 2015; De Melo et al. 2019; Johnson et al. 2020; Pereira et al. 2021).

Nevertheless, fecal starch digestibility was somewhat lower than that reported by Fornazari Neto et al. (2021), who estimated an average digestibility of 97% using the equation of Zinn et al. (2007). Fecal pH is a reliable indicator of intestinal pH, with reduced pH values associated with greater starch levels in cattle feces fed greater-grain diets (Wheeler and Noller 1977). Dietary changes influenced fecal pH by day 71, as animals switching from 12% to 7% fNDF exhibited a decrease, while those shifting from 7% to 12% fNDF observed an increase in pH.

Conclusion

A 12% fNDF diet improved DMI, gain, and efficiency during the first 50 days of the feedlot period compared to a 7% fNDF diet, consistent with previous studies by our group and others that suggest energy intake peaks at around 11–12% fNDF. However, no differences in performance were observed throughout the feeding period. The hypothesis that increasing fiber from forage could enhance performance in animals fed reduced fiber diets (7→12% fNDF) was not confirmed. Additionally, decreasing fiber levels from 12% to 7%, a common practice in finishing diets, also did not affect overall performance.
