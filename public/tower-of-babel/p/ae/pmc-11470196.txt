A virulent milRNA inhibits host immunity by silencing a host receptor‐like kinase MaLYK3 and facilitates infection by Fusarium oxysporum f. sp. cubense
Authors: Jiahui He, Jiaqi Zhong, Longqi Jin, Yike Long, Junjian Situ, Chengcheng He, Guanghui Kong, Zide Jiang, Minhui Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractMicroRNA‐like RNAs (milRNAs) play a significant role in the infection process by plant‐pathogenic fungi. However, the specific functions and regulatory mechanisms of fungal milRNAs remain insufficiently elucidated. This study investigated the function of Foc‐milR138, an infection‐induced milRNA secreted by Fusarium oxysporum f. sp. cubense (Foc), which is the causal agent of Fusarium wilt of banana. Initially, through precursor gene knockout and phenotypic assessments, we confirmed that Foc‐milR138 acts as a virulent milRNA prominently upregulated during the early stages of Foc infection. Subsequent bioinformatic analyses and transient expression assays in Nicotiana benthamiana leaves identified a host receptor‐like kinase gene, MaLYK3, as the direct target of Foc‐milR138. Functional investigations of MaLYK3 revealed its pivotal role in triggering immune responses of N. benthamiana by upregulating a suite of resistance genes, bolstering reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation and callose deposition, thereby fortifying disease resistance. This response was markedly subdued upon co‐expression with Foc‐milR138. Expression pattern analysis further verified the specific suppression of MaLYK3 by Foc‐milR138 during the early root infection by Foc. In conclusion, Foc secretes a virulent milRNA (Foc‐milR138) to enter the host banana cells and inhibit the expression of the plant surface receptor‐like kinase MaLYK3, subverting the disease resistance activated by MaLYK3, and ultimately facilitating pathogen invasion. These findings shed light on the roles of fungal milRNAs and their targets in resistance and pathogenicity, offering promising avenues for the development of disease‐resistant banana cultivars.Keywords: disease resistance, Fusarium wilt of banana, infection process, pathogenicity, small RNAs

INTRODUCTION

Banana (Musa spp.), an important agricultural crop, is widely cultivated in tropical and subtropical regions of the world (Drenth & Kema, 2021; Ordonez et al., 2015). However, banana production worldwide faces a serious threat from Fusarium wilt caused by Fusarium oxysporum f. sp. cubense (Foc), a typical soilborne fungus that produces a large number of chlamydospores that can survive in soil for decades in the absence of host plants, making it difficult to control (Ma et al., 2013; Ploetz et al., 2015). Among the four documented races of Foc, tropical race 4 (TR4) has become prevalent and is recognized as the most devastating pathogen affecting bananas worldwide (Dita et al., 2018; Kema et al., 2021).

Breeding disease‐resistant cultivars is widely recognized as the most effective approach to control Fusarium wilt of banana. However, limitations of sterile triploid commercial varieties in banana production poses significant challenges for banana breeding to produce stable resistance (Zorrilla‐Fontanesi et al., 2020). Most existing banana cultivars such as Cavendish are triploid and do not produce seeds, making conventional crossbreeding impractical for developing disease‐resistant cultivars (Zorrilla‐Fontanesi et al., 2020). In China, some cultivars with resistance to Fusarium wilt, such as Baodao, Nan Tian Huang, Zhongjiao 4 and Nongke 1, have been selected through the introduction of resistant germplasm from abroad followed by bud mutation (Hwang & Ko, 2004; Xu et al., 2017; Zeng et al., 2023). These cultivars are not completely resistant to Fusarium wilt, and under intense pathogen pressure, their disease incidence can still reach as high as 50% during the seedling stage, rendering them unsuitable for commercial production (Li, Li, & Nie, 2019). Recent breakthroughs in biotechnology, such as the CRISPR/Cas9 genome editing system and host‐induced gene silencing techniques, have opened promising avenues for exploring alternative disease‐resistance breeding approaches in bananas (Dou et al., 2020; Hu et al., 2017; Shao et al., 2020; Tripathi et al., 2021). Nonetheless, the adoption of transgenic technologies and consumer acceptance of genetically modified products remain significant hurdles. Therefore, identification of candidate genes for disease resistance and the related research of resistance mechanisms are urgently needed in Foc–banana interactions.

As typical noncoding RNAs, microRNA‐like RNAs (milRNAs) produced by fungi play crucial roles in the interactions between pathogenic fungi and host plants (Huang et al., 2019). An increasing number of milRNAs in phytopathogenic fungi have been found to act as effectors that suppress host immunity (Ji et al., 2021; Zhao & Guo, 2019). For instance, milRNAs of Botrytis cinerea have been identified as small RNA effectors that suppress host immunity and facilitate fungal infection through cross‐kingdom RNA interference (RNAi) (Wang, Weiberg, et al., 2017; Weiberg et al., 2013). Similarly, Vm‐milR1 from the apple canker pathogen Valsa mali functions as a small RNA (sRNA) effector to silence host receptor‐like kinase genes, thereby suppressing host defence responses and facilitating pathogen infection (Xu et al., 2022). A B. cinerea sRNA effector, Bc‐siR37, targets three Arabidopsis genes (AtWRKY7, AtPMR6 and AtFEI2) involved in defence against B. cinerea (Wang, Weiberg, et al., 2017). The aforementioned research on fungal milRNAs contributes to the identification of resistance genes or susceptibility genes that could be considered in the development of disease‐resistant plants. However, the potential roles of soilborne fungal milRNAs in interactions between plant and pathogen are not well understood.

In the pathogen–host interactions, pathogens often secrete effectors to block pattern‐triggered immunity (PTI) activated by pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) on the surface of the plant cell, thereby facilitating pathogen infection (Wang et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2010). The innate immune system in plants is composed of interconnected defence layers: PTI, initiated by pathogen‐associated molecular patterns (PAMPs), and effector‐triggered immunity (ETI), which activates the immune response through interactions between plant intracellular nucleotide‐binding domain and leucine‐rich repeat receptors (NLRs) and pathogen effectors (Jones & Dangl, 2006). Despite their differences, PTI and ETI induce similar defence responses involving complex signal transduction networks to integrate various immune signals, such as phytohormones, reactive oxygen species (ROS), mitogen‐activated protein kinase (MAPK) cascades, transcriptional reprogramming, and calcium fluxes (Wang et al., 2022). However, unlike PTI, ETI often leads to programmed cell death (PCD) at the infection site, also known as the hypersensitive response (HR), which restricts pathogen spread (Cui et al., 2015).

Although the regulatory role of endogenous milRNAs in pathogenicity has been characterized in some fungi such as B. cinerea, Puccinia striiformis f. sp. tritici, and V. mali (Feng et al., 2021; Wang, Sun, et al., 2017; Weiberg et al., 2013), little is known about the role of milRNAs in the interactions between the soilborne fungus Foc and its host banana. This study aims to clarify the function of Foc‐milR138, which is significantly induced during the initial infection process of Foc, through the knockout of its precursor and phenotypic analysis of resultant mutants. Based on the transcriptomic analysis, we further identified target genes of this milRNA in the host banana and investigated the roles of host targets in the interaction between Foc and the host. The results provide potential molecular targets for combatting Fusarium wilt and developing disease‐resistant banana cultivars.

RESULTS

Foc‐milR138 is involved in the pathogenicity of F. oxysporum f. sp. cubense

In our previous study, six milRNAs were identified, and these exhibited significantly induced expression during Foc infection of banana plantlets through sRNA sequencing and bioinformatic analysis (Xie et al., 2024). Foc‐milR138 is a milRNA with a typical hairpin structure (Figure S1a). In this study, we further examined the expression pattern of Foc‐milR138 during Foc infection from 0 to 96 h, and the results showed that expression was gradually upregulated during the early infection stage (Figure 1a). Based on the precursor gene sequence of Foc‐milR138, BLAST searches against the ENSEMBL fungal database (https://fungi.ensembl.org/Tools/Blast?tl=Kl7PcOATzPbMf4XF‐21631558), revealed 19 fungal milRNA genes homologous to Foc‐milR138, including 12 from various F. oxysporum forma speciales and five from F. fujikuroi, F. verticillioides, F. nygamai, F. mangiferae and F. proliferatum. The phylogenetic analysis revealed that the Foc‐milR138 precursor gene and its homologues from other F. oxysporum formae speciales were closely clustered with a bootstrap support of 57%, and they were distantly clustered with homologues from F. fujikuroi and F. verticillium in one group, while homologues from F. nygamai, F. mangiferae, and F. proliferatum served as outgroups. The results indicated that Foc‐milR138 is highly conserved and specific in publicly available genomes of F. oxysporum species complex (Figure S1b).

In order to further investigate the role of Foc‐milR138, the precursor gene of 263 bp, located in the intergenic region of FOIG_07637 and FOIG_07638, was knocked out by homologous recombination as we described previously (Xie et al., 2024), resulting in the generation of three ΔmilR138 mutants (Figure S1c). In addition, the expression of Foc‐milR138 was further examined by stem‐loop reverse transcription‐quantitative PCR (RT‐qPCR) in the ΔmilR138 mutants and the wild‐type (WT) strain XJZ2 (Figure S1d). Phenotypic analysis of the ΔmilR138 mutants revealed no significant differences in mycelial growth, conidiation, or sensitivity to cell wall inhibitors compared to the WT strain, XJZ2 (data not showed). Banana plantlets of cultivar Cavendish were inoculated with conidial suspensions of XJZ2 and ΔmilR138 mutants, while double‐distilled water was used as negative control (CK). The pathogenicity of mutant strains was assessed in comparison to the WT strain. The pathogenicity tests revealed that the ΔmilR138 mutants displayed reduced capacity to induce discernible vascular discolouration in the corms of banana plantlets and exhibited significantly decreased virulence on banana plantlets compared to the WT strain (Figure 1b,c). The findings suggested that Foc‐milR138 might be involved in the pathogen–host interactions and play important roles in suppressing plant immunity and facilitating pathogenesis of host plants.

To further verify secretion of Foc‐milR138 into host plant cells, we attempted to detect by RT‐PCR Foc‐milR138 in protoplasts prepared from banana roots previously inoculated with WT strain XJZ2. Protoplasts from banana roots not inoculated with the pathogen were used as negative control (CK). The results showed that Foc‐milR138 could be detected in the protoplasts derived from infected banana roots, but not in the negative control (Figure 1d). Furthermore, RT‐qPCR indicated a highly significant upregulation of Foc‐milR138 in the infected banana roots compared to the noninoculated one (Figure 1e). These above findings suggested that Foc‐milR138 can be secreted into the plant cell and is involved in the interaction of Foc and the host.

Foc‐milR138 suppresses expression of MaLYK3 through a sequence‐specific mechanism during initial infection by Foc

To investigate the function of the virulent milRNA in Foc–banana interactions, 62 potential candidates in the genome of banana (Musa acuminata subsp. malaccensis ‘Pahang’) were predicted as potential target genes of Foc‐milR138, and their expression profiles were analysed in response to Foc infection at 0 (CK), 24, and 48 h post‐inoculation (hpi). Given the upregulation of Foc‐milR138 during early infection stages (Figure 1a), its targets were focused on banana genes showing lower expression levels at the onset of infection (Figure 2a). Among these targets, MaLYK3, which encodes a LysM domain‐containing receptor‐like kinase 3, exhibited a reduced expression at 24 and 48 hpi in response to Foc infection (Figure 2a). Subsequent stem‐loop RT‐qPCR analysis revealed that during the initial stages of Foc infection (at 6, 12, 24, 48, 72, and 96 hpi), the expression of MaLYK3 did not display a notable decrease. Rather, it exhibited a mild increase at 6 and 12 hpi, followed by a gradual decline from 24 to 96 hpi, without statistical significance when compared to the uninfected control at 0 hpi (Figure 2b). Intriguingly, upon inoculation with the Foc‐milR138 deletion mutant (ΔmilR138), MaLYK3 expression showed a consistent increase, with the upregulation becoming increasingly evident as the duration of infection extended (Figure 2c).

The predicted targeted site of Foc‐milR138 was found to be located at the end of the coding sequence (CDS) and at the beginning of the 3′ untranslated region (UTR) of MaLYK3 (Figure 3a). To validate this target site, a construct containing the CDS and 3′ UTR of MaLYK3, along with a mutated version (MaLYK3‐m) unable to bind to Foc‐milR138 (Figure 3a), were generated and expressed in Nicotiana benthamiana leaves. At 48 hpi of agroinfiltration, green fluorescent protein (GFP) fluorescence was observed and recorded. The GFP signal was notably suppressed by co‐expression with both MaLYK3 and Foc‐milR138, while co‐expression with both MaLYK3‐m and Foc‐milR138 did not attenuate the GFP signal (Figure 3b). This was further supported by consistent transcript levels and protein abundance of GFP co‐expressed with or without Foc‐milR138 (Figure 3c,d). Co‐expression experiments demonstrated that Foc‐milR138 specifically targeted MaLYK3 at the site between the CDS and 3′ UTR, resulting in a suppression of MaLYK3 expression. These findings suggested that Foc‐milR138 has the potential to selectively regulate the expression of MaLYK3 in host banana cells through a sequence‐specific mechanism.

Characterization and subcellular localization of MaLYK3

To dissect the functions of MaLYK3, we screened the whole genome of wild banana and found a total of eight MaLYK3 genes annotated in the genome of M. acuminata subsp. malaccensis ‘Pahang’, each corresponding to 10 LysM motif (LysM) receptor‐like kinase 3 (RLK3) proteins. Among these, the MaLYK3 gene designated by accession number LOC103972094 encodes a LysM domain‐containing receptor‐like kinase 3 (accession no. XP_009384571.1), consisting of 458 amino acids and featuring a conserved serine/threonine protein kinase domain belonging to the Pkc_like superfamily (Figure S2a). The phylogenetic analysis revealed that the 10 MaLYK3 proteins are classed in two distinct clades. Five MaLYK3 proteins form a cluster that shows 100% bootstrap support alongside the Arabidopsis thaliana AtPKs (NP_191271.2), whereas the remaining five MaLYK3 proteins form another cluster closely affiliated with AtLYK3 (NP_175606.2) from A. thaliana with a 96% bootstrap support value. Surprisingly, our milRNA‐targeted MaLYK3 was in the first group, distinctly apart from the AtLYK3 and Vitis vinifera VvLYK3 proteins grouped in the second group (Figure S2b).

Previous research suggested that LYK3 is probably situated on the plasma membrane. To ascertain the subcellular localization of MaLYK3, we conducted transient expression in N. benthamiana using Agrobacterium. The overlap of MaLYK3‐mGFP with the red fluorescence of a plasma membrane‐located protein, REM1.3‐RFP, used as a control, suggests that MaLYK3 is also likely to be situated on the plasma membrane (Figure 4a). The expression patterns of MaLYK3 in various organs of banana plant revealed differential expression in banana roots, pseudostems, and leaves, displaying a pronounced inclination towards root expression (Figure 4b).

Expression of MaLYK3 activates plant immune responses in N. benthamiana

To assess the potential of transiently expressed MaLYK3 to trigger plant immune responses, the local HR, accumulation of ROS, and deposition of callose were examined in N. benthamiana leaves through agroinfiltration. The findings revealed that transient expression of MaLYK3 induced HR in N. benthamiana leaves at 5 days post‐agroinfiltration (dpa), albeit with less severe cell death compared to the positive control INF1. Additionally, co‐expression of MaLYK3 and Foc‐milR138 prevented plant cell death (Figure 5a). To further explore the association between MaLYK3 expression and activation of plant defence signalling pathways in N. benthamiana, the expression of defence‐related genes was assessed using RT‐qPCR. The results confirmed that transient expression of MaLYK3 stimulated the expression of numerous resistance marker genes involved in phytohormone signalling pathways: salicylic acid (SA) pathway marker genes NbPR1 and NbPR2; jasmonic acid (JA) pathway markers NbPR4 and NbLOX; ethylene response genes NbEF1, NbOsmotin and NbACCO1, and HR‐related gene NbHir1 (Figure 5b).

Plants have the ability to amass molecules with antipathogenic properties, such as ROS, which serve to eliminate pathogens or act as signalling molecules to trigger subsequent immune responses upon pathogen recognition. Overexpression of MaLYK3 in N. benthamiana leaves significantly augmented ROS accumulation compared to the negative control, GFP, while co‐expression of MaLYK3 and Foc‐milR138 resulted in attenuated ROS accumulation at 48 h post‐infection (Figure 5c,d). Callose, a β‐1,3‐glucan, is known to accumulate at the periphery of plant sieve pores, typically located outside the cell wall, thereby reinforcing the physical integrity of the cell wall and aiding in the prevention of pathogen invasion. Overexpression of MaLYK3 led to a substantial increase in callose deposition compared to the negative control, GFP, while co‐expression of Foc‐milR138 and MaLYK3 resulted in reduced but still elevated levels of callose deposition. These findings suggest that MaLYK3 positively stimulates plant immune responses. However, co‐expression of MaLYK3 with Foc‐milR138 led to the inhibition of MaLYK3 expression by Foc‐milR138, consequently weakening the activation of the plant immune responses (Figure 5e,f). These results indicated that the expression of MaLYK3 triggered multiple immune responses in N. benthamiana and induced the expression of genes in phytohormone signalling pathways. Conversely, Foc‐milR138 was capable of suppressing immune responses and defence‐related genes by targeting MaLYK3.

MaLYK3 contributes to N. benthamiana resistance to fungal pathogens

To delve deeper into the potential function of MaLYK3 in plant defence against diseases, we employed the necrotrophic fungus Alternaria alternata to infect N. benthamiana leaves after expressing either MaLYK3 or Foc‐milR138. Upon comparative analysis, leaves expressing MaLYK3 (n = 26) exhibited significantly smaller lesions as opposed to those infiltrated with a GFP control (n = 26) (Figure 6a,b). In contrast, leaves infiltrated with Foc‐milR138 (n = 26) showed notably larger lesions when compared with the GFP control group (n = 26) (Figure 6c,d). Intriguingly, when N. benthamiana leaves were co‐infiltrated with both MaLYK3 (n = 26) and Foc‐milR138 (n = 26), the resulting lesions were larger than those observed in leaves treated solely with MaLYK3 (Figure 6e,f). These results suggested that MaLYK3 plays a role in enhancing resistance against fungal pathogens in N. benthamiana, while Foc‐milR138 appears to counteract this resistance, thereby facilitating the infection by A. alternata.

DISCUSSION

In this study, we have characterized Foc‐milR138, a miRNA‐like RNA identified in Foc, which is only present in a few Fusarium species, including F. oxysporum species complex, F. fujikuroi, F. amgiferae, F. verticilliodes, F. nygamai and F. proliferatum. Our findings indicate that Foc‐milR138 is significantly induced during the early stages of Foc infection, and its deletion notably attenuates the pathogen's virulence without affecting fungal growth and conidiation. This contrasts with the phenotype observed in Foc‐milR87 deletion mutants, which displayed substantial decreases in growth, conidiation, tolerance to hydrogen peroxide, penetration through cellophane, and virulence against banana plantlets (Li et al., 2022). Despite similarities in their roles as virulent effectors during the early stages of Foc infections, the mechanisms of pathogenicity for Foc‐milR138 differ from those of Foc‐milR87.

Lysine motif (LsyM)‐containing receptor‐like kinases (LYKs) represent a subfamily of receptor‐like kinases in plants that play essential roles in mediating plant–microbe interactions, particularly in the recognition and response to bacterial and fungal signals (Buendia et al., 2018; Hu et al., 2021). The plant LYK ectodomain is composed of two parts: the extracellular ligand‐binding domain LysM and the intracellular kinase structural domain with either an active or inactive kinase domain (Gust et al., 2012). Canonical LYKs, such as AtLYK5 and VvLYK5, possess multiple characteristic LysMs that recognize chitin in fungal cell walls, triggering the PTI response and acting as PRRs in response to beneficial or pathogenic microbes (Buendia et al., 2018; Cao et al., 2014; Roudaire et al., 2023). In A. thaliana, AtCERK1 interacts with AtLYK5 to form a tetramer to mediate chitin signalling, concurrently undergoing phosphorylation (Erwig et al., 2017). While AtLYK5 or AtLYK4 serves as a chitin sensor, AtCERK1 is responsible for chitin signalling transduction due to lack of kinase activity in AtLYK5 or AtLYK4 (Cao et al., 2014). Similarly, a tetramer is also produced in rice, yet LYK5 is replaced by CEBiP, a protein that contains only the LsyM domain but no kinase domain (Hayafune et al., 2014). In our study, we identified an unconventional MaLYK3 with only a protein kinase domain but no LysM domain, which could also activate plant immunity responses. However, the role of MaLYK3 involved in plant immunity still needs to be explored further.

N‐acetyl glucosamine (GlcNAc)‐containing molecules, including chitin from pathogenic fungi, peptidoglycan from pathogenic bacteria, Nod factors from symbiotic rhizobia, and Myc factors from symbiotic arbuscular mycorrhizal (AM) fungi, are important microbial signalling factors (Zipfel, 2014). Upon recognition of these molecules, LYKs can activate the plant's innate immunity or lead to beneficial symbiotic relationships (Kelly et al., 2017; Liang et al., 2014). LYK genes have subsequently been mined in different plants, including apple and pear (Chen et al., 2020), and Lotus japonicus (Bozsóki et al., 2017).

Regarding the role of target genes of Foc‐milR138 in host defence, our data showed that MaLYK3 positively contributes to banana resistance to Foc. Transient expression of MaLYK3 in N. benthamiana induced HR; enhanced disease resistance to the necrotrophic pathogen A. alternata might be attributed to the high expression levels of several defence signalling pathway genes, such as NbPR1 and NbPR2 for SA, NbPR4 and NbLOX for JA, NbEF1, NbOsmotin, and NbACCO1 for ethylene, and NbHir1 for HR. Moreover, we found that MaLYK3 could contribute to plant resistance by activating ROS accumulation and callose deposition. This implied that MaLYK3 would play a positive regulatory role in plant immunity. However, the molecular basis underlying MaLYK3‐mediated immune signalling still needs to be investigated.

Recent studies have shown that fungal milRNAs can act as novel sRNA effectors mediating cross‐kingdom RNAi. They can be delivered to host cells and target genes at the post‐transcriptional level, repressing the expression of various host immunity‐associated genes and promoting pathogen infection. Examples of such milRNAs include Vm‐milR1, Pst‐milR1, and Fol‐milR1 (Ji et al., 2021; Wang, Sun, et al., 2017; Xu et al., 2022). Importantly, Foc‐milR138 was found to contribute to fungal virulence by silencing host plant receptor‐like kinase gene MaLYK3 by putative cross‐kingdom RNAi. This suggests that fungal cross‐kingdom RNAi mediated by milRNAs is also significant in the Foc–banana interaction system.

In conclusion, our study uncovers a sophisticated fungal pathogenesis strategy where Foc employs the sRNA effector Foc‐milR138 to dampen host immunity and promote infection by silencing host LYKs gene. These candidate genes conferring resistance in bananas have been pinpointed for further investigation to facilitate the development of molecular markers and expedite the integration of enhanced resistance traits into elite banana varieties. Furthermore, the potential use of Foc‐milR138 as a target for RNAi pesticides is also suggested.

EXPERIMENTAL PROCEDURES

Fungal strains and growth conditions

The isolate Foc tropical race 4 strain XJZ2 was used in this study (Li et al., 2011). A. alternata, isolated from leaf spots of tomato, was used for detection of disease resistance on N. benthamiana leaves. Both fungal strains were cultured on potato dextrose agar (PDA) to facilitate conidiation and mycelial growth. Conidiation was induced following the previously described methods (Li et al., 2014).

Fungal invasion assays and pathogenicity test

The virulence on banana plantlets was evaluated following established protocols (Li et al., 2013). Each treatment involved 30 banana plantlets. The extent of internal symptoms was documented based on the disease levels, and disease indexes were computed to assess disease severity.

Nucleic acid extraction, milRNA detection and RT‐qPCR analysis

Total RNA was extracted from freshly cultured mycelia of Foc using TRIzol (Invitrogen) following the manufacturer's instructions. Similarly, total RNA was extracted from Foc‐infected banana using CTAB (Dingguo) following the manufacturer's protocol. Gene expression was assessed using RT‐qPCR as previously documented (Li et al., 2014). The expression level of Foc‐milR138 was detected by stem‐loop RT‐PCR assay described by Chand et al. (2016). The first‐strand cDNA was synthesized using Foc‐miR138 specific stem‐loop RT primer (Table S1). Then expression of the milRNA was detected by RT‐qPCR using stem‐loop RT primer and Foc‐miR138 specific forward primer, as previously described (Jeseničnik et al., 2022). The relative transcript levels of defence‐related genes NbPR1, NbPR2, NbPR4, NbLOX, NbERF1, NbOsmotin, NbACCO1 and NbHir1 (Li, Zhao, et al., 2019; Zhang et al., 2017) were evaluated by RT‐qPCR using NbEF1α as a reference gene. There were three biological replicates for each treatment.

Protoplast preparation of banana roots in response to Foc infection

The freshly generated roots of banana plantlets were inoculated with Foc at a concentration of 108 spores/mL or mock‐inoculated with water, and then incubated for 36 h, after which the root tips were collected and chopped into small pieces (<1 mm). Then the chopped root tips were placed in an enzyme solution (3% cellulase R10, 1.5% macerozyme R10, 1% hemicellulase, 0.4 M mannitol, 20 mM KCl, 20 mM MES, pH 5.7), and incubated for 3 h at 26°C with gentle shaking at 80 rpm. The cell solution was filtered through a 40‐μm filter to remove undigested root material. The filtered solution was rinsed with 15 mL washing solution by gentle hand shaking, and pelleted protoplasts were precipitated at 200g for 5 min at 4°C, before removing the supernatant. This rinsing was repeated, and total RNAs of the protoplasts were extracted using TRIzol following manufacturer's instructions.

Sequence alignment, phylogenetic analysis, and heatmap drawing

Based on the precursor gene sequence of Foc‐milR138, BLAST searches were carried out against the ENSEMBL fungal database (https://fungi.ensembl.org/Tools/Blast?tl=Kl7PcOATzPbMf4XF‐21631558), and the homologues were downloaded for phylogenetic analysis. The conserved domains of proteins were analysed from the conserved domain database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Structure/cdd/wrpsb.cgi). The homologous protein sequences were identified using BLASTP (Protein BLAST: search protein databases using a protein query). Neighbour‐joining trees were constructed using the MEGA X software (Kumar et al., 2016) with 1000 bootstrap replicates.

A heatmap was created according to gene expression data from banana plants infected by Foc at 0, 24 and 48 hpi. The raw gene expression values were normalized by Z‐score transformation (Cheadle et al., 2003). Genes or samples with similar expression patterns were then clustered using the ggplot2 and pheatmap software packages in R (v. 3.0.3). The colour intensities in the heatmap reflect the scales of expression values, with red indicating upregulation and blue indicating downregulation.

Target identification of Foc‐milR138

The potential target genes of Foc‐milR138 in the banana genome were anticipated using the online tool psRNATarget, with an expected alignment score set at ≤4.5. Genes exhibiting significantly reduced expression in M. acuminata ‘Baxijiao’ (Cavendish, AAA) in response to infection by the WT strain XJZ2 compared to mock inoculation with water (CK) were identified as potential targets for further investigation. Candidate target genes identified by both psRNATarget and downregulated genes in transcriptomic analysis of Foc‐milR138 on banana cv. Baxijiao at 24 and 48 hpi were chosen for subsequent analysis.

Plasmid construction

To analyse the co‐expression of Foc‐milR138 and its target gene, the precursor of Foc‐milR138 was obtained from the genomic DNA of Foc strain XJZ2 using specific primers. It was then combined with pCAMBIA1302 that had been digested with NcoI and BstEII to create pCAMBIA1302:Foc‐milR138. To temporarily activate banana genes in N. benthamiana, MaLYK3 was isolated from the cDNA of banana cv. Baxijiao using gene‐specific primer pairs (Table S1). Similarly, MaLYK3‐m, which harbours a mutated target site as depicted in Figure 3a, was amplified through modification of the third nucleotide within the codon. This specific alteration does not alter protein sequence encoded by the modification. They were then combined with pCAMBIA1302 that had been digested with NcoI to produce the pCAMBIA::MaLYK3 and pCAMBIA::MaLYK3‐m constructs.

Agrobacterium‐mediated transient expression in N. benthamiana

The recombinant vectors were introduced into Agrobacterium tumefaciens GV3101. To achieve transient expression, the A. tumefaciens cultures were infiltrated into N. benthamiana leaves (Situ et al., 2020). Subsequently, the injected leaves were photographed and then collected after 48 h for the assessment of mRNA and protein levels of GFP and MaLYK3, and the transcript levels of disease‐resistant marker genes previously characterized in N. benthamiana.

Protein extraction and western blot analysis

The leaves of N. benthamiana that had been infiltrated were pulverized into powder using liquid nitrogen and then transferred to a 2 mL RNase‐free centrifuge tube along with 1 mL of protein extraction buffer (comprising 90% GTEN, 1% cocktail, 1% PMDF, 1% dithiothreitol, and 1% NP‐40). The mixture was thoroughly vortexed and then placed on ice for 10 min before being centrifuged at 4°C for 10 min at 13,523g twice to obtain the protein. Subsequently, SDS‐PAGE was used to segregate the total proteins after the addition of loading buffer and boiling for 7 min. The proteins were then transferred to PVDF membranes (Bio‐Rad) and blocked in a 5% nonfat milk solution in phosphate‐buffered saline +0.1% Tween 20 (PBST). In order to identify the pertinent fusion proteins, a 1:5000 dilution of mouse anti‐GFP monoclonal antibody (Abmayt) was used. The membranes were rinsed three times with PBST and incubated with a goat anti‐mouse antibody (Dingguo). The proteins were visualized using the Efficient Chemiluminescence kit (Genview), and images were captured using a Bio‐Rad imaging system.

ROS and callose staining in N. benthamiana

In the ROS accumulation assay, the entire leaves of N. benthamiana were submerged in a 3,3′‐diaminobenzidine (DAB) staining solution (1 mg/mL, pH 3.8) with the abaxial side facing downwards for 12 h in darkness at 26°C. Subsequently, the leaves were boiled in anhydrous ethanol for 15 min to remove the colour, and then dried by pressing with filter paper for photography. For the callose deposition assay, leaves were immersed with the abaxial side facing downwards in an aniline blue staining solution (0.1 mg/mL, pH 9.0) and left in darkness at 26°C for imaging using an Olympus BX53 microscopy system, following the method outlined by Situ et al. (2020). The quantification of ROS accumulation and callose deposition was carried out using ImageJ software. Each independent experiment involved at least three leaves.

Disease resistance detection

A. alternata was cultured on PDA at 25°C. After 7 days, circular colonies, 3 mm in diameter, comprising a mixture of conidia and mycelia, were placed on the abaxial surface of N. benthamiana leaves. The inoculated plants were then maintained at 25°C with adequate moisture, and the size of leaf spots was assessed after 5 days post‐inoculation. Each individual experiment involved the inoculation of a minimum of 10 detached leaves.
