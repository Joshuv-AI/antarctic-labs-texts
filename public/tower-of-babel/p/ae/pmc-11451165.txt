Investigating the biomechanics of falls in older adults in long-term care using a video camera: a scoping review
Authors: Anastasia Traverso, Aysun Bayram, Giacomo Rossettini, Stefania Chiappinotto, Alessandro Galazzi, Alvisa Palese
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFalls are a worrying and growing phenomenon worldwide that especially affects the elderly. With the development of technology, one way of studying the real-life falls that occur in healthcare settings is by using video cameras.AimsTo (a) map the patterns of the research on real-life falls among older adults in healthcare settings as assessed with digital video camera supports; and (b) highlight the advances, the evidence produced, and the gaps still present regarding the biomechanics of falls as assessed technologically.MethodsA scoping review following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews. CINAHL, PubMed and Google Scholar were searched. All studies regarding falls investigated with video cameras among older individuals in healthcare settings published from 1st January 1990 to 1st January 2022 were eligible. Findings were summarised according to the Patterns, Advances, Gaps, Evidence and Recommendations framework for Scoping Reviews.ResultsIn total, 1943 studies were retrieved, and 16 met the inclusion criteria. Studies were mostly conducted in Canada. They described the real-life falls that occurred mainly in common and living areas of long-term facilities among older individuals, mainly females. Thirteen studies investigated falls through biomechanics, while three provided advances in the reliability of the measures as collected with video cameras. Studies reported that the biomechanics of a fall, reflecting the direction of the fall and protective responses, increase or decrease the likelihood of serious impact. In addition, the direction of the landing after a fall has been determined as having a significant impact on the severity and outcome of the fall.ConclusionThe use of video cameras to investigate the biomechanics of falls is a well-established research area that offer interesting insight regarding (a) how to prevent falls and their injuries and (b) the direction of the research in the field of falls.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-024-05395-2.

Background

Falls may affect the functional independence of older adults [1] and an increasing number of falls with age are associated with increased morbidity, long-term care and mortality [2, 3]. A systematic review and meta‑analysis conducted in 2020 defined the worldwide prevalence of falls in the older adults at 26.5% (95% confidence interval [CI] 23.4–29.8%) [4], and in the United States of America alone 50 billion dollars in medical costs annually are spent in aspects older-adult falls-related [1]; moreover, only in 2020, falls were the cause of 58.1% of unintentional injuries, leading to more than 36,000 deaths [5].

Falls have recently been projected to become an increasing public health burden worldwide [6]. At the individual level, they may affect quality of life due to the consequences of injuries and the self-imposed restrictions on movement triggered by the fear of falling again [7]. The first fall doubles the likelihood of falling a second time [8].

Several multidimensional intrinsic and extrinsic factors increasing the risk of falls have been documented to date, including balance issues, visual impairments and the use of medications (e.g. sedatives, antidepressants), as well as hazards in the environment [1, 6]. However, despite the large body of knowledge produced in this field making falls a well-investigated phenomenon [9], their prevention is still an important public health goal [6] and a research priority given that the evidence is still insufficient to inform health-planning decision-making [10, 11]. Nurses perceive their limited capacity to prevent falls effectively [12], resulting in increased stress, anxiety, guilt, concern for liability and self-doubt about the quality of care provided [13].

One strategy for increasing the capacity to prevent falls effectively has been recommended through investigating more data on fall biomechanics [14, 15]. Specifically, the priority is to identify fall patterns during the descent and impact phase from the beginning of the fall to its end [16], the impact velocity [17], risk of injury, the time duration of the fall, and the ability to initiate a protective response [18]. In fact, the fall configuration, the response/movements enacted by patients to protect themselves, the falling speed and the time elapsing between landing and touching the ground, may all affect the consequences of falls [19, 20].

Several studies performed to date are based on self-reported data [21], witnessed falls [22] or post-fall data collection where accuracy may be affected by the emotional implications of falls, by the cognitive issues of the patients and the recall bias [23]. To overcome these issues, falls have started being investigated in simulated environments with video cameras [24, 25], but data on artificial falls have limited meaning regarding unintentional and unexpected falls that may occur in daily life [24, 25]. The spread of technology has led to the use of video cameras not only for surveillance but also for clinical purposes, developing an innovative method to investigate the biomechanics of falls as they occur in real life [26]. However, no summary of studies to date has summarised the main progresses accumulated in the field, the gaps still present, and the preventive measures recommendations for healthcare professionals [27]. Therefore, this study was intended at mapping the knowledge produced to date in the field.

Aims

This study aimed (a) to map the patterns of the research on real-life falls among older adults in healthcare settings as assessed with digital video camera support and (b) to highlight the advances, the evidence and the gaps regarding the biomechanics of falls as evaluated technologically.

Methods

Design

A scoping review was performed according to the conceptual framework of Arksey and O’Malley [28] and further developed by Levac et al. [29]. The Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Review (PRISMA-ScR) guidance [30] was followed to design the scoping review (Supplementary Table 1). Moreover, the Patterns, Advances, Gaps, Evidence and Recommendations (PAGER) framework [27] was used to report the results.

Search question

The following research questions were addressed:What are the patterns of the research on real-life falls among older adults in the healthcare settings as assessed with digital video camera supports? andWhat advances, evidence, gaps and recommendations can be summarised regarding falls as investigated technologically?

Central to our investigation was the concept of the biomechanics of falls, such as all fall configurations, protective responses, velocity, duration and impact [16].

Search strategy and inclusion/exclusion criteria

Electronic databases including the Cumulative Index to Nursing and Allied Health Literature (CINAHL), PubMed and Google Scholar, were searched up to 1st January 2022 (Supplementary Table 2). Initially, two researchers (AT and AB) performed an unsystematic pilot literature review. No studies on this topic before 1990 [31] have been published, whereas some keywords were detected, allowing the establishment and refinement of the search string. Therefore, the following terms were considered keywords: ‘accidental falls’, ‘mechanisms of falls’, ‘dynamics of falls’, ‘biomechanics of falls’, ‘fall characteristics’ and ‘circumstances of falls’. Moreover, four additional terms were combined with the previous words: ‘inpatients’, ‘older adults’, ‘video’ and ‘video capture’. The review involved three researchers (AT, AB and AP), and the search strategy was improved by team discussion. In addition, disagreements among researchers on the study selection process were solved by consensus and discussion. Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Search outcomes

All studies retrieved were recorded. After excluding duplicates, the remaining studies were screened by title, abstract and keywords against the inclusion criteria. Two researchers (AT and AB) screened independently, and in the case of disagreement, further discussion occurred to reach a consensus by involving a third researcher (AP). The same process was performed regarding all eligible studies identified that were carefully read in their full text. The assessment regarding their final inclusion was performed independently and then agreed upon.

Data extraction

The following variables were identified and extracted as relevant to the review questions: (a) author(s), year of publication, country and affiliation(s); (b) aim(s), study design and ethical issues addressed; (c) the setting and study period; (d) the sample and its main characteristics; (e) the data collection tools (including the main characteristics of the digital video camera support); (f) the data collected at the fall, individual and environment/facility levels; (g) the data on the validity of fall measures investigated, when available; and the findings, which were divided into two categories: (h) key messages and (i) specific findings. First, the data extraction grid was developed and then tested in two studies by two researchers (AT and AP), after which no further changes were required. Then, data extraction was performed independently by two researchers (AT and AB), and a third researcher (AP) was consulted in the case of disagreements.

Data synthesis

During the process of collating, summarising and reporting the study findings [28], data were categorised according to the PAGER framework [27] around: (a) the research patterns in this field of investigation, describing their main features (e.g., specific aims, outcomes, data collection tool, settings); (b) the advances and the evidence produced to date in the dynamics of falls in the initiation, descendent and impact phases [16] as measured with digital video camera support; and (c) the main gaps and recommendations informing the future research and the practice, respectively. All researchers (AT, AB, GR, SC) were involved, along with two researchers (AG, AP) in resolving disagreements.

As reported in Fig. 1 [32], a total of 16 studies were retrieved. They were published between 2013 and 2021, in Canada, with two exceptions: one in Belgium [23] and one in the United States of America [33]. Studies were conducted by multidisciplinary groups involving, for example, physiology, physical, kinesiology, mathematics and engineering scientists (Table 2, Supplementary Table 3). Apart from three studies that were methodological in nature [16, 20, 35], all were observational in their design. They were designed to achieve different aims, ranging from describing the characteristics of falls [23, 33] and the injury risk during falling [38], stratifying them among older adults with and without Parkinson's disease [40], to ascertaining the causes and circumstances of falls [36, 39] and their consequences, such as hip fractures [44]. Studies also investigated the prevalence of objects held during falls [34], the role of fall velocity [17], considering the impact on some parts of the body such as the hips [41], and the role played by the flexor muscles of the neck and hip [35]. Risk factors [42] and biomechanical/situational factors that worsen or prevent head impact [37] were also investigated. One study compared the mechanisms of imbalance leading to falls between men and women [43]. In addition, two studies aimed to develop and validate the Fall Video Analysis Questionnaire (FVAQ) [16] and the degree of agreement between falls as recorded in the incident reports and that which emerged from the analysis of the videos [20].

Most studies were conducted in two long-term care facilities, while the remaining were carried out in an assisted living and residential care facility [23] and an assisted living community [33]. Data collection ranged from one year [38] to 11 years [44].

All studies involved older adults and their falls as a sample. The number of participants and falls varied from three participants – 26 falls [23] to 638 participants – 2,350 falls [44]. The participants were mainly female, ranging from 47% [16] to 64% [36]; one study [38] omitted to report this data. The average age of the participants ranged from 78 years [36, 37] up to 85 years [44]. Some studies detailed residents’ clinical conditions [16, 20, 34, 36–38, 40–44] and the medications [23, 42, 43] and other additional data included height and weight [17, 35, 38, 44] and one described the nursing resources available in the facility [20].

Video cameras with resolutions ranging from 640 × 480 (e.g., [36]), to 1920 × 1080 pixels [40] were used. These were placed in common or main living areas such as dining rooms, lounges, activity rooms and hallways, except for one study, where four video cameras were in each participant’s bedroom and bathroom, with the residents being allowed to switch them off to protect their privacy [23].

The fall video footages were identified based on the incident reports in all studies, except for that of O’Connor et al. [33], where a probable fall was immediately communicated with a fall alert system to the staff; two studies [39, 43] did not explained this. Trained evaluators performed the video footage analysis [23, 34, 37, 40, 41, 43], in some studies there was specified their expertise, e.g. biomedical and physiology/kinesiology [35, 44], their academic role, e.g. research assistants and graduate students [16, 17, 20, 23, 33, 36, 42] or if they were the authors of the study [38, 44]. Moreover, in ten studies, data on inter-rater or intra-rater agreement of video footage analysis was also provided [16, 17, 33, 35–37, 39, 41–43].

The FVAQ was used to identify fall videos in 13 studies, e.g. [33, 37, 39], while one [36] adopted a structured questionnaire not further described. One study [35] used the Kinovea software program and another one [17] the Digital Linear Transforms to digitize the videos. The fall classification (pre-fall, critical, post-fall and recovery phase) developed by Noury et al. [45] was used in the analyses in addition to the FVAQ questionnaire [23].

Evidence and advancements

As reported in Table 2, 13 studies (e.g. [17]), investigated fall biomechanics, while three [16, 20, 35] provided new methods regarding the measures in the field. At the overall level, studies showed that the biomechanics of the fall, reflecting the direction of the fall and protective responses, increase or decrease the likelihood of serious impact or fracture [16, 17, 20, 33, 37, 41, 42, 44]. Moreover, the landing direction of the fall has also been determined to significantly impact the severity (e.g., superficial signs vs fractures) and outcomes (e.g., abrasions, hematomas, dislocations) of falls [33, 37, 38, 44].

Evidence regarding the initiation stage of falls [16] was investigated by Robinovitch et al. [36], who discovered that the most common cause was the incorrect transfer or shifting of body weight, reaching an estimated proportion of 51% of falls, an average of 0.72 falls/participant. This cause was also confirmed in other studies [17, 33, 38–40, 43], and O’Connor et al. [33] documented that 56% of falls were due to the incorrect shifting of body weight and nearly a quarter (27%) to losing the support of an external object (e.g. an unlocked wheelchair). In addition, it was noted that residents with Parkinson’s disease were 1.3 times more likely to fall because of incorrect weight shifting [40].

In the descendent phase, residents have been reported to flex their hips and necks, preventing head impact during backward falls [35]. Moreover, given the high head impact frequency, particularly in forward falls, Schonnop et al. [37] reported that a backward rotation during the descent phase represents a protective motor strategy. Furthermore, patients affected by Parkinson’s disease took 1.6 times longer to recover balance while trying to grasp an external object and 5.0 times longer to secure a grasp of the object, reporting different reactive stepping responses compared to those individuals without Parkinson’s disease [40].

With regard to the impact phase, around one-third of falls resulted in a head impact; residents at a higher risk were female (odds ratio [OR] = 2.2, 95% CI [1.4, 3.3]) than male, with mild cognitive impairment (OR = 2.8, 95% CI [1.5, 5.0]) than those with severe impairments [42]. In contrast, in Vlaeyen et al. [23], residents fell mainly backwards on the torso or pelvis, and no one was able to get up unaided. In this context, several mechanisms have been documented as reducing the pelvis impact velocity, such as hand impact and attempts to recover balance by stepping [41]. Moreover, other measures of different vertical impact velocities for the pelvis, the head and the hand, by calculating the average fall duration from the initiation to the pelvis impact, have been documented to improve injury prevention interventions [17]. While analysing 30 hip fractures, Yang et al. [44] reported that all of them involved falls at standing height, followed by a pelvic impact on the ground with an increased risk of resulting hip fractures for sideways landing configuration (relative risk [RR] = 5.50, 95% CI [2.36–12.78]). A lower risk among residents using mobility aides was observed (RR = 0.30, 95% CI [0.09, 1.00]) as well as among those with hip protectors (RR = 0.45, 95% CI [0.21, 0.99]) [44]. However, when on the floor, only 54% of patients used the call alarm, with times before being activated ranging from 15 to 224 s. By comparison, for those residents calling with the alarm and those who did not, the average time on the ground was between 11 and 28 min; during this time, residents were inactive [23].

Three studies provided reliability data regarding the FVAQ by documenting an inter- and intra-rater agreement of over 80% and a Cohen’s k greater than 0.60, indicating good reliability for classifying biomechanical, behavioural, situational and environmental aspects of falls captured on video in common areas of long-term care facilities [16, 33, 37]. In addition, one study examined the agreement between the FVAQ, and the characteristics of falls as reported in the incident reports, detecting (a) over-reported falls due to slips and falls while rising, using a wheelchair or walker, and (b) under-reported falls due to hit/bump, loss of support, and falling while standing and sitting down [20].

Discussion

We reviewed studies using video cameras to investigate the biomechanics of falls in real life, map study patterns, and highlight the advances, the evidence, and the gaps, following the PAGER framework [27].

Study patterns

A total of 16 studies were conducted up to the beginning of 2022, and nearly all were performed by an extraordinarily productive Canadian research team, which has been working in this field since 2010 [36] in close contact with two facilities with many video cameras positioned in the common areas. This research line has accumulated progressive evidence, working over 11 years on the same data gradually expanded both for the database accessed [44] and the aims of the work, e.g. from cause [36] to characterise falls among patients affected by Parkinson’s and non-Parkinson’s [40], suggesting how a complex research problem can be developed over time under different perspectives with substantial stable methodologies. On the other hand, only two isolated studies in Belgium and the United States have pursued the same attempts to introduce interesting methodological innovations, such as the analysis of videos recorded in private spaces [23] and video footage analysis from a video camera system that alerts nurses in case of a probable fall [33] instead of starting from incident reports filled in routinely.

This field involves primarily multidisciplinary basic discipline research teams and, on a few occasions, clinical teams, whose responsibility it was mainly to fill in the incident fall reports, enabling the identification of appropriate video footage [20].

Overall, the video cameras were installed for safety purposes by ensuring non-intrusive surveillance where video footage was extracted after the fall event and only related to it. The use of video cameras has consistently raised concerns due to their intrusiveness, as a form of restraint for the prevention of security issues and as a potential violation of privacy [46]. However, all studies were based on a solid ethical foundation in which residents gave informed consent upon admission to the facility to be observed for safety purposes in common and not private spaces, except for one study in which the residents were offered the ability to turn off the video camera when deemed necessary [23]. However, investigators never detected the cameras switched off except for some accidental episodes [23]. Images were transformed into silhouettes to protect privacy [33], a useful strategy to prevent ethical issues.

Beyond the classification reported in the studies, nearly all appear to be population-based studies, followed over time, in which multiple aims are achieved, ranging from (a) epidemiological (dimensioning the falls by reporting incidence and prevalence data) to (b) investigating their biomechanics and (c) the underlying methodology adopted to increase the validity of data collected with video footages.

Studies have been multi-centre, engaging mainly two Canadian long-term facilities for many years, transforming them into places where falls are investigated over time in their natural settings. From a laboratory-type research phase, as represented in the first studies, the two facilities have become natural places to study falls, where recording technologies will likely become ubiquitous and normalised in their presence for both residents and staff [47].

The eligible population was identified from the total number of residents living in the facilities and the falls that occurred to them, as well as from very select groups (e.g. three residents [23]). In describing the flow of the participants and/or falls included, some have reported that the flow diagram [17], which indicated the reason for exclusion (e.g. falls occurred too far from the video camera), has led to a methodological approach that has been encouraged to be reported in future studies to increase transparency. However, except for the O’Connor study [33], all video footage has been scrutinised according to the incident report filled in by the staff, and therefore studies available have extended the acknowledged falls because of witnesses or reports, leaving those not recorded a still-hidden world.

Some authors collected clinical data at the individual level, such as medications (e.g. Vlaeyen et al. [23]), while a few also collected data at the facility level, such as the size of the beds (e.g. Yang et al. [20]). However, nearly all studies used the FVAQ to assess each fall episode in detail and they estimated the velocity and time interval between imbalance and impact through video digitization methods or by counting the number of frames between important events. This homogeneity seems exemplary, considering that different tools are used in other research lines, challenging each attempt to compare and accumulate the evidence produced. Vlaeyen et al. [23] also used the broader framework of Noury et al. [45] to evaluate and classify fall detectors, where falls were analysed in the pre-fall, critical, post-fall and recovery phases. Although the FVAQ classification and that of Noury and colleagues [45] respond to different aims, it might be interesting to consider their elements as the basis of the incident reports being filled in by nurses to increase their homogeneity across settings and countries and their capacity to produce valid information [20] even in contexts where there are no video cameras available.

Evidence and advancements

Study findings inform how to prevent the initial phase of falls, instructing and exercising patients on how to transfer or shift the body weight correctly [36], how to adopt safe landing strategies during a fall [48] and how to secure external support aides during ambulation by providing automatic systems, locking wheelchairs and/or ensuring fixed objects that residents may grasp and then balance their weight [49]. In fact, falls occurred twice as often in the rising phase as in the stabilisation phase of the transfer, but falls during the stabilisation phase were more likely to cause injury [49].

Training should also be focused on the descendant fall phase regarding effective reactive responses – for example, optimising neck stiffness to maintain the head upright [35] or enacting a backward rotation [37] to prevent head impacts, shaping the educational intervention according to the patient’s risk (Parkinson’s vs non-Parkinson’s disease) [40]. Older individuals may find turning the body easier than coordinating the other limbs due to low muscle control, weakened motor-response ability and reflex perception [37, 50]. The success of protective movements is associated with the energy with which the fall occurred, the environmental constraints, the speed of response and the clinical and physical condition [51]. Even if it seems difficult, older adults in long-term facilities can execute stepping and grasping reactions and maintain a grip on objects during falls [49]. Exercises that include balance, strength, dual-task and cognitive-motor training may be needed to promote increased capacity in performing stepping and reaching reactions [52, 53].

Given that using the arms to arrest a fall would effectively decrease the risk of a severe head impact, it would be advisable to increase the strength in patients’ arms through targeted exercises [53]. Since the muscles of upper extremity and upper trunk would be needed simultaneously to decelerate the body mass during the descent, the strengthening exercises should especially consider those body areas. All these aspects should be included in the staff’s ongoing education programmes.

Moreover, since residents have been documented as being inactive when on the ground [23], tailoring the acoustics system to alert the staff when residents are unable to call and provide immediate help is essential. On the other hand, discovering how to help residents avoid inactivity and get up or gain a comfortable position while on the ground could be important to prevent any additional damage related to the time spent waiting for staff assistance [54]. Furthermore, given that the body does not always immediately impact the ground (it impacts the wall or supplies before hitting the floor), strategies to modify not only the flooring but also the objects that fill the rooms in which patients spend most of their time are crucial. In residents’ rooms, bathrooms or common areas, covers can be helpful for walls, furniture edges and floor surfaces [55].

The evidence produced supports the reliability of the FVAQ [16] in long-term facilities. In contrast, incident reports, often as a unique data source in several healthcare settings, have been underlined as under-reporting data compared to the FVAQ in analysing video footage [20].

Gaps and recommendations

All countries are encouraged to support and invest in extensive studies to accumulate evidence capable of reflecting different cultures, settings, contexts and technologies. Paradoxically, the emerging studies have never been conducted in hospitals compared to the broad inclusion criteria used in our search string. Long-term facilities are environments where it is possible to investigate issues for a long time due to the limited patient turnover, allowing time to expand their safety issues. However, the findings of long-term facilities may not be valid for other contexts [17]. Video cameras in hospitals are used mainly to continuously monitor clinical conditions or patient needs while preventing avoidable disturbances (e.g. sleep). Some video cameras are also available at home for safety purposes. Therefore, expanding the research to other contexts to compare falls across settings by involving older individuals with different clinical conditions, and exposed to different environments and staff resources, is recommended.

A variability in the methods and findings reporting has emerged across studies given that no formal guidelines have been established in this field [56]. In line with the complexity of this research, standardising research protocols may help in accumulating evidence, increasing the quality of studies. Moreover, in identifying eligible falls, some studies have included many falls from the same patient or excluded several falls, thus introducing possible imbalance by observing the same resident several times or by excluding others [17, 20, 23, 33, 34, 36–44]. Providing a flow diagram explaining each step and reporting the reasons for exclusion might be important to increase transparency. Furthermore, rendering the analysis and databases publicly available might expand and maximise the investigation capacity given the complex analysis required (e.g. downloading the video footage and analysing it in groups), which is time-consuming.

To date, the analysis of video footage has been carried out mainly starting from the incident reports, which can have different formats, change over the years and be filled with variable accuracy [20, 38]. Consequently, studies available have increased the number of acknowledged falls because of witnesses or reports, leaving those not recorded in a still-unknown world [40], which could have great relevance to studying what works in preventing falls. However, this gap is not easily manageable, and a combination of measures provided by direct observations, digital video cameras and wearable sensors and devices might be necessary for future research [57].

Collecting clinical data that represent the intrinsic risk factors of falls [20, 34, 36–38, 40–44] is essential. However, data available in long-term facilities can be dated (e.g. derived from the admission assessments), or they may miss important elements (e.g. functional dependence) that are relevant for falls (e.g. muscle strength, gait characteristics) [42] and were not part of a routine assessment. Furthermore, some authors have underlined the difficulty of getting residents to participate by obtaining their consent for access to clinical documentation [23, 34, 37, 40, 44]. In this sense, nurses who are closer to residents can play an important role in facilitating accessibility and promoting research trust. These strategies might also overcome another gap underlined by nearly all authors [36] regarding the fact that only common areas have been investigated to date, thereby missing falls occurring in the bedroom, bathroom or other private settings. In this area, qualitative [23] or mixed-method studies can help in understanding the reason(s) for participating or not in research, those factors involved in the falls and the acceptability of the video cameras. Increased participation and support by facility personnel [20] can, in turn, implicitly influence the study involvement of residents.

To increase the comparability of the data and improve our understanding of the phenomenon, it could be interesting to develop a minimum data set including clinical, environmental and resource data to describe the situational and clinical context in which studies are conducted [43]. Having a common ground of data allows researchers to describe the clinical profile of residents, including some index frailty, and to combine environmental, resources, clinical and kinaesthetic data that might be useful while evaluating the external validity of the findings.

Moreover, given the inaccuracies documented in the incident reports [16] that are also variable across facilities and countries, rendering it challenging to collect and compare reliable data, it could be interesting to integrate them with those elements contained in the FVAQ [16] tool. In addition, given that validity data of the FVAQ have been provided only for long-term facilities [16], promoting similar studies in other contexts (hospital, home) could be valuable.

Due to their complex measures [35], sometimes the findings of the studies cannot be interpreted easily by clinicians to enable their implementation in daily practice, and thus a summary of the clinical implications could enhance their practical dissemination.

Limitations

This scoping review has several limitations. First, although designed according to a systematic approach by including all languages, some studies may have been missed. Moreover, all studies were included without evaluating their methodological quality: although this is consistent with the methodologies available in scoping reviews [28]. In addition, all the long-term care studies developed in British Columbia (Canada) involved analysing the same data set, albeit with different research questions and larger samples as the years progressed. Therefore, the generalisability of the evidence mapped may have been influenced.

Second, no critical analysis or comparison was conducted across the technologies used by researchers: the rapid developments expected in the digital video camera support will improve the quality of the studies in this field. For example, in some studies, falls were not easily viewable due to shadows [33] because of the distance between the camera and the resident, or because of the occlusion of the body section [36], and the number of seconds collected: Robinovitch et al. [36] suggested 60 s but O’Connor et al. [33] collected only 10.

Third, the search process and the analysis of the studies required long time to be completed accurately after the data search ended in the beginning of the 2022: future research may cover the time gaps by mapping the studies produced in the last year. However, the methods used in this field and the findings mapped may inform and give insights to other lines of investigation as that exploring the association between the amount of nursing resources and occurrence of falls expanding the research capacity to fully understanding the phenomenon [59].

Conclusions

This scoping review provided a map of studies using video cameras to investigate the biomechanics of real-life falls among older adults in long-term care. The intent was to summarise the evidence available in this field but also the gaps and the recommendations to inform which preventive mechanisms can be implemented to reduce the high occurrence of falls among older individuals cared for in health care settings and how to address the future research in the field.

The use of video footage of falls mainly occurring in common areas is recent, involves multidisciplinary research teams, the largest one being based in Canada. The study processes conducted are complex, lasting up to 11 years, triggered by falls recorded in the incident reports and include the analysis of the video footage, using substantially the homogeneous methods.

Studies documented that the biomechanics of a fall, reflecting the direction of the fall and protective responses, increase or decrease the likelihood of serious impact. In addition, the direction of the landing after a fall has been determined as having a significant impact on the severity and outcome of the fall. Therefore, they may inform healthcare professionals and patients regarding the prevention, e.g., how to prevent the initial phase of falls, instructing and exercising patients on how to transfer or shift the body weight correctly; how to adopt safe landing strategies during a fall and how to secure external support aides during ambulation by providing automatic systems, locking wheelchairs and/or ensuring fixed objects that residents may grasp and then balance their weight.

Video cameras are important for deepening our knowledge of real-life falls and moving forward in research in this area by assuming a complementary role to that conducted by other traditional research methodologies due to their capacity to provide useful information. Future studies should consider the several challenges faced by this research field as the time required to analyse video; moreover, the ethical implications and the poor adherence of patients to research projects, as well as the continuous developments in the technological field may improve the accuracy of the finding but, on the other hand, may increase the complexity of the observational studies.
