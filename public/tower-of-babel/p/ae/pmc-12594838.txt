Analyzing the enhancement of CNN-YOLO and transformer based architectures for real-time animal detection in complex ecological environments
Authors: Ali Raza, Fareeha Hanif, Heba Abdelgader Mohammed
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAutomatic animal detection has become a critical capability in ecology, conservation, agriculture, and public safety, driven by the rapid growth of visual data collected through camera traps, UAVs, and remote sensors. The necessity of this study arises from the increasing demand to understand and apply these underlying detection techniques in practical domains such as animal husbandry, farming, and livestock management, where timely and accurate animal identification directly impacts productivity, welfare, and safety. Traditional convolutional neural networks (CNNs) have demonstrated strong accuracy in static or controlled environments but often face limitations in computational cost and inference speed. In contrast, the You Only Look Once (YOLO) family of one-stage detectors has revolutionized animal detection by achieving real-time performance while maintaining competitive accuracy across challenging geospatial environments. This review provides a chronological synthesis of detection approaches, tracing the evolution from handcrafted features and two-stage CNN-based models to modern YOLO architectures and transformer-enhanced frameworks. A detailed comparative analysis is presented, highlighting trade-offs in accuracy, speed, robustness, and deployment feasibility across diverse datasets, including camera trap imagery, UAV-based surveys, and satellite observations. Persistent challenges such as small-object detection, class imbalance, and limited cross-geographical generalization are discussed alongside enhancement strategies, including attention mechanisms, few-shot learning, and domain adaptation. Furthermore, practical deployment considerations are explored, with emphasis on edge computing platforms such as Jetson Nano, Coral TPU, and UAV-embedded systems. This review adopts a systematic methodology following PRISMA guidelines, covering studies published between 2015 and 2025, from which 142 were included after screening. Comparative findings show that on camera-trap datasets, transformer-augmented YOLO variants achieve up to 94% mAP under controlled illumination, while lightweight YOLOv7-SE and YOLOv8 architectures offer superior real-time performance ( 60 FPS) on UAV-based imagery. However, large-scale deployment remains constrained by edge-device memory limits and cross-domain generalization challenges.Subject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Ecology, Ecology, Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

In recent years, the field of computer vision has undergone remarkable growth, driven by advances in deep learning algorithms, increased computational power, and the availability of large annotated datasets. Among its diverse applications, automatic animal detection has emerged as a critical research area with profound implications for wildlife conservation, ecological monitoring, agriculture, and public safety. The use of camera traps, drones, and surveillance systems has made it possible to collect vast amounts of visual data in natural and semi-natural environments. However, processing and analyzing this data manually is time-consuming and labor-intensive, highlighting the urgent need for automated, intelligent systems. Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) and You Only Look Once (YOLO) object detection models have proven especially effective for real-time and high-accuracy image classification and localization tasks. These models are increasingly being adapted to detect, classify, and track animals in a variety of ecological and operational contexts. This review explores the evolution, capabilities, and limitations of such techniques in animal detection, providing a comprehensive understanding of current trends, datasets, tools, and future directions.

Context and importance of animal detection

Automatic detection of animals in images and video has become a crucial task in contemporary ecology, conservation biology, smart agriculture, and road safety applications. The proliferation of camera traps, drones, and remote sensors produces vast volumes of visual data, outpacing manual annotation workflows. Efficient animal detection systems therefore are vital for extracting meaningful ecological insights at scale1. In wildlife monitoring, camera traps enable non-invasive, 24-hour sampling of fauna across diverse habitats, yielding imagery used to estimate species richness, population densities, behaviors, and activity patterns. However, the bottleneck lies in the time-consuming manual processing of millions of images. Deep learning techniques, especially convolutional neural networks (CNNs), dramatically reduce that burden by automating detection with high precision and speed2. Similarly, modern ecological studies leverage machine learning to interpret large-scale visual data from drones, satellites, audio sensors, and bio-logging devices, enabling real-time insights into animal movements and behavior, and ultimately supporting data-driven conservation and policy decisions3,4. Moreover, in human-animal conflict contexts (for example, wildlife crossing roads or entering human settlements), accurate and timely animal detection can reduce collision risks and improve safety. Transfer learning pipelines adapted to these environments show promising performance but face challenges in generalizing from natural-habitat data to urban or roadside settings5. Recent advancements have further improved multi-modal image registration in complex field environments. For instance,6 presented a comprehensive evaluation of multispectral image registration strategies designed for heterogeneous agricultural conditions, demonstrating robust alignment performance across varying spectral and spatial resolutions. This work provides a valuable foundation for developing adaptive registration pipelines suitable for multi-sensor ecological and environmental monitoring systems. . So, animal detection via computer vision represents a foundational capability at the intersection of ecological research, conservation, agriculture, and public safety. Its importance stems from the urgent need to process large-scale image and video datasets automatically, accurately, and efficiently, enabling actionable intelligence for biodiversity protection and human–animal coexistence.

Methodology of literature selection

To ensure the rigour and transparency of this review, a structured methodology was adopted, adhering to best practices for systematic literature reviews such as the PRISMA guidelines8. First, precise research questions were formulated following the PICO framework (Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcome) to define the scope of investigation, focusing on deep-learning models (CNN and YOLO) for animal detection across modalities and environmental contexts9. A review protocol documenting inclusion and exclusion criteria, search strategies, and planned analyses was developed prior to commencement and registered in advance to avoid bias. A broad search strategy was then implemented across multiple academic databases including IEEE Xplore, PubMed, Scopus, ACM Digital Library, and Google Scholar. Boolean logic was used to combine topic-relevant terms such as “deep learning,” “YOLO,” “CNN,” “animal detection,” “wildlife,” and “camera trap.” Grey literature sources and conference proceedings were also searched, and reference lists of key articles were examined for additional relevant studies9,11. Title and abstract screening were performed independently by two reviewers applying the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Criteria included publication in English, relevance to object detection or classification of animals, use of CNN or YOLO architectures, and availability of performance metrics on ecological datasets. Disagreements in inclusion decisions were resolved by discussion and, when needed, consultation with a third reviewer to ensure consistency and reduce subjective bias10,12.

Full-text eligibility screening followed for studies that passed the initial screening. Selected studies were assessed for methodological quality, relevance to the research objectives, and completeness of reported results. Data were systematically extracted into a structured spreadsheet, capturing metadata such as model architecture, dataset used, performance metrics, deployment context, and any identified limitations. Quality and risk-of-bias assessment was conducted using consensus guidelines adapted from PRISMA and Cochrane methodologies. Extraction and coding were piloted on a random subset of articles to refine criteria and ensure inter-rater reliability. Cohens-Kappa statistics were calculated to evaluate agreement between reviewers, with iterative adjustments made until near-perfect agreement (K > 0.8) was achieved13. Finally, data synthesis entailed both qualitative and quantitative analyses. Quantitative metrics such as mAP, inference speed, F1-score, and error rates were tabulated and compared across models and datasets. Qualitative synthesis identified common themes, challenges, and enhancement strategies emerging in the literature. Background and fundamental concepts

Animal detection is a specialized task in computer vision that involves locating and identifying animals within images or videos. It relies heavily on object detection models, especially convolutional neural networks (CNNs) and real-time detectors like the YOLO family. Key concepts include detection accuracy, bounding box regression, and performance metrics such as precision, recall, and mean average precision (mAP).

Object detection overview

Object detection addresses both localization and classification: locating each object in an image via a bounding box and assigning it a categorical label. In early computer vision, detection systems relied on handcrafted features, such as Haar-like features in the Viola–Jones cascade and Histogram of Oriented Gradients (HOG) in SVM classifiers, which were effective in controlled settings but lacked robustness against occlusion, background clutter, and scale variability19,20. These classical pipelines were often slow due to the sliding-window mechanism and multiple stages of processing. The advent of deep learning around 2012 marked a paradigm shift. Region-based CNN approaches such as R-CNN, Fast R-CNN, and Faster R-CNN combined region proposals with learned features, significantly improving detection accuracy albeit at considerable computational cost25,26. Mask R-CNN further extended this framework by adding pixel-level segmentation capabilities, enabling instance-level object delineation in complex scenes21. These two-stage detectors became a benchmark for precision in object detection. As demands for speed and real-time performance increased, one-stage detectors like SSD (Single-Shot MultiBox Detector) and YOLO (“You Only Look Once”) models emerged. SSD introduced multi-scale default box predictions from convolutional feature maps to allow detection of varying object sizes efficiently, achieving over 70 percent mAP at approximately 58 fps on PASCAL VOC datasets29. YOLO framed detection as a single regression problem: it splits the input image into a grid, predicts bounding box coordinates and class probabilities in one forward pass, and achieves inference speeds exceeding 40 fps even in early versions27. Later YOLO versions (v3 through v8) improved both accuracy and speed by introducing architectural enhancements such as feature pyramids, anchor refinements, and attention modules28.

More recent innovations include anchor-free detectors and transformer-based object detection frameworks, offering improved performance on small, occluded, or densely packed objects, conditions often encountered in wildlife imagery. Feature Pyramid Networks (FPN), attention mechanisms, and loss functions like focal loss (as in RetinaNet) have further bridged the gap between real-time speed and high precision31. Together, these advances enable robust detection even in challenging environments, making them highly relevant for ecological and agricultural monitoring where animals often appear small, partially hidden, or camouflaged. Loss functions across versions

YOLO v1 (2015) introduced a unified regression-based loss, combining mean squared error (MSE) for bounding box center coordinates and dimensions (scaled by a weight ), along with MSE-based objectness confidence and classification losses. The inclusion of a square-root transformation for width and height helped balance error sensitivity between large and small boxes. This design emphasized simplicity but suffered from slow convergence and suboptimal handling of localization accuracy under moderate overlap. YOLO v3 (2018) retained v1’s overall structure but replaced classification and objectness MSE with binary cross-entropy (BCE). Localization terms still relied on MSE over square-rooted width/height. This shift improved gradient behavior and classification stability, especially in multi-label or overlapping object scenarios. Despite these gains, v3 still struggled with non-overlapping box regressions and underrepresented object boundaries.

YOLO v4 / v5 (2020–2021) marked a key transition by adopting IoU-based regression losses such as GIoU and CIoU, which directly measure overlap and penalize poor spatial alignment. CIoU extended GIoU by adding penalties for center distance and aspect ratio differences. These changes significantly reduced localization errors and improved alignment with evaluation metrics like mAP. BCE remained the default for classification and objectness tasks, contributing to smoother training convergence and greater precision in detection109.

YOLO v6 / v7 / v8 (2022–2023) further enhanced the loss framework with innovations such as SIoU (Soft IoU), EIoU, and Focal or Varifocal loss for classification. SIoU introduces angle-aware penalization, shaping the optimization landscape for faster convergence and improved performance on small and dense objects. Focal and Varifocal variants mitigate class imbalance by emphasizing hard examples and weighting high-quality samples higher during training, which enhances recall and reduces false positives in crowded scenes43.

YOLO v9 (2024) introduced novel loss functions like Focaler-IoU and Inner-Focaler combined with traditional CIoU, enabling adaptive focus on high-quality bounding boxes and challenging examples. These were deployed alongside architecture-level features such as Programmable Gradient Information (PGI) and GELAN to maintain training stability. The overall loss strategy in v9 aimed to unify classification and localization improvements under a gradient-aware regime, boosting performance across variable object scales.

YOLO v10 and v11 (2024–2025) focus on high inference efficiency and end-to-end training without relying on Non-Maximum Suppression (NMS). Their loss formulations continue to use CIoU for bounding-box regression, BCE for classification, and Focal variants for objectness. The dual-assignment training mechanism in YOLO v10 removes the need for post-processing filters, streamlining training and inference pipelines while preserving competitive accuracy.

YOLO v12 (2025) refines existing loss schemes further by incorporating domain-specific optimizations, especially for remote sensing and agricultural datasets. In a study using synthetic data generation via large language models, YOLO v12 achieved mAP@50 = 0.978 on apple detection tasks. Its loss remains CIoU + BCE + Focal components but benefits from fine-tuned weighting and synthetic-data-tailored hyperparameters that substantially enhance accuracy and convergence speed in small-object benchmarks49. Lightweight YOLO modelling for tiny and small object detection

Detecting animals that appear as very small objects (for example birds in aerial imagery, small mammals in camera-trap scenes, or insects in smart-agriculture contexts) poses a distinct challenge: the objects may occupy only a few pixels, suffer heavy background clutter, or be partially occluded. To address these constraints, several recent works have explored lightweight versions of the YOLO-family detectors that reduce model size and computation while improving sensitivity to small-scale features. For example, Hu et al. proposed the “Efficient-Lightweight YOLO” architecture which enhances fine-grained feature extraction via an efficient spatial-pyramid pooling module tailored for small objects while retaining low-end GPU compatibility22. Similarly, Yue et al. developed a streamlined YOLOv8-n variant for UAV imagery, integrating lightweight blocks to improve tiny target detection under power and memory constraints23. In another line of work, Ji et al. introduced YOLO-TLA (Tiny-Layer Attention) built on YOLOv5, which adds a dedicated small-object detection layer and global attention mechanism, achieving higher mAP with fewer parameters24. These approaches share common architectural strategies: (1) introducing an additional feature map scale (e.g., a P2/P3 level) to better represent small targets; (2) reducing down-sampling and preserving spatial resolution deeper in the network; (3) integrating attention or module-fusion blocks that emphasise small-object features over background; (4) applying model-compression techniques (such as depth-wise separable convolutions, channel-shuffling, or quantization) for edge deployment. In the context of animal detection, such lightweight YOLO models are particularly promising: they enable deployment on resource-limited platforms (e.g., UAVs, camera-trap systems, farm robots) where power, compute and connectivity are constrained. However, despite these advances, challenges remain: small-object datasets in wildlife & agriculture are often class-imbalanced, cross-domain generalisation is weak (e.g., wildlife vs. livestock vs. pests), and many models trade off accuracy for speed in cluttered natural scenes. Future research in this area should thus emphasise domain-specific augmentation (e.g., copy-paste of small animals, tiling strategies), anchor-scale adaptation for tiny animals, and hardware-aware model design to maximise real-world utility in ecological and agricultural systems.

Comparison: two-stage vs one-stage detectors

Deep learning-based object detection models are generally divided into two broad categories: two-stage detectors and one-stage detectors. These frameworks differ in how they generate region proposals, classify objects, and optimize for accuracy and speed. Two-stage detectors, such as Faster R-CNN, operate by first generating candidate object regions using a Region Proposal Network (RPN). These proposals are then passed to a second stage that performs classification and bounding box regression. This approach typically results in high detection accuracy, especially in challenging scenarios such as detecting small or partially occluded objects. However, it also introduces computational overhead, making two-stage detectors relatively slower and resource-intensive25,26,31. In contrast, one-stage detectors such as YOLO and SSD integrate region proposal, classification, and localization into a single neural network architecture. This streamlined design allows for significantly faster inference speeds, making these models ideal for real-time applications. For instance, YOLOv3 and its successors can achieve inference speeds of over 40 frames per second, while still maintaining respectable detection accuracy. These models are especially beneficial in applications where low latency is essential, such as drone-based animal monitoring or mobile conservation platforms27–29.

Several comparative studies have highlighted the fundamental trade-off between these two approaches. While two-stage detectors tend to outperform one-stage detectors in terms of mean average precision (mAP), particularly on complex datasets, one-stage models offer superior speed and computational efficiency. Newer architectures have also begun to blur this distinction. For example, Light-Head R-CNN optimizes the region-wise classifier for speed, and YOLOv8 incorporates transformer-based modules for enhanced accuracy. These developments show that accuracy and speed are no longer mutually exclusive, and hybrid models are being designed to combine the strengths of both paradigms28,30. The choice between two-stage and one-stage models often depends on application requirements. In use cases such as animal detection in dense, cluttered environments like camera trap datasets, two-stage models provide better accuracy. On the other hand, one-stage detectors are more suited for real-time wildlife surveillance using UAVs or embedded devices due to their lightweight and fast processing capabilities. This comparison remains central to the selection of models for field-based animal monitoring, where a balance must be achieved between precision and processing constraints.

Metrics for evaluation

To rigorously assess the performance of object detection algorithms, a variety of evaluation metrics are used. These metrics help measure how accurately a model can identify and localize objects under diverse conditions such as occlusion, scale variation, and cluttered backgrounds. Below is an overview of the most widely used evaluation metrics in object detection research:

Intersection over Union (IoU): IoU quantifies the overlap between the predicted bounding box and the corresponding ground-truth box. It is computed as the area of intersection divided by the area of union of the two bounding boxes. An IoU of 1 indicates a perfect match. In practical settings, a prediction is considered correct if its IoU exceeds a predefined threshold, typically 0.5 (IoU@0.5). IoU serves as the basis for many detection-related metrics and directly affects recall and precision calculations31.

It is computed as the area of intersection divided by the area of union of the two bounding boxes. An IoU of 1 indicates a perfect match. Precision and Recall: Precision is the ratio of correctly predicted positive detections to the total number of positive detections made by the model. It measures how reliable the detections are. Recall, on the other hand, is the ratio of correctly predicted positives to all actual positives in the dataset, indicating the model’s ability to capture all relevant instances. In high-stakes applications such as wildlife conservation, high recall is vital to ensure no target species go undetected32.

A higher AP value suggests the detector is capable of consistently identifying the object class with high precision and recall across different confidence levels33.

Deep learning models for animal detection

Deep learning has transformed the landscape of animal detection by offering powerful tools capable of learning hierarchical patterns from complex visual data. Traditional computer vision techniques often relied on handcrafted features, which proved inadequate in variable environments like forests, aerial footage, or nighttime monitoring. With the advent of convolutional neural networks (CNNs), models began to automatically learn spatially localized features, enabling higher detection accuracy and better generalization across species, environments, and lighting conditions. CNN-based architectures such as Faster R-CNN and Mask R-CNN have shown high precision in animal recognition tasks, particularly in static settings like camera traps. For instance, Schneider et al. (2018) demonstrated that Faster R-CNN could achieve over 93% accuracy in animal detection within the Snapshot Serengeti dataset. These region-based models, while highly accurate, tend to be computationally intensive and less suited for real-time applications.

To address these limitations, one-stage detectors like YOLO (You Only Look Once) have emerged as the dominant solution for real-time animal detection tasks. YOLO models, especially versions from YOLOv5 through YOLOv8, offer a favorable balance between detection speed and accuracy as shown in Fig. 4. They are extensively used in UAV-based animal tracking, drone-mounted surveillance, and highway wildlife monitoring. Enhanced variants such as YOLO-SAG and WildARe-YOLO incorporate lightweight backbones, attention modules, and novel loss functions to improve detection of small or occluded animals, which are common in ecological datasets. Beyond standard architectures, research has explored data-efficient approaches like active learning and few-shot learning to overcome the challenge of limited labeled animal data. For example, models trained with active sample selection strategies have reached high recall using only a fraction of labeled datasets. These advances make it feasible to deploy detection models in data-scarce or low-resource settings, such as conservation areas and rural farmland. Overall, deep learning models continue to evolve in accuracy, speed, and adaptability. Their deployment in real-world ecological contexts, whether through embedded edge devices, mobile applications, or large-scale surveys, has enabled better monitoring, population counting, and protection of biodiversity across the globe.

Small-object detection in aerial data Small targets remain the main challenge for UAV surveys, where animals may occupy fewer than 30 pixels. WildLive’s onboard implementation49 sustained 17 FPS at HD resolution on Jetson Orin AGX but only 7 FPS for 4K input, showing how input resolution and model size directly constrain real-time UAV operations. In embedded scenarios, quantised or pruned YOLOv8-Nano and YOLOv11-Tiny configurations typically retain 90% of baseline accuracy while halving inference latency—a critical step for solar-powered deployments.

Dataset landscape in animal detection

The advancement of automated animal-detection systems is fundamentally linked to the availability of large, diverse, and well-annotated datasets. These datasets vary markedly in scale (image/volume count), taxonomic breadth (species covered), geographic and temporal span (sites, seasons, years), annotation quality (bounding boxes vs image-labels vs segmentation), and imaging modality (camera traps, UAV/airborne, satellite, thermal/infrared, underwater, synthetic). Camera trap datasets

Camera-trap datasets continue to form the backbone of many wildlife detection pipelines due to their extended temporal coverage, realism in natural habitats, and diversity of ambient conditions (illumination, occlusion, background clutter). For example, the Snapshot Serengeti project deployed 225 camera traps across 1,125 km in Serengeti National Park, Tanzania, between 2010 and 2013, accumulating 99,241 camera-trap days and 1.2 million image sets (of which 322,653 contained animals)45. The dataset comprises 40 mammal species, with over 10.8 million volunteer classifications from 28,000 registered participants59. Other camera-trap datasets (e.g., regional North-American sets) capture North-temperate species, occluded habitats, varying lighting and seasons, further enhancing generalisation research.

Strengths: Large image volumes and natural habitat diversity. Rich annotation metadata (species ID, count, behaviour). Temporal sampling enabling longitudinal population/ecology studies. Proven utility in training detection and classification models (e.g., deep nets >93% accuracy on 3.2 M images)124.

Gaps and challenges: Limited geographic diversity; many datasets are ecosystem-specific, reducing cross-site generalisation. Class imbalance: frequent species dominate; rare species often under-represented (leading to poor detection recall). Annotation type often image-level rather than bounding-box; some support bounding boxes only on subsets ( 78,000 images in Snapshot–Serengeti)119. Many “empty” frames: Snapshot indicates 76% are empty–models must effectively discriminate absence/presence. Domain shift across camera models, habitat, species behaviour complicates transfer learning.

What the evidence says – Camera-trap datasetsLarge, long-duration camera-trap datasets (e.g., 7.1 M images, 40 species in Snapshot) provide essential training and ecological insight. However, systems trained on one site often drop performance when applied to new habitats, rare-species detection remains weak, and many datasets lack bounding-box granularity.

UAV and satellite-based datasets

Datasets collected via drones and satellites extend monitoring capabilities across wide open landscapes, large herds, remote terrain, and inaccessible habitats. UAV surveys like WAID emphasise small-object detection (target sizes 20–50 pixels) and multi-sensor inputs (RGB, thermal, multispectral). Satellite collections like MammData span thousands of square kilometres at 0.3–1 m resolution and provide point-location annotations for 14 megafauna species45. These datasets are critical for scaling detection beyond ground-based sensors, enabling aerial census, migration mapping, and habitat-wide monitoring.

Strengths: Large spatial coverage and high altitude vantage points. Access to open landscapes, herds, species that avoid ground traps. Multi-sensor and multi-temporal data enabling cross-modal research.

Gaps and challenges: Small-object sizes: animals often occupy less than 20 pixels detection error increases substantially. Atmospheric, illumination, terrain and registration artefacts: e.g., haze, parallax, motion blur. Limited annotation depth: often point-locations rather than bounding boxes or segmentation masks. Few public datasets relative to camera-trap sets; many remain proprietary or less annotated for CV use.

What the evidence says – UAV/Satellite datasetsUAV and satellite datasets (e.g., WAID aerial, MammData satellite) enable wide-area monitoring but face steep small-object detection challenges (<20 px), sensor/registration noise and annotation scarcity–these remain major bottlenecks for operational deployment.

Other modalities: thermal, underwater, synthetic

Detection tasks now extend beyond visible imagery into thermal/infrared, underwater imaging and synthetic photorealistic renders. For instance, synthetic datasets provide hundreds of thousands of fully annotated samples (bounding boxes, segmentation, pose) for species and conditions that are rare or unsafe to capture in the wild. Thermal datasets capture nocturnal, hidden or crepuscular species under infrared conditions; underwater datasets entail fish, marine mammals and deep-sea fauna. These modalities open new domains of animal surveillance but bring unique technical issues.

Strengths: Expand coverage across modalities and species (underwater, nocturnal, low visibility). Synthetic data alleviates annotation cost and rare species scarcity. Multi-modal fusion (RGB + thermal + LiDAR) can boost detection robustness.

Gaps and challenges: Domain shift: syntheticreal gap remains large; underwater/thermal physics differ substantially from RGB. Annotation cost: thermal and underwater datasets often have fewer samples and less species breadth. Hardware and sensor complexity: underwater/thermal rigs are costly, less accessible for broad use.

What the evidence says – Other modalities & synthetic datasetsDatasets from thermal, underwater and synthetic domains significantly broaden species and environment coverage, yet deployment is constrained by limited real-world annotations, modality-specific noise, and synthetic-to-real generalisation gaps.

Dataset limitations: class imbalance, rare species, low-resolution

While dataset volumes have grown, structural limitations remain critical hindrances. Key issues include:

Class imbalance: Some species dominate training sets (hundreds of thousands of images) whereas others may have <100 samples, causing low recall for rare species. Low resolution and small target size: Particularly in UAV and satellite imagery, target animals may occupy only a few pixels (2–20 px), increasing miss-rates significantly. Domain shift and generalisation gaps: Models evaluated on new sites, new sensors or seasons often incur drops of 5–15 % mAP compared to source domain. Annotation consistency: Varied annotation types (image-level labels, bounding boxes, segmentation masks) and metadata across datasets hinder cross-comparison and meta-analysis.

To mitigate these issues several strategies have emerged: class-balanced and curriculum sampling, cross-dataset transfer learning, domain adaptation (e.g., weak supervision for syntheticreal) and super-resolution for small-object enhancement. When combined, these approaches form a multi-pronged response to the structural bias and quality limitations in wildlife datasets.

What the evidence says – Dataset structural gapsEven with large datasets, major performance bottlenecks remain: rare-species representation is weak, small-object detection (<20 px) remains error-prone, and many datasets lack cross-site or cross-sensor diversity–these hamper model generalisation and fairness.

Taxonomy of animal detection tasks

Automated animal detection tasks span a broad spectrum of objectives, ranging from simple binary presence checks to fine-grained behavioral tracking over extended periods. A clear understanding of this taxonomy is crucial for aligning task complexity with suitable modeling approaches, dataset structures, and deployment contexts. Below, we detail the major categories of animal detection tasks, enriched with methodological nuances, dataset examples, and application-specific challenges.

Detection, Classification, and Segmentation: Detection refers to the process of localizing animals within an image by generating bounding boxes and simultaneously assigning a species label. For example, convolutional neural networks fine-tuned on the Snapshot Serengeti dataset have achieved over 90% localization accuracy across diverse habitats61. Classification, in contrast, is a less granular task that determines the presence or absence of animals without specifying their location. This is especially valuable for filtering large collections of images, such as motion-triggered camera trap sequences where a significant proportion may be empty due to false triggers. Segmentation provides pixel-level delineation of animals, offering high-resolution shape masks that can be used for morphometric analysis, posture estimation, and precise occlusion handling. Modern segmentation pipelines often leverage encoder–decoder architectures (e.g., Mask R-CNN, DeepLabV3+) and have been instrumental in ecological studies requiring accurate measurement of body size, tracking limb positions, or resolving overlapping individuals in dense groups.

High-performance object detection models such as YOLOv5, Faster R-CNN, and RetinaNet have been successfully adapted for wildlife monitoring by fine-tuning on species-specific datasets. Species-level detection must contend with challenges such as visual camouflage, seasonal coat changes, and partial occlusion from vegetation. Additionally, multi-spectral or thermal data fusion is increasingly being used to enhance detection robustness under low-light or high-contrast conditions.

Behavior Recognition: Behavior recognition seeks to detect and classify specific animal activities or states, such as feeding, resting, aggression, courtship, or parental care. Unlike static detection, this task requires temporal modeling of sequences, often using architectures like 3D CNNs, temporal convolutional networks (TCNs), or transformer-based video models. In livestock applications, deep learning systems have accurately distinguished between feeding and non-feeding visits in pig farming environments without the need for individual identification63. In wildlife contexts, datasets like MammalNet support classification of behaviors including locomotion, social interaction, and environmental exploration, leveraging multi-camera setups to reduce viewpoint bias. Behavior recognition is also critical in conservation to assess stress responses, detect early signs of disease, or monitor the effectiveness of wildlife corridors.

Counting and Density Estimation: This task estimates the total number of individuals or computes density maps within a scene, which is especially important in aerial surveys, marine ecology, and large-scale migratory monitoring. Density-map regression using encoder–decoder architectures like U-Net and CSRNet has been effective in counting large herds or schools of animals under heavy occlusion. For example, sea lion population surveys have benefited from minimal point annotations, while uncertainty-regularized CNNs have improved fish counting accuracy in underwater videos by modeling the confidence of density predictions64. Furthermore, satellite imagery-based counting of elephants has demonstrated the potential of high-resolution remote sensing when combined with domain-adapted deep learning models.

Multi-Animal Tracking and Re-Identification: Multi-animal tracking (MOT) extends detection across frames to maintain identity consistency, while re-identification (Re-ID) focuses on recognizing the same individual across time, locations, or camera viewpoints. This is foundational for studies of long-term movement patterns, social hierarchy, and mating behavior. The BuckTales dataset65, featuring over 1.2 million bounding box annotations and 680 identity tracks of blackbuck antelopes captured by UAVs, is an example of a resource designed for joint MOT and Re-ID. State-of-the-art approaches integrate deep appearance embeddings with motion models such as Kalman filters or ByteTrack, handling challenges like rapid movement, body orientation changes, and temporary occlusions. In marine biology, similar pipelines have been applied to track individual whale sharks, using unique skin patterns as biometric identifiers. Emerging trends include integrating multi-modal identity cues (thermal signatures, acoustic signals) and leveraging self-supervised representation learning to improve tracking reliability in environments where labeled identity data is scarce.

Comparative performance analysis

Understanding task-specific performance trade-offs is critical for selecting the most effective detection model for wildlife monitoring. Below is a detailed comparative evaluation across multiple dimensions, integrating benchmark results, modality-specific considerations, and deployment constraints, also summarized in Table 7.

CNN vs YOLO: Speed vs Accuracy: YOLO-style detectors offer exceptional real-time performance due to their unified end-to-end architecture, where localization and classification are performed in a single network pass. The original YOLO model processes up to 45 FPS, while Fast YOLO achieves approximately 155 FPS with moderate mean Average Precision (mAP). By contrast, two-stage detectors such as Faster R-CNN deliver superior accuracy–particularly for small, camouflaged, or partially occluded wildlife–but typically operate at slower rates (around 5–7 FPS)62. For instance, in recent wildlife video benchmarks, YOLOv8 maintained real-time inference speeds while delivering accuracy close to Faster R-CNN66. This makes YOLO well-suited for UAV-based surveys, solar-powered remote stations, and real-time anti-poaching systems, whereas Faster R-CNN remains advantageous for offline post-processing where precision is more critical than speed. Furthermore, the latest YOLO versions incorporate transformer blocks and adaptive anchor-free heads, narrowing the accuracy gap while retaining speed advantages. Static images from camera traps allow straightforward application of conventional object detectors, benefiting from large labeled datasets. Video data enables temporal smoothing, motion-compensated frame differencing, and sequence-based architectures (e.g., ConvLSTM, Temporal Shift Modules), which reduce false positives caused by transient noise or foliage motion. Aerial imagery introduces unique challenges–animals often occupy only a few dozen pixels, requiring high-resolution backbones and techniques like Feature Pyramid Networks (FPN) to preserve small-object details68. Infrared (IR) video data, while robust against illumination variation, demands domain-specific augmentation (e.g., simulated thermal blur, noise modeling) and spectral-aware backbone initialization for optimal performance69. These adaptations ensure that models remain generalizable when deployed across different ecological survey platforms.

Environmental and deployment challenges

Deploying deep learning models for wildlife monitoring in natural environments introduces a range of technical and operational obstacles. These challenges arise from environmental variability, the physical limitations of sensors, and the intrinsic constraints of ecological data. The following categories summarize the most significant issues and their implications for system performance.

Real-World Variations: Occlusion, Lighting, and Camouflage: Occlusion frequently occurs when animals are partially hidden by dense vegetation, rugged terrain, or even by other animals in group settings, leading to incomplete visual cues and a higher probability of missed detections72. Lighting variations–ranging from the bright glare of midday sun to the near-complete darkness of nocturnal activity–cause substantial fluctuations in image contrast, color balance, and noise levels. Such changes can disrupt model generalization, particularly when the training dataset lacks diverse illumination scenarios73. Camouflage adds another layer of difficulty, as many species have evolved coat patterns or coloration that blend seamlessly with their habitat. Detecting such animals often requires specialized Camouflaged Object Detection (COD) networks, which emphasize fine-grained texture cues and contextual background modeling to differentiate targets from their surroundings74.

Small Object Detection: Many wildlife monitoring applications, especially those involving UAV imagery or distant camera-trap placements, must detect animals that occupy only a handful of pixels in an image. This low spatial resolution can lead to the loss of discriminative features necessary for accurate classification and localization. Traditional detectors often fail under these conditions unless enhanced with architectures such as Feature Pyramid Networks (FPN) that maintain multi-scale feature representations, or preprocessing strategies like image tiling and super-resolution. These methods allow models to retain fine details and improve recall rates for small-bodied species, such as birds, rodents, or reptiles75.

Weather and Seasonal Effects: Environmental factors such as rain, snow, fog, dust, or high humidity can alter visibility and introduce visual artifacts, leading to detection errors. Seasonal changes, including variations in foliage density, ground cover, and background coloration, can drastically shift the visual context between training and deployment phases. For instance, a model trained during a dry season in savanna grasslands may fail to detect animals in the same location during the wet season due to changes in vegetation height and coloration73. Incorporating weather-diverse training datasets, along with domain-specific data augmentation techniques (e.g., synthetic rain or fog overlays), has proven effective for enhancing robustness to such environmental variability.

Dataset Bias and Annotation Quality: The reliability of deep learning systems is highly dependent on the quality and balance of the training data. Class imbalance, where common species dominate the dataset while rare or threatened species are underrepresented, can skew model predictions toward overrepresented classes. Annotation errors–including inaccurate bounding boxes, misidentified species, or inconsistent labeling conventions–reduce both training efficiency and evaluation reliability77. These issues are particularly prevalent in crowd-sourced datasets, where non-expert annotators contribute labels. Mitigation strategies include active learning frameworks that prioritize uncertain or underrepresented samples for expert review, and semi-supervised learning pipelines that leverage both labeled and unlabeled data to improve annotation coverage and consistency78.

Advanced techniques for enhancing animal detection systems

Recent advances in machine learning have driven the development of sophisticated methods to improve animal detection performance under diverse and challenging conditions. These techniques address limitations in training data, generalization across species, and deployment in resource-constrained environments. By combining innovations in data generation, learning paradigms, model optimization, and multimodal sensing, researchers are achieving significant gains in accuracy, robustness, and adaptability. The following sections present state-of-the-art approaches that collectively advance the reliability and scalability of wildlife monitoring systems.

Data augmentation and synthetic data generation

Modern animal detection systems benefit significantly from advanced data augmentation techniques that go beyond traditional image transformations. Recent work by88 has demonstrated that habitat-specific augmentation strategies can improve model robustness by up to 18%. These techniques include biologically-informed transformations such as fur pattern variations, seasonal coat color changes, and realistic occlusion simulations using vegetation models. For endangered species with limited training data,89 developed a generative adversarial network (GAN) framework that produces photorealistic animal images with 32 controllable parameters including body posture, lighting conditions, and environmental context. Their system achieved 0.91 correlation between synthetic and real image performance on tiger detection tasks. More recently,90 introduced a physics-based rendering pipeline that simulates animal behaviors in 3D environments, enabling the generation of rare scenarios like predator-prey interactions or nocturnal activities. When combined with domain adaptation techniques, their synthetic data improved real-world detection accuracy by 22% for low-density species populations. Current challenges include maintaining anatomical accuracy across diverse species and simulating complex environmental interactions, but the field is rapidly advancing with the integration of neural rendering techniques.

Few-shot and zero-shot learning approaches

The biological diversity of animal species necessitates learning paradigms that can generalize from limited examples. Reference91 developed a hierarchical meta-learning framework that organizes species taxonomically, enabling knowledge transfer between related organisms. Their approach achieves 84% accuracy with just five examples per species by leveraging shared visual features within taxonomic groups. For completely unseen species,92 created a vision-language model that connects visual features with textual descriptions from ecological databases, achieving 78% zero-shot accuracy on novel species identification. The model uses a cross-modal attention mechanism to align visual patterns with semantic attributes like “striped fur” or “aquatic habitat”. Recent breakthroughs by93 combine these approaches with phylogenetic constraints, using evolutionary relationships to guide feature sharing between species. Their framework demonstrates particular effectiveness for cryptic species complexes, improving identification accuracy by 31% over conventional methods. Current research focuses on incorporating behavioral cues and ecological niche modeling to further enhance few-shot capabilities in field conditions.

Semi-supervised and self-supervised learning strategies

The abundance of unlabeled wildlife imagery presents both a challenge and opportunity for animal detection systems. Reference94 developed EcoContrast, a self-supervised framework that learns robust visual representations from camera trap footage without labels. Their method uses temporal consistency and multi-view contrastive learning to achieve 0.72 linear evaluation accuracy on the LILA benchmark. For semi-supervised learning,95 introduced an adaptive pseudo-labeling system that dynamically adjusts confidence thresholds based on habitat characteristics and species prevalence. Their approach processes millions of camera trap images while requiring labels for only 12% of the data, achieving within 3% of fully supervised performance. The most recent advancement by96 combines noisy student training with spatial-temporal consistency regularization, enabling effective learning from partially labeled video sequences. Their system demonstrates particular strength in handling long-tailed species distributions, improving recall for rare species by 19% compared to standard approaches. These techniques are revolutionizing large-scale ecological monitoring by making comprehensive biodiversity assessment computationally feasible.

Model compression and optimization for edge deployment

Deploying animal detection systems in field conditions requires careful optimization for resource-constrained devices. Howard et. al. introduced a compound scaling method that systematically balances network width, depth, and resolution to create efficient architectures for edge devices. Their optimized models achieve 90% of the accuracy of full-sized networks while reducing parameters by 18x. For extreme compression scenarios,97 developed a quantization-aware training framework that maintains 8-bit integer precision without significant accuracy loss, enabling deployment on solar-powered camera traps. Reference98 took a biologically-inspired approach to model pruning, using evolutionary algorithms to remove redundant filters while preserving performance on critical species. Their method reduces computation by 12x for tropical forest monitoring systems. The most comprehensive solution comes from99, who created an automated neural architecture search pipeline specifically for wildlife applications. Their system considers not just accuracy but also energy consumption, memory footprint, and real-time constraints, producing tailored models for various deployment scenarios from drones to underwater sensors.

Multimodal fusion techniques

Integrating multiple sensing modalities significantly enhances animal detection capabilities across diverse environments. Reference100 developed an attention-based fusion network that optimally combines thermal and RGB information based on time of day and weather conditions. Their system improves nocturnal detection rates by 37% while maintaining daytime performance. For aquatic environments,101 created a hybrid audio-visual model that synchronizes underwater acoustic signatures with visual detections, enabling 3D tracking of marine mammals with 89% accuracy. The most sophisticated approach by102 uses transformer architectures to fuse six modalities (RGB, thermal, depth, audio, motion, and weather data) in wildlife monitoring stations. Their framework dynamically weights input sources based on reliability estimates, achieving robust 24/7 operation across seasons. Recent work has also explored behavioral cue integration, where movement patterns and social interactions complement visual detections. These multimodal systems are particularly valuable for biodiversity hotspots where single-modality approaches often fail due to environmental complexity.

Tools, frameworks, and benchmarks for wildlife detection systems

The development of robust wildlife detection systems requires a carefully curated stack of software tools and frameworks that address the unique challenges of ecological monitoring. Modern pipelines integrate specialized components for data annotation, model training, performance benchmarking, and deployment, each contributing to the overall system effectiveness. This section examines the current technological landscape through the lens of practical implementation considerations, performance tradeoffs, and domain-specific adaptations that distinguish wildlife detection from generic computer vision applications.

Training frameworks

The choice of machine learning framework significantly impacts both development velocity and final model performance. PyTorch has emerged as the preferred platform for research prototypes and iterative development, owing to its dynamic computation graph that enables flexible architecture modifications during experimentation. Recent studies by116 demonstrate that PyTorch’s eager execution mode reduces debugging time by approximately 40% compared to static graph alternatives, particularly when testing novel attention mechanisms for animal pose estimation. The framework’s native support for mixed-precision training through NVIDIA’s Apex library has proven invaluable for handling high-resolution camera trap imagery, allowing researchers to maintain batch sizes of 32-64 even with input resolutions exceeding 20002000 pixels. However, TensorFlow maintains strong adoption in production systems due to its superior deployment tooling, particularly for edge devices. The TensorFlow Lite converter’s ability to optimize models for specific hardware accelerators (e.g., Coral Edge TPUs or NVIDIA Jetson GPUs) often results in 3-5 faster inference speeds compared to generic ONNX exports. Specialized frameworks like MMDetection have filled crucial niches by providing pre-configured implementations of advanced architectures like Cascade R-CNN and Swin Transformers, which have shown particular promise in handling the extreme class imbalance common in wildlife datasets. Benchmark testing on the Snapshot Serengeti dataset reveals that MMDetection’s implementation of Hybrid Task Cascade achieves 12% higher mean average precision than baseline Faster R-CNN implementations when detecting rare species with fewer than 100 training examples.

Annotation tools and data management

High-quality annotated datasets remain the foundation of effective wildlife detection systems, necessitating tools that can handle the unique characteristics of ecological imagery. The Computer Vision Annotation Tool (CVAT) has become the platform of choice for large-scale collaborative projects due to its sophisticated video annotation capabilities. In field tests conducted by118, CVAT’s interpolation features reduced annotation time for continuous camera trap videos by 72% compared to frame-by-frame labeling, while maintaining 98% bounding box consistency through optical flow-based propagation. The tool’s recently added AI-assisted labeling functionality, which integrates active learning loops with human verification, has demonstrated the ability to reduce total annotation effort by 60% on the Wildlife Insights dataset while actually improving label quality through consensus mechanisms. For smaller projects or static image collections, LabelImg provides a lightweight alternative with significantly lower computational requirements, though at the cost of advanced features like attribute annotation or hierarchical taxonomy management. Emerging tools like VGG Image Annotator (VIA) have gained traction for specific use cases requiring detailed part annotations (e.g., wing markings on birds or pelage patterns on mammals), offering polygon and point annotation modes that are particularly valuable for individual identification tasks. Practical experience across multiple conservation projects suggests that the choice of annotation tool should be driven by three key factors: the volume of temporal data (video vs. still images), the complexity of the taxonomic hierarchy, and the need for distributed collaboration among domain experts.

Benchmarking platforms

Standardized evaluation platforms have become critical for advancing the field of wildlife detection by enabling reproducible comparisons across methods. WildlifeInsights represents the most comprehensive effort to date, hosting over 14.7 million images from camera trap networks across 37 countries while providing curated evaluation subsets for controlled benchmarking. The platform’s data ingestion pipeline, described in detail by117, incorporates automated quality control filters that reject 18-22% of incoming images due to motion blur, overexposure, or empty triggers, significantly reducing noise in training datasets. For citizen science initiatives, Zooniverse has developed specialized workflows that combine inputs from multiple non-expert annotators through probabilistic consensus models, achieving expert-level accuracy on common species identification while maintaining throughput rates of over 50,000 classifications per hour. Recent innovations in benchmark design have addressed longstanding challenges in wildlife detection evaluation, including the introduction of sequence-aware metrics that account for temporal consistency in video data and habitat-specific evaluation subsets that control for background variation. The LILA benchmark suite139 has proven particularly influential by providing carefully curated test sets that isolate specific challenges like occlusions, camouflage, and rare species occurrences, enabling more granular analysis of model strengths and weaknesses.

Challenges and research gaps

Wildlife monitoring using computer vision, while showing remarkable progress, still faces several critical challenges that hinder its robustness, scalability, and practical deployment in diverse ecosystems. These challenges arise from technical limitations, ecological complexities, and ethical considerations. The following points summarize the key issues and research gaps in the field.

Poor generalization to new environments: One of the most pressing challenges in wildlife monitoring using computer vision is the poor generalization of models to new environments. Deep learning models trained on specific ecosystems often fail to maintain accuracy when deployed in different geographical locations or under varying environmental conditions119. This limitation stems from domain shift problems where training data fails to represent the full diversity of habitats, lighting conditions, and weather patterns encountered in real-world deployments. Researchers have noted that even state-of-the-art object detection models experience significant performance drops when applied to unseen camera trap locations, sometimes showing up to 50% reduction in accuracy metrics120. The development of domain adaptation techniques and more robust feature extraction methods that can handle environmental variability remains an active area of research.

This imbalance creates significant challenges for machine learning models, which tend to be biased toward majority classes, potentially overlooking endangered species that are ecologically most important to monitor122. Current approaches to address this issue include class-balanced sampling strategies, loss function modifications, and data augmentation techniques, but these methods often fail to maintain satisfactory performance on tail classes123. The development of specialized architectures and training paradigms that can effectively learn from imbalanced wildlife data while preserving rare species detection capability represents a critical research gap in the field.

Future directions

The field of wildlife monitoring using computer vision is rapidly evolving, and several promising research avenues could substantially enhance detection accuracy, robustness, and conservation impact. These future directions span innovations in model architectures, multi-modal sensing, human–machine collaboration, autonomous aerial systems, evaluation frameworks, and integration with conservation decision-making processes. The following points outline these potential paths forward.

Vision Transformers for Animal Detection: The application of Vision Transformers (ViTs) in wildlife monitoring represents a paradigm shift from conventional convolutional approaches. Recent work by131 has demonstrated that transformer architectures can outperform CNN-based models in large-scale image recognition tasks by capturing global contextual relationships through self-attention mechanisms. For animal detection specifically, ViTs could address critical challenges such as recognizing partially occluded animals in dense vegetation or distinguishing cryptic species that rely on camouflage139. However, significant research gaps remain in adapting these architectures for ecological applications, including developing lightweight versions for edge deployment in camera traps, handling extreme class imbalance (where some species may have orders of magnitude fewer training examples than others), and creating interpretable attention maps that can provide ecological insights beyond simple detection. Future work should also investigate hybrid architectures that combine the local feature extraction strengths of CNNs with the global reasoning capabilities of transformers, particularly for multi-scale animal detection where targets may range from tiny distant specks to large close-up individuals within the same image sequence.

The human-computer interaction research by136 highlights the importance of designing interfaces that support ecological domain experts in efficiently validating and correcting model outputs without requiring machine learning expertise. Key challenges include developing: (1) collaborative annotation systems that can reconcile conflicting expert opinions when species identification is ambiguous, (2) real-time feedback mechanisms that allow field researchers to immediately flag and correct errors during deployment, and (3) continuous learning architectures that can incrementally incorporate new expert knowledge without catastrophic forgetting of previously learned information. Particularly impactful would be systems that can quantify and communicate model uncertainty to human reviewers, enabling experts to focus their attention on the most problematic cases while trusting the model’s high-confidence predictions.

Swarm UAV Networks for Dynamic Animal Tracking: The coordinated use of unmanned aerial vehicle (UAV) swarms for wildlife monitoring presents transformative potential but requires solving complex technical and operational challenges. Recent advances in swarm robotics137 have demonstrated the feasibility of decentralized control algorithms for area coverage and target tracking, but adapting these approaches for ecological applications introduces unique constraints. As noted by138, UAV systems must minimize disturbance to sensitive species while maintaining effective observation capabilities - requiring novel approaches to quiet electric propulsion, non-threatening flight patterns, and optimal altitude/speed profiles for different taxa. From a technical perspective, key research directions include: developing energy-aware cooperative path planning algorithms that balance coverage and endurance, creating robust inter-UAV communication protocols for operation in remote areas without cellular infrastructure, and designing computer vision pipelines that can process imagery in real-time across distributed nodes. Regulatory frameworks will also need to evolve to enable large-scale scientific deployment of UAV swarms while addressing airspace management and privacy concerns. The ultimate goal would be autonomous systems capable of tracking animal migrations over hundreds of kilometers while adapting to changing environmental conditions and animal behaviors.

Universal Wildlife Detection Benchmarks: The establishment of comprehensive, standardized evaluation frameworks is critical for advancing the state-of-the-art in wildlife monitoring technologies. Current computer vision benchmarks focus primarily on common objects and lack the taxonomic diversity and ecological relevance needed for conservation applications. Pioneering work by139 has begun addressing this gap for camera trap data, but much remains to be done in creating benchmarks that: (1) represent global biodiversity across biomes and taxonomic groups, (2) include challenging real-world conditions like adverse weather, motion blur, and occlusions, (3) provide hierarchical annotation schemes that support identification at multiple taxonomic levels (e.g., family, genus, species), and (4) incorporate temporal context for behavior recognition. Such benchmarks should also include carefully designed test sets that evaluate model performance on rare species and novel environments to assess generalization capability. Equally important is the development of appropriate evaluation metrics beyond simple accuracy - including ecological relevance scores that weight species differently based on conservation status or monitoring priorities. Community efforts to create and maintain these benchmarks will require unprecedented collaboration between computer scientists and ecologists, with shared data governance frameworks that respect sensitive location information for threatened species. Conclusion

Animal detection through deep learning has emerged as a powerful solution to a range of challenges in environmental monitoring, ecological conservation, and agricultural automation. This review has thoroughly examined the capabilities of Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) and the You Only Look Once (YOLO) family of models, outlining their architectural innovations, application-specific strengths, and evolving roles in animal detection tasks across various settings. CNNs have shown superior performance in tasks involving fine-grained feature extraction, species classification, and behavior recognition, particularly when large labeled datasets and computational resources are available. YOLO models, on the other hand, offer exceptional real-time detection capabilities and are highly suitable for scenarios demanding high-speed object localization such as aerial surveillance, real-time video analysis, and mobile deployments in remote or challenging environments. Both architectures have been applied successfully in contexts such as wildlife monitoring, poaching prevention, livestock tracking, roadkill detection, and biodiversity documentation, demonstrating their relevance to pressing ecological and societal concerns.

Despite the rapid advancements, several significant challenges remain. Many current detection systems struggle with generalization when exposed to new environments, rare species, or varying lighting and weather conditions. Data scarcity is particularly problematic in the field of animal detection, where acquiring large, annotated, and balanced datasets is often labor-intensive and expensive. Moreover, the deployment of deep models in real-world applications often encounters hardware constraints, especially in embedded or battery-powered systems that require low latency and efficient energy usage. While model compression techniques and lightweight architectures like MobileNet and YOLOv8n offer partial solutions, maintaining high accuracy under such constraints remains an open problem. Furthermore, deep learning models often operate as black boxes, raising concerns about interpretability and trust, particularly when used in critical ecological decision-making. The integration of explainable AI, attention mechanisms, and contextual learning could help improve both model transparency and robustness in uncertain or dynamic conditions.

Looking to the future, it is clear that interdisciplinary collaboration will be essential for progress. Researchers in computer vision, ecology, hardware engineering, and data ethics must work together to develop systems that are accurate, efficient, explainable, and scalable. The development of shared, open-access animal detection datasets, domain adaptation techniques for unseen species or environments, and context-aware models that incorporate temporal and spatial metadata will be key to advancing this field. Real-world impact will depend not only on technical innovation but also on policy integration, public engagement, and responsible data practices. As climate change, habitat loss, and species extinction accelerate, the role of deep learning in monitoring and protecting animal populations becomes not just an opportunity but a necessity. CNN and YOLO models have already demonstrated their transformative potential; with continued innovation, responsible deployment, and ecological awareness, they can serve as a foundational technology for global wildlife conservation and sustainable development.
