Association of physical activity levels with frailty index in elderly Chinese: evidence from the China health and retirement longitudinal study (CHARLS)
Authors: Qianqian Li, Jiayi Guan, Ronghui Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
As the global trend of population aging intensifies, frailty among the elderly has emerged as a significant public Health concern. This study examines the relationship between physical activity levels and the Frailty Index in older adults, utilizing data from the 2018 China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS). The study sample included 10,240 Chinese adults aged ≥ 60 years.Frailty was assessed using the Frailty Index (FI), which was calculated based on the accumulation of age-related health deficits. The level of physical activity were determined using the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ) and expressed in terms of metabolic equivalent (MET) units. The dose-response relationship between physical activity and the Frailty Index was examined using restricted cubic splines (RCS) and logistic regression analysis. The results demonstrated a significant inverse relationship between physical activity level and the risk of frailty. Specifically, participants in the highest quartile of physical activity had a 45% lower risk of frailty compared to those in the lowest quartile (OR = 0.55, 95% CI: 0.48–0.63). The protective effect of physical activity remained robust across various models, even after adjusting for potential confounders, such as age, sex, education level, marital status, and health behaviors. The RCS model revealed a nonlinear relationship between physical activity and frailty risk, higher levels of physical activity significantly reduce the risk of frailty in the elderly, with moderate-to high-intensity physical activity playing a crucial role in decreasing the prevalence of frailty. However, further increase in activity beyond a certain point may not yield additional benefits.

Introduction

Over the past decade, the global demographic structure has undergone significant changes due to the rapid growth of the aging population. This shift is primarily driven by increased Life expectancy, declining birth rates, and overall population growth.The WHO projects that by 2030, one in six people worldwide will be 60 years or older. The population of those aged 60 and above is expected to grow from 1 billion in 2020 to 1.4 billion by 2030, doubling to 2.1 billion by 2050 [1]. China, the world’s most populous country, is witnessing an even sharper aging trend, with the number of individuals over 60 projected to reach 402 million by 2040 [2]. This demographic shift presents a significant challenge for the frailty of older adults. Frailty is a dynamic, age-associated condition marked by the deterioration of various physical and physiological functions, such as strength and endurance, which increases vulnerability to stressors [3, 4]. A growing body of epidemiological research indicates that frailty significantly increases the likelihood of death in the elderly and is closely linked to higher risks of falls and dementia [3, 5–9]. Therefore, in the context of an aging population, frailty has become a major public health concern, posing considerable challenges to reducing the associated social and medical burdens.

Geriatric health encompasses multiple dimensions including cognitive, physical, and psychological well-being. Preventing or delaying the decline of various functional states and the progression to disability in older adults, as well as maintaining their self-sufficiency, has long been one of the primary and enduring goals of geriatric medicine and remains a continuous focus of research [3]. Therefore, in recent decades, frailty has received increasing attention as a window of opportunity for avoiding or postponing disability. Frailty is commonly assessed using two prevalent models: the Frailty Phenotype (FP) model and the Cumulative Deficit Index (CDI) model [10, 11]. The Cumulative Deficit Index (CDI) model, also referred to as the Frailty Index (FI) model, is developed by aggregating at least 30 health deficits [12]. These deficits are age-related, linked to poor health outcomes, and encompass various systems, including cognitive function, mobility, and psychological health [13, 14]. As a result, the Frailty Index (FI) is a systematic and widely used tool for measuring frailty in older adults. Research suggests that the FI offers a more accurate assessment and prediction of mortality risk in older adults, both in community and hospital settings, compared to the Frailty Phenotype (FP) model [2, 15, 16].

According to the definition of physical inactivity outlined in the WHO physical activity guidelines, the prevalence of physical inactivity has increased among individuals aged 60 years and older in all regions and sexes [17]. However, increasing evidence has reported the benefits of exercise interventions on the functional status of older adults, including frailty. Higher PA levels are associated with decreased odds of frailty, showing a protective effect against frailty risk in community-dwelling middle-aged and older adults [18–22]. Greater time spent in moderate-to-vigorous physical activity and higher daily step counts were associated with a lower prevalence of frailty among community-dwelling older adults in Japan [23]. Less active older adults showed frailty indicators related to lower limb strength and a higher incidence of falls, both significantly associated with their PA levels [24]. Evidence from the English Longitudinal Study of Aging highlights that higher intensities of PA are crucial for significantly reducing frailty trajectories among older adults; Light Physical Activity(LPA) was found to be insufficient in slowing down the progression of frailty, while moderate and vigorous activities showed more promising results [25]. However, some studies have reported that PA did not significantly reduce frailty risk over 2 years in older adults; it improved chair rise ability but did not affect major mobility disability differently based on frailty status [26]. No relevant studies have been conducted on the relationship between PA and frailty in a large cohort in China.

Restricted cubic spline (RCS) functions serve as robust tools for elucidating the dose-response relationship between continuous exposure and outcomes [27]. The use of RCS methodology to assess dose-response associations has been extensively documented in the literature [28]. Therefore, RCS and logistic regression were used to examine the dose-response relationship between the FI and PA in a large, nationally representative sample of older adults, offering a valuable reference for frailty prevention.

Methods

Data source

The CHARLS is a comprehensive, interdisciplinary research initiative led by the National School of Development at Peking University and executed by the Chinese Social Science Survey Center. This project provides high-quality data on households and individuals aged 45 and above in China, aiming to establish a nationally representative database of Chinese residents [2, 29, 30]. Further details are available in the cohort profile [29].

This study utilized data from the year 2018. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) participants aged 60 years or older, (2) availability of complete data from the physical activity level questionnaire, and (3) the ability to calculate the frailty index based on the available data. The exclusion criteria included individuals who did not meet the age requirement or those with missing data on physical activity or the frailty index. Ultimately, 10,240 individuals were included in the analysis for the year 2018.

Assessments

Frailty Index(FI)

Frailty was assessed using the FI, calculated from the accumulation of age-related health deficits according to established protocols previously documented [31–33]. A total of 32 items were selected from the CHARLS data to construct the FI, covering variables related to comorbidities, physical function, disability, depression, and cognition [33]. With the exception of Item 32, each variable was dichotomized into a binary format, assigning a value of 0 to indicate the absence of a deficit and 1 to indicate its presence. Item 32 was treated as a continuous variable, ranging from 0 to 1, where higher values reflect poorer cognitive function. Due to the low rate of missing data across the 32 items (< 5%), missing values were imputed using the mean values for continuous variables or through random imputation for categorical variables to optimize sample size [34]. For each participant, the 32-item FI was calculated by summing the present Health deficits, dividing by 32, and multiplying by 100. Consequently, the 32-item FI is a continuous variable ranging from 0 to 100, with higher scores indicating greater frailty. Based on previous research, frailty was defined as a 32-item FI score of ≥ 25 [4].

Physical activity(PA)

In the CHARLS cohort, Questions DA051 to DA055 were employed to assess the duration and frequency of weekly physical activities, categorized by intensity as vigorous, moderate, or light [30]. Given that CHARLS provides only broad time intervals for each type of PA, we converted these responses into average time intervals to more accurately quantify the duration of PA. This method enabled a more precise measurement of the time participants spent engaging in different levels of PA [30]. If the reported duration of PA was between 10 and 30 min, it was classified as 20 min of PA. For durations reported as more than 30 min but less than 2 h, it was defined as 75 min of PA. If the duration was between 2 and 4 h, it was categorized as 180 min of PA. For activities lasting more than 4 h, we categorized them as 300 min of physical exercise.To account for the varied impacts of different physical activities on overall health, we applied the guidelines from the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ). This approach enabled a more accurate assessment of physical exertion intensity using metabolic equivalent (MET) units. We then calculated MET scores for each participant based on the time they allocated to various physical activities over the course of the week [30, 35]. The MET multipliers were calculated using the following formula: MET score = 8.0 × time spent on vigorous PA in a week + 4.0 × time spent on moderate PA in a week + 3.3 × time spent on light PA in a week [36].

Covariates

Consistent with prior studies, the research included sociodemographic and Health-related variables as covariates. The sociodemographic covariates included age, grouped into the categories of 60–64, 65–74, and 75 years or older [37], sex, educational attainment, marital status, region of residence (rural or urban), and insurance coverage (hospitalization insurance and endowment insurance).

Health-related covariates included smoking status (yes or no), alcohol consumption (yes or no), and sleep duration (< 7 h/day, 7–8 h/day, ≥ 9 h/day).

Statistical analysis

The baseline characteristics of the participants were summarized using frequencies (n) and percentages (%). To examine the association between physical activity (PA) and the Frailty Index (FI), we employed a logistic regression model to estimate the Odds Ratio (OR) and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (95% CI). The model-building process began by assessing multicollinearity through the variance inflation factor (VIF) for each variable. With all VIF values below 5, multicollinearity was not a concern [37, 38]. A multivariable regression model was then developed using stepwise regression, starting with all listed covariates, with variable selection guided by the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC). During this process, some variables, including age, were excluded based on AIC. However, due to the established importance of age in frailty, as noted in previous studies, it was retained in the final model. To evaluate the impact of different covariates on the relationship between PA levels and the FI, three models were constructed, with covariates added sequentially. Model 1 adjusted for sex and age. Model 2 extended Model 1 by including domicile, region, education, and marital status. Model 3 incorporated all the covariates. RCS were used to examine the dose-response relationship between PA and FI, with the 25th, 50th, and 75th percentiles of exercise serving as fixed nodes. The data were transposed and analyzed using R language (version 4.1.2), and statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05.

Comparison of characteristics between non-frailty and frailty groups

This table provides a detailed comparison of the distribution between non-frail and frail individuals across three key domains: demographic characteristics, social security, and health behaviors, with significant differences confirmed through statistical tests (p < 0.01).

Regarding the age distribution, the non-frail group predominantly consisted of individuals aged 60–64 years, comprising 32.8%), whereas the frail group had a higher proportion of individuals aged 65–74 years, 51.7%). The proportion of patients aged 75 years and above was notably higher in the frail group than in the non-frail group (24.7%). Females were significantly overrepresented in the frail group, accounting for 62.2%, compared to 47.1% in the non-frail group. Educational attainment further highlights disparities: 54.8% of the frail group had an education level below elementary school compared to 50.0% in the non-frail group. Moreover, only 7.1% of the frail group had received high school education or above, compared to 10.5% in the non-frail group. In terms of residence, 64.3% of frail individuals Lived in rural areas, a higher percentage than 59.8% in the non-frail group. Conversely, 40.2% of the non-frail group resided in urban areas compared to 35.7% in the frail group. Regional distribution also revealed that a greater proportion of frail individuals live in Western China (35.6%) than in the non-frail group (32.1%).

In terms of social security, significant differences were observed in pension insurance coverage. A higher proportion of frail individuals (93.1%) had pension insurance than did the non-frail group (90.5%). Regarding hospitalization insurance, both groups showed almost identical coverage, with 97.5% in the frail group and 97.6% in the non-frail group, indicating no significant differences.

An analysis of health behaviors revealed that the frail group had a lower proportion of current smokers (19.2%) than did the non-frail group (28.6%). Similarly, the proportion of current drinkers was lower in the frail group (23.0%) than that in the non-frail group (34.1%). PA levels also differed, with 25.8% of the frail group engaging in Light PA, which was significantly higher than the 17.0% of the non-frail group. In contrast, a smaller proportion of the frail group (45.1%) engaged in vigorous PA than did the non-frail group (56.6%). Finally, sleep duration analysis showed that 78.8% of frail individuals slept less than 7 h per day, a higher percentage than 68.7% in the non-frail group.

In terms of geographic regions, individuals from Eastern China showed a significantly lower risk than those from Northeast China (OR: 0.67; 95% CI: 0.55–0.81), while those from Central (OR: 0.83; 95% CI: 0.67–1.03) and Western China (OR: 0.86; 95% CI: 0.71–1.04) did not show statistically significant differences. Urban residency was associated with a reduced risk compared to rural residency (OR: 0.83; 95% CI: 0.75–0.91). Education level was also a significant factor; middle school (OR: 0.79; 95% CI: 0.69–0.91) and high school or above (OR: 0.62; 95% CI: 0.51–0.74) were associated with lower risks than elementary school or below.

Regarding marital status, being married and cohabiting (OR: 0.79; 95% CI: 0.71–0.89) or being married but separated (OR: 0.56; 95% CI: 0.42–0.75) showed significant protective effects compared to being widowed.

Smoking and drinking were both associated with lower risks, with ORs of 0.59 (95% CI: 0.53–0.67) and 0.58 (95% CI: 0.52–0.65), respectively. Endowment insurance was associated with a higher risk (OR: 1.41; 95% CI: 1.18–1.70), while hospitalization insurance showed no significant effect (OR: 0.98; 95% CI: 0.72–1.32). Sleep duration of 7–8 h/day and ≥ 9 h/day were associated with lower risks, with ORs of 0.47 (95% CI: 0.42–0.52) and 0.61 (95% CI: 0.52–0.72), respectively.

In summary, higher PA levels, moderate sleep duration, urban residency, higher education, stable marital status, and non-smoking/non-drinking behaviors were significantly associated with lower risk. Conversely, advanced age and social security insurance are associated with higher risks.

Model 1, which adjusts for age, sex, domicile, region, education level, and marital status, shows consistent results. The OR for Q2, Q3, and Q4 were 0.80 (95% CI: 0.69–0.94, p = 0.0051), 0.66 (95% CI: 0.58–0.75, p < 0.0001), and 0.56 (95% CI: 0.49–0.64, p < 0.0001), respectively. These findings indicate that, even after adjusting for these demographic and socioeconomic factors, higher PA remains strongly associated with a lower risk of frailty.

Model 2 was further adjusted for additional factors, including endowment insurance, hospitalization insurance, smoking status, drinking status, and sleep duration. The protective effect of higher PA remains robust, with OR of 0.82 (95% CI: 0.70–0.96, p = 0.0141) for Q2, 0.67 (95% CI: 0.59–0.76, p < 0.0001) for Q3, and 0.57 (95% CI: 0.50–0.66, p < 0.0001) for Q4. This finding suggests that the inverse relationship between PA and frailty is independent of these additional health and lifestyle factors.

In summary, after controlling for various covariates, PA remained an independent factor associated with frailty prevalence. Using the RCS model, the predicted frailty probability was shown across different levels of PA, along with 95% confidence intervals. Initially, at low PA levels, the frailty prevalence remained high, indicating a strong association between insufficient PA and a higher risk of frailty. As PA increased, the probability of frailty began to decline significantly, suggesting that moderate-intensity PA plays a crucial role in reducing frailty risk. However, at higher PA levels, the probability of frailty stabilized, indicating that further increases in PA yield diminishing returns in terms of reducing frailty prevalence. The shape of the curve highlights the significant nonlinear relationship between PA and frailty, with a nonlinear p-value < 0.001 and an overall model p-value < 0.001, both demonstrating strong statistical significance. These findings suggest that while increased PA is negatively associated with frailty risk, this effect plateaus at moderate-to-high activity levels. Discussion

This study advances the current understanding of frailty by providing robust evidence for the independent and nonlinear relationship between PA and frailty prevalence in older adults. Consistent with prior research [39], our findings confirm that higher levels of PA are significantly associated with a reduced risk of frailty. Through the application of the Restricted Cubic Spline (RCS) model, critical thresholds for PA levels were identified. The results indicate that frailty risk remains elevated at lower PA levels and begins to decline significantly around a critical threshold. However, beyond a higher threshold, this decline stabilizes, suggesting diminishing marginal benefits with further increases in PA. These findings highlight the importance of achieving and maintaining moderate-intensity PA as an effective strategy to mitigate frailty risk.

Our results align with existing Literature on the protective effects of PA against frailty. For instance, a systematic review and meta-analysis reported that higher PA levels reduce frailty risk by up to 41%, regardless of the assessment tools used [18]. Similarly, studies involving community-dwelling older adults emphasized the association between physical frailty and locomotive PA of moderate to vigorous intensity, underscoring the importance of step counts and mobility in preventing frailty [39]. A 2024 study explored how an active lifestyle could offset the negative impacts of poor lifestyle behaviors on frailty, demonstrating that PA was associated with lower frailty indices across different age groups, even in individuals with unhealthy lifestyle behaviors [40]. Integrating these findings, our study refines the understanding of PA’s role by identifying specific thresholds and highlighting the nonlinear nature of its impact on frailty risk. These insights offer actionable guidance for designing interventions aimed at promoting adequate PA levels among older adults.

In addition to the impact of PA, this study emphasizes the significant influence of sociodemographic factors on frailty risk and PA engagement among older adults. Age and gender disparities were evident, with advanced age increasing frailty vulnerability and males exhibiting lower risks, suggesting the necessity of age- and gender-specific interventions. Urban residency and higher educational attainment were identified as protective factors, likely due to better access to PA resources and higher health literacy, whereas rural and less-educated populations faced greater barriers [41]. Economic security revealed mixed effects; for instance, endowment insurance was linked to higher frailty risk, possibly reflecting financial and health resource constraints among retirees [42]. Social contexts also played a pivotal role: marriage appeared to provide protective benefits, likely due to spousal support, while widowed individuals showed heightened risks, emphasizing the need for community-based social interventions [43, 44]. Overall, these findings underscore the importance of developing tailored strategies to address sociodemographic disparities. Efforts should focus on improving PA access in rural and underserved regions, enhancing health education for less-educated populations [45], and fostering social support systems for vulnerable groups [46]. Such targeted approaches are essential for reducing frailty prevalence and promoting healthy aging.

Frailty is a dynamic, age-associated condition marked by the deterioration of various physical and physiological functions, such as strength and endurance, which increases vulnerability to stressors [3, 4]. The protective effects of PA against frailty are mediated through several interconnected physiological mechanisms. Regular PA enhances muscle mass and strength, which are vital in preventing sarcopenia, a significant contributor to frailty [47]. Studies highlight that regular exercise can counteract the effects of muscle atrophy by stimulating protein synthesis and reducing protein breakdown For instance, Cohen [48] emphasize that exercise is the only widely accepted strategy to prevent or slow down muscle wasting. Exercise plays a crucial role in enhancing cardiovascular health, which in turn has significant implications for delaying the onset of frailty, particularly in older adults. Regular PA enhances cardiovascular health by strengthening the heart, improving circulation, and ensuring better oxygen delivery to tissues. This process supports cellular function and metabolic efficiency while aiding in the removal of metabolic waste, which reduces oxidative stress. As oxidative stress is a significant contributor to aging and frailty, exercise plays a critical role in preserving muscle strength, mobility, and overall physical function, thereby delaying frailty in older adults [49, 50]. Additionally, PA helps regulate inflammatory responses by reducing pro-inflammatory cytokines and enhancing anti-inflammatory markers [51, 52], which are essential in preventing chronic inflammation—a known driver of frailty. Moreover, PA supports neuroplasticity and cognitive function, protecting against cognitive decline [53–55], which is often associated with frailty in older adults. These combined physiological effects underscore why maintaining regular PA is essential in mitigating the risk of developing frailty and slowing its progression.

This study reveals the dynamic relationship between PA and frailty risk through the FI model, which integrates the cumulative effects of multi-dimensional health deficits and demonstrates unique advantages in predicting long-term aging risks. Notably, the Fried Frailty Phenotype (FP) [56], as a physiological function-oriented assessment tool, offers operational indicators (such as grip strength decline and reduced walking speed) that complement the methodology of FI. While FI excels at quantifying the continuous process of systemic decline, FP is proficient at capturing threshold-based deterioration of key physiological functions. This complementarity provides the theoretical basis for the establishment of a multidimensional vulnerability assessment system, which, as Collard [57] have pointed out, can more comprehensively identify states of weakness and thus provide a basis for early intervention. However, due to data gaps in the CHARLS database regarding core FP parameters (such as standardized walking speed measurements and longitudinal tracking of involuntary weight loss), this study was unable to validate both frameworks in tandem. Current aging database designs predominantly rely on single assessment frameworks, lacking longitudinal tracking of key physiological indicators for functional performance [58, 59]. This structural mismatch between data and theoretical foundations may result in systematic omissions of vulnerable populations such as those with pre-existing frailty, thereby constraining the development of precision prevention and intervention strategies [60]. Particularly, the continuous risk assessment based on FI is relatively insensitive to early frailty signals (i.e., pre-frailty states), which means that potential intervention populations meeting 1–2 FP criteria were not fully captured in this study. Although cohort study evidence suggests that this subgroup may exhibit hypersensitivity to PA interventions, its dose-effect relationship and underlying mechanisms remain to be systematically analyzed. To address these limitations, future research should focus on constructing multi-center aging databases compatible with both FI and FP indices, to address the current data-methodological asymmetry. Particular attention should be directed toward elucidating the dose-response relationship of PA within the pre-frail demographic — a group exhibiting elevated sensitivity to behavioral interventions, yet often underrepresented in intervention trials [61]. The establishment of a cross-framework protective threshold verification system, linking FP and FI metrics, is urgently needed to enhance methodological consistency and cross-cohort comparability [59]. Furthermore, there is strong evidence that risk stratified physical activity guidelines should be developed for individuals with varying levels of frailty. This approach is supported by recent meta-analyses and observational data demonstrating its clinical utility and prevention potential [62, 63]. Ultimately, these efforts could advance the paradigm shift from population-wide prevention to precision-targeted intervention in geriatric care.

These findings carry significant practical implications for policymakers, who can prioritize the promotion of appropriate physical activities in public health policies. Specifically, the government can increase investment in and maintenance of community fitness facilities to ensure that older adults have easy access to engage in moderate or higher-intensity exercises [64], such as walking and fitness training. Additionally, the government can raise awareness about the benefits of PA among the elderly through various media channels and community activities. Policymakers might also consider designing and funding exercise intervention programs specifically aimed at older adults to reduce the risk of frailty nationwide [65, 66]. For healthcare providers, the research suggests the importance of developing personalized exercise prescriptions for elderly patients in clinical practice [67, 68]. These prescriptions should be based on regular assessments of the health and physical fitness of the elderly and could involve collaboration with physical therapists and nutritionists to provide comprehensive health management services. Furthermore, the findings underscore the significance of preventive healthcare measures, indicating that healthcare providers can use this information to promote PA among the elderly, effectively preventing the onset of frailty. Through health education, early intervention, and personalized health guidance, healthcare providers can help older adults maintain an active lifestyle and prevent further decline in physical function.

The study does have certain limitations. Firstly, all the samples were sourced from the CHARLS database, indicating that the study relied on a single data source, which somewhat restricts its scope. Secondly, much of the data was obtained through participant questionnaires and self-reports, rather than more precise laboratory or clinical trials. For example, MET values were estimated based on self-reported PA data. Although self-reported data are widely used in epidemiological research, they may be subject to reporting bias and may differ from MET values obtained through objective measurement methods, such as accelerometers or direct metabolic assessments. Therefore, self-reported MET values may either overestimate or underestimate an individual’s actual level of physical activity. Third, the generalizability of the research findings is constrained by the sample composition. Prior research has demonstrated that social determinants of health and frailty clustering patterns may vary significantly across ethnic groups [59]. Recent studies have further highlighted the limitations of single-ethnic cohort data—such as CHARLS—in extrapolating findings to multi-ethnic populations [69]. Similarly, Lu et al. (2025) caution that regional and demographic biases may confound associations derived from CHARLS-based health studies [70], underscoring the importance of cross-group validation in future research. In addition, the presence of missing data, even when statistically imputed, may still introduce bias and compromise the robustness of the conclusions. The current sample also lacks granular ethnic subgroup classifications within the Chinese population, consisting primarily of individuals of Chinese nationality and Asian ethnicity, but without further disaggregation. These methodological limitations reveal several critical areas for refinement in future research. To improve the objectivity and external validity of future findings, researchers are encouraged to validate results using independent datasets and incorporate greater ethnic diversity. This approach is particularly crucial for understanding how intra-national racial or ethnic heterogeneity may shape aging-related outcomes. Indeed, evidence suggests that residual racial disparities persist in many populations and are not fully explained by known socioeconomic or structural factors [71]. Finally, longitudinal study designs are essential to establish causal relationships between PA and frailty, as well as to elucidate the differential effects of specific types of PA on frailty progression.

Conclusions

Higher levels of PA are significantly negatively correlated with the occurrence of frailty, increasing PA can substantially lower the risk of frailty in older adults.However, further increase in activity beyond a certain point may not yield additional benefits.
