Construction and verification of a nomogram to estimate middle-aged and older individuals’ susceptibility to sarcopenia
Authors: Xiaozheng Yang, Hui Wang, Yuehua Wang, Zijun Cai, Longteng Liao, Qian Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSarcopenia raises the risk of falls, fractures, and death, in addition to having a direct impact on the form and function of the skeletal muscles. Thus, our research aimed to create and evaluate a nomogram indicating the probability that middle-aged and older adults may acquire sarcopenia.MethodsIn this study, data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey covering 2011 to 2018 were split randomly into training and validation datasets. We then used univariate and multivariate logistic regression analysis to create a nomogram prediction model. The model’s accuracy was measured using calibration curves, and its clinical applicability and discriminatory power were evaluated using receiver operating characteristic curves and decision curve analysis.ResultsThis study encompassed 39,175 participants, with 5,342 participants in the training dataset and 2,290 participants in the validation dataset. Age, race, education, poverty income ratio, energy, protein, carbohydrate, albumin, blood uric acid, serum iron, diabetes, depression, arthritis, and leisure-time physical activity were independent risk variables for sarcopenia by multivariate logistic regression analysis. A nomogram risk prediction model was then built, and in the training and validation datasets, the model’s operating characteristic curves values were 0.720 (95% CI 0.701–0.739) and 0.705 (95% CI 0.685–0.725), respectively. The calibration curves showed high agreement between the model’s predictions and the actual results. Decision curve analysis showed that the nomogram model was beneficial across a range of probability thresholds.ConclusionThe nomogram demonstrated high clinical predictability, enabling clinicians to utilize this tool to identify the incidence of sarcopenia in middle-aged and elderly individuals.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12891-025-09203-9.

Introduction

Sarcopenia, termed by Rosenberg in 1989, describes the age-related loss of lean body mass that impairs nutrition and mobility [1, 2]. Sarcopenia was defined As a clinical syndrome in 2010 by the European Working Group on Sarcopenia in Older People (EWGSOP). This syndrome is characterized by a progressive and widespread loss of muscle mass and strength, which leads to physical disability, a lower quality of life, and an increased risk of unfavorable outcomes, such as death [3]. There is no universally accepted diagnostic criteria for sarcopenia [4, 5]. The prevalence of sarcopenia rises with age, As the decline in muscle function generally commences around age 35, decreasing from 1% to 2% annually. Following age 50, this decline rate accelerates, with further acceleration observed after reaching 60 [6–8]. Currently, it is estimated that approximately 50 million people worldwide are affected by sarcopenia, with projections suggesting that by 2050, this number will escalate to 500 million. The incidence of sarcopenia is notably higher in European and American populations compared to Asian populations. Hence, the elevated incidence of sarcopenia and the gravity of its associated complications will profoundly impact patients’ quality of life and impose a substantial burden on socio-economic development [9–11].

The pathogenesis of sarcopenia remains incompletely understood, with numerous factors closely associated with its onset and progression. Previous studies have indicated that age, lack of physical activity, hormonal imbalances, chronic inflammation, metabolic disturbances, genetic inheritance, and neuromuscular dysfunction are major risk factors for muscle loss [12, 13]. Furthermore, several studies have shown a connection between decreased skeletal muscle mass and several physical and mental health issues, including cancer, type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular disease, and respiratory disorders [14–16]. Assessments of muscle mass, muscle strength, and physical performance are frequently employed to identify and diagnose sarcopenia. These complex methods require specialized personnel, equipment, and significant time investment. Furthermore, sarcopenia is very difficult to assess because its prevalence in the older population varies greatly, from 5% to 50%, and there is no standard therapy or evaluation technique [17]. There is an urgent demand for enhanced diagnostics, prevention strategies, and personalized healthcare interventions, and the emergence of Nomogram prediction models offers promise in addressing these needs.

Nomograms are graphical tools employed in predictive modeling to estimate the probability of a particular disease based on multiple variables. Unlike traditional statistical models that rely on complex equations, nomograms are user-friendly, facilitating quick interpretation of results without requiring extensive calculations. These graphical aids comprise a series of scales representing various variables incorporated in the prediction model. Each variable is allocated a value along its respective scale, and the probability of the outcome of interest is determined by drawing a line connecting these values. Nomograms can handle both continuous and categorical variables. They visually depict the associations between predictors and specific diseases, enabling clinicians to make informed decisions swiftly and intuitively [18, 19]. Therefore, the purpose of this work was to create a nomogram prediction model to assess middle-aged and older adult’s risk of developing sarcopenia, to screen and identify high-risk individuals beforehand, and to offer a trustworthy source for early therapeutic intervention.

Methods

Data accession and study population

The National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), which may be accessed at (https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/index.htm), provided the data used in this investigation. The NHANES is a continuous survey that is carried out using a multi-stage, cross-sectional, stratified probability sampling technique. It has been conducted every two years since 1999 to compile a representative sample of the American populace. The data collected includes participant demographics, dietary habits, examination results, laboratory data, and questionnaire answers.

The National Center for Health Statistics Institutional Review Board has approved all NHANES protocols, and each participant has provided written informed permission. Comprehensive explanations of the survey methodology have been released [20, 21]. As this study involves secondary analysis of clinical data, a clinical trial number is not applicable. The corresponding author may provide statistical codes and working datasets upon reasonable request.

Definition of sarcopenia

NHANES provides a dependable set of dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DEXA) evaluations of muscle mass data. Hologic Discovery model A densitometers (Hologic, Bedford, Massachusetts, USA) were used to perform DEXA measurements. The appendicular skeletal muscle mass (ASM) was first calculated by adding the four limbs’ total mass and subtracting their combined fat mass [22]. We then calculated the appendicular skeletal muscle mass index (ASMI) by dividing the ASM (kg) by the body mass index (BMI, kg/m2). The National Institutes of Health (NIH) defined indicative values of sarcopenia As those with an ASMI of less than 0.789 for men and less than 0.512 for women [23].

Definition of covariates

Using the standardized questionnaire used in NHANES, we collected information on age, gender, race, education, poverty-to-income ratio (PIR), nutrition, blood biochemistry tests, smoking, alcohol, primary medical condition, sedentary time, and leisure-time physical activity (LTPA). Mexican Americans, non-Hispanic Whites, non-Hispanic Blacks, and other races were included in the racial categories. The categories for educational achievement were less than ninth grade, ninth through eleventh grade, high school, college, and above. There were three categories for marital status: never married, widowed, divorced, and cohabiting. PIR fell into < 1.3, 1.3–3.5, and > 3.5. The study population was divided into three categories: never smokers (those who had never smoked more than 100 cigarettes in their lifetime), current smokers (those who had smoked more than 100 cigarettes in their lifetime and did so during the study period), and former smokers (those who had smoked more than 100 cigarettes in their lifetime and had given up smoking during the study period). People who had drunk 12 or more alcoholic beverages in their lifetime were considered drinkers. Based on interviews conducted with respondents using the Global Physical Activity Questionnaire, self-reported daily sedentary time and LTPA were calculated [24, 25]. The question participants were given was, “On a typical day, how much time do you usually spend sitting at school, at home, traveling to and from places, or with friends, including time spent sitting at a desk, traveling by car or bus, reading, playing cards, watching TV, or using a computer (excluding time spent sleeping)?” The daily hours were then calculated from these responses [26, 27]. The total amount of time spent on LTPA was determined by adding the minutes of moderate-intensity recreational activity to twice the minutes of high-intensity recreational activity [28, 29].

Statistical analysis

A 7:3 random partitioning of the NHANES dataset separated the training and validation cohorts. Categorical variables were shown as the mean; standard error represented percentages and continuous variables. Categorical variables were evaluated using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test for univariate and multivariate analyses, while continuous variables were assessed using the Student’s t-test or the rank-sum test. Missing data were imputed using the k-nearest neighbors (KNN) algorithm. Variables or individuals with a missing rate of ≥ 20% were excluded from the analysis. Predictor selection was guided by univariate analysis, clinical relevance, and literature review; multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIF), and variables with VIF > 5 were excluded.

The data from the validation cohort was then used to validate the created nomogram internally. The area under the curve (AUC) for each model was computed using receiver operating curve (ROC) analysis, which was used to Assess the stability of the prediction models developed from the training and validation cohorts. The receiver operating characteristic curve was used to evaluate nomogram performance. The calibration ability, which shows the agreement between observed and anticipated probability, was determined using calibration plots. The 45-degree line on the calibration figure, representing equal observed and anticipated probability, would represent a wholly calibrated model. Lastly, the best advantage of the prediction model was ascertained by applying decision curve analysis (DCA). All statistical analyses were performed with STATA 17 and R (version 4.3.1).

Results

Essential characteristics of the population

39,175 people in the NHANES database were evaluated for this study between 2011 and 2018. Individuals under the age of 35 (n = 22,257) and those without information on their ASM and BMI (n = 9,286) were not included. As a result, data from 7,632 participants were included in the final analysis (Fig. 1; Table 1). There were 2,290 patients in the validation set and 5,342 patients in the training set. Sarcopenia was diagnosed in 1,076 patients (20.14%) in the training dataset and 437 patients (19.08%) in the validation dataset. Regarding gout, triglycerides, and vitamin C, statistically significant variations were found between the training and validation datasets. On the other hand, no significant statistical differences (P < 0.05) were found between the training and validation sets for any variables included in the analysis, suggesting comparability between the two groups.Fig. 1Flow chart of the study

Univariate and multivariate logistic regression

The results of univariate and multivariate logistic regression are presented in Table 2. A univariate logistic regression model was utilized to analyze various factors among the study population, including age, race, education level, marital status, poverty income ratio, dietary intake, common biochemical indicators, sedentary behavior, physical activity, diabetes, cardiovascular diseases, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, stroke, cancer, arthritis, gout, depression, insomnia, smoking, and alcohol consumption. The findings revealed that, except dietary intake variables such as Vitamin C, Calcium, and Iron, and biochemical indicators such as TC (total cholesterol), ALT (alanine aminotransferase), Scr (serum creatinine), Total protein, 25-(OH)2D (25-hydroxyvitamin D), and cancer, all sarcopenia risk factors were statistically significant (P < 0.05). Further multivariate logistic regression analysis demonstrated that age, race, education level, poverty income ratio, energy intake, protein intake, carbohydrate intake, albumin (ALB) levels, blood uric acid (BUA) levels, serum iron levels, diabetes, depression, arthritis, and LTPA identified As independent risk factors for sarcopenia. The full regression coefficients and intercept of the logistic prediction model developed in this study are provided in Supplementary Table 1, allowing calculation of individual risk probabilities for sarcopenia.

Development and validation of the nomogram

We incorporated the independent risk factors for sarcopenia identified in the multivariate logistic regression into constructing the nomogram prediction model (Fig. 2) and Assessed the discriminative ability of the nomogram model using ROC curves in the training and validation sets. In this study, the training set included 1,076 positive outcome events, and 14 predictors were included in the final model, yielding an events-per-variable (EPV) ratio of approximately 76.9. This substantially exceeds the commonly recommended threshold of EPV ≥ 10, indicating a low risk of model overfitting. The AUC was 0.720 (95% CI 0.701–0.739) for the training dataset (Fig. 3A) and 0.705 (95% CI 0.685–0.725) for the validation dataset (Fig. 3B), indicating strong discriminative power and predictive value of the nomogram model. To assess the model’s optimism and internal stability, we performed 1,000 bootstrap resampling iterations, the optimism-corrected AUC was 0.711, with a shrinkage factor of 0.990, indicating minimal risk of overfitting and demonstrating good robustness and generalizability of the model. To enhance the clinical interpretability of the model, we stratified the predicted probabilities into three risk categories: low risk (< 10%), moderate risk (10–20%), and high risk (> 20%). Discussion

To date, there has been a lack of satisfactory predictive tools for identifying middle-aged and elderly patients at risk of sarcopenia. Therefore, we utilized data from the NHANES database from 2011 to 2018 to construct a nomogram prediction model, which accurately assesses the Likelihood of sarcopenia in this population. The nomogram model comprises 14 predictive factors: age, race, education, PIR, energy intake, protein intake, carbohydrate intake, ALB levels, BUA levels, serum iron levels, diabetes, depression, arthritis, and LTPA. This model demonstrated excellent discriminative ability on the training and validation sets, with AUC values of 0.720 and 0.705, respectively. Calibration curve testing indicated the high accuracy of the model in both datasets. Additionally, DCA demonstrated the model’s clinical applicability in both datasets.

Undoubtedly, age stands as a crucial assessment marker for sarcopenia. As individuals age, their body’s protein synthesis and utilization efficiency decreases, leading to muscle protein depletion due to a higher breakdown rate than synthesis. Concurrently, reductions in neuron count and connectivity within the nervous system and slowed nerve conduction velocity affect neuromuscular control, ultimately resulting in diminished muscle function [30]. Furthermore, declines in hormone levels (including testosterone, estrogen, growth hormone, etc.) and increased inflammation contribute to muscle tissue damage and inflammation, mechanisms that underlie sarcopenia with aging [31, 32]. Moreover, differences in genotype and phenotype exist among different racial groups, which may impact the genetic basis for muscle mass and function. Some studies suggest that certain ethnicities may possess genetic factors favoring muscle development and maintenance, thereby potentially reducing the risk of sarcopenia. For instance, research by Marie et al. [33] found a significantly higher prevalence of sarcopenia among African Americans aged 51–64 compared to Caucasians. Similarly, studies by Bigman et al. [34] have demonstrated variations in sarcopenia incidence by race, with Hispanics exhibiting the highest prevalence, followed by Asian/other races, non-Hispanic blacks, and non-Hispanic whites. This aligns somewhat with our predictive model, which indicates a lower incidence of sarcopenia among Asian/other racial groups, possibly attributed to healthier diets and lower rates of diabetes and hypertension-related complications. Current research suggests using height-adjusted methods to diagnose sarcopenia in Asian populations [35, 36]. Additionally, disparities in socioeconomic status and education level among different racial groups may influence factors such as living conditions, healthcare access, and nutritional status, consequently affecting muscle health and the probability of sarcopenia occurrence [37].Lower education and income levels may lead to higher psychological stress and lifestyle factors such as work pressure, environmental pollution, and unhealthy habits, which could increase the risk of inflammation and chronic diseases, thereby exacerbating the likelihood of sarcopenia occurrence [38].

Furthermore, dietary factors play a critical role in the onset of sarcopenia. Protein is the primary component of muscle tissue, and adequate protein intake is essential for muscle synthesis and maintenance. Protein synthesis occurs through the transcription, translation, and subsequent modification of muscle proteins. Sufficient protein intake can increase muscle protein synthesis rates, thereby aiding in maintaining and auguring muscle mass. Prolonged inadequate protein intake may increase protein degradation, resulting in negative nitrogen balance and accelerating muscle loss [39]. Research indicates that older adults require 1.0–1.3 g/kilogram/day of protein to maintain muscle mass and function, and higher protein intake doses can reduce muscle mass loss by up to 40% [40, 41]. Carbohydrates represent one of the body’s primary sources of energy. Energy encompasses the calories provided by various foods and plays an inseparable role alongside protein, especially during high-intensity and sustained exercise. Sufficient carbohydrate intake can provide adequate power to support muscle movement and activity, thereby maintaining muscle mass and function. Carbohydrate intake can influence protein metabolism, mainly when carbohydrates and protein are consumed together. Moderate carbohydrate intake can help reduce protein breakdown, increase protein synthesis rates, promote insulin secretion, and suppress fat and protein breakdown, thus facilitating muscle synthesis and maintenance [42, 43]. Moreover, evidence suggests the importance of paying attention to the dietary inflammatory index (DII) in food composition. This quantifies food to achieve an anti-inflammatory effect, thereby reducing the incidence of sarcopenia [44]. A cross-sectional study targeting older adults demonstrated a higher probability of sarcopenia among individuals with a more substantial pro-inflammatory effect in their diet (higher DII values) [45].

The prevalence of common underlying conditions in middle-aged and elderly patients is closely associated with sarcopenia. Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) patients have a significantly high risk of developing muscle wasting syndrome (prevalence ≥ 25%). This association between rheumatoid arthritis and sarcopenia may be linked to an increased risk of falls, fractures, and physical disability [46]. Studies indicate that RA muscle bundles exhibit heightened cytokine activity, producing more granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor, IL-1β, IL-10, IL-12, and vascular endothelial growth factor over time. Chronic inflammation-mediated cytokines such as TNF, IL-6, C-reactive protein, and IFNγ can lead to muscle imbalance, exacerbating muscle protein breakdown [47, 48]. Moreover, diabetes accelerates the progression of sarcopenia through various mechanisms, including insulin resistance, advanced glycation end products (AGEs), inflammatory responses, nutritional status, and exercise capacity. Insulin resistance, a hallmark of type 2 diabetes, reduces cellular responsiveness to insulin, leading to elevated blood sugar levels. Insulin resistance and high blood sugar levels can impair mitochondrial function, oxidative metabolism, and energy utilization, thereby reducing muscle protein synthesis [49–51]. Elevated blood glucose levels contribute to the accumulation of AGEs, which can adversely affect the structure and function of muscle tissue. Furthermore, limitations in exercise capacity due to the effects of diabetes or its complications, such as neuropathy or arteriosclerosis, can also lead to a decrease in muscle mass. Therefore, diabetes has significant predictive value for sarcopenia [52]. Patients with depression often exhibit dysregulation of the neuroendocrine system, including abnormal secretion of adrenal cortical hormones, adrenaline, and noradrenaline, as well as reductions in neuron count and connectivity. Additionally, depression patients frequently experience increased chronic inflammatory responses, manifested by elevated release of inflammatory mediators, which can directly or indirectly affect muscle tissue metabolism and function [53, 54].

We also found that specific blood biochemical indicators have diagnostic value for sarcopenia. Iron, an essential micronutrient, is a crucial oxidant that maintains redox balance, reduces oxidative stress, and participates in the repair and regeneration processes following muscle injury. Adequate serum iron levels can promote healing after muscle injury, contributing to maintaining muscle mass and function [55, 56]. Additionally, as a critical component of the mitochondrial electron transport chain, iron is involved in adenosine triphosphate (ATP) production and cellular energy metabolism. Insufficient iron levels may lead to mitochondrial dysfunction and disrupted energy metabolism, affecting protein synthesis and the energy supply required for normal muscle strength [57]. Aside from mechanisms mentioned earlier, elevated blood uric acid levels, such as promoting inflammation, oxidative stress, inflammatory mediator release, and insulin resistance [58], can also accelerate the onset and progression of sarcopenia by disrupting vascular function. High uric acid levels can disturb vascular tone regulation, reducing blood and nutrient supply to muscle tissues and affecting muscle health and function. Thus, controlling blood uric acid levels is crucial in preventing and managing sarcopenia [59]. Lastly, hypoalbuminemia is closely related to malabsorption because albumin plays a significant role in the transport and delivery of nutrients within the body. Insufficient albumin levels directly impact the normal absorption and utilization of nutrients (such as proteins, vitamins, etc.). Hypoalbuminemia is also associated with chronic inflammation since inflammatory responses can decrease albumin synthesis and release. Prolonged chronic inflammation may increase muscle protein breakdown, accelerating the development of sarcopenia [60, 61]. Therefore, controlling serum iron, blood urea nitrogen, and albumin levels is essential in reducing the risk of sarcopenia during its prevention and management.

It is widely recognized that exercise can effectively reduce the incidence of sarcopenia. Skeletal muscle aerobic exercise can induce the production of ATP in mitochondria, enhancing aerobic capacity, metabolic control, and cardiovascular function. It also helps activate mitochondrial biogenesis and dynamics, restore mitochondrial metabolism, reduce the expression of catabolic metabolism genes, and increase muscle protein synthesis [62, 63]. Research by Beckwée et al. [64] demonstrated that high-intensity resistance training programs are the optimal exercise regimen for patients with muscle wasting, while low-intensity resistance training may suffice to induce strength gains. High-intensity interval Training also provides physiological benefits by activating peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor-γ coactivator 1 α, increasing the body’s antioxidant capacity, glucose absorption ability, etc., thereby reducing the incidence of sarcopenia [65]. Therefore, the nomogram model established based on these 14 variables in this study allows clinicians to calculate the risk of event occurrence based on the total score of the nomogram. Moreover, this study has a relatively large sample size and has undergone external validation, which is of guiding significance for identifying high-risk elderly patients with sarcopenia.

Limitation

Our study also has several limitations. Firstly, our model did not include several potentially important variables associated with sarcopenia risk, such as inflammatory biomarkers (e.g., C-reactive protein, interleukin-6), hormonal levels, specific medication use (e.g., corticosteroids), and functional measures like grip strength and gait speed. These factors, although well-documented in the literature, were unavailable in the NHANES dataset we used. Their absence may lead to residual confounding or underestimation of model performance. Future studies incorporating these variables could help improve prediction accuracy and provide a more comprehensive risk assessment. Secondly, in handling missing data, we excluded variables and individuals with ≥ 20% missingness. This approach may have introduced a certain degree of selection bias, as the missingness in NHANES is not necessarily completely random. Such exclusion could affect the representativeness of the sample and thereby Limit the generalizability of the findings. Third, the AUC of our prediction model ranged from 0.705 to 0.720, indicating moderate discriminative ability. We acknowledge that sarcopenia is a complex syndrome resulting from multiple interacting factors, and developing a high-performance predictive model based on cross-sectional data such as NHANES presents inherent challenges. To enhance the generalizability of the model, we deliberately included only variables that are routinely and easily accessible in clinical practice, which may have limited the model’s predictive accuracy to some extent. Additionally, while we conducted preliminary internal validation to evaluate model performance, this does not constitute rigorous external validation. The lack of validation on an independent dataset may lead to an overestimation of model performance due to the inherent optimism of internal validation. Future studies are needed to externally validate the model in independent populations or prospective cohorts to further assess its generalizability.

Conclusion

In summary, we have developed a predictive model using data from the NHANES database. This model, which includes 14 demographic, dietary, biochemical, and relevant comorbidity factors, demonstrates satisfactory predictive performance in assessing the risk of sarcopenia in middle-aged and elderly individuals. It can effectively assist clinicians in making informed clinical decisions and facilitate early clinical interventions for patients, thereby reducing the incidence of sarcopenia and improving the quality of life for elderly individuals.
