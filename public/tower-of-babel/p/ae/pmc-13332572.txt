Psychosocial Determinants of Pain in Muscle Biopsy
Authors: Beatrice Labella, Emmanuelle Lacene, Maud Beuvin, Anaïs Chanut, Angeline Madelaine, Favienne Levy‐Borsato, Guy Brochier, Clémence Labasse, Guillaume Bassez, Danielle Seilhean, Sarah Leonard‐Louis, Teresinha Evangelista
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroduction/AimsOpen muscle biopsy (OMB) is a key diagnostic tool for neuromuscular diseases, though patients often worry about risks and discomfort. This study aimed to assess pain during and after OMB, factors influencing pain perception, and adverse events.MethodsIn this single‐center prospective study at the Neuropathology Unit of the Pitié‐Salpêtrière Hospital, Paris, 100 patients (68 women; mean age 55.4 years) aged ≥ 18 years with suspected myopathy were enrolled from August 2024 to June 2025. Exclusion criteria were inability to understand French, pregnancy, and no social security coverage. Patients completed the numerical rating scale (NRS) for pain assessment and the Patient Health Questionnaire‐9. Descriptive statistics summarized patient characteristics. Univariate and multivariate linear regression analyses identified factors associated with procedural pain. Follow‐up phone calls were performed at 15 and 30 days postbiopsy.ResultsProcedural pain was mild (median NRS = 2) with moderate and weak positive correlations with preprocedural anxiety (r = 0.54, p < 0.01) and Clinical Frailty Scale scores (r = 0.32, p = 0.02), respectively. Weak negative correlations were observed with satisfaction with staff communication (r = −0.29, p = 0.02) and comfort with medical staff (r = −0.27, p = 0.05). Postprocedural discomfort occurred in 42/83 patients, mostly short‐lived. No major complications occurred.DiscussionOMB is a safe procedure. Pain intensity was lower than that generally reported in the literature. Frailty may influence acute pain perception. Preprocedural anxiety emerged as the strongest predictor of pain intensity. Therefore, preprocedural anxiety should be routinely addressed in patients undergoing OMB.

Introduction

Although several biopsy techniques are available, open muscle biopsy (OMB) enables the collection of larger tissue samples [1, 2, 3, 4]. However, patients frequently express concerns about the surgical risks and potential discomfort. Few studies have explored pain perception during muscle biopsy, mostly focusing on differences between procedural techniques. A small study of 33 patients undergoing consecutive needle biopsy (NB) and OMB reported higher pain with NB than OMB using the numerical rating scale (NRS) [5] and similar procedural pain levels with NB (NRS = 5) were observed in another retrospective study [6]. Despite this, NB remains a viable alternative, particularly due to its esthetic advantages, such as smaller scarring, and its lower likelihood of postprocedural complications [5]. One study has assessed the Weil‐Blakesley conchotome technique, reporting extremely low pain scores but assessed by a visual analog scale (VAS) [7]. A study addressing the existing knowledge gap regarding patients' perspectives on muscle biopsy was conducted in 2024 [8], but the heterogeneous biopsy methods employed (OMB, NB, conchotome, or unspecified) and the absence of standardized numeric pain assessments limited the comparability of the findings.

Understanding patients' perspectives on OMB is clinically relevant as anticipation of pain and complications is a frequent source of anxiety and may influence acceptance of the procedure. Systematic evaluations of patient experience are lacking with respect to the role of psychosocial factors such as anxiety, communication, and satisfaction with care. Therefore, the aim of this study was to characterize pain during and after OMB, assess the prognostic factors that may influence patients' pain perceptions, and evaluate the frequency of adverse events.

Methods

This was a prospective, single‐center observational study conducted at the Neuropathology Laboratory of the Pitié‐Salpêtrière Hospital, Paris, and approved by the local ethics committee (Code project APHP240433/No IDRCB: 2024‐A00823‐44). The inclusion period lasted 10 months, with a total study duration of 11 months to follow‐up of the last enrolled patient (August 2024–June 2025). One hundred consecutive adult patients who underwent OMB in the outpatient setting of the muscle biopsy unit were enrolled. The inclusion criteria were age ≥ 18 years, clinical suspicion of myopathy and consent to participate in the study. Exclusion criteria included inability to understand French, pregnancy, lack of social security coverage, deprivation of liberty by judicial or administrative decision, or being under legal protection measures in accordance with French biomedical research regulations. Clinical data and frailty assessment by Clinical Frailty Scale (CFS), a validated 9‐point scale that categorizes individuals based on their level of vulnerability from 1 (“Very Fit”) to 9 (“Terminally Ill”), were collected prior to the intervention [9]. A detailed standardized explanation of the procedure was given to each patient before the OMB was performed.

OMB was performed by three neurologists specialized in neuromuscular disorders. During the procedure (Supporting Information S1), patients were engaged through the use of distraction techniques, including light conversation and background music.

Following the OMB, patients completed a structured questionnaire divided into seven sections, including the NRS for anxiety and pain assessment. In addition, the Patient Health Questionnaire‐9 (PHQ‐9) was administered for depression screening [10]. All questionnaires were self‐administered without assistance from the staff involved in the biopsy. Follow‐up interviews were conducted by phone at 15 and 30 days postintervention using a standardized questionnaire. Demographic and clinical variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median with quartiles or frequencies for continuous and categorical variables.

Pain perception during OMB was assessed using the variable Procedural Pain, rated on a numeric scale (0–10), as the primary dependent variable. The following variables were included as potential predictors based on clinical relevance: age, sex, diabetes status, malignant disease, current treatment for chronic pain, PHQ‐9 score, CFS, muscle biopsied, and questionnaire‐derived measures reflecting patient experience (e.g., preprocedural anxiety, comfort with medical staff during muscle biopsy, satisfaction with professionalism and kindness of medical staff, satisfaction with the information provided before the procedure, and staff response to concerns).

Statistical Analysis

To explore the relationship between each independent variable and procedural pain, a univariate analysis was performed. For continuous variables, Pearson's correlation coefficient and simple linear regression were applied; for categorical variables, one‐way ANOVA was used. Variables with a univariate p < 0.05 were retained for the multivariate linear regression model, where the variable Procedural Pain was entered as the dependent variable. Correlation strength was interpreted according to Schober et al. [11], with coefficients classified as negligible (0.00–0.09), weak (0.10–0.39), moderate (0.40–0.69), strong (0.70–0.89), and very strong (0.90–1.00). Model assumptions (normality and homoscedasticity of residuals) were verified. The final model was evaluated using the coefficient of determination (R 2), adjusted R 2, and Akaike/Bayesian information criteria (AIC/BIC) to assess model fit. A two‐tailed p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

The mean NRS pain score was 2 (IQR range: 1–4, Figure 1), with the most painful phase identified as the muscle sampling (40%), followed by the initial administration of anesthesia (25%). The NRS pain score during OMB was not influenced by demographic factors (sex, age, BMI), site of biopsy or the use of chronic pain treatment. In univariate analysis, preprocedural anxiety (r = 0.54, p < 0.01, Figure 1) and the CFS (r = 0.32, p = 0.02) showed moderate and weak positive correlations with procedural pain, respectively. In contrast, satisfaction with communication provided by the medical and paramedical staff (r = −0.29, p = 0.024) and subjective comfort with medical staff during the biopsy (r = −0.27, p = 0.031) were weakly and negatively associated with pain intensity. These variables remained significant in the multivariate linear regression model (Table 4), explaining 26% of the variance in procedural pain (R 2 = 0.26; adjusted R 2 = 0.21; p < 0.001).

PHQ‐9 scores were weakly and positively correlated with preprocedural anxiety (r = 0.26, p = 0.014), as confirmed by linear regression analysis; however, no significant association was observed between PHQ‐9 scores and procedural pain.

Follow‐up data were available for 83 patients at 15 days and for 65 patients at 30 days (Table 5). At 15 days, postbiopsy pain was reported by 42 patients (50.6%), usually lasting less than 3 days and well managed by acetaminophen therapy. Only three patients reported pain persisting beyond 1 week, including the only patient who required analgesic treatment throughout the entire 15‐day period. This patient was also one of the two individuals who reported experiencing very mild, persistent pain at the 30‐day follow‐up.

There were no significant differences in procedural or follow‐up pain scores based upon final histopathological diagnosis.

Only two patients noted esthetic complications. In one, the complication was linked to the suture technique, as the patient had a known allergy to surgical materials and, due to intraoperative limitations, fascial closure was performed by advancing cutaneous sutures to a deeper plane. In the other, the complication appeared to be related to a pre‐existing dermatological condition, which may have contributed to a localized inflammatory response and delayed wound healing. To assess potential attrition bias, a sensitivity analysis was conducted comparing baseline demographic, clinical, psychosocial variables, and biopsy site between participants who completed follow‐up and those lost to follow‐up. No significant differences were observed at 15 and 30 days.

Discussion

Pain during OMB was generally mild, and postprocedural discomfort was usually transient and well controlled with simple analgesia. The mean NRS score of pain of two is lower than that reported in previous studies using both open and needle techniques [5, 6]. Among the available literature, only the study by Patel et al., which utilized a conchotome for muscle sampling, reported lower pain scores, but these were assessed using a different scale (VAS) [7]. Furthermore, the lack of standardized guidelines for OMB across institutions likely contributes to the variability in patient experiences, as institution‐specific protocols and surgical practices may influence both intraoperative and postoperative outcomes.

Despite the inherent variability in patient profiles, demographic and clinical factors did not significantly influence pain perception, as also noted in previous studies [5]. The only clinical factor associated with procedural pain was the CFS, a scale originally validated in older adults that reflects an individual's overall vulnerability to external stressors [12]. Although its use in younger populations remains debated, emerging evidence—as well as our findings—suggests that it may have predictive value in individuals with reduced physiological reserve, beyond chronological age [13]. In addition, recent studies have reported an association between frailty and chronic pain [12, 14], supporting the hypothesis that frailty may also contribute to acute pain perception during invasive or minimally invasive procedures. Given the weak strength of the association observed in our study, this relationship should be interpreted cautiously. Further studies are needed to determine whether assessment of CFS in routine clinical practice has predictive value in identifying individuals at higher risk of experiencing procedural discomfort, with the goal of optimizing periprocedural care.

Our data also emphasize the role of psychosocial factors rather than demographic or clinical variables in shaping pain perception. Higher preprocedural anxiety correlated with increased pain intensity, consistent with evidence from other procedural care settings [15, 16]. Anxiety has been linked not only to higher anesthetic agent requirement but also to heightened postoperative pain intensity [17, 18, 19, 20]. It is believed that preoperative anxiety triggers the activation of the autonomic nervous system, subsequently leading to neuroendocrine alterations and hemodynamic alterations such as elevated heart rate and increased blood pressure [19, 20]. In our cohort, higher PHQ‐9 scores were associated with greater preinterventional anxiety, suggesting that patients with greater depressive symptom burden may be more vulnerable to anticipatory distress before invasive procedures. This well‐known relationship has been attributed to overlapping neurobiological mechanisms [15]. However, depressive symptoms did not directly correlate with procedural pain, suggesting an indirect pathway mediated by anxiety.

Importantly, the quality of interaction between patients and medical staff emerged as a protective factor. This highlights the importance of the patient‐doctor relationship and supports the implementation of communication‐based strategies—such as clear explanations, engaging patients in conversation on familiar topics, and simple measures such as relaxing background music to mitigate procedural anxiety. Our findings align with evidence from radiological and urological procedures, during which background music or empathetic dialog were used to reduce procedural pain [21, 22]. These practical strategies should therefore be considered as integral components of OMB protocols.

Follow‐up data at 15 and 30 days further strengthen the reassuring message for patients. Adverse events were uncommon and mostly minor. Esthetic complications were extremely rare and, in our series, occurred only in patients with predisposing conditions. Moreover, postprocedural pain did not occur systematically and, when present, was generally transient. Clinicians are advised to recommend first‐line analgesic treatments only on an as‐needed basis. This evidence reinforces the safety of OMB and may be used to reassure patients who express concerns about persistent pain or disfiguring scars.

Some limitations should be acknowledged. Most biopsies were performed on the deltoid muscle, which may be associated with lower discomfort compared to deeper or less accessible sites, potentially biasing the results. No significant differences in pain perception were observed between different biopsy sites; however, this finding may be limited by the small sample size of the nondeltoid biopsy subgroup. Furthermore, as the survey data were collected through a self‐administered questionnaire, the potential influence of self‐report bias cannot be excluded. This type of bias may arise from patients' subjective interpretation of questions, memory recall limitations, or the tendency to respond in a socially desirable manner. This study was conducted in a specialized neuromuscular referral center with a high procedural volume and established expertise in muscle biopsy. Therefore, our findings may not be fully generalizable to institutions without dedicated neuromuscular units or similar levels of experience. Multicenter studies including varied biopsy sites, as well as randomized evaluation of anxiety‐reducing interventions, will be necessary to confirm and expand our findings.

Conclusion

Our results shift the emphasis from surgical factors to psychosocial determinants in understanding patient discomfort during OMB. Recognizing the role of preprocedural anxiety and staff–patient communication provides clinicians with a clear opportunity to improve the patient experience through simple, low‐cost, and easily generalizable interventions. This study provides clinicians with data to better counsel patients concerned about postbiopsy pain or scarring while highlighting areas for further research on histological correlates of discomfort and standardized procedural guidelines.
