A PEGylated nanobody against HBsAg exhibits pleiotropic antiviral efficacy in hepatitis B models
Authors: Qun Yang, Wenhua Liu, Meiling Huang, Mingyi Lin, Jieli Hu, Jie Wei, Qian Yang, Sisi Wang, Deqiang Wang, Ailong Huang, Xuefei Cai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Hepatitis B virus (HBV) remains a global health burden, with current therapies rarely achieving functional cure, highlighting an unmet need for novel strategies. Here, we isolate a high-affinity nanobody, VHH4917, against the viral surface antigen (HBsAg) from an alpaca nanobodies phage library. VHH4917 exhibits a multi-phenotypic antiviral profile that extends beyond mere neutralization. It not only accelerates HBsAg clearance in vivo but also potently suppresses HBV replication, transcription, and particle secretion through distinct mechanisms. Further engineering by site-specific PEGylation markedly prolongs its circulating half-life and enhances in vivo antiviral efficacy in mouse models of chronic HBV infection. Our work characterizes VHH4917 as a potent, novel therapeutic candidate and provides a promising protein-engineering framework for advancing nanobody-based immunotherapy against chronic hepatitis B.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12951-026-04611-y.

Introduction

HBV infection remains among the most significant global public health challenges, with more than 80 million individuals currently living with chronic infection. Among these, approximately 30 million require clinical intervention [1]. Although preventive vaccines for hepatitis B have been shown to significantly reduce the incidence of new infections, existing treatment options, such as nucleoside analogues (NAs) and interferon, still struggle to provide a functional cure for chronic hepatitis B [2]. Therefore, the development of novel therapeutic agents targeting HBV, including antibody-based therapies, is urgently needed [3].

Hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg), a key component of the HBV envelope, plays a critical role in viral entry, assembly, and immune evasion. Its unique self-assembly polymorphism enables it to spontaneously form spherical or tubular subviral particles in quantities far exceeding those of intact virions [4]. This characteristic allows HBsAg to act as an immunological decoy that depletes the host immune response and promotes immune tolerance. Consequently, sustained clearance of HBsAg is considered a key clinical endpoint for achieving a functional cure. Among the various therapeutic strategies, monoclonal antibodies targeting HBsAg and its derivatives (e.g., single-chain antibodies) have attracted considerable attention because of their ability to efficiently neutralize viruses, form immune complexes, and potentially restore immune function. For example, VIR-3434 is a fully human monoclonal antibody developed by Vir Biotechnology [5]. Its mechanism of action includes in vitro neutralization of all 10 HBV genotypes to inhibit viral entry, a reduction in circulating HBsAg in vivo, and potential conversion into a therapeutic vaccine through Fc engineering. A single low dose of 6 mg of VIR-3434 rapidly induces a decrease in HBsAg levels, which is suppressed for up to two weeks post-administration. Additionally, the monoclonal antibody mAb E6F6, which targets HBsAg, effectively suppressed sustained HBV replication in multiple mouse models [6]. Transgenic animal models have shown effects lasting several weeks. In human liver chimeric mouse models, E6F6 effectively prevents primary HBV infection and limits secondary viral spread among infected hepatocytes.

Because of their special physicochemical characteristics, nanoantibodies (VHH) have shown great promise in the antiviral sector. For example, Phase II clinical studies for the nanoantibody ALX-0171, which targets the respiratory syncytial virus (RSV), have concluded, confirming its viability for inhaled delivery [7]. SARS-CoV-2 variants can be efficiently and widely neutralized by engineered multivalent nanoantibodies against novel coronaviruses [8], and nanobodies that target conserved epitopes are being investigated as novel inhibitors in the fight against highly changeable viruses such as HIV [9]. All of these achievements point to nanobodies as a potentially effective new antiviral therapy platform. In previous studies, a broad-spectrum nanobody targeting HBsAg—designated Nb125s—has shown promising therapeutic potential. Its epitope resides within a highly conserved region of HBsAg, potentially preventing viral escape mutations [10]. However, the mechanisms of action for these therapeutic agents are predominantly limited to extracellular virus neutralization and Fc-mediated immune activation. Building upon this foundation, we are dedicated to exploring their application in chronic hepatitis B treatment.

In this study, we characterize a novel nanobody, VHH4917. Treatment with VHH4917 in both cellular and animal models led to reductions in multiple HBV-related markers, including pgRNA, HBV DNA, and HBV covalently closed circular DNA (cccDNA). In addition to effectively suppressing viral capsid assembly, VHH4917 treatment also impaired the secretion of viral antigens and particles. These data demonstrate that VHH4917 effectively interferes with the levels of key determinants of the HBV life cycle by targeting HBsAg, although the specific intracellular mechanisms driving these phenotypes remain to be further elucidated.

Transcriptomic analysis suggested a potential mechanism underlying this unique profile: treatment downregulated key host transcription factors involved in HBV transcriptional regulation. This implies a mechanism that may extend beyond conventional antibody–antigen interactions, possibly involving broader modulation of the host cell transcriptional environment. In AAV/HBV mouse models, sustained administration (20 mg/kg) reduced serum HBsAg levels. To address the short plasma half-life typical of nanobodies, we engineered PEGylated derivatives. This modification prolonged circulation and significantly enhanced both HBsAg clearance and overall antiviral efficacy in vivo.

This work systematically delineates a nanobody with a novel, multi-phenotypic antiviral profile. Our findings highlight that nanobodies targeting viral proteins can act as potent tools to indirectly modulate host cell functions via the virus–host interface, a potential that remains largely untapped.

Results

Screening of nanobodies and prediction of their binding ability to the hepatitis B surface antigen

To identify HBsAg-specific nanobodies, HBsAg was deposited onto immunotubes, and a library of 410 natural camelid nanobodies [11] was screened to select those with high affinity for HBsAg. Following three rounds of panning and washing, we successfully enriched the candidate sequences, as shown in Table 1. The third-round eluate was subjected to high-throughput sequencing [12], which produced the top five sequences with the highest enrichment levels. To predict potential interactions, we first modelled the three-dimensional structures of the selected nanobodies using AlphaFold3. We then performed protein–protein docking between these models and the HBsAg structure (NCBI:8YMK) (https://www.rcsb.org/structure/8YMK) using the HDOCK server with its hybrid docking algorithm. The resulting complex models were analysed and visualized with PyMOL 2.1. As shown in Fig. 1, the models predict binding of all five top candidates (VHH4917, VHH2054, VHH1636, VHH1038 and VHH683) to HBsAg, and identify the specific amino acid residues involved in the interaction interfaces.

Expression and purification of the nanobody

The top five sequences (which represent VHH4917, VHH2054, VHH1636, VHH1038, and VHH683) obtained through high-throughput sequencing were subsequently cloned and inserted into the pET28a vector, which was subsequently transformed into E. coli Rosetta. The functional epitope of nanobodies is closely linked to proper protein folding [13]; however, VHH2054, VHH1636, VHH1038, and VHH683 exhibited insoluble expression in this system. To address this limitation, the Shuffle T7-cell line, which constitutively expresses the disulphide isomerase DsbC, was utilized. This enzyme facilitates the formation of effective disulphide bonds and acts as a molecular chaperone, aiding in the proper folding of proteins that lack disulphide bonds [14]. The expression of VHH2054, VHH1636, VHH1038, and VHH683 in Shuffle T7 resulted in successful soluble expression of these four nanobodies. All five nanobodies were subsequently purified, and the purification results are shown in Supplementary Figure S1d. Notably, VHH1038 exhibited abnormal migration under these electrophoresis conditions, likely attributable to its highly stable conformational structure; however, its protein integrity was validated (Supplementary Figure S1c). Additional workflow diagrams detailing the expression process are provided in Supplementary Figure S1a–S1c.

Affinity assessment of VHH for the hepatitis B surface antigen

The binding activity of the five candidate nanobodies (VHH4917, VHH2054, VHH1636, VHH1038, and VHH683) to recombinant HBsAg was first assessed by indirect ELISA (Fig. 2a). VHH4917 showed the strongest signal, indicating the highest apparent affinity among the candidates. To confirm binding to natively assembled antigen, a capture ELISA was performed (Fig. 2b). VHH4917, VHH2054, VHH1636, VHH1038, and VHH683 were coated as capture antibody and incubated with purified HBsAg subviral particles and bound HBsAg was detected using a commercial anti-HBsAg enzyme conjugate (Kehua Biotech., Shanghai, P. R. China) VHH4917 effectively captured HBsAg in its native conformation. The binding between the screened VHH and the hepatitis B surface antigen was assessed using isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC). The titration results for the five nanobodies and the hepatitis B surface antigen are shown in Fig. 2c and g, respectively. The dissociation constants (KD) for the five VHH are as follows: VHH4917 = 117 nM, VHH2054 = 183 nM, VHH1636 = 162 nM, VHH1038 = 598 nM, and VHH683 = 266 nM. These findings demonstrate that the binding affinities of the five nanobodies to the hepatitis B surface antigen are within the nanomolar range, with VHH4917 showing the highest affinity. In this study, surface plasmon resonance (SPR) was used to further characterize the interactions between the five nanobodies and the HBV surface antigen, as SPR is regarded as the gold standard for affinity analysis. Affinity measurements were performed, with the results shown in Fig. 2h and l: KD VHH4917 = 1.44 nM, KD VHH2054 = 2.935 nM, KD VHH1636 = 3.688 nM, KD VHH1038 = 5.422 nM, and KD VHH683 = 16.3 nM. SPR-based affinity measurements aligned with the previously established enrichment ranking. The affinity assessment results indicated that the three nanobodies with the highest binding affinities were chosen for further cellular-level antiviral studies.

Size and dispersion of nanobody–HBsAg complexes

Smaller, more dispersed antigen–antibody complexes are typically cleared more efficiently by the reticuloendothelial system (RES; e.g., macrophages), a property linked to improve in vivo safety and clearance kinetics [14]. To assess the complexes formed between HBsAg and the lead nanobodies (VHH4917, VHH2054, and VHH1636), we analysed their morphology by transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and size distribution by dynamic light scattering (DLS). TEM revealed distinct differences (Fig. 3a). Complexes formed by VHH4917 with HBsAg were notably smaller and more uniformly dispersed, whereas VHH2054 and VHH1636 induced the formation of larger aggregates. DLS quantification confirmed this size disparity (Fig. 3b). The average hydrodynamic diameter of VHH4917–HBsAg complexes was approximately 900 nm, significantly smaller than those formed by the other two nanobodies. The complex structure observed for VHH4917 indicates it may possess favourable pharmacokinetic and safety properties in vivo.

In vitro antiviral activity

Nanobodies dose-dependently inhibit HBsAg and HBeAg secretion in AD38

To assess the ability of the nanobodies to effectively inhibit hepatitis B virus secretion at the cellular level, the three highest-affinity nanobodies—VHH4917, VHH2054, and VHH1636—were chosen and evaluated in AD38 to determine their inhibitory effects in relation to increasing concentrations. First, AD38 cells were plated in 24-well plates until the cells reached approximately 70% confluence. After 24 h of treatment at predefined concentrations, the culture supernatants were collected and examined using ELISA to evaluate the HBsAg and HBeAg levels. As shown in Fig. 4a and b, the concentrations of HBsAg and HBeAg decreased in a dose-dependent manner as the nanobody concentration increased. The inhibition rates of the three nanobodies against HBsAg and HBeAg, determined in parallel comparisons, are presented in Figure S1e and S1f. And Immunofluorescence tests with VHH4917-FITC were performed to determine whether VHH4917 could penetrate cells. The results showed that VHH4917-FITC could permeate the cell membrane and colocalize with intracellular surface antigens (Fig. 4c). In contrast, the negative group showed that VHH4917-FITC did not colocalize with intracellular HBcAg. There is a difference in the signal pattern of VHH4917-FITC between the negative group and the experimental group. It is important to note here that the negative group was a later supplementary experiment, and the imaging equipment conditions and parameter settings differed from those of the experimental group. However, this does not affect the core conclusion that VHH4917-FITC specifically colocalizes with the hepatitis B surface antigen. The observed antiviral effects suggest that VHH4917 may enter cells through some mechanism to exert its activity, though the precise internalization pathway remains to be fully elucidated. The results of the cytotoxicity tests for VHH4917, VHH2054, and VHH1636 are shown in Supplementary Figure S1g, S1h, and S1i, respectively.

Nb(TAO), a tau-targeting nanobody, shows no anti-HBV activity

To control for potential off-target effects of the nanobody scaffold, we used Nb(TAO)—a nanobody targeting tau protein, an antigen unrelated to HBV—as a non-specific control in both AD38 and HepG2.2.15 cell assays. Nb(TAO) treatment showed no statistically significant effect on HBV replication, transcription, and antigen secretion; levels of HBsAg, HBeAg, and other viral markers in the supernatant remained unchanged compared with the untreated control (Supplementary Figure S2). These results demonstrate that the antiviral activity of VHH4917 is specific to HBsAg targeting and is not due to inherent properties of the nanobody scaffold.

VHH4917 effectively neutralizes hepatitis B virus and reduces the infection of HepG2-NTCP cells

To evaluate the neutralizing capacity of VHH4917 against HBV, we used a HepG2 cell line stably expressing the sodium taurocholate cotransporting polypeptide (NTCP), referred to as HepG2-NTCP cells. Subsequently, HepG2-NTCP cells were inoculated overnight with HBV [15] at an inoculum of 500 viral genome equivalents (GE) per cell. On day 5 postinoculation, the effect of VHH4917 on viral infection was assessed by measuring the HBeAg levels in the culture supernatants using ELISA. This experiment was designed to evaluate the extracellular neutralizing activity and viral entry inhibition capacity of VHH4917. The results demonstrated that VHH4917 effectively inhibited HBV infection, with a half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC₅₀) of 1.721 nM (see Supplementary Figure S1o).

VHH4917 significantly inhibits the secretion of hepatitis B virus particles from AD38 cells

As the AD38 model does not support NTCP-mediated de novo infection, the following experiments assessed the direct inhibitory effect of VHH4917 on intracellular HBV replication and transcription. Surface Plasmon Resonance (SPR) experiments identified VHH4917 as having significantly higher affinity (lower KD value) for HBsAg compared to VHH2054 and VHH1638, and three nanobodies reduced HBsAg and HBeAg levels in dose-dependent assays using HepAD38 cells, but VHH4917 exhibited the most pronounced and consistent inhibitory effects. Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM) analysis of immune complexes formed between nanobodies and HBsAg showed distinct differences: VHH4917 formed smaller, more dispersed complexes, whereas VHH2054 and VHH1636 led to large aggregates. Literature suggests smaller, soluble complexes are cleared more efficiently by the reticuloendothelial system, like macrophages, in vivo. These properties imply VHH4917 may offer superior efficacy and safety profiles, which drove the decision to focus subsequent studies on VHH4917—standard practice in drug development. AD38 cells were treated with 2.004 µM VHH4917 (refreshed every 24 h) or left untreated (Control group). After 48 h, culture supernatants were collected, and cells were washed with PBS and lysed for analysis. Western blot analysis revealed that VHH4917 treatment significantly reduced intracellular levels of the viral large (L) surface protein (by ~ 27%; Fig. 5a and c), small (S) surface protein (~ 60%; Fig. 5a and d), and core (C) antigen (~ 57%; Fig. 5b and e). At the RNA level, RT-qPCR and Northern blot showed that the 3.5 kb viral RNA was reduced by approximately 65% (Fig. 5f and g). Viral DNA replication was also strongly suppressed, with intracellular DNA levels decreased by ~ 80% (Supplementary Fig. S1n; Southern blot in Fig. 5h) and cccDNA by ~ 55% (Supplementary Figure S3a). Furthermore, using non-denaturing electrophoresis to detect intact nucleocapsid complexes, intracellular nucleocapsid production was markedly reduced (Fig. 5i), indicating interference with capsid assembly. In parallel, ELISA of the supernatant showed significant decreases in both secreted HBsAg and HBeAg (Fig. 5j). Extracellular viral DNA levels were ~ 50% lower in the treated group (Fig. 5k). Particle gel electrophoresis of concentrated supernatant confirmed a strong reduction in viral capsids (Fig. 5l), SVPs and virions (Fig. 5m). Collectively, these results demonstrate that VHH4917 exerts potent intracellular activity, suppressing HBV RNA transcription, DNA replication, protein expression, capsid assembly, and viral particle release.

VHH4917 treatment significantly suppressed virion secretion in HepG2.2.15 cells

To further test the potential therapeutic efficacy of VHH4917 in the context of chronic hepatitis B, the HepG2.2.15 cell line was chosen for parallel validation to assess antiviral activity. The antiviral effects of VHH4917 on HepG2.2.15 cells were examined at the protein, transcriptional, and replication levels. HepG2.2.15 cells were grown in media supplemented with 2.004 µM VHH4917. Untreated HepG2.2.15 cells served as the control group. The medication was renewed every 24 h. After 48 h of treatment, the culture supernatants were collected, and the cells were then rinsed with PBS and lysed for Western blot. Compared with those in the control group, intracellular hepatitis B surface antigen L protein levels were reduced by approximately 50% (Fig. 6a and c), S protein levels by 61% (Fig. 6a and e), and core antigen levels by approximately 55% (Fig. 6b and d). Furthermore, this nanobody affected transcription levels in HepG2.2.15 cells, compared with the untreated control, VHH4917 treatment substantially reduced 3.5 kb RNA levels (Fig. 6f and g), and a marked decrease in 2.4 kb and 2.1 kb RNA levels was also observed (Fig. 6g). What’s more, in HepG2.2.15, a significant reduction in intracellular nucleocapsid production was detected between the two groups (Fig. 6h). At the level of replication, a 60% decrease in intracellular DNA levels is shown in Fig. 6i and extracellular viral DNA levels were approximately 70% lower than those in the untreated group, as shown in Fig. 6l. More importantly, a 55% decrease in cccDNA levels is displayed in Supplementary Figure S3b. In addition, particle gel electrophoresis revealed a significant decrease in the number of extracellular virus particles, as shown in Fig. 6j, and the extracellular HBsAg and HBeAg levels were determined using ELISA. The results revealed substantial suppression of HBsAg and HBeAg (Fig. 6k). In conclusion, VHH4917 treatment dramatically reduced virion release in HepG2.2.15 cells. The half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC₅₀) of VHH4917 was 6.914 µM in HepG2.2.15 cells (see Supplementary Figure S1j).

In vivo antiviral efficacy and pharmacokinetics

VHH4917 demonstrated dose-dependent antiviral efficacy in the AAV/HBV mouse model

We evaluated the therapeutic potential of VHH4917 in an AAV/HBV mouse model, considering current clinical treatment strategies [16]. After confirming model establishment by baseline serology, mice were randomly assigned to groups and treated with VHH4917 at 5, 10, or 20 mg/kg. Entecavir (0.5 mg/kg) and PBS served as positive and negative controls, respectively. Blood was collected 24 h after the first dose and subsequently every 4 days. Serum HBsAg levels decreased in a dose-dependent manner, with the highest dose (20 mg/kg) showing the most pronounced reduction (Fig. 7a). Continuous administration suppressed HBsAg and maintained it at a low level; however, after treatment cessation (Day 8), HBsAg rebounded.

PEGylation of VHH4917 extended its serum half-life and enhanced the clearance of HBV particles in mice

Nanobodies have a small molecular weight and are rapidly cleared by the kidney. To prolong their half-life and improve their efficacy, we modified VHH4917 with polyethylene glycol (PEG) [17]. VHH4917 was conjugated with either linear PEG-20 or octa-arm PEG-40 and then purified and concentrated (Figure S1k). PEGylation markedly extended the serum half-life of VHH4917 and reduced its renal clearance. Pharmacokinetic analysis showed that the half-life of VHH4917-PEG20 increased approximately 3-fold, and that of VHH4917-PEG40 approximately 5-fold, compared with the unmodified nanobody (Fig. 7c). Biodistribution imaging 24 h after administration revealed lower renal accumulation of the PEGylated variants (Supplementary Figure S3c), and quantitative fluorescence analysis confirmed significantly reduced kidney signals (Fig. 7d), which indicates reduced renal clearance of the PEGylation.

Based on the effective dose of 20 mg/kg established for unmodified VHH4917 (Fig. 7a), we compared different dosing regimens: unmodified VHH4917 every 2 days, VHH4917-PEG20 every 3 days, and VHH4917-PEG40 every 5 days. While unmodified VHH4917 maintained reduced HBsAg levels with bi-daily dosing, PEG40-VHH4917 administered every 5 days induced a stronger and more sustained suppression of serum HBsAg (Fig. 7b). Moreover, PEG40-VHH4917 reduced HBeAg levels by approximately 50% (Supplementary Figure S1l) and significantly lowered serum HBV DNA (Fig. 7e).

Modified VHH4917-PEG40 can enter the liver and inhibit viral replication

To evaluate the liver-targeting ability and antiviral efficacy of nanobodies, we administered VHH4917, VHH4917-PEG20, and VHH4917-PEG40 to the mice at 20 mg/kg. Liver tissues were collected 24 h postinjection, and immunohistochemistry was used to assess hepatic distribution. Both unmodified and PEGylated nanobodies successfully entered the liver and bound to HBsAg as shown by clear colocalization signals (Fig. 8). After 12 days of treatment, hepatic HBsAg expression was markedly reduced in all treatment groups, with VHH4917-PEG40 having the strongest effect (Fig. 7h and i). Liver samples were further analysed with ELISA and qPCR, both modified and unmodified nanobodies inhibited HBeAg expression (Figure S1m) and viral replication, the HBV DNA of liver as shown in Fig. 7f, whereas VHH4917-PEG40 achieved the greatest reduction, lowering hepatic viral DNA by ~ 80% (Fig. 7f) and HBeAg by ~ 60% (Figure S1m). What’s more, VHH4917-PEG40 significantly reduced HBV RNA in mouse livers more markedly than the other two treatments, as shown in Fig. 7g.These results demonstrate that VHH4917 exerts potent antiviral activity in the AAV/HBV mouse model and that PEGylation, particularly with PEG40, enhances therapeutic efficacy by prolonging circulation time and maintaining effective plasma concentrations.

Discussion

Nanobodies represent an emerging class of biologics with considerable diagnostic and therapeutic potential. In this study, we developed VHH4917, a nanobody with a high affinity for the hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg). Furthermore, VHH4917 exhibits good solubility when expressed in E. coli, a prokaryotic expression system. Its binding affinity is similar to that of the monoclonal antibody G12, which targets the preS1 region of HBsAg. Furthermore, VHH4917 strongly suppressed HBV particle secretion in both AD38 and HepG2.2.15 cell models and demonstrated potent neutralization in HepG2-NTCP-based infection assays—a key system for validating therapeutic efficacy. Future work will focus on further optimizing this infection model for standardized post-infection therapeutic evaluation. Additionally, at the animal level, the PEGylated version of VHH4917-PEG40 displayed increased serum stability and efficacy in lowering blood HBsAg levels. Both the modified and unmodified forms of VHH4917 were able to enter the liver, decrease viral replication, and promote clearance of viral antigens and particles.

VHH4917 exerts its anti-HBV effect by targeting HBsAg, which is associated with the concurrent decrease of multiple key HBV indicators

VHH4917, which was selected to target HBsAg, has a high affinity of 1.44 nM. When VHH4917 was added to AD38 and HepG2.2.15 cell culture supernatants at a therapeutic concentration of 2.004 µM, it reduced the HBsAg and HBeAg levels in the supernatants of both stable cell lines. Moreover, VHH4917 decreased the intracellular L, S, and C antigen concentrations, the observed differences in L-HBs banding patterns likely reflect cell model-specific variations in glycosylation states, which do not affect the core conclusion that VHH4917 effectively reduces intracellular HBsAg levels. Further investigation revealed that this nanobody affected replication levels and transcriptomic profiles in both cell lines. This surpasses the ability of traditional neutralizing antibodies, which merely block viral entry and clear circulating antigens. VHH4917 is the first reported nanobody that targets HBsAg alone and effectively reduces the levels of multiple key HBV parameters, including transcription, capsid assembly, DNA replication, and secretion. Based on literature review, comparative analysis and positioning among existing HBV-Targeting strategies about nanobody are summarized in Table 2. Building upon our findings, we propose that the potent antiviral activity of VHH4917 stems from its capacity to induce profound transcriptional and metabolic reprogramming in host hepatocytes. Transcriptomic profiling reveals a coordinated suppression of hepatic transcription factor networks critical for HBV persistence. Notably, expression of HNF4α, a master regulator of hepatocyte function and HBV transcription [18, 19], is significantly down-regulated.(see Supplementary Figure S4a-S4c) This perturbation is further reflected in the concomitant reduction of its canonical target gene, APOA1 [20, 21], as visualized in the companion volcano plot (Figure S3d), this coherent downregulation of a key transcription factor and its effector targets strongly implies a functional impairment of the HNF4α signaling axis. Similarly, the observed decrease in CPT1A [22] expression—a rate-limiting enzyme in fatty-acid oxidation, aligns with dampened PPARα pathway activity, which is known to support HBV pgRNA transcription. These data suggest an integrated model in which engagement of HBsAg by VHH4917 triggers a host-directed response that simultaneously attenuates key transcriptional drivers of viral gene expression (e.g., HNF4α, PPARα) and restricts metabolic pathways that supply energy and substrates for replication. This dual strategy—transcriptional silencing coupled with metabolic constraint—effectively disrupts the intracellular niche that HBV exploits, providing a mechanistic basis for the observed multi-step inhibition.

In summary, VHH4917 represents a new class of antiviral agent that moves beyond passive neutralization to actively reprogram the host-cell environment. Our work suggests a paradigm for targeting chronic viral infections by disrupting the host regulatory and metabolic networks essential for pathogen persistence. Future studies will focus on validating this model at the protein and functional level.

VHH4917 effectively neutralizes HBV infection and inhibits the secretion of hepatitis B virus particles

VHH4917 is expressed at high levels in prokaryotic systems with proper folding and functional epitopes. Its specific binding to HBsAg was confirmed by indirect ELISA, ITC, and SPR, and its cellular internalization and intracellular colocalization with HBsAg were visualized by immunofluorescence in AD38 cells. Furthermore, VHH4917 showed potent neutralization activity in HBV infection assays using HepG2-NTCP cells, confirming its ability to block viral entry. The nanobody binds HBsAg with an affinity (KD) of 1.44 nM and exhibits a half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC₅₀) of 1.721 nM in neutralization experiments (Supplementary Figure S1o). We hypothesized that this was related to the affinity between the antibody and antigen, as well as the epitopes of VHH4917 binding to the hepatitis B surface antigen or specific amino acids [23, 24]. The precise mechanism by which VHH4917 reduces secretion of both viral and subviral particles in HepG2.2.15 and AD38 cells remains unclear. Importantly, the AD38 model—which supports HBV replication from an integrated template but is non-permissive to de novo NTCP-mediated infection—allows us to attribute the observed suppression of antigen secretion and intracellular replication markers specifically to intracellular inhibitory activity, independent of entry blockade.

Currently, it is unclear whether the inhibitory effect of S-specific VHH4917 occurs during intact granule formation or at a later stage. In the former case, interactions between small molecules that retain VHH4917 in the endoplasmic reticulum and the mature coat in the cytoplasm may be disrupted. Alternatively, interactions between individual S molecules in the endoplasmic reticulum may be blocked. Both scenarios result in impaired granule formation [25]. In the latter scenario, we hypothesize that complete particles formed in the secretory pathway. However, VHH4917 retention in the pathway inhibits further transport. Since intracellular HBsAg particle levels did not increase despite a significant decrease in extracellular levels, this inhibitory effect is unlikely caused solely by this mechanism [26]. On the basis of these speculations, subsequent work will further identify the epitope targeted by VHH4917 and examine the expression levels of endoplasmic reticulum/Golgi-associated functional proteins. This work provides a foundation for future studies on antiviral therapeutic mechanisms.

What’s more, treatment with VHH4917 led to a reduction in HBV cccDNA. Whether this decline reflects a direct action of the nanobody—for instance, by modulating host factors involved in cccDNA stability or transcriptional regulation—or is an indirect consequence of its potent suppression of viral replication and core protein synthesis (thereby limiting the de novo formation or maintenance of the cccDNA pool) remains to be determined. Elucidating this distinction represents a key objective for future investigation.

A discussion on the cellular uptake of VHH4917

The immunofluorescence data in this study clearly demonstrate that VHH4917 can enter hepatocytes expressing HBV such as AD38. We did not include an internal control for permeabilization efficiency (e.g., anti-tubulin staining), a minor effect of cell-state differences on permeabilization cannot be completely excluded. However, considering the strictly uniform conditions, clear negative controls, consistent replicates, and literature support, we believe that this potential influence does not affect our main conclusion: VHH4917-FITC can colocalize with endogenous HBsAg. This observation raises a critical mechanistic question: how does an antibody targeting an extracellular viral envelope protein gain intracellular access? Based on current findings and established cell biological principles, we propose two non-mutually exclusive possibilities, which have not yet been rigorously distinguished.

First, ligand-receptor co-internalization mediated by binding to membrane-anchored HBsAg. In infected hepatocytes, HBsAg exists not only as secreted particles but also in transmembrane forms—such as the large surface protein (LHBs)—that are displayed on the plasma membrane or reside in secretory compartments (ER, Golgi). By binding to these membrane-integrated forms, VHH4917 may form antigen–antibody complexes that exploit constitutive endocytic and membrane-trafficking pathways, leading to active internalization. This hypothesis aligns with the well-established principle that ligand engagement of membrane proteins often triggers clathrin- or caveolin-mediated endocytosis, delivering complexes into endosomal–lysosomal routes [27].

Second, “Active Cellular Uptake” Driven by the Inherent Properties of VHH4917. VHH4917 may possess a low but functionally relevant capacity for cellular entry independent of HBsAg binding. Certain nanobodies, due to their unique surface charge distribution, amphipathicity, or compact size, can be internalized via non-specific pinocytic mechanisms. Alternatively, VHH4917 might interact with endogenous endocytic receptors, thereby initiating receptor-mediated endocytosis. Once inside the cell through such routes, VHH4917 could encounter and bind to HBsAg in compartments such as endosomes, accounting for the observed co-localization signals. This possibility draws support from the conserved, intrinsic endocytic potential reported for some conventional antibodies and engineered nanobodies.

VHH4917 further reduced serum hepatitis B surface antigen levels in AAV/HBV-infected mice after PEG modification

Nanobodies are inherently constrained by their low molecular weight, which results in rapid renal clearance. In the AAV/HBV model, a therapeutic dose of 5 mg/kg was observed to exert minimal antiviral activity, whereas sustained administration at 20 mg/kg consistently reduced serum HBsAg levels. To address this limitation, PEGylation was employed to prolong the serum half-life of the nanobody, leading to more effective suppression of HBsAg titres [17]. While PEGylation effectively extends the serum half-life of nanobodies, this benefit often comes at the expense of cellular uptake. The conjugated PEG chains increase the hydrodynamic radius and create steric hindrance, which can significantly impair endocytosis and intracellular delivery. Surprisingly, both the PEGylated and unmodified forms were found to penetrate the liver, where they contributed to HBV particle clearance and inhibition of viral replication. We propose the following hypotheses to explain the observed intracellular activity: First, the nanobody may be internalized as part of a complex bound to membrane-associated HBsAg. Second, the specific PEGylation strategy employed—characterized by relatively short chain lengths or a unique conjugation —may retain sufficient paratope accessibility. Third, the construct may be recognized and internalized by specific receptors on the hepatocyte surface.

For the linear PEG20-conjugated VHH4917, its high systemic exposure (extended half-life) may be counterbalanced by a potential reduction in instantaneous binding efficiency per molecule due to steric hindrance. Consequently, at the specific time point assessed in this experiment, the pharmacokinetic advantage of prolonged circulation may not have been fully translated into superior antigen reduction, as the two effects might have reached a dynamic equilibrium. Among the tested constructs, VHH4917-PEG40 exhibited the most pronounced activity in lowering serum HBsAg levels and suppressing intrahepatic viral replication. These enhanced effects may be attributed to the long PEG chain, which is presumed to promote noncovalent self-assembly, higher-order aggregation of nanobody molecules [28], or the PEG modification-mediated enhancement of the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect, thus facilitating liver penetration and viral clearance [29]. In light of the promising therapeutic potential, further investigations are planned to evaluate combination strategies with nucleos(t)ide analogues such as entecavir (ETV) and NA [30] and to enhance immune-restorative properties through the incorporation of structural elements, such as the Fc functional domain. Moreover, therapeutic efficacy will be further validated in advanced preclinical models, including human liver chimeric mice.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates a nanobody-based intervention strategy that pioneers a novel therapeutic approach: antibodies targeting viral proteins whose efficacy may stem from their ability to reprogram host cell functions. This strategy was revealed through the anti-HBV effects of this nanobody, including in vitro neutralization activity and the capacity to downregulate multiple key indicators of hepatitis B virus in vivo. Furthermore, our study revealed additional viral clearance effects mediated by VHH4917-PEG40. This research advances our understanding of antibody-based immunotherapeutic strategies in animal models. This “potency-enhanced” PEG40 conjugation strategy prolongs the duration of drug treatment and significantly increases antiviral efficacy, offering novel insights for antibody engineering. We propose combining active immunizations (including vaccines), immune checkpoint inhibitors, and anti-HBV small molecules with passive immunizations based on VHH4917-PEG40 to achieve superior outcomes for HBsAg loss and seroconversion in chronic hepatitis B patients. As hepatitis B treatment technologies advance, the functional cure rate for chronic hepatitis B is poised to increase substantially.

Methods and materials

Screening natural phage libraries from 410 alpacas

Hepatitis B surface antigen was coated at a concentration of 50 µg/ml to immunotubes. After three rounds of screening, the resulting phages were validated using monoclonal ELISA. In addition, high-throughput sequencing was performed on the final eluate to achieve sequence enrichment.

Molecular docking

Structural predictions of the amino acid sequences of the nanobodies were performed using AlphaFold3, yielding the three-dimensional conformations of the top three nanobodies identified by high-throughput sequencing. Subsequently, the HDOCK server was used to apply a hybrid docking strategy to predict the binding complexes between these five nanobodies and the hepatitis B surface antigen. After calculation and optimization, the model was visualized and analysed using PyMOL 2.1 software [31].

Expression and purification of nanobodies

The five most enriched sequences were subcloned and inserted into the prokaryotic expression vector pET28a+-his-VHHX-his and subsequently transformed into the E. coli Rosetta and Shuffle T7 strains for nanobody expression. To increase VHHX production, the transformed hosts were cultivated in Luria–Bertani broth supplemented with 0.5 mM isopropyl β-D-thiogalactoside (IPTG) at 16 °C overnight. The bacterial particles were disrupted in lysis buffer using ultrasonication. Soluble VHHX was extracted from the supernatant using nickel affinity chromatography after centrifugation at 12,000 g for 30 min at 4 °C. The eluates were subsequently subjected to dialysis in PBS and concentrated using centrifugal filters with a molecular weight limit of 3 kDa.

ELISA

Indirect ELISA

First, the diluted HBsAg was coated in a 96-well plate overnight. Excess HBsAg solution was eliminated, and each well was filled with blocking buffer at 37 °C for 2–3 h. Thereafter, equivalent quantities of diluted VHHX were added, and the samples were incubated at 37 °C for one hour. Following the washing phase, anti-His-HRP secondary antibody was added, and the samples were incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. After the samples were washed, TMB colour development solution was added to initiate the reaction. The reaction was terminated after 15 min of incubation by the addition of 0.5 M H2SO4. An enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) reader was used to measure the OD450 value to evaluate the reaction results. ( The HBsAg genotyping used above is: HBV genotype C, and it is derived from Jingda Btech., with the catalog number P2205)

Double antibody sandwich ELISA

For capture ELISA, microwell plates were coated with each VHHX or with control proteins—unrelated nanobodies (targeting HBcAg) or bovine serum albumin (BSA). After blocking, purified HBsAg (genotype C) was added and incubated for 1 h at 37 °C. Plates were washed and incubated with a horseradish peroxidase-conjugated anti-HBsAg antibody (Kehua Biotech., Shanghai, P. R. China) for 30 min at 37 °C. Following further washes, color was developed by adding substrate solution and stopped with stop solution. Absorbance at 450 nm (OD₄₅₀) was measured on a microplate reader.

ITC

ITC experiments were performed at 25 °C using a MicroCal PEAQ-ITC [32]. Prior to titration, both HBsAg and nanobody samples were buffer-exchanged into the same buffer (PBS) to minimize dilution effects. The nanobody solutions were titrated into HBsAg at a molar ratio of approximately 20:1. A preliminary injection of 0.4 µL was followed by 12 successive injections of 3.0 µL each at 150 s intervals. Raw heat signals were integrated, corrected for baseline, and analysed using MicroCal ITC data analysis software, and a single-site binding model was applied to obtain the thermodynamic parameters.(The HBsAg genotyping used above is: HBV genotype C, and it is derived from Jingda Biotech, with the catalog number P2205).

SPR

SPR experiments were performed using a Biacore instrument equipped with an NTA sensor chip to characterize the interaction between the nanobodies and HBsAg. Nanobodies were immobilized onto the chip surface via Ni²⁺–NTA coordination, and increasing concentrations of HBsAg (serial dilutions covering the expected affinity range) were injected over the surface at a constant flow rate. The association and dissociation phases were monitored in real time, and the corresponding response units (RUs) were recorded to generate sensorgrams. A control reference channel was subtracted to correct for nonspecific binding and bulk refractive index changes. Sensorgrams were globally fitted to a 1:1 Langmuir binding model to derive the kinetic parameters (k_on, k_off) and equilibrium dissociation constant (KD). Data acquisition and analysis were performed using Prime8 software (GE Healthcare).( The HBsAg genotyping used above is: HBV genotype C, and it is derived from Jingda Biotech, with the catalog number P2205.).

TEM

Antibody solutions were diluted to a final concentration of 800 µg/mL, and HBsAg was added to 400 µg/mL, followed by incubation of the mixtures at 37 °C for 30 min. Negative staining was subsequently conducted, and images were obtained via transmission electron microscopy.

In vitro neutralization test

Given that the VHH4917 in this study target the hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg), it is anticipated to function as neutralizing antibodies by blocking viral entry. To directly validate this expected function, we employed a standard viral neutralization assay utilizing the HepG2-NTCP cell infection model (The cell line was kindly provided by the laboratory of Professor NingShao Xia at Xiamen University). HBV was incubated with a serial dilution of VHH4917 at an inoculum of 500 viral genome equivalents (GE) per cell and then added to cells expressing NTCP (called HepG2-NTCP). Five days after infection, secreted HBeAg was detected for quantitative analysis [6].

Cell experiments

Cell culture and processing

AD38 (not treat with tetracycline) and HepG2.2.15 cells were cultured in DMEM and MEM, respectively, supplemented with 10% foetal bovine serum (Lonsera). The cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂ and passaged three times prior to plating. The cultures were allowed to reach 60–80% confluency before treatment with the nanobodies. Cells maintained under identical conditions without nanobody exposure served as the control group.

Cell viability assay (CCK-8)

Cytotoxicity was assessed using the CCK-8 assay. HepAD38 or HepG2.2.15 cells were seeded in 96-well plates. After allowing cells to adhere, they were treated with serially diluted nanobodies for 24 h. Then, 10 µL of CCK-8 reagent was added to each well, and plates were incubated for 30–60 min at 37 °C. Absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader. Cell viability was calculated as: (OD₄₅₀ treated − OD₄₅₀ blank) / (OD₄₅₀ control − OD₄₅₀ blank) × 100%. Dose-response curves were plotted from mean ± SD values, and half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC₅₀) values were determined.

Western blotting

Cells were lysed on ice for 20 min in RIPA buffer supplemented with protease inhibitors. The lysates were subsequently centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 20 min at 4 °C, after which the supernatants containing the intracellular proteins were collected. Protein expression was subsequently analysed by Western blotting (WB) [33].

Q-PCR

Viral DNA was extracted from cell culture supernatants using a Tiengen reagent kit, while intracellular DNA was isolated with replication intermediate lysis buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0), 1 mM EDTA, 1% NP-40, and 2% sucrose). Real-time quantitative PCR (qPCR) was subsequently performed using SYBR green-based reagents to quantify HBV DNA. The primer pair used for amplification was HBV-F (5′-CCTAGTAGTCAGTTATGTCAAC-3′) and HBV-R (5′-TCTATAAGCTGGAGGAGTGCGA-3′), targeting a conserved region of the HBV genome. Amplification was carried out on an ABI 7500 Real-Time PCR System with the following cycling conditions: 94 °C for 20 s, followed by 39 cycles of 94 °C for 10 s and 60 °C for 20 s. Viral DNA levels were normalized to those of plasmid standards and analysed using the ΔΔCt method [34].

Isolation and quantification of HBV cccDNA

HBV covalently closed circular DNA (cccDNA) was selectively extracted from infected hepatocytes (HepG2.2.15 or HepAD38) using a modified Hirt procedure [35]. Briefly, cell pellets were lysed in pre-chilled Hirt buffer (Quantification of the hepatitis B virus cccDNA: evidence-based guidelines for monitoring the key obstacle of HBV cure) (10 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 10 mM EDTA, 0.6% SDS) for 10 min at room temperature. High-molecular-weight chromosomal DNA and protein complexes were precipitated by adding 5 M KCl to a final concentration of 0.5 M, followed by gentle mixing and overnight incubation at 4 °C. The supernatant containing cccDNA was collected after centrifugation (12,000 × g, 30 min, 4 °C), treated with Proteinase K and RNase A, and purified by phenol: chloroform: isoamyl alcohol (25:24:1) extraction and ethanol precipitation. The purified cccDNA was finally resuspended in TE buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl, 1 mM EDTA, pH 8.0) and stored at − 20 °C. Absolute quantification of cccDNA was performed by quantitative PCR (qPCR) targeting its unique gap region, using the following primers and probe: HBV-F: 5′-ACTCGTGGTGGACTTCTCTC-3′ ;HBV-R:5′-TGAGGCATAGCAGCAGGATG-3′ HBV-Probe: 5′-ATGATAAAACGCCGCAGACACATCCA-3′.

RT‒qPCR and northern blot

Total RNA was extracted from cells using TRIzol reagent. For transcript quantification, reverse transcription was carried out with a commercial cDNA synthesis kit, and gene expression levels were analysed by qPCR using SYBR green chemistry [34]. The primer sequences used for RT-qPCR in this study are presented in Table 3. For transcript validation, equal amounts of RNA from the experimental and control groups were resolved by formaldehyde–agarose gel electrophoresis, transferred onto nylon membranes, and subjected to Northern blotting with specific probes, followed by chemiluminescence detection [36].

Animal experiments

Animals

Male C57 mice (6–8 weeks old) were obtained from the Laboratory Animal Center of Chongqing Medical University. All procedures were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Chongqing Medical University. To establish the HBV replication model, mice received a hydrodynamic injection of AAV/HBV1.2 (MOI = 1.0 × 1012 IU) diluted in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) via the tail vein within 5 s. Following model establishment, the mice were randomly divided into experimental and control groups (n = 3 per group).

Dose escalation

Mice with AAV/HBV infection were administered VHH4917 intraperitoneally at doses of 5, 10, or 20 mg/kg. Blood samples are collected 24 h after the first administration, followed by every 48 h thereafter. All subsequent sampling is uniformly scheduled every four days starting from the first administration.Serum hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg) levels were quantified using a commercial ELISA kit (Kehua Biotech., Shanghai, P. R. China) to determine the dose–response relationship.

Drug half-life assay

To evaluate the half-life of VHH4917 and its PEGylated forms in vivo, we conducted experiments using normal C57 mice. The mice were administered VHH4917-RhoB, VHH4917-PEG20-RhoB, and VHH4917-PEG40-RhoB at a dose of 0.5 µmol/kg via tail vein injection. Blood samples were collected at various time points: 30 min, 1 h, 4 h, 8 h, 18 h, and 24 h post-injection. The absorbance of Rhodamine B was measured at 555 nm (OD555) to determine the concentration of the compounds. Quantification was achieved utilizing a standard curve. This curve was prepared by diluting blood samples obtained from untreated mice to account for any matrix effects inherent to mouse blood. We then plotted a linear regression of the logarithm of drug molar concentration against time. From this regression, the elimination rate constant (k) was derived, and the half-life (t₁/₂) was subsequently calculated using the equation t₁/₂ = 0.693/k.

PEG modification of nanobodies and treatment

VHH4917 was conjugated with PEG20 or PEG40 and purified by ultrafiltration, as described previously [38]. Both unmodified and PEGylated VHH4917 were administered tail vein at 20 mg/kg. The mice were divided into three groups receiving distinct dosing regimens: the unmodified VHH4917 group was dosed every 2 days, the VHH4917-PEG20 group every 3 days, and the VHH4917-PEG40 group every 5 days. Blood was first collected 24 h after the initial administration. Subsequently, samples were taken from each group immediately before the next scheduled dose. The purpose of adopting distinct dosing regimens was not to create an inequitable comparison, but was instead based on established dosing precedents and supported by experimental half-life data. This approach was designed to simulate the peak or sustained therapeutic effect achievable for each molecule at its own pharmacokinetically optimized dosing frequency. Therefore, these regimens more accurately reflect their respective potential value in translational development. Serum levels of HBsAg, hepatitis B e antigen (HBeAg), and HBV DNA were assessed at specified intervals. Liver tissues were subjected to immunohistochemistry to evaluate intrahepatic distribution and antiviral efficacy. In addition, Western blotting and immunohistochemistry with anti-HBsAg monoclonal antibodies [39] were employed to assess hepatic protein expression. Viral DNA was extracted from liver tissues and analysed by quantitative PCR to evaluate the inhibition of HBV DNA replication.

In vivo liver targeting and antigen engagement assay

To evaluate the hepatic delivery and target engagement of VHH4917 and its PEGylated derivatives in vivo, the following experiments were performed. Fluorescent probe preparation: Purified VHH4917, VHH4917-PEG20, and VHH4917-PEG40 were separately conjugated to RhoB at a molar ratio of 1:5 (protein: dye). Following the reaction, unconjugated dye was thoroughly removed by dialysis, yielding the fluorescently labelled nanobodies VHH4917-RhoB, VHH4917-PEG20-RhoB, and VHH4917-PEG40-RhoB. Animal experiments and tissue collection: AAV/HBV chimeric mouse models were used. Mice were administered PBS or the fluorescently labelled nanobodies via tail vein injection at a dose of 10 mg protein per kg body weight. Liver tissues were harvested 24 h post-injection. Immunofluorescence staining and imaging: Harvested liver tissues were embedded in OCT compound, sectioned, and processed for immunofluorescence staining. HBsAg was detected using a commercial mouse anti-HBsAg primary antibody followed by an Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated secondary antibody (green signal). Cell nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. The subcellular localization of the nanobodies (red signal from RhoB) and their colocalization with HBsAg were visualized and analysed using laser scanning confocal microscopy to assess hepatic accumulation and specific antigen binding.

Assessment of Liver Toxicity by H&E Staining

Mice of AAV/HBV were administered VHH4917, VHH4917-PEG20, VHH4917-PEG40, or PBS (control) via tail vein injection. Liver tissues were collected at the indicated time points, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, and embedded in paraffin. Section (5 μm) were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) for histological examination. Liver toxicity was assessed by a pathologist blinded to the treatment groups, evaluating hepatocellular necrosis, inflammation, and morphological abnormalities.
