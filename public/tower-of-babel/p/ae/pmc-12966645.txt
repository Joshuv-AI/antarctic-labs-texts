Early Nonsteroidal Anti-Inflammatory Drug Prescriptions and Nonunion After Scaphoid Fractures: A TriNetX Matched Cohort Study
Authors: Amy Phan, Christopher M Dussik, Danielle Wilbur, Bilal Mahmood, Ronald Gonzalez, Constantinos Ketonis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThe purpose of our study was to evaluate the relationship of nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug (NSAID) use and nonunion rates in nonoperatively and operatively managed scaphoid fractures.MethodsWe queried the TriNetX database to identify all patients diagnosed with scaphoid fractures. Patients were stratified by operative versus nonsurgical management, and by a prescription for NSAIDs within 1 month of scaphoid fracture diagnosis. We then assessed the incidence of scaphoid nonunion and the rate of salvage procedures for nonunion within 3 months to 2 years of the initial diagnosis of scaphoid fracture or scaphoid surgery. Chi-squared testing and odds ratio analysis were used to determine statistical significance.ResultsAfter matching, there were 11,629 patients in each nonsurgical group and 1,063 patients in each operative group. Within the matched nonsurgical treatment cohorts, patients with prescription NSAIDs had a significantly increased incidence of scaphoid nonunion with an odds ratio of 1.7 (95% CI: 1.4−1.9) and significantly increased incidence of salvage procedures (odds ratio: 1.5; 95% CI: 1.2−1.9). In contrast, within the matched operative fracture group with and without perioperative NSAID prescriptions, there were no notable differences between the incidence of scaphoid nonunion, or of salvage procedures.ConclusionsAmong patients with nonoperatively managed scaphoid fractures, those prescribed NSAIDs within 1 month of diagnosis demonstrated an increased risk of nonunion and subsequent salvage procedures. This association was not observed in operatively managed patients with perioperative prescribed NSAIDs, suggesting that mechanical fixation may offset the potential adverse effects of NSAIDs. These findings highlight the importance of appropriate patient counseling when deciding on NSAID use in the early postinjury period after scaphoid fractures, especially when nonsurgical management is planned.Type of study/level of evidencePrognostic IIc.Key words: Nonunion, NSAIDs, Scaphoid fractures

Scaphoid fractures account for approximately 60% to 80% of all carpal fractures, with reported nonunion rates ranging from 5% to 50%.1, 2, 3, 4, 5 These injuries occur most commonly in men between 15 and 40 years of age.5,6 Diagnosis may be challenging, as up to 30% of scaphoid fractures are missed on initial radiographs at presentation.7 Established risk factors for scaphoid nonunion include delayed treatment, fracture displacement, fracture location, carpal instability, and smoking/nicotine use.1,3,4,8,9 Failure to achieve union is clinically significant, as scaphoid nonunion can lead to carpal malalignment and progressive radiocarpal arthritis.2,7

Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) are commonly prescribed for analgesia following fractures.10,11 However, their impact on bone healing remains controversial. Prior studies evaluating fractures of the spine, pelvis, and long bones have suggested a potential association between NSAID use and impaired fracture healing,11, 12, 13 whereas other investigations have demonstrated minimal or no effect on rates of nonunion or delayed union.10,14 In contrast, there is a paucity of literature examining the role of NSAIDs in the management of hand fractures, and most of our data on the association of NSAIDs and fracture nonunion is derived from retrospective studies in nonhand cases.13,15,16 It is therefore imperative to further evaluate NSAID use in hand surgery, as they are being used increasingly to help reduce opioid consumption.16

In this study, we aimed to leverage a large, multinational database to determine whether perioperative NSAID utilization in the setting of scaphoid fractures is associated with an increased risk of nonunion and/or the need for salvage procedures. By clarifying the potential relationship between NSAID use and fracture nonunion, we sought to better facilitate shared decision-making regarding multimodal pain control options, particularly in patients with medical comorbidities that may predispose them to impaired bone healing.

Materials and Methods

This was a retrospective cohort study performed using the TriNetX research network, which was queried through a combination of International Classification of Diseases (ICD) and Current Procedural Terminology (CPT) codes. The TriNetX database is a deidentified patient database, which is based from medical claims for patient encounters, and permits query-based data extraction.17 Within this database, data from inpatient and outpatient clinical encounters, prescription, and laboratory data are extracted from the participating health care organization’s electronic medical record system.18 At the time of this study, the TriNetX database included >160 million patients from 113 health care organizations from January 1, 2010, to December 31, 2023. The deidentification process is attested by a qualified expert as defined in Section §164.514(b)(1) of the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act Privacy Rule.17 This study was found to be exempt from Institutional Review Board Approval, as all data from the TriNetX research network is deidentified and Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act compliant, and does not include any interventions or interactions with human subjects.

We queried the database on December 26, 2025, to identify patients who had sustained scaphoid fractures, and who had undergone both nonsurgical and operative management from January 1, 2010, and December 21, 2023. This query was used to identify patients who had sustained new scaphoid fractures between January 1, 2010, and December 21, 2023. Patients under the age of 18 and those aged 90 years and above were excluded.

For the nonsurgical scaphoid fracture group, we identified patients using the ICD-10 code S62.0, and excluded patients with the CPT code 25628. Conversely, for the operative scaphoid fracture group, we identified patients using the ICD-10 code S62.0 and included all patients with the CPT code 25628. We then further stratified these groups by whether the patient had received a prescription for NSAIDs (including meloxicam, ketorolac, ibuprofen, and naproxen) within 1 month of being diagnosed with a nonoperatively managed scaphoid fracture or within 1 month of surgical fixation. Patients with longstanding NSAID prescriptions prior to injury/surgery were excluded from our analysis. Furthermore, matched cohorts were created, controlling for the following comorbidities and ICD-10 codes: nicotine dependence (F17), obesity (E65-68), alcohol use disorder (F10), diabetes (E08-E13), kidney disease (N17-N19), heart disease (I20-I25), malnutrition (E40-E46).

After creating these groups, we queried the TriNetX database for scaphoid nonunion by the following ICD-10 codes: S62.001K, S62.002K, S62.009K, S62.011K, S62.012K, S62.013K, S62.014K, S62.015K, S62.016K, S62.021K, S62.022K, S62.023K, S62.024K, S62.025K, S62.026K, S62.031K, S62.032K, S62.033K, S62.034K, S62.035K, S62.036K. We also queried for scaphoid nonunion salvage procedures by the following CPT codes: 25440, 25210, 25215, 25820, 25825. We assessed for scaphoid nonunion and scaphoid salvage procedures between 3 months and 2 years after scaphoid fracture diagnosis for the nonsurgical group, and between 3 months and 2 years after scaphoid open reduction and internal fixation for the operative treatment group to ensure that captured interventions represent true salvage procedures secondary to progression to nonunion rather than delayed recovery. Patients with pre-existing scaphoid nonunion or those who had previously undergone salvage procedures were excluded from the outcome analyses.

Categorical variables were compared using chi-square tests, and continuous variables were analyzed with independent-sample t tests. Propensity scores were generated via logistic regression incorporating patient age at injury, race or ethnicity, and relevant comorbidities (including diabetes, malnutrition, nicotine dependence, alcohol use disorder, heart disease, and renal disease). One-to-one greedy matching without replacement was subsequently used to create comparable cohorts for analysis. This matching process successfully eliminated notable differences across baseline covariates. Associations between groups were reported as odds ratios with corresponding 95% confidence intervals, and statistical significance was defined as P < .05 for all analyses.

Results

Unmatched data

After applying the inclusion and exclusion criteria, 96,532 patients with nonoperatively managed scaphoid fractures who did not receive NSAID prescriptions were identified, with a mean age of 44.4 ± 18.7 years. In comparison, 11,630 patients with nonoperatively managed scaphoid fractures who were prescribed NSAIDs were younger, with a mean age of 39.6 ± 16.9 years (Table 1). Among operatively managed patients, those without NSAID prescriptions (n = 5,050) had a mean age of 36.5 ± 17.1 years, whereas those prescribed NSAIDs (n = 1,068) had a mean age of 33.7 ± 14.7 years.

Sex distribution differed across cohorts, with a progressively higher proportion of males observed among patients receiving operative management and NSAID prescriptions. Within this study, the term "sex" refers to a classification, generally male or female, according to the reproductive organs derived from the chromosomal complement. Considerable heterogeneity in racial and ethnic composition was observed within the nonsurgical cohorts, particularly with respect to White, Black, or African American, and Hispanic or Latino patients. In contrast, the operative cohorts demonstrated more uniform racial and ethnic distributions, with the primary difference being a higher proportion of Asian patients among those prescribed NSAIDs.

The prevalence of medical comorbidities also varied across treatment and medication cohorts. Patients managed nonoperatively without NSAIDs exhibited higher rates of diabetes, obesity, and ischemic heart disease compared with NSAID-prescribed and operative cohorts, whereas nicotine dependence and alcohol use disorder were more prevalent among operatively managed patients. Many of these differences reached statistical significance (P < .05).

Matched analysis

Propensity score-matched cohorts were created separately for nonoperatively and operatively managed scaphoid fracture patients to evaluate the association between peri-injury NSAID prescriptions and clinical outcomes (Table 2). After matching, all previously observed notable differences in demographic characteristics and medical comorbidities were eliminated, resulting in well-balanced cohorts across all measured covariates. In the nonsurgical treatment group, 11,629 patients were included in each cohort with and without peri-injury NSAID prescriptions, with a comparable mean age of approximately 40 years in both groups. Similarly, in the operative treatment group, 1,063 patients were included in each cohort, with a mean age of approximately 34 years.

Following matching, patients with nonoperatively managed scaphoid fractures who received peri-injury NSAID prescriptions demonstrated significantly higher incidence of scaphoid nonunion between 3 months and 2 years after injury compared with those who did not receive NSAIDs (4.3% vs 2.6% (OR, 1.7; 95% CI, 1.4–1.9)) (Table 3). Additionally, the incidence of salvage procedures during the same 3-month to 2-year postinjury interval was significantly higher in the NSAID-prescribed nonsurgical cohort (1.3% vs 0.9% (OR, 1.5; 95% CI, 1.2–1.9)). In contrast, among operatively managed scaphoid fracture patients, there were no considerable differences in the incidence of nonunion or salvage procedures between 3 months and 2 years following surgery between patients with and without peri-injury NSAID prescriptions (Table 3).

Discussion

Postoperative pain management is a critical aspect of surgical care, and with increasing efforts to reduce opioid utilization, NSAIDs have played an important role in limiting narcotic consumption.13 However, the use of NSAIDs in the setting of fractures remains controversial because of concerns about impaired bony union, and no consensus currently exists regarding their impact on nonunion risk.13 The purpose of our study was to evaluate the effect of NSAIDs on patients diagnosed with scaphoid fractures.

There have been several studies demonstrating cyclo-oxygenase-2 (COX-2) as being essential to bone healing, raising concerns about the use of NSAIDs in the setting of fractures.19, 20, 21, 22 In addition, George et al19 investigated the risk of nonunion with nonselective NSAIDs, COX-2 inhibitors, and opioids. The authors used private insurance claims data from Optum’s Clinformatics Data Mart database to identify adults with single long-bone fractures or commonly paired long-bone fractures.19 They then investigated the associations of NSAIDs, COX-2 inhibitors, and opioid prescription fills with the risk of nonunion, defined by an inpatient or outpatient diagnosis.19 The study found that filling a prescription of a nonselective NSAID within 30 days of the fracture was not associated with nonunion. In contrast, COX-2 inhibitor prescriptions were associated with increased risk of a nonunion diagnosis plus procedure (adjusted OR, 1.84 [95% CI, 1.38−2.46]) and of a nonunion diagnosis alone (adjusted OR, 1.48 [95% CI, 1.16−1.89]).19 The authors therefore concluded that COX-2 inhibitor prescriptions was associated with greater nonunion risk.19 Interestingly, within the present study, we found that there was an increased incidence of scaphoid nonunion for nonoperatively managed scaphoid fractures with peri-injury NSAID prescription, but this effect was not seen with operatively managed scaphoid fractures. It is possible that mechanical fixation of scaphoid fractures mitigates the potential adverse effects of NSAIDs on bone healing by providing stability at the fracture site. Rigid stabilization may reduce reliance on early biological healing pathways, such as prostaglandin-mediated osteogenesis, that could otherwise be inhibited by NSAID use.

There is a paucity of literature regarding the risk of NSAID use and scaphoid fracture nonunion. Moreover, to the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate this relationship using a large, multicenter database. Dy et al,7 in a retrospective analysis of 8,923 closed scaphoid fractures from the Marketscan database, reported nonunion rates of 10.8% after surgery and 3% after casting, with the highest incidence observed in younger patients. Younger age, male sex, and prior surgical treatment were identified as risk factors, although nonunion was not clinically defined but instead inferred from subsequent surgical procedures, such as nonunion repair or vascularized bone grafting. In contrast, our matched analysis demonstrated a 6.7% incidence of scaphoid nonunion and a 2.9% incidence of salvage procedures in operatively managed fractures without NSAID use. Moreover, with peri-injury NSAID use, we observed a 7.2% nonunion rate and a 2.1% incidence of salvage procedures. Our results highlight the potential influence of NSAID exposure—an effect that was not addressed in previous work with scaphoid fractures and which warrants further investigation given the paucity of literature on the effect of NSAID use with hand fracture management.

Previous research on the effect of NSAIDs on fracture nonunion has been centered on long-bone fractures, rather than fractures of the carpus. Recently, Pereira et al11 conducted a retrospective cohort study using TriNetX to examine the relationship between postoperative ketorolac administration and nonunion in pediatric long-bone fractures. After propensity score matching, the authors included 48,778 patients, and concluded that ketorolac administration was associated with a 2- to 3-fold increase in nonunion risk.23 Moreover, the authors reported that the incidence of nonunion for upper-extremity fractures was 1.49% (521 patients) in the ketorolac group and 0.71% (247 patients) in the nonketorolac group, with a hazard ratio of 2.11 (95% CI, 1.84–2.32; P < .001).23 Specifically, the incidence of radial and/or ulnar nonunion was 1.88% (292 patients) in the ketorolac group and 0.92% (146 patients) in the nonketorolac group (HR, 1.89; 95% CI, 1.65–2.11; P < .001).23 Our study differs from this in that we used peri-injury NSAID prescriptions after scaphoid nonunion as a proxy for NSAID use. However, we were able to demonstrate that nonoperatively managed scaphoid fractures had notably increased incidence of nonunion and salvage procedures with peri-injury NSAID prescriptions after controlling for age, sex, diabetes, obesity, nicotine dependence, and alcohol use disorder.

As with all retrospective database studies, our analysis has limitations. Our work relies on accurate coding, and we cannot account for errors in documentation. Second, NSAID exposure was assessed using prescription fill data, but we could not assess adherence on an individualized level.19 Variations in compliance with postoperative/postinjury NSAID regimens have the potential to confound results in our NSAID-using cohorts. Moreover, over-the-counter NSAID use was also not captured, which may bias results toward the null.19 In addition, because the TriNetX database does not support adjustments for multiple comparisons, such as Bonferroni correction, we were unable to perform subanalyzes evaluating the effects of individual NSAIDs on fracture healing. Accordingly, this study focuses on the impact of a group of nonselective NSAIDs in the treatment of scaphoid fractures, and further investigation may be warranted to evaluate the effects of individual NSAIDs on scaphoid healing. Similarly, we did not assess whether other components of a multimodal pain regimen (eg, opioids) may have independent deleterious effects on fracture union. Finally, radiographs and detailed clinical notes were not available for review in this large database study; therefore, clinical confirmation of reported nonunions was not possible.11

In conclusion, this study is clinically relevant as pain management strategies increasingly shift away from opioids because of their potential for addiction.15 However, there has been controversy regarding the effect of NSAIDs on bone healing because of their association with cyclo-oxygenase 2 inhibition and their associated risks of fracture nonunion or delayed union.15,20,22 For patients with nonoperatively managed scaphoid fractures, patients with a prescription for NSAIDs within 1 month of diagnosis demonstrated an approximate 2-fold increase in the risk of nonunion and subsequent salvage procedures. This association was not seen in operatively managed patients with perioperative prescribed NSAIDs, suggesting that mechanical fixation may offset the potential adverse effects of NSAIDs on bone healing. NSAIDs have become an important part of postoperative/postinjury pain regimens, and this study highlights a potential risk of increased scaphoid fracture nonunion for patients treated nonoperatively with recent NSAID prescriptions. This data may be used in patient counseling when deciding on NSAID use in the early postinjury period after scaphoid fractures, especially when nonsurgical management is planned.
