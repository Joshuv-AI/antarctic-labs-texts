Knowledge, Attitudes, and Practices of Pediatric Nurses in Dubai Regarding Oral Health Care: A Cross-sectional Study
Authors: Aisha Almemari, Manal Al Halabi, Iyad Hussein, Anas Salami, Amar Hassan Khamis, Mawlood Kowash
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOral health care is a crucial part of nursing for pediatric patients. General pediatric nurses need appropriate knowledge to provide effective preventive care, yet oral health is often overlooked and insufficiently integrated into daily practice.AimTo assess the oral health knowledge, attitudes, and practices of nurses caring for pediatric patients in Dubai.Materials and methodsA cross-sectional study using a validated questionnaire gathered socio-demographic data and assessed pediatric dental knowledge and attitudes. A 5-point Likert scale evaluated opinions on preventing children's oral diseases and willingness to pursue further oral health education. Knowledge and attitude scores were calculated; Mann–Whitney U and Kruskal–Wallis tests assessed associations.ResultsAmong 125 nurses, the mean knowledge score was 69.5%. Female nurses and those over 30 years had significantly higher scores (p < 0.05). Most nurses performed oral care screenings for hospitalized children, but over half rarely assessed orthodontic appliances or preventive programs for children with special health care needs. Only 33.6% recognized xylitol's anticaries role, and 63.2% knew the recommended age for a first dental visit. Despite 57.6% perceiving nursing-led oral care as ineffective, 86.9% requested hands-on training, and 84.2% sought further education.ConclusionPediatric nurses in Dubai demonstrate adequate theoretical oral health knowledge but notable practice gaps. Structured training programs and integration of oral health into pediatric nursing protocols could enhance preventive practices, strengthen early detection and referral, and help reduce the high prevalence of early childhood caries in the UAE.Keywords: Attitude of health personnel, Early childhood caries, Healthcare delivery, Oral health care, Pediatric nursing

Introduction

Dental caries is a progressive and irreversible disease that affects the hard tissues of the teeth.1 It poses a significant public health concern for children across the Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC) countries.2 In the United Arab Emirates (UAE), the situation is particularly alarming—about 76.1% of 5–6-year-old children are affected, with an average decayed, missing, and filled surfaces (DMFS) score of 10.2.3 This high prevalence is linked to a range of adverse outcomes, including physical pain, sleep disturbances, poor nutrition, missed school days, and broader social and economic consequences.4 Despite ongoing public health efforts, the UAE's dental caries rate remains persistently high.2,3

Oral health is a vital part of overall health. Children with systemic illnesses are especially vulnerable to dental caries and often have poorer oral hygiene compared to their healthy peers.5 If left unaddressed, poor oral health can contribute to a decline in general health, underscoring the importance of instilling good oral hygiene and healthy eating habits early on.5 Interestingly, young children tend to visit pediatric medical clinics more frequently than dental clinics.6,7 This makes it crucial for medical professionals who care for children to be well-informed about pediatric oral health as part of holistic care—yet, this awareness is often lacking.8

Pediatric nurses are uniquely positioned to make a difference. They frequently interact with children and their parents—especially mothers—and can play a key role in promoting oral health.9 To do so effectively, nurses should have the proper knowledge about oral hygiene, preventive practices, and early intervention strategies.10 They should also be able to identify children at risk for poor oral health, offer tailored advice, and refer them to dental professionals when needed.10,11 Additionally, pediatric nurses must be aware of the serious oral health consequences that can result from child abuse and neglect.12

While previous studies have looked into pediatricians' knowledge of oral health in the UAE,8 there is a gap in understanding how nurses—particularly those in Dubai—perceive and approach oral disease prevention in children. There is also limited insight into their willingness to pursue further education and training. This study aims to fill that gap by evaluating pediatric nurses' knowledge, practices, and attitudes in Dubai, focusing on their role in preventing oral diseases and their interest in enhancing their expertise in pediatric oral health.

Materials and Methods

This study received ethical approval from the Research Ethics Review Committee at Mohammed Bin Rashid University of Medicine and Health Sciences (MBRU IRB-2021-88). An implied/electronic informed consent was obtained.

Study Design

It is a prospective questionnaire-based study was carried out across pediatric hospitals and clinics in Dubai, including both government and private healthcare settings. Data were collected through an online survey targeting nurses who care for pediatric patients. The study adhered to the STROBE guidelines for reporting observational research.13

The survey was distributed electronically using Microsoft Forms®. It was designed to take approximately 7 minutes to complete. To encourage participation, a reminder email was sent 2 weeks after the initial invitation. To prevent duplicate responses, participants were required to enter their email addresses and could only submit the survey once.

Sample Size Calculation

To determine the appropriate sample size, we assumed that 50% of the target population would exhibit the factor of interest. Given an estimated population of 2,800 pediatric nurses in Dubai, a minimum of 93 participants was required. The study was conducted from April 2023 to February 2024. This sample size allows for a 10% margin of error with 95% confidence, ensuring reliable estimates.

Data Collection

Permission was obtained to use a previously validated and piloted questionnaire developed in Saudi Arabia.10 The survey consisted of 45 questions divided into five key sections:

Demographic details: 11 multiple-choice questions covering gender, age, practice setting, and years of experience.

Knowledge: 12 multiple-choice questions assessing basic pediatric dental knowledge.

Knowledge: 12 multiple-choice questions assessing basic pediatric dental knowledge.

Attitudes: Six statements rated on a 5-point Likert scale, exploring nurses' roles in preventing oral diseases and their willingness to pursue further education.

Oral screening and referral: 14 questions on the frequency of oral cavity screening for various purposes (rated on a Likert scale: never, rarely, sometimes, always), along with nine single-best-answer questions.

Provision of dental care and prevention: Six multiple-choice questions.

Attitude toward oral health and further education: Seven multiple-choice questions.

Statistical Analysis

Scores for knowledge, practice, and attitudes were calculated by assigning 1 point for each correct answer and 0 for incorrect ones. Based on total scores, responses were categorized as follows: fair: 50–59%; adequate: 60–64%; good: 65–69%; excellent: Above 70%

All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 22. Descriptive statistics were presented as means and standard deviations. Since the data did not follow a normal distribution, nonparametric tests—Mann–Whitney U and Kruskal–Wallis—were used. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Despite this positive outlook, 57.6% of nurses felt that oral care provided by nursing staff was not particularly effective. Nevertheless, there was a clear willingness to improve: 84.16% were open to gaining more knowledge, and 86.88% expressed interest in receiving hands-on training in pediatric oral health.

Attitudes were generally consistent across different demographic groups. However, a statistically significant relationship was found between nurses' willingness to receive oral health education and their practice setting (p = 0.015).

Discussion

The World Health Organization has long emphasized the importance of integrating oral health into primary healthcare systems, recognizing that oral diseases share risk factors with major noncommunicable conditions.14 This integrated approach is especially vital for children, where early preventive efforts can lead to long-term health benefits. Pediatricians and nurses—who frequently interact with children during routine checkups, vaccinations, and acute care visits—are ideally positioned to deliver early oral health education. These regular touchpoints with parents offer valuable opportunities to promote preventive practices such as fluoride use, dietary guidance, and establishing a dental home by the age of one.

The American Academy of Pediatric Dentistry (AAPD) supports this shared responsibility among primary care providers.15 In the UAE, research has shown that pediatricians with better oral health knowledge are more likely to offer preventive advice and make timely referrals.8 Nurses, who often serve as the first point of contact for children and their families—especially mothers, who play a central role in shaping children's oral health habits—have similar potential. Our findings highlight this untapped opportunity to reduce the high prevalence of early childhood caries (ECC) in the UAE.3 Previous studies also emphasize the influence nurses have during pediatric visits, particularly in promoting oral health behaviors within communities.6

The predominance of female nurses in our sample (90.4%) reflects broader workforce trends in the region, as reported by Al-Jobair et al.10 and Khanagar et al.16 Most participants worked in government hospitals, which typically serve more diverse and larger populations than private facilities. This suggests that public healthcare institutions are strategic settings for implementing oral health initiatives with wide-reaching public health impact. Additionally, since 72% of nurses cared for short-stay patients, there is a clear opportunity to incorporate oral health assessments into routine admission procedures.

While overall knowledge levels were adequate (69.5%), specific gaps were evident. For instance, only one-third of nurses recognized the anticaries benefits of xylitol, and just 63.2% correctly identified the recommended age for a child's first dental visit—falling short of the universal recommendation by the AAPD.15 These findings are consistent with global research pointing to inconsistent oral health education in nursing programs.17–19 Interestingly, while 88% of nurses understood the link between systemic health and oral conditions, practical knowledge—such as when children can begin brushing independently—was less robust (only 39.2% answered correctly). This disconnect between theoretical understanding and practical application underscores the need for experiential learning opportunities.

Although 60% of nurses reported receiving oral health education after graduation, the training appeared fragmented. Only 59.2% recalled formal instruction, echoing Ibrahim et al.'s call for structured, mandatory workshops.20 Recent evidence from Oladayo et al. supports this approach, showing that competency-based training for nurses and community health workers significantly improves both knowledge and referral practices.21

Encouragingly, nurses in this study showed strong motivation to improve their skills. Nearly 87% expressed interest in hands-on training, and over 84% were eager to pursue further education. This contrasts with their self-assessment (57.6%), which felt that nursing-led oral care was ineffective, suggesting a clear awareness of existing skill gaps. Moreover, 90.4% acknowledged their role in preventing oral diseases, a figure notably higher than the 72% reported by Rabiei et al.22 Unlike findings from Saudi Arabia23 and the United States,24 most nurses in our study routinely performed oral assessments. This difference may reflect evolving protocols in the UAE or individual initiative, highlighting the potential to standardize oral screening practices across healthcare settings.

Limitations

This study has limitations. First, as with many survey-based designs, there is a risk of selection bias—participants may represent nurses who are more engaged or interested in oral health, which could limit the generalizability of the findings. Second, recall bias may have influenced responses, particularly regarding past education, training, and clinical practices. Lastly, the survey did not explore the sources of oral health education or nurses' self-perceived knowledge levels, which could have provided deeper insight into the gap between perceived and actual competence.

Conclusion and Recommendations

This study found that female nurses and those aged over 30 had significantly better oral health knowledge. While most nurses routinely perform oral care screenings for hospitalized children, many do not assess orthodontic appliances or special healthcare needs, nor do they consistently participate in preventive oral health programs.

To address these gaps and improve pediatric oral health outcomes in the UAE, we recommend the following:

Targeted education: Provide structured training for pediatric nurses based on AAPD/AAP guidelines, covering topics such as the timing of dental visits, caries risk factors, and prevention strategies to enhance awareness and encourage timely referrals.
