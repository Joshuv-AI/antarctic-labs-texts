Quantitative Electroencephalography for Predication of Neurological Dysfunction in Type A Aortic Dissection: A Prospective Observational Study
Authors: Ya‐peng Wang, Yong‐qing Cheng, Hanghang Wang, Huanhuan Wang, Wen‐xue Liu, Yi Jiang, Yun‐xing Xue, Yang Chen, Qing Zhou, Xuan Luo, Qingxiu Zhang, Jason Zhensheng Qu, Dong‐jin Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundType A aortic dissection presents challenges with postoperative cerebral complications, and this study evaluates the predictive value of quantitative electroencephalography for perioperative brain function prognosis.Methods and ResultsAmplitude‐integrated electroencephalography (aEEG) processes raw signals through filtering, amplitude integration, and time compression, displaying the data in a semilogarithmic format. Using this method, postoperative relative band power (post‐RBP) α% and dynamic aEEG (ΔaEEG) grade were significantly associated with neurological dysfunction in univariate and multivariable analyses, with area under the receiver operating characteristic curve of 0.876 (95% CI, 0.825–0.926) for the combined model. Postoperative relative band power α% and ΔaEEG were significantly associated with adverse outcomes, with area under the receiver operating characteristic curve of 0.903 (95% CI, 0.835–0.971) for the combined model. Postoperative relative band power α% and ΔaEEG were significantly associated with transient neurological dysfunction and stroke, with areas under the receiver operating characteristic curve of 0.818 (95% CI, 0.760–0.876) and 0.868 (95% CI, 0.810–0.926) for transient neurological dysfunction, and 0.815 (95% CI, 0.743–0.886) and 0.831 (95% CI, 0.746–0.916) for stroke. Among 56 patients, the Alberta Stroke Program Early Computed Tomography score was superior to ΔaEEG in predicting neurological outcomes (area under the receiver operating characteristic curve of 0.872 versus 0.708 [95% CI, 0.633–0.783]; P<0.05).ConclusionsPerioperative quantitative electroencephalography monitoring offers valuable insights into brain function changes in patients with type A aortic dissection. ∆aEEG grades can aid in early detection of adverse outcomes, while postoperative relative band power and ∆aEEG grades predict transient neurological dysfunction. What Are the Clinical Implications?

Quantitative electroencephalography provides cardiac surgeons with a noninvasive, effective tool for timely identification of brain complications in patients with type A aortic dissection, enhancing patient care during the critical perioperative period.

Quantitative electroencephalography provides cardiac surgeons with a noninvasive, effective tool for timely identification of brain complications in patients with type A aortic dissection, enhancing patient care during the critical perioperative period.

Type A aortic dissection (TAAD) is a challenging condition for cardiac surgeons, despite significant advances in medical technology over the past 50 years. Deep hypothermic circulatory arrest is currently the most common brain protection technique in surgical intervention for TAAD. However, postoperative cerebral complications remain a major concern. 1 The long‐term survival rate is reduced by the incidence of postoperative permanent neurological dysfunction (PND), which ranges from 10% to 30%. 2 , 3 , 4 Furthermore, transient neurological dysfunction (TND) including postoperative delirium can affect a considerable proportion of patients, with reported incidence rates of up to 40%, 5 , 6 , 7 increasing the length of stay in the intensive care unit and the total cost of hospitalization. 6 Studies suggested that postoperative delirium may lead to long‐term cognitive decline, particularly in an aging population. 8 , 9 , 10

Although most studies have focused on identifying pre‐ and intraoperative risk factors in patients with TAAD, 1 , 11 , 12 , 13 few have directly investigated changes in brain function during the perioperative period. While intraoperative cerebral oxygen monitoring of TAAD has become commonplace, its sensitivity and specificity in diagnosing postoperative neurological dysfunction are lacking. 14 In comparison, the electroencephalogram is highly sensitive to cortical ischemia and brain dysfunction, enabling the detection of focal or widespread brain dysfunction at an early stage. 15 , 16 However, interpreting electroencephalography poses challenges, making it very difficult to implement in the field of cardiac surgery.

Due to its ease of use and ability to directly reflect neuronal activity, quantitative electroencephalography (QEEG) is a valuable tool for nonneurologists to accurately diagnose and treat brain function disorders. QEEG comprises 2 key parameters: the amplitude‐integrated electroencephalogram (aEEG), which represents the overall integration background, and the relative band power, which reflects the relative proportions of α, β, θ, and δ waves. The aEEG has proven useful in assessing the neurological prognosis of infants undergoing congenital heart surgery and experiencing cardiac arrest. 17 , 18 , 19 In perioperative patients, increases in θ and δ waves, and decreases in α waves are closely associated with TND and cognitive impairment. 20 , 21 , 22

The potential predictive value of QEEG on brain function prognosis in patients with TAAD remains unknown. To address this gap in knowledge, this study proposes the use of QEEG to monitor brain function in patients with TAAD during the perioperative period for cardiac surgeons. These results could have significant implications for the diagnosis, treatment, and prognosis of brain function disorders in patients with TAAD.

METHODS

Data Availability Statement

The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Ethics Approval and Consent to Participate

The Institutional Review Board of Nanjing Drum Tower Hospital approved the study and protocol (approval number: 2022‐034‐01). The trial was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, the Ethical Review Measures for Biomedical Research Involving Humans of the National Health Commission of China, and other relevant national laws and regulations. Informed consent by the study participant or a legally authorized representative was given before inclusion in the study.

Study Design, Setting, and Participants

This was a prospective observational study conducted at the Department of Cardiac Surgery of Nanjing Drum Tower Hospital of the Affiliated Hospital of Nanjing University Medical School. The trial procedures were carried out between February 2022 and January 2023. Before the study, institutional review board approval was obtained from the Nanjing Drum Tower Hospital of the Affiliated Hospital of Nanjing University Medical School, and the study was registered on the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2200055980).

We informed the families of all potential risks and provided information sheets to each patient, and written informed consent was obtained before study participation. Our study included patients aged >18 years who underwent open surgery under general anesthesia for TAAD. We excluded patients who had a clear diagnosis of malignancy with a life expectancy of <6 months, those with a history of cerebral infarction, pregnancy, or psychiatric illness within the past 6 months, and those with other serious unrelated diseases that could affect their life expectancy. Additionally, we did not include subjects who did not undergo aortic computed tomography angiography or other relevant examinations. These measures were taken to maintain the robustness and validity of our research findings. A total of 220 patients were enrolled in our study (Figure 1).

Surgical Procedures

During surgical procedures involving the aortic arch, combined intravenous and inhalation anesthesia were adopted, and the arterial blood pressure was continuously monitored. In addition, a transesophageal echocardiography probe was routinely inserted, except in cases with contraindications. Open surgical procedures employed a midline sternotomy approach, and cardiopulmonary bypass was established via cannulation of the selected artery after systemic heparinization. After completing the distal aortic arch manipulation, the core temperature recovery process was initiated, and aortic root lesions were dealt with during the rewarming phase. Only patients with severe aortic valve damage required aortic valve replacement, while most patients were able to manage aortic root lesions through surgical repair (see Wang et al 23 and Pan et al 24 for specific methods).

Intraoperative Brain Protection and Monitoring

During surgical procedures involving the aortic arch, it is critical to ensure adequate brain oxygenation. To this end, various cerebral perfusion strategies are used, including anterograde cerebral perfusion, retrograde cerebral perfusion, and deep hypothermic circulatory arrest. Anterograde cerebral perfusion is achieved via the cannulation of the axillary or innominate/carotid artery, with an initial flow rate of 3 to 5 mL/kg per min, which is adjusted on the basis of near‐infrared spectroscopy readings. Retrograde cerebral perfusion, on the other hand, involves retrograde infusion via the superior vena cava and is carefully monitored to maintain venous pressure below 25 mm Hg. The effectiveness of cerebral perfusion is monitored using near‐infrared spectroscopy, with additional measures necessary when the near‐infrared spectroscopy value falls below 20% of the baseline or drops below 50%. The duration of circulatory arrest and the target temperature for cerebral protection are selected on the basis of the specific surgical approach, with a range of 20 to 24 °C and ≥25 °C used for the different procedures.

QEEG Recording

Brain activity was monitored in each participant by bedside QEEG using a brain function instrument (Nicolet Monitor, NicoletOne 5.9.4; Natus Neurology Incorporated, Middleton, WI). Four recording electrodes in the right frontal, left frontal, right parietal, and left parietal positions were revealed with ground and reference electrodes in the midline, as defined by the international 10 to 20 electroencephalography system. 25 Raw data from each hemisphere was subjected to filtering and compression using computer‐generated algorithms implemented in the data recording device (Figure 2). The quality of the recorded data was evaluated using strict criteria: Electrode impedance was required to be below 10 kΩ, raw data were checked for motion artifacts and electroencephalography interference, and potential interference from diathermy or other electrical equipment was ruled out.

During the perioperative period, the collection of QEEG would be divided into 3 phases. Phase 1 involved collecting an aEEG 1 hour before surgery. In the second phase (Figure 3), an intraoperative aEEG would be collected during 5 specific time periods: (A) commencement of anesthesia, (B) onset of hypothermia and maintenance of target low temperature, (C) start of selective cerebral perfusion, (D) initiation of rewarming from hypothermia, and (E) rewarming to 36.5 °C (Figure 3). In phase 3, postoperative QEEG would be collected for 2 hours in the cardiac care unit without the use of sedative analgesic drugs.

An experienced physician specializing in QEEG interpretation, who was blind to the patients' clinical information, analyzed all QEEG recordings offline. The classification of aEEG activity was based on the previously described system, which classified background patterns as continuous normal voltage (CNV), discontinuous normal voltage, low voltage, flat trace, and burst suppression. 26 , 27 , 28 Suspicious seizures identified on amplitude integration components were verified on the original electroencephalogram and reviewed by a second blind reviewer. The aEEG background patterns were classified into the following categories by using the voltage method: CNV, discontinuous normal voltage, low voltage, flat trace, and burst suppression (Figure S1). Dynamic aEEG (ΔaEEG) grade was defined as the trend of aEEG changes from stage D to stage E of phase 2 to phase 3. According to prognostic studies of aEEG in the context of hypothermia during cardiac arrest and neonatal heart surgery, we have classified ΔaEEG into 3 distinct categories (Figure S1). 17 , 18 , 19 , 26 The dominant patterns of predefined phases were used to classify the background patterns (as illustrated in Figure S1 and Table 1). Notably, QEEG signals obtained during this prospective observational trial were used for research purposes only, as they did not influence any medical decision‐making during the perioperative period.

Definition of End Points

The primary end point of our study was neurological dysfunction (ND), which encompassed adverse outcomes (AOs) and TND. AO was defined as death occurring during the intraoperative period or hospitalization, as well as the presence of PND. PND was identified by the manifestation of nerve dysfunction at discharge, including localized injuries such as poststroke complications or systemic injuries such as coma. Continuous cerebral function assessment was conducted specifically for patients with PND by QEEG. TND was evaluated in surgical survivors without PND and defined as postoperative delirium, diagnosed using the Confusion Assessment Method for the Intensive Care Unit 29 and assessed twice daily for 7 consecutive days. The diagnosis of TND was jointly completed by neurologists and cardiothoracic intensive care unit physicians. Patients who regained consciousness were evaluated for decannulation and neurology using the cerebral performance categories. Cardiac surgeons conducted a follow‐up evaluation 30 days after discharge, where a cerebral performance category score of 1 to 2 was considered a good neurological outcome, and a cerebral performance category of 3 to 5 indicated a poor outcome. The secondary end point was cerebral infarct. For patients who remained unconscious for >6 hours postoperatively and were suspected of having a stroke, provided the vital signs were stable, a brain computed tomography (CT) scan was conducted. These scans were then assessed by neurologists and radiologists using the Alberta Stroke Program Early CT (ASPECT) score. 30

Statistical Analysis

The sample size calculation was performed using Power Analysis and Sample Size software version 15.0 (NCSS Statistical Software, Kaysville, UT). A comprehensive review indicated that the prevalence of ND following surgery for TAAD ranges from 32.5% to 52.0%. 31 In this study, a conservative estimate of 45% prevalence was adopted to ensure more precise estimates. 32 A sample of 200 patients achieves 80% power to detect a difference of 0.1000 between the area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve under the null hypothesis of 0.7500 and an area under the curve (AUC) under the alternative hypothesis of 0.8500 using a 2‐sided z test at a significance level of 0.050. 33 , 34 The data are discrete (rating scale) responses. The AUC is computed between false‐positive rates of 0.00 and 1.00. The ratio of the SD of the responses in the negative group to the SD of the responses in the positive group is 1.00. The sample size was then increased by 10% to account for potential shedding and other factors, resulting in a final sample size of 220 patients.

The perioperative cerebral function data of these 220 patients were analyzed in 2 steps. First, dynamic trend changes in intraoperative aEEG and postoperative 2‐hour aEEG were graded to predict postoperative ND in patients with TAAD. Second, TND was predicted on the basis of the relative band power (RBP) of the 2‐hour postoperative QEEG.

Continuous variables were assessed for normality using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Normally distributed continuous variables were reported as mean ± SD and analyzed using Student's t test, while nonnormally distributed variables were reported as median with interquartile ranges (quartile 1–quartile 3) and analyzed using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages (n [%]) and analyzed using the χ2 test or Fisher's exact test. All statistical analyses were 2‐tailed, and a P value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Logistic regression analysis was used to evaluate the relationship between different variables and the outcome, calculating odds ratios (ORs) and 95% CIs. Univariate binary logistic regression analysis was performed to evaluate different variables and calculate the ORs and 95% CIs. Continuous variables that did not follow a normal distribution were log‐transformed with the natural logarithm e as the base. QEEG variables with a P value <0.05 were then included in the stepwise (forward: conditional) multivariable logistic regression analysis model. The performance of our model was evaluated through internal validation using 1000 bootstrap resamples. Model validation was assessed in terms of both discrimination and calibration. Discrimination was measured by the C‐statistic, which is equivalent to AUC under the ROC curve. Calibration was assessed by drawing calibration curves and calculating the Brier score using the formula (Y–P), 2 where Y represents the actual observed outcome of the dependent variable, and P represents the predicted probability given by our model. Delong's test was applied to compare the AUC between different models. 35 Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 25.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY) and R version 4.2.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Statistical significance was considered when P<0.05.

Results

Patient Characteristics and Neurological Outcomes

During the study period from February 2022 to January 2023, a total of 220 patients were included in our analysis. The patient selection process is outlined in Figure 1. The overall postoperative ND rate was 48.6%, with 15% being AO and 39.6% being TND. Table 2 highlights several significant differences between the ND and non‐ND groups. The ND group had a higher proportion of elderly patients (≥60 years) compared with the non‐ND group (35.5% versus 21.2%; P=0.028). Additionally, there were more emergent operations in the ND group (P=0.025) and a higher incidence of hemopericardium (25.2% versus 9.7%; P=0.004). Patients in the ND group also underwent more coronary artery bypass grafting procedures (7.5% versus 0.9%; P=0.033) and had longer cardiopulmonary bypass (median, 190.00 versus 172.00 minutes; P=0.001) and cross‐clamp times (median, 139.00 versus 126.00 minutes; P=0.041). Postoperative assessments showed that the ND group had higher postoperative relative band power (post‐RBP) δ% values (median, 74.89 versus 66.32; P<0.001) and significantly lower post‐RBP α% and β% values (P<0.001 for both). The dynamic amplitude‐integrated electroencephalogram (ΔaEEG) revealed more severe injuries in the ND group (P<0.001). Furthermore, the ND group experienced longer stays in the cardiac care unit (median, 7.00 versus 4.00 days; P<0.001) and overall hospital stays (median, 19.00 versus 15.00 days; P=0.005). Finally, neurological outcomes were worse in the ND group, with fewer patients achieving a good cerebral performance category score (81.3% versus 100%; P<0.001). Predictive Value of QEEG for Postoperative ND

Table 3 presents the results of univariate and multivariable linear regression analyses for ND. Post‐RBP δ% showed a significant association with ND in the univariate analysis (OR, 1.056 [95% CI, 1.030–1.085]; P<0.001), but it was not included in the multivariable analysis. Post‐RBP θ% was not significantly associated with ND in the univariate analysis (OR, 1.032 [95% CI, 0.994–1.073]; P=0.100) and was also excluded from the multivariable model. In contrast, post‐RBP α% demonstrated a significant association with ND both in univariate (OR, 0.133 [95% CI, 0.070–0.250]; P<0.001) and multivariable analyses (OR, 0.291 [95% CI, 0.143–0.559]; P<0.001). Similarly, post‐RBP β% was significantly associated with ND in the univariate analysis (OR, 0.427 [95% CI, 0.282–0.646]; P<0.001), but it was not retained in the multivariable analysis. Finally, ΔaEEG emerged as a strong and significant predictor of ND in both univariate (OR, 18.828 [95% CI, 9.753–38.708]; P<0.001) and multivariable analyses (OR, 11.523 [95% CI, 5.386–25.701]; P<0.001).

Discussion

With the continuous advancement of surgical and monitoring technologies, the incidence of postoperative neurological injury following TAAD remains unchanged despite the use of cerebral protection techniques, such as hypothermia, cerebral perfusion, and blood gas management. Currently, the diagnosis of postoperative PND is still dependent on brain CT in clinical practice. However, for TAAD ‐related AOs, the risk of examination is often high due to hemodynamic instability and the inability to conduct continuous monitoring of the nervous system. In this study, we report, for the first time, the use of perioperative QEEG monitoring to assess nerve function recovery in this population. First, during the operation, 15% of patients experienced severe brain damage related to AOs after deep hypothermic circulatory arrest, and 39.6% of patients experienced mild brain damage related to TND after the surgery. Second, failure to recover CNV in aEEG within 2 hours postoperatively was associated with AOs. Third, the postoperative RBP and ΔaEEG can improve the sensitivity and accuracy for predicting TND after surgery.

Using ∆aEEG Grades for Predicting AOs

For ∆aEEG grade III, within 2 hours after surgery, the aEEG fails to recover to CNV or transitions from CNV to discontinuous normal voltage, indicating cortical brain damage (Figure 5). Using ROC analysis of aEEG trend changes, we obtained an AUC of 0.804, which can be used to predict AOs following surgery. Potential causes of AOs include intraoperative hypotension leading to inadequate cerebral perfusion and cortical ischemia, resulting in low voltage and flat trace on aEEG. Furthermore, we also consider incomplete the circle of Willis (CoW), embolic detachment during surgery, or cerebral hemorrhage as causes of brain injury.

The CoW comprises the bilateral terminal branches of the internal carotid arteries, the A1 segment of the anterior cerebral artery, and the anterior communicating artery in its anterior component. The posterior component encompasses the posterior communicating arteries and the P1 segment of the posterior cerebral artery. The presence of collateral blood flow within the CoW plays a crucial role in maintaining adequate cerebral perfusion and mitigating the risk of ischemia in the presence of arterial pathology. It is worth noting that considerable morphological variations exist in cerebral arterial circulation within the general population, with a prevalence ranging from 55% to 80%. 36 , 37 , 38 , 39 In Figure S2, different variations of the CoW can result in hemodynamic changes during selective cerebral perfusion, ultimately leading to ischemia of the contralateral cerebral cortex and ischemic stroke. 40

In our study, although continuous quantitative brain function assessment allows for early detection of cerebral damage within 2 hours postoperatively, it cannot replace CT scans of the brain. However, for patients with TAAD undergoing aortic repair surgery, frequent CT scanning is impractical and increases the risk of patient transportation. Therefore, bedside continuous quantitative brain function monitoring provides real‐time information on cerebral functional changes, aiding clinicians in the early detection of brain injury and allowing for timely intervention by neurointerventional teams or initiation of neuroprotective treatments, as time is crucial for the viability of brain cells.

In our study, ΔaEEG assessments were conducted within 2 hours postoperatively, while head CT scans, used to evaluate the ASPECT score, were performed 6 hours postoperatively for patients with TAAD who remained unresponsive, as noted in our Methods section. This timing difference suggests that ΔaEEG may have an advantage in the earlier detection of brain strokes, particularly in patients with cortical damage. Additionally, stroke was diagnosed using head CT scans performed 6 hours postoperatively, and our analyses demonstrated that ΔaEEG, assessed within 2 hours postoperatively, was significantly associated with stroke in both univariate and multivariable analyses, with an AUC of 0.815. This indicates that ΔaEEG can detect early brain electrical activity changes associated with stroke, which were later confirmed by head CT. Furthermore, although the ASPECT score derived from head CT performed better in predicting neurological outcomes, ΔaEEG and ASPECT showed comparable performance in predicting adverse outcomes, with no statistically significant difference between them. These findings collectively support our conclusion that ΔaEEG may detect stroke up to 4 hours earlier than head CT in the perioperative setting, especially in patients with cortical damage, providing critical time for early intervention and potentially improving patient outcomes.

Continuous Monitoring of Post‐RBP to Predict Neurological Prognosis in Patients With PND

The use of RBP provides insights into the dynamic changes of electroencephalography frequencies, particularly in the context of acute focal cerebral ischemia. 41 It allows for the identification of affected brain regions on the basis of different lead representations and facilitates the evaluation of disease progression through decreasing RBP values in α and β waves, accompanied by increasing RBP values in θ and δ waves. 42 , 43 Additionally, RBP analysis proves valuable in monitoring the sleep–wake cycles of patients with postoperative disorders of consciousness, aiding in the prediction of neurological outcomes. 44

In the case of patients with postoperative stroke secondary to TAAD, we observed 33 individuals with coma, among whom 22 patients displayed a sleep–wake cycle (Figure S3), and all of these patients successfully regained consciousness within 3 months after surgery. However, to comprehensively evaluate and devise recovery strategies for overall limb function, it is essential to consider the severity of functional area involvement resulting from brain damage. The extent of brain injury correlates closely with the affected functional regions, consequently influencing the recovery of limb function in patients. Thus, a comprehensive assessment and rehabilitation plan for limb function necessitate the integration of both the severity of brain damage and the corresponding functional areas involved.

Predictive Value of QEEG for Postoperative TND

For ∆aEEG II, in patients undergoing deep hypothermic circulatory arrest, the aEEG remains devoid of CNV until the completion of the surgical procedure. However, it is only after a 2‐hour period of postoperative monitoring in the intensive care unit that the aEEG finally exhibits CNV. Remarkably, patients belonging to this group exhibit a 71.62% probability of experiencing postoperative TND.

Significantly, among the group of patients undergoing unilateral ASCP, approximately half of them showed slower recovery of left‐sided aEEG (Figure 7) when compared with recovery in the right hemisphere. However, these patients eventually achieved normal cerebral neurological function within 2 hours after the operation. Importantly, this subgroup of patients had a higher occurrence of TND at 55.3%. It is plausible to consider that incomplete CoW might contribute to the asymmetrical restoration of cerebral blood flow between the left and right hemispheres. Nonetheless, it should be noted that confirmation of the completeness of the CoW has not been obtained through cerebral vascular computed tomography angiography.

The RBP allows for the identification of affected brain regions on the basis of different lead representations and facilitates the evaluation of disease progression through decreasing RBP values in α and β waves, accompanied by increasing RBP values in θ and δ waves. 42 , 43 , 45 However, in the context of combined indicators, we found that ∆aEEG grades and post‐RBP α% hold greater significance in predicting postoperative TND. The combined predictive AUC value of these 2 indicators reached an impressive 0.8902. Our findings underscore that the comprehensive consideration of ∆aEEG grades and post‐RBP α% allows for a more accurate prediction of the risk of postoperative TND.

Comparing ΔaEEG and ASPECT Scores for Predicting AOs and Neurological Outcomes

In our study, we observed that perioperative ΔaEEG grades can be used for early predictions about AOs, showing diagnostic efficacy comparable with the ASPECT score obtained from brain CT scans. However, ΔaEEG falls short in predicting overall neurological functional prognosis compared with ASPECT scoring. This is primarily due to ΔaEEG's technical limitations in providing detailed brain structure and local blood flow information, its focus on superficial brain functions, and the differing time windows of assessment, with ΔaEEG being assessed before the more comprehensive ASPECT score. Additionally, ΔaEEG and ASPECT scoring may reflect different aspects of brain function and structure, with ASPECT scoring offering a more direct and accurate visual representation of brain tissue damage. These conclusions, however, are based on specific research designs and sample sizes and may have inherent limitations. Despite these, bedside QEEG monitoring offers significant advantages, including real‐time and continuous monitoring capabilities, noninvasive nature, ease of use, applicability in specific patient scenarios, and cost effectiveness. 46 , 47 These benefits make QEEG an indispensable tool in comprehensive postoperative neurological monitoring. Furthermore, our approach has advanced the early detection window for perioperative brain strokes by at least 4 hours, providing more time for treatment and potentially better outcomes. Bedside QEEG's continuous monitoring also enables timely adjustments in treatment plans and facilitates communication with patients' families.

Limitations

However, several limitations and issues remain to be addressed in our study. First, the sample size was insufficient. Although we calculated the sample size on the basis of the overall incidence of neurological events following TAAD surgery, the incidence of adverse outcomes postoperatively was ≈15%, indicating an inadequate sample size for this subgroup. Second, QEEG is primarily designed to monitor cortical brain function, and its sensitivity is relatively poor in detecting brain injuries that do not involve the cortex, as well as those in the cerebellum and brainstem. Third, the differences in brain electrical activity among individuals of different ages could lead to variations in QEEG measurements, necessitating a larger sample size to validate these findings. Fourth, the limited number of electrodes used may not adequately represent perfusion in specific brain regions, such as the temporal and occipital areas. Finally, the current study lacks external data validation, highlighting the need for further expansion of the sample size and multicenter studies to corroborate our results.

CONCLUSIONS

We have demonstrated that perioperative QEEG can detect early stages of postoperative ND in patients with TAAD. Furthermore, ΔaEEG grades may detect signs of stroke in patients with cortical damage within 2 hours postoperatively, potentially identifying stroke up to 4 hours earlier than what is typically confirmed by head CT in our previous practice. Postoperative RBP and ∆aEEG grades exhibit predictive value for the occurrence of TND. These findings underscore the clinical significance of QEEG as a valuable tool for cardiac surgeons to assess postoperative brain function and enable early interventions that have the potential to improve clinical outcomes.
