Elevated serum ferritin as a predictor of complications in cirrhotic patients: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Maissae Rahaoui, Amri Fakhrddine, Ouiam Elmqaddem, Hajar Koulali, Abdelkrim Zazour, Zahi Ismaili, Ghizlane Kharrasse
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionCirrhosis is a major cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide [1]. While serum ferritin is recognized as a marker of liver injury and inflammation, its prognostic value for cirrhosis-related complications is not well established. We investigated whether serum ferritin levels could predict complications in cirrhotic patients.Materials and methodsIn this retrospective observational cohort study, which included both descriptive and analytical components, serum ferritin levels were measured in adult cirrhotic patients admitted to the gastroenterology department of a University Hospital Center between March 2017 and March 2024. The normal reference range was defined as 22–275 ng/mL for men and 4.63–204 ng/mL for women. Patients were monitored for the occurrence of complications over a one-year period. Statistical analyses were performed to assess the association between serum ferritin levels and complications, and to determine whether elevated ferritin was an independent predictor of adverse outcomes.ResultsThe study included 200 cirrhotic patients (111 females and 89 males) with a mean age of 58,06 ± 15.21 years (range: 19–95). The most common etiologies were metabolic-associated steatohepatitis (MASH) (49.7%) and viral hepatitis (19%). Serum ferritin levels were significantly higher in decompensated patients compared to compensated patients (173 ± 295.22 ng/mL vs. 121.32 ± 251.98 ng/mL). During one-year follow-up, 68.5% of patients with elevated ferritin levels developed complications, compared to 27.7% of those with normal ferritin levels. Serum ferritin levels were significantly higher in Child-Pugh B patients (424.9 ng/mL) compared to Child-Pugh A (74.8 ng/mL, p = 0.002). Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis demonstrated that serum ferritin has moderate discriminative power in predicting the occurrence of complications in cirrhotic patients (AUC = 0.670). Elevated ferritin (≥ 45 ng/mL) was associated with a significantly increased risk of complications (p < 0.001). Multivariate analysis revealed the following predictors of hepatic decompensation: elevated serum ferritin (aOR 5.76, 95% CI 2.08–15.90, p = 0.001), hyponatremia (aOR 0.39, 95% CI 0.18–0.85, p = 0.017), and vitamin D deficiency (aOR 6.52, 95% CI 1.86–22.92, p = 0.003). These findings highlight the complex and multifactorial nature of hepatic decompensation and support the inclusion of serum ferritin in a comprehensive prognostic assessment.ConclusionElevated serum ferritin levels are independently associated with an increased risk of complications in cirrhotic patients. Given its wide availability and low cost, serum ferritin may serve as a simple prognostic biomarker to guide risk assessment and optimize clinical management in this population.Keywords: Ferritin, Cirrhosis, Complications, Prognosis

Background

Cirrhosis is a prevalent chronic liver disease resulting from diverse etiologies such as MASH, chronic alcohol use, viral hepatitis B and C, and autoimmune liver disorders [2]. Despite well-recognized complications arising from portal hypertension and liver dysfunction, reliable and accessible prognostic biomarkers remain limited. Recent evidence suggests that serum ferritin may not only reflect liver injury and systemic inflammation but also carry prognostic value in cirrhosis. However, data on this association are scarce in North African populations. This study aimed to evaluate the relationship between serum ferritin levels and the occurrence of complications in cirrhotic patients from a Moroccan cohort.

Patients and methods

We conducted a retrospective cohort study of 200 cirrhotic patients admitted to the gastroenterology unit of our University Hospital Center between March 2017 and March 2024. As this was a retrospective study, no formal sample size calculation was performed; all eligible patients admitted during the study period were included. For each patient, demographic and clinical data were collected at the time of admission, including age, sex, cirrhosis etiology, and any history of prior decompensations. Biological assessments included serum ferritin levels (reference ranges : 22–275 ng/mL for men and 4.63–204 ng/mL for women), measured in the certified laboratory of our University Hospital using the ARCHITECT Ferritin assay (Abbott Diagnostics, USA), a two-step chemiluminescent microparticle immunoassay (CMIA, Chemiflex protocol). Additional laboratory tests comprised a complete blood count (CBC), liver biochemistry tests (including transaminases, albumin, and bilirubin levels), prothrombin time, a serum vitamin profile, and C-reactive protein (CRP) levels. Patients were followed for one year to monitor the development of cirrhosis-related complications, including variceal bleeding, hepatic encephalopathy, and ascites.

Therefore, the risk of substantial selection bias introduced by these exclusion criteria is considered minimal.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS (Statistical Package for the Social Sciences) Statistics, version 24 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The incidence of complications within one year following serum ferritin measurement was compared between patients with normal and elevated ferritin levels.

Quantitative variables were compared using Student’s t-test, while one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was employed to assess differences in serum ferritin levels across Child–Pugh groups. Post hoc tests were conducted for pairwise comparisons when ANOVA results were statistically significant.

Additionally, ROC curve analysis was performed to evaluate the prognostic accuracy of serum ferritin levels in both compensated and decompensated patients. The area under the curve (AUC) was calculated to estimate the diagnostic performance of ferritin as a prognostic biomarker.

To explore factors associated with hepatic decompensation, a univariate logistic regression analysis was conducted. Variables that showed statistically significant associations were subsequently included in a multivariate logistic regression model to identify independent predictors of decompensation. Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIF) to ensure the validity of the model.

Results

A total of 200 cirrhotic patients were included in the study, comprising 111 females and 89 males, with a mean age of 58.06 ± 15.21 years (range : 19–95). The most frequent underlying etiology was MASH, accounting for 49.7% of cases, followed by viral hepatitis (19%), including Hepatitis B (6.5%) and Hepatitis C (11.5%), and autoimmune liver diseases (6%). Based on the clinical stage of liver disease, 61 patients (30.5%) were classified as having compensated cirrhosis, while 139 patients (69.5%) had decompensated cirrhosis at the time of inclusion. Among the decompensated group, complications were distributed as follows: 30.21% had isolated ascites, 27.33% had variceal bleeding, and 3.59% presented with hepatic encephalopathy alone. Additionally, 30.21% experienced both ascites and variceal bleeding, while 8.63% had ascites associated with hepatic encephalopathy.

Serum ferritin levels were compared between compensated and decompensated cirrhotic patients. The mean serum ferritin level was higher in the decompensated group (173 ± 295.22 ng/mL) compared to the compensated group (121.32 ± 251.98 ng/mL). Elevated serum ferritin levels were found in 21.6% of decompensated patients, versus 14.8% in the compensated group. The proportions of patients with decreased ferritin levels were 24.5% and 29.5% in the decompensated and compensated groups, respectively, while normal levels were observed in 54% and 55.7% of patients, showing comparable distributions.

Over the one-year follow-up period, 27.86% of patients initially classified as compensated progressed to decompensated cirrhosis, and 50% of these individuals had elevated baseline ferritin levels. Among patients already decompensated at inclusion, 40.28% experienced a new episode of decompensation, and 39.28% of them had elevated serum ferritin at baseline. Among those who developed decompensation (regardless of initial status), 85.71% presented with a new type of decompensating event, including variceal hemorrhage (n = 23), hepatic encephalopathy (n = 20), and ascites (n = 5). In contrast, 14.28% experienced a recurrence of a previous complication. A significant association was found between elevated serum ferritin levels and the occurrence of decompensation in both groups. However, this association was more pronounced in decompensated patients (p < 0.001) than in those initially compensated (p = 0.021).

When stratified according to Child–Pugh classification, 99 patients were categorized as Child A, 80 as Child B, and 21 as Child C (Fig. 1).

Significant differences in serum ferritin levels were observed across the groups (ANOVA, p = 0.002), with mean values of 424.9 ± 832 ng/mL in Child B, 174.4 ± 192 ng/mL in Child C, and 74.8 ± 141ng/mL in Child A (Fig. 2). Post hoc analysis confirmed that serum ferritin levels in Child B were significantly higher than in Child A (mean difference = 350.1, 95% CI [113.3–586.9], p = 0.002). By contrast, no significant differences were observed between Child A and Child C (mean difference = 99.6, 95% CI [–478.0 to 278.7], p = 0.808), or between Child B and Child C (mean difference = 250.5, 95% CI [–135.7 to 636.6], p = 0.278). Homogeneous subset analysis further indicated two overlapping clusters— {A, C} and {C, B}— suggesting that Child C values were intermediate and did not differ significantly from either group. The comparatively lower serum ferritin levels in Child C compared to Child B may be explained by the small sample size, frequent bleeding-related iron loss, and malnutrition in this subgroup, all of which may contribute to reduced serum ferritin levels.

When comparing patients based on ferritin status, 68.5% of those with elevated serum ferritin levels experienced decompensation within the year following the measurement, compared to only 27.7% of patients with normal serum ferritin levels (Fig. 3), suggesting a significant association between elevated serum ferritin levels and the occurrence of complications within the subsequent year (p < 0.001). The most frequently observed complication was variceal bleeding (40.27%), followed by hepatic encephalopathy (31.94%) and ascites (27.78%).

Bar graph showing the incidence of complications within one year following ferritin measurement, according to ferritin status (normal vs. elevated)

ROC analysis was performed to assess the discriminative ability of serum ferritin in predicting the occurrence of hepatic decompensation in patients with cirrhosis (Fig. 4).

: ROC curve assessing the predictive performance of serum ferritin levels for complications in cirrhotic patients (n = 200)

The AUC was 0.670 (95% CI : 0.589–0.751, p < 0.001), indicating a moderate ability of serum ferritin to discriminate between patients with and without hepatic decompensation. The visually estimated optimal cut-off value was 45 ng/mL, corresponding to a sensitivity of 65.8% and a specificity of 56.7%. Although this threshold lies within the conventional reference range, serum ferritin levels ≥ 45 ng/mL were associated with an increased risk of decompensation.

This suggests that even ferritin levels considered “normal” may reflect subclinical inflammation, early disturbances in iron metabolism, or low-grade hepatocellular injury, processes that are pathophysiologically relevant in cirrhosis and may precede overt clinical deterioration. While the overall discriminative performance of ferritin alone is modest, its widespread availability and potential to act as an early warning marker support its use in conjunction with established clinical and biochemical parameters. These findings highlight ferritin’s potential role as a supplementary prognostic biomarker, best applied as part of a multimodal risk assessment rather than as an isolated decision-making tool.

We also performed a univariate analysis using univariate logistic regression to evaluate the association between each variable and the occurrence of decompensation. Variables that were significantly associated with decompensation included hyponatremia, low phosphorus levels, vitamin D deficiency, elevated mean platelet volume (MPV), leukocytosis, increased C-reactive protein (CRP), low prothrombin percentage (TP%), and elevated serum ferritin levels (Table 1).

Subsequently, multivariate logistic regression analysis was conducted to identify independent predictors of decompensation. Variables that were significant in univariate analysis were included in the model, and adjusted odds ratios (aOR) with 95% confidence intervals were calculated. Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIF), with all values < 5, indicating minimal collinearity.

In the multivariate model, hyponatremia (aOR 0.39, 95% CI 0.18–0.85, p = 0.017), vitamin D deficiency (aOR 6.52, 95% CI 1.86–22.92, p = 0.003), elevated serum ferritin levels (aOR 5.76, 95% CI 2.08–15.90, p = 0.001), and increased MPV (aOR 1.29, 95% CI 1.11–1.50, p = 0.001) remained independently associated with the risk of decompensation (Table 2). These findings suggest that elevated serum ferritin is strongly associated with decompensation, highlighting its potential role as a prognostic biomarker. This aligns with emerging evidence linking iron overload to adverse liver outcomes and corroborates our earlier results. Additionally, serum sodium demonstrated a strong inverse association with the risk of decompensation, reinforcing the well-established prognostic significance of hyponatremia in cirrhosis. Meanwhile, higher serum vitamin D levels were significantly associated with a reduced risk, suggesting a possible protective immunomodulatory effect.

Overall, these results underscore the multifactorial nature of hepatic decompensation and suggest that incorporating serum ferritin into risk stratification models could improve early identification of high-risk patients. Further studies are warranted to validate these associations and elucidate the underlying pathophysiological mechanisms that confer ferritin its predictive value.

Discussion

Ferritin is a ubiquitous protein primarily found in the liver, spleen, bone marrow, and skeletal muscles. It serves as the primary intracellular iron-storage protein, maintaining iron balance within cells. By sequestering excess iron, it prevents the accumulation of potentially toxic free iron ions that can lead to oxidative damage. When iron is required for metabolic processes, ferritin releases it in a controlled manner. In the liver, iron is stored mainly in parenchymal cells, whereas in other tissues it is predominantly stored in macrophages. Hepcidin, a key regulatory hormone, controls dietary iron absorption by modulating iron release from enterocytes and macrophages, thereby regulating systemic iron homeostasis [3, 4].

High levels of ferritin are observed both in conditions of iron overload and as a consequence of increased release from damaged cells, in several pathologies such as inflammation, infection, and liver disease. Recent studies suggest that cirrhotic patients exhibit elevated serum ferritin levels due to various factors, including hepatic necro-inflammation, the release of ferritin from damaged hepatocytes, and secondary to macrophage activation [5, 6]. Additionally, decreased hepcidin levels—resulting from reduced production by the cirrhotic liver, chronic inflammation, and recurrent infections—further contribute to increased serum ferritin concentrations. Hepcidin is a key regulator of iron homeostasis, as it inhibits iron absorption by binding to ferroportin on the basolateral membrane of enterocytes. Consequently, reduced hepcidin levels lead to increased iron availability and elevated serum ferritin [7].

Liver cirrhosis is the result of advanced liver disease, in which normal hepatic tissue is replaced by fibrotic tissue, leading to a progressive decline in liver function. It has various etiologies, most commonly alcoholism, infection with hepatitis B and C viruses, MASH, autoimmune diseases, and genetic disorders. The course of liver cirrhosis is marked by the onset of serious complications, including variceal hemorrhage, hepatic encephalopathy, and ascites, which represent a turning point in the prognosis of affected patients [8]. The transition from compensated to decompensated cirrhosis significantly worsens prognosis, reducing median survival from over 10 years to approximately 2 years, and increasing the five-year mortality rate to as high as 85% without liver transplantation [9]. This underscores the crucial need for continuous monitoring and accurate predictive tools for early detection and effective management of decompensation in cirrhotic patients. There are different prognostic systems that evaluate severity and predict the onset of complications in cirrhotic patients. With the Child-Pugh and MELD scores being the most commonly used [10].

While these traditional scores provide valuable information on hepatic synthetic function and overall disease severity, they do not account for systemic inflammation or disturbances in iron metabolism, both of which are increasingly recognized as contributors to adverse outcomes in cirrhosis. Integrating additional biomarkers, such as serum ferritin, into existing or future prognostic frameworks could enhance risk stratification and improve the precision of clinical decision-making. Ferritin reflects both inflammatory activity and iron stores and may identify high-risk patients who might otherwise be classified in intermediate categories. For example, in patients with a moderate MELD score, markedly elevated serum ferritin levels could prompt closer monitoring, earlier consideration for liver transplantation listing, or proactive management of potential complications. Similarly, ferritin could be particularly valuable in acute decompensation, where inflammatory burden is a key driver of short-term prognosis.

Serum ferritin may thus serve as a valuable marker of disease severity, as its levels have been shown to correlate with histological liver lesions in patients without hemochromatosis [11]. Furthermore, ferritin is a widely available and inexpensive biochemical parameter. Prospective studies are warranted to validate these approaches and to define optimal ferritin cut-off values for clinical decision-making.In our study, we aimed to evaluate the potential of serum ferritin levels as a predictor of complications in patients with cirrhosis, given that it is a routinely measured parameter in this population. Our findings demonstrated a significant association between elevated serum ferritin levels and an increased risk of complications among cirrhotic patients.

These results align with previously published studies (Table 3). For instance, Walker et al. demonstrated that serum ferritin serves as an independent predictor of mortality in cirrhotic patients awaiting liver transplantation, with elevated levels correlating with a higher incidence of liver-related complications. In their cohort of 191 liver transplantation candidates, baseline serum ferritin > 200 ng/mL was independently associated with mortality at 6 and 12 months, and all deceased patients had levels above 400 ng/mL [12]. Similarly, Maiwall et al., in a study of 318 hospitalized patients with decompensated cirrhosis, revealed ferritin as a significant biomarker for predicting early liver-related mortality [8].

Oikonomou et al. further confirmed the prognostic role of ferritin, showing in 192 patients with stable decompensated cirrhosis that higher serum ferritin levels independently predicted long-term outcomes, including death or liver transplantation [13]. In a meta-analysis by Dan Liu et al., involving 1,829 patients with decompensated cirrhosis, the pooled prevalence of elevated ferritin was 40.6%, with higher levels observed in non-survivors compared to survivors, and in patients with Child–Pugh grade C cirrhosis [14].

However, differences in optimal ferritin cut-off values across studies are notable. While our identified threshold of 45 ng/mL is lower than the > 55 ng/mL reported by Oikonomou et al. and the > 200 ng/mL observed by Walker et al., such discrepancies may be due to heterogeneity in study populations (including etiology, cirrhosis severity, and prevalence of systemic inflammation), sample sizes, inclusion and exclusion criteria, as well as variability in analytical methods and assay standardization.

Some evidence suggests a more complex relationship between ferritin and outcomes. Tornai et al. reported a U-shaped relationship, in which both low (< 40 ng/mL) and high (> 310 ng/mL) serum ferritin levels were associated with poor prognosis [15]. This pattern is biologically plausible, as low ferritin could reflect malnutrition or iron deficiency, whereas high ferritin may indicate ongoing inflammation, hepatocellular injury, or iron overload. Although our study did not specifically evaluate such a non-linear association, this remains a relevant avenue for future research.

These studies collectively underscore the potential of serum ferritin as a prognostic marker in cirrhotic patients, with elevated levels often correlating with an increased risk of complications and mortality. Several mechanisms may explain these findings. First, hyperferritinemia can lead to endothelial dysfunction, exacerbating the circulatory abnormalities already present in cirrhosis and thereby contributing to hemorrhagic decompensation and thrombosis. Additionally, excess iron within hepatocytes induces oxidative stress, which promotes inflammation and fibrosis and accelerates the progression of cirrhosis [16], thereby increasing the risk of decompensation. Furthermore, iron overload is associated with metabolic disturbances, including insulin resistance and dysregulation of lipid metabolism. These conditions may complicate the management of cirrhotic patients, particularly those with hepatic steatosis [17].

Our study provides new evidence from a North African population, highlighting the prognostic value of serum ferritin in real-world, resource-constrained settings, where local limitations may have influenced patient selection and the completeness of follow-up. Unlike prior studies focusing exclusively on decompensated patients or transplant candidates, our cohort included both compensated (30.5%) and decompensated (69.5%) patients, offering a more comprehensive assessment of ferritin’s prognostic value across the cirrhosis spectrum. The ferritin threshold identified here (45 ng/mL) is lower than reported in previous studies, and the AUC of 0.670—slightly higher than that reported by Oikonomou et al.—suggests modest but clinically relevant predictive performance. While our study does not provide new mechanistic insights into ferritin biology, it reinforces the clinical relevance of ferritin and indirectly supports the role of iron metabolism and systemic inflammation in cirrhosis progression. These findings are generally consistent with the literature, while highlighting the need for standardized research to validate results across diverse populations and study designs. Several limitations should be noted: the retrospective, descriptive, and analytical design may introduce selection bias; data were obtained from a single tertiary care center; and the sample size was relatively small. This single-center approach limits generalizability, particularly to high-income settings where healthcare systems, diagnostic strategies, and patient populations differ substantially. Therefore, large-scale, multicenter studies are warranted to confirm these observations and overcome current limitations.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study demonstrates a significant association between elevated serum ferritin levels and an increased risk of complications in cirrhotic patients, suggesting that ferritin may serve as a valuable prognostic marker for assessing disease severity and anticipating adverse outcomes. Its accessibility and ease of measurement further support its potential integration into routine clinical practice to enhance decision-making and patient care. However, serum ferritin should not be used as a standalone marker but rather incorporated into a comprehensive, multimodal approach to risk assessment. Further research is needed to confirm these findings and to better establish its clinical utility.
