Integrative effects of irrigation and aeration on root morphology, yield, and quality of tomatoes cultivated in coastal saline-alkali lands
Authors: Lingchao Meng, Yunfei Zheng, Haiwen Ren, Zhicheng Li, Xi Zheng, Miaoye Kang, Liang Li, Lan Qi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Poor soil structure and aeration in coastal saline-alkaline lands severely constrain crop productivity. This study evaluated the efficacy of aerated drip irrigation in improving tomato (Solanum lycopersicum L.) cultivation under these conditions. A field experiment compared aerated (A1) and non-aerated (A0) irrigation across three water levels: severe deficit (W1, 0.6 Ep), mild deficit (W2, 0.8 Ep), and full irrigation (W3, 1.0 Ep), where Ep represents cumulative pan evaporation. Relative to conventional irrigation, aeration significantly improved plant performance by promoting root development, which supported enhanced canopy growth and a higher net photosynthetic rate. The A1W3 treatment produced the highest yield (1561.6 g plant⁻¹), a 22.70% increase over the non‑aerated full irrigation control, while also improving fruit quality attributes including lycopene and vitamin C contents. Principal component analysis identified A1W3 as the most effective strategy under our experimental conditions for integrated yield and quality. These results indicate that aerated drip irrigation offers a practical approach to enhance tomato production in marginal coastal regions.

Introduction

With the continuous growth of the global population and the increasing scarcity of arable land, saline-alkaline soils have emerged as vital reserve resources for agricultural development. In particular, coastal saline-alkaline regions encompass vast areas and hold considerable potential for agricultural expansion1,2. However, these regions face significant challenges due to severe soil salinization, which is influenced by multiple factors, including riverine salt transport, seawater intrusion, ocean currents, tides, and wave action3–8. Compared to inland alkaline-saline soils, coastal alkaline-saline soils present even greater challenges to crop growth, further exacerbating the challenges of agricultural production9. Therefore, enhancing the sustainability of agriculture in these regions is essential to ensuring national and global food security10.

Tomato (Solanum lycopersicum L.), one of the most economically important crops worldwide, is particularly sensitive to soil salinity, which severely limits its growth and yield in saline-alkali environments11. In recent years, significant progress has been made in the development of strategies to improve crop cultivation under saline-alkali conditions. Research has demonstrated that optimizing the root-zone microenvironment is crucial for enhancing crop salt tolerance and alleviating salinity-induced stress12. Current approaches to improving crop growth in saline-alkali soils primarily include chemical amendments, organic amendments, and various agronomic management techniques13,14. While chemical amendments effectively modify soil properties and enhance crop performance, they are often costly and may pose environmental drawbacks15. Similarly, biomass amendments, though beneficial, require long-term application before yielding substantial improvements in soil quality16. Among agronomic management strategies, optimizing irrigation techniques has proven to be one of the most effective methods for regulating salt transport within the root zone, alleviating osmotic stress, and improving overall crop growth conditions. Consequently, innovative irrigation strategies offer promising solutions for promoting sustainable agricultural development in coastal saline-alkali regions17.

Given the widespread challenges of soil compaction and limited oxygen diffusion in coastal alkaline-saline soils, aerated irrigation has emerged as an effective approach for improving root-zone conditions. This method involves injecting micro or nano bubbles into irrigation water, thereby enhancing soil aeration and alleviating hypoxic stress18. Previous studies conducted in inland saline-alkali regions have demonstrated that aerated irrigation can significantly improve crop photosynthesis, improve fruit quality attributes, and increase yield19,20. These findings highlight the potential of aerated irrigation to mitigate salinity-induced stress and optimize crop performance in challenging soil environments.

However, although the benefits of aerated irrigation in inland saline-alkali regions have been documented, its application in coastal saline soils remains insufficiently understood. Coastal saline soils differ significantly from inland counterparts due to seawater intrusion, high groundwater levels, and severe salinization, all of which influence soil water-salt dynamics3–5. In particular, soil compaction induced by high sodium adsorption ratio (SAR) creates unique and severe hypoxic conditions that may fundamentally alter crop responses to aerated irrigation, necessitating a re-evaluation of this technology’s applicability in coastal regions. Furthermore, under coastal saline conditions, how aeration and irrigation volume synergistically affect root development, whether such improvements can translate into actual gains in yield and fruit quality, and how to optimize the combination of these two factors, all warrant further investigation.

This study hypothesizes that aerated drip irrigation can enhance soil aeration in saline-alkali lands, improve root-zone conditions, mitigate hypoxia caused by soil compaction, and ultimately increase tomato yield and fruit quality. To test this hypothesis, a systematic evaluation of the effects of aerated irrigation on tomato growth under coastal saline-alkali conditions was conducted. The specific objectives of this study were to: (1) investigate the effects of different irrigation-aeration combinations on key tomato growth parameters, including plant height, leaf area, root growth characteristics, and net photosynthetic rate; (2) evaluate the impact of aerated irrigation on tomato yield components and fruit quality; (3) utilize principal component analysis (PCA) to identify the optimal irrigation-aeration strategy by integrating multiple growth and yield indicators. The findings of this study will provide valuable theoretical insights and technical guidance for improving water resource utilization efficiency in coastal saline-alkali regions, thereby contributing to the advancement of sustainable coastal agriculture.

Materials and methods

Experimental site

The experiment was conducted from April 5 to August 1, 2024, in a solar-heated greenhouse at the research base of Tianjin Ren’ai College, located in Jinghai District, Tianjin, China (38°56′N, 117°07′E). The experimental site is situated within a warm temperate continental monsoon climate zone on a coastal saline alluvial plain. The region experiences an average annual sunshine duration of 2571.9 h and a frost-free period of 208 days. Annual precipitation ranges from 570 to 690 mm, with approximately 70% occurring between June and August, leading to significant seasonal variability in water availability. The average annual evaporation rate is 1100 mm. Figure 1 provides an overview of the study area, including its geographical and climatic characteristics.

The greenhouse is equipped with an external shading system and insect-proof nets, which facilitate the regulation of photoperiod, solar radiation intensity, humidity, and temperature while also aiding in pest control. Standard greenhouse management practices were followed in accordance with local agricultural guidelines21.

The soil at the experimental site was classified as sodium chloride-dominated saline-alkaline soil. According to the routine soil survey records of the experimental station, the baseline soil properties in the 0–20 cm layer were as follows: bulk density, 1.45 g cm⁻³; organic matter content, 11.51 g kg⁻¹; total nitrogen, 0.76 g kg⁻¹; available phosphorus, 8.40 g kg⁻¹. The soil had a pH of 8.35 and a salinity of 2.40 g kg⁻¹.

Experimental design and treatment

The experiment was conducted using the tomato (Solanum lycopersicum L.) variety ‘Baili’, which is a determinate hybrid cultivar widely cultivated for greenhouse production in northern China. The growth cycle was divided into four distinct stages: the seedling stage (April 5–May 4, 2024), the flowering and fruit-setting stage (May 5–May 20), the fruit expansion stage (May 21–June 13), and the maturity and harvesting stage (June 14–August 1). Pollination was assisted by manual application of a fruit-setting hormone (plant growth regulator) to the flower pedicels, following local standard greenhouse practice, to ensure consistent fruit development across all treatments. Plants were managed to retain a total of three fruit clusters per plant, with 3–4 fruits retained per cluster.The fertilization regime followed standard local agronomic practices for solar greenhouse tomato production. All fertilizers were applied as a single basal dose prior to transplanting. The basal fertilization consisted of a commercial organic fertilizer (N-P2O5-K2O ≥ 10%, organic matter ≥ 45%) applied at a rate of 3500 kg hm⁻2, and a compound chemical fertilizer (N: P₂O₅:K₂O = 15:15:15) applied at 2250 kg hm⁻2. Both fertilizers were uniformly incorporated into the soil during soil preparation. No topdressing was applied throughout the entire tomato growing season. Plants were trained to a single-stem system following regional practices21. When plant height exceeded 30 cm, plants were supported using nylon strings. Axillary shoots were removed regularly on sunny days. Except for irrigation and aeration treatments, all other agronomic practices followed standard local management protocols.

A Φ20 cm standard evaporating dish (E601 type)was placed at the center of the greenhouse at a height consistent with the plant canopy22. Beginning at the transplanting stage, daily evaporation measurements were recorded at 08:30 AM.

A two-factor completely randomized design was adopted with two factors: aeration treatment (A) and irrigation level (W). Irrigation volume was set at three levels: full irrigation (W3, 1.0 Ep), mild deficit irrigation (W2, 0.8 Ep), and severe deficit irrigation (W1, 0.6 Ep), where Ep represents the cumulative evaporation by the Φ20 cm standard evaporating dish, and 0.6, 0.8, and 1.0 are the pan evaporation coefficients (Kcp). Irrigation was applied at intervals of 5–7 days, with water volume controlled using water meters (Nanjing Yuanchuan Water Meter Co., Ltd., Nanjing, China). Aeration treatments were set at two levels: aerated treatment (A1, Venturi injector at 1× injection rate) and non-aerated control (A0, conventional drip irrigation without aeration). This design resulted in six treatment combinations: A1W1, A1W2, A1W3, A0W1, A0W2, and A0W3 (CK). The CK treatment (A0W3) served as the conventional full irrigation control without aeration. Each treatment was replicated three times, yielding a total of 18 experimental plots. All plots were randomly assigned within the greenhouse to minimize the effects of spatial variability.

Each experimental plot measured 6.0 m² (1.0 m × 6.0 m). To suppress weeds and regulate soil temperature, white polyvinyl chloride (PVC) film, a standard material commonly used by local farmers, was laid along the planting rows. Each plot contained two rows of 14 plants each, with a row spacing of 50 cm and an intra-row plant spacing of 45 cm. Adjacent plots were separated by a 0.8 m wide buffer zone to prevent cross-contamination of water and nutrients between treatments.

A subsurface drip irrigation (SDI) system was installed in the greenhouse with a one-tube-per-row configuration. Drip tapes with emitters spaced at 30 cm intervals (Tianjin Huaxu Shengtai Technology Co., Ltd., China) were buried at 15 cm depth and had a discharge rate of 2.5 L h⁻¹. Aeration was induced using a Mazzei Model 287 Venturi injector (Mazzei Injector Co., USA), which entrains air via pressure-driven vacuum. The aerated irrigation system consisted of a water pump, Venturi unit, and circulation pipeline. The Venturi injector operated at inlet/outlet pressures of 0.20/0.10 MPa, achieving an air intake volume of 17% of the irrigation water volume as quantified by the exhaust method. Aeration was applied continuously throughout each irrigation event. A total of 23 irrigation events were applied throughout the entire tomato growing season, with a cumulative total irrigation volume of 228 mm for the W3 (full irrigation) treatment.

Measurement items and methods

Measurement of growth traits

Prior to fruit harvest, three plants with uniform growth were randomly selected from each plot for plant height and leaf area measurements. Plant height was measured using a tape measure as the vertical distance from the stem base to the apex23. Leaf area index (LAI) was determined using the hole-punch method23. Ten leaves were randomly selected from all detached leaves, and leaf discs were sampled using a hole puncher. The discs were oven-dried at 75 °C to constant weight to determine the leaf area per unit dry mass, and total leaf area per plant was calculated based on the dry mass ratio. The underground portions of the plants were carefully excavated to ensure complete root collection, then thoroughly washed and gently dried. The root system was scanned using an Epson Expression 1600 Pro scanner (Model EU-35; Epson, Suwa, Japan) at a resolution of 300 dpi. The scanned images were analyzed using the WinRHIZO Pro image processing system (Regent Instruments Inc., Quebec, QC, Canada) to quantify key root growth parameters, including mean root diameter, root surface area, root length density, and root volume density.

Measurement of net photosynthetic rate in tomato leaves

At 65 days after transplanting (fruit enlargement stage), the net photosynthetic rate (Pn) of tomato leaves was measured under clear-sky conditions. Three plants were randomly selected from each plot, and three fully expanded leaves from the top of the canopy per plant at consistent leaf positions with adequate light exposure were chosen for measurement. Measurements were conducted between 09:00 and 11:00 h using a portable photosynthesis system (LI-6400, LI-COR Inc., Lincoln, NE, USA). An open gas-exchange system was employed with the following settings: CO₂ concentration was maintained using ambient air sampled from 2 to 3 m above ground to ensure stability, photosynthetic photon flux density (PPFD) was set at 600 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹, and airflow rate was set at 500 µmol s⁻¹.

Measurement of tomato yield and quality

Yield: Tomato fruits were harvested at commercial maturity, defined as ≥ 70% color change on the fruit surface, and immediately weighed to calculate the yield per plant.

Quality: The first mature fruit cluster was selected for quality assessment. Five fully ripened tomatoes of uniform maturity were randomly collected from each plot. The soluble sugar content was measured using the anthrone colorimetric method, organic acid content was determined via acid-base titration, vitamin C content was analyzed using the molybdenum blue colorimetric method, and lycopene content was quantified through ultraviolet spectrophotometry22.

Data analysis

A two-factor factorial analysis of variance (two-way ANOVA) was performed to evaluate the main effects of aeration (A) and irrigation volume (W), as well as their interaction (A×W). Duncan’s Multiple Range Test was used for post-hoc mean separation at α = 0.05. Principal component analysis (PCA) was conducted to integrate multiple correlated yield and quality parameters into comprehensive indices for objective identification of optimal irrigation-aeration treatments. All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 23.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Graphical representations were generated using OriginPro 2021 (OriginLab Corp., Northampton, MA, USA).

Results

The effect of aerated irrigation on tomato root morphology

Irrigation volume and aerated irrigation significantly influenced tomato root growth and development (Fig. 2). Across all irrigation levels, aerated treatments (A1) consistently produced significantly greater root parameters compared to non-aerated treatments (A0) (P < 0.05). Specifically, compared to A0, A1 treatment increased root diameter, root volume density, root length density, and root surface area by 10.21%, 9.56%, 12.68%, and 13.57%, respectively. Among the measured parameters, root surface area and root length density showed the strongest response to aeration. Root traits also exhibited a significant positive response to irrigation volume, with W3 producing the most favorable root development across all parameters (P < 0.05). Compared to W2 and W1, W3 treatment increased root length density by 19.75% and 17.66%, and root surface area by 41.40% and 34.18%, respectively. A significant interaction between aeration and irrigation was observed, with the A1W3 treatment achieving the highest values for root length density and root surface area among all treatment combinations (P < 0.05; Fig. 2c and d).

Effects of aerated irrigation on above ground growth of tomatoes

The effects of aerated irrigation on tomato plant height and leaf area index (LAI) are summarized in Table 1. Irrigation volume significantly influenced plant height (P < 0.05), while aeration treatment and its interaction with irrigation volume had no statistically significant effects.

Plant height generally increased with irrigation volume, reaching its maximum in the A1W3 treatment (124.45 cm), which was significantly higher than all other treatments, except for A0W3 (P < 0.05). Within the A1 group, plant height in the W3 treatment increased by 9.87% and 6.95% compared to the W1 and W2 treatments, respectively (P < 0.05). A similar trend was observed in the A0 group, where the A0W3 treatment exhibited significantly greater plant height than A0W1 and A0W2 (P < 0.05), with increases of 8.70% and 4.71%, respectively.

In contrast, LAI was significantly influenced by aeration, irrigation volume, and their interaction (P < 0.01). The A1W3 treatment exhibited the highest LAI (1.19), significantly greater than all other treatments (P < 0.05). Within the A1 group, LAI in W3 was 36.78% and 20.20% higher than in W1 and W2, respectively (P < 0.05). At each irrigation level, aerated treatments produced significantly higher LAI than non-aerated counterparts, with the enhancement most pronounced at W3 level (33.71% increase; P < 0.05; Table 1).

Impact of aerated irrigation on the net photosynthetic rate of tomato

Aerated irrigation had a significant effect on the net photosynthetic rate (Pn) of tomato plants. As shown in Fig. 3, at the same irrigation level, the net photosynthetic rate in the aerated treatment (A1) was significantly higher than in the non-aerated treatment (A0). At each irrigation level, Pn in aerated treatments (A1) was significantly higher than in non-aerated treatments (A0), with increases of 28.49%, 25.47%, and 20.07% at W1, W2, and W3 levels, respectively. Additionally, Pn increased progressively with irrigation volume under both aerated and non-aerated conditions, reaching its maximum in the A1W3 treatment (17.83 µmol·m⁻²·s⁻¹), which represented a 15.93% and 7.41% increase over A1W1 and A1W2, respectively (P < 0.05).

Effects of aerated irrigation on tomato yield

Both aerated irrigation and irrigation volume had significant effects on tomato yield (Fig. 3). Tomato yield under aerated irrigation (A1) was consistently higher than under non-aerated irrigation (A0). Aerated irrigation (A1) consistently enhanced yield across all irrigation levels, with increases of 16.1%, 13.3%, and 22.7% compared to their respective A0 treatments at W1, W2, and W3 levels. The A1W3 treatment achieved the highest yield (1561.6 g plant⁻¹), representing a 22.7% increase over the non-aerated control A0W3 (P < 0.05).

Irrigation volume also had a significant impact on tomato yield. As irrigation increased, total yield exhibited a consistent upward trend. Under aerated irrigation, yield in the W3 treatment was significantly higher than in W1 and W2, increasing by 30.3% and 15.5%, respectively. A similar pattern was observed under non-aerated conditions, where W3 yield exceeded that of W1 and W2 by 23.3% and 6.6%, respectively.

Effects of aerated irrigation on tomato fruit quality

The effects of aeration and irrigation on tomato fruit quality parameters are presented in Fig. 4. Aeration, irrigation volume, and their interaction significantly affected lycopene content (P < 0.05 or P < 0.01). On average, lycopene content in A1 treatment was 67.26% higher than in A0 treatment, with increases of 81.74%, 70.09%, and 59.43% at W1, W2, and W3 levels, respectively. In contrast to yield, lycopene content decreased with increasing irrigation volume, with the highest value observed in A1W1, exceeding A1W2 and A1W3 by 10.05% and 29.59%, respectively (P < 0.05).

Vitamin C content followed a similar trend. The average vitamin C content in A1 was 63.53% higher than in A0, with increases of 36.92%, 101.06%, and 60.09% at W1, W2, and W3 levels, respectively. Vitamin C content also decreased with increasing irrigation volume, with W3 showing 21.43% lower content than W1. The highest vitamin C content (3.82 mg 100 g-1) was recorded in A1W1 (P < 0.05).

Soluble sugar content displayed a variation pattern similar to yield. On average, A1 treatment resulted in a 38.27% increase compared to A0, with enhancement effects of 35.76%, 27.12%, and 63.68% at W1, W2, and W3 levels, respectively. Unlike lycopene and vitamin C, soluble sugar content increased with irrigation volume, reaching its maximum in A1W3, which exceeded A1W1 and A1W2 by 25.33% and 15.66%, respectively (P < 0.01).

Titratable acid content exhibited a distinct pattern. No significant difference was observed between A1 and A0 treatments; however, irrigation volume had a significant effect (P < 0.01). The highest titratable acid content was recorded at W1 level, surpassing W2 and W3 by 27.85% and 15.00%, respectively.

Correlation analysis between indicators

A total of 25 significant or highly significant correlations were identified among the 12 indicators of tomato yield, quality, shoot growth, photosynthetic characteristics, and root traits (Fig. 5). Tomato yield exhibited a highly significant positive correlation (P < 0.01) with root length density, root surface area, root volume density, leaf area index, and net photosynthetic rate, while a significant correlation was also observed with plant height. Additionally, plant height was highly significantly correlated with leaf area.

Among the quality indicators, lycopene exhibited significant positive correlations with both vitamin C and soluble sugar; however, the correlation between vitamin C and soluble sugar was not statistically significant. Similarly, titratable acid content showed no significant correlation with any other quality parameters.

Given the interrelationships among these 12 indicators, no single parameter can comprehensively evaluate tomato performance. A direct assessment would result in information redundancy, thereby compromising evaluation reliability. To address this, principal component analysis (PCA) was applied to consolidate correlated yield and quality indicators into a set of mutually independent comprehensive indices. These indices improved assessment robustness, enhancing the reliability of evaluation outcomes.

The cumulative variance contribution rate was 90.70%, representing the combined effect of all treatments on yield and fruit quality (Table 2). Principal Component 1 accounted for 70.39% of the variance, while Principal Component 2 contributed 20.31%. Discussion

This study systematically evaluates the effects of aeration levels and irrigation amounts under subsurface drip irrigation on tomato growth, yield, and fruit quality in coastal alkaline-saline soils. It examines the response patterns through which water-aeration interactions are associated with crop agronomic traits. The experimental results support the hypothesis that aerated irrigation can alleviate growth inhibition under saline-alkali stress, with concurrent improvements in yield and quality that are consistent with improved rhizosphere conditions. These findings align with previous observations in non-saline-alkali regions, where aerated drip irrigation has been shown to improve crop growth, yield, and quality24,25. Furthermore, the comprehensive evaluation model developed using PCA provides a scientific framework for determining the optimal combination of irrigation amount and aeration level, offering essential technical guidance for the integrated management of saline-alkali farmlands.

Saline-alkali stress is a multifaceted environmental challenge that induces chemical damage, osmotic stress, ion toxicity, nutrient deficiency, and hypoxia in plants26,27. These adverse effects inhibit crop growth and development, reduce yield and quality, and, in severe cases, lead to plant mortality. The external morphology and overall growth status of plants serve as direct indicators of saline-alkali stress severity. Previous research by Ma et al. confirmed that saline-alkali stress suppresses plant height and leaf area expansion28. Recent studies suggest that aerated irrigation can mitigate these negative effects and enhance plant growth under stress conditions29. For instance, Yu et al. demonstrated that maize plants subjected to aerated irrigation exhibited significantly greater plant height and leaf area than those in non-aerated treatment groups30. Consistent with these findings, the present study observed that, under the same irrigation level, tomatoes receiving aerated drip irrigation exhibited greater plant height and leaf area than those in non-aerated treatments.

Roots serve as the primary interface for nutrient transport between crops and their external environment. Soil properties, including moisture content, nutrient availability, and temperature, directly influence root growth and distribution patterns. A previous study demonstrated that coastal soils in Tianjin are predominantly saline due to chloride accumulation, with chloride and sodium accounting for 60–88% of the total anion and cation concentrations, respectively. A high sodium adsorption ratio (SAR) induces clay mineral swelling, leading to pore closure, soil compaction, and reduced permeability, which in turn diminishes aeration31. These soil alterations subject plant roots to both saline-alkali and hypoxic stress simultaneously. Previous studies suggest that under this combined stress, the balance of redox reactions and electron transport in plant cells can be disrupted, leading to increased reactive oxygen species (ROS) pressure, which may constrain root development32,33. In this study, aerated irrigation significantly improved root growth parameters compared with conventional irrigation. This improvement is likely attributed to enhanced soil aeration, which alleviates hypoxic conditions in the root zone and provides sufficient oxygen for root respiration and metabolism34. Additionally, increased soil oxygen availability may contribute to better oxidative balance under saline-alkali and hypoxia stress conditions, although this mechanism was not directly measured in this study. These findings underscore the potential of aerated irrigation as an effective strategy for improving root development and enhancing crop resilience in saline-alkali environments35.

Photosynthesis is the fundamental process by which plants synthesize organic compounds essential for growth, and is a key determinant of crop yield. Previous research has demonstrated that elevated sodium ion concentrations in the soil increase osmotic pressure beyond that of plant cells, thereby reducing the efficiency of water absorption and utilization by crop roots36. To mitigate water loss, plants restrict stomatal opening; however, this simultaneously limits CO₂ diffusion into the leaves, leading to reduced photosynthetic efficiency due to the decreased availability of CO₂, a key substrate for photosynthesis. Aerated irrigation has been shown to enhance the activities of soil urease and alkaline phosphatase, thereby alleviating the negative impact of osmotic stress on root water uptake37. Additionally, improved soil aeration promotes root development, facilitating greater water absorption and utilization, which in turn enhances photosynthetic performance38. In this study, the net photosynthetic rate of the aerated irrigation treatment group was significantly higher than that of the A0 treatment group, consistent with findings from previous research39. Further analysis revealed a highly significant positive correlation (P < 0.01, Fig. 4) between tomato yield and key physiological parameters, including root length density, root surface area, root volume density, leaf area index, and net photosynthetic rate. These correlations are consistent with the interpretation that the increased tomato yields observed in the aerated irrigation treatment group may be associated with improved root development and enhanced photosynthetic capacity. These results suggest that increasing oxygen levels in irrigation water improves soil aeration in the root zone, alleviates hypoxic stress in the rhizosphere, and promotes root surface area expansion and proliferation. This, in turn, promotes greater root proliferation and nutrient uptake, thereby enhancing the translocation of water and nutrients from the root zone to aboveground plant parts. Consequently, net photosynthesis is improved, ultimately leading to higher fruit yield.

Furthermore, this study demonstrated that increasing irrigation volume facilitates root growth, aboveground biomass accumulation, and photosynthetic activity. This is primarily because higher irrigation volumes effectively leach salts below the root-active zone while simultaneously diluting soil salt concentrations, thereby mitigating the adverse effects of salinity stress on plant physiological processes40,41.

Nevertheless, in coastal saline-alkali regions, increasing irrigation volume may have trade-offs for tomato cultivation. Our findings indicate that while higher irrigation volumes enhance tomato yield, they also induce an asynchronous effect on fruit quality regulation. Specifically, the concentrations of lycopene and vitamin C (VC) decreased as irrigation volume increased. This decline may be attributed to elevated soil moisture levels, which increase fruit water content and result in a “dilution effect,” thereby reducing the accumulation of quality-related compounds. This observation is consistent with findings from previous studies42,43.

To further investigate the differential effects of irrigation-aeration interactions on fruit quality regulation, we employed PCA, a widely used statistical approach in agricultural research. This method enables a comprehensive and objective assessment of the overall impact of different treatments on fruit quality, providing a scientific basis for refining irrigation-aeration strategies. The results showed that the three aerated irrigation treatments consistently ranked among the highest, confirming the significant role of aerated irrigation in enhancing tomato yield, root morphology, and fruit quality.

While this study provides strong quantitative evidence for the agronomic and physiological benefits of aerated drip irrigation in coastal saline-alkali soils, certain limitations should be noted to guide future research. First, as this was a single-season proof-of-concept study conducted with a single tomato cultivar (‘Baili’) under greenhouse conditions, the generalizability and stability of the findings across different climatic years and diverse open-field coastal regions remain to be verified. Future multi-year, multi-location, and multi-environment field trials are necessary to systematically confirm the reliability of this irrigation strategy. Second, although the significant improvements observed in root morphology and photosynthetic capacity align well with the hypothesis of alleviating rhizosphere hypoxia, this study did not involve direct in situ measurements of soil physical dynamics (e.g., dissolved oxygen concentration) or rhizosphere biochemical indicators. Consequently, the discussion of related micro-mechanisms is primarily based on literature-based inferences. Therefore, future studies should combine real-time soil oxygen monitoring, biochemical indicators, and soil microbiome analysis to further elucidate the soil-root interaction processes and underlying regulatory mechanisms in coastal saline-alkali environments.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that aerated drip irrigation significantly enhances tomato growth, yield, and fruit quality in coastal alkaline-saline soils. The A1W3 treatment (aerated irrigation with the highest irrigation level) exhibited the optimal favorable performance, increasing yield by 22.7% compared to non-aerated irrigation while substantially improving root morphology, leaf area index, and photosynthetic rate. Aeration particularly enhanced fruit quality, leading to increases of 67.26% in lycopene content, 63.53% in vitamin C, and 38.27% in soluble sugar content.

Principal component analysis confirmed A1W3 as the most effective treatment, yielding the highest comprehensive score (2.79), indicating a synergistic interaction between aeration and irrigation volume. These findings provide a practical strategy for sustainable agricultural development in coastal saline-alkali regions, demonstrating that aerated drip irrigation can effectively ameliorate soil constraints while optimizing crop performance and resource use efficiency. The implementation of this technology has the potential to enhance the productive utilization of coastal saline-alkali lands, contributing to sustainable food production.
