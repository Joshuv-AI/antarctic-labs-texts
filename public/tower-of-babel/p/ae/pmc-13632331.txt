Diagnostic accuracy of ophthalmology residents in emergency department consultations: trends and potential implications for training
Authors: Rachel Shemesh, Mai Mizrachy, Mattan Arazi, Maysan Hussen, Ruth Huna-Baron
Published: 2026

Introduction

Ophthalmological disorders present a wide array of symptoms, leading to a broad spectrum of potential diagnoses. Accurate diagnosis is crucial, since misdiagnoses can significantly affect patient care and quality of life [1–3]. Data on the percent of incorrect diagnoses made by ophthalmology residents are very limited. Hashemi et al. studied the diagnostic accuracy involving 148 consultations of postgraduate year 1 (PGY-1) ophthalmology residents over 30 call days during a period of 7 weeks. The mean percent accuracy surpassed 70% during weeks 3–4, and improvement continued to week 7, suggesting the need for supervision during the first weeks of PGY-1 ophthalmology residents [4]. Nari et al. examined the accuracy of referral diagnoses of ophthalmic problems from ophthalmologists and other healthcare practitioners and found that the former had the highest diagnostic accuracy, with 83% of referral diagnoses being correct. However, their study focused upon ophthalmologists in practice post-residency and not upon ophthalmology residents in training [5]. Several studies analyzed the diagnostic accuracy of residents in various specialties with the aim of highlighting the risk of medical errors made by residents [6–8], but there are currently no publications on medical error-related studies throughout the years of training of ophthalmology residents. This study aims to evaluate the diagnostic accuracy of ophthalmology residents in an emergency department (ED) and identify factors contributing to diagnostic errors. By identifying these factors, we aim to enhance educational programs and improve patient management.

Methods

Study site

Our medical center is a tertiary, university-affiliated hospital as well as primary ophthalmology emergency care center. It is one of the major ophthalmology centers nationwide. It provides care to patients from newborns to the elderly. The ophthalmology ED (OED) provides 24-hour consultation service 7 days a week.

Study design

This retrospective cohort study was validated by a smaller population variant of the study with a prospective arm (37 cases) and a retrospective arm (63 cases). The accuracy of the diagnosis in both arms was examined, and it emerged that there was no significant difference between the two arms, with agreement between resident physicians and attending physicians of 70% and 73% in the prospective and retrospective studies (p = 0.77), respectively. We were therefore able to proceed with the current retrospective study, which included 10 residents and 1452 patient visits to the OED during a period of 67 days.

Participants and assesment

As part of routine clinical care and record-keeping, a record was kept of all clinical files containing anamnesis, examination, diagnosis, and treatment plan of all patients examined by ophthalmology residents in our medical center. We examined these consecutive data which contained the information of all patients seen in the OED from December 27, 2022 to March 3, 2023. Included were the recordings of patients that received medical care by a resident in the OED and those that had a follow-up appointment with an attending ophthalmologist within 3 months of the OED visit. A total of 1452 patients were identified and reviewed, of whom 1147 were excluded as detailed in Fig. 1.

Data collection

The patients were referred from one of three sources: (1) directly from a general ED; (2) emergent referral from a local family physician; or (3) transfer from another hospital for further evaluation and management. Along with the recording of these data, a record was made of the patient’s age, the examination date, including whether it was on a weekday or a weekend, the time of diagnosis (day or night), the year of training of the resident, yet, the name of the resident remained anonymous throughout the study, the diagnosis made by the resident divided into nine categories (cornea, retina, foreign body, trauma, infection, neuroophthalmology, glaucoma, cataract, strabismus), and the treatment provided by the resident. The information collected from the follow-up appointment by the attending ophthalmologist included the attending’s diagnosis and provision of treatment. The diagnostic accuracy of the resident was divided into “agree”, and “disagree” with the attending’s diagnosis. The agreements or disagreements between the resident’s and attending’s diagnoses were examined by two independent ophthalmologists (R.S. and R.H.B.).

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were described as frequency and percentage. Age distribution was evaluated by means of a histogram. Since age was not normally distributed, it was reported as median and interquartile range [IQR]. The association between diagnosis agreement and categorical variables was evaluated by either the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. The association with age was evaluated by the Mann-Whitney test. The same methods were applied to study the association between the year of residency with the degree of diagnostic inaccuracy. The McNemar test was used to compare the senior physicians’ and the residents’ recommendations. All statistical tests were two-sided, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS software (IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 28, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA, 2021).

Results

Number of consultations and demographics

A total of 305 consultations were seen by 10 ophthalmology residents in their first to fifth year of residency over 67 call days during the study period. The number of consultations was distributed evenly between weekdays (n = 32–54), and there were significantly more consultations during weekends (n = 64) when community ophthalmologists were not available. The accuracy decreased during weekend significantly from 86% to 74% (Fig. 2).

Diagnosis accuracy in weekday calls in comparison to weekend calls

Diagnostic accuracy of the ophthalmology residents

Agreement between the resident’s diagnosis and the attending physician’s diagnosis was high (82.3%, 251/305). There was no significant difference in diagnostic accuracy based upon the patient’s arrival hour to the OED (day time 79.3% vs. night time 79.7% p = 0.48) or whether or not the patient had been referred to the OED by a consultant ophthalmologist (no referral-83% vs. with referral 76% p = 0.859). In addition, the patients’ ages that ranged between 0 and 89 years (median 51 [IQR 22–67]) were non-significant in affecting diagnostic accuracy (p = 0.966). There was also no significant difference if the resident did or did not consult an attending physician by telephone (18%vs. 8% p = 0.325), or according to the resident’s sex (16% vs. 19% p = 0.381). However, there was a significant difference in diagnosis accuracy between weekday and weekend calls. The agreement rate between attendings and residents was significantly higher during the week (86%) compared to the weekend (74.2%, p = 0.012) (Fig. 2). Likewise, there was a significant difference in the diagnosis accuracy according to year of residency, with accuracy increasing with years of residency. Specifically, the agreement rate was higher for the residents in PGY-3 (90.5%) and PGY-4 (87.3%) compared to those in PGY-1 (71.4%), and PGY-2 (80.2%) (p = 0.025) (Fig. 3).

Diagnosis accuracy by year of residency

Importantly, residents in all years of residency were less accurate in their diagnosis of neuroophthalmological cases, with an 84.8% agreement rate of non-neuroophthalmological cases and a 67.5% agreement rate of neuroophthalmological cases (p = 0.008) (Fig. 4). The most prevalent neuro-ophthalmic conditions were optic neuritis (16.7%), papilledema (16.7%), six nerve palsy (16.7%), and Bell’s palsy (16.7%). There were no other significant differences between other ophthalmology subspecialties (anterior segment 18%, retina 15%, oculoplastic 16%).

Management of cases by residents

Residents were more likely to recommend a follow-up appointment in clinics within the hospital (40.3%) compared to clinics in the community (20.0%) in comparison to attendings who recommended a follow-up appointment in clinics within the hospital in 13.1% of cases and in clinics in the community in 26.6% of cases (p = 0.046). Also, residents tended to recommend hospitalization significantly more frequently than attendings (8.9% vs. 1.0%, respectively, p < 0.001).

Discussion

Several earlier studies have examined the clinical performance of pediatric residents [9] and the importance of graduate medical education [9–10]. To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to examine and compare the diagnosis accuracy of ophthalmology residents throughout their years of training. We reviewed 305 OED consultations over a 67-day period and calculated a diagnostic accuracy of 82.3%. This rate is considerably higher than that ascribed to other medical fields, such as 64% for neurology [7], 68% for the accuracy of clinical diagnosis of otitis media [8], and 66% for internal medicine [11]. One possible explanation for this difference could be that the ophthalmological diagnosis is based upon a clinical examination during which the pathology is frequently visible during slit lamp and funduscopic evaluations. Indeed, a high accuracy rate of diagnosis by artificial intelligence and deep learning technology using precise slit-lamp photographs has been reported in several studies [12–14]. Our finding is further supported by Nari et al.’s study on the accuracy of diagnoses of ophthalmic problems referred by healthcare practitioners. Those authors found that ophthalmologists had the highest diagnostic accuracy, with an 83% rate of correct diagnoses of those referred patients [5]. However, that study focused upon ophthalmology experts and not residents.

Residents in ophthalmology may face a difficult transition period from medical student to clinical ophthalmologist in which they need to rapidly develop strong diagnostic abilities for unsupervised calls. Given the relatively limited exposure to ophthalmology in medical school, a high error rate may be inevitable at the beginning of residency. Hashemi et al. studied the diagnostic accuracy of PGY-1 ophthalmology residents over 30 call days over a period of seven weeks that included 148 consultations during weeks 3–4 of training and found a similar accuracy rate (70%) in comparison to our study (71.4%) [4]. As could be expected, we also found that the accuracy of the diagnosis increased in parallel with the increasing year of residency. Although PGY-4 residents showed slightly lower accuracy than PGY-3 (87.3% vs. 90.5% ), this likely reflects the greater volume and complexity of cases managed independently by more senior residents rather than a true decline in diagnostic ability. Greater confidence in diagnosis was apparently associated with fewer recommendations of a follow-up appointment in the hospital’s outpatient clinics.

Residents were more accurate in their diagnoses on weekdays (86%) in comparison to weekends (74%) in our study. Previous studies have associated fatigue and distress with medical errors [15–17], we also observed that residents who are in their earlier years of training performed the majority of weekend calls and they were more likely to demonstrate lower diagnosis accuracy as result of lack of experience.

In our study residents were less accurate in their diagnosis of neuro-ophthalmological cases, which are arguably the most complicated eye pathologies to identify and treat. Among non-neuro-ophthalmological subspecialties, disagreement rates were comparable (anterior segment 18%, retina 15%, oculoplastics 16%), with no significant differences, suggesting that the diagnostic challenge is specific to neuro-ophthalmology. Stunkel et al. reported that neuro-ophthalmology consultation affected 489 of 496 patients (99%), and directly saved the patient’s life or vision in 2% of cases [1]. Moreover, harmful treatment was avoided or appropriate urgent referral was provided in an additional 10%, while neuro-ophthalmology provided a diagnosis and direction to the patient’s care in an additional 48%. Similarly, others had demonstrated high rates of misdiagnosis for neuro-ophthalmic conditions by attending physicians as well [18–21]. There are several likely explanations for this finding. First, most neuroophthalmological pathologies require an especially thorough history and anamnesis of conditions that may also impact the eye. Secondly, the complete physical examination includes not only the ophthalmological slit-lamp examination but a full cranial nerve evaluation that requires wider clinical knowledge and greater experience. Thirdly, neuroophthalmological diagnoses are more susceptible to diagnostic error because of their complex and varied clinical presentations [22–25]. In order to ensure accuracy and reliability of residents’ diagnoses in the OED, an urgent call consultation with an attending neuroophthalmologist is imperative. We aim to raise the level of awareness that such action may not only be vision saving but lifesaving as well.

This study has several limitations. First, these results from a single academic medical center may not be generalizable to other training programs nationally and internationally. However, this study was conducted in a tertiary medical center and included the entire range of ophthalmological consultations. Second, only 10 residents participated in this study and most weekend shifts were done by PGY-1 and PGY-2 residents which could bias the results. Thirdly, this is a retrospective study, and as such, the diagnosis accuracy could be biased also due to exclusion of 214 cases due to unavailable specialist follow-up diagnosis even though the cases were reviewed by two experienced ophthalmologists. Future prospective studies should include residents from multi-site and multi-country analysis in order to validate our findings.

In summary, the findings of this study may indicate a trend toward better diagnostic accuracy of ophthalmology residents in the OED. Greater inaccuracy was associated with a lack of clinical experience and occurred more in neuroophthalmological cases. The lower diagnostic accuracy of neuroophthalmological cases is worrisome and warrants a higher level of awareness of the educational needs of future clinical ophthalmologists.
