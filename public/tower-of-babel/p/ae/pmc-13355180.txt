Electric vs Manual Toothbrushing Effects on QLF-Assessed Plaque in Smokers and Non-Smokers: A 24-Week RCT
Authors: Andrea Di Stefano, Debora Gangi, Giusy Rita Maria La Rosa, Gaetano Isola, Valeriu Fala, Amaliya Amaliya, Jan Kowalski, Iain Chapple, Jakub Weglarz, Cinzia Del Giovane, Marida Nancy Pellegrino, Sebastiano Antonio Pacino, Riccardo Polosa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundElectric toothbrushes are known to outperform manual devices in plaque removal, yet most comparative trials rely on conventional examiner-based indices and none have evaluated efficacy across smoking profiles using objective fluorescence-based measurement.MethodsThis 24-week, 2-arm, parallel-group randomized controlled trial compared an oscillating-rotating electric toothbrush (Oral-B iO 6) with a manual toothbrush in 126 adults aged 18 to 50 years attending a dental clinic for routine scaling and polishing. Current smokers and never-smokers were enrolled to allow prespecified subgroup analysis by smoking status. Dental plaque accumulation was quantified using quantitative light-induced fluorescence (QLF) technology. The primary endpoint was ΔR30 (percentage of tooth surface with fluorescence increase ≥30%, reflecting total mature plaque); ΔR120 (fluorescence increase ≥120%, reflecting thick plaque and calculus-like deposits) was the secondary endpoint. The primary analysis used analysis of covariance adjusting for baseline plaque levels, treatment arm, smoking status, and their interaction.ResultsUse of the electric toothbrush was associated with significantly lower ΔR30 at 24 weeks compared with manual brushing (β = −1.84, 95% CI [−3.27, −0.41], p = .012), an effect that remained robust after adjustment for age, sex, and habitual oral hygiene behaviours. Baseline plaque level was the strongest predictor of follow-up values in both outcome models. No significant treatment effect was observed for ΔR120 (β = −0.77, 95% CI [−1.71, 0.18], p = .112). Subgroup analyses showed significant between-arm differences on both outcomes among never-smokers; among smokers, differences were directionally consistent but did not reach statistical significance, and the treatment-by-smoking interaction was not statistically significant.ConclusionsOscillating-rotating electric toothbrushing produced significantly lower plaque accumulation than manual brushing over 24 weeks, as indexed by QLF-derived ΔR30. These findings support the potential advantage of oscillating-rotating powered toothbrushes for reducing plaque accumulation in adult patients and further support the utility of QLF as a precise and reproducible outcome measure for toothbrush efficacy trials.Clinical trial registrationClinicalTrials.gov, NCT06358482.

Introduction

Maintaining optimal oral hygiene is central to the prevention of dental caries and periodontal disease, 2 of the most prevalent oral conditions globally.1 Dental plaque is the primary cause of gingivitis and is implicated in the progression to periodontitis, a more serious inflammatory condition affecting the tissues that support the teeth.2 When plaque accumulation is not adequately controlled, gingival inflammation progresses clinically through swelling, bleeding on probing, and, in susceptible individuals, irreversible periodontal destruction.3,4 The health implications of poor oral hygiene extend beyond the oral cavity: epidemiological evidence has linked inadequate plaque control with an elevated risk of systemic conditions, including cardiovascular disease, underscoring the broader public health relevance of effective daily toothbrushing.5, 6, 7

Self-performed mechanical plaque control through daily toothbrushing remains the most widely recommended and practiced approach to oral hygiene maintenance.8,9 Its effectiveness, however, is not solely a function of the device used. Brushing performance is modulated by individual-level factors including age, manual dexterity, oral health literacy, and motivation, all of which introduce variability in plaque removal outcomes.10, 11, 12 Powered toothbrushes were developed, in part, to reduce this technique-dependence and to compensate for individual variability in brushing skill. Evidence from clinical trials and systematic reviews supports a statistically significant, if modest, benefit of powered toothbrushes over manual devices with respect to plaque reduction and gingival health in both the short and long term. The most comprehensive synthesis to date, a Cochrane review of 56 trials involving 5,068 participants, reported an 11% reduction in plaque scores in the short term and 21% in the long term in favour of powered toothbrushes compared with manual devices.13 More recent evidence from a systematic review and network meta-analysis focusing on single-brushing efficacy confirmed, with high certainty, a small but consistent benefit of powered over manual toothbrushes in plaque removal.14

Notwithstanding this body of evidence, most comparative trials have relied on traditional examiner-based plaque indices, such as the Quigley–Hein index modified by Turesky or the Silness and Löe index. Although well validated and widely adopted, these instruments are inherently dependent on examiner judgment and retain a degree of subjectivity that, even under calibrated conditions, can introduce scoring variability and limit sensitivity to subtle inter-group differences in plaque accumulation.15 Advances in imaging technology have introduced more standardized alternatives. Quantitative light-induced fluorescence (QLF) enables the visualization and quantitative measurement of dental plaque through its fluorescence properties, providing a reproducible and operator-independent assessment of plaque distribution and dynamics over time. Full-mouth QLF-derived plaque scores have been shown to correlate significantly with established clinical indices, including the gingival index, bleeding on probing, and probing pocket depth, supporting their validity as primary endpoints in interventional studies.15, 16, 17 The adoption of QLF-based measurement may therefore address key methodological limitations of conventional indices and yield a more refined evaluation of plaque reduction in randomized trial settings.18

The question of effective plaque control is particularly relevant in populations at heightened risk of periodontal disease. Smokers exhibit poorer oral health profiles than never-smokers and have been shown to accumulate greater quantities of dental plaque.19, 20, 21 QLF-based plaque quantification has demonstrated that current smokers present with greater and more mature plaque buildup compared with former and never-smokers,22 a finding consistent with the established role of smoking as an independent risk factor for periodontitis.23 In this context, evaluating whether the comparative efficacy of powered vs manual toothbrushing differs across smoking profiles represents a clinically meaningful and underexplored research question.

The present 24-week randomized controlled trial was designed to evaluate the efficacy of a commercially available oscillating-rotating electric toothbrush compared with a standard manual toothbrush in reducing dental plaque accumulation, as assessed by QLF technology, in young adults attending a dental clinic for routine scaling and polishing. Uniquely, both current smokers and never-smokers were enrolled as distinct subgroups, given that smoking is known to alter oral biofilm composition and plaque accumulation dynamics, and whether powered toothbrushing confers a differential benefit across smoking profiles remains an underexplored clinical question. By combining an objective fluorescence-based plaque assessment method with a longitudinal randomized design and prespecified subgroup analysis by smoking status, this trial aimed to generate evidence on the comparative effectiveness of powered and manual toothbrushing in a clinically heterogeneous population.

Materials and methods

The trial was prospectively registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT06358482). No patients or members of the public were involved in the design, conduct, or reporting of this trial. The paper was reported in accordance with the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) 2025 guidelines24; the completed checklist is provided as Appendix 1.

Participants

Consecutive adults aged 18 to 50 years attending a dental clinic for routine scaling and polishing were considered for enrolment. Eligible participants were current smokers or never-smokers with at least ten natural anterior teeth (canine to canine, both arches). Smoking status was self-reported. Current smokers were defined as individuals smoking more than ten cigarettes per day for at least 3 years. Never-smokers were defined as individuals who had never smoked or had smoked fewer than 100 cigarettes in their lifetime.

Individuals were excluded if they presented with clinical signs of periodontitis, significant oral soft-tissue pathology, gingival overgrowth other than plaque-induced gingivitis, fixed or removable orthodontic appliances, or removable dentures. Additional exclusion criteria included: a recent history (within 30 days) of oral or dental infection or inflammation; a recent course (within 15 days) of antibiotics or anti-inflammatory medications; and pregnancy. Participants were also excluded if, in the judgment of the principal investigator, any concurrent medical condition would compromise participant safety or threaten the validity of study results. No specific qualification criteria were required for the dental practitioners conducting assessments, beyond familiarity with the QLF technology used for plaque quantification.

Trial design

This was a 2-arm, parallel-group, randomized controlled trial of 24 weeks' duration designed to objectively assess and compare dental plaque accumulation between participants assigned to a manual toothbrush (Arm A) and those assigned to an oscillating-rotating electric toothbrush (Arm B) (Figure 1). Dental plaque and calculus accumulation were quantified by QLF technology. The trial was designed as a superiority study with a 1:1 allocation ratio. Because of the inherent nature of the intervention, allocation could not be concealed from participants or clinical staff after assignment; accordingly, the trial was conducted as an open-label study. Participants attended 3 scheduled clinic visits: an initial visit for professional scaling and polishing (V0, Day 0) to standardize the oral environment by removing pre-existing calculus, plaque, and stain, a baseline visit at which randomization and QLF assessment were performed (V1, Day 14), and a final follow-up visit at which the primary outcome was assessed (V2, Day 180, corresponding to Week 24). The 14-day interval between scaling and polishing (V0) and baseline assessment (V1) was included to allow resolution of post-scaling gingival inflammation and to permit standardised de novo plaque re-accumulation under habitual oral hygiene conditions; during this period, all participants used their own manual toothbrush and received standardised oral hygiene instructions.

Participants assigned to Arm A received a standard manual toothbrush (medium head) together with standardized instructions on correct brushing technique. They were instructed to brush once daily for 2 minutes per session.

Participants assigned to Arm B received an oscillating-rotating electric toothbrush (Oral-B iO 6, medium head) and equivalent standardized instruction on its correct use. The Oral-B iO 6 was set to “Daily Clean” mode, and participants were instructed to brush a different quadrant every 30 seconds, following the device’s built-in 2-minute timer and 30-second alert. To minimize interference with outcome measurements, all participants were required to maintain their usual oral hygiene routine throughout the study and to observe the following restrictions before each visit: no flossing for at least 72 hours, no mouth rinsing for at least 24 hours, no toothbrushing for at least 2 hours, and no eating or drinking (except water) for at least 2 hours. No additional scaling or polishing procedures were permitted during the study.

At Visit 1 (Week 0), baseline QLF measurements were obtained and the following variables were recorded: age, sex, smoking status, toothbrushing frequency, mouthwash use, and flossing frequency. QLF measurements were repeated at Visit 2 (Week 24).

Study endpoints

Dental plaque and calculus accumulation were quantified at each visit using QLF technology. QLF measures dental plaque by quantitatively analysing the red fluorescence emitted by bacterial biofilm when illuminated by blue visible light at a wavelength of 405 nm, without requiring additional disclosing procedures. Images of the vestibular aspect of the anterior teeth (canine to canine, upper and lower arch) were acquired using the Q-ray cam Pro (AIOBIO, Seoul, Republic of Korea), a high-resolution, handheld, auto-focus QLF camera. Prior to image acquisition, participants rinsed with water; excess saliva was removed by dental suction, and cheek retractors were placed to ensure adequate visualization of all 12 anterior teeth. Ambient light was standardized across all assessments. Images were analysed using the proprietary QLF software (QA v.1.41, Inspektor Research Systems BV, Amsterdam, Netherlands), which determines for each pixel on the dental surface the value of ΔR, a measure of increase in red fluorescence relative to the sound tooth surface. QLF assessment was restricted to the anterior dentition, consistent with established practice in the QLF literature, as imaging of posterior teeth is technically constrained by limitations in camera positioning and intraoral light source accessibility.17,19,22 QLF images were acquired by a single trained and calibrated examiner at each visit; intra-examiner reliability was confirmed prior to study commencement. A single measurement per participant per visit was performed.19,22

The primary outcome was the change from baseline (Week 0) to Week 24 in ΔR30, defined as the percentage of the total tooth surface exhibiting a fluorescence intensity increase of at least 30% and reflecting the total area of mature dental plaque. A secondary outcome was ΔR120, defined as the percentage of the total tooth surface exhibiting a fluorescence intensity increase of at least 120%, reflecting regions of greater plaque thickness or maturation, including calculus and tartar. Both metrics are expressed as percentage of the analysed tooth surface area (range 0%-100%).

Adverse events

Adverse events were monitored throughout the trial via a combination of systematic assessment and participant self-report. At each scheduled clinic visit, participants were asked to report any adverse events experienced since the previous visit, and clinical staff conducted a structured oral examination to identify any signs of treatment-related harm, including gingival abrasion and dentinal sensitivity. All reported events were recorded and reviewed.

Sample size

The sample size was calculated a priori using G*Power (version 3.1) for a 2-sample independent t-test, based on the following assumptions: a medium effect size (Cohen's d = 0.50), a 2-sided significance level of α = 0.05, a statistical power of 80%, and an estimated standard deviation of 1.5 for ΔR30, the primary outcome. Under these assumptions, a minimum of 63 participants per arm (126 total) was required. Accounting for an anticipated dropout rate of 10%, the target sample size was set at 70 participants per arm (140 total).

Statistical analysis

The randomization sequence was computer-generated prior to trial commencement, with a 1:1 allocation ratio. Randomization was stratified by smoking status (current smoker vs never-smoker) to ensure balanced distribution of this key participant characteristic across the 2 study arms. Allocation was concealed using sequentially numbered, sealed envelopes, which were opened in sequence at the time of enrolment following eligibility confirmation. The personnel responsible for enrolling participants did not have prior access to the allocation sequence.

Due to the nature of the interventions, blinding of participants and clinical staff to group assignment was not feasible. The trial was therefore conducted as an open-label study. To limit the risk of performance bias in outcome assessment, all data analysts remained blinded to treatment allocation until the completion of all prespecified analyses. All other trial staff with access to outcome data were similarly kept blinded throughout the analysis phase.

Baseline characteristics were summarized by randomized arm, with continuous variables reported as mean (SD) and categorical variables as count (%). Between-arm differences in change scores from baseline to Week 24 were assessed using independent samples t-tests for each outcome, overall, and stratified by smoking status. We then conducted an analysis of covariance (ANCOVA), using the Week 24 value of each outcome (ΔR30 and ΔR120) as dependent variable, and the corresponding baseline value, treatment arm, smoking status, and their interaction as covariates. We assessed the normality of residuals and homoscedasticity to examine the distributional assumptions underlying t-tests and ANCOVA. Diagnostic plots for the baseline-adjusted ANCOVA models are reported in Appendices 2 and 3. We further adjusted the model included age, sex, toothbrushing frequency, flossing, and mouthwash use. Model fit was evaluated using adjusted R² and the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC). Statistical significance was defined as a 2-sided p < 0.05 throughout. All analyses were performed using RStudio (version 4.3, Posit Software, PBC).

Results

Participant flow and baseline characteristics

Recruitment began on January 30, 2023, and data collection was completed in April 2024, with a total follow-up period of 24 weeks per participant. Of 142 individuals assessed for eligibility, 14 were excluded, and 128 were randomized to the Manual arm (n = 61) or the Oral-B iO arm (n = 67) (Figure 2). Two participants in the Oral-B iO arm were subsequently excluded from the analysis due to missing outcome data at week 24, yielding a final analytic sample of 126 participants (61 Manual, 65 Oral-B iO). Mean age was 34.25 years (SD = 10.02) in the Manual group and 31.26 years (SD = 8.45) in the Oral-B iO group. Baseline ΔR30 and ΔR120 values were comparable across arms: mean ΔR30 at baseline was 0.69 in the Manual group and 0.74 in the Oral-B iO group; mean ΔR120 at baseline was 0.31 and 0.26, respectively. Discussion

The present trial demonstrated that use of an oscillating-rotating electric toothbrush was associated with significantly lower plaque accumulation, as measured by ΔR30, compared with manual brushing over a 24-week period. This effect was independent of baseline plaque levels, smoking status, and habitual oral hygiene behaviours, and was consistent across both the unadjusted and fully adjusted models. The findings extend the existing literature by providing objective, technology-derived evidence that a clinically relevant difference in plaque control emerges as early as 6 months after initiating use of a powered toothbrush, supporting its role as an effective tool for long-term oral hygiene management.13,25,26

The absence of a statistically significant treatment effect on ΔR120 in the adjusted analysis warrants careful interpretation. This parameter reflects the distribution of thick, mature plaque and calculus-like deposits, a component of the oral biofilm that is inherently less sensitive to short-term behavioural interventions and more dependent on individual biological characteristics, salivary composition, and the cumulative effects of professional oral hygiene.27,28 Unlike ΔR30, which captures the total area of active plaque formation and responds more readily to changes in brushing efficacy, ΔR120 is influenced by factors that extend beyond the mechanical action of the toothbrush and may require longer observation periods or more frequent professional intervention to show meaningful modification.22,29 The directional consistency of the effect across both outcomes nonetheless supports a coherent biological interpretation; the absence of a statistically significant effect on ΔR120 may partly reflect the limited sample available to detect a difference on this parameter, and larger studies may be needed to fully characterize its magnitude.

The differential pattern observed across smoking subgroups represents an exploratory finding of potential clinical interest. Among never-smokers, the Oral-B iO toothbrush was associated with significantly lower accumulation on both ΔR30 and ΔR120, whereas among smokers the between-arm differences, while directionally consistent, did not reach statistical significance. Cigarette smoking is known to alter the composition, maturation rate, and structural properties of the oral biofilm, increasing supragingival plaque accumulation and promoting conditions that favour calculus formation.19,30,31 These tobacco-related modifications to the biofilm environment may attenuate the mechanical advantage conferred by the powered toothbrush, though this hypothesis requires confirmation in larger trials specifically designed to test this interaction. Crucially, the treatment-by-smoking interaction was not statistically significant in the adjusted models, indicating that the available data do not support a formally different treatment effect between smokers and never-smokers. The apparent divergence in unadjusted subgroup results most likely reflects the smaller sample size and greater outcome variability within the smoker stratum, which reduces statistical power to detect a between-arm difference of the same magnitude.

The finding that baseline plaque levels were the strongest predictor of follow-up values in both outcome models has a direct clinical reading: patients who present with higher plaque burden at the start of a brushing intervention are likely to show greater absolute accumulation at follow-up, irrespective of the toothbrush used. This underscores the clinical importance of professional scaling and polishing as the starting point for any plaque control intervention, as it standardizes the oral environment and maximizes the window in which differences attributable to brushing technology can be observed and measured.22

The beneficial effect of the Oral-B iO toothbrush on plaque accumulation was maintained after adjustment for toothbrushing frequency, flossing, and mouthwash use, indicating that the observed advantage reflects the intrinsic properties of the oscillating-rotating technology rather than differences in oral hygiene compliance between groups. This is a clinically relevant observation, as it suggests that the device confers a measurable benefit even within a population that is broadly comparable in terms of oral hygiene habits.11,13

The adjusted between-arm difference in ΔR30 of -1.84 percentage points corresponds to a medium effect size, consistent with meta-analytic evidence reporting medium-range effects for oscillating-rotating versus manual toothbrushes using conventional plaque indices,13 and further supports the suitability of powered toothbrushes for plaque control in adult patients.

Some limitations of the present study merit consideration. The trial was conducted at a single dental clinic in Italy, which may limit the generalizability of findings to other clinical settings and populations with different baseline oral health profiles. The unblinded design, necessitated by the nature of the intervention, introduces the possibility of performance bias, although the objective nature of QLF measurements reduces the risk of detection bias. The 24-week follow-up, while sufficient to demonstrate differences in plaque accumulation, may not capture longer-term dynamics in calculus formation or periodontal outcomes. In addition, the trial was powered on ΔR30 as the primary outcome; the observed effect on ΔR120 was smaller than the effect size used for sample size calculation, and both this finding and the exploratory subgroup analyses stratified by smoking status should be interpreted with appropriate caution, as the trial was not specifically powered to detect treatment-by-smoking interaction effects. The proportion of variance in plaque outcomes explained by the fitted models reflects the inherent biological complexity of oral biofilm dynamics, which are shaped by individual-level factors, including salivary composition, microbial community structure, and brushing compliance, that extend beyond the scope of a standard clinical trial protocol. Importantly, the treatment coefficient remained statistically significant and directionally consistent across both adjusted and unadjusted models, supporting the robustness of the observed between-arm difference independently of overall model fit. The QLF metrics employed quantify plaque burden and surface maturation but do not capture microbiological or biochemical characteristics of the biofilm; future studies integrating fluorescence-based imaging with microbiome profiling would provide a more comprehensive characterization of plaque dynamics across smoking profiles. Furthermore, QLF assessment was restricted to the anterior buccal surfaces, consistent with the established methodology of published QLF-based plaque trials.19,22,29 Although plaque assessments in the anterior sector appear to be more accurate than total plaque scores15 and may provide a reliable indicator of whole-mouth plaque burden,16 the inclusion of multiple anatomical sites, particularly posterior regions, would have strengthened the quantitative assessment of the findings. Finally, brushing adherence was monitored through self-report only; objective compliance data, such as device usage logs or sensor-based brushing duration monitoring, were not collected. Although standardised instructions were provided to all participants, differential engagement between arms cannot be entirely excluded, as the primary aim of the trial was to evaluate toothbrush efficacy under real-world conditions of use rather than to assess protocol adherence.

Notwithstanding these limitations, the present study contributes several methodologically novel elements to the field. It is among the first RCTs to use QLF technology as the primary measurement tool for comparing electric and manual toothbrushing efficacy, providing an objective and reproducible alternative to conventional semiquantitative plaque indices. The inclusion of both smokers and never-smokers as distinct subgroups introduces a clinically important dimension that is largely absent from the existing toothbrush trial literature. From a translational perspective, the capacity of QLF technology to objectively quantify plaque accumulation in real time offers a promising avenue for monitoring oral hygiene in smokers within routine clinical practice. In a population where the visual detection of plaque differences may be challenging and motivational barriers to behaviour change are high, the immediate, patient-visible feedback provided by fluorescence-based imaging may serve as a practical tool for engaging smokers in oral health improvement and supporting cessation counselling.

Conclusions

Results of this randomized controlled trial indicated that an oscillating-rotating electric toothbrush produced significantly lower plaque accumulation than a manual toothbrush over 24 weeks, as indexed by QLF-derived ΔR30. This effect was robust to adjustment for baseline plaque levels, smoking status, and habitual oral hygiene behaviours. No significant effect on ΔR120 was observed, consistent with the known resistance of thick, mature plaque and calculus-like deposits to short-term behavioural interventions, and this finding warrants further investigation in larger trials. Subgroup analyses suggested that the treatment benefit may be attenuated in smokers, though the treatment-by-smoking interaction was not statistically significant, and these findings should be regarded as hypothesis-generating. Collectively, these findings suggest that oscillating-rotating powered toothbrushes may provide greater reduction of QLF-assessed plaque accumulation compared with manual toothbrushes in adults attending routine dental care. Future trials incorporating multiple anatomical sites and clinically relevant periodontal outcomes are needed to further clarify the extent and clinical significance of these benefits.
