Structural basis for auto-inhibition of the Rac1/Cdc42 guanine nucleotide exchange factor DOCK6 by oligomer formation
Authors: Mutsuko Kukimoto-Niino, Kazushige Katsura, Kota Yoshimura, Yoshiko Ishizuka-Katsura, Yuki Miyamoto, Mayumi Yonemochi, Kazuharu Hanada, Junji Yamauchi, Richard W. Wong, Mikako Shirouzu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The guanine nucleotide exchange factor DOCK6 is important for neurite outgrowth, as well as cell migration and invasion, through the activation of Rac1 and Cdc42—members of the Rho family of GTPases that regulate the actin cytoskeleton. However, the precise molecular mechanisms by which DOCK6 regulates the intracellular GTPase signaling remain unclear. Here, we present cryo-electron microscopy structures of DOCK6 alone and in complex with Rac1 and Cdc42. The DOCK6–Rac1 and DOCK6–Cdc42 complexes exhibit similar homodimeric structures, with local differences in the catalytic domain of DOCK6 owing to distinct interactions with Rac1 and Cdc42. In contrast, apo-DOCK6 exhibits a closed auto-inhibited conformation in tetrameric and octameric assemblies, with the catalytic and membrane-binding domains contacting each other between two DOCK6 dimers. High-speed atomic force microscopy reveals transitions among multiple oligomers in solution. Biochemical and cellular functional analyses demonstrate that the N-terminal region of DOCK6 plays an auto-inhibitory role, supporting the structural findings. Overall, we propose a mechanism by which DOCK6 activity is spatiotemporally regulated within cells through oligomerization. These findings provide a framework for future studies of DOCK-family GEFs and their broader roles in cell regulation and human disease.

Introduction

The Rho family of small guanosine triphosphatases (GTPases), Rho, Rac, and Cdc42, regulates many biological processes through the reorganization of the actin cytoskeleton1,2. They act as molecular switches that cycle between inactive GDP-bound and active GTP-bound states. They are activated by guanine nucleotide exchange factors (GEFs), which catalyze the exchange of GDP for GTP, and are inactivated by GTPase-activating proteins, which accelerate their intrinsic GTPase activity. Additionally, their cytosolic and membrane localization is regulated by guanine nucleotide dissociation inhibitors, which conceal the C-terminal geranylgeranyl group.

The DOCK family represents a group of GEFs that serve as critical regulators of small Rho family GTPases, orchestrating a wide range of cellular processes, including cytoskeletal organization, polarity, migration, and morphogenesis3. Among them, DOCK6 has emerged as a particularly important member due to its central role in regulating actin cytoskeletal rearrangements, modulating cell signaling pathways, and orchestrating dynamic morphological changes in cells and tissues. Through these functions, DOCK6 contributes to essential biological events such as tissue development, wound repair, and neuronal connectivity.

Clinically, mutations in the DOCK6 gene have been identified as a major cause of a recessive form of Adams–Oliver syndrome (AOS)—a rare congenital disorder characterized by aplasia cutis congenita, terminal limb defects, and, in some cases, neurological anomalies4,5. Various DOCK6 mutations have been reported in AOS patients, including nonsense and frameshift mutations, as well as large deletions and insertions in the DHR-2 domain, which may lead to loss of function or loss of DOCK6 protein expression6,7. However, the precise functional consequences of missense mutations in DOCK6 remain unclear. The pathogenic mechanism is thought to arise from disruption of cytoskeletal dynamics, leading to abnormal morphogenesis and defective tissue development. Previous findings have highlighted the indispensable role of DOCK6 in maintaining proper cellular signaling and cytoskeletal function, while also underscoring the severe consequences of its dysfunction for cell morphogenesis and development.

The DOCK family is an atypical, evolutionarily conserved family of GEFs for Rho GTPases8,9. Unlike the typical Dbl family GEFs, DOCK family GEFs are characterized by a phospholipid-binding domain called the Dock homology region-1 (DHR-1) and a catalytic domain called DHR-28,10. Eleven human proteins, DOCK1–DOCK11, function in various developmental processes and in the immune system through the spatiotemporal activation of Rac and Cdc42 proteins3,11,12. DOCK proteins are classified into four subfamilies based on their sequence homology: DOCK A–D. DOCK-A/B subfamily proteins (DOCK1/2/3/4/5) interact with their partner ELMO proteins to mediate intracellular Rac1 activation13. Recent cryo-EM analyses of DOCK2 and DOCK5 have revealed a common structure of the DOCK–ELMO1 complex bound to Rac114,15. The N-terminal domain (NTD) of ELMO1 is involved in the auto-inhibition of the DOCK–ELMO1 complex, which is relieved by binding of the NTD to upstream signaling proteins, such as RhoG GTPase and the BAI G protein-coupled receptor14,16. In contrast, the DOCK-C/D subfamily proteins (DOCK6/7/8/9/10/11) lack the sequences required for binding to ELMO proteins. These proteins primarily activate Cdc42, and some activate both Cdc42 and Rac17. Although the crystal structures of the isolated domains have been determined18–22, the full-length structures of the DOCK-C/D subfamily proteins, which are essential for understanding their mechanism of action, have yet to be elucidated.

DOCK6 belongs to the DOCK-C subfamily and activates Rac1 and Cdc42 to promote neurite outgrowth and axonal extension during neuronal development23,24. Expression of DHR-2, the catalytic domain of DOCK6, induces cellular morphological changes, whereas the expression of full-length DOCK6 does not, suggesting that DOCK6 is auto-inhibited via an unknown mechanism23,25. Furthermore, DOCK6 is activated by dephosphorylation by PP2A during the early stages of neural development and is inhibited by phosphorylation of Ser1194 by Akt during later stages24. These studies have suggested that DOCK6 activity is regulated spatiotemporally and reversibly, although the related structural basis remains elusive.

Here, we report the cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM) structures of DOCK6 alone and in complex with Rac1 and Cdc42, revealing the structural basis of DOCK6 regulation and its dual GTPase specificity.

Results

Cryo-EM structure determination

Human DOCK6 was expressed in mammalian cells, and we obtained cryo-EM structures of the full-length ~470 kDa homodimer complexed with Cdc42 and Rac1. For complex formation with DOCK6, we used the GTP-binding domains of Cdc42 and Rac1 carrying the nucleotide-deficient mutation G15A, because GEFs stabilize the nucleotide-free state of GTPases26. However, the purified DOCK6 protein did not form a stable complex with either Cdc42G15A or Rac1G15A, as determined by size-exclusion chromatography. This was expected, given the relatively low GEF activity of DOCK6 toward both Cdc42 and Rac122. Therefore, DOCK6 protein was cross-linked with Cdc42G15A using bis(sulfosuccinimidyl)suberate (BS3)—a chemical cross-linker that facilitates cryo-EM analysis of highly dynamic macromolecular complexes27.

Cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM) single-particle analysis of the DOCK6–Cdc42 complex yielded two dimeric reconstructions with nearly identical particles, in which the two side domains are positioned differently relative to the central domain, revealing the structural flexibility (Supplementary Fig. 1; see the next section for details). Merging the two reconstructions yielded a dimeric reconstruction at 4.5 Å resolution (Fig. 1a and Table 1). Subsequent symmetry expansion and focused refinement allowed us to reconstruct a single DOCK6–Cdc42 protomer at 3.9 Å resolution. The maps were of sufficient quality to allow for fitting the AlphaFold228 model of DOCK6. The N- and C-termini, and some internal regions, were largely disordered and were therefore excluded from the final models (Supplementary Fig. 2a). Cryo-EM data collection, refinement, and validation statistics

Using DOCK6 samples cross-linked with Rac1G15A, the cryo-EM structure of the DOCK6–Rac1 complex was similarly determined at 5.3 Å resolution for the dimer and 4.2 Å resolution for the protomer region (Fig. 1a, Table 1, and Supplementary Fig. 3). During the course of single-particle analysis, we further obtained tetrameric reconstructions of DOCK6 alone and in complex with Rac1, at 4.3 Å and 6.9 Å resolution, respectively (Fig. 1b, Table 1, and Supplementary Figs. 3, 4).

Size-exclusion chromatography of purified, uncrosslinked DOCK6 protein showed a single peak at approximately 610 kDa (Fig. 1c), which corresponds to a molecular weight intermediate between the dimer (474 kDa) and tetramer (948 kDa). Blue-native polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (Fig. 1d) and mass photometry (Fig. 1e, f) confirmed that the purified, uncrosslinked DOCK6 protein formed dimers and tetramers, but not monomers. Mass photometry also revealed the presence of minor oligomers, including hexamers (1.4 MDa), octamers (1.9 MDa), decamers (2.4 MDa), and dodecamers (2.8 MDa).

Although cryo-EM analysis of uncrosslinked DOCK6 samples failed due to their flexibility, analysis of cross-linked samples identified tetramers, octamers, and larger oligomers of DOCK6 in micrographs and 2D class averages (Fig. 1g and Supplementary Fig. 5). Subsequent 3D classification and refinement allowed us to reconstruct the DOCK6 octamer at 7.5 Å resolution (Fig. 1h, Table 1). Although these maps were of sufficient quality to unambiguously assign domain arrangements, the limited resolution indicated that the significance of interactions had to be verified by functional experiments. The determined DOCK6 structures and their main features are described below.

DOCK6–Cdc42 and DOCK6–Rac1 complexes

The DOCK6–Cdc42 and DOCK6–Rac1 complexes form similar curved dimers (Fig. 1a). DOCK6 consists of an N-terminal domain (NTD), C2 domain (or DHR-3 domain29), DHR-1 domain, armadillo repeats (ARM) domain, and DHR-2 domain (Fig. 2a). The catalytic DHR-2 domain, which binds Cdc42 or Rac1, is located at the bottom center of the dimer, while the C2 and DHR-1 domains are positioned in tandem at the top tip of each protomer (Figs. 1a, 2b). A segment of the NTD (residues 44–256) folds into several helices and β-strands, interacting extensively with the C2 and DHR-1 domains, which share a common β-sandwich fold, and with the ARM domain, a long superhelical domain. The DHR-1 domain contains Arg563 and Arg568, which correspond to PIP2/PIP3-binding residues in DOCK821 (Fig. 2c). The C2 domain contains a cluster of lysine and arginine residues that are conserved in the DOCK-C subfamily and its plant homolog SPIKE1, whose C2 domain has been reported to bind phospholipids29. The ARM domain is followed by a three-lobed DHR-2 domain (lobes A–C), with lobe A forming a dimeric interface conserved among the DOCK family members18,30 (Supplementary Fig. 2b, c). Cdc42 and Rac1 are bound to the catalytic region of the DHR-2 domain, specifically lobes B and C (Fig. 1a). The structure of the DOCK6–Cdc42 dimer complex showed deviations in the relative positions of the N-terminal region (NTD, C2, DHR-1, and ARM domains) with respect to the DHR-2 domain, which were attributed to a conformational change in the hinge region between the ARM and DHR-2 domains (Supplementary Fig. 2d, e). Similar conformational changes have also been observed in cryo-EM analyses of the DOCK2–ELMO1 and DOCK5–ELMO1 complexes14–16. Therefore, flexibility between the ARM and DHR-2 domains is considered a common structural feature of the DOCK family. In the current structural models of the DOCK6–Cdc42 and DOCK6–Rac1 dimers, when the DHR-1 domain binds to the membrane, Cdc42 or Rac1 is separated by ~100 Å from the membrane, making simultaneously binding unlikely (Fig. 1a). However, considering the observed structural flexibility, it is possible that the DOCK6 dimer may adopt a flatter conformation on the membrane, allowing the DHR-2 domain to access the membrane-anchored Rac1 or Cdc42 (Supplementary Fig. 6). This idea is consistent with our recent findings that the DOCK5–ELMO1 homodimer adapts from a curved to a flat conformation upon membrane binding due to flexibility between the ARM and DHR-2 domains31.

Dual GTPase specificity

DOCK6 forms an interface with Cdc42 and Rac1, with buried surface areas of ~2000 Å2 and ~1800 Å2, respectively. Superimposition of the two complex structures based on Cdc42 and Rac1 showed that the bound Cdc42 and Rac1 adopt similar nucleotide-free conformations (Fig. 2d). In contrast, in the DOCK6–Rac1 complex, lobe C in the DHR-2 domain was ~2.2 Å farther from the bound GTPase (as measured by the α12 displacement of DOCK6) than in the DOCK6–Cdc42 complex. These differences are likely due to distinct interactions of DOCK6 with Cdc42 and Rac1. Therefore, we compared the interactions of DOCK6 with Cdc42 and Rac1, focusing on the key interfaces involved in determining GTPase specificity17,20,22,25,30 (Fig. 2e–h). First, the GTPase switch 1 region contains Lys27 and Ser30 of Cdc42, which potentially form hydrogen bonds with residues in DOCK6 (lobe B of the DHR-2 domain), whereas Ala27 and Gly30 of Rac1 cannot form equivalent interactions (Fig. 2e, f). In contrast, Ile33 of Rac1, containing a larger side chain than Val33 of Cdc42, might partially fill the space occupied by Lys27 of Cdc42. Second, the interface involving the GTPase interswitch and switch 2 regions contains Trp56 of Rac1, displaying a larger side chain than Phe56 of Cdc42. This difference might lead to lobe C of the DOCK6 DHR-2 domain interacting more distantly with Rac1 than with Cdc42 (Fig. 2g, h). Thus, these structures suggest that DOCK6 binds more tightly to Cdc42 than to Rac1.

We performed surface plasmon resonance (SPR) analysis to determine the binding affinity of the DOCK6 DHR-2 domain for Cdc42 and Rac1. First, we analyzed the binding of wild-type Cdc42 and Rac1 (GDP-bound state) to the DOCK6 DHR-2 domain captured on a sensor chip (Fig. 2i). The binding response of Cdc42WT to DOCK6 DHR-2 was fitted using a 1:1 binding model, and the affinity was determined from the binding kinetics (association rate constant, ka and dissociation rate constant, kd), yielding an equilibrium dissociation constant (KD) of 0.86 μM. In contrast, the binding level of Rac1WT to DOCK6 DHR-2 (reflected in response units, RU) was weaker than that of Cdc42WT, making it difficult to determine the KD even when tested at 10-fold higher concentrations. We next analyzed the binding of G15A mutants of Cdc42 and Rac1 (nucleotide-free state) to DOCK6 DHR-2, because these mutants have been reported to enhance binding to GEF24 and were used for structural determination in this study. As expected, both Cdc42 and Rac1 G15A mutants exhibited higher levels of binding to DOCK6 DHR-2 compared to their wild-type counterparts (Fig. 2i, j). Based on the fitting to a 1:1 binding model, the KD values of Cdc42G15A and Rac1G15A for DOCK6 DHR-2 were 0.36 μM and 8.9 μM, respectively. Therefore, the affinity of DOCK6 DHR-2 for Cdc42G15A was estimated to be ~25-fold higher than its affinity for Rac1G15A. The results of the SPR-based binding assay are consistent with previous pull-down assays showing that DOCK6 DHR-2 binds more strongly to Cdc42 than to Rac123, supporting our structural analysis.

DOCK6 tetramer

The structure of the apo-DOCK6 tetramer consists of two DOCK6 dimers, forming a compact assembly with a total size of approximately 150 Å × 150 Å × 190 Å (Fig. 1b). The DHR-1 domain of one dimer is adjacent to the DHR-2 domain of the other dimer (Fig. 3a, b and Supplementary Fig. 4d). Thus, the phospholipid binding site (in the DHR-1 domain) of one dimer and the GTPase binding site (in the DHR-2 domain) of the other dimer are mutually occluded, suggesting a closed, auto-inhibited conformation. The two dimers are likely in contact with each other via the convex surfaces of their ARM domains, including Val918, Ser920, and Arg924 (Fig. 3a). The structure of the DOCK6 tetramer in complex with Rac1 is slightly wider than that of the apo-DOCK6 tetramer, measuring approximately 170 Å × 170 Å × 190 Å (Fig. 1b). Unlike the apo-DOCK6 tetramer, the two DOCK6 dimers are loosely assembled, and the ARM domains of the two dimers are separated by a distance of 20 Å (Fig. 3c). The loose assembly of the two DOCK6 dimers allows Rac1 to bind in the space between the DHR-2 domain of one dimer and the DHR-1 domain of the other dimer (Fig. 3d and Supplementary Fig. 4h). Compared with the DOCK6 tetramer alone, in the Rac1-bound state, the N-terminal region (NTD, C2, DHR-1, and ARM domains) shifts outward due to a conformational change in the hinge region between the ARM and DHR-2 domains (Fig. 3e). Furthermore, lobe C of the DHR-2 domain shifts inward compared to the apo state due to interaction with Rac1. Similar conformational changes in lobe C between the Rac1-bound and unbound states have also been observed in DOCK2–ELMO1 and DOCK5–ELMO1 complexes14,16. Although the closed conformation of the DOCK6 tetramer is quite different from that of the ELMO1-bound DOCK2 and DOCK5 homodimers, these structures suggest a common autoinhibitory mechanism involving a conformational change in the catalytic DHR-2 domain.

DOCK6 octamer

The structure of the DOCK6 octamer consists of two DOCK6 tetramers in a closed conformation and spans a total length of ~380 Å (Fig. 1f). The two DOCK6 tetramers contact each other at the interface between the ARM domain and the DHR-2 domain (lobe C), forming a pseudo-two-fold symmetric interface (Fig. 4a, b). Furthermore, a short segment C-terminal to the DHR-2 domain (residues 2020–2025) occupies space at the ARM domain–DHR-2 domain interface and may be involved in intermolecular interactions (Supplementary Fig. 5f). Based on the observed binding mode between tetramers, we hypothesized that one tetramer could simultaneously interact with two other tetramers. Auto-inhibition of DOCK6-tetramer

The closed conformation of the apo-DOCK6 tetramer suggests that the DHR-1 domain sterically suppresses binding of the DHR-2 domain to Rac1 and Cdc42 through intermolecular interactions that occur only in the tetramer (Fig. 3a, b). Therefore, we tested the effect of removing steric hindrance caused by the DHR-1 domain on the GEF activity of DOCK6. A deletion mutant, DOCK6 ΔN, was constructed by deleting from the N-terminus to the DHR-1 domain (Fig. 2a). The DOCK6 ΔN mutant was expressed and purified in the same manner as the wild-type (full-length) protein and used for in vitro GEF assays. As expected, the DOCK6 ΔN mutant showed a 1.8-fold increase in GEF activity for Cdc42 compared to wild-type DOCK6 (Fig. 5a, b). We used the same assay to assess ARM domain–ARM domain interactions observed in the apo-DOCK6 tetramer structure (Fig. 3a). We hypothesized that disruption of ARM domain contacts might destabilize the closed conformation, thereby facilitating DOCK6 binding to substrate GTPases. To test this, we generated a DOCK6 mutant in which Val918, Ser920, and Arg924 of the ARM domain were simultaneously replaced with Ala. As expected, the V918A/S920A/R924A triple mutant exhibited a 2.0-fold increase in GEF activity compared to the wild-type protein (Fig. 5c, d). These results suggest that interactions between ARM domains stabilize the closed conformation of DOCK6.

Val918, Ser920, and Arg924 of DOCK6 are conserved in the other DOCK-C subfamily proteins, DOCK7 and DOCK8, with the only exception that Ser920 of DOCK6 is conservatively replaced by Thr in DOCK8. Therefore, DOCK7 and DOCK8 may also form closed tetramer conformations similar to that of DOCK6.

We performed mass photometry analysis to determine the oligomeric state of DOCK6 mutants. The V918A/S920A/R924A triple mutant showed a significant increase in dimers and a decrease in tetramers compared to wild-type DOCK6 (Fig. 5e, f). Furthermore, hexamers and octamers were absent in the triple mutant. This result suggests that the interface between the ARM domains is involved in tetramer formation of DOCK6. In contrast, the ΔN mutant showed a loss of hexamers and octamers, resulting in an increase in tetramers but no change in dimers. This suggests that deletion of the NTD, C2, and DHR-1 domains affects the inter-tetramer interface near the DHR-1 domain (Fig. 4a, b) while maintaining tetramer formation.

The N-terminal region of DOCK6 auto-inhibits its cellular function

As we demonstrated, using purified proteins, that the region from the N-terminus to the DHR-1 domain is involved in the auto-inhibition of DOCK6 in vitro, we next assessed the functional role of this region in cells. FLAG-tagged DOCK6 wild type (WT) and DOCK6 ΔN were transiently expressed in COS-7 cells, and their expression and effects on actin structure were investigated (Fig. 5g, h). Expression of DOCK6 WT did not affect actin structure, consistent with a previous report that full-length DOCK6 is functionally suppressed in cells23. In contrast, expression of DOCK6 ΔN significantly increased the number of cells with lamellipodia and filopodia, suggesting intracellular activation of both Rac1 and Cdc42. These results indicate that the deleted N-terminal region is involved in the autoinhibition of DOCK6 in cells. Furthermore, expression of the V918A/S920A/R924A triple mutant also significantly increased the number of cells with lamellipodia (Fig. 5g, h). Taken together, these results suggest that DOCK6 forms closed conformations in unstimulated cells.

To examine whether DOCK6 oligomerization occurs in cells, we performed cross-linking experiments in cell lysates using phosphate-buffered saline, which is close to physiological conditions. FLAG-tagged DOCK6 was expressed in FreeStyle 293-F cells, and lysine residues in the cell lysates were cross-linked with BS3. Immunoblotting revealed that FLAG-tagged DOCK6 was detected in the cell lysates as a much larger species than the dimer (470 kDa), similar to the purified conditions (Fig. 5i). The results suggest that oligomerization occurs in purified DOCK6 as well as in the cellular environment, supporting the idea that DOCK6 form a closed conformation within cells.

DOCK6 dynamics in solution

To directly capture the dynamic oligomerization behavior of DOCK6 in solution, we performed high-speed atomic force microscopy (HS-AFM) under near-physiological buffer conditions32. Time-lapse imaging revealed successive spontaneous association events between DOCK6 particles, visualizing both dimer-to-tetramer and tetramer-to-octamer transitions in real time (Fig. 6a–d and Supplementary Movies 1 and 2). In a representative sequence, two laterally mobile dimers approached each other, aligned via side-by-side contact, and formed a symmetric tetramer within approximately 30 s (Fig. 6a, c). In a separate observation, two preformed tetramers similarly underwent gradual docking to form an elongated octameric complex over a ~2.5-min time window (Fig. 6b, d). The final octamer structure exhibited a double-lobed architecture at each end, consistent with the modeled octamer composed of two tetramers aligned via their DHR-2 regions. Intermediate stages of partial contact and transient separation were observed for both transitions, suggesting a flexible and reversible assembly pathway.

These HS-AFM results provide direct nanoscale evidence that DOCK6 dynamically interconverts between oligomeric states via a hierarchical assembly mechanism. These transitions occurred spontaneously during low-invasive HS-AFM imaging, indicating that oligomer formation is an intrinsic, self-regulated property of DOCK6 in solution. Together with the static cryo-EM structures and biochemical GEF activity assays, these observations support a regulatory model in which dimer–dimer and tetramer–tetramer associations stabilize the closed, auto-inhibited conformation of DOCK6. The observed oligomer dynamics likely reflect a tunable conformational equilibrium that governs DOCK6 activation in response to upstream signaling and membrane localization.

PIP2 binding and Ser1194 phosphorylation alter DOCK6 oligomerization

Considering the proximity of the DHR-1 and DHR-2 domains in the closed conformation of the DOCK6 tetramer (Fig. 3a–d), phospholipid binding to the DHR-1 domain may inhibit DOCK6 oligomerization, thereby shifting the conformational equilibrium toward the dimer. The DHR-1 domain of DOCK8, whose phospholipid-binding site is highly conserved with that of DOCK6, preferentially binds PI(4,5)P221. Therefore, we performed mass photometry analysis of DOCK6 in the presence of PI(4,5)P2 diC4, a soluble analog of PI(4,5)P2, to examine the effect of phospholipid binding on DOCK6 oligomerization. As expected, addition of PI(4,5)P2 diC4 significantly increased the proportion of DOCK6 dimers, with a concomitant decrease in tetramers and elimination of hexamers and octamers (Supplementary Fig. 7a, b). These results suggest that increased PIP2/PIP3 levels in response to upstream signaling promote the conformational transition of DOCK6 from closed tetramers to open dimers.

Previous studies have demonstrated that phosphorylated DOCK6 and its mimic S1194E mutant have reduced activity compared to unphosphorylated DOCK6 or its mimic S1194A mutant24. The segment containing Ser1194 (residues 1185–1242) is disordered in all DOCK6 structures in this study and is predicted to be located on the concave surface of the ARM domain (Supplementary Fig. 2a). To investigate whether phosphorylation of S1194 affects DOCK6 oligomerization, we performed mass photometry analysis of the S1194E and S1194A mutants (Supplementary Fig. 7c, d). Compared with the S1194E mutant, the S1194A mutant exhibited a significant increase in dimers and a decrease in tetramers and octamers. The observed difference in oligomer distribution is consistent with the suppressed activity of the S1194E mutant compared to the S1194A mutant24. Therefore, Ser1194 phosphorylation and dephosphorylation (by Akt and PP2A, respectively24) may reversibly alter the dimer–tetramer conformational equilibrium to fine-tune the intracellular activity of DOCK6. Future studies are needed to clarify the structural basis by which phosphorylation at Ser1194 promotes the dimer-to-tetramer transition of DOCK6.

AOS-associated mutations

The multiple DOCK6 structures presented in this study provide an opportunity for dissecting the molecular basis of AOS, a disease caused by DOCK6 mutations. We purified and biochemically characterized four AOS-associated DOCK6 mutants V263D, L1016P, E1052K, and R1596W. The SEC profiles of all four DOCK6 mutants showed increased void elution compared to the wild type, suggesting destabilization of the expressed proteins (Fig. 7c). Additionally, the V263D, L1016P, and E1052K mutants showed broad peaks centered at molecular weights of 1.2–1.6 MDa, suggesting higher degree of oligomerization compared to the wild type (Fig. 7c). In contrast, the R1596W mutant eluted at an apparent molecular weight of approximately 590 kDa, comparable to that of the wild type (Fig. 7c). Detailed analysis using mass photometry revealed that the R1596W mutant existed primarily as a dimer (Fig. 7d, e), suggesting that the R1596W mutation inhibits tetrameric or higher-order oligomer formations, presumably by affecting adjacent ARM domain conformation. These results indicate that all four missense mutations affect the stability and quaternary structure of the DOCK6 protein.

We next tested the GEF activity of four AOS-associated DOCK6 mutants using purified proteins (Fig. 7f, g). The V263D, L1016P, and E1052K mutants showed GEF activity comparable to that of the wild-type DOCK6. The reduced activity of these mutants is likely due to higher-order oligomerization (Fig. 7c), whereas the reduced activity of wild-type DOCK6 is due to the closed conformation. The R1596W mutant also exhibited activity comparable with that of wild-type DOCK6. As this mutant was observed to exist primarily as a dimer (Fig. 7d, e), its reduced activity comparable to that of the wild type suggests that the R1596W mutation affects the GTPase-binding site within the same DHR-2 domain (Fig. 7a). Taken together, these results suggest that the pathogenic mechanisms of the four DOCK6 missense mutations are primarily due to reduced protein production and activity.

Discussion

Beyond its established role in congenital syndromes, recent evidence suggests that DOCK6 may also contribute to the pathophysiology of cancer33–35. Cytoskeletal remodeling and intracellular signaling govern normal morphogenetic processes and play pivotal roles in tumor progression. Dysregulated cell motility and adhesion, both of which are tightly controlled by Rho GTPase signaling pathways, are fundamental drivers of invasion and metastasis. Accordingly, alterations in DOCK6 expression or function may provide tumor cells with an enhanced capacity to disseminate and colonize distant tissues33,36. Although this area of research is still emerging, the potential oncogenic or tumor-suppressive role of DOCK6 warrants further exploration. Importantly, studying DOCK6 provides a unique opportunity to connect developmental biology with cancer research. On one hand, genetic disruption of DOCK6 in AOS underscores its indispensable role in tissue morphogenesis and cytoskeletal regulation. On the other hand, aberrant regulation of the same cellular processes appears to facilitate malignant transformation and metastatic spread. This duality suggests that DOCK6 may serve as a molecular hub at the intersection of developmental disorders and cancer biology.

Understanding the precise molecular mechanisms by which DOCK6 regulates Rho family GTPases, reorganizes actin cytoskeletons, and integrates intracellular signaling will therefore be essential. Such insights have the potential to clarify the pathogenesis of rare genetic disorders, such as AOS, and reveal potential therapeutic avenues in oncology. Ultimately, research for DOCK6 might provide a foundation for the development of targeted therapies that address both inherited defects in morphogenesis and acquired dysregulation in tumor progression.

Using cryo-EM, we showed that DOCK6 is a large homodimer characterized by a central catalytic domain (DHR-2) and membrane-binding side domains (C2 and DHR-1) flexibly linked by a superhelical (ARM) domain. DOCK6 exists not only in dimers but also as tetramers and larger oligomers in solution. This is notable, given that previous studies showed that DOCK proteins are homodimers14,15,37. The DOCK6 tetramer adopts a closed conformation, with the catalytic and membrane-binding domains occluded by the assembly of two dimers. Based on these findings, we propose a mechanism for intracellular DOCK6 regulation through the formation of multiple oligomers (Fig. 6e). In the basal state, DOCK6 exists primarily in the cytoplasm as an open dimer, which tends to form closed, auto-inhibited tetramers and octamers. In response to cell stimulation, DOCK6 dimers bind to PIP2 or PIP3 and translocate to the plasma membrane, where they activate Rac1 and Cdc42 to initiate actin cytoskeletal remodeling. As the catalytic DHR-2 domain approaches membrane-bound Rac1 and Cdc42, DOCK6 is predicted to undergo a conformational change from a curved dimer to a flat dimer. DOCK6 oligomers undergo dynamic transitions, and the conformational equilibrium can be altered by factors such as PIP2 or PIP3 levels and the phosphorylation or dephosphorylation of Ser1194. Future studies will be required to clarify the conformational dynamics of DOCK6 on the membrane.

The regulatory model proposed in this study might retain broader implications for related proteins that share structural or functional features with DOCK6. In particular, regulation through oligomeric state alterations could represent a general mechanism within the DOCK family. Previous studies on DOCK1, DOCK2, DOCK4, and DOCK9 have described interactions between the N-terminal and the catalytic DHR-2 domains, suggesting intramolecular regulatory contacts38–40, although the structural basis and regulatory effector mechanism remains unclear. These results are compatible with the idea that structural rearrangements, potentially coupled to oligomeric-state transitions, contribute to the functional control in DOCK proteins, providing a framework for future investigations.

Here, we combined cryo-EM with biochemical and cell-based analyses to propose that DOCK6 regulates its molecular activity through changes in its oligomeric state, and HS-AFM analyses provided supportive observations of dynamic structural transitions. Nevertheless, the structures obtained here represent conformations stabilized under specific experimental conditions and might not fully capture the dynamic and heterogeneous cellular environment. In addition, the analysis of disease-associated DOCK6 mutations, at least based on in vitro data, did not fully align with our proposed model, in which enhanced oligomerization promotes autoinhibition and thereby reduces activity. For example, R1596W exhibited reduced oligomerization but did not show increased GEF activity, suggesting that additional effects, such as impaired GTPase binding or altered cellular regulation, may contribute to its functional defect. These discrepancies indicate that the molecular basis of DOCK6 activation is likely more complex in vivo. Although our data support a link between changes in oligomeric state and cellular function, direct causal relationships remain to be established through further spatiotemporal and quantitative analyses. Therefore, the mechanism proposed here should be considered a working model, the physiological relevance of which should be refined and validated by future studies integrating in situ structural approaches, live-cell imaging, high-resolution dynamic measurements, and the quantitative analyses of dynamic interactions.

Methods

Expression and purification of the human DOCK6

The gene encoding human DOCK6 (residues 1–2047) was subcloned into the mammalian expression vector pOriP41 with FLAG and streptavidin-binding peptide tags, followed by a tobacco etch virus (TEV) protease cleavage site at the N-terminus. The DOCK6 expression vector was transfected into FreeStyle 293-F cells (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) using 293fectin™ transfection reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The cells were grown in FreeStyle293 Expression medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific) using an incubation shaker equipped with a CO2 controller (TAITEC, Saitama, Japan) and harvested 48 h post-transfection. The cells were disrupted by sonication in buffer containing 100 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0), 300 mM NaCl, 100 mM arginine-HCl, 100 mM glutamic acid-Na, 500 mM sucrose, 5 mM DTT, and complete protease inhibitor cocktail (Sigma-Aldrich, St Louis, MO, USA). Cell debris was removed by centrifugation at 10,000 × g for 20 min. The supernatant was incubated with Streptavidin Sepharose resin (Cytiva, Marlborough, MA, USA) for 2 h. The resin was washed two or three times with 20 column volumes of buffer containing 100 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0), 300 mM NaCl, 100 mM arginine-HCl, 100 mM glutamic acid-Na, 500 mM sucrose, 5 mM DTT, 1 mM ethylenediamine tetraacetic acid, and 2 mM tris(2-carboxyethyl)phosphine (TCEP). Proteins were eluted in wash buffer supplemented with 4 mM desthiobiotin. The eluate was treated with TEV protease at 4˚C overnight. The protein was loaded onto a HiLoad 16/600 Superose 6 pg (Cytiva) size-exclusion column equilibrated with buffer containing 20 mM 4-(2-hydroxyethyl)-1-piperazineethanesulfonic acid-sodium hydroxide (HEPES-NaOH; pH 8.0), 300 mM NaCl, and 1 mM TCEP. Peak fractions were pooled, concentrated to 0.2−0.3 mg/mL, and used for the preparation of cross-linked samples for cryo-EM.

Cryo-EM sample preparation

To prepare DOCK6–Cdc42 and DOCK6-Rac1 complexes, nucleotide-deficient G15A mutations were introduced into human Cdc42 (residues 1–188, C188S) and Rac1 (residues 1–177) fragments, which were subcloned into the expression vector pCR2.1 (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The Cdc42G15A and Rac1G15A mutants were expressed as fusions with an N-terminal His affinity tag and a TEV protease cleavage site using an Escherichia coli cell-free system42,43 and purified by affinity chromatography, tag removal, and size exclusion chromatography, as previously described18. DOCK6 (1 μM) was mixed with excess Cdc42G15A (19 μM) or Rac1G15A (27 μM) and incubated with 1 mM bis(sulfosuccinimidyl)suberate (BS3) (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 15 min at 23 °C. The samples were quenched with 20 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0) and loaded onto a HiLoad 16/600 Superose 6 pg size-exclusion column equilibrated with buffer containing 20 mM HEPES-NaOH (pH 8.0), 300 mM NaCl, and 1 mM TCEP.

To prepare apo-DOCK6 samples, DOCK6 alone (1.2 μM) was incubated with 1 mM BS3 for 15 min at room temperature, quenched with 20 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0), and loaded onto a HiLoad 16/600 Superose 6 pg size exclusion column equilibrated with buffer containing 20 mM HEPES-NaOH (pH 8.0), 300 mM NaCl, and 1 mM TCEP.

Cryo-EM grid preparation and data collection

Quantifoil R1.2/1.3 300 mesh copper grids were coated with a thin layer of continuous carbon film using a JEE-420 vacuum evaporator (JEOL, Tokyo, Japan). The carbon-coated grids were freshly glow-discharged using a PIB-10 Plasma Ion Bombarder (Vacuum Device, Ibaraki, Japan) at 5 mA for 9 s. Protein samples were prepared at 0.2 μM (DOCK6–Cdc42 and DOCK6–Rac1) or 0.8 μM (apo-DOCK6) in buffer containing 20 mM HEPES–NaOH, pH 8.0, 300 mM NaCl, 1 mM TCEP, and 0.06% digitonin. Each protein sample (3 μL) was applied to the discharged grids in a chamber set at 4 °C and 100% humidity, blotted for 1 s, and plunge-frozen in liquid ethane using Vitrobot Mark IV (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

All datasets were collected using a Titan Krios G4 electron microscope (Thermo Fisher Scientific) operating at 300 kV and equipped with a K3 direct electron detector (Gatan, Pleasanton, CA, USA) in counting mode. The movie datasets were collected at a nominal magnification of 64,000×, corresponding to a calibrated pixel size of 1.33 Å/pixel. For the DOCK6–Cdc42 dataset, each movie contained 48 frames over a 3.80 s exposure time, using a dose rate of 22.9 e−/pixel/s, resulting in a total accumulated dose of 48.5 e− Å−2. The nominal defocus range was set between −0.8 and −2.0 μm. For the DOCK6–Rac1 dataset, each movie contained 48 frames over a 3.80 s exposure time, using a dose rate of 23.4 e−/pixel/s, resulting in a total accumulated dose of 49.5 e− Å-2. The nominal defocus range was set between −0.8 and −2.0 μm. For the apo-DOCK6 dataset, each movie contained 48 frames over a 3.45 s exposure time, using a dose rate of 26.1 e−/pixel/s, resulting in a total accumulated dose of 50.1 e− Å−2. The nominal defocus range was set between −0.8 and −2.2 μm. Data were automatically collected using EPU software (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with the image-shift method.

Cryo-EM image processing

Movie frames were motion-corrected and dose-weighted using MotionCor244, and defocus parameters were estimated using CTFFIND445. Particles were automatically selected using crYOLO46, and subsequent 2D and 3D analyses were performed using Relion 3.147.

For the DOCK6–Cdc42 dataset, 1,936,198 particles were extracted from 3,377 movies in a box of 340 pixels, down-sampled to 2.66 Å/pixel, and subjected to multiple rounds of 2D classification, yielding 627,168 particles of sufficient quality. These particles were subjected to 3D classification with C2 symmetry. The two best classes, showing heterogeneity between protomers, contained 370,326 particles, which were then re-extracted at the original pixel size of 1.33 Å/pixel and subjected to 3D refinement with C2 symmetry, yielding a 4.5-Å map after Bayesian polishing. The particles were further expanded to 740,652 subparticles and signal-subtracted around the protomers; subsequent 3D classification was performed using a mask covering the protomer portion. The best class was selected and subjected to 3D refinement, yielding a map with a resolution of 3.9 Å according to the FSC = 0.143 criterion. The processing strategy is described in Supplementary Fig. 1.

For the DOCK6–Rac1 dataset, 2,197,7481 particles were extracted from 5,048 movies in a box of 340 pixels, down-sampled to 2.66 Å/pixel, and subjected to multiple rounds of 2D classification, yielding 892,724 particles of sufficient quality. These particles were subjected to 3D classification with C2 symmetry. The class representing the DOCK6–Rac1 complex dimer, containing 303,033 particles, was then re-extracted at the original pixel size of 1.33 Å/pixel and subjected to 3D refinement with C2 symmetry, yielding a 5.3-Å map after Bayesian polishing. The particles were further expanded to 606,066 subparticles and signal-subtracted around the protomer; subsequent 3D classification was performed using a mask covering the protomer portion. The two best classes were then selected and subjected to 3D refinement, yielding a map with a resolution of 4.2 Å. The initial global 3D classification also yielded other classes representing apo-DOCK6 tetramers and DOCK6 tetramer–Rac1 complexes, containing 163,250 particles and 112,745 particles, respectively, which were similarly subjected to 3D refinement, yielding 4.3-Å and 6.9-Å maps, respectively, after polishing and resampling to 1.33 Å/pixel. The processing strategy is described in Supplementary Fig. 3.

For the apo-DOCK6 dataset, 1,793,089 particles were extracted from 4,317 movies in a box of 340 pixels, down-sampled to 2.66 Å/pixel, and subjected to multiple rounds of 2D classification, yielding 552,716 particles of sufficient quality. These particles were subjected to a 3D classification with C2 symmetry. The two classes of DOCK6 octamers, containing 102,384 particles, were re-extracted at the original pixel size of 1.33 Å/pixel, recentered, and subjected to 3D refinement with C2 symmetry, yielding a 7.5-Å map after Bayesian polishing. The processing strategy is described in Supplementary Fig. 5.

Model building and refinement

The human DOCK6 model (AlphaFold database, Q96HP0) was fitted into the cryo-EM map using UCSF Chimera48 as a rigid body and then manually adjusted using COOT49. Model building of the DOCK6–Cdc42 and DOCK6–Rac1 complexes was facilitated using the crystal structures of the DOCK7DHR-2 –Cdc42 and DOCK10DHR-2 –Rac1 complexes (Protein Data Bank codes 6AJ4 and 8I5W), respectively, as references. The final model was refined using Phenix50.

In vitro GEF assays

In each assay, 10 μM Cdc42 (residues 1–188, C188S) was incubated with 2.4 μM fluorescent boron-dipyrromethene-fluor (BODIPY-FL)-GTP in reaction buffer containing 20 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.0), 150 mM NaCl, 5 mM MgCl2, and 0.2 mg/mL bovine serum albumin. The exchange reaction was initiated by adding 2 μM DOCK6 or control buffer. Nucleotide exchange was measured by monitoring the increase in fluorescence upon incorporation of BODIPY-FL-GTP into Cdc42 at excitation/emission wavelengths of 485/535 nm at 30 °C using a SPECTRAMAX iD3 multimode plate reader (Molecular Devices, San Jose, CA, USA). The observed rate constants (kobs) of each reaction were determined by non-linear least-squares fitting of the data with a single exponential rise model using the KaleidaGraph software (Synergy Software, Pleasanton, CA, USA). All experiments were performed at least in triplicate.

Surface plasmon resonance (SPR)

The N-terminal FLAG- and streptavidin-binding peptide-tagged DOCK6 DHR-2 domain (residues 1587–2047) was purified similarly to full-length DOCK6 described above, omitting TEV protease digestion. SPR experiments were performed on a Biacore T200 instrument (Cytiva). DOCK6 DHR-2 was captured on a Sensor Chip SA (Cytiva) via the N-terminal streptavidin-binding peptide tag. Five different concentrations of analyte (wild-type or G15A mutant Cdc42 and Rac1) were injected sequentially over the captured DOCK6 DHR-2 in single-cycle experiments. Responses were measured in buffer containing 10 mM HEPES (pH 7.5), 150 mM NaCl, 3 mM EDTA, and 0.005% Surfactant P20. Data were processed by global fitting with a 1:1 binding model using the manufacturer’s software.

Mass photometry

Non-crosslinked wild-type and mutant DOCK6 proteins were used for all mass photometry measurements. Each DOCK6 protein was diluted to the indicated concentrations (87, 100, or 300 nM) in buffer containing 20 mM HEPES (pH 8.0) and 200 mM NaCl. For phospholipid-containing conditions, wild-type DOCK6 (100 nM) was mixed with soluble PI(4,5)P2 diC8 (100 mM, Avanti Polar Lipids) immediately before measurement. All data were acquired using a TwoMP mass photometer (Refeyn Ltd, Oxford, UK). Measurements were performed in a six-well silicon gasket attached to a pre-cleaned glass coverslip (24 × 50 mm) using a MassGlassTM UC sample preparation kit (Refeyn Ltd). Prior to addition of the protein sample, buffer was added to the gasket and the focus position was adjusted for optimal contrast. For each measurement, the protein sample was loaded into the gasket containing buffer, and a 60-s movie was recorded with a field of view of 4.3 × 10.9 μm2. Data were analyzed using the manufacturer’s software (DiscoverMP, Refeyn Ltd).

Cross-linking assay

FreeStyle 293-F cells were transfected with the N-terminal FLAG- and streptavidin-binding peptide-tagged DOCK6 expression plasmid and harvested after 2 days by washing with phosphate-buffered saline (PSB). The cells were sonicated in PBS and centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 10 min to remove cell debris. Cleared cell lysate or purified protein were incubated at 23 °C for 30 min in the presence or absence of 2 mM BS3 (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Protein aliquots were detected by immunoblotting using an anti-FLAG antibody covalently conjugated to horseradish peroxidase (Sigma-Aldrich, A8592, 0.2 μg/mL).

Cell functional assay

Cell culture and transfection

COS-7 cells (JCRB, Osaka, Japan) were cultured on cell culture dishes (Nunc brand, Thermo Fisher Scientific) in high-glucose Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (DMEM, Nacalai Tesque, Kyoto, Japan) containing 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and penicillin–streptomycin solution (Nacalai Tesque) in 5% CO2 at 37 °C. The cells were transfected with the respective plasmids using the ScreenFect A transfection kit (Fujifilm Wako Chemicals, Osaka, Japan) in serum- and antibiotic-free DMEM, in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. Serum was added to the medium 4 h post-transfection, and the medium was replaced at 24 h post-transfection. Under these conditions, the percentage of attached cells incorporating trypan blue was estimated to be <5% in each experiment.

Immunofluorescence

Cells on coverslips were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde or 100% cold methanol (both from Nacalai Tesque) and blocked with Blocking One (Nacalai Tesque). The slides were incubated with anti-FLAG primary antibodies (Sigma-Aldrich, F1804) preloaded with fluorescent dye–conjugated secondary antibodies. Coverslips were mounted using VECTASHIELD mounting medium containing 4’, 6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (Vector Laboratories, Newark, CA, USA). Fluorescent images were collected using an FV4000 laser scanning microscope equipped with a Fluoview apparatus (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) and Fluoview software (ver. 2023, Olympus). The images presented in the figures are representative of multiple experiments. They were analyzed and merged using NIH Fiji software (ver. Java 8).

HS-AFM

HS-AFM instrumentation and settings

HS-AFM imaging was performed using a laboratory-built tapping mode high-speed atomic force microscope, as previously described51,52. Small cantilevers (BL-AC10-DS-A2; Olympus, Tokyo, Japan; USC-F1.2-k0.15 or USC-F1.5-k0.6; NanoWorld, Neuchâtel, Switzerland) with nominal spring constants of 0.1, 0.15, or 0.6 N/m and resonance frequencies of 1.2–1.5 MHz were employed. To enhance spatial resolution, the cantilever tips were coated with amorphous carbon via electron-beam deposition using a field-emission scanning electron microscope (ELS-7500; Elionix Inc., Tokyo, Japan). A 670 nm laser beam was focused onto the processed cantilever tip using a 20× long-working-distance objective lens (CFI S Plan Fluor ELWD; Nikon, Tokyo, Japan). Cantilever oscillations were detected using a position-sensitive two-segment PIN photodiode.

During imaging, the free oscillation amplitude of the cantilever (A₀) was adjusted to 1.5–2.5 nm, and the set-point amplitude was maintained at 80–90% of A₀ to minimize tip–sample interaction forces while maintaining stable imaging. The samples were scanned on mica substrates fixed on a glass stage and mounted on the HS-AFM scanner.

Sample preparation for DOCK6 oligomer imaging

To visualize native DOCK6 oligomerization, purified DOCK6 protein was diluted to 2–10 ng/μL in imaging buffer containing 20 mM HEPES-NaOH (pH 8.0), 200 mM NaCl, and 1 mM TCEP. Freshly cleaved muscovite mica was pretreated with either 0.01% (v/v) (3-aminopropyl)triethoxysilane or 10 mM NiCl₂ to promote protein adsorption. The treated mica was rinsed thoroughly with imaging buffer or deionized water to remove residual reagents, followed by immediate application of the protein solution. After 5 min of incubation at room temperature, unbound proteins were removed by rinsing with imaging buffer, and the samples were subjected to HS-AFM scanning under liquid conditions. All imaging experiments were repeated independently at least three times to ensure reproducibility.

HS-AFM image processing and analysis

HS-AFM image sequences were processed using ImageJ software (https://imagej.nih.gov/ij/). Background flattening was performed by applying a first-order polynomial correction in both x- and y-directions. Image quality was further enhanced by Gaussian filtering using the “Gaussian Blur” plugin. Cross-sectional height profiles and spatial measurements were obtained directly using ImageJ software. Time-lapse sequences were saved in AVI format, and the final image panels and videos were assembled and annotated using Adobe Creative Cloud tools (https://www.adobe.com/creativecloud.html).

Statistics and reproducibility

Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation from at least three independent experiments. Two samples were statistically compared using an unpaired two-sided Student’s t-test or one-way analysis of variance with Fisher’s protected least significant difference test; statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.
