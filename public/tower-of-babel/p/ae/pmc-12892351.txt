Deep brain stimulation of the anterior nucleus of the thalamus in animal models of mesial temporal lobe epilepsy: A literature review
Authors: Emma Acerbo, Thomas Eggers, Alejandra M Fernandez, Robert E Gross, Claire-Anne Gutekunst
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractEpilepsy affects approximately 1 % of the global population, presenting as a complex and heterogeneous disorder characterized by spontaneous, recurrent seizures. Focal onset seizures comprise half of all epilepsy cases. Mesial temporal lobe epilepsy (MTLE), the most prevalent form of focal seizures in adults, originates in the temporal lobe and is challenging to manage with standard antiseizure medications. Deep brain stimulation (DBS) targeting the anterior nucleus of the thalamus (ANT) has emerged as a therapeutic option for drug-resistant cases. Although clinical effects are well documented, pre-clinical research remains limited. To date, only 28 studies have been published, mostly in rodents and few in primates and sheep. This narrative review examines the anatomical organization of the ANT across human, rodent, and non-human primate models and synthesizes findings from the preclinical studies investigating ANT-DBS in experimental MTLE animal models. Collectively, these studies demonstrate an approximate 50 % reduction in seizure frequency, mirroring recent clinical outcomes. However, the majority of studies have focused on high-frequency stimulation (>100 Hz), with few exploring multiple stimulation frequencies. Additionally, outcomes from intermittent ON/OFF stimulation were inconclusive or showed worsening effects in two out of three studies. Thus, several crucial parameters, including stimulation frequency, pattern, and other variables remain inadequately explored in preclinical MTLE models yet hold potential to enhance therapeutic efficacy and patient outcomes.Keywords: Mesial temporal lobe epilepsy, Deep brain stimulation, Anterior nucleus of the thalamus

Introduction

One percent of Americans suffer from epilepsy, a complex and heterogenous disorder characterized by spontaneous, recurrent seizures (SRS) (Fiest et al., 2017). Recurrent seizures diminish quality of life, impair daily functioning, and increase dependence on healthcare systems. They can also cause severe complications, including sensorimotor deficits, language loss, cognitive decline, and even sudden death (Gonzalez-Martinez et al., 2022; Pamela et al., 2018; Simmatis et al., 2020). Half of these patients have focal onset seizures, and over half of patients with focal onset seizures have mesial temporal lobe epilepsy (MTLE), the most common and the most drug-resistant adult form. MTLE is characterized, typically, by focal impaired awareness seizures that originate in the limbic part of the temporal lobe (Engel, 2001) and may progress to bilateral tonic-clonic seizures, either of which can cause injuries and be life-threatening.

Although the exact cause of MTLE is unknown, it has been associated with a family history of epilepsy, prolonged febrile seizures, pediatric trauma, mesial temporal sclerosis, or infection. Brain injury is also one of the suspected causes of acquired MTLE and the disorder often develops from an initial insult to the brain (Ramantani and Holthausen, 2017). After such an initial insult, some patients will become epileptic within a week (Pitkänen and Immonen, 2014; Voges et al., 2015), while others will undergo a period of hippocampal and network remodeling before developing SRS (Engel, 1993; Mathern et al., 1996).

While the prevalence of MTLE specifically due to hippocampal sclerosis is decreasing, it remains at ~50 per 100,000 persons a year, and many of the remaining patients with MTLE have seizures originating from an essentially normal (by imaging and pathology) hippocampus (Prayson, 2018). Less than 25 % of these patients become seizure-free for even as long as one year with antiseizure drugs (Pohlen et al., 2017). Surgical resection or ablation can be curative in ~65 % of patients with drug resistant MTLE cases. However, the majority of patients - with either bilateral onsets or normal hippocampi - face the risk of moderate-to-severe memory decline following surgery (Munger Clary, 2024). Thus, alternative neuromodulation strategies have been developed for these individuals. These approaches include vagal nerve stimulation (Bartolomei et al., 2016; Carron et al., 2023; George et al., 2000), deep brain stimulation (DBS) (Boon et al., 2007; Li and Cook, 2018) and intracerebral, responsive neurostimulation (Morrell and RNS System in Epilepsy Study Group, 2011). DBS is increasingly employed for drug-resistant epilepsy, with over 70 % of patients showing a positive response at seven years of follow-up, and approximately 10 % achieving seizure freedom for more than two years. Potential targets for DBS in epilepsy include various thalamic subnuclei (Fisher et al., 2010; Salanova et al., 2015; Warren et al., 2020), hippocampus (Cukiert et al., 2017; Velasco et al., 2007a,b), and basal ganglia (Chabardès et al., 2002; Vesper et al., 2007; Wille et al., 2011). Validated through its efficacy in double-blinded multicenter studies and approved by the U.S. FDA, European CE Mark, DBS targeting the anterior nucleus of the thalamus (ANT) is currently the most widely accepted and substantiated intervention (Fisher, 2023; Peltola et al., 2023). The rationale for the ANT target comes from early work demonstrating a reduction in drug-induced seizures in a rodent model with lesions or high-frequency stimulation of the circuit of Papez (Mirski et al., 1997; Mirski and Ferrendelli, 1984; Mirski and Fisher, 1994). This narrative review will focus on summarizing recent studies using ANT-DBS in animal models of MTLE. We will first discuss the current animal models of MTLE, the anatomy of the anterior nucleus of the thalamus and the state of clinical ANT-DBS. We then outline the structure of the literature search in two databases before reviewing the relevant articles.

Animal models of MTLE

It has been argued that the study of epilepsy and testing of potential epilepsy treatments is best done in animal models that share brain circuitry and capture the relevant pathology of the human condition (Mazarati, 2007). Such models should show construct validity, recapitulating the cause of the human disorder; face validity, showing the same behavioral phenotype as patients; and finally predictive validity, responding to treatments that are effective in patients (Kandratavicius et al., 2014; Marshall et al., 2021). In 2012, researchers conducted a survey involving 82 European neurologists from 33 countries with expertise in the treatment of the epilepsies, seeking their opinions on animal models of epilepsy. They identified the most important clinical features for an ideal animal model of temporal lobe epilepsy to be the recurrence of focal seizures with mild behavioral expression, involvement of the relevant brain structures, characteristic histopathology, and EEG patterns of the recurrent seizures (Depaulis and Hamelin, 2015). Models of MTLE should therefore include some degree of hippocampal damage (not all patients exhibit sclerosis), and the appearance of SRS after a latency period (Curia et al., 2008).

There are only a few models that meet all these criteria: systemic intraperitorneal (i.p.) or intracerebral injection of pilocarpine or kainic acid (KA). These models have been characterized in rodents, sheep, and non-human primates (Chen et al., 2019; Perez-Mendes et al., 2011), and many reviews have been written about them (Croll et al., 2019; Depaulis and Hamelin, 2015; Lévesque and Avoli, 2013). Briefly, a chemoconvulsant such as KA or pilocarpine can be administered either intracerebrally or systemically. Systemic administration of pilocarpine requires prior administration of antimuscarinic drugs such as methyl atropine nitrate or methyl scopolamine bromide. Such drugs do not cross the blood-brain barrier but block the adverse effects of pilocarpine in the periphery. Whether administered systemically or intracerebrally, pilocarpine or KA results in status epilepticus (SE), characterized by a period of repeated seizures lasting between 30 min and several hours with little-to-no interruption. This acute period of SE mimics an initial brain insult that subsequently leads to a “latent” or “silent” period of brain remodeling including neuronal death, inflammation, synaptic reorganization, and aberrant neurogenesis (Cho et al., 2015; Lévesque and Avoli, 2013), also referred to as the epileptogenic period. This latent stage typically lasts days to weeks and is followed by the chronic period, characterized by SRS (Perez-Mendes et al., 2011). Frequency of SRS varies greatly in both pilocarpine and KA models. Similar to human epilepsy, seizure clustering is sometimes observed in rodent epilepsy models (Drexel et al., 2012; Goldenholz et al., 2018; Pitsch et al., 2017).

It is important to note that systemic models often result in extensive bilateral hippocampal damage, whereas intracerebral injections tend to result in unilateral damage. Furthermore, intrahippocampal injections may also result in varying degrees of damage when compared to intra-amygdala injections (Welzel et al., 2020), and variability in damage may depend on the species. There are also models that use intracerebral injection of penicillin, electrical kindling, or pentylenetetrazole (PTZ)-kindling, and that meet most MTLE criteria. PTZ is a GABA-A receptor antagonist, i.p. injection of which induces an acute, severe seizure at a high dose; however, sequential injections of a subconvulsive dose have been used for the development of chemical kindling. This method is simple and widely applicable to the investigation of the pathophysiology of epilepsy (Shimada and Yamagata, 2018).

Given some differences between rodents’ and primates’ hippocampal anatomy and physiology (e.g., neurogenesis and basal dendrites on granule cells), it can be argued that modeling MTLE in larger animals, including marmosets and macaques, may recapitulate the human disorder better than rodent models. Modeling MTLE in larger animals is more complex, requires specialized facilities, and entails significant ethical issues (Pirzada, 2022). Thus, alternative approaches have been developed for larger animals. For example, local (single or repeated) hippocampal penicillin injections have been used to model MTLE in sheep (Stypulkowski et al., 2011) and non-human primates (NHPs) (Connolly et al., 2024b, 2024a; Sherdil et al., 2020). However, these only induce recurrent seizures within a defined temporal window of up to 3 h and do not simulate the chronic aspect of the disorder. A single intracerebral injection of KA in the amygdala and temporal lobe in baboons produced a focal status epilepticus that lasted up to 5 hrs, with some animals retaining abnormal spiking for several days (Menini et al., 1980). These injections create lesions in the site of injection but also in the ipsilateral hippocampus described as consequent to SE. However, multiple injections in the amygdala (2–4, spaced by 2 weeks between each injection) lead to SRS, absence seizures, interictal epileptiform discharges (IEDs) and neuronal loss in the hippocampus (Chi et al., 2017). As with rodent studies, NHP studies have also reported that a single injection of KA directly in the hippocampus creates SRS, IEDs, and neuronal loss in the hippocampus (Chen et al., 2013; Y. Chen et al., 2019). Pilocarpine injections were also performed in NHP, with presence of SRS, neuronal loss, and a high mortality rate (Perez-Mendes et al., 2011; Pontes et al., 2016).

ANT anatomy and connections

The ANT is comprised of three nuclei: the anteromedial, anteroventral, and anterodorsal nuclei, all of which are consistently present across various species. Their anatomical arrangement within the thalamus exhibits similarity across humans, NHPs, and rodents (Fig. 1). The ANT is locally comprised of both glutamatergic and GABAergic neurons. In rodents and NHP models, focal injections of the GABA-agonist, muscimol, significantly ameliorated behavioral seizure scores, while use of GABA antagonists had the opposite effect (Kuramoto et al., 2009; Sherdil et al., 2020). Injecting bicuculline, a GABA antagonist, into the ANT exacerbated seizures and increased fast ripples, a biomarker of epilepsy. This was accompanied by vocalizations time-locked with interictal spikes and intense teeth-grinding, along with typical symptoms such as oral automatisms, loss of balance, contralateral dystonia, and occasional secondary generalization (Sherdil et al., 2019). These results suggest that certain ANT components, including neurons, may be part of the seizure circuit.

The ANT is part of the circuit of Papez, connecting the hippocampus to cortical regions. ANT-DBS could be acting through network effects, with the ANT acting as a bottleneck for distributed neuromodulation (Jankowski et al., 2013; Nelson, 2021). There are many studies and reviews on projections from and to the ANT in various species (Aggleton et al., 2016; Child and Benarroch, 2013; Jankowski et al., 2013; Mathiasen et al., 2020). A major output of the ANT is the cingulate/retrosplenial cortex (RSCx) (Brennan et al., 2021), which also receives direct input from the hippocampus and sends projections to the motor cortex (Yamawaki et al., 2016). In addition, the ANT projects both directly and indirectly to the hippocampal formation: a direct pathway via the cingulum bundle and an indirect route via the RSCx. These pathways are highly conserved between rodents and primates (Bubb et al., 2017), as shown in Fig. 2. ANT-DBS may also work antidromically to modulate inputs to the ANT. A major input to the ANT comes from the subiculum, which outputs information from the hippocampus to various regions, including ANT, RSCx, and the mammillary bodies (MB) (Kitanishi et al., 2021). A recent study in mice suggests that a subpopulation of dorsal subicular neurons projecting to ANT is critical for the generalization of seizures (Fei et al., 2022). Another major input to the ANT comes from the MB (Dillingham et al., 2015), which receives direct input from the hippocampal formation via the descending component of the post-commissural fornix and from the tegmental nuclei of Gudden via the mammillary peduncle (Umaba et al., 2021). The MB are known to be important for the normal function of the anterior thalamus (Dillingham et al., 2015), and electrolytic lesions of its connection to the ANT prevent the convulsant effect of PTZ in guinea pigs (Mirski and Ferrendelli, 1984).

ANT-DBS as a treatment for MTLE

Long approved for movement disorders such as Parkinson’s disease and essential tremor, DBS was approved for epilepsy in Europe in 2010 and in the U.S. in 2018. The first work on ANT-DBS for epilepsy was done by Irving Cooper and colleagues in 1987. Six subjects with intractable partial seizure disorders underwent intermittent electrical stimulation of the ANT, resulting in significant clinical control for four patients, including one who remained seizure-free for two years, albeit in an open-label, non-controlled study (Upton et al., 1987). Typically, bilateral electrodes are implanted and a subcutaneous neurostimulator sends high-frequency stimulation (HFS) continuously for 1 min ON, alternating with 5 min OFF (Peltola et al., 2023). ANT-DBS attracted considerable attention following the report of the SANTÉ (Stimulation of the Anterior Nucleus of Thalamus for Epilepsy) trial, a well-powered double-blind, randomized, sham-stimulation controlled study involving 110 patients with localization-related epilepsy and using 145 Hz and 5 volts as the stimulation parameters (Bouwens van der Vlis et al., 2019; Fisher et al., 2010; Salanova et al., 2015). Patients with temporal lobe epilepsy undergoing stimulation exhibited a notable 40 % reduction in seizures during the 3 month blinded evaluation period, surpassing the 15 % reduction observed in the sham-stimulation control group. While other targets have been considered for DBS, ANT-DBS remains the most supported option, with patients achieving a mean 75 % reduction in seizure frequency up to 7 years from the surgery in the open-label follow-up study. However, only 18 % of patients reported a seizure-free period of at least 6 months (Salanova et al., 2015).

Although a promising option for patients with drug resistant focal-impaired awareness seizures, ANT-DBS does not reliably provide seizure freedom and presents significant inter-subject variability in outcomes. And, despite its increasing use to treat refractory epilepsy, the mechanism(s) of action underlying its antiseizure effects remain unknown. Proposed ANT-DBS mechanisms of action include acute, direct effect of the electrical stimulation as well as long-term effects such as changes in neurochemical and network plasticity. HFS could have desynchronizing effects, potentially acting through both excitatory and inhibitory mechanisms (Yu et al., 2018). It could reduce firing activity of nearby neurons and has demonstrated suppressive and excitatory effects on stimulated network activity (Boraud et al., 1996; Tai et al., 2003). The electrical current delivered by DBS can also lead to long-term depression and alter synaptic efficacy, ultimately reducing the excitability of the epileptic network. The effects include seizure suppression, seizure termination by modulating depolarization and conduction, and potentially disease modification by inducing chronic changes in gene expression and network activity, suggesting plasticity of the underlying epileptic network (Foutz and Wong, 2022). Elucidating these mechanisms should enable improvement of this therapy, as well as potentially identify novel therapeutic targets.

Methods

To identify studies that investigated ANT stimulation in animal models of MTLE, we conducted a comprehensive literature search using two databases – PubMed and Web of Science. The search was performed using the following query in PubMed: anterior[Title/Abstract] AND (thalamic[Title/Abstract] OR thalamus[Title/Abstract]) AND stimulation[Title/Abstract] AND (epilepsy[Title/Abstract] OR epileptic[Title/Abstract] OR seizure*[Title/Abstract]) AND (mice OR rat OR rats OR sheep OR macaque OR monkey*). The initial query yielded 74 publications. The search in Web of Science was performed using the following query: (ant[All Fields] OR atn[All Fields]) AND (stimulation[All Fields] OR neuromodulation [All Fields]) AND (epilepsy[All Fields] OR seizure [All Fields]) AND (mice OR rat OR rats OR sheep OR macaque OR monkey OR canine), yielding 53 results. Studies were included if they focused on ANT stimulation in animal models of MTLE and were published through 2024. We excluded review articles and studies involving human subjects, non-MTLE animal models, or ex vivo experiments as well as studies that did not focus on the suppression of ANT-DBS on seizures. Other studies were eliminated because they did not directly address the effect of ANT-DBS on seizures. This resulted in 30 eligible publications that tested ANT-DBS in several animal models, including rats, mice, sheep, and NHP. Upon further review, we identified instances where multiple papers reported findings from the same original dataset. For example, three studies described results from the same group of kainic acid-injected monkeys treated with ANT-DBS (Du et al., 2020, 2021a, 2021b). In such cases, only the original study detailing the effects of ANT-DBS was included (Du et al., 2020). After removing these duplicates, 28 unique publications remained for analysis (see Table 1). A qualitative synthesis was undertaken to summarize the main findings from the included studies. We focused on the effects of ANT stimulation on seizure outcomes, such as seizure frequency and neuronal damage, and we performed a detailed comparison of the stimulation parameters and their effects across different animal models.

Results

Studies included in this analysis are listed in Table 1 with their stimulation parameters and primary outcomes listed in Tables 2 and 3, respectively. These studies used a variety of epilepsy models, which fall under two broad classes: acute (penicillin) and chronic (KA, pilocarpine injection, electrical and chemical kindling). Acute models either produce seizures or seizure-like events immediately after presentation of a certain drug that persist for a period of hours. Chronic models require an intervention resulting in SRS that persists for weeks to months. The primary outcome measures in these studies revolve around seizures. Acute studies can measure the time to develop a seizure after the presentation of a proconvulsant and, once seizures begin, their severity and duration. Chronic studies analyze seizure frequency and are often reported as rates of partial and generalized seizures during DBS or after DBS has been performed. An interesting commonality is that almost all studies (acute and chronic) reduce seizures by ~50 %.

ANT-DBS studies usually attempt to replicate the stimulation parameters used in clinical settings. Typical clinical stimulation devices deliver pulse stimulation with amplitudes in the range of 1–5 V, frequencies > 100 Hz, and pulse widths around 100μs. For researchers, determining the equivalent, or even appropriate, amplitude is a more difficult problem, as neither the electrodes nor the intended target are the same scale as clinical equivalents. To address this issue, researchers have commonly either tried several different amplitudes or developed a heuristic approach to determine appropriate amplitude, for instance, pilot testing to determine the amplitude that caused behavioral differences and choosing some fraction of that amplitude. Chronic stimulation is typically delivered over a period of days to weeks for many hours per day either during or after epileptogenesis. Acute stimulation typically begins before or just after the epilepsy challenge is given to the animal. An exception to the above parameters is low frequency stimulation (LFS) studies. These experiments stimulate at much lower frequencies, typically around 1 Hz. LFS has been shown to work in several animal studies and a human clinical trial but has not been studied as extensively as HFS.

Studies in acute models have demonstrated that ANT-DBS can lessen seizure burden and provide immediate seizure suppression during both SE in chronic models and kindled seizure challenges. For example, Chen et al. found in two studies that 12hrs of continuous stimulation started right after the onset of SE in IHKA rats and 24hrs of stimulation started right after the onset of SE in i.p. pilocarpine in rats reduced both partial and generalized seizures by ~50 % for the duration of the stimulation (Chen et al., 2017c, 2017b). Takebayashi et al. found similar results in the rat intra-amygdala KA model adding that bilateral ANT-DBS stimulation provided superior protection compared to unilateral stimulation. Hamani et al. demonstrated that bilateral stimulation was more effective than unilateral stimulation in preventing seizures and SE in i.p. pilocarpine rats (Hamani et al., 2004). On the other hand, Ferreira et al. saw minimal effect of DBS on latency to first seizure during SE in i.p. pilocarpine rats (Ferreira et al., 2018). ANT-DBS was also effective in both electrically and chemically induced kindling models. Zhang et al. delivered ANT-DBS after seizures were induced with daily electrical amygdala stimulation, reducing seizure severity, after-discharge duration and seizure incidence (Takebayashi et al., 2007; Zhang et al., 2012b). Two other studies by Zhong et al. (Zhong et al., 2011) and Zhang et al. (Zhang et al., 2012a) showed that 15 min of stimulation, either HFS or LFS, reduced the incidence of generalized seizures in amygdala-kindled rats. ANT-DBS applied during PTZ-induced kindling sessions reduced the incidence of full kindling, prolonged latency to the first seizure, decreased seizure duration, and increased survival rate (Gimenes et al., 2022; Tseng et al., 2020). Gimenes et al. further noted that ANT-DBS changed the dominant seizure frequency from the beta range to the theta range, and provided lasting effects reducing seizures in a post-kindling chemical challenge.

A few acute studies have examined the effects of stimulation amplitude and frequency in their experimental designs, but the varying results make it difficult to draw a conclusion. In a pilocarpine rat model, Hamani et al. varied both frequency and amplitude of stimulation (Hamani et al., 2004). They report that varying the frequency had minimal effect, low amplitude had no effect, a medium amplitude increased the latency to the first seizure, while high amplitude had the opposite effect (Hamani et al., 2004). In the hippocampal penicillin-induced sheep model, Stypulkowski et al. studied the frequency-dependent, evoked responses to ANT-DBS using clinical DBS leads (Stypulkowski et al., 2011). They found that HFS (>80 Hz) caused suppression in the local field potential of the hippocampus and reduced ictal spikes, while LFS (<80 Hz) did not cause similar hippocampal suppression. However, opposite effects were reported by Sherdill et al. in the hippocampal NHP penicillin-induced model with LFS (40 Hz) reducing seizures by 50 % while HFS at the same amplitude (130 Hz) did not (Sherdil et al., 2020).

Studies in chronic models have largely mirrored those in acute models, showing that ANT-DBS applied during the chronic phase can reduce seizure frequency. In all but one study in rats, ANT-DBS decreased hippocampal excitability (Miranda et al., 2014), delayed the onset of recurrent spontaneous seizures (Jou et al., 2013; Zhu et al., 2018), decreased their duration (Bae et al., 2022) and suppressed their frequency (Liu et al., 2020; Meng et al., 2016; Tang et al., 2021). One study found that ANT-DBS increased seizures; however, each of the 8 rats received different stimulation, and the placement of the stimulation electrodes varied across the animals, which prevents drawing a definitive conclusion (Lado, 2006). In general bilateral intervention had a stronger effect than unilateral, and one study suggested that left ANT stimulation demonstrated greater effectiveness compared to right stimulation (Jou et al., 2013; Zhang et al., 2012a). In one study, LFS (1 Hz, 300 μA) administered 30 min/day was more efficient than HFS (100 Hz, 30 μA) at reducing seizure frequency (Wang et al., 2016). LFS also reduced high frequency oscillation and interictal spike rates. As shown in the acute models, higher amplitudes tend to exacerbate seizure outcome (Covolan et al., 2014). ANT-DBS in NHP models reduced seizures by 40–50 % using DBS leads and stimulation parameters similar to clinical devices (Chen et al., 2016; Du et al., 2020; Yang et al., 2015).

It is worth mentioning that these studies use varied methodologies to quantify the number of seizures, including, for example, electrographic versus behavioral assessments and averaging over different time frames (e.g., daily, weekly, or over several weeks). Additionally, there are inconsistencies in the timing of stimulation across studies. While broad conclusions can be drawn from all studies involving epileptic animal models (not limited to MTLE), such generalizations do not precisely capture the specific impact on MTLE epilepsy.

Discussion

In summary, studies investigating ANT-DBS in models of MTLE demonstrate an average reduction in seizure frequency of 45 %, which is derived by calculating the mean reduction from the data provided in all the publications. This suggests a potential universal mechanism(s) that might be relevant to clinical applications. These findings are similar to the ones described in epileptic patients, in which there is a median seizure frequency reduction of 56 % after 5 years (Kaufmann et al., 2024).

Current literature, however, highlights a substantial gap in determining the optimal stimulation parameters. Among the 28 studies reviewed, only one tried to define an effective frequency range for ANT stimulation, but it did not succeed in establishing a specific range (Stypulkowski et al., 2011). Just four studies compared the effects of low (below 20 Hz) versus high (above 100 Hz) frequency stimulation, with one reporting that 1 Hz was more effective in suppressing seizures and epileptic biomarkers (Hamani et al., 2004; Sherdil et al., 2019; Stypulkowski et al., 2011; Wang et al., 2016). Most studies (23 out of 28) focused on HFS, particularly 130 Hz, the most commonly used frequency in patients, which may be similar or different in different species. Although LFS has also been successfully used in human patients, it is much less explored. A recent study suggests that the frequency of ANT-DBS stimulation may affect a patient’s cognitive function, independent of its impact on seizure rates. In that study, patients who received low-frequency ANT-DBS had higher verbal memory scores than those who received other stimulation frequency (Marks et al., 2024). The stimulation current amplitude also remains minimally explored; only three study investigated the impact of different amplitudes, finding that lower amplitudes enhanced seizure control, whereas higher amplitudes exacerbated seizure activity (Covolan et al., 2014; Hamani et al., 2008). The limited success in some studies, as reported by Lado et al. and Wang et al., underscores the critical need for precise parameter tuning, highlighting that appropriate frequency and amplitude are crucial for effective therapy. This aligns also with clinical observations that not all patients respond to ANT-DBS, suggesting that optimal parameter settings may vary among individuals.

Furthermore, only three studies explored the effects of intermittent (ON/OFF) versus continuous stimulation. This is significant considering that intermittent stimulation could prolong battery life and reduce the frequency of surgical interventions for battery replacement. Yet no study has directly compared the outcomes of these stimulation strategies in an MTLE animal model. Additionally, the impact of pulse width on therapeutic outcomes has not been investigated.

Finally, the duration of stimulation in studies varies widely, from a few seconds in acute experiments to several months in studies with NHPs and rats. However, these durations do not mirror the multi-year ANT-DBS treatments in patients, where benefits appear to accumulate over time in open-label studies. This consideration is particularly important in rodent studies, given the significantly shorter lifespan of rodents compared to humans. Some studies have sought to correlate rodent and human lifespans (Dutta and Sengupta, 2016; Jackson et al., 2017), emphasizing the importance of selecting the correct life stage for experiments. Yet many studies simply label rodents as “adults” without specifying age, overlooking the variability this introduces in outcomes.

Proposed mechanisms for ANT-DBS include the creation of a reversible lesion in the ANT, which helps suppress the network activity and prevent hyper-synchronization that leads to generalized seizures (Takebayashi et al., 2007). Despite this, about 50 % of seizures remain uncontrolled, indicating additional unknown factors that prevent complete suppression. Research has also hinted at a neuroprotective effect of ANT-DBS, demonstrated by reduced neuronal loss and inflammation in the hippocampus in animal models of MTLE (Bae et al., 2022; Chen et al., 2017c, 2017a; Du et al., 2020; Ferreira et al., 2018; Xie et al., 2023; Xu et al., 2023; Yang et al., 2015). This effect has been observed in both mice and monkeys, but a direct link between neuroprotection and seizure freedom following ANT-DBS remains unestablished. Overall, while ANT-DBS shows promise as a treatment for MTLE, significant uncertainties remain regarding the optimal stimulation parameters—such as frequency, amplitude, and whether stimulation should be continuous or cyclic—and the underlying molecular and cellular mechanisms. Despite the size difference, rodents exhibit brain connectivity that is quite similar to humans, particularly in epilepsy-related circuits. Studies of ANT-DBS in rodent models of temporal lobe epilepsy have shown effects like those observed in patients. This suggests that rodents can serve as valuable preclinical models for studying ANT-DBS mechanisms. Additionally, the availability of transgenic mouse models and advanced tools in rodent research, which are more challenging to apply in larger animals, further enhances their utility for such studies. With the advancement of cell-specific targeted control through techniques like optogenetics and chemogenetics, rodent studies are poised to provide deeper insights into the mechanisms underlying ANT-DBS. For example, one study used chemogenetics to neuromodulate ANT parvalbumin expressing neurons and reported their participation in PTZ-induced spike and wave discharges (Zhang et al., 2023). A direct injection of the chemogenetic-expressing viruses allowed targeting of the ANT neurons, which would not have been possible with traditional electrical neuromodulation. The continuous identification of ANT, subnuclei-specific markers (Kapustina et al., 2024; Roy et al., 2021) affords the development of Cre recombinase-expressing transgenic mouse lines that can be used to target expression to anterior thalamic circuits (Roy et al., 2022). One such line, C1ql2-Cre knock-in, has already been developed and can be used for AD specific expression (Roy et al., 2021). Viral vectors with retrograde and anterograde capability can also be used to target ANT using knowledge of the circuit. Retrograde virus can be injected into cingulate/retrospenial cortex, or anterograde virus can be injected in mammillary bodies to target the ANT-cingulate/retrosplenial or mammillothalamic tract respectively (Nelson et al., 2020). To deconstruct the complex effects of ANT-DBS, these approaches can be combined to manipulate specific neuronal populations within the ANT or networks during ANT stimulation, potentially revealing why DBS fails to achieve seizure freedom in most patients and identifying more precise therapeutic targets.
