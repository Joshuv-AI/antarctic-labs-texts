Roles of TGF-β in the therapeutic potential of hypoxic mesenchymal stem cells for treating osteoarthritis in a Rabbit model
Authors: En-Rung Chiang, Kun-Hui Chen, Hsuan-Hsiao Ma, Jung-Pan Wang, Hsiao-Li Ma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOsteoarthritis (OA) is the most prevalent musculoskeletal disease worldwide. Cell-based therapies have emerged as a promising approach for OA therapy. The therapeutic potential of mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) has been demonstrated in a wide array of inflammatory diseases. A previous study demonstrated that the injection of the allogeneic MSCs cultured under hypoxia could reduce the progression of osteoarthritis in an anterior cruciate ligament transection (ACLT) model of OA in rabbits. The possible factors involved in the therapeutic effect of hypoxic MSCs in OA were further analyzed in the current study.MethodsUsing the ACLT model of OA, we investigated gene expression changes in the groups of control, OA, and OA treated by hyaluronic acid (OA + HA), and additionally injected with hypoxic MSCs (OA + HA + MSC) by PCR array.ResultsIncreased expression of TGF-β in the group treated with hypoxic MSCs was observed. Significant higher transcript and protein expressions of TGF-β were also demonstrated in cartilage treated with hypoxic MSCs. We further generated TGF-β-knockout (KO) MSCs and observed notably reduced levels of TGF-β in cartilage treated with hypoxic TGF-β KO MSCs. Immunohistochemical analysis revealed that the group treated with TGF-β KO MSCs exhibited significantly decreased type II collagen (COL II) and increased type X collagen (COL X) as compared to that treated with MSCs without TGF-β-KO.ConclusionsCollectively, we suggest hypoxic MSCs might exert therapeutic effects mediated by stimulating TGF-β and subsequently promote and inhibit the expressions of COL II and COL X respectively.

Introduction

Osteoarthritis is a highly prevalent musculoskeletal disorder, affecting an estimated 7.6% of the global population [1, 2]. The pathophysiology of OA is characterized by progressive degeneration of articular cartilage, subchondral bone sclerosis, and synovial inflammation [3–5]. The progressive deterioration of the cartilaginous cushions at the articulating ends of bones leads to the exacerbation of pain and disability associated with OA.

Owing to the high global burden of OA, the development of effective therapeutic strategies is a crucial area of research. Conventional approaches to OA management include pharmacological interventions for pain relief and anti-inflammatory effects, exercise regimens aimed at weight reduction and muscular strengthening for improved joint support, and surgical procedures for total joint replacement in advanced cases [6–8].However, most of these modalities only temporarily relieve the pain and symptoms. Cell-based therapyhas emerged as a promising therapeutic modality foreffectivelytreatingOA, particularly involving mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) [9, 10].

The therapeutic potential of MSCs in a wide array of inflammatory conditions havebeen extensively investigated due to their immune evasive properties [10, 11]. Additionally, MSCs are known to have the potential for self-renewal and differentiation [12, 13]. Human bone marrow-derived MSCs have been demonstrated with the capability to differentiate into osteoblasts, chondrocytes, and adipocytes [12]. A in vivo anterior cruciate ligament transection (ACLT) model of osteoarthritis in rabbits was established previously [9]and demonstrated that injection of the allogeneic MSCs cultured under hypoxia (1% O2) could reduce the progression of osteoarthritis. We have further investigated the therapeutic potential of hypoxic MSCs in intervertebral discs (IVDs), and the results revealed that injection of hypoxic MSCs was more effective than injection of MSCs that cultured under normoxic conditions for reducing the degeneration progression of IVDs [14]. These studies provide evidence regarding the hypoxic MSCs-based therapy for tissue repairing.

MSCs have been reported to express a range of cellular factors, including a variety of cytokines and chemokines to benefit for repairing degenerative tissues and counteracting inflammation [10, 15]. Pathological signals, such as TNF-α, IL-1β, and hypoxia, stimulate MSCs to secrete an abundance of cellular factors [16].

Transforming growth factor β (TGF-β) is a multifunctional cytokine regulates immune responses and homeostasis in human cells and plays a key role during hypoxic conditions in cancer [17]. Hypoxia is able to induce the secretion of TGF-βin MSCs to promote breast cancer cell progression [18].The functional role of TGF-β in hypoxic MSCs-based therapy for the treatment of OA is not clear.

The objective of the current study was to further clarify the possible molecular factors involved in the therapeutic potential of hypoxic MSCs in the ACLT model of osteoarthritis in rabbits.

Materials and methods

Isolation of MSCs and cell culture

The materials and methods for isolating rabbit bone marrow MSCs were similar as described in previous studies [9, 14]. The New Zealand white rabbits are used for the experiment. Briefly, bone marrow was aspirated aseptically from the femurs of mature rabbits using an 18-gauge needle. Mononuclear cells were isolated from the bone marrow tissues by density gradient centrifugation and suspended in complete culture medium consisting of α-minimal essential medium (α‐MEM; Gibco‐BRL, Gaithersburg, MD) supplemented with fetal bovine serum (FBS, 16.6%), 2 mM L‐glutamine, and penicillin-streptomycin (100 U/ml). Non-adherent cells were removed after 24 h. The cells were harvested for sub-culturing, and starting from passage 1 after 9 days. For hypoxic treatments, cells were cultured in a hypoxic condition in low oxygen (1% O2, 5% CO2, 94% N2).

ACLT osteoarthritis model

The detailed procedures to develop the ACLT osteoarthritis model were described in a previous study [9]. The experimentally induced OA in the rabbit knee was completely described elsewhere [19]. A total of 10 rabbits with 20 legs were initially sub-grouped into 4 groups, including sham (control, N = 4), OA (N = 4), OA with HA treatment (OA + HA, N = 5), and OA with HA and MSCs treatment (OA + HA + MSC, N = 5) (Fig. 1). For the procedure of ACLT, a medial arthrotomy was performed. The patella was dislocated laterally, and the anterior cruciate ligament was transected. At 8 weeks after ACLT, the OA + HA group was injected with 0.4 mL of hyaluronic acid (HA, SciVision Biotech, Taipei, Taiwan), as previously described in similar rabbit OA models [9]. The OA + HA + MSCs group was injected with 106of MSCs from passage 1 in 0.4 mL of HA, with the cell number selected based on prior studies [20]. The right legs of 3 rabbits were further sub-grouped as OA + HA + MSCs_KO that injected with 106TGF-β knockout (KO) MSCs from passage 1 in 0.4 mL of HA (Fig. 1), based on prior studies. The rabbits were sacrificed with pentobarbital (3%) followed by CO2 inhalation at six weeks at 6 weeks post treatment.

To monitor the presence of injected MSC, superparamagnetic iron oxide (SPIO) particles were prepared as described previously [9, 21]. Briefly, Fe₃O₄ nanoparticles were synthesized by mixing 4 mL of 0.1 M FeCl₃ (Fe³⁺) and 1 mL of 0.2 M FeCl₂ (Fe²⁺) solutions, with the pH adjusted to 11. The resulting precipitate was collected using a magnet and washed with deionized water. Then, 3 g of organic acid was added to coat the nanoparticle surfaces. Excess coating material was removed by centrifugation, and the particles were redispersed in deionized water. For labeling, 50 µg/mL of SPIO was mixed with 0.75 µg/mL poly-L-lysine in culture medium and incubated at room temperature for 1 h. MSCs were then cultured in the SPIO-containing medium for 24 h and washed thoroughly with PBS.

Sample Preparation

Rabbits were sacrificed at 6 weeks after surgery. Samples of meniscus, cartilage, and synovium were obtained from knees of the rabbits. Part of the samples were immediately fixed in 10% formalin for a week, embedded in paraffin, and cut into 4-µm sections for the immunohistochemistry (IHC) analysis. The rest of the parts of the samples were immediately immersed in RNA later™ (Invitrogen) for RNA or protein extraction.

PCR array

Total RNA from the tissues or MSCs was isolated by TRIZOL (Invitrogen)and reverse transcription using Super-Script™ III Reverse Transcriptase (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA) following the manufacturer’s protocol. The gene expression profiles from the collected tissues of each group were performed and analyze during the RT² Profiler™ PCR Array (330001PPN0045A, QIAGEN, Germany) following manufacturers’ instructions. Differential expression of the genes in collected tissues was compared with the sham control group. A heat map was also generated to show the candidate genes which was directly associated with ACLT-induced OA treatment.

Quantitative reverse transcription PCR (RT-qPCR)

The mRNA expression of ACAN (aggrecan), TNF (TNF-α), and TGFB1 (TGF-β) was analyzed by real-time quantitative PCR (qPCR). Total RNA from the tissues or MSCs was isolated by TRIZOL and reverse transcription using Super-Script™ III Reverse Transcriptase as described above. PCR was performed using cDNA as the template in a 20-µl reaction mixture containing specific primer pairs (Table 1). The primer for analyzing TGFB1 (XM_002722312.1) was usedwith the primer from the RT2 Profiler PCR Arrays (330001PPN0045A). The qPCR was performed usingiScript™ cDNA Synthesis Kit(Bio-Rad). Each cycle comprised the following steps: denaturation for 45 s at 94 °C, annealing for 1 min at 51–58 °C, and 90 s of elongation at 72 °C. Glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) as a housekeeping gene was used to normalize input template cDNA to analyze relative gene expression.

Primer sets for RT-qPCR analysis

Real-time amplification of the genes was performed using the THUNDERBIRD ™SYBR®Green(TOYOBO CO.,) on the ABI StepOnePlus PCR instrument according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Applied Biosystems)with at least three technical repeats.

Immunohistochemistry analysis (IHC)

IHC was performed according to similar procedures as described previously [9, 14]. Briefly, the femur articular sections were rehydrated while the endogenous peroxidase was blocked with 3% hydrogen peroxide (Sigma). COLII was retrieved with a mixture containing hyaluronidase (2.5%, Sigma) and of pronase (1 mg/mL) in PBS at 37 °C for 1 h. Tissue sections were then blocked with Ultra V block (Thermo Scientific) for 10 min andincubated with mouse monoclonal antibody against COLII (1:200; CP18; Calbiochem) or rat polyclonal antibody against type X collage (1:200; ab58632; Abcam). The sections were then incubated with biotin-labeled goat anti-mouse immunoglobulin (Dako, Carpinteria) and goat anti-rabbit immunoglobulin for COLIIand COLX, respectively, followed by a reaction with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated streptavidin. All the sections were counterstained with Mayer-Hematoxylin (Sigma-Aldrich). The expression levels of COL II and COLX based on the stained areas to the total area in the section were measured using Image-Pro Plus software, version 5.0.

Safranin O–Fast green staining

Safranin O–Fast Green staining was conducted to evaluate cartilage matrix integrity and proteoglycan distribution. Briefly, paraffin-embedded femoral condyle sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and stained with 0.1% Safranin O solution for 5 min, followed by counterstaining with Fast Green for 3 min. After dehydration and mounting, the stained sections were examined under a light microscope. The intensity and area of red staining, which indicates the presence of proteoglycans, were used to assess the structural integrity of the cartilage matrix. Semi-quantitative analysis of Safranin O-positive areas was performed using Image-Pro Plus software, version 5.0.

Modified Mankin score

The level of articular cartilage degeneration in the knees was evaluated using the modified Mankin score, that includes the following four variables: (i) surface (0 = normal, 1 = irregular, 2 = fibrillation or vacuoles, and 3 = blisters or erosion), (ii) hypocellularity (0 = normal, 1 = small decrease in chondrocytes, 2 = large decrease in chondrocytes, and 3 = no cells), (iii) clones (0 = normal, 1 = occasional duos, 3 = duos or trios, and 3 = multiple nested cells), and (iv) alcianophilia (0 = normal, 1 = small decrease in color, 2 = large decrease in color, and 3 = no color). The higher the score, the greater the level of OA. The entire histological evaluation was performed by seven investigators who were blinded to the group allocation.

TGF-β knockout MSCs

TGF-β in the MSCs was knocked out using the CRISPR/Cas9 lentivirus. Briefly, sgRNA of TGF-β with sequence GCCTA GTGTT GACGC CCGGC, designed and purchased from the RNA technology platform and gene manipulation core of Academia Sinica (Taipei, Taiwan), was inserted into the lentiCRISPRv2 vector at the BsmBIsite. The constructed plasmids were amplified by transforming them into DH5α competent cells. Purified plasmids were transfected into HEK293T cells alone with pMD2G (VSV-G envelope-expressing plasmid) and psPAX2 (HIV-1 gag pol-expressing plasmid) for lentivirus production. Prepared lentiviruses were used to transfect cultured MSCs. Transfected MSC cells were selected with puromycin. After selection, cells were maintained in puromycin-containing complete medium. Cell extract was prepared for quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) and western blotting to confirm the efficiency of TGF-β knockout.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed to compare mRNA expression levels obtained from RT-qPCR and protein expression levels derived from immunohistochemistry (IHC) images across multiple experimental groups. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to assess overall differences among the groups. When significant differences were detected, Tukey’s post hoc multiple comparisons test was applied to identify specific group-to-group differences. All quantitative data are presented as mean values ± standard deviation (SD) based on at least three independent experiments. A two-tailed p-value of ≤ 0.05 was considered indicative of statistical significance. All statistical computations and graphical representations were performed using GraphPad Prism version 6 (GraphPad Software Inc., La Jolla, CA, USA).

Results

Establishment of the ACLT OA model

We first establish the ACLT OA model in rabbits. Seventeen knees from 10rabbits were sub-grouped into 5 different groups, including sham group (control), ACL transected [2], ACLT and treated by hyaluronic acid (OA + HA), ACLT and treated by hyaluronic acid and hypoxic cultured MSC(OA + HA + MSC), and ACLT and treated by hyaluronic acid and TGF-β knock-out MSC[OA + HA + MSC(KO)] (Fig. 1A).

MSCs with SPIO particles were prepared to monitor the presence of injected MSC in the healing of cartilage. The result showed that the signal of SPIO can be observed in the cartilage, indicating MSCs did infiltrate the cartilage and were involved in the healing of OA(Fig. 1B). Labeling MSCs permits in vivo cell localization and tracking by real-time MRI.

Significantly increasedTGF-β expression in OA knee joints injected with hypoxic MSCs

To further explore the possible molecular factors participating in the therapeutic function of hypoxic MSCs in OA, we analyzed the candidate gene expressions in control, OA, OA + HA, and OA + HA + MSCs groups using PCR array. Results revealed that several factors, including integrin beta 8 (ITGB8), platelet-derived growth factor subunit A (PDGFA), interleukin-4 receptor (IL4R), collagen type I alpha 2 chain (COL1A2), tissue inhibitor of metalloproteinases 2 (TIMP2), transforming growth factor beta-1 (TGFB1), TGF-β receptor 1 (TGFBR1), and tumor necrosis factor (TNF), were up-regulated in MSC-treated OA knee joints (Fig. 2A). The heatmap revealed TGFB and TNF specifically up-regulated in MSC-treated knee joints (Fig. 2B).

Generation of TGF-β knockout MSCs

To investigate the possible function of TGF-β in the treatment of hypoxic MSCs, we further generated TGF-β knockout_KO MSCs using the CRISPR/CAS9 system. TGF-β knockout MSCs were successfully selected. Both the protein and mRNA expression of TGF-β obviously decreased in TGF-β knockout MSCs, validating the knockout efficiency (Fig. 5A-B). Furthermore, TGF-β exhibited lower expression levels in the cartilage of the rabbit injected with hypoxic TGF-β KO MSCs (Fig. 5C). These results demonstrated the efficiency and effect of hypoxic MSCs with TGF-βknockout.

Effects of TGF-β knockout of hypoxic MSCs on the expressions of COLII and COL X collages

COL II is the key component of the mature matrix and plays an essential role in maintaining the structure of articular cartilage [22–24]. Since TGF-β has been frequently reported to be able to stimulate the expression of COL [23, 25, 26], we finally investigate whether TGF-β knockout influences the expression of type 2collagen.

Safranin-O staining revealed a marked loss of proteoglycan content in the OA and OA + HA groups, as evidenced by reduced red staining intensity. Notably, treatment with hypoxic MSCs restored Safranin-O staining, indicating improved cartilage matrix composition. However, such restorative effect was diminished in the TGF-βKO MSC-treated group (Figs. 6A and 7A). The expression of COL II and COL X collagens in cartilages collected from each group was analyzed by IHC. COL II exhibited a significantly lower level in both the cartilage in OA and OA treated with HA as compared to the control group. Injection of hypoxic MSCs into the OA rabbits notably increased the level of COL II compared to those without MSCs treatment, whereas hypoxic MSCs with TGF-β knockout did not exhibit the enhancement of COL II (Figs. 6A and 7B). On the contrary, an obviously increase of COL X was observed in the OA and OA plus HA groups. A Significant reduction of COL X was found in hypoxic MSCs treated cartilage, while the regulatory effect was not observed in the group treated with TGF-βKO MSCs (Figs. 6A and 7C). The results demonstrated TGF-β had the ability to stimulate COL II and down-regulated COL X in hypoxic MSCs. Furthermore, histological grading using the Mankin scoring system showed significantly higher scores in the OA and OA + HA groups compared to controls, reflecting severe cartilage degeneration. Hypoxic MSCs treatment significantly reduced the Mankin score, suggesting cartilage repair, while the TGF-βKO MSC group showed less improvement, indicating that TGF-β signaling contributes to MSC-mediated cartilage protection (Fig. 7D).

Discussion

MSCs have been successfully employed in preclinical models for the therapy for OA [10]. Increasing studies provide evidence to propose possible mechanisms for the repair of the degenerated articular cartilage. In the current study, we systematically demonstrated the possible cellular factors and pathways involved in the hypoxic MSCs-mediated treatment of OA using the ACLT model in rabbits. We identified TGF-β as the candidate factor involved by PCR array (Fig. 2). The transcript of ACAN was also significantly increased in the meniscus, cartilage, and synovium treated with hypoxic MSCs(Fig. 3).Relative increases of TGF-β in both transcript and protein levels were further demonstrated in cartilage treated with hypoxic MSCs (Figs. 3 and 4). IHC analysis revealed a relative higher level of COL II and a lower level of COL X in the hypoxic MSCs-treated OA group compared to those without treatment. Notably, TGF-β-KO MSCs reverse the expression change exerted by hypoxic MSCs (Figs. 6 and 7).

Aggrecan is encoded by the ACAN gene, which is one of the most abundant proteoglycans inarticular cartilage [27], and has been found to up-regulate in stem cells cultured under hypoxic conditions [28]. We currently examined the expression of aggrecan, a key component of the extracellular matrix crucial for enhancing cartilage’s load-bearing capacity [29]. Previous research has highlighted the pivotal role of synovial mesenchymal progenitor-derived aggrecan in regulating cartilage homeostasis and facilitating its natural repair processes [30]. The presence of aggrecan has been associated with indications of cartilage regeneration [30]. Given that TGF-β has been shown to stimulate the expression of both COLII and aggrecan [26]. it was expected that their expression patterns would align. Our findings revealed that hypoxic-cultured MSCs notably upregulated aggrecan expression in the meniscus, synovium, and cartilage. While significant TGF-β expression was only observed in cartilage. The results suggest a potential paracrine mechanism, wherein MSCs in the cartilage secrete TGF-β, consequently inducing aggrecan expression in the synovium [31].

The TGF-β superfamily, comprising TGF-β and bone morphogenetic proteins (BMPs), modulates various physiological processes. Our current study demonstrated increased expression of TGF-β in hypoxic MSCs-treated cartilage tissue. Hypoxia has been known to stimulate TGF-β expression in MSCs and play a key role that is active during hypoxic conditions in cancer [17, 18]. In fact, hypoxia-inducible factor-1α (HIF-1α) has been known to activate TGF-β/Smad signaling to increase collagen deposition in dermal fibroblasts [32]. The activation of TGF-β/Smad pathway by HIF-1α has also been found in kidney tubular epithelial cells [33]. Thus, we suggest the hypoxia/HIF-1α/TGF-β/Smad axis also play a functional role in MSCs for treating OA. Additionally, several confounding factors may influence TGF-β expression in the experimental setting. Beyond HIF-1α-mediated pathways, hypoxia can trigger a range of cellular stress responses that may independently modulate TGF-β levels [34]. For instance, the accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress, and mechanical stimuli such as shear stress have all been implicated in the upregulation of TGF-β expression in various cell types [35, 36]. Further investigation into downstream signals such as the TGF-β/Smad pathway will help clarify its mechanistic role in cartilage protection.

TGF-β is a crucial growth factor for the maintenance of articular cartilage [37]. OA-related inflammation was reported to be able to impair the protective effect of TGF-β on chondrocyte hypertrophy [38]. Our in vivo ACLT model of OA clearly demonstrated that injection of hypoxic MSCs significantly up-regulated the expression of TGF-β in both cartilage and synovium. Thus, we suggest hypoxic MSCs have great potential to compensate for the blockage of TGF-βexerted protective effects induced by OA-related inflammation.

TGF-β signaling has been known to stimulate the expression of both COL II and aggrecan [26] and to inhibit the expression of COLX. Previous research has highlighted the pivotal role of synovial mesenchymal progenitor-derived aggrecan in regulating cartilage homeostasis and facilitating its natural repair processes [30]. COL II is the predominant collagen type found in cartilaginous tissues and serves as a scaffold for cartilage regeneration [39]. In contrast, COL X is a marker of hypertrophic cartilage, which is a hallmark of OA progression [40–42].Hypoxic MSCs have been demonstrated to exhibit better extracellular matrix milieu by increasing COLII and decreasing type X collagen compared to discs that received normoxic MSCs [14]. These findings suggest that hypoxic culture conditions influence the expression of COL II and COL X, although the underlying mechanisms remain partially undefined. Our current findings are in agreement with the previous study that increased expression of TGF-β was correlated with a higher level of aggrecan and COL II and a lower level of COL X in hypoxic MSCs. Analysis of the expressions of COL II and COL X in TGF-β KO MSCs-treated cartilage further demonstrated TGF-βmay promote COL II expression and inhibit COL X expression during hypoxic MSCs therapy. We therefore propose a model that hypoxia induces TGF-β expression. Injection of hypoxic MSCs in knee joints promotes COL II expression and down-regulates COL X level to repair OA mediated by TGF-β (Fig. 7).

Aside from TGF-β, a wide range of factors are known to be induced by hypoxia-inducible factors (HIFs), including vascular endothelial growth factor A (VEGF-A), microRNAs such as miR-210, matrix metalloproteinases (MMP-1 and MMP-3), and SRY-box transcription factor 9 (SOX9) [43, 44]. Notably, intra-articular injection of miR-210 has been shown to promote repair of the avascular (white) zone of the meniscus by increasing COL II expression [45], suggesting that miR-210 may also contribute to the favorable extracellular matrix environment observed in tissues treated with hypoxic MSCs. In contrast, a previous study reported that SOX9 expression was not associated with COL II production in adult articular chondrocytes [42]. While MMPs are essential for normal collagen turnover, their specific roles in modulating COL II and COL X expression in cartilage treated with hypoxic MSCs remain unclear.

While our study provides compelling evidence supporting the role of TGF-β in hypoxic MSC-mediated cartilage repair in a rabbit OA model, it lacks validation in clinically relevant systems. Specifically, the absence of human cartilage explant cultures and large animal OA models limits the immediate translational applicability of our findings. To enhance the clinical relevance of this strategy, future studies should include in vitro culture of human cartilage tissue blocks to further support its therapeutic potential.

In addition to bone marrow-derived mesenchymal stem cells, human urine-derived stem cells (USCs) have demonstrated comparable cartilage repair efficacy [46]. Similarly, adipose-derived mesenchymal stromal cells (ADSCs) also show promising therapeutic potential for treating cartilage defects [47]. However, differences in their immunomodulatory profiles, differentiation capacity, and secretome composition may affect therapeutic outcomes. Standardizing protocols and identifying optimal cell sources remain critical for clinical translation. Moreover, challenges such as donor variability, regulatory hurdles, and limited data from human cartilage explants or large animal models must be addressed. Future studies should focus on these translational aspects to better bridge bench and bedside.

Taken together, our study for the first time demonstrated the hypoxia/TGF-β/COLII_COLX axis inhypoxicMSCs-treated OA model (Fig. 8). TGF-β plays a crucial role as a key factor tofacilitate cartilage regenerationand exert the therapeutic effect of hypoxic-cultured MSC for the treatment of OA.
