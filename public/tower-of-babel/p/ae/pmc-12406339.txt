Metal profiling of Hass avocados: a cross-sectional study using ICP-MS and pXRF
Authors: Michael Anastario, Delaney Watkins, Aaron J. Specht, Ashlyn Zilch, Cynthia Armendáriz Arnez, Courtney Roper
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveMetals in agricultural products can vary substantially based on numerous factors. This pilot study aimed to quantify metal concentrations in the mesocarp and exocarp of Hass avocados available to U.S. consumers using inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS), and to evaluate the feasibility of portable X-ray fluorescence (pXRF) as a rapid screening tool for elemental analysis.ResultsTwenty-four elements were assessed across 24 avocado samples stratified by country of origin and agricultural practice. ICP-MS detected multiple metals—including K, Mg, Ca, Fe, Co, Ni, Zn, and Hg—with higher concentrations generally observed in the exocarp. Several elements exhibited strong co-occurrence patterns, such as Co–Ni in mesocarp and Mg–Fe–Mo–Hg in exocarp, with notable variation by source and farming method. Principal components and K-means clustering identified distinct metal mixture profiles. pXRF element counts correlated moderately with ICP-MS concentrations for K, Ca, Fe, and Zn, supporting its potential as a field-based prescreening method. These findings provide baseline data for environmental monitoring of avocados and illustrate the value of pXRF for future field-based biosocial and agricultural research.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13104-025-07430-w.

Introduction

Avocados (Persea americana) can accumulate metals. Assessing their distribution in avocado tissues is important for evaluating dietary exposure risks and how agricultural practices modify concentrations. Research employing ICP-MS has demonstrated significant variability in concentrations of metals such as nickel (Ni), arsenic (As), molybdenum (Mo), cadmium (Cd), and lead (Pb) in avocado mesocarp [1]. Hass avocados have notably high Hg concentrations relative to other tropical fruits, possibly due to Hg accumulation in lipophilic matrices like avocado mesocarp [2].

This research note presents results from a pilot study that aimed to: (1) Measure the presence and concentration of multiple metals in the mesocarp and exocarp (Fig. 1) of Hass avocados available to USA consumers using ICP-MS; (2) Characterize metal mixtures in avocado tissues; and (3) Examine calibration curves between ICP-MS and portable X-ray fluorescence (pXRF) results. The methods and data presented serve as referents for a larger, ongoing biosocial study designed to characterize the movement of metals through the avocado supply chain.

Methods

Study design

This pilot study employed a cross-sectional observational design to analyze 24 avocado samples, categorized by country of origin (Mexico, USA) and agricultural practice (organic, conventional). Avocado samples were commercially sourced, purchased from grocery stores and wholesale suppliers in the USA. Country of origin (Mexico or USA) and agricultural practice (organic or conventional) were assigned based on supplier or store labeling. All samples were Hass avocados. From November 11, 2024 to January 15, 2025, twelve avocados sourced from California, USA (6 organic, 6 conventional) and twelve avocados sourced from Michoacán, Mexico (6 organic, 6 conventional), all of which were available to USA consumers, were obtained for analysis.

Sample preparation

Avocados were washed with deionized water. Exocarp and mesocarp were separated using porcelain knives and spoons. Exocarp samples were dehydrated at 57.2 °C for 48 h, and mesocarp samples were dehydrated at 57.2 °C for 96 h. Samples were ground using a ceramic mortar and pestle, then stored in 5mL Eppendorf tubes.

Metal analysis

ICP-MS

To prepare avocado mesocarp and exocarp samples for ICP-MS analysis, a microwave-assisted acid digestion method was employed. Dried samples were first weighed to approximately 0.1 g and transferred into MARSXpress digestion vessels. Approximately 0.1 g of dried avocado mesocarp or exocarp was weighed into 5 mL polypropylene tubes and digested with 2 mL of trace metal–grade nitric acid (HNO₃) in a MARS 6 microwave digestion system (CEM Corporation, Matthews, NC). This protocol was modeled after a validated method for high-fat organic matrices, such as olive oil, developed by CEM Corporation, which involves digestion of 0.5 g of sample with 10 mL of HNO₃. Our protocol maintained similar acid-to-sample ratios, and digestion was deemed complete based on the resulting clear, colorless solutions with no visible residue (CEM Corporation, 2015).[3] Due to residual oils in the mesocarp, samples were treated with 1 mL nitric acid (HNO₃) and pre-reacted overnight to minimize pressure buildup. One milliliter (mL) of Milli-Q (MQ) was added before samples were sealed in preparation for digestion. Samples were then subjected to a controlled heating program using a MARS 6 microwave digestion system (CEM Corporation). The temperature was ramped to 200 °C over 20 min and held at this temperature for an additional 10 min. Given the lipid content in avocado samples, potential interference from undigested organic matter was mitigated by ensuring complete oxidation through the nitric acid digestion step. After digestion, samples were cooled to room temperature. The digested samples were subsequently diluted to a final volume of 15 mL with ultrapure water and spiked with 0.15mL of concentrated hydrochloric acid (HCl). This digestion protocol ensured the efficient breakdown of organic material while preserving the integrity of trace metal concentrations in the samples. As a quality control measure, two replicates of NIST 1515 Apple Leaves were included in the ICP-MS run. These CRMs were analyzed under the same digestion and instrumental conditions as the avocado samples to evaluate digestion efficiency and analytical accuracy. While the CRMs were not subjected to the grinding process used for avocado tissues, their inclusion provides verification of the performance of the digestion and measurement phases of the protocol. Elemental concentrations are reported in ug/g to reflect the final concentrations measured in the digested solution analyzed via ICP-MS.

pXRF

Assessment of metals in the avocado exocarp and mesocarp was conducted using a Thermo Niton XL5 Plus pXRF analyzer, operated in Soils Mode under factory calibration. The instrument automatically cycled through multiple filter combinations and adjusted tube settings (voltage and current) across distinct measurement phases to optimize detection across a broad range of elements, from low to high atomic numbers. Measurement conditions included tube voltages up to 50 kV and currents up to 333 µA, depending on the active filter and elemental group being analyzed. Each opened 5mL tube was placed in an XRF cup vertically, ensuring full X-ray tube coverage. Each sample was analyzed for a duration of 180 s. Prior to sample analysis, four certified reference materials provided by Thermo Scientific (QC Standard Multi Element RCRA, QC Standard Soil, QC Standard Blank SiO₂, and Control Sample Soil) were analyzed to verify instrument performance and sensitivity, with measured values falling within ± 20% of certified concentrations. To account for potential background signal, we subjected three unused 5 mL Eppendorf tubes to pXRF analysis and subtracted the average signal values for each element from the corresponding sample measurements. The normalized count values for each element of interest within the analyzer’s range (which excluded Mg and Al) were calculated. pXRF was selected as a second analytical method as it is being evaluated for field application and screening to prioritize samples for ICP-MS when conducting field research in Mexico, where sample volumes often exceed laboratory processing capacity that occurs for samples transported to the USA. Given the high cost and logistical constraints of ICP-MS, the use of pXRF provides a rapid, lower-cost approach for preliminary assessment, enabling prioritization of samples for more detailed laboratory-based analyses.

Statistical analysis

To evaluate the presence and concentration of multiple metals in the mesocarp and exocarp, elemental concentrations obtained from ICP-MS were first compared against established limits of detection (LOD). The geometric mean and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were computed for each element stratified by mesocarp vs. exocarp, country of origin (Mexico vs. USA), and agricultural practice (organic vs. conventional).

To characterize metal mixtures and explore co-occurrence patterns, several tests were conducted. First, one overall multivariate F test (from a MANOVA model) was conducted to provide a “protected test” of the effect of the interaction between country of origin, agricultural practice, and tissue type on multiple metal concentrations. Pearson correlation coefficients were computed to examine associations among metal concentrations within mesocarp and exocarp sub-samples, and between mesocarp and exocarp concentrations. Principal components analysis (PCA) was applied separately to standardized values in mesocarp and exocarp samples. Components retained were determined by scree plots and eigenvalues (> 1). PCA loadings were examined to identify metals that contributed most to variance within each tissue type. Finally, unsupervised clustering via K-means (k = 3) was performed to identify distinct metal mixture signatures across samples. The optimal number of clusters was determined using the elbow method and silhouette analysis. Chi-square tests were used to assess associations between cluster membership and sample attributes (country of origin, farming type). Metal signatures were identified if PCA loading was > 0.30 and the standardized cluster center from K-means clustering was > 1.0 for two elements within the same cluster.

To develop calibration curves for the pXRF readings, we compared pXRF measurements with ICP-MS reference values for each element and performed analyses separately for the mesocarp and exocarp samples. Linear regressions were then fit to model pXRF element counts per second as a function of ICP-MS values in each tissue subset. To account for possible non-linearities and outliers, Spearman’s rank correlation between measurements was also computed.

Results

Among the 24 elements analyzed, several metals were consistently detected above the LOD in avocado mesocarp tissues, including Na, Mg, K, Ca, Al, Mn, Fe, Co, Ni, Cu, Zn, Mo, and Ba. However, Be, Ag, and Sn were not detected in any samples.

Geometric mean concentrations of detected elements varied substantially by tissue type, country of origin, and agricultural practice (Table 1). Elemental concentrations were generally higher in exocarp compared to mesocarp. K, Mg, and Ca demonstrated notably higher concentrations in exocarp compared to mesocarp. K concentrations were highest in conventional USA-sourced exocarp (GM = 39,111 ug/g, 95% CI: 36,112 − 42,359) and lowest in organic USA mesocarp (GM = 18,084 ug/g, 95% CI: 13,480 − 24,261). Metals such as Fe, Al, Mn, and Zn followed a similar pattern of higher concentrations in exocarp than in mesocarp, with considerable variability across agricultural practices. Metals such as As, Cd, and Pb generally presented low concentrations across all sample groups. Hg was generally higher in avocado exocarp sourced from USA.

The protective F test for multiple dependent variables was significant (Pillai’s trace = 0.84, F(20, 21) = 4.9, p < 0.001). Evaluation of elemental co-occurrence across avocado tissues identified several pairs exhibiting consistently strong correlations (r ≥ 0.50) within mesocarp, exocarp, and cross-tissue analyses (Tables S1a-S1c). Stronger correlations were observed between K and Mo (mesocarp r = 0.62; exocarp r = 0.72; cross-tissue r = 0.67), Mn and Ba (mesocarp r = 0.63; exocarp r = 0.54; cross-tissue r = 0.54), Co and Ba (mesocarp r = 0.62; exocarp r = 0.72; cross-tissue r = 0.59), Hg and Zn (mesocarp r = 0.70; exocarp r = 0.71; cross-tissue r = 0.52), and Hg and K (mesocarp = 0.55; exocarp = 0.87; cross-tissue r = 0.74).

The PCA yielded three components within mesocarp and exocarp samples. Within the mesocarp (Table S2a), PC1 showed relatively high loadings of Pb, Ni, and Na, whereas PC2 exhibited strong contributions from Se, As, Zn, and K. PC3 was characterized most notably by Ni and Co loadings. Within the exocarp (Table S2b), PC1 featured moderately broad contributions from Fe, Mo, Mg, and Hg. PC2 showed strong contributions from Sb, Na, Al, and As, and PC3 showed strong contributions from Ni, Ba, Mn, and Na.

For K means clustering, a three-cluster solution was identified for both mesocarp (n = 24) and exocarp (n = 24) samples. Among mesocarp samples (Table S3a), the first cluster (n = 3) was notable for higher Mn, Co, Ni, and Ba. The second cluster (n = 14) was notable for moderate Pb, and the third cluster (n = 7) for Mg, Zn, As, and Hg. Cluster membership was significantly associated with country of origin (χ²=8.19, p = 0.017), and weakly associated with farming type (χ²=3.69, p = 0.158). Exocarp findings are reported in Table S3b.

Further evaluation of PCA and K means cluster results suggested evidence of a metal signature in the mesocarp between Co and Ni. In the exocarp, there was evidence of a metal signature between Mg, Fe, Mo, and Hg, as well as Sb with Na and Na with Ni. Further analyses revealed that the mesocarp signature of Co with Ni (where ICP-MS values exceeded 1 standard deviation for both elements) was only present in organic avocados from USA (see Table S4). The exocarp signature of Mg, Fe, Mo, Hg was more frequently present in avocados from USA (χ²=4.29, p = 0.038) (Table S4).

pXRF measurements were calibrated against ICP-MS for the following elements in mesocarp and exocarp samples: K, Ca, Fe, Cu, Zn, As, Mo, Cd, Sb, Pb, and Hg (Table S5). Limited variability prevented calibration for V, Cr, Mn, Co, Ni, Se, and Ba. In the mesocarp, K, Ca, and Zn displayed significant linear relationships (K beta = 1.12, 95% CI 0.48–1.77; Ca beta = 0.85, 95% CI 0.52–1.17; Zn beta = 1.26, 95% CI 0.86–1.66; Fig. 2). In exocarp samples, K, Ca, Fe, Cu, and Zn displayed the strongest linear relationships.

Discussion

This pilot study illustrates multi-elemental metal presence in both the mesocarp and exocarp of Hass avocados available to USA consumers, with variation across tissue types, country of origin, and agricultural practice. ICP-MS demonstrated higher sensitivity and reliability for quantifying trace elements, while pXRF may be feasible as a field-deployable screening method with moderate correlation to ICP-MS for K, Ca, and Zn in the mesocarp.

Higher elemental concentrations in exocarp likely reflect direct environmental exposure or mesocarp-to-exocarp uptake. While detection of Pb, Cd, and As in the avocado mesocarp occurred at relatively low levels, Hg was elevated. After adjusting for moisture content to estimate fresh-weight concentrations, maximum mesocarp Hg levels ranged from approximately 0.012 to 0.021 mg/kg. These concentrations exceed stringent reference values for produce (e.g., 0.01 mg/kg) [4] but remain well below the FAO/WHO guideline range for mercury in fish (0.5–1.0 mg/kg). [5] Although the potential health risk is likely low for adults who are not pregnant or breastfeeding—given typical portion sizes and the likelihood that inorganic mercury predominates in plant matrices—these findings warrant further investigation, potentially using Cold Vapor Atomic Fluorescence Spectroscopy for evaluating elemental mercury in samples. Future work should assess mercury speciation in avocados and determine whether produce-specific thresholds are needed to guide consumer safety.

Metal mixture analysis suggested shared uptake pathways or sources. For example, the consistent pairing of Co and Ni in organic USA mesocarp samples may occur due to their similar properties and their co-occurrence in geological formations, such as ultramafic rocks and volcanic soils.[6] The co-occurrence of Mg, Fe, Mo, and Hg in USA exocarp samples may suggest specific geochemical or agronomic factors. These metals may be more prominent in serpentine soils, which are formed from the weathering of ultramafic rocks and point to soil as the potential source of metals. These metals may interact similarly with the plant, leading to fruit uptake [7].

Calibration analyses confirmed the feasibility of pXRF for non-destructive screening, though performance varied by element and tissue. While K, Ca, Fe, and Zn showed strong linear relationships, other elements, particularly those with frequent non-detects or low variability, were less amenable to pXRF-based prediction. As pXRF becomes more widely used in agricultural research, these findings offer a first round of results on its current limitations and potential in the context of this pilot study.

Our findings contribute to a growing body of literature characterizing elemental concentrations in avocados. We compared our ICP-MS-derived elemental concentration data to values reported by USDA FoodData Central (2024),[8] Ford et al. (2023),[9] and Salazar-García et al. (2021).[10] Observed variations across studies likely stem from differences in analytical methods, avocado cultivars (Hass vs. Mendez), environmental conditions, and agricultural practices. Nonetheless, concentrations of major (K, Mg, Ca) and trace elements (Fe, Zn, Cu) exhibited general consistency, highlighting the robustness and reliability of ICP-MS for comprehensive elemental profiling. Importantly, our study measured trace elements relevant to food safety (e.g., Hg, Cd, Pb), which were not reported in the comparative studies mentioned above. Additionally, we compared our results to those of Muñoz-Redondo et al. (2022),[1] who similarly employed ICP-MS to measure elemental concentrations in Hass avocados sourced internationally, including from Mexico. Their reported values for major elements (Mg, K, Ca, Zn, Cu, Mn, Fe) relatively matched ours, reinforcing methodological consistency across ICP-MS analyses. Minor differences observed for certain trace elements (Ni, Ba, Cd, Pb) likely reflect variations in analytical sensitivity, sample preparation protocols, and natural geographic or agricultural variability.

Limitations

This study is limited by its small sample size, which restricts generalizability and statistical power. Sample preparation steps may influence metal recovery and detection. As a pilot study relying on commercially purchased samples, detailed farm-level sourcing data and soil composition information were not available. This limits the ability to attribute elemental concentrations to specific growing conditions, such as soil type, in this phase of research. Future phases of this research will involve directly sourcing avocados from farms in Mexico and collecting paired soil samples from the trees from which the fruit are harvested to better characterize environmental contributors to elemental concentrations. The small sample size, combined with subdivision into multiple clusters, limits the interpretability of observed metal co-occurrence patterns. The absence of paired soil data further limits the ability to distinguish whether observed co-occurrences reflect meaningful environmental influences or are incidental. In addition, although we used a ceramic mortar and pestle that was cleaned between each sample, we did not analyze blank samples that had undergone the same grinding protocol. Therefore, we cannot exclude the possibility of trace contamination introduced during sample homogenization. Future work will include grinder blanks to evaluate this potential source of contamination. Nevertheless, the pilot findings provide an important baseline for future research on environmental monitoring and potential health dimensions of metal exposure through avocado consumption and highlight the potential need for further monitoring across transnational agricultural supply chains.
