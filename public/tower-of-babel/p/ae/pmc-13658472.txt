Temporal and spatial RNA expression dynamics in postmortem skin support PMI estimation and reveal spatial signatures of antemortem injury
Authors: Ken Sato, Takehito Sugasawa, Jiawei Wan, Suzuka Shimmura, Takeki Nishi, Marie Inaba, Hisashi Akuzawa, Fujio Ishizawa, Yukiko Sugano, Yoichiro Takahashi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRNA expression dynamics have emerged as a promising focus in forensic research; however, their practical applications remain limited. In this study, we focused on skin, a biologically stable, forensically practical, and accessible tissue, to characterize postmortem and injury-related RNA expression dynamics and explore their forensic relevance. First, under well-controlled experimental conditions, mouse skin was analyzed up to 8 h postmortem using transcriptomic profiling. Genes exhibiting characteristic time-dependent expression patterns were identified, and a postmortem interval (PMI) estimation model achieved a coefficient of determination of 0.900 and a root mean squared error of 0.894. These findings were subsequently examined in human autopsy skin samples, where several RNAs demonstrated temporal expression changes consistent with those observed in mice. Beyond the initial 8-h transcriptomic analysis, similar expression dynamics in mice were further validated up to 72 h postmortem, partially confirming RNA dynamics shown in human samples. In particular, EGR1 and FOS showed expression patterns consistent with their known functions in the stress response. Furthermore, spatial transcriptomic analysis of injured skin revealed region-specific gene expression alterations according to wound proximity and tissue layer (epidermis and dermis). These patterns were evident specifically in antemortem injuries when compared with postmortem injuries, potentially distinguishing antemortem from postmortem injuries in autopsy samples. In summary, our findings indicate that the skin RNA expression exhibits characteristic temporal and spatial dynamics following death and injury. Introduction

The estimation of the postmortem interval (PMI) is a crucial task in forensic medicine, as it plays a central role in the reconstruction of crime scenes and in determining the cause and manner of death [1]. Although scene investigation and environmental information often contribute substantially to the estimation of the PMI, these data are not always available or sufficiently reliable. In such situations, findings obtained directly from the body become the primary source of information for estimating the PMI.

Traditionally, PMI estimation based on body findings of the deceased has relied on postmortem changes, such as postmortem lividity, rigor mortis, and rectal temperature decline; however, these methods are influenced by individual variability, and their objectivity and reproducibility are limited [2, 3]. Although more objective approaches for PMI estimation, such as electrolyte concentration changes in postmortem blood, have been investigated [4], these methods often demonstrate limited predictive accuracy and are strongly influenced by environmental factors [1]. Consequently, the development of objective and practically applicable methods for PMI estimation remains challenging.

Some existing indicators primarily reflect passive physical or chemical processes following the cessation of the physiological function, such as substance dissipation or redistribution, whereas others, including rigor mortis, arise from intracellular biochemical alterations associated with postmortem metabolic failure. Importantly, these macroscopic phenomena provide temporal information because they indirectly represent time-dependent changes occurring at the cellular level. Therefore, a direct evaluation of such microscopic alterations may offer a more mechanistically grounded and potentially accurate framework for PMI estimation.

After death, cells are abruptly exposed to ischemic conditions and undergo a series of molecular biological changes as they progress toward cell death [5]. Cell death proceeds through temporally organized and partly regulated molecular events, during which intracellular degradation and local tissue responses are accompanied by coordinated alterations in gene and protein expression levels. For example, apoptosis is a programmed form of cell death that is initiated by cellular stress and proceeds under genetic regulation. It is executed through the activation of caspase cascades via both the mitochondrial (intrinsic) pathway and the death receptor (extrinsic) pathway [5, 6]. During this process, multiple molecules, including upstream transcriptional regulators, are sequentially activated, resulting in dynamic changes in gene expression levels, leading to specific alterations in RNA levels, followed by the regulation of protein levels. Therefore, if RNA expression dynamics within postmortem cells and tissues can be comprehensively and temporally characterized, these changes may serve as indicators for estimating the PMI.

To date, several studies have attempted to utilize RNA expression levels in postmortem tissues to estimate the PMI; however, most of these investigations have primarily focused on the degradation of RNA after death [7]. For example, some studies have exploited the relative stability of shorter RNA fragments relative to longer transcripts to estimate the PMI based on differences in the abundance of RNA fragments of varying lengths [8]. In contrast, in recent years, studies have reported that the expression of RNA does not disappear randomly after death but instead exhibits temporally ordered changes, a phenomenon referred to as the thanatotranscriptome [9]. Currently, a substantial proportion of these findings have been derived from animal models [10], and investigations using human autopsy cases remain limited. Despite emerging evidence of temporal regulation of the postmortem RNA expression, its application to PMI estimation has not been reported. Although some studies have examined the postmortem RNA expression in humans, such approaches have generally been evaluated under constrained experimental settings [11]. Moreover, most previous investigations have relied on internal organs obtained during autopsy, which typically require invasive tissue sampling and may limit their applicability in routine forensic practice [12].

In the present study, we investigated postmortem RNA dynamics using both mouse and human samples and examined whether these insights relating to the thanatotranscriptome could be applied to PMI estimation. Furthermore, considering that injured tissues inevitably result in local ischemia at the cellular level, which could be illustrated by temporally and spatially distinctive RNA expression patterns at the site [13, 14], we subsequently hypothesized that the findings could be extended to the estimation of human wound age. Specifically, we focused on skin tissue, which can be relatively easily obtained during death investigations and autopsies. In addition, skin has a well-developed barrier function that provides resistance to environmental factors such as microbial invasion, and is more resistant to autolysis than internal organs such as the liver and spleen [15]. Furthermore, tissues with lower vascularity have been shown to better preserve RNA quality in the postmortem period [16]. Indeed, skin RNA quality is known to be well preserved after death [17, 18], supporting its suitability for postmortem transcriptomic analysis.

First, using a mouse model, we demonstrated the presence of RNAs that exhibit characteristic postmortem expression dynamics and may be useful for PMI estimation. Subsequently, we analyzed the RNA expression in human autopsy skin samples obtained through routine forensic practice and identified RNAs that are closely associated with the PMI. Finally, we utilized a spatial transcriptomic analysis, an approach with limited application in forensic medicine, to skin samples from cases of antemortem injury. These findings suggest that a skin RNA expression analysis may serve as a molecular biological indicator for both PMI estimation and the evaluation of antemortem injuries.

Materials and methods

Preparation of RNA from mouse postmortem skin

RNA extraction, purification, and RNA-seq were performed as previously described [19]. All animal experiments were approved by the Animal Care Committee of the University of Tsukuba. The mice were obtained from CREA Japan (Meguro, Tokyo, Japan) at 6 weeks of age and underwent a 2-week acclimation period. The mice were bred and maintained under specific pathogen-free conditions in an air-conditioned animal facility with a 12-h light/12-h dark cycle and standard mouse pellets and water provided ad libitum. Upon commencement of the experiments, the mice were 8 weeks old. Mice were euthanized by CO2 inhalation. Immediately after confirmation of death, the bodies were kept at ambient room temperature (approximately 22–25 °C) under room air exposure for up to 72 h. Dorsal full-thickness skin samples were collected at predefined postmortem time points. Short-term (0, 1, 2, 4, and 8 h) and long-term (0, 12, 24, 48, and 72 h after death) experiments were performed independently, and six mice were assigned to each time point. Briefly, 50–100 mg of mouse skin collected at each time point was placed in a 2-mL screw-cap tube with crushing beads and frozen and crushed in liquid nitrogen. Total RNA from the crushed tissue was extracted using RNAiso Plus (Cat# 9108; Takara Bio Inc., Shiga, Japan), according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

RNA-seq for the mouse skin-derived samples

Libraries for RNA-seq were prepared using 500 ng of RNA from each sample using the NEBNext Ultra II RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina with the NEBNext rRNA Depletion Kit v2 (Cat# E7770S and E7400L; New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The final PCR cycle was 15. Concentrations and size distributions of the libraries were measured using an Agilent DNA 7500 Kit (Cat#5067-1506; Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) with a Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies). The libraries were pooled, and concentrations were adjusted to 1 nM. The pooled libraries were denatured and neutralized. The libraries were then diluted to 1.8 pM and subjected to an NGS run using NextSeq500/550 v2.5 (75 cycles) kits (Cat#20024906; Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) on the NextSeq 500 System (Illumina). Sequencing was performed using 36-base paired-end reads. In the long-term experiments, RNAs were extracted using a similar method. The RNAs were sent to an external service provider, Novogene (Chuo City, Tokyo, Japan), where they were processed on their large-scale sequencing platform, from library creation to sequencing.

Quantitative PCR for mouse RNA

RNA was converted to cDNA using the PrimeScript™ RT Master Mix (Takara Bio Inc.) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The synthesized cDNA was diluted 10-fold prior to downstream applications. qPCR was performed using the designed primers, cDNA, and SYBR Green qPCR Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Most primer pairs were originally designed using Primer-BLAST or Primer3 to ensure specificity for the intended target sequences, and the primer sequences for 18S were adopted from previously published studies [20] (Supplementary Table 1). Each lyophilized primer was reconstituted by adding ddH2O to a final concentration of 100 µM. The final qPCR reaction volume was 10 µL, containing 5 µL TB, 2.6 µL ddH2O, 0.2 µL each primer, and 2 µL cDNA. Amplification was performed on a QuantStudio 5 Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and the data were analyzed using QuantStudio™ Design and Analysis Software v1.5.2. The cycling conditions consisted of an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 2 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 1 s and 60 °C for 20 s for annealing/extension. A melt curve analysis was then performed with a hold at 95 °C for 1 s, 60 °C for 20 s, and a subsequent temperature increase to 95 °C at a ramp rate of 0.1 °C/s. Negative controls were included by substituting ddH2O for cDNA to monitor primer-dimer formation. All experiments were performed in duplicate, and the average Ct values were used for analysis. For normalization of the target gene expression, 18S rRNA was used as the housekeeping gene. Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the comparative Ct (ΔCt) method, in which the mean Ct value of 18S rRNA was subtracted from that of the target gene for each sample.

Preparation of human skin samples

Postmortem skin specimens were collected during medicolegal autopsies performed at the Department of Forensic Medicine, Faculty of Medicine, University of Tsukuba, between February 2023 and December 2024, under the approval of the institutional review board and the appropriate regulatory authorities. From the 114 autopsy cases, the 5-mm diameter skin samples were obtained from the anterior chest, and each skin specimen was immediately placed in RNAlater (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and stored at − 30 °C until further processing. The anterior chest was selected as the sampling site because it is routinely exposed along the standard autopsy incision line, allowing minimal additional invasiveness. Furthermore, the underlying sternum serves as a stable anatomical landmark enabling consistent and reproducible sampling compared to sites such as the abdomen where subcutaneous fat thickness varies considerably between individuals.

RNA extraction from human postmortem skin

For RNA extraction, skin tissue samples preserved in RNAlater were thawed on ice. Prior to homogenization, each sample was manually divided into four smaller sections using scissors and placed into a single 2-mL tube containing a metal stick and 900 µL of QIAzol Lysis Reagent (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). Mechanical homogenization was performed using a TissueLyser III (Qiagen) at 30 Hz for 2 min. To minimize RNA degradation and improve mechanical disruption efficiency, the TissueLyser adapter was pre-cooled to − 30 °C prior to use, and samples were maintained on ice before and immediately after homogenization. Following homogenization, samples were briefly centrifuged to collect the contents, the metal stick was removed, and the lysates were transferred to clean tubes, vortexed for 20 s, and stored at − 80 °C until further processing.

Total RNA was extracted from the disrupted tissue and buffer using the RNeasy Plus Universal Mini Kit (Qiagen), according to the manufacturer’s instructions, with some steps automated on a QIAcube Connect (Qiagen). The concentration and integrity of the extracted RNA were assessed using a NanoDrop Lite Plus spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies), providing both RNA concentration and RNA Integrity Number (RIN) values for quality control.

In this study, we investigated postmortem RNA dynamics. Given that refrigeration substantially attenuates postmortem molecular changes, including RNA degradation [8, 21–25], accurate evaluation requires consideration of the interval prior to cold storage. We therefore analyzed the pre-refrigeration interval, defined as the time between the estimated time of death and placement of the body in a refrigerator, as the primary temporal indicator in this study. Of the 114 samples from which RNA was successfully extracted, 11 samples were removed from the analysis since their pre-refrigeration interval was not reliably determined. Furthermore, 15 samples with RNA concentration < 95 ng/µL or RIN < 5.7 were excluded from the primary analysis. These thresholds were selected empirically based on the distribution of samples in the RNA concentration–RIN scatter plot (Supplementary Fig. 1), which suggested a relatively homogeneous cluster of samples above these values. Details of the excluded cases were summarized in Supplementary Table 2.

cDNA conversion and quantitative PCR for human RNA

First, the extracted total RNA was adjusted to a concentration of 50 ng/µL by adding ddH2O. cDNA conversion and qPCR were performed similarly to the aforementioned mouse experiments. Primer pairs for most target genes were originally designed using Primer-BLAST or Primer3 based on reference sequences obtained from NCBI, and the primer sequences for ZFP36 and GAPDH were adopted from previously published studies [26, 27]. All primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Table 3. Experiments were performed in duplicate, and for samples judged to require confirmation based on melt curve profiles or other quality checks, additional replicates were performed. For samples with more than two replicates, Ct values were arranged in descending order, and the mean of the middle two or three values was used as the representative Ct value. GAPDH was used as the housekeeping gene for normalization. Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the comparative Ct (ΔCt) method by subtracting the representative Ct value of GAPDH from that of the target gene for each sample.

Spatial transcriptome analysis

Spatial transcriptomic analysis was performed using the GeoMx Digital Spatial Profiler (NanoString Technologies, Seattle, WA, USA) with the Human NGS Whole Transcriptome Atlas RNA panel (ver. 1.0). Skin samples were obtained from a male in his 50s with a scalp laceration at autopsy. Two specimens were collected from the injured skin, which had been subjected to sharp-force injury 6 h before death and refrigerated for 24.8 h after death. An adjacent autopsy-incised skin specimen was collected as a control. All samples were formalin-fixed and paraffin-embedded (FFPE). Tissue sections of 5 μm thickness were prepared from each FFPE block and mounted on the same glass slide. RNA quality was assessed by in situ hybridization using β-actin, confirming adequate preservation of RNA in all sections. Subsequently, immunofluorescent staining was performed for fibronectin, CD31, and CD45, as well as DAPI staining. Regions of interest (ROIs) were defined based on the immunostaining images, selecting areas in which the epidermis and dermis could be clearly distinguished, while avoiding structures such as blood vessels and hair follicles.

For the injured sites, ROIs were defined in both the epidermis and dermis at three distinct locations relative to the injury: proximal, intermediate, and distal from the injury, with three ROIs per layer at each location, resulting in a total of 18 ROIs. For the non-injured site collected at autopsy, ROIs were defined in both the epidermis and dermis at two locations near the tissue edge and one location at the central portion of the tissue, resulting in a total of six ROIs. The size of each ROI was adjusted to include at least 200 cells (range, 201–336). The surface areas of the ROIs ranged from 14,727.435 to 302,832.991 µm2. Third-quartile (Q3) normalization was applied to minimize variability related to differences in RNA abundance among ROIs.

Total RNA from each ROI was collected, libraries were prepared, and transcriptome data were obtained by next-generation sequencing using an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform with 27 bp paired-end reads. Sequencing data were processed using the GeoMx NGS pipeline to generate digital count (DCC) files for a downstream analysis.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R (ver. 4.4.0) and CLC Genomics Workbench (ver. 24.0.1).

For mouse gene expression data, initial filtering and heatmap visualization were conducted using the CLC Genomics Workbench. Genes with a maximum group mean expression > 10 were included in the analysis. Multiple testing correction was performed using the Bonferroni method, and genes with adjusted p-values < 0.05 were retained for heatmap generation.

From the genes displayed in the heatmap, protein-coding genes with Bonferroni-adjusted p-values < 0.001 and known homology to human genes were selected as candidate variables. These genes were further subjected to lasso regression using the glmnet package in R (ver. 4.1.8.) to identify genes strongly associated with the PMI. The top 10 genes with the strongest associations were selected.

To construct a PMI estimation model while reducing the influence of multicollinearity, stepwise variable selection was additionally applied because lasso-based screening alone did not fully eliminate multicollinearity, and five genes with variance inflation factor values < 5 were retained as explanatory variables. Ridge regression was then used to derive the final PMI estimation formula. The regularization parameter was determined using 5-fold cross-validation, and a fixed random seed was used to ensure reproducibility. All 30 mouse samples were included in the ridge regression analysis, and model performance was assessed using predictions generated for the full dataset by the final model.

For analyses using human skin samples, scatter data were evaluated using R. For each RNA, samples within the 72-h pre-refrigeration interval were analyzed using a quadratic regression model. Samples with standardized residuals exceeding ± 3 were defined as outliers and excluded from the analysis for the corresponding RNA. After outlier removal, the same regression model was re-applied, and scatter plots with fitted quadratic curves were generated. Ridge regression was subsequently performed using outlier-filtered data to construct a PMI estimation model.

A spatial transcriptomic analysis was performed using the GeoMx Digital Spatial Profiler (NanoString Technologies). Gene expression values for each ROI were filtered to exclude targets below the limit of quantitation and subsequently normalized using Q3 normalization. Statistical analyses and data visualization were conducted using R. For the volcano plot analysis, the differential gene expression between ROIs proximal and distal to the injury site (proximal vs. distal) was assessed using linear models with empirical Bayes moderation implemented in the limma package in R (ver. 3.60.6.), applied to Q3-normalized expression values. P-values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the false discovery rate (FDR). Genes were considered statistically significant if the FDR was < 0.05 and the absolute log2 fold change was ≥ 1. Gene expression distributions were visualized using box plots, with ROIs grouped according to their spatial distance from the injury site. Box-plot analyses were performed separately for the epidermis and dermis and were used for descriptive visualization of expression patterns.

Results

Correlation of RNA expression levels in mouse skin with the PMI

As an initial step toward potential clinical applications in human samples, we first examined the relationship between RNA expression levels and the PMI in mouse skin. Given that smaller animals generally decompose more rapidly than larger ones, and that recent findings suggest postmortem molecular changes occur approximately ten times faster in rodents than in humans [28, 29], the time points of 0–8 h in the mouse model were selected to capture early postmortem transcriptomic dynamics in a timeframe that may approximate the early PMI period in humans. Specifically, skin samples were collected at 0, 1, 2, 4, and 8 h after euthanasia, and total RNA was extracted for transcriptome analysis.

This analysis identified approximately 94 genes whose expression levels were significantly correlated with the PMI. Among them, 62 genes exhibited a gradual decrease in their expression, whereas 32 genes showed an increase (Fig. 1A). To enhance translatability to human analyses, those RNAs that were coding and showed homology to human sequences were further selected if they exhibited stronger correlation with the PMI. A detailed examination of the 10 genes identified by lasso regression as showing the strongest association with the PMI revealed that seven genes, namely, Acot1, Cpz, Evovl7, Klk10, Lyz1, Rptn, and Sec14l4, decreased with an increasing PMI, whereas three genes, Egr1, Fos, and Ifrd1, increased (Fig. 1B). The RNA-seq findings were independently validated by qPCR, demonstrating strong concordance between the two methods, except for Acot1 (Supplementary Fig. 2). Importantly, the observation of the increased expression of certain genes, such as Fos, Egr1, and Ifrd1, after death suggests that these changes may reflect biologically meaningful regulatory processes, rather than mere RNA degradation after death.

Proof-of-concept for estimation of the PMI from skin RNA expression data

Next, we evaluated whether these postmortem gene expression changes could be valuable for estimating the PMI. To ensure practicality, we utilized data from the 10 genes identified as having the strongest association with the PMI to construct a regression model. Using these 10 genes, a regression equation with a correlation coefficient of 0.958 was obtained (Fig. 1C, left), with a coefficient of determination (R2) of 0.917 and a root mean squared error (RMSE) of 0.815. When the number of genes was reduced to five candidates deemed statistically more appropriate, the regression model retained high predictive performance, with a correlation coefficient of 0.949, an R2 of 0.900, and an RMSE of 0.894 (Fig. 1C, right). These findings support the feasibility of using selected gene expression profiles as a basis for PMI estimation.

Selection of candidate genes for human application

The above results clarified that RNA data from postmortem skin have the potential to be used for PMI estimation. Based on the mouse results, we next sought to identify candidate genes, of which human orthologs are well-characterized and can be utilized for PMI estimation. Among the mouse genes correlated with the PMI, we first extracted those with known human orthologs. We further cross-referenced these genes with the publicly available GTEx database to identify transcripts expressed at sufficiently high levels with reliable detectability and stability in human postmortem skin. Through this process, eight candidate genes were selected for potential application in human samples: DUSP1, EGR1, FOS, H3F3B, JUNB, MTND3, RPLP2, and ZFP36 (Supplementary Table 4). The mouse homologs of the eight genes demonstrated significant correlation with the PMI, with expression levels increasing progressively with the PMI (Fig. 2). These genes, showing increased expression levels along with an increased PMI, are suggested to hold promise as molecular markers for PMI estimation in human skin.

Human skin RNA expression and its correlation with the PMI

We subsequently evaluated the expression of the eight selected genes in human skin. The study included 88 cases selected based on RNA concentration, RIN values, and relatively reliable PMI information (see Materials and Methods).

RNA was extracted from subcutaneous fat-free sections of the anterior thoracic skin, predominantly obtained along the dissection line at autopsy. For each gene, quadratic regression models were applied to evaluate the relationship between expression levels and the pre-refrigeration interval within 72 h, excluding outliers, followed by regression analyses. Among the eight genes, the expression levels of FOS, H3F3B, and JUNB showed significant correlations with the pre-refrigeration interval (Fig. 3A), whereas the other five genes did not show statistically significant correlations. To construct a regression equation, data for all eight genes were combined, resulting in the generation of a model with a correlation coefficient of 0.528, an R2 of 0.267, and an RMSE of 12.611 (Fig. 3B). Given that RNA degradation is likely to become more pronounced as the PMI progresses, we reanalyzed the same dataset by restricting it to shorter pre-refrigeration intervals, such as within 60, 48, and 36 h (Supplementary Figs. 3–5). When the analysis was restricted to these shorter intervals, EGR1 showed statistically significant correlations with the pre-refrigeration interval. However, even under these conditions, regression models constructed using all eight genes yielded correlation coefficients of up to 0.520, which represented no substantial improvement over the models based on the full 72-h dataset. Furthermore, when the analysis was restricted to samples with RIN ≥ 6.5 or ≥ 7.0, no significant improvement in correlation was observed compared with the scatter plots generated based on the initial inclusion criteria (Supplementary Figs. 6 and 7).

To ensure that the observed associations were not influenced by potential confounders, we evaluated relevant demographic and procedural factors. The pre-refrigeration intervals in this study were not significantly correlated with age, sex, or autopsy month (Supplementary Fig. 8A-C). Although a significant overall effect of cause of death was detected by the Kruskal–Wallis test, no significant pairwise differences were identified following Dunn’s post hoc test with Holm correction (Supplementary Fig. 8D). In addition, the expression levels of the eight genes were largely independent of age and sex, although MTND3 and RPLP2 reached nominal statistical significance with age (p < 0.05) with weak correlations (|r| < 0.3) (Supplementary Fig. 9). RIN values were not significantly correlated with the pre-refrigeration interval, age, sex, or cause of death (Supplementary Fig. 10A, B). Although a significant overall effect of autopsy month was detected by the Kruskal–Wallis test, no significant pairwise differences were identified following Dunn’s post hoc test with Holm correction (Supplementary Fig. 10C, D). Therefore, we concluded that factors such as age, sex, RIN, cause of death, and autopsy month did not affect the present analysis.

Additional validation of RNAs exhibiting characteristic expression dynamics

Experiments using human skin revealed that RNAs, such as FOS, exhibit postmortem expression dynamics that can be approximated by a quadratic curve. This observation led us to hypothesize that extending the postmortem observation period over 8 h in mouse RNA expression analyses would allow the identification of RNAs showing not only monotonic increases but also quadratic-like expression patterns, similar to those in humans, thereby strengthening the reliability of the human skin analysis.

Accordingly, mouse skin samples were collected at postmortem intervals of 0, 12, 24, 48, and 72 h using the same experimental procedures as in the initial experiments, and RNA expression analyses were performed. Although we demonstrated that the relevant transcripts continued to increase throughout the first 8 h of the PMI as shown in Fig. 2, in the present experiments, all eight transcripts (except for mt-Nd3) decreased at later postmortem intervals (Fig. 4). Therefore, most of the relevant genes showed monotonic increases, similar to those in humans, with the exception of mt-Nd3, which demonstrated a constant increase in expression levels throughout the 72-h postmortem period.

Skin RNA status as a potential marker for wound age estimation

Collectively, these findings indicate that while postmortem changes in human skin RNA expression levels display measurable correlations with the pre-refrigeration interval, their precision may be insufficient for routine forensic estimation of the PMI. In contrast, the observed gene expression changes suggest that, at least in part, the variation reflects active biological responses within postmortem tissue rather than passive degradation alone.

Based on this consideration, we hypothesized that the RNA expression dynamics in human skin could potentially be used to estimate the age of skin injuries. In addition, we inferred that the evaluation of injuries within the same individual may reduce the impact of inter-individual variability, leading to a more accurate assessment. To explore this possibility, spatial transcriptomic analysis was performed (see Materials and Methods) to compare gene expression dynamics between regions proximal and distal to the injury (Fig. 5A-C).

In the epidermis, a volcano plot comparing ROIs proximal and distal to the injury demonstrated the significantly higher expression of FOSL1 in the region proximal to the injury (Fig. 5D). To confirm and further characterize this spatial expression pattern, a box-plot analysis of the FOSL1 expression across injury-proximal, intermediate, and distal regions in antemortem tissue, as well as marginal and central regions in postmortem-excised tissue, was performed, revealing that the increased FOSL1 expression was observed exclusively in the injury-proximal epidermis of antemortem samples (Fig. 5E), suggesting that a localized increase in the FOSL1 expression may represent a promising candidate biomarker to indicate the site of antemortem injury.

A similar spatial analysis was performed on the dermis. In the dermal layer, a volcano plot analysis revealed that FASN, FADS2, and NR4A1 were significantly upregulated in ROIs proximal to the injury, whereas the expression of collagen-related genes, including COL1A1 and COL3A1, increased in distal ROIs (Fig. 5F). To confirm and further delineate these spatial expression differences, box plots were generated for the five RNAs identified as significantly differentially expressed in the antemortem injury-related volcano plot; none of these genes demonstrated consistent changes in their expression in postmortem-excised dermal tissue (Fig. 5G). These results suggest that the spatial RNA expression changes observed in the dermis, similar to those in the epidermis, are specific to antemortem injury and are not preserved in postmortem-excised skin.

Discussion

In this study, we performed transcriptomic analyses to elucidate postmortem RNA expression dynamics, or thanatotranscriptomics, and to achieve an objective estimation of the PMI. Skin was selected as the target tissue given its practical accessibility in forensic settings and its biological properties that contribute to postmortem RNA preservation. Using both mouse and human tissues, we successfully identified several RNAs that could be utilized to estimate the PMI. PMI estimation can be achieved with high accuracy in mice based on these RNA expression data. However, the accuracy in humans was insufficient, demonstrating the difficulty in applying these data to the practical estimation of the PMI. In contrast, we demonstrated that the present findings may have potential applications for wound age estimation in human skin and for spatial transcriptomics in forensic situations. In conclusion, the present report evaluated the temporal dynamics of the RNA expression in postmortem and injured tissues and provided novel insights that may contribute to PMI estimation and the assessment of wound vitality.

First, in mouse experiments, we identified sets of RNAs whose expression levels increased or decreased in a time-dependent manner after death. Using these transcripts under controlled experimental conditions, this model enabled highly accurate prediction of the PMI. These results indicate that postmortem RNA expression dynamics retain a certain degree of temporal organization, rather than representing random degradation, and that such structured expression patterns can be exploited for PMI estimation. Importantly, these temporally sensitive and ordered RNA changes were identified in skin tissue, even though the skin is generally considered more resistant to physical stress than internal organs [30, 31]. The observation of such robust temporal signals in skin suggests that this approach may provide valuable insights for PMI estimation using readily obtainable and minimally invasive tissue samples.

Based on mouse experiments, which demonstrated that postmortem RNA expression dynamics remain ordered even after organismal death, we investigated whether similar patterns could be observed in human autopsy cases. We selected RNAs abundantly expressed in human skin and which are likely to correlate with the PMI and quantified their expression levels using qPCR, although estimation of the PMI in humans is highly challenging. This may be due to variations in environmental temperatures of the deceased, and the thanatotranscriptome may not be completely halted even under cold conditions, whereas the present human study focused on the pre-refrigeration interval. Nevertheless, several RNAs exhibited time-dependent changes in expression levels, particularly when a short pre-refrigeration interval was obtained, especially for EGR1, FOS, and JUNB. This trend was also confirmed in additional mouse experiments extending the postmortem interval up to 72 h, in which Egr1 and Fos apparently demonstrated characteristic expression dynamics (Figs. 2 and 4). These genes are known to be associated with ischemia and cell death, suggesting that the ischemic insult triggered by organismal death and the subsequent progression toward cellular demise may drive their coordinated postmortem expression changes.

EGR1 showed a statistically significant correlation with the pre-refrigeration interval in the early postmortem period within 60, 48, and 36 h (Supplementary Figs. 3–5). EGR1 is a transcriptional regulator that plays an important role in growth factor signaling and ischemic stress responses [32–34]. It has been implicated in the regulation of genes, such as TP53 and TGFB1, thereby contributing to tumor-suppressive pathways, as well as in the modulation of pro-inflammatory mediators, including IL1B and various chemokines [34, 35]. Through these regulatory activities, EGR1 is thought to participate in coordinating cellular responses to ischemia and hypoxia, including processes associated with tissue injury. In the present study, the transient increase in the expression of EGR1 observed after organismal death, followed by a subsequent decline, may reflect the activation and gradual attenuation of these stress-responsive intracellular pathways during postmortem cellular degeneration.

In contrast, FOS and JUNB form heterodimers that constitute the AP-1 transcription factor complex [36]. AP-1 has been implicated in the regulation of stress responses, inflammation, and the expression of apoptosis-related genes [37]. In certain contexts, MAPK-mediated activation of AP-1 has been shown to modulate the transcription of caspases, thereby contributing to the progression of apoptosis [38]. Taken together, the observation that FOS and JUNB expression levels changed over time following organismal death, exhibiting characteristic temporal patterns during the progression toward cellular demise, is highly consistent with the molecular mechanisms described above. Notably, the FOS expression exhibited a transient increase, followed by a decline, whereas the JUNB expression was best approximated by a convex quadratic pattern over the 72-h observation period in this study (Fig. 3). These differences raise several possibilities, including differential transcriptional kinetics, distinct protein stability and turnover rates, or participation in regulatory pathways independent of AP-1 activity. Further investigation is necessary to elucidate the precise molecular mechanisms underlying these divergent temporal dynamics in postmortem tissue.

In addition to EGR1 and FOS, several other transcripts demonstrated early upregulation, followed by subsequent downregulation, in the longer time-course experiment in mice. This pattern suggests a transient postmortem activation, similar to that observed for EGR1 and FOS. However, mt-Nd3 exhibited a constant increase in its expression throughout the 72-h postmortem observation period in mice (Fig. 4). Although a decline was not observed within this timeframe, considering that all other genes showed convex expression patterns, it would be estimated that MTND3 would eventually transition to a decrease beyond the examined interval. Therefore, MTND3 could serve as a potential biomarker for estimating more advanced PMIs, even after other early-responsive transcripts have declined. This requires further evaluation in future studies.

Temporal alterations in the RNA expression during the progression from ischemia to cell death may be informative not only for PMI estimation but also for assessing vital reactions to injury and estimating the timing of trauma. Based on this hypothesis, we conducted spatial transcriptomic analyses of injured regions in an autopsy case involving trauma-related deaths. In the evaluation of injuries within the same individual, inter-individual variability, such as temperature differences, would be eliminated, and therefore, a more accurate assessment may be achievable. Although the present data are a preliminary proof-of-concept based on a single case, spatial transcriptome analyses demonstrated that the FOSL1 expression was increased exclusively in the epidermis proximal to antemortem injury. Notably, no increase in the expression of FOSL1 was observed in postmortem-injured skin, suggesting that the upregulation of FOSL1 reflects an antemortem vital reaction rather than a postmortem artifact. Similar to FOS, FOSL1 interacts with JUNB to form AP-1 transcriptional complexes [39]. Given the established role of AP-1 in cellular responses to ischemia, stress, and injury, the observed increase in the FOSL1 expression in the spatial transcriptome analysis may be biologically plausible. Specifically, the altered expression dynamics of FOSL1 may reflect cellular responses during the progression toward ischemia-induced degeneration or apoptosis following injury. Furthermore, recent studies employing single-cell RNA sequencing and spatial transcriptomics have demonstrated that FOSL1 plays a critical role in regulating cellular migration in basal and suprabasal keratinocytes at the wound edge during human skin repair [40], consistent with the present findings. Furthermore, our data indicated that such transcriptional activation remained detectable in antemortem-injured skin even a pre-refrigeration interval of > 24 h, whereas it was not observed in postmortem-injured skin. This distinction suggests the potential utility of FOSL1-associated expression dynamics for discriminating antemortem from postmortem injury. Further examination with a larger number of cases is needed to validate these findings.

In the dermis, spatial transcriptomic analyses also identified RNAs that were upregulated exclusively in regions proximal to antemortem injury. In addition, a distinct subset of RNAs exhibited increased expression levels specifically in regions distal to the injury site. These alterations were observed exclusively in antemortem injury samples and, similar to the FOSL1 expression in the epidermis, may have potential utility in distinguishing antemortem from postmortem wounds. COL1A1 and COL3A1, which exhibited increased expression levels predominantly in regions located slightly away from the immediate wound margin, encode type I and type III collagen, respectively [41]. These collagens represent the principal structural components of the dermal extracellular matrix and are primarily produced by dermal fibroblasts. A recent single-cell RNA-seq study of injured skin has suggested that fibroblasts may undergo differentiation as they proliferate from the outer wound region toward the inner wound area [42]. Although the present study captures only a single time point and therefore does not allow direct assessment of temporal progression, our observation that the expression levels of COL1A1 and COL3A1 were elevated in regions spatially distal to the wound edge may reflect an early proliferative or matrix-producing fibroblast population that subsequently contributes to wound remodeling. Taken together with recent findings, these results may provide additional insight into the spatial dynamics of fibroblast activation during skin repair.

As demonstrated above, we identified RNAs in both the epidermis and dermis that exhibited significant alterations in expression dynamics following antemortem injury, considering their spatial proximity to the wound site. Moreover, these findings may contribute to a better understanding of the mechanisms involved in skin wound repair. Because the present spatial transcriptomic data are derived from a single case, future studies should focus on acquiring well-characterized samples from multiple cases to assess inter-individual variability, and should examine the temporal relationship between injury and spatial gene expression patterns across a range of wound ages. Additional investigations should also address the potential influence of sampling sites, injury types including sharp-force injuries, blunt-force injuries, gunshot wounds and ligature marks, and the impact of cardiopulmonary resuscitation on the observed expression dynamics. Elucidation of the molecular foundation of the postmortem skin transcriptome would substantially contribute to the better assessment of the PMI and wound age estimation in forensic scenes in the future.
