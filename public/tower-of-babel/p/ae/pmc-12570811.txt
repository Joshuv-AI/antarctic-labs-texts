The PTP4A3 inhibitor KVX-053 reduces Severe Acute Respiratory Syndrome Coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) virulence, inflammation, and development of acute lung injury in K18-hACE2 mice
Authors: Ruben M L Colunga-Biancatelli, Pavel A Solopov, Caitlin M Woodson, Irving Coy Allen, Ivan Akhrymuk, Maryna Akhrymuk, Brittany N Heath, Hannah M Ivester, Tierney Day, Dan E Austin, Kylene Kehn-Hall, John S Lazo, Elizabeth R Sharlow, John D Catravas
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSevere Acute Respiratory Syndrome Coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) has caused a global health crisis, marked by high transmissibility and virulence. Despite widespread vaccination efforts, effective treatments for COVID-19, particularly for severe cases leading to Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome (ARDS), remain limited. This study investigates the efficacy of KVX-053, a protein tyrosine phosphatase type IVA (PTP4A3) small molecule inhibitor, in modulating SARS-CoV-2-induced inflammation and lung injury using in vitro cell models and in vivo K18-hACE2 transgenic mice. KVX-053 reduced in vitro pro-inflammatory cytokine expression, including TNFα, CXCL10, and CXCL11, without impacting viral replication or cell viability. K18-hACE2 mice treated with KVX-053 demonstrated marked improvement in clinical scores and reduced histological evidence of lung injury compared to untreated SARS-CoV-2-infected controls. KVX-053 mitigated the activation of key inflammatory mediators in the lung, including NLRP3 inflammasomes, IL-6, and phosphorylated STAT3, effectively curbing the “cytokine storm” associated with severe COVID-19. Importantly, treatment preserved lung parenchymal integrity, reduced edema, and minimized macrophage infiltration. Our findings highlight PTP4A3 as a potential critical regulator of the inflammatory response during SARS-CoV-2 infection. KVX-053, a potent and selective PTP4A3 inhibitor, emerges as a promising host-directed therapeutic strategy for mitigating ARDS and inflammation-driven lung injury in SARS-CoV-2 and potentially other respiratory viral infections. Future studies are required to optimize dosing strategies, elucidate precise molecular mechanisms, and validate these findings in clinical settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12931-025-03349-9.

Background

The COVID-19 pandemic, caused by Severe Acute Respiratory Syndrome Coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2), has placed an unprecedented burden on global healthcare systems, leading to over 676 million infections and nearly 7 million deaths worldwide [1]. Unlike most viruses that evolve towards reduced virulence to maintain host integrity, SARS-CoV-2 has retained high transmissibility and pathogenicity, a mechanism initially attributed to potential maladaptation mechanisms of the virus to the human host, contributing to severe disease outcomes [2, 3]. Only the application of broad vaccination strategies along with the evolution towards Omicron (B.1.1.529) or other milder variants, have resulted in more benign respiratory infections and lung injuries, allowing the cessation of curfews and lockdown protocols.

Additionally, despite the advent of SARS-CoV-2 targeted vaccines, the treatment of SARS-CoV-2-related disease (COVID-19) is based on limited therapeutic interventions with sparse beneficial effects. Hospitalized patients may receive treatments based on short courses of the corticosteroid, dexamethasone [4], the antiviral, remdesivir, the immunomodulators, tocilizumab, sarilumab [5], or baricitinib, the JAK inhibitor, tofacitinib [6–8], chloroquine or hydroxychloroquine [9], convalescent plasma, azithromycin [10] and the protease inhibitors, lopinavir and ritonavir [11] despite lack of beneficial effects in randomized clinical trials.

The epidemics of SARS in 2003 [12], Middle Eastern respiratory syndrome (MERS) in 2017 [13] and SARS-CoV-2 pandemic in 2020 [14] have historically posed significant global threats, necessitating continued research into their pathophysiology and the development of innovative therapeutic approaches. Therapeutic interventions targeting viral replication have shown limited efficacy due to the complex and elusive mechanisms of viral pathogenesis. As an alternative, host-directed therapies that focus on modulating the host response—mitigating inflammation, and preventing tissue injury—offer promising potential for addressing underlying disease mechanisms. Optimal host-directed therapies for SARS-CoV-2 are those that can be repurposed to new coronaviruses, with faster response times in future pandemics. SARS-CoV-2 infection can result in a spectrum of clinical manifestations, ranging from mild cold-like symptoms to severe conditions such as Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome (ARDS), with associated mortality rates of up to 50% in critically ill patients [15].

COVID-19 patients display a strong inflammatory response guided by IL-1β, IL-6, IL-8 and TNFα with elevated concentrations of D-dimer, ferritin and C-reactive protein [16]. This inflammatory milieu leads to diffuse alveolar damage, fluid exudation, and interstitial fibrosis, hallmarks of severe lung injury in COVID-19 [17]. COVID-19 patients display an aberrant pulmonary infiltration of monocyte-derived macrophages [18]. In addition, the RNA of SARS-CoV-2 has been found in monocytes and macrophages [19], a mechanism implicated in the prolonged systemic symptoms characteristic of “long COVID” [20]. The direct involvement of macrophages further amplifies the production of cytokines triggering NLRP3 and NLRP1 inflammasome-mediated pyroptosis [21]. Single-cell sequencing studies of COVID-19 patients nasopharyngeal mucosa revealed that macrophages expressed CCL2, CCL3, CXCL10, and CXCL8 and in severe cases guided the infiltration of neutrophils in the lungs [22].

We have previously investigated the effects of exposing lungs of K18-hACE2 mice to the SARS-CoV-2 Subunit 1 of the Spike Protein (S1SP), discovering for the first time that the S1SP, differently from the non-cleaved spike protein, is able to provoke endothelial hyper-permeability and acute lung injury (ALI), and acts like a toxin released systemically during host infection [23]. Furthermore, we demonstrated that a protein tyrosine phosphatase type IVA (PTP4A3) small molecule inhibitor reduced the S1SP-mediated ALI, by blocking the expression of PTP4A-sensitive NLRP3 assembly, IKBα and STAT3 activation (phosphorylation) and the consequent “cytokine storm [24, 25].” In a recent viewpoint, Drs. Montuschi and Adcock highlighted the potential for PTP inhibitors in ALI and stressed the importance of additional studies on tolerability, selectivity, and safety [26]. Our findings strengthened the hypothesis that the release of the S1SP is a viral mechanism to provoke inflammation and injury to surrounding tissues. Indeed, PTP4A3 is strategically positioned at the plasma membrane were it modulates the intracellular signaling of external stimuli [27]. PTP4A3 binds Integrin β receptors, removes phosphate groups from p38 and MAPK proteins, influences GTPases (RhoA, ROCK1) and importantly is involved in the modulation of STAT proteins and NF-κB activation [28–30]. Despite the crucial role of phosphatases in cellular inflammation, the lack of pharmaceutical agents able to provide a strong but reversible inhibition of PTPs has limited the preclinical investigation on these class of drugs and its cellular effects during disease. In the current manuscript we have extended our findings, by employing a humanized mouse model (K18-hACE2) of live SARS-CoV-2 infection and explored the effectiveness of the PTP4A inhibitor, KVX-053, to reduce SARS-CoV-2 -induced lung injury.

Methods

Lung study summary

The Single Cell portal of the Broad Institute (https://singlecell.broadinstitute.org/single_cell accessed on 11 November 2024) was interrogated for single cell RNA sequencing data. A search was performed for PTP4A3 expression in the lungs of patients with COVID-19 infection, returning two studies. Both of them presented data in postmortem human lungs from non-COVID-19 (control) and COVID-19 patients [18, 31]. In the first study, the authors shared 116,314 snRNA-seq profiles across 20 frozen lungs obtained from 19 COVID-19 decedents and seven control patients with short postmortem interval (PMI) autopsies. The COVID-19 cohort comprised seven female and 12 male decedents, including 13 patients of Hispanic ethnicity, with an age range from 58 to > 89 years who had acquired SARS-CoV-2 infection and succumbed to the disease. The average time from symptom onset to death was 27.5 days (range, 4–63 days). After rapid autopsy with a median PMI of 4 h (range 2–9 h) collected tissues were either flash-frozen or frozen following OCT (optimal cutting temperature) embedment and subjected to snRNA-seq using a droplet-based platform (10x Genomics). All included patients had underlying hypertensive disorder and frequently one or more additional co-morbidities associated with increased risk for severe COVID-19. Single-cell sequencing data was demultiplexed into FASTQ files and aligned to a custom-built joint human and SARS-CoV-2 genome using Cell Ranger (v.5.0.0). CellBender (v.0.2.0) was used to remove technical artifacts and ambient RNA counts from the gene expression matrices. The resulting individual count matrices underwent quality control and were integrated using Seurat (v.3.2.3). Different cell types were identified iteratively on three levels of granularity (main, intermediate, and fine) using a three-pronged approach for cell type identification: unbiased identification of cluster markers, discovery of cell types using signatures from reported atlases, and manual curation to sub-stratify cell populations and cell states that may be unique to COVID-19 using expert knowledge [18].

The second study, published simultaneously [31] (Supplemental Fig. 1), displayed an atlas of 106,792 single cells and nuclei collected from the lungs of 16 SARS-CoV-2 infected COVID-19 autopsy donors (23 total samples), ranging from > 30 to > 80 years of age. The autopsies were performed across three hospitals in the Boston area. Sequencing data was demultiplexed into FASTQ files, aligned to a custom-built joint human and SARS-CoV-2 genome using the Cumulus cellranger_workflow, and ambient RNA was removed using CellBender. These individual count matrices underwent quality control using the Cumulus single cell analysis workflow, and then they were pooled together and batch corrected using Harmony-PyTorch. Draft cell type assignments were made using both an automatic prediction approach and manual curation of marker genes and gene signatures.

Cell culture

Human lung epithelial cells (Calu-3) were acquired from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, HTB-55). Calu-3 cells were maintained in 50% Ham’s-F12 medium and 50% Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 1% L-glutamine, 1% penicillin/streptomycin, 1% sodium pyruvate and 1% nonessential amino acids. Vero (ATCC, CCL-81) cells were maintained in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS, 1% L-glutamine, and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. All cells were maintained at 37 °C with 5% CO2.

In-house harvested human lung microvascular endothelial cells (HLMVEC) were maintained in M199 media supplemented with 20% FBS andantibiotics/antimycotics as described previously [33]. To assess possible changes in viability following PTP4A3 inhibition, HLMVEC were incubated with different concentrations of KVX-053 (5, 10, 20 or 50 µM). After 24 h, cells were resuspended, Trypan Blue-treated, and counted by hemocytometer. Experiments were run in triplicates, and data are reported as fold of control (vehicle).

Viral plaque assay

Crystal violet plaque assays were performed to determine viral titers from supernatants collected from in vitro experiments and supernatants collected from homogenized lung tissue from in vivo experiments. Infected supernatants were serially diluted in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) with 1% FBS and added to confluent 6-well plates of Vero cells. Cells were incubated with serially diluted viral inoculum at 37 °C for 1 h, with rocking every 15 min. After infection, 2 mL of a 1:1:1 mixture of supplemented Minimum Essential Eagle Medium (MEM), supplemented DMEM, and 1.5% agarose in deionized water (diH2O) was added to each well. Plates were fixed with 10% formaldehyde in diH2O after 48 h. Cells were stained using 1% Crystal violet in 20% ethanol and diH2O. Viral titers were determined as previously described [34].

PTP4A inhibitor treatment

The PTP4A inhibitor, KVX-053, was tested for cellular cytotoxicity in Calu-3 cells to determine nontoxic concentrations. For cellular viability experiments, Calu-3 cells were seeded at 2 × 104 cells/well in 96-well, white-walled plates (Corning, Cat# 3903) and allowed to attach for 24 h. Next, KVX-053, over a range of concentrations, was added to the cells. After 24 and 48 h of exposure, ATP production was quantified as a measure of cell viability using Cell Titer-Glo assay (Cat# G7570, Promega, Madison, WI, USA). Nontoxic concentrations of inhibitors were identified and utilized in subsequent inhibitor-treatment experiments. To assess the effects of KVX-053 on chemokine/cytokine induction and SARS-CoV-2 infection/replication, we seeded Calu-3 cells into 12-well cell culture plates at 5 × 105 cells/well. Following attachment, cells were pretreated for 1 h with KVX-053. After pretreatment, cells were infected with SARS-CoV-2 (MOI 1) for 1 h. Following initial infection, cells were washed and were post-treated with the same inhibitor. Cell lysates were collected at 48 dpi and subjected to RNA extraction and RT-qPCR analysis.

For animal studies, KVX-053 was dissolved in 30% Captisol, 40% PEG400, 30% PBS and intraperitoneally (IP) injected at the dose of 10 mg/kg once every 24 h, while mice in the SARS-CoV-2 + vehicle group received 100µL of 30% Captisol, 40% PEG400, 30% PBS injected IP every 24 h.

Clinical scoring

Starting one day before infection with SARS-CoV-2 and during the entire experimental period, mice were monitored for changes in their clinical status. We employed a scoring system using different criteria to address a mouse’s health such as appearance, mobility, attitude, and body condition. For each criterion, a value between 0 (best) and 3 (worst) was given and summed to the others. Mice that had a comprehensive score below 5 were monitored daily, mice that scored between 6 and 10 were monitored twice a day, while mice with scores higher than 11 or that had the highest score in any criteria were recommended for euthanasia, as shown in Table 1 below.

Histopathology

At the end of the experimental period, animals were euthanized, a small transverse incision was made in the middle of the trachea, and lungs were instilled and inflated with 10% formaldehyde solution to a pressure of 15 cm H2O through a 20 G catheter. The trachea was then ligated, and lungs were removed from the thorax and placed in 10% formaldehyde solution for 72 h before processing. Mid-transverse slices were made from the formalin-fixed lungs and were embedded in paraffin. Sections (5 μm thick) were prepared from the blocks and stained with H&E. Following the 2011 ATS report [35], ten randomly selected fields from each slide were examined for signs of acute lung injury such as the (1) presence of neutrophils in the alveolar or interstitial spaces, (2) hyaline membranes, (3) proteinaceous debris filling the airspaces and (4) alveolar septal thickening. Lung sections were examined under 10× and 20× magnification and each parameter scored either 0, 1 or 2 depending on the severity of injury. Weighted parameters were then averaged for number of fields and plotted as previously described [23]. The observer was blinded to the treatment.

Tissue collection

Immediately after euthanasia, the chest was opened, blood was aspirated from the heart through the right ventricle, and the pulmonary circulation was flushed out with sterile PBS containing EDTA injected into the right ventricle. Lungs were dissected from the thorax, snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and kept at − 80 °C for subsequent processing and analysis.

Western blotting

Proteins in lung tissue homogenates were extracted from frozen lungs by sonication (50% amplitude, three times for 10 s) in ice-cold RIPA buffer with added protease inhibitor cocktail (100:1). Protein lysates were gently mixed for 3 h at 4 °C, centrifuged twice at 14,000× g for 10 min. Equal amounts of protein, were mixed with Tricine Sample Buffer 1:1, boiled for 5 min, and then separated on a 10–12% polyacrylamide SDS gel by electrophoresis, transferred to a nitrocellulose membrane, incubated with the appropriate primary antibody, followed by incubation with the secondary antibody, and then, detected by digital fluorescence imaging (LI-COR Odyssey CLx, Dallas, TX, USA). Beta-actin was used as the loading control. ImageJ software v.1.8.0 was used to perform densitometric quantification of the bands (http://imagej.nih.gov/ij; National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA, accessed on 10 September 2024). Membranes were stripped in a stripping buffer for 20 min, blocked, and incubated with other primary and secondary antibodies.

RNA extraction and Real-Time PCR (qPCR)

Calu-3 cells cultures were exposed to different KVX-053 concentration (0.16 to 10µM) with or without SARS-CoV2 infection and at the end of the experiments cells were collected in TRIzol™ LS reagent (ThermoFisher Scientific) and subjected to RNA extraction. Following extraction, RNA samples were subjected to RT-qPCR using the TaqMan™ RNA-to-CT™ 1-Step Kit by ThermoFisher Scientific. TaqMan Gene Expression Assays were used for host-gene expression analysis: CXCL10 (Hs00171042_m1), CXCL9 (Hs00171065_m1), TNFα (Hs00174128_m1), CXCL11 (Hs00171138_m1), 18 S rRNA (pan-eukaryotic). Fold change was calculated relative to 18 S and normalized to samples from uninfected Calu-3 cells using the ΔΔCt method.

For animal studies, lung tissue was homogenized in TRIzol®, followed by a cleaning step using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Thermofisher Scientific). The purified RNA was transcribed into cDNA using the SuperScriptTM IV VILO Reverse transcriptase Kit and was analyzed by Real-time qPCR with SYBR Green Master Mix on a StepOne Plus Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems v.2.3). The results were evaluated using the standard curve method and were expressed as the fold of the control values, normalized to housekeeping gene β-actin. Primer pairs and standard for IL-6 (qMmuCID0005613) and TNFα (qMmuCED0004141) were purchased from BioRad (Bio-Rad, CA, USA) and qPCR conditions were applied according to the manufacturer instructions.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed with GraphPad Prism Software (version 10.4, GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA, accessed on 15 July 2025) and significance was set for p < 0.05 determined by either 1- or 2-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by appropriate post hoc test (Tukey’s, Bonferroni’s, or Brown-Forsythe test).

Results

PTP4A3 is overexpressed in the lungs of patients with COVID-19

To confirm that PTP4A3 is involved in COVID-19 disease, we searched the Single Cell Portal of the Broad Institute. We accessed the single cell sequencing (snRNA-seq) data for PTP4A3 expression in the lungs of 19 decedents COVID-19 patients (7 females and 12 males) and compared it to 7 healthy controls [18]. In the figure below, we initially divided the Atlas for main cell types (Fig. 1A), and then we focused our attention on the relative pulmonary expression of PTP4A3 in individuals with COVID-19 across cell types compared to control samples. As shown, in Fig. 1B, lethal COVID-19 is guided by the expression of PTP4A3 mostly in alveolar epithelial cells, endothelial cells and lymphocytes type B cells. Further quantification of individual cell expression indicates that B cells, endothelial cells, and epithelial cells express the highest levels of PTP4A3 genes during acute infection of SARS-CoV-2 (Fig. 1C). Similar results were also obtained searching PTP4A3 expression in a second, independent, sc-RNA atlas [31] (Supplemental Fig. 1). Collectively, these results indicate PTP4A3 is overexpressed in a subpopulation of pulmonary cells from COVID-19 patients, most notably endothelial and epithelial cells.

KVX-053 blocks SARS-CoV-2 -induced cytokine release, in vitro, without affecting viral titer

To determine the impact of KVX-053 on SARS-CoV-2 replication and inflammation, we next used human Calu-3 lung epithelial cells as an in vitro model system. Calu-3 cells were infected with SARS-CoV-2 (MOI 1) and cytokine expression assessed 48 h post-infection. SARS-CoV-2 significantly increased the expression of CXCL10, TNFα, CXCL11, and CXCL9 (Fig. 2A–D). Compared to cells infected with SARS-CoV-2 and treated with the vehicle control (1% DMSO), 10 µM KVX-053 abolished the SARS-CoV-2-induced expression of CXCL10, TNFα, CXCL11 but not of CXCL9. As a positive control, the antiviral remdesivir (10 µM) also reduced cytokine expression in SARS-CoV-2 infected cells. The effect of KVX-053 was not significantly different from that of remdesivir for CXCL10 and TNFα (Fig. 2A and B). Importantly, treatment with KVX-053 for 48 h did not affect viral titers of SARS-CoV-2 confirming the hypothesis that, unlike remdesivir, KVX-053 acts on the host-expressed (Calu-3) PTP4A3 and not on the virus (Fig. 2E). Concentrations of KVX-053 up to 10 µM were well tolerated in Calu-3 cells over 48 h (Fig. 2F). We also previously reported that KVX-053 was not toxic to human epithelial cells in culture up to the highest concentrations tested: 25µM [36]. Similarly, we observed no toxicity with 10 µM KVX_053 with Human Lung Microvascular Endothelial cells (HLMVEC), and only slight toxicity with 20 and 50µM, respectively (Fig. 2G). Taken together this data suggests that the reduced cytokinemia was not related to KVX-053 impact on host viability.

Effects of PTP4A inhibition on SARS-CoV-2 clinical progression

Mice infected with SARS-CoV-2 and treated with KVX-053 10 mg/kg IP/24 h or vehicle (100µL of 30% Captisol, 40% PEG400, 30% PBS) were monitored for signs of distress and weight changes. Mice infected with SARS-CoV-2 started losing weight at 4 dpi and at 5 dpi we observed a 15% reduction in body weight. There was no significant weight difference observed between mice infected with SARS-CoV-2 treated with KVX-053 or vehicle during the experimental period (Fig. 3A). Similarly, mice infected with SARS-CoV-2 displayed a dramatic worsening in clinical condition around 4 dpi and 5 dpi, compared to pre-exposure weight (p < 0.0001). The group of mice infected with SARS-CoV-2 and receiving 10 mg/kg KVX-053 however, showed a better clinical aspect at 4 dpi, which became significant at 5 dpi, as quantified by the Clinical Scoring System (Fig. 3B).

KVX-053 ameliorates SARS-CoV-2-induced lung injury

Mice infected with SARS-CoV-2 (1 × 105 PFU) intranasally were treated with vehicle or KVX-053 at a concentration of 10 mg/kg administered IP. Compared to uninfected mice (Fig. 4A), SARS-CoV-2 provoked the development of areas of parenchymal compaction, infiltration of mononuclear cells, and edema as early as at 3 days post infection (Fig. 4B). At 5 days, a further increase in alveolar cellularity, edema and cluster areas of macrophagic activity was observed (Fig. 4C). Treatment with the PTP4A inhibitor, KVX-053, reduced macrophagic infiltration, and maintained parenchymal integrity both at days 3 and 5 (Fig. 4D and E). We also quantified the SARS-CoV-2-mediated injury via the Lung Injury Score: SARS-CoV-2 provoked a significant increase compared to control at days 3 that worsened on day 5. KVX-053-treated mice showed a reduction in the Lung Injury Score, that became significant at day 5 (Fig. 4 bottom-left corner).

KVX-053 reduces SARS-CoV-2 -induced lung inflammation

We analyzed the lungs of mice uninfected and infected with SARS-CoV-2 and treated with vehicle or with the PTP4A inhibitor KVX-053 10 mg/kg IP. Tissues harvested at 3 or 5 dpi were analyzed by western blot analysis or RT-PCR. SARS-CoV-2 provoked an early activation of the inflammasome NLRP3 with increased transcription of IL-6 and TNFα already at 3 dpi (Fig. 5A, C, D). At 5 dpi, the overall levels of the inflammasome had returned to control levels, but an increase in the activation (phosphorylation) of STAT3, with persistent IL-6 increased transcription was observed (Fig. 5E, F, G). Mice infected with SARS-CoV-2 and treated with KVX-053 showed a lack of initial NLRP3 activation, complete blockage of the IL-6 pathway at 3 dpi with consequent lack of STAT3 phosphorylation at 5 dpi (Fig. 5A, C, F). We did not observe a reproducible change in CLCX10 expression. Additional studies should be conducted to measure CLCX9 and CLCX11.

Discussion

Various animal models have been utilized to investigate the pathophysiology of SARS-CoV-2-related disease [37]. Among them, the K18-hACE2 mouse model, which expresses the human ACE2 receptor under the keratin-18 promoter, is particularly valuable, by enabling epithelial cells, including alveolar cells, to be infected by SARS-CoV-2, making it a widely used platform for preclinical research [38]. In this study, K18-hACE2 mice infected with 1 × 105 PFU of SARS-CoV-2 exhibited a marked clinical deterioration by day 4, reaching peak severity by day 5 (Fig. 3). Analysis of lung sections showed edema, inflammation, diffuse alveolar damage and clusters of macrophage activation (Fig. 4).

Protein Tyrosine Phosphatase Type IVA (PTP4A3) is anchored to the inner leaflet of the plasma membrane of cells and modulates the intracellular signaling of a large number of stimuli [27, 39]. PTP4A3 functions as a dual specificity phosphatase and, removes phosphate groups from target tyrosine, serine and threonine residues of various molecules; thus blocking PTP4A3 function represents a novel single targeted but multi-signaling modulatory strategy [40]. scRNA sequencing data indicates increased expression of PTP4A3 in alveolar epithelial, endothelial and B cells of patients with lethal COVID-19 (Fig. 1) [18]. Protein Tyrosine Phosphatase Type IVA (PTP4A), also known as proteins of regenerating liver (PRLs), is a subfamily of PTPs that includes three proteins (PTP4A1, PTP4A2, PTP4A3) [41]. PTP4A3 participates in inflammatory responses by activating both STAT3 [42] and NF-kB [43]. It also participates in the pathologic proliferation of vasculature in response to VEGF [44] via MAPK [45], and its inhibition restores LPS-induced endothelial permeability [36]. PTP4A3 modulates the function of a STAT3 modulator [42, 46], and mediates p38 dephosphorylation, modulating ERK signaling [47]. PTP4A3 dephosphorylated PI3K, as a part of a negative loop involved in integrins signaling [48] (Fig. 6). Many of the intracellular signaling pathways found in Fig. 6 are vital for maintaining pulmonary microvascular integrity. Because of earlier work with PTP4A inhibitors in cancer, and reports that PTP4A3 null cell lines result in a strong anti-inflammatory profile [28], PTP4A3 inhibitors are now investigated as potential, novel anti-inflammatory agents [49]. In addition, as showed by us and others, PTP4A3 expression selectively increases during stress conditions [24, 50], suggesting that PTP4A inhibitors may have limited side effects in non-inflamed tissues.

Schematic representation of PTP4A3 proposed involvement in the intracellular signaling of stress receptors. PTP4A3 promotes Inflammation, Cytokine production, Cytoskeletal remodeling, angiogenesis and cellular migration via deactivation (dephosphorylation) of multiple proteins involved in double negative feedback loop. (1) PTP4A3 dephosphorylates PI3K, a cascade factor but also an activator of ARAP3 an inhibitor of Integrin receptor activation; (2) PTP4A3 dephosphorylates SHP-2, a STAT3 modulator; (3)_PTP4A3 dephosphorylates RAP1 which in turn blocks NF-κB nuclear translocation; (4) PTP4A3 dephosphorylates p38, which can imped ERK signaling via inhibition of Ras kinase

In the current manuscript, we have employed KVX-053, also known as JMS-053, a novel allosteric and reversible inhibitor of PTP4A3 [51, 52]. KVX-053 is the most potent known PTP4A3 inhibitor with an IC50 of 25 nM and a long half-life of ~ 24 h when injected IP [53]. KVX-053 exerts its effects at nontoxic concentrations as previously shown in endothelial cells [36] and here in Calu-3 cells. Our in vitro studies, indicate that KVX-053 was able to modulate inflammation by reducing the expression of the pro-inflammatory cytokine TNFα and of the chemokines CXCL10 and CXCL11 (Fig. 2). The intracellular signaling pathways depicted in Fig. 6 that are regulated by PTP4A3 were blocked by KVX-053. Importantly, this effect was not mediated by a reduction of viral replication or decrease in viral titers, suggesting that inhibition of host PTP4A reduced the cellular damage following normal viral cycles and replication. Mice receiving KVX-053 showed mild improvements in clinical scores that became significant at day 5 post-infection (Fig. 3). Additionally, inhibition of PTP4A reduced the overall levels of the inflammasome NLRP3, suggesting again that this phosphatase may be involved in the inflammatory response following immune cell recruitment. KVX-053 was further able to block the transcription of IL-6, TNFα and phospho-STAT3 in vivo, suggesting that PTP4A3 signaling in the host response to infection, and its modulation may serve as mechanisms involved with reduced inflammation and injury. Indeed, lung sections of mice infected with SARS-CoV-2 and treated with the PTP4A inhibitor KVX-053 showed reduced alveolar edema, lung injury score and infiltration of interstitial macrophages (Fig. 4).

A limitation of this work is that it only employed a single dose of SARS-CoV-2 and of KVX-053 (10 mg/kg IP). Additional studies are needed, including dose-response, to investigate potentially higher beneficial effects. Although, due to the complexity of working with BSL-3 pathogens, we did not perform studies to elucidate precise pathways related to the reduction of SARS-CoV-2 induced inflammation in cells and tissues, previous work suggests that PTP4A3 may be fundamental for immune cell chemotaxis during inflammation, thus supporting the lower macrophage clusters observed in lung sections [54]. Importantly, by regulating STAT3 activation, PTP4A3 may prevent the transcription of inflammatory cytokines, such as IL-6, and TNFα, which are critically involved in SARS-CoV-2 pathogenesis (Fig. 5). We previously demonstrated that KVX-053 mitigates inflammation, barrier dysfunction and lung tissue injury induced by the SARS-CoV-2 spike protein subunit 1 (S1SP) [55]. S1SP elicits an inflammatory response comparable to that of the live SARS-CoV-2 virus, characterized by IL-6 and TNFα-driven inflammation, along with increased phosphorylation of STAT3 and NF-κB. Notably, S1SP promotes greater neutrophil infiltration, whereas the infectious virus primarily induces interstitial and alveolar mononuclear recruitment. Importantly, the PTP4A3 inhibitor effectively inhibited the activation of both STAT3 and NF-κB, leading to reduced circulating cytokine levels and diminished lung injury, regardless of whether the injury was triggered by spike proteins or the intact virus. Similarly, the intravenous administration of of a PTP4A3 monoclonal antibody (PRL3-zumab) was able to prevent VEGF signaling, vascular barrier dysfunction, and immune cell recruitment in a mouse model of neovascular eye disease [56]. These findings underscore the protective effects of PTP4A3 inhibition in reducing lung epithelial and endothelial inflammation, vascular permeability, and immune cell infiltration, demonstrating its potential as a cause-agnostic therapeutic strategy. Further investigations are also needed to explore the therapeutic potential of PTP4A3 inhibition in other models of acute lung injury and ARDS, thereby enhancing our understanding of PTP4A3’s role in pulmonary inflammation and its broader applications in the post-pandemic era.

Conclusion

Inhibition of PTP4A3 may represent a novel host-targeted strategy to combat pulmonary inflammation, ALI and ARDS provoked by coronaviruses or other noxious agents.
