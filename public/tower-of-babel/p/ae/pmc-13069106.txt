Authors: Srishti Agarwal, Devki Nandan Gupta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
We demonstrate the generation of high-energy \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\gamma$$\end{document}-photons and pair electrons resulting from the interaction of an ultrahigh-intensity laser pulse with a near-critical density plasma target. Through polarization-resolved analysis and three-dimensional particle-in-cell simulations, we investigate how linearly and circularly polarized laser pulses differentially influence photon emission spectra, angular distributions, and pair-production. In our configuration, circular polarization yields higher-energy \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\gamma$$\end{document}-photons, attributed to smoother electron trajectories and sustained interaction with the laser field. These \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\gamma$$\end{document}-photons subsequently trigger the production of Breit–Wheeler pair electrons, whose efficiency is strongly dependent on both the laser intensity and the polarization state. This study highlights the potential of polarization shaping as a control tool for optimizing gamma-ray sources and QED pair plasma generation in next-generation ultraintense laser facilities.

Introduction

The emergence of petawatt and multi-petawatt laser systems has opened a new frontier in high-field physics, enabling the experimental study of strong-field quantum electrodynamics (SF-QED) phenomena in extreme laser-matter interaction regimes1–7. When laser intensities exceed \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$10^{22}~\mathrm {W/cm^2}$$\end{document}, the electromagnetic fields experienced by electrons become strong enough to induce nonlinear QED processes8–10. In particular, two cornerstone phenomena dominate: nonlinear Compton scattering, where relativistic electrons emit high-energy gamma photons in strong laser fields, and nonlinear multiphoton Breit–Wheeler (BW) pair production, in which these gamma photons interact with the same or a secondary field to create electron-positron pairs11–14.

Understanding and controlling these processes is critical for multiple reasons. First, they represent essential tests of strong-field QED theory, including radiation reaction, quantum recoil, and stochastic photon emission. Second, these processes are key to generate ultrabright gamma-ray beams and dense electron–positron plasmas15, with applications in nuclear photonics, laboratory astrophysics, and even next-generation particle colliders based on laser-driven sources16–18. Several studies19,20 have shown that in the so-called radiation-dominated regime, where radiative losses significantly affect electron dynamics, emissions become highly nonlinear and quantum in nature. A central parameter governing these interactions is the quantum nonlinearity parameter, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\chi _e = \frac{e \hbar }{m^3 c^4} \sqrt{-(F_{\mu \nu }p^\nu )^2} \sim \gamma _e E/E_S$$\end{document}, which characterizes the strength of the quantum interaction21. When \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\chi _e \gtrsim 0.1$$\end{document}, quantum corrections to classical emission become important; when \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\chi _e \sim 1$$\end{document}, pair production becomes significant.

In addition to laser intensity, target composition, and multi-beam configuration, the polarization state of the driving laser pulse plays an important role in shaping electron dynamics, radiation patterns and pair creation. Polarization effects in the SF-QED regime have been explored in both analytical and particle-in-cell (PIC) simulations22–28. However, a systematic, quantitative comparison of polarization-dependent \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\gamma$$\end{document}-photon and BW pair production at ultra-intense laser intensities approaching \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$10^{24}~\mathrm {W/cm^2}$$\end{document}, using a single-laser three-dimensional setup, remains an active area of investigation. In this paper, we investigate this interplay using fully relativistic 3D QED-PIC simulations of ultra-intense laser-plasma interactions at intensities exceeding \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$10^{22}~\mathrm {W/cm^2}$$\end{document}, with both linear and circular laser polarizations. We focus on polarization-dependent differences in particle energy spectra, angular distributions, yields and laser-to-particle energy conversion efficiencies. Our results reveal that circularly polarized (CP) pulses can produce higher-energy and more collimated \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\gamma$$\end{document}-ray emission compared to linearly polarized (LP) laser pulses on interaction with a near-critical density hydrogen target. This enhancement is attributed to two key effects: (i) Electrons in CP fields experience sustained exposure to the laser field, especially when guided along self-generated channels or magnetic vortices, allowing them to radiate multiple high-energy photons over longer trajectories, and (ii) The smoother acceleration in CP fields mitigates quantum radiation losses, allowing electrons to retain higher kinetic energy throughout the interaction, which in turn raises the energy of the emitted photons. These high-energy photons subsequently undergo nonlinear Breit–Wheeler pair production, with the pair electron yield also showing sensitivity to the laser polarization state. CP fields produce higher-energy BW electrons localized in narrow angular ranges, while LP fields generate electrons with broader angular spreads, lesser energy emissions and higher yield.

Current multi-PW laser systems operate at peak intensities of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sim 10^{23}~\mathrm {W/cm^2}$$\end{document}, approximately an order of magnitude lower than those considered in this work29. Nevertheless, rapid advances in laser technology at next-generation facilities, such as the Extreme Light Infrastructure (ELI-NP), Apollon and SEL are expected to reach the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$10^{24}~\mathrm {W/cm}^2$$\end{document} regime, enabling controlled laboratory access to SF-QED processes previously beyond reach30–32. Our study is particularly relevant for such intensities, where SF-QED effects become increasingly significant. We consider an ultra-intense laser pulse propagating through a near-critical density hydrogen target. Near-critical density plasmas offer a realistic and experimentally viable platform for exploring such interactions, as demonstrated by recent studies20,33–38. Motivated by these developments, we present a model incorporating the role of laser polarization and QED pair production, supported by three-dimensional particle-in-cell (3D PIC) simulations carried out using the fully relativistic EPOCH code39. Results

PIC simulations were performed (see Methods) to investigate the influence of laser polarization on high-energy photon generation and Breit–Wheeler pair electron production in the strong-field QED regime. When the ultra-intense laser pulse interacts with the fully pre-ionized hydrogen target, it drives relativistic electrons via the ponderomotive force40, leading to strong electron density modulations and the formation of plasma waves. The radiation reaction force, incorporated via the QED module in the simulation plays a critical role by altering electron trajectories and energy spectra through stochastic photon emission. Thus, the simulation captures both classical plasma dynamics and quantum electrodynamic effects, allowing for a self-consistent study of laser-driven electron acceleration, photon emission, and pair production in an ultra-relativistic hydrogen plasma. We also note that dimensionality plays a significant role in this study based on different laser-driven acceleration mechanisms induced by polarized laser pulses41. While 2D3V numerical simulations are generally indispensable for qualitative analyses, the inaccuracies observed in peak energies, collimation and yield of CP-produced QED species in our 2D3V runs can not be ignored due to the neglect of contribution of ponderomotive force in the third spatial dimension. Therefore, fully 3D simulations are strongly recommended for accurately capturing the dynamics in the present strong-field QED regime.

Our simulations reveal that, as shown in Fig. 1a and b, relativistic plasma electron bunch formed during the early stages of laser–plasma interaction gets accelerated to much higher energies when QED effects are turned off in case of both CP (green curve) and LP (orange curve) lasers. The reduction in electron energy when QED processes are enabled (CP: red, LP: blue) arises from radiation losses associated with nonlinear Compton scattering of the highest-energy electrons, leading to depletion of the high-energy tail. The CP laser produces a more collimated beam (mean tilt = \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$9.68^\circ$$\end{document}, FWHM divergence = \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$4.35^\circ$$\end{document} for electrons with energies \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$>2$$\end{document} GeV) and higher peak energies (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$>6$$\end{document} GeV) compared to the LP case (mean tilt = \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$17.69^\circ$$\end{document}, FWHM divergence = \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$6.93^\circ$$\end{document}) when QED effects are enabled. This indicates better beam collimation, resulting in enhanced \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\gamma$$\end{document}-photon and Breit–Wheeler electron emission under circular polarization compared to linearly polarized lasers due to an improved \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\chi _e$$\end{document}.

In this interaction regime, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\gamma$$\end{document}-photon emission arises from radiation of ultra-relativistic electrons interacting with both the intense laser field and self-generated magnetic fields in the plasma. The corresponding electron quantum nonlinearity parameter reaches peak values of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\chi _e \approx 10.97$$\end{document} for CP (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$a_0 = 679$$\end{document}) and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\chi _e \approx 4.28$$\end{document} for LP (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$a_0 = 961$$\end{document}), while the fraction of electrons with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\chi _e \gtrsim 1$$\end{document} remains below 1% in both cases (CP: 0.63% and LP: 0.09%). As a result, most \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\gamma$$\end{document}-photon emission occurs in the synchrotron-like regime (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\chi _e < 1$$\end{document}), with nonlinear Compton scattering becoming important for electrons reaching \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\chi _e \gtrsim 1$$\end{document} and enhancing the high-energy photon yield. The laser polarization directly determines the electron trajectories and photon angular distribution. Ring-like structures are observed in these distribution plots, signifying periodicity of the laser pulse. The overlay of magnetic field with the relatively smooth spatial distribution of emitted \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\gamma$$\end{document} photons in Fig. 2c shows regions of high transverse field strength due to improved trapping and continuous transverse acceleration of plasma electrons, leading to persistent \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\gamma$$\end{document} emission. These regions correspond to potential hot-spots of BW pair production42. For LP laser (Fig. 2d), transverse acceleration of the electrons vanishes periodically due to the oscillatory nature of electron’s motion, thus reducing photon emission in regions of high magnetic field strength.

The energy spectra (Fig. 2e) further confirm higher photon energies for CP (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$E\approx 6.43$$\end{document} GeV, in red) than for LP (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$E\approx 4.31$$\end{document} GeV, in blue) at \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$dN/dE = 2 \times 10^{8}$$\end{document} particles/GeV due to larger quantum non-linearity parameter, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\chi _e \propto \gamma \, E_\perp$$\end{document}. In CP fields, electrons experience continuous transverse acceleration without sign reversal, maintaining a high \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$E_\perp$$\end{document} and sustaining large \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\chi _e$$\end{document} over many laser cycles. In contrast, the alternating transverse acceleration of electrons by LP fields causes \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\chi _e$$\end{document} to oscillate between large and small values which reduces the time-averaged \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\chi _e$$\end{document} and hence the net radiation output. Angular distribution plots (Fig. 2f, g) showing improved collimation for CP as compared to LP laser are in agreement with Fig. 2a, b. These observations demonstrate that CP lasers are more suitable for generating collimated, high-energy \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\gamma$$\end{document}-ray sources in the SF-QED regime by improving electron-field coupling. For CP lasers, a self-generated plasma channel is formed, enabling acceleration of plasma electrons and the emission of forward-directed high-energy \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\gamma$$\end{document} photons43. These photons subsequently interact with the laser fields to generate BW pairs. For CP laser, the electric field vector rotates at the laser frequency, generating helical trajectories of electrons with steady longitudinal velocity. The motion is symmetric about the propagation axis, so the BW pairs are emitted predominantly in a narrow forward cone defined by the relativistic beaming angle \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\theta \sim 1/\gamma$$\end{document}. The angular width can be approximated by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta \theta \sim \frac{p_\perp }{p_\parallel }$$\end{document}, where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p_\perp$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p_\parallel$$\end{document} are the transverse and longitudinal components of the electron momentum, respectively. The angular spectra in Fig. 3a, b show collimation of the highest-energy BW electrons for CP and LP lasers. For CP (Fig. 3a), BW \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$e^-$$\end{document} are generated near the self-generated channel axis, where the quasi-static azimuthal magnetic field and longitudinal electric field provide large and steady \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p_\parallel$$\end{document}, accelerating them forward. This produces a narrow forward-directed beam with a FWHM azimuthal spread of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$12.06^\circ$$\end{document}, as the transverse oscillations are suppressed by CP field symmetry. In contrast, LP-driven \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$e^-$$\end{document} (Fig. 3b) exhibit a broader FWHM spread of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$55.9^\circ$$\end{document} due to the absence of a stable guiding channel and reversal of transverse momentum every half-cycle. This leads to figure-eight trajectories of electrons in the linearly polarized laser frame, with large periodic variations in both \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p_\perp$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p_\parallel$$\end{document}. During certain phases, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p_\parallel$$\end{document} reduces significantly, therefore increasing the emission angle. The energy spectra in Fig. 3c further confirm that the BW \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$e^-$$\end{document} cutoff energy is higher for CP laser (4.35 GeV) than for LP lasers (3.12 GeV) at \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$dN/dE= 10^9$$\end{document} particles/GeV.

Temporal evolution (Fig. 3d, e) shows CP-produced and driven BW \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$e^-$$\end{document} attaining a peak Lorentz factor \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\gamma = (1-v^2/c^2)^{-1/2} \approx 1.4 \times 10^4$$\end{document} and a sharp population peak of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$N_{e^-} = 2.77\times 10^{11}$$\end{document} at \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$116.26~T_0$$\end{document}, followed by a decline and saturation (see Fig. 3d: red curve), corresponding to the escape of some untrapped electrons through the open simulation boundaries44. In the LP case, the absence of sustained guiding structures and weaker laser-particle coupling leads to smaller peak \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\gamma \approx 0.8 \times 10^4$$\end{document} (Fig. 3e) but higher overall peak yield of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$N_{e^-}=8.25\times 10^{11}$$\end{document} at \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$116.26~T_0$$\end{document} (Fig. 3e: blue curve). Consistent with the temporal evolution of the number of species \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$N_{e^-}$$\end{document} shown in Fig. 3d–e, an \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$e^\pm$$\end{document} plasma is created in both the cases, with volume-averaged (mean) BW electron number density \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$n_{e^-_{BW}}$$\end{document} reaching \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\approx 10^{-3}~n_0$$\end{document} (for CP) and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\approx 2.98 \times 10^{-3}~n_0$$\end{document} (for LP). Here, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$n_0$$\end{document} is the initial electron number density.

The similarity between the dynamics of BW electrons and plasma electrons is evident in the 3D isosurface distribution of Fig. 3f for the CP case, where a dense BW electron beam forms near the channel axis and undergoes efficient channel-guided acceleration by self-generated electromagnetic fields45. In contrast, LP-generated BW \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$e^-$$\end{document} (see Fig. 3b, c, e, g) show a more isotropic distribution with weaker particle confinement and lower energy.

To analyze the amount of laser energy absorbed in both the laser polarization cases, we calculated the laser-to-particle conversion efficiency (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\eta$$\end{document}) for \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\gamma$$\end{document}-photons and BW electrons from the simulation. The laser-to-\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\gamma$$\end{document}-photon conversion efficiency is found to be higher for CP (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\eta _\gamma = 12.03\%$$\end{document}) than for LP lasers (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\eta _\gamma = 11.35\%$$\end{document}). In contrast, LP exhibits a higher BW electron conversion efficiency (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\eta _{\textrm{BW}} = 0.296\%$$\end{document}) compared to CP (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\eta _{\textrm{BW}} = 0.0984\%$$\end{document}). This behavior originates from polarization-dependent electron dynamics. In the CP case, smoother electron motion due to continuous transverse acceleration leads to efficient emission of higher-energy and better-collimated \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\gamma$$\end{document}-photons. In contrast, LP lasers induce strong instantaneous electron oscillations but the absence of sustained guiding structures and weaker laser-particle coupling creates a trade-off for BW electrons. Although BW electrons produced in the LP case have lower energies, their larger yield results in a higher overall \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\eta _{\textrm{BW}}$$\end{document}, as also indicated in Fig. 3d, e. Consistent with the hierarchy of SF-QED processes, BW pair production is a secondary process and therefore shows significantly lower laser-conversion efficiency than \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\gamma$$\end{document}-photon generation in both the cases. Further, we observe \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\eta _{\textrm{BW}}$$\end{document} to be dominated by kinetic energy, with negligible contribution from rest-mass energy. This confirms the ultra-relativistic nature of BW electrons.

Theoretical model

We present a model incorporating the role of laser polarization and QED cascade development. Let the laser be described by a vector potential amplitude \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$a_0 = eE_0/(m_e c \omega )$$\end{document}, where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$E_0$$\end{document} is the electric field amplitude, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\omega$$\end{document} is the laser frequency, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$m_e$$\end{document} is the electron rest mass. In a cold plasma, the critical density is defined as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$n_c = \frac{\varepsilon _0 m_e \omega ^2}{e^2}$$\end{document}. For a hydrogen plasma with electron density \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$n_0> n_c$$\end{document}, the medium is classically overdense. However, athe laser field is modeled as a plane wave with four-potent high intensities (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$a_0 \gg 1$$\end{document}), the relativistic mass increase of electrons leads to a reduced effective plasma frequency, and the slightly overdense plasma becomes relativistically transparent when \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\gamma = \sqrt{1 + a_0^2} \gtrsim \frac{n_0}{n_c}$$\end{document}46,47. For LP laser, we write the electric field as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{E}(z,t) = E_0 \cos (kz - \omega t) \hat{x}$$\end{document}, resulting in oscillatory electron motion along the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{x}$$\end{document}-axis. Electrons experience sharp momentum reversals, producing high-energy bursts of radiation in the laser polarization direction. For CP laser, the electric field is expressed as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{E}(z,t) = E_0 \left[ \cos (kz - \omega t) \hat{x} + \sin (kz - \omega t) \hat{y} \right]$$\end{document}, leading to continuous helical electron motion and smoother energy gain. This favors prolonged gamma photon emission and more efficient development of QED cascades.

The interaction strength is governed by the quantum nonlinearity parameter48 as1\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \chi _e = \frac{e \hbar }{m_e^3 c^4} \sqrt{-(F^{\mu \nu } p_\nu )^2} \approx \frac{a_0 \gamma _e \hbar \omega }{m_e c^2}, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\gamma _e$$\end{document} is the Lorentz factor of the electron and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F^{\mu \nu }$$\end{document} is the electromagnetic field tensor. A similar parameter \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\chi _\gamma$$\end{document} governs photon decay, which can be written as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\chi _\gamma \approx \frac{a_0 \hbar \omega _\gamma }{m_e c^2}$$\end{document}. The regime \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\chi _{e,\gamma } \gtrsim 1$$\end{document} signifies SF-QED dominance21.

Electrons accelerated by the laser field emit high-energy photons via nonlinear Compton scattering: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$e^- + n \gamma _{\text {laser}} \rightarrow e^- + \gamma$$\end{document}, with emitted photon energies reaching \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hbar \omega _\gamma \lesssim \chi _e m_e c^2$$\end{document}. Here, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$n \ge 1$$\end{document} is the number of photons from the laser field that are simultaneously absorbed by the electron to emit a single high-energy photon. The emission is more burst-like for LP and more continuous for CP. Photons with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\chi _\gamma \gtrsim 1$$\end{document} can decay via the nonlinear Breit–Wheeler process: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\gamma + n \gamma _{\text {laser}} \rightarrow e^- + e^+$$\end{document}. The pair production rate is exponentially sensitive to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\chi _\gamma$$\end{document}, which can be written as2\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} W_{\gamma \rightarrow e^+e^-} \sim \alpha \frac{m_e^2 c^4}{\hbar \omega _\gamma } \exp \left( -\frac{8}{3\chi _\gamma }\right) , \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha = \frac{e^2}{4\pi \varepsilon _0 \hbar c}$$\end{document} is the fine-structure constant, characterizing the strength of electromagnetic interaction. The process is governed by the laser polarization vector and determines the probability distribution of emitted photons. Discussion

In this work, we have demonstrated that laser polarization acts as a decisive control parameter for shaping high-energy photon generation and Breit–Wheeler pair production in the strong-field QED regime. In the ultra-high \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$a_0$$\end{document} Gaussian-pulse regime considered here, we find that circular polarization yields up to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sim$$\end{document}49% higher peak energies for \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\gamma$$\end{document} photons and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sim$$\end{document}40% higher peak energies for BW electrons as compared to linear polarization. Further, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sim$$\end{document}78.4% improved collimation of BW electrons is observed owing to sustained electron–field interaction and enhanced trapping in the case of circularly polarized lasers. These quantitative results highlight polarization shaping as a powerful tool for optimizing high-energy photon sources and dense QED pair plasmas. The forthcoming generation of ultraintense multi-petawatt laser facilities such as ELI, will enable experimental validation of these results as laser intensities soon approach the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$10^{24}~\mathrm {W/cm}^2$$\end{document} intensity regime30.

To ensure experimental practicality, additional simulations were performed at the same resolution to study the polarization-dependent effects of obliquely incident laser irradiation at \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$10^\circ$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$20^\circ$$\end{document} angles on the plasma target. All other simulation parameters were kept identical to the normal-incidence cases reported.

The oblique incidence introduces a transverse component of the laser electric field that enhances the longitudinal acceleration and modifies the local field symmetry at the plasma interface. Numerical modeling

To confirm the presented laser-plasma interaction, PIC simulations are carried out using the fully relativistic EPOCH-3D code39, with QED routines enabled to account for radiation reaction and multiphoton Breit–Wheeler pair production. To ensure accurate particle dynamics in the ultra-relativistic regime, the Higuera–Cary particle pusher is employed51.
