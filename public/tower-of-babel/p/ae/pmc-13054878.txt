Advancing from Compliance to Innovation in Medical Education: a strategic roadmap for MEUs in colleges of Korean medicine based on FGI
Authors: Min Jung Lee, Hye-Yoon Lee, Myung Ho Jin, Seon Kyoung Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesThis study sought to develop a strategic, phased roadmap for advancing Medical Education Units (MEUs) in colleges of Korean Medicine, grounded in qualitative insights derived from focus group interviews (FGIs) and contextualized within the existing literature.MethodsQualitative data were obtained from key stakeholders across 12 institutions. Data were analyzed using thematic analysis guided by a goal-oriented, competency-based framework.ResultsA three-phase developmental model was identified. Phase 1 emphasizes foundational compliance with accreditation and institutional requirements; Phase 2 focuses on strengthening faculty development and implementing systematic quality control processes; and Phase 3 advances toward innovation through the integration of artificial intelligence (AI) and quality improvement (QI) systems.ConclusionThis study proposes a feasible and scalable framework for the development of Educational Affairs Units (EAUs), supported by both domestic and international benchmarks, and provides practical guidance for enhancing educational quality in Korean Medicine education.

INTRODUCTION

Medical education is undergoing profound transformation in response to rapidly evolving social and academic environments. Large-scale developments, including the Fourth Industrial Revolution, the expansion of digital healthcare, and the emergence of artificial intelligence (AI), are redefining the competencies required of future healthcare professionals. Accordingly, the assurance of educational quality and the promotion of continuous innovation have become critical priorities [1]. In Korea, the establishment of the Korean Institute of Medical Education and Evaluation in 2004 provided a foundational framework for accreditation, prompting universities to systematize curricula and transition toward competency-based education [2, 3]. These developments highlight the necessity not only of meeting formal accreditation requirements but also of fundamentally restructuring educational governance and quality assurance systems [4].

Against this evolving context, the role of the Medical Education Unit (MEU) within colleges of Korean Medicine has assumed increasing significance. Initially established to provide administrative support and facilitate accreditation processes, MEUs have progressively expanded their scope to encompass curriculum planning and implementation, faculty and student support, and the management of accreditation-related activities. In certain institutions, their functions have further extended to include faculty development, student counseling, and the promotion of educational innovation initiatives [2, 3]. Since 2010, the establishment of MEUs has accelerated, and the incorporation of MEU operations into the accreditation standards of KAS2022 has conferred formal institutional legitimacy [5]. This evolution reflects a transition from auxiliary administrative units to central entities responsible for quality assurance in Korean Medicine education. At present, all 12 colleges of Korean Medicine have established MEUs and appointed full-time faculty members with doctoral-level training in education to satisfy accreditation requirements. Nevertheless, persistent limitations in human and financial resources, together with the lack of structured competency frameworks, have constrained their development and have largely confined their activities to accreditation-related tasks.

Academic departments continue to serve as the traditional administrative foundation of universities, overseeing teaching assignments, student records, and faculty affairs. However, in practice, their responsibilities in curriculum implementation frequently overlap with those of MEUs, underscoring the need for clearer role delineation and coordinated collaboration. At the institutional level, curriculum committees function as formal policy-making bodies responsible for curriculum design, revision, evaluation, and continuous improvement. The KAS2022 standards have mandated their establishment, thereby reinforcing their strategic importance in guiding educational policy [5]. While departments and MEUs primarily undertake operational and administrative functions, curriculum committees are expected to provide strategic oversight and institutional direction; however, concerns remain that their activities may be limited to procedural functions.

Accordingly, although MEUs, departments, and curriculum committees fulfill distinct roles, ambiguities in role boundaries and the persistence of formalistic practices constitute ongoing challenges. Clarifying the functions of MEUs and redefining their relationships with departments and curriculum committees are therefore essential not only for enhancing administrative efficiency but also for ensuring the quality and sustainability of Korean Medicine education. In this context, comparative analyses of institutional practices, combined with insights from faculty and administrators, are required to elucidate roles, collaborative structures, and pathways for improvement. Focus Group Interviews (FGIs), as a qualitative methodological approach, provide a valuable means of capturing diverse stakeholder perspectives, identifying shared themes, and examining inter-organizational dynamics [6].

The present study employed FGIs across colleges of Korean Medicine nationwide to examine the roles of MEUs, their interactions with academic departments, and their relationships with curriculum committees. The findings are intended to provide an evidence-based foundation for strengthening the institutional role of educational support units and to contribute to broader efforts aimed at enhancing educational quality and promoting systemic innovation in Korean Medicine education.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

1. Study design

A qualitative research design was employed to examine the current status and developmental strategies of MEUs in colleges of Korean Medicine. Specifically, twelve in-person FGIs were conducted, one at each of the twelve colleges and graduate schools of Korean Medicine nationwide. This design was intended to capture institution-specific contexts while facilitating the identification of shared themes and distinctive characteristics through in-depth, interactive discussion.

1) Recruitment and selection of participants

Participants for the FGIs were recruited using purposive sampling, targeting faculty members and administrative personnel engaged in educational affairs within colleges of Korean Medicine. A total of 72 participants were included, organized by institution into 12 groups comprising 5-8 members each. All participants were provided with detailed information regarding the study objectives and procedures, voluntarily submitted written informed consent, and subsequently participated in the interviews (Table 1).

2) Designing focus group interview

To structure the FGI questionnaire, the GROW model was employed. The GROW model is a well-established framework for coaching dialogue, comprising four stages: Goal (goal setting), Reality (assessment of the current situation), Options (identification of potential strategies), and Will (commitment to action). Rather than adhering to a strictly linear sequence, the model permits flexible transitions between stages and iterative application (Grant, 2012).

Although originally developed as an evidence-based coaching framework, the GROW model was not applied as a strategic planning tool in this study; rather, it was utilized as an analytic scaffold to structure focus group inquiry and to organize participants’ reflections along a developmental continuum, from current conditions to future-oriented actions.

The FGI questions were designed to align with the study objectives by integrating elements from each stage of the GROW framework. During the Reality stage, participants were asked to describe the current division of roles and operational functions of MEUs, academic departments, and curriculum committees, as well as factors impeding the activation of MEUs. The Goal stage explored optimal role delineation and the strategic directions in which MEUs should be strengthened. The Options stage elicited potential roles, strategies to address identified barriers, and specific support measures to enhance roles and competencies. The Will stage was intended to generate concrete directions for the implementation and improvement of MEUs.

The questionnaire was reviewed and validated by three researchers (one specializing in education and two in Korean Medicine) through iterative discussion and consensus, ensuring clarity and alignment with the study objectives. The finalized structure of the interview questions is presented below (Table 2).

3) Interview procedure

The focus group interviews were conducted in person in 2025, with each session lasting approximately 90 minutes. One researcher served as the facilitator, maintaining neutrality while encouraging discussion and ensuring balanced participation among participants. With participants’ consent, all sessions were audio-recorded, and field notes were documented concurrently.

4) Ethical considerations

The study received approval from the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Dong-Eui University (DIRB-202509-HR-E-51) on September 11, 2025. It was conducted as part of a broader research initiative by the Institute of Korean Medicine Education and Evaluation to inform the development of future accreditation standards for Korean Medicine education, with a particular focus on a detailed examination of the competencies and roles of Medical Education Units.

5) Data collection and analysis

All interviews were transcribed verbatim, and key themes were identified through qualitative analysis. Given that the FGIs were structured using the GROW framework, the analysis was conducted with consideration of each stage—Reality, Goal, Options, and Will/Way forward.

Two researchers independently performed descriptive coding of the transcripts, followed by categorization and thematic refinement. Instead of calculating numerical inter-rater reliability coefficients, a consensus-based qualitative analytic approach was adopted. Discrepancies in coding were resolved through iterative discussion and peer debriefing until full agreement on thematic interpretation was achieved.

RESULTS

1. Differentiation of roles among the MEU, department, and curriculum committee

1) A broad spectrum of views on role delineation

Participants reported a wide range of perspectives regarding the delineation of roles among the MEU, the department (chair), and the curriculum committee. Some participants perceived these roles as clearly defined—“separated,” “each role is well distinguished.” In contrast, many others emphasized blurred boundaries, describing the situation as “role delineation is ambiguous,” “the three roles are divided but overlapping,” and “not clearly distinguished.”

Perceptions varied substantially according to the timing of MEU establishment and the level of organizational maturity. In institutions where the MEU had been established relatively recently, participants described an “early preparation stage” and noted that “there was almost no work performed by the MEU until before 2025,” indicating an early phase of role formation. By contrast, participants from institutions with longer-established MEUs described more clearly defined and detailed role differentiation.

The current role of the MEU was generally understood to center on curriculum operations and accreditation-related activities, including “performing all tasks related to the curriculum,” “matters related to accreditation,” and “curriculum monitoring and special lectures on teaching–learning methods.” The department (chair) was perceived as responsible for overall administrative operations, including “leading administrative operations,” “overall departmental affairs,” and “academic affairs management.” The curriculum committee was variably described as “the final decision-making body” and “a deliberative body for curriculum development and revision,” although in some institutions it was perceived as “nominal.”

2) Shared preferences for an ideal balance of independence and collaboration

Perspectives on optimal role delineation consistently reflected a preference for a balanced model that ensures independence while fostering collaboration. Participants emphasized the importance of “clear separation” and the “need for independent role delineation,” while simultaneously highlighting the value of “organically complementary roles” and a “mutually cooperative relationship.” Expectations regarding the MEU underscored its role as a leading professional entity, with statements such as “the MEU should lead education-related work,” “the MEU should operate independently,” and “ideally, the MEU oversees the entire curriculum.” With respect to the department (chair), participants emphasized the need for “administrative backing” and “supporting the MEU’s work,” with the chair providing “overall oversight.” The curriculum committee was expected to assume responsibility for policy decisions and long-term strategic direction, functioning as “a decision-making body,” engaging in “direction setting and deliberation for the curriculum,” and “taking the lead in establishing annual plans for improvement.”

3) Navigating the gap between ideals and reality

Participants demonstrated a clear awareness of practical constraints, noting conditions such as “provided that MEU staffing is expanded,” “in a reality where expanding staffing is difficult,” and “complete separation is unrealistic,” and consequently proposed pragmatic approaches that balance ideal models with operational limitations.

A central challenge concerned the integration of expertise. Some participants supported exclusive MEU leadership “assuming sufficient understanding of Korean Medicine,” whereas others expressed concern regarding the integration of disciplinary and educational expertise, noting that “the MEU director still lacks understanding of Korean Medicine.” These perspectives highlight the need for mutual understanding and collaborative arrangements across domains.

Participants expressed a preference for a horizontal, collaborative operational model rather than traditional hierarchical structures, emphasizing the need to “avoid one-way vertical relationships” and to “consider regular joint workshops and feedback loops,” thereby promoting a cyclical system characterized by bidirectional communication and continuous feedback.

Participants further articulated differentiated responsibilities within a three-tier “MEU–department–curriculum committee” structure, with the MEU responsible for “operations and R&D,” the department for “administrative support and overall management,” and the curriculum committee for “policy decision-making and deliberation,” thereby enabling both specialization and collaboration (Table 3).

2. Perceptions of the MEU’s role

1) From accreditation focus to a hub for educational innovation

Current MEU activities were predominantly perceived as focused on accreditation preparation, including “preparing for accreditation,” “matters related to Hanpyeongwon (the accreditation body),” and “preparing documents for accreditation,” with specific tasks such as “collecting data and writing reports for Hanpyeongwon” and “preparing for KAS 2022.”

Participants expressed mixed perspectives, including concern regarding a distorted organizational identity—“perceived as an organization that prepares for accreditation”—as well as criticism of the accreditation system itself, noting that “unrealistic and formal provisions in Hanpyeongwon’s standards need improvement.”

In contrast, participants articulated expectations for the MEU to assume a leadership role in educational innovation and strategic direction, including “present directions for Korean Medicine education,” “propose desirable education and assessment methods,” “collect best practices,” “monitor policy trends and propose improvements,” and “develop programs aligned with trends.”

2) From ad hoc individual support to systematic, overarching coordination

Participants largely characterized current MEU activities as fragmented and individually oriented, including “student counseling,” “mediating and counseling between faculty and students,” “supporting student assessment,” and reactive “practical operation and coordination of the curriculum.”

The MEU was frequently perceived as operating in a supportive and reactive capacity, reflected in descriptions such as “various educational support” and “meetings and educational support,” indicating an auxiliary, request-driven function rather than a leadership role in curriculum management.

To address these limitations, participants emphasized the need for a comprehensive coordinating function, including “overall coordination of the curriculum,” “overall education-related work,” integration through “linkage with clinical medicine” and “basic–clinical integration,” and functioning as an “accreditation/policy response hub.”

3) From segmented tasks to integrated linkage

Participants highlighted the fragmented nature of current activities, including “running various education programs,” “linking intra- and extra-university programs,” and “creating venues for meetings between external experts and students,” noting that these efforts often lacked systematic integration. Individual evaluation processes—such as “Po measurement (by learning domain, time, course domain) and analysis”—were described as insufficiently connected to program-level improvement.

Limitations in staffing and expertise were identified as underlying contributors to this fragmentation, with participants noting that “it feels like we do everything related to education,” reflecting both workload burden and challenges in implementing systematic approaches.

There was a strong demand for integrated linkage, including the need to “seek a mid- to long-term curriculum that strengthens basic–clinical linkage,” as well as horizontal integration through “common curriculum materials across Korean Medicine colleges nationwide to reduce faculty teaching burdens.”

Finally, the call to “secure a logical rationale for curriculum reform to cultivate practitioners of integrative medicine” underscored expectations for the MEU to articulate a coherent and overarching educational direction beyond the management of discrete programs (Table 4).

3. Need to strengthen the MEU’s role and support strategies

1) An urgent call to strengthen an integrated, quality-oriented role

Participants consistently prioritized the enhancement of educational quality, as reflected in statements such as “improving educational quality,” “developing methods to enhance quality,” and “raising the quality of education,” with specific emphasis on “quality management and enhancement of existing programs” and “improving overall efficiency.”

They expressed a preference for qualitative deepening rather than expansion, emphasizing that “rather than expanding new programs, manage and enhance the quality of existing ones,” and advocating for “setting task priorities to deliver higher-quality work.”

In the domain of faculty development, participants highlighted the need for systematic and continuous programming, including “various methods and regular seminars to strengthen faculty competence” and “teaching-methods lectures for all faculty,” reflecting a perception of the MEU as a strategic development entity grounded in educational expertise.

2) Foundational infrastructure: personnel and budgets—with realistic limits

The most urgent priorities identified were staffing and financial resources, as reflected in statements such as “staffing is urgently needed,” “dedicated personnel are required,” and “reinforcing personnel is a priority,” alongside recognition of practical constraints, including “it is realistically difficult to expand staffing” and “current limits in staffing.”

Participants emphasized the importance of sustainable financial support, including “budget expansion,” “financial support,” and “budgets for stable mid- to long-term operations,” while also acknowledging challenges related to recruitment and retention, such as the need to “recruit competent and passionate MEU personnel and allow continuous experience,” as well as the importance of establishing layered staffing structures comprising “experts in education and dedicated faculty, plus TAs and administrative staff.”

3) Establishing organizational standing through independence and expertise

Organizational independence was identified as a critical factor, reflected in statements such as “independence,” “strengthening status,” and “the MEU should operate independently,” including the need to function “separate from the department” and to be “empowered as a unit also independent of the dean.”

Participants further emphasized the importance of sustained expertise, noting that “professional personnel should continuously handle tasks to secure educational expertise and set the right direction,” and highlighting the need for capacity-building infrastructure, such as “on/offline lectures or shared educational-materials infrastructure to strengthen the MEU’s capacity.”

To address domain-specific expertise gaps, participants proposed integrative models, including the suggestion that “a Korean Medicine major interested and experienced in KM education should work with an education professor for about 3-5 years,” thereby facilitating the development of convergent expertise.

4) Collective progress via collaborative networks

Participants advocated for nationwide collaboration, emphasizing initiatives such as “the 12 institutions jointly promote research and tasks” and the development of “common curriculum materials across colleges to reduce teaching burdens.”

They further called for a “unified operation of Korean Medicine MEUs” while maintaining sensitivity to institutional contexts, suggesting strategies such as “share cases among MEU staff” and “regional exchanges and cooperation.”

The importance of case-sharing and network-building was also highlighted, including recommendations for “mandatory participation of MEU faculty in national education conferences” and to “develop good programs by university and hold performance-sharing meetings,” thereby positioning MEUs within an interconnected national framework (Table 5).

4. Barriers to activating the MEU and ways to overcome them

1) Isolation and a vicious cycle driven by indifference and limited understanding

The most frequently cited barriers were indifference and limited understanding among faculty and institutional members, as reflected in statements such as “lack of awareness among other faculty,” “faculty indifference,” and “indifference among clinical faculty,” which contributed to the isolation of MEUs. One participant noted that “faculty members have little interest in teaching methods or educational studies.”

This indifference was closely linked to an unclear understanding of the MEU’s role, as reflected in statements such as “lack of understanding of the MEU’s meaning and tasks,” “lack of clear standards,” and “insufficient sharing of specific roles,” representing a structural issue that fosters skepticism regarding the value of the MEU.

A self-reinforcing cycle was described, whereby “lack of cooperation” and “lack of consensus on the need for cooperation” lead to reduced effectiveness, which in turn exacerbates indifference, as reflected in the succinct expressions “indifference” and “no interest.”

2) Fundamental trust gaps stemming from expertise disparities

Participants identified a bidirectional expertise gap that undermines trust, including “MEU staff lacking KM knowledge” and “education scholars lacking understanding of KM characteristics.” Particular concern was expressed regarding educational experts’ understanding of Korean Medicine, as reflected in the statement that “the MEU director still lacks understanding of Korean Medicine,” underscoring the importance of domain-informed leadership.

Conversely, faculty trained in Korean Medicine were often perceived as lacking educational expertise, as reflected in statements such as “the quality of personnel is the problem” and “MEU staff lack job development,” indicating persistent structural inefficiencies.

To address these challenges, participants proposed integrative solutions, including the recommendation to “appoint a KM major with interest/experience as MEU director and have them co-work with an education professor for 3-5 years,” thereby promoting mutual learning and convergence of expertise.

3) Accreditation-centered structures distorting identity and degrading core functions

Participants expressed concern that the MEU is “perceived as an organization that prepares for accreditation,” which constrains its capacity to fulfill core functions related to educational improvement and innovation. The tendency to “offload anything with the word education onto the MEU” was identified as contributing to role ambiguity and uncontrolled expansion of responsibilities.

The burden of accreditation-related work was described as substantial, including “enormous administrative effort” and “so much to prepare,” which was perceived to divert resources from core educational functions; one participant remarked that the role felt “closer to an administrative agency than an educational unit.”

Participants also recognized the dual nature of accreditation, noting that “paradoxically, the degree requirement led to seating an administrator with an education degree,” thereby both legitimizing the MEU and reinforcing procedural formalism.

4) Structural capacity limits and sustainability risks due to weak infrastructure

Core structural constraints included limitations in “personnel and budget,” “lack of dedicated personnel and full-time faculty,” and “lack of personnel and budget,” with participants emphasizing the urgency of addressing these issues through statements such as “staffing is urgently needed” and “dedicated personnel are required.”

Budgetary limitations were similarly identified as a threat to sustainability, with calls for “budget expansion,” “financial support,” and “stable mid- to long-term funding.”

Institutional support was perceived as insufficient, as reflected in statements such as “institutional deficiencies at headquarters,” “improve headquarters’ perception of the MEU,” and “lack of school interest.” One participant emphasized the need for broader institutional alignment, noting that “we need a campus-wide consensus on the MEU’s standing,” highlighting the relationship between institutional perception and support.

Finally, the absence of incentives was identified as a critical concern, reflected in statements such as “no incentives” and “insufficient rewards,” suggesting that the current reliance on individual commitment is not sustainable (Table 6).

DISCUSSION

Using FGIs conducted across twelve colleges of Korean Medicine nationwide, this study examined the roles of MEUs, their relationships with departments and curriculum committees, and the key tasks required to strengthen their organizational positioning. The findings are interpreted in relation to existing literature and prior research as outlined below. Consistent with the methodological rationale described above, the GROW framework was applied as a structuring tool for qualitative inquiry rather than as a strategic planning methodology.

First, ambiguity in role delineation and a corresponding need for collaborative operational structures were identified. Participants reported unclear and overlapping boundaries among MEUs, departments, and curriculum committees, while expressing a preference for an optimal model that balances independence with collaboration. This observation is consistent with prior scholarship suggesting that, within increasingly complex health-professions education systems, multilayered and cyclical organizational models are more effective than singular, hierarchical approaches [7]. Accordingly, the three-phase roadmap proposed in this study is inductively derived from these qualitative insights; specifically, the “operational constraints” identified through FGIs informed the initial compliance phase, whereas the “demands for qualitative maturation” directly shaped the subsequent phases focused on quality control and innovation. In alignment with this direction, international educational support structures—such as the quality management system at Harvard Medical School [8] and the Teaching and Learning Center at Yale School of Medicine [9]—function as hubs for educational innovation through close coordination with departments and committees. Within Korea, comparable institutional mechanisms are being strengthened (e.g., the educational operations system at Chonnam National University Medical School [10] and the Center for Educational Outcomes and Innovation at Chungbuk National University [11]), further underscoring the need for clearer role delineation and collaborative governance models in MEUs within colleges of Korean Medicine.

Second, the limitations of accreditation-centered operations and the necessity of transitioning toward an innovation-oriented role were prominent. Participants indicated that MEUs are largely preoccupied with accreditation-related activities, thereby limiting their capacity to fulfill their core function of educational innovation. This finding aligns with the historical development of Korean Medicine education in Korea, in which educational scholarship has traditionally received comparatively less emphasis [12]. Participants identified several desirable roles for MEUs, including contributions to educational policy development, faculty development initiatives, and the strengthening of basic–clinical integration—domains that correspond with the CanMEDS 2015 framework’s focus on Quality Improvement, Professional Development, and Interprofessional Collaboration [13]. In this context, MEUs should evolve toward systems in which routine Quality Improvement (QI) activities serve as embedded evidence for accreditation processes, thereby reframing accreditation from a discrete administrative obligation into a mechanism that supports sustained educational innovation. Accordingly, MEUs should extend beyond administrative support functions to operate as practical platforms for the implementation of competency-based education.

Third, both expertise gaps and structural constraints were evident. Across institutions, participants consistently identified staffing shortages and limited financial resources as primary barriers, alongside a bidirectional expertise gap characterized by limited pedagogical expertise among Korean Medicine faculty and insufficient understanding of Korean Medicine among education specialists. These findings highlight the need not only for increased resources but also for integrative models that bridge disciplinary domains. In this regard, the proposed collaborative model is conceptually aligned with the TPACK (Technological Pedagogical Content Knowledge) framework, suggesting that effective curriculum delivery and institutional trust depend on the integration of domain-specific knowledge and pedagogical expertise. Furthermore, the application of the GROW coaching model—which structures Goal, Reality, Options, and Will within an evidence-based framework—to Korean Medicine education [14] offers a mechanism through which MEU personnel can integrate coaching and assessment approaches with domain-specific knowledge. Within the proposed phased framework, Stage 3 (Innovation) does not assume the immediate availability of advanced technological infrastructure; rather, it represents a long-term developmental objective that becomes attainable following the establishment of foundational staffing, financial stability, and institutional trust through Stages 1 and 2. In resource-constrained settings, learning analytics and QI systems should be conceptualized not as resource-intensive innovations, but as tools for improving efficiency and reducing manual workload once minimal data infrastructure is in place. Accordingly, Stage 3 represents a forward-looking developmental trajectory that integrates participant expectations with relevant international evidence, with examples such as AI-based learning analytics serving as illustrative rather than immediate outcomes of the FGIs.

Finally, participants emphasized the importance of nationwide collaborative networks and standardization. They advocated for inter-institutional sharing of educational materials, joint research initiatives, and dissemination of outcomes, reflecting models observed in international educational support networks. As illustrated by the Harvard and Yale examples [8, 9], such collective strategies enable capacities that extend beyond those achievable by individual institutions (Table 7).

This study intentionally focused on stakeholders directly involved in educational affairs, as its primary objective was to examine how MEUs perceive their roles, constraints, and developmental priorities within institutional contexts. Consequently, perspectives from university leadership or clinical faculty not directly engaged in educational administration were not included, which may limit the representativeness of the findings.

Nevertheless, dissenting or critical perspectives were indirectly captured through participants’ accounts of faculty indifference, limited institutional support, and resistance to MEU-led initiatives, suggesting that alternative viewpoints were present in a secondary, mediated form.

Although formal member checking was not conducted, the credibility of the qualitative findings was strengthened through prolonged engagement with the data, verbatim transcription, and iterative peer debriefing among researchers with complementary expertise in education and Korean Medicine.

Future quantitative and mixed-methods research is warranted to evaluate whether and to what extent administrative restructuring and increased MEU autonomy lead to measurable improvements in educational outcomes.

CONCLUSION

Using qualitative methods, this study examined the roles and challenges of MEUs in colleges of Korean Medicine. Rather than testing causal relationships between MEU independence and student learning outcomes, the study conceptualizes organizational conditions necessary for sustainable quality improvement based on participant perspectives and existing literature. The integrated conclusions are as follows.

Clarification of role boundaries among MEUs, departments, and curriculum committees, together with the establishment of a horizontally collaborative operational structure, is essential.

MEUs should reposition their identity from a focus on accreditation responsiveness to functioning as central hubs for educational innovation, faculty development, and integrated education.

To address structural constraints—including shortages of personnel and financial resources, as well as gaps in expertise—there is a need to establish a convergent expertise model and a nationwide collaborative network. Furthermore, by benchmarking the independent governance structures and evidence-based decision-making processes of leading global institutions, MEUs can evolve into strategic entities that ensure the quality and sustainability of Korean Medicine education.

Taken together, these measures support the evolution of MEUs into strategic, core entities that safeguard and advance the quality and sustainability of Korean Medicine education in Korea (Table 8).
