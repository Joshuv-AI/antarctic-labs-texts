Community participation in secular and religious contexts across sociodemographic groups in 22 countries
Authors: Ying Chen, Brendan W. Case, Katelyn N. G. Long, Robert D. Woodberry, Eric S. Kim, R. Noah Padgett, Byron R. Johnson, Tyler J. VanderWeele
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
In an increasingly fragmented and isolated world, community participation remains an essential source of social connection. However, how community participation varies across countries and sociodemographic groups remains unclear. Using data from 202,898 adults across 22 countries, we examined participation in both secular and religious communities across sociodemographic groups. There was substantial cross-national variation in participation rates for both secular and religious communities. On average across countries, younger, male, single, and more educated people were more likely to participate weekly+ in secular groups. Notably, people who attended religious services were also more likely to engage in secular groups, suggesting these forms of participation may be complementary rather than substitutive. Conversely, older, married or widowed, and less educated people attended religious services more often. Country-specific analyses further suggest that sociodemographic variation in community participation differed across countries, indicating diverse societal influences. These findings pave the way for future investigations into how sociocultural factors shape community participation across different national contexts.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-22806-5.

Introduction

Social participation empowers individuals to actively engage with society, which helps foster a sense of agency, belonging, and fulfillment1,2. It also provides a meaningful approach to enhancing social inclusion, strengthening community bonds, and promoting individual and community flourishing3,4. Social participation is often desirable in its own right, and it also provides a promising avenue for addressing prevailing societal challenges such as the rising pandemic of loneliness5. Certain forms of social participation such as civic participation are even considered as a cornerstone of democratic societies6,7. However, there has been a decline in some traditional forms of social participation over recent years, which was exacerbated during the COVID-19 pandemic8–10. It is, therefore, essential to understand the global distribution of social participation and identify sociodemographic groups for whom intervention programs that aim at enhancing social engagement may be most meaningful.

Participation in community groups (also called formal social participation) is a major avenue for social participation for many people, which refers to individuals’ active interactions with established organizations11,12. Some examples include formal volunteering, religious service attendance, and involvement in civic, cultural, sports, and leisure groups. As compared to informal social participation (i.e. social interactions that occur outside of organizational or institutional settings)11, community-based social participation often has stronger associations with improved well-being, possibly because it provides both social integration and a sense of identity and belonging1. Prior public health research suggests that frequent community participation is associated with lower risks of mortality13,14 and mental distress15,16, and with higher levels of happiness16,17, meaning and purpose14, and quality of life18. Participation in faith-based communities (e.g., religious service attendance) may have stronger associations with improved health outcomes (e.g., lower risks of mortality) compared to participation in secular groups13, but the dynamics may vary by contexts (e.g., country-level religiosity and religious regulation)17. Moreover, scholarship from a wide range of disciplines, including sociology, economics, and political science, has highlighted the importance of community participation for a wide range of important social and economic outcomes, including fostering social trust10,19, stabilizing neighborhoods20, promoting effective democratic institutions21, and generating economic mobility22.

Community participation is likely shaped by a complex interplay of individual-, interpersonal-, and contextual-level factors. People tend to engage in groups that affirm their sense of belonging and self-identity23. Their capacity to participate is further influenced by access to resources, time, and social connections, which may be unequally distributed across sociodemographic groups24,25. These processes are, in turn, shaped by broader social norms, cultural values, and structural factors within society26,27. As a result, the dynamics of community participation may differ across sociodemographic groups and national contexts. Below, we described prior evidence on participation patterns across several key sociodemographic groups.

First, the avenues, motivations, and forms of community participation may differ across age groups. For example, in the United States engagement in civic life among young people has declined over recent years as compared to previous generations10,25. Recent national surveys indicate that young adults reported low levels of satisfaction with democracy and institutional trust, along with lower voter turnout than other age groups. However, they also reported low levels of partisan polarization and strong support for democratic values28. Moreover, the ‘16–17 year-olds’ have the highest rate of formal volunteering among all age groups29, which may be partly because many civic and voluntary activities are specifically available to young people through schools and their participation is sometimes driven by instrumental (e.g., career-related) motivations. Taken together, these patterns indicate that young people have potential and motivations to engage in civic life, but they need to be mobilized in meaningful ways that resonate with their values and priorities. In comparison, in the United States, older adults reported more frequent attendance at religious services than all younger adult age groups30. This may be because older adults tend to participate in structured activities or faith-based groups that provide a sense of generativity, meaning, and identity, aligned with the developmental needs of later life6,31.

Second, community participation may vary across gender groups. For example, in the United States women on average reported more frequent religious service attendance than men32, whereas in some Muslim and Jewish nations men attended religious services more than women33. As another example, men and women volunteered at different rates and in different domains, and women got to volunteer more often in countries with higher levels of gender equity31,34. This indicates that patterns of participation may vary by gender groups, potentially reflecting broader gendered cultural norms and expectations.

Third, factors related to socioeconomic status (e.g., education, income, employment) are strong correlates of community participation. For instance, educational attainment is the most often-used predictor of political participation in the United States, although whether education causally increases political engagement remains unclear7. Likewise, more educated people and those in higher socioeconomic status volunteered more often, possibly because they are more likely to affiliate with voluntary groups, possess resources and civic knowledge, hold higher-status jobs, and stay informed of current affairs8,31. In comparison, evidence from prior cross-national studies suggests that more educated people are less likely to attend religious services on average across countries35, although the pattern may be reversed in some individual countries (e.g., United States), depending on the historical and religious contexts of the country36. Likewise, other correlates of socioeconomic status, such as income and employment status, also shape people’s capacity to contribute resources and time to community life25.

Fourth, community participation may also vary by other social identity factors such as marital status and immigration status. For instance, prior studies generally found that married people are more likely to volunteer and attend religious services than people with other marital statuses, which may be because marriage often provides stronger social networks and shared values to engage in community life37,38. For another example, community also provides opportunities of social bridging for new immigrants to integrate into host societies, which is meaningful for the effective functioning of democratic societies. However, some studies suggest that immigrants have lower rates of participation in mainstream voluntary organizations and face more barriers in integrating into the civic life than non-immigrants6,39,40, although the patterns may vary by the integration policies of the host country6,41.

Fifth, many organized religions primarily function as community-based activities, and individuals who participate in religious groups may tend to engage in secular groups as well (e.g., civic groups, volunteering)42. Religious participation provides opportunities of social bridging and may also reinforce a sense of mutual responsibilities and altruistic values43,44. However, not all religious traditions may be equally hospitable to all forms of civic life (e.g., political engagement), and the dynamics may be further shaped by the broader religious landscape of the country25.

It is worth further noting that contextual factors such as cultural values (e.g., individualism/collectivism)45,46 and structural factors (political system, income inequality, social welfare system, religious regulation)1,17,26,35,47 of the society may interplay with above-mentioned individual-level sociodemographic characteristics and shape people’s community participation. Therefore, the unique opportunities for and constraints on community participation across social groups in each society need to be considered.

Prior studies have substantially advanced our understanding of the social distribution of community participation, but some knowledge gaps remain. First, the rising rates of secularization in many societies around the world may influence people’s participation in faith-based groups for those who hold religious/spiritual beliefs and may shape national policies that aim to enhance social engagement48. However, the sociodemographic groups who participate in secular and religious communities have seldom been compared, thus it remains unclear to what extent the trend toward secularization may affect community participation differentially across sociodemographic groups. Such knowledge will be essential for informing culturally appropriate intervention programs that aim at enhancing various forms of social integration and promoting social inclusion, and for identifying the social groups most in need of such programs. Second, prior studies on demographic predictors of community participation are often based on data from single countries. Among cross-national studies that do exist, the focus is often either on religious service attendance, on volunteering, or on the prevalence of voluntary organizations, and do not typically compare secular and religious community involvement across countries directly in the same sample. This limits our understanding of the sociocultural influences that shape individuals’ participation in different forms of community groups broadly across cultures and national contexts. Third, prior research on sociodemographic distribution of community participation, broadly defined, is mostly based on data from Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, and Democratic (WEIRD) countries and/or used non-representative samples from those countries49,50, which limits the generalizability of the study findings.

To begin addressing these knowledge gaps, this study used data from 202,898 adults from 22 countries (with the sample weighted to be nationally representative within each country) to examine the extent of community participation across countries and across sociodemographic groups, with secular and religious community participation examined separately. We hypothesized that (1) the population proportions of frequent secular and religious community participation would both vary across countries, (2) on average across countries, the proportions of frequent participation in both forms of communities would exhibit variations across key sociodemographic groups (e.g., age, gender, marital status, education, employment status, immigration status), and (3) the extent of variations in community participation across sociodemographic groups would also vary by country.

Methods

This study used data from the Global Flourishing Study (GFS). The description of the methods below has been adapted from VanderWeele et al.51. Further methodological detail regarding the Global Flourishing Study is available elsewhere52–57. The present study is a secondary analysis of the existing GFS data.

Study population

This study used Wave 1 data from the Global Flourishing Study, a longitudinal study that enrolled 202,898 adults (age range: 18 to 99 years) from 22 culturally and geographically diverse countries, with the sample weighted to be nationally representative within each country. Survey items included aspects of well-being such as happiness, health, having a sense of meaning and purpose, character, social relationships, and financial stability, along with various demographic, socioeconomic, political, religious, personality, childhood, community, and behavior factors. Data collection was carried out by Gallup via a combination of modes (e.g., in-person, phone, web) that varied across countries. Data for Wave 1 were collected principally during 2023, while some countries began data collection in 202252. The following countries were included in wave 1 data collection: Argentina, Australia, Brazil, Egypt, Germany, Hong Kong (Special Administrative Region of China), India, Indonesia, Israel, Japan, Kenya, Mexico, Nigeria, the Philippines, Poland, South Africa, Spain, Sweden, Tanzania, Turkey, United Kingdom, and the United States. The precise sampling design to ensure nationally representative samples varied by country52. Four additional waves of panel data on the participants will be collected annually from 2024 to 2027. During the survey translation process, Gallup adhered to TRAPD model (translation, review, adjudication, pretesting, and documentation) for cross-cultural survey research (ccsg.isr.umich.edu/chapters/translation/overview)53. The data that supports this research are publicly available via the Center for Open Science (COS, https://www.cos.io/gfs). Details about the GFS study methodology and survey development are reported elsewhere52.

The present study used data from all participants in Wave 1 of GFS (N = 202,898). Sampling weights were provided by Gallup (the weighting factors and weighting methods varied by country, depending on the specific sampling design in each country), and poststratification and nonresponse adjustments were performed to ensure the sample was representative of the adult population in each country52. Ethical approval was granted by the institutional review boards at Baylor University and Gallup, and all participants provided informed consent. All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant ethical guidelines and regulations.

Assessment of community participation

Secular community participation. The participants’ involvement in secular community groups was assessed with this question: “How often do you participate in groups that are not religious, such as book clubs, sports, or political organizations?”. Response options include “never”, “a few times a year”, “one to three times a month”, “once a week”, and “more than once a week”. Because prior studies suggest that at least once/week of community participation is often associated with improved health and well-being outcomes17,42,58, we dichotomized the responses as at least once/week vs. less than once/week or never.

Religious service attendance. Frequency of religious service attendance was assessed with one questionnaire item: “How often do you attend religious services?”. Response options include “never”, “a few times a year”, “one to three times a month”, “once a week”, and “more than once a week”. Similar to secular community participation (see above), we dichotomized religious service attendance as at least once/week vs. less than once/week or never.

Assessment of sociodemographic characteristics

Sociodemographic factors were assessed through participant self-reports. These included age (in years, responses grouped into “18–24’, “25–29”, “30–39”, “40–49”, “50–59”, “60–69”, “70–79”, and “80+”), gender (male, female, or other), marital status (never married, married, separated, divorced, widowed, or domestic partner), employment status (employed by an outside organization, self-employed, retired, student, homemaker, unemployed and searching, or other), educational attainment (up to 8 years, 9–15 years, or 16 + years), and immigration status (born in this country, born in another country). Religious affiliation was also assessed, and response options included 15 major religions [e.g., Christianity, Islam, Hinduism, Buddhism, Judaism, etc.], “some other religion”, or “No religion/Atheist/Agnostic”; response categories varied by country59. Race/ethnicity was assessed in most countries (except in Germany, Japan, Spain, and Sweden, due to restrictions in collecting such data), and response options varied by country as appropriate. For the analyses on secular community participation, we additionally included religious service attendance (> once/week, once/week, one-to-three times/month, a few times/year, or never), an indicator of religious involvement, as a sociodemographic variable. In comparison, secular community participation is not typically considered as a demographic factor, thus we did not include it as a sociodemographic predictor in the analyses for religious service attendance.

Statistical analyses

The descriptive analyses showed participant sociodemographic characteristics in individual countries and in the total sample, weighted to be nationally representative within each country. The proportions for at least once/week of participation in secular and religious communities were estimated for each country separately (adjusted for complex survey design and weighted to be nationally representative), and for presentation purposes, we ordered the estimates from highest to lowest along with 95% confidence intervals. Variation in proportions of weekly community participation by sociodemographic categories was also estimated (secular and religious community participation were examined separately), with all analyses initially conducted within country (reported in online supplement). Global (joint) tests were conducted (with global p-values calculated) to assess whether community participation varied statistically significantly across categories of each sociodemographic variable within each country.

In the primary analyses we conducted random effects meta-analysis to pool the country-specific proportion estimates (logit transformed first, and then back-transformed to report all statistics on the proportion scale) for at least once/week of community participation within each specific sociodemographic category60,61, along with 95% confidence intervals and standard errors. The lower and upper limits of the prediction intervals, heterogeneity (τ), and I2 estimates are also reported for evidence concerning variation in the estimated proportions within a particular sociodemographic variable across countries62. Forest plots of estimates are shown in the online supplement. We also examined religious affiliation and race/ethnicity (when available) in country-specific analyses but not with the meta-analyses, because the observed response categories for these two demographic factors varied by country. We reported pooled global p-values63 across countries to evaluate whether the proportion estimates varied statistically significantly by each sociodemographic variable within at least one country. Bonferroni corrected p-value thresholds are provided in the meta-analytic results based on the number of sociodemographic variables64,65.

All analyses were performed in R (R Core Team, 2024; https://www.R-project.org/)66 and the meta-analyses used the metafor package67. As a supplementary analysis, population weighted meta-analysis was performed to pool the country-specific estimates. The population weighted meta-analysis effectively treats each person equally, rather than treating each country equally as the random effects meta-analysis does. All analyses were pre-registered prior to data access at Center for Open Science (COS; 10.17605/OSF.IO/KX369 and 10.17605/OSF.IO/35XFV); all code to reproduce analyses are openly available in an online repository54.

Missing data

In the combined cross-national sample, 0.31% of participants had missing data on secular community participation and 0.35% had missing data on religious service attendance. Missing data on sociodemographic factors ranged from 0.01% to 1.01% (except for race/ethnicity, which was not measured in some countries). We conducted multivariate imputation by chained equations (5 imputed datasets created) to impute missing data on all variables in each country separately68–70. We included sampling weights in the imputation models to account for specific-variable missingness that may have been related to probability of inclusion in the study56.

Accounting for complex sampling design

The GFS used different sampling schemes across countries based on availability of existing panels and recruitment needs52. All analyses in this study accounted for the complex survey design components by including weights, primary sampling units, and strata. Additional methodological details are reported elsewhere56.

Exploratory (Not Pre-Registered Analyses)

After examining the results, we identified that it was worth exploring the correlation between the country-level proportion of frequent community participation and the country’s economic development status. To this end, we computed polyserial correlation (a correlation between an ordinal variable—economic development classification of the country as defined by the World Bank71 —and a continuous variable— the proportion of at least once/week participation in community groups). An overall correlation coefficient and standard error were computed for secular and religious community participation separately using the polycor package in R.

Results

Descriptive analyses

In the pooled cross-national sample (weighted to be nationally representative within each country), participants’ age ranged from 18 to 99 years, and there were fewer participants from the oldest age groups (older than 70 years) than from younger groups (Table 1). The sample was 51% female and 49% male, with a small proportion (0.3%) who identified as other gender. Over half of the participants were married (53%), employed (57%), had 9–15 years of education (57%), and most were native-born (94%). Among all participants, 17.7% participated in secular groups at least once/week and 32% attended religious services at least weekly. Participant characteristics for each individual country are reported in Supplementary Tables S1a to S22a.

Ordered proportions of ‘At least weekly community participation’ by country

The left columns of Table 2 show the ordered proportions of people who participated in non-religious groups once a week or more—a measure referred to hereafter as “weekly+”. Nigeria had the highest proportion of weekly+ participation, 0.33 (95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.31, 0.35), followed by Indonia, 0.32 (95% CI: 0.31, 0.34) and Australia, 0.28 (95% CI: 0.26, 0.30). Conversely, the lowest proportion of weekly+ participation was in Egypt, 0.07 (95% CI: 0.06, 0.08), Japan, 0.09 (95% CI: 0.09, 0.10) and Poland, 0.10 (95% CI: 0.09, 0.12). Thus, the gap between the highest and lowest proportion was 0.26 (i.e., 0.33 − 0.07 = 0.26) or 26% points. The ordered proportion of weekly+ secular group participation was not patterned by the countries’ economic development status (r = -.09, SE = 0.25, based on our post-hoc exploratory analysis).

The right columns of Table 2 show the ordered proportions of people who attended religious services once a week or more. Again, Nigeria was the highest ranked country, with a proportion of 0.87 attending weekly+ (95% CI: 0.86, 0.89). Next was Kenya, 0.78 (95% CI: 0.76, 0.79), and Tanzania, 0.76 (95% CI: 0.75, 0.77). Conversely, the lowest proportion was in Japan, 0.03 (95% CI: 0.03, 0.04), Sweden, 0.04 (95% CI: 0.04, 0.05), and Germany, 0.07 (95% CI: 0.07, 0.08). Therefore, the gap between the highest and lowest proportion was 0.84 (i.e., 0.87 − 0.03 = 0.84) or 84% points.

Interestingly, the proportion of ‘weekly+ religious service attendance’ seems closely tied with the country’s economic development status (r = -.80, SE = 0.08, based on our post-hoc exploratory analysis). Countries with higher rates of religious service attendance tend to be lower to upper middle-income countries (e.g., Nigeria, Kenya, Tanzania, Indonesia, Philippines, Turkey). In contrast, almost all high-income nations are lower-ranked countries in service attendance (e.g., Sweden, Germany, Australia, United Kingdom, Spain, United States, Japan).

Sociodemographic variation in secular community participation: pooled estimates across countries

On average across countries the proportion of weekly+ secular community participation was patterned by all sociodemographic factors that we examined (Table 3). All global p-values met the Bonferroni corrected threshold of p <.007, which indicates that weekly+ participation in secular groups varied between the categories of each variable in at least one of the 22 countries.

Secular community participation decreased nearly monotonically with age on average across countries, whereby a higher proportion of younger vs. older participants engaged in secular groups at least weekly (e.g., proportion of weekly+ participation age 18−24 =0.24 [95% CI: 0.21, 0.28] vs. proportion age 80+ =0.10 [95% CI: 0.04, 0.24]). Moreover, the mean proportion of weekly+ participation among men (proportion = 0.21, 95% CI: 0.18, 0.25) was somewhat higher than women (proportion = 0.17, 95% CI: 0.14, 0.20) and was considerably higher than those identified as other gender (proportion = 0.07, 95% CI: 0.01, 0.37). Concerning marital status, the proportion of weekly+ participation was highest among the never married participants (proportion = 0.21, 95% CI: 0.18, 0.25), followed by married people (proportion = 0.19, 95% CI: 0.15, 0.22), and was lowest among widows (proportion = 0.15, 95% CI: 0.11, 0.19).

Secular community participation also varied by employment status. For instance, individuals who were employed for an outside organization (proportion = 0.19, 95% CI: 0.16, 0.23) or self-employed (proportion = 0.20, 95% CI: 0.17, 0.23) had a higher proportion of weekly+ participation, as compared to the unemployed (proportion = 0.16, 95% CI: 0.12, 0.20) or the homemaker (proportion = 0.14, 95% CI: 0.11, 0.18). Students on average also reported a high proportion of weekly+ participation (proportion = 0.27, 95% CI: 0.24, 0.30), which might be related to their age differences from participants in other employment statuses (i.e., confounding by age, as younger people participated more than older people in this sample) or possibly due to their access to community groups through school. In addition, the proportion of weekly+ participation also increased monotonically with years of education (e.g., proportion16+years of education=0.22 [95% CI: 0.19, 0.25] vs. proportion up to 8 years of education=0.17 [95% CI: 0.14, 0.21]).

Notably, the proportion of weekly+ secular group participation also increased monotonically with the frequency of religious service attendance. For instance, the proportion of participants who reported weekly+ participation in secular groups was 0.33 (95% CI: 0.27, 0.39) among those who attended religious services more than once/week, whereas the proportion was only 0.12 (95% CI: 0.10, 0.16) among those who never attended religious services.

With regard to variation in estimates across countries, the heterogeneity estimate τ (i.e. the estimated standard deviation of the proportions across countries) was often low (below 0.10). There were only two categories with relatively higher τ values s, i.e., those who were ‘80 + years old’ (τ = 0.22) and those who identified as ‘other’ gender identities (τ = 0.28). The calculated I2 was relatively high across all sociodemographic categories, indicating that a substantial proportion of the variability in the proportion estimates across countries is not attributable to sampling variability. The estimated prediction intervals were also wide across sociodemographic categories, providing further evidence on heterogeneity across countries.

Sociodemographic variation in religious service attendance: pooled estimates across countries

Table 4 presents the random effects meta-analysis of the proportion of weekly+ religious service attendance by sociodemographic category. All global p-values met the Bonferroni corrected threshold of p <.008, which provides evidence that weekly+ service attendance varied between the categories of each variable in at least one country.

On average across countries, the proportion of weekly+ service attendance was generally higher among older vs. younger groups (e.g., proportion of weekly+ attendance age 80+ =0.49 [95% CI: 0.27, 0.72] vs. proportion age 18−24 =0.31 [95% CI: 0.20, 0.44]). The proportion of weekly+ attendance was similar between men and women (e.g., proportion men=0.33, 95% CI: 0.22, 0.46), but was considerably lower in those identified as other gender (proportion = 0.15, 95% CI: 0.02, 0.63). Concerning marital status, married and widowed participants reported a higher proportion of weekly+ attendance (proportion married=0.37, 95% CI: 0.25, 0.51; proportion widowed=0.36, 95% CI: 0.22, 0.52) than other marital statuses (e.g., proportion never married=0.29, 95% CI: 0.19, 0.42).

Religious service attendance also varied by employment status. For instance, a lower proportion of unemployed participants attended services weekly+ (proportion = 0.26, 95% CI: 0.16, 0.40), as compared to the employed participants (e.g., proportion employed for an outside organization=0.31, 95% CI: 0.20, 0.45). Moreover, the proportion of weekly+ attendance also decreased monotonically with years of education (e.g., proportion up to 8 years of education =0.39 [95% CI: 0.26, 0.53] vs. proportion 16+years of education=0.31 [95% CI: 0.20, 0.44]).

Considering variation in the estimated proportions of weekly+ service attendance across countries, the heterogeneity estimate τ suggests that greater variation was again present within the oldest age group (τ = 0.57) and the group of other gender identities (τ = 0.63), as compared to other sociodemographic categories (τ estimates did not exceed 0.37) (Table 4). The I2 estimates were relatively high across sociodemographic categories, suggesting that a substantial proportion of variability in the proportion estimates is not attributable to sampling variability.

It is worth noting that by comparing Tables 3 and 4, on average across countries, the estimated proportion of weekly+ religious service attendance was higher than weekly+ secular community participation in almost every single sociodemographic category.

Sociodemographic variation in secular community participation: Country-Specific estimates

Weekly+ secular community participation was patterned by almost all sociodemographic factors (global p-values < 0.05) in more than half of the 22 countries, except immigration status (Supplementary Tables S1b-S22b and Supplementary Figures S1-S34). The most consistent demographic correlate of weekly+ secular group participation was religious service attendance. The proportion of weekly+ secular community participation was higher among frequent religious service attenders vs. never attenders consistently in all 22 countries, and some of the differences were quite large. For example, in Hong Kong only 3% of those who ‘never attended religious services’ were involved in secular groups weekly+, whereas an estimate of 84% of ‘weekly+ religious service attenders’ participated in secular groups weekly+. This is a difference of 81% points. Similarly in the United Kingdom, the difference is 31% points.

In several countries secular group participation generally decreased with age (e.g., Argentina, Egypt, and Indonesia), whereas the pattern appeared curvilinear, with the youngest and oldest participating more in some other countries (e.g., Japan, United Kingdom, and United States). Weekly+ participation also generally increased with years of education in most countries (e.g., Brazil, Germany, Japan, Nigeria, Kenya, United States), but the pattern was reversed in Hong Kong and Poland.

While the specific patterning for marital status and employment status varied across countries, in many countries the proportion of weekly+ secular community participation was highest among the never married vs. other marital statuses (e.g., India, Kenya, Mexico, Turkey), and was often low among those who were unemployed (particularly in high-income countries, e.g., Australia, Germany, Japan, Sweden, United States). The country-specific analyses on religious affiliation and race/ethnicity are also reported in Supplementary Tables S1b-S22b.

Sociodemographic variation in religious service attendance: Country-Specific estimates

In the country-specific analyses, weekly+ religious service attendance varied in at least half of the countries for all the sociodemographic factors that we examined, except for immigration status (Supplementary Tables S1b-S22b; Supplementary Figures S1-S34).

A higher proportion of those above age 60 attended religious services weekly+ than younger adults in Australia, Mexico, Poland, Tanzania, and the United States. Conversely, in Israel and Sweden, the youngest group attended more than older people, and in Israel the trend was quite steep (e.g., 45% among the 18 to 24-year-olds vs. 19% among the 80+ year-olds in Israel). In the United Kingdom, the oldest and some of the youngest attended most, while in Kenya the middle-aged attended most. The age patterning was less clear in other countries.

In ten predominantly Christian-majority countries (Argentina, Australia, Brazil, Kenya, Mexico, Philippines, Poland, South Africa, Tanzania, and United States), a higher proportion of women attended weekly+ than men. However, in seven other countries that are mostly Muslim, Jewish, or Hindu societies (Egypt, India, Indonesia, Israel, Japan, Turkey, and United Kingdom), men on average attend services more often than women.

In many countries (e.g., Argentina, Brazil, Hong Kong, Kenya, Mexico, Poland, South Africa, Turkey, and United States), people with less education tended to attend services more. However, in Japan, Tanzania, and United Kingdom those with most education attended more, and in India and Philippines those with the least and most education attended more.

The proportion of weekly+ attendance is higher among the married and the widowed vs. other marital statuses in several countries (e.g., Australia, Brazil, Tanzania, Turkey, United States). Concerning evidence on variation by employment status, weekly+ attendance is often high among the retired and the homeworker, whereas the proportion was often low among those who were unemployed, especially in high-income countries/territories (e.g., Australia, Hong Kong, Israel, United Kingdom, United States). The country-specific estimates for weekly+ service attendance by religious affiliation and race/ethnicity are also reported in Supplementary Tables S1b to S22b.

Additional analysis

The population weighted meta-analysis (Supplementary Table S23) that pooled the country-specific estimates accounting for population sizes in each country yielded largely similar findings as the primary analysis with random effects meta-analysis. One exception was that the estimated proportion of weekly+ religious service attendance was higher in the population weighted meta-analysis vs. the random effects meta-analysis across almost all sociodemographic categories, which might be due to the large weight given to India in the population weighted meta-analysis.

Discussion

In an increasingly fragmented and isolated world, community participation remains an important source of social connection43. Individuals from various sociodemographic backgrounds may engage in different types of communities (e.g., secular and religious communities) to varying extents. Understanding these differences may have important implications for developing tailored, culturally appropriate, and socially inclusive programs that enhance social participation across diverse populations. To our knowledge, the sociodemographic characteristics of people participating in different types of community groups across societies have seldom been compared. This study extends the literature by examining the rates of secular and religious community participation simultaneously across key sociodemographic groups in 22 diverse countries, with the samples weighted to be nationally representative within each country.

This study found that the estimated range for weekly+ religious service attendance across countries (i.e., from 3% in Japan to 87% in Nigeria, an 84% point difference) is much wider than for weekly+ secular community participation (i.e., from 7% in Egypt to 33% in Nigeria, a 26% point difference). Moreover, on average across countries, the proportion of weekly+ religious service attendance was larger than weekly+ secular community participation in nearly every category of every sociodemographic variable that we examined. This indicates that attendance in religious congregations may remain an important avenue for social participation across sociodemographic groups in many countries.

Interestingly, while secular community participation was not patterned by the countries’ economic development levels, there appears to be a negative correlation between the country’s economic develop status and the proportion of people attending religious services weekly+. The broad measure of secular community participation in this study covers a wide range of secular groups (e.g., civic, voluntary, sports, leisure groups). While the rates of some types of secular group participation may be patterned by the country’s economic status (e.g., voter turnout is often higher in economically developed countries72, there may be less clear correlations for other types of community participation (e.g., leisure group participation), leading to a null association overall. The findings on religious service attendance may be understood within the framework of the secularization theory73. Specifically, the theory posits that while religion provides a meaningful avenue for people to understand and find meaning in times of stress and insecurity, people’s demands for religion would decline as societies modernize and material security increases73. Scholars have used similar cross-sectional patterns in other datasets (e.g., the World Values Survey) to support a particular version of the secularization theory74. For instance, the argument of Pippa Norris, Ron Inglehart and others, is that as ‘existential security’ – the possession of good health care, economic provision, and security from violence – increases, each new generation becomes less religious74. Other researcher such as Gill & Lundsgaarde argue that, by undermining individuals’ and communities’ reliance on the religious provision of charity, schooling, and social insurance, “state welfare spending has a detrimental, albeit unintended, effect on long-term religious participation and overall religiosity”75.

Beyond economic development status and existential security, other country-level characteristics, such as the civic, cultural, and historical context of a society, may also contribute to cross-country variations in community participation. For instance, societies with stable democratic institutions, high levels of institutional trust, and strong civic traditions (e.g., Australia, Germany, Spain, Sweden) may foster active citizen engagement in public spheres (e.g., voting) as effective avenues for collective actions76. For another example, people in societies that emphasize self-expression values (e.g., Western Europe, Nordic countries) may tend to engage more in civic and other voluntary organizations, whereas individuals in countries that value traditionalism and close-knit networks (e.g., parts of Africa, Asia, and Latin America) may participate more in faith-based groups77. In addition, religious regulation and historical legacies of state-church relations also influence civic norms, community structures, and institutional trust by different demographic groups, shaping their access to and inclusion in various communities78. It is worth noting that no single factor is likely to fully account for the observed cross-country variations in community participation; rather, the variations may emerge from a dynamic interplay among these macro-level factors and individual-level characteristics, which warrants more nuanced investigation in future studies.

This study also found that, on average across countries, younger people reported more frequent secular community participation, whereas older people reported more frequent religious service attendance, which is consistent with prior evidence6,31. However, the country-specific analyses revealed that the age patterning in both forms of community participation varied widely across countries, thus there does not seem to be a universal secularizing trend. It is, however, worth noting that for both secular and religious group participation, in several countries older adults on average participated more often than young adults and/or middle-aged adults. For instance, in the United States the proportions of weekly+ participation in both secular and religious groups were higher in those aged 70+ than all younger groups in this sample, which echoes prior evidence suggesting that the needs of old-old adults to engage in civic and community life may have been seriously underestimated79. This finding may be understood with Erikson’s theory80 which posits eight stages of psychosocial development across the life course, with older adulthood characterized by the desire to attain a sense that life is meaningful. To this end, for older adults engaging in generative activities in an organizational setting (e.g., formal volunteering, environmental protection associations, civic groups) may confer a deep sense of fulfillment, and for those with religious beliefs returning to religious communities can be a meaningful approach for attaining an integrated sense of identity within the frame of their religious or spiritual beliefs81. This finding may also indicate the need for societal support to reduce structural barriers (e.g., ageism, lack of transportation, challenges due to physical health limitations) for older adults to access community resources and enhance their social engagement. However, we acknowledge that the analyses in this study cannot distinguish between age and cohort effects, thus the findings may be due to differences between cohorts rather than age groups. For example, more recent (vs. older) generations are characterized by longer years of education, delayed family formation, fewer numbers of children, and increased digital media use, which are all important societal tends that may shape people’s social engagement82.

This study provides evidence that the proportion of weekly+ secular group participation was higher among frequent religious service attenders vs. never attenders consistently across all 22 countries. This indicates that, at least under some circumstances, secular and religious groups can be complements rather than competitors (i.e., people may not replace religious community participation with secular group participation. Rather, for many people those who participate in religious groups are more likely to engage in non-religious associations as well). According to Putnam’s Theory of Social Capital43, religious congregations provide a gateway to bridging social networks, bringing people from diverse backgrounds to face-to-face contact, keeping people informed of public affairs, and encouraging engagement in broader community life that creates collective goods (e.g., joining voluntary associations such as environment protection groups, parent-teacher associations, food banks, the Red Cross, etc.), which may all contribute to interpersonal trust and a deeper sense of mutual obligations43,44. Religious communities also often directly sponsor or feed into other forms of community participation apart from regular services, whether through church softball leagues, affiliation groups (e.g., for parents of young children or college students), or community service ventures in partnership with local charities. This is why Putnam argued in his book Bowling Alone that, at least in the United States, “faith communities in which people worship together are arguably the single most important repository of social capital in America”10. Moreover, many of the world’s religious and spiritual traditions emphasize the central importance of community to human life, from the Book of Genesis’s observation, “It is not good for man to be alone” (Gen. 2:18) to Buddhism’s elevation of the spiritual community or “sangha” to the status of one of the “three jewels” of the Way, alongside the Buddha and his Dharma83. While causality cannot be inferred based on cross-sectional data in this study, the findings provide suggestive evidence that religious communities may be important stakeholders as well as potential partners in convening and supporting efforts to enhance social participation in many countries.

This study also found that on average across countries the proportion of weekly+ secular group participation increased with years of education, whereas weekly+ religious service attendance decreased with education. The finding on secular group participation is consistent with prior literature suggesting that the rates of formal volunteering and civic participation increase with educational attainment, at least in wealthy Western countries7,8,31. Some researchers hypothesized that education increases civic and voluntary participation directly by teaching specific knowledge and skills and by fostering a sense of civic responsibility, whereas other researchers argued that educational attainment is a proxy for socioeconomic status, which is more directly related with access to and resources for civic and voluntary participation7. With regard to evidence on religious community participation, earlier studies suggest an overall negative correlation between educational attainment and religious service attendance across countries, but the direction of the correlation was reversed in the United States35,36. In our sample (data collected recently in 2022–2023), religious service attendance decreases with higher educational attainment in most countries, even including the United States. Prior researchers posited that a key mechanism whereby modernization may be associated with a decline in religion is through “the growth of education, science, and a technological worldview”35. For example, education provides opportunities for developing activist ideologies and rationalized worldviews (e.g., rationalism and scientific naturalism), which may weaken traditional religious views35. Education may also confer greater material, political, and social security for individuals, leading to less dependence on religious communities to provide instrumental and psychosocial support74.

It is worth noting that contrary to what the secularization theory might predict35, in this sample the unemployed participants had lower rates of participation in both secular and religious communities than the employed participants. This may indicate that basic financial security (e.g., food security, safe housing, access to transportation) is necessary for any form of community participation. Moreover, in this sample unemployment appeared to affect community participation particularly severely in high-income countries. For instance, in the United States the proportion of weekly+ secular and religious group participation was 8 and 16% points higher among the self-employed vs. the unemployed. We speculate that in wealthy and more individualistic countries people emphasize personal achievement84, thus career challenges and financial difficulties are often associated with stigma, loneliness, and a low sense of self-worth. The competitive job market and high living cost in wealthy countries may intensify these challenges, which may all negatively influence people’s capacities for social participation. These findings altogether suggest that policies and programs targeting at reducing structural barriers for vulnerable social groups (e.g., job training and placement services, unemployment insurance) may be important for enhancing social participation among marginalized groups and for promoting population equity in access to community resources.

Our findings on variation in community participation across demographic groups can be further understood through the lens of evolutionary behavioral theories, which propose that human behaviors are shaped by evolutionary pressures aimed at enhancing survival, reproduction, and group cohesion85. Community groups are one of the oldest and most widespread expressions of human sociality86, and they likely have persisted because they foster social solidarity, shared emotional experiences through communal rituals, and a sense of meaning and belonging, among other goods87,88. The Life History Theory posited that people allocate their limited time and resources across competing life goals, which tend to vary across different stages of life89. For young adults, major life tasks often include establishing social status, expanding social connections, and seeking romantic partners. Secular communities often provide flexible and open environments where individuals can explore diverse identities, and demonstrate autonomy, leadership, and abilities to navigate existential uncertainty, all contributing to building social status and perceived attractiveness90,91. These social and status-related motivations may be particularly pronounced for men and single individuals, who often face greater pressure in mate competition and social positioning91. In comparison, older adults tend to prioritize stability, cohesion, and transmission of cultural and generational values89. To this end, religious communities, with their structured practices, established norms, and emphasis on continuity, may better fulfill these later-life priorities. Additionally, the Costly Signaling Theory further suggests that community activities, such as those involving activism, public discourse, and costly religious rituals (e.g., fasting, dietary restriction, pilgrimage), often require effortful demonstrations of conviction and commitment92. These costly signals can enhance social standing, reputation, and trust within communities, reinforcing bonding and group identity93–95.

This study has several limitations. First, the Global Flourishing Study included only single-item measures that broadly assessed participation in both secular and religious groups, which may not capture the diversity and specificity of participation. Therefore, due to the lack of data we cannot examine different subtypes of secular (e.g., political groups, volunteering, leisure groups) or religious groups (e.g., worship services, bible study groups, meditation and contemplating gatherings) separately. Individuals from different sociodemographic, cultural, and religious backgrounds may participate in these group activities to different extents. For example, within secular communities, younger adults may be inclined to participate in advocacy-oriented groups, whereas older adults may tend to engage more in service-based communities96,97. Disaggregating specific types of community groups may facilitate a more nuanced understanding of the diverse patterns of participation across social groups. Second, with the drastic increase in digital media use especially among younger generations, new forms of communities such as online communities have become increasingly common. However, there have been growing concerns over unequal access to digital technologies and disparities in digital literacy across sociodemographic groups98. These gaps may give rise to new forms of inequalities in community participation through differential capacities of engagement in digital communities, which warrants further understanding. Third, the findings on ranked proportions of community participation by country need to be interpreted cautiously, due to methodological complexities (e.g. survey translation, survey mode, and seasonality in data collection) in cross-cultural survey research. Fourth, there are other important sociodemographic factors (e.g., neighborhood conditions, community cohesion) that may shape people’s community participation99, which warrants further investigation. These limitations are, however, balanced by important strengths of this study. To our knowledge, this is the first study that examined secular and religious community participation simultaneously across sociodemographic groups, using data from multiple countries. It would be worthwhile to leverage future waves of data from the Global Flourishing Study when they become available, to investigate temporal changes in community participation across societies, along with its predictors and implications for population well-being.

Participation in community groups- both secular and religious - can promote individual and communal flourishing alike, whether by increasing individual subjective wellbeing, health, or economic mobility, or by promoting the strength of the social fabric and the efficacy of civic institutions3,4,10. This study provides novel insights into the sociocultural influences on community participation and helps inform the sociodemographic groups for whom culturally appropriate and socially inclusive programs for enhancing different forms of community participation may be most meaningful. The findings also highlight the importance of addressing structural factors for promoting social participation, which may be associated with a more equitable distribution of population well-being and greater collective flourishing.
