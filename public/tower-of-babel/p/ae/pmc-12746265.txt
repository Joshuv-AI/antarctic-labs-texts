Beyond the first course: Re-irradiation practices in Ontario — Insights from a provincial survey☆
Authors: Brian Liszewski, Timothy P Hanna, Nareesa Ishmail, Kyle Malkoske, Laura D’Alimonte, Jason Pantarotto, Eric Gutierrez, Julie Kraus, Angelica Ramprashad, Kristin Berry
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Re-irradiation is increasingly common as patients live longer and often receive care across multiple treatment centres.•A provincial survey revealed wide variation in re-irradiation practices, including inconsistent use of formal policies and protocols.•Re-irradiation workflows vary across centres, including differences in team roles, review timing, and dose summation methods.•Findings highlight the importance of standardizing assessment protocols, improving access to treatment history, and strengthening collaboration across disciplines.•The study supports continued efforts toward consensus-building, system integration, and quality improvement in re-irradiation planning and delivery.

Introduction and background

In the province of Ontario, Canada, radiation therapy is delivered through a publicly funded, distributed system comprising 15 Regional Cancer Centres (RCCs). These centres vary in their size and complexity, ranging from large academic institutions to smaller community-based programs. Ontario Health (Cancer Care Ontario)’s Radiation Treatment Program (RTP) coordinates these centres, promoting consistency, collaboration, and continuous quality improvement across the province.

Re-irradiation, defined as delivering additional radiation to a previously treated site, has become increasingly relevant due to rising cancer survivorship and the expanded use of stereotactic and targeted therapies, especially in patients with oligometastatic or oligoprogressive disease [10,12]. Throughout this manuscript, the term “re-irradiation” is used broadly to refer to patients receiving more than one course of radiation therapy. This includes cases aligning with ESTRO/EORTC definitions of Type 1 (overlapping volumes) and Type 2 (non-overlapping volumes with cumulative toxicity risk) Andratschke et al. [1], as well as repeat organ irradiation that may not involve volume overlap or expected toxicity concerns. While re-irradiation offers potential for meaningful disease control, re-irradiation introduces significant clinical complexity. Challenges include evaluating cumulative dose, respecting organ-at-risk tolerances, and balancing tumor control with the risk of late toxicity, including necrosis, fistula, or loss of function [5,7]. Moreover, uncertainties remain about the extent of normal tissue recovery between treatment courses [8].

Contemporary planning technologies such as deformable image registration and EQD2 modeling offer clinicians tools to assess and plan re-irradiation with greater confidence [3,4]. However, dosimetric evaluation alone does not ensure clinical safety. Consistent application of institutional policies, interdisciplinary assessment frameworks, and structured workflows remain essential to support complex decision-making [6,9]. Yet, international data continue to highlight heterogeneity in practice, including variations in re-irradiation definitions, timing, thresholds, and documentation [11].

In Ontario, a re-irradiation-related incident reported in 2022 to the RTP’s Radiation Incident Safety Committee (RISC) underscored these concerns. Subsequent review identified fragmented access to historical treatment data and the lack of standardised assessment protocols as key contributors. This incident catalyzed a province-wide response. The RTP convened a knowledge-sharing symposium and launched a provincial survey to characterize current re-irradiation practices and identify opportunities to strengthen policy, safety, and workflow integration

The aim of this study is to evaluate re-irradiation practices across Ontario’s cancer centres, identify areas of variation and strength, and inform the development of provincial guidance to support consistent and safe care delivery.

Methods

Current state summary of re-irradiation

To reflect the real-world complexity of clinical practice, this review included all patients with more than one course of radiation therapy, whether or not they met formal ESTRO/EORTC Type 1 or Type 2 re-irradiation criteria.

To evaluate the current landscape of re-irradiation practices, data were sourced from Ontario Health (Cancer Care Ontario)’s Quality Based Procedure (QBP) protocols. The Quality-Based Procedure (QBP) model in Ontario links funding to standardized radiation treatment protocols, enabling detailed tracking of patient care trends and supporting equitable, evidence-based practice across the province [2]. This dataset provided insight into the utilization of radiation therapy within the province.

To better understand the frequency and complexity of multiple radiation therapy courses, QBP data from the 2022/23 fiscal year were analyzed in conjunction with 25 years of historical Activity Level Reporting (ALR) data. This approach enabled a province-wide view of the prevalence of reirradiation, evolving complexity of techniques and those patients who received radiation therapy at more than one centre over their care continuum.

Survey of re-irradiation practice

In addition to leveraging provincial data, the RTP also performed a structured provincial survey (Appendix I) to capture insights into current practices and opportunities for improvement related to re-irradiation procedures. The survey was designed via a working group organized by the RTP to explore provincial re-irradiation practices and develop a symposium for knowledge translation and exchange based on these findings. The working group was composed of three co-chairs representing medical physics (KM), radiation oncology (TPH) and radiation therapy (LD) as well as members of the RTP (BL, JP, EG, JK, AR, KB). Designed with 22 targeted questions, the survey focused on key areas such as existing policies, methods for gathering treatment history, dose summation techniques, and the timing of re-irradiation assessments.

Results and discussion

Re-irradiation trends in Ontario

Provincial activity level reporting data over the past 15 years show an increasing number of patients receiving multiple courses of radiation therapy (Fig. 1). While this trend reflects the increasing complexity of care, not all additional treatment courses meet formal ESTRO/EORTC criteria for re-irradiation. This confirms that re-irradiation is becoming more common across the province. In 2022/23, approximately one-third of metastatic radiation treatments were delivered using stereotactic techniques, reflecting a trend toward more complex, high-precision approaches. Compounding this complexity, at least 4 % of patients had previously received radiation at a different RCC, underscoring the growing frequency of inter-institutional care. As re-irradiation becomes more prevalent, complex, and geographically distributed, coordinated provincial systems are essential to manage treatment histories, support dose summation, and ensure patient safety. A consistent provincial process is also needed to ensure centres have timely access to accurate treatment records and maintain standardized planning and documentation, regardless of where prior care was delivered. As re-irradiation becomes a routine part of cancer care, shared evaluation frameworks and supporting infrastructure will be key to sustaining quality and safety across centres.

Policy infrastructure and institutional readiness

The survey highlighted variability in re-irradiation policies across Ontario's RCCs, echoing the findings of Willman et al (2024) in their international patterns of care survey. Even among centres with established policies, their application in clinical practice varied, highlighting a lack of standardization within and between institutions. Barriers to consistent implementation included staffing and time limitations (33 %), difficulty in developing a universally applicable process (33 %), and perceptions that a formal policy was unnecessary or redundant with existing workflows (22 %). Some centres noted they were using informal processes while formal procedures were still in development, and all centres with established re-irradiation policies expressed interest in sharing their resources with others. The variation in policy use, along with strong interest in sharing resources, points to an opportunity to develop practical, shared guidelines that support safer and more consistent re-irradiation across centres.

Workflow and assessment practices

Most centres reported that re-irradiation assessments are triggered during plan review (87 %), with fewer initiating them at earlier points such as planning (60 %) or pre-simulation (33 %). Radiation oncologists were universally involved (100 %), while medical physicists participated in most centres (87 %). However, the involvement of dosimetrists and radiation therapists was more variable. Several centres noted that assessments were often informal or left to clinical discretion. This variation underscores the need for clearer, standardized workflows to ensure timely and consistent interprofessional collaboration in re-irradiation planning. As a first step in response to this need, Ontario Health (Cancer Care Ontario) is supporting a thoracic re-irradiation working group to explore body region specific assessment and planning practices. This interprofessional initiative, intentionally ensuring representation from radiation oncology, medical physics and radiation therapy, will inform broader alignment efforts across tumour sites.

Data access and historical treatment integration

Most centres identify prior radiation through patient history (87 %) and internal electronic medical records (EMRs) or planning systems (73 %), with additional verification occurring at CT simulation (20 %) or during multidisciplinary rounds (7 %). When patients had been treated at another RCC, treatment records were requested in only 27 % of cases, a process applied inconsistently and often dependent on clinical urgency or preference.

With respect to retrieving external records, only 54 % of centres considered their processes robust. Although prior treatment documents were often requested, workflows were inconsistent, and 60 % of centres reported at least occasionally proceeding without complete data. These findings highlight a broader system issue: reliable, interoperable record retrieval is essential for identifying prior treatment and reducing the risk of unintended re-irradiation and its associated long-term harms.

Recognizing the risks associated with incomplete treatment histories, Ontario Health is pursuing multiple system-level strategies to improve cross-institutional data access. These include reviewing legislative frameworks to support verification of prior radiation at other centres. In addition, developing a centralized treatment plan repository to ensure long-term access to historical treatment data. A provincial contact list has also been established to streamline inter-centre coordination when retrieving external records.

Technical approaches and dosimetric evaluation

Planning practices for re-irradiation varied across Ontario centres, particularly in how the dosimetric impact of prior treatment is evaluated. While 80 % reported having a standard process to combine previous and current plans, there was wide variation in dose tolerances, image registration methods, and calculation approaches. Workflow inconsistencies and unclear interprofessional roles further contributed to local variation, underscoring the importance of coordinated team-based planning—a principle echoed by Murray et al. [9]. These findings align with international observations from Chargari et al. [4] and Willmann et al. [11], who noted similar variability in assessment and planning.

To address these challenges, Ontario Health (Cancer Care Ontario) continues to monitor and engage with national and international consensus efforts, including those led by the Reirradiation Collaborative Group (ReCOG), ESTRO/EORTC, ASTRO, AAPM, and COMP. Aligning with these emerging frameworks will support the development of consistent, evidence-informed approaches to re-irradiation planning and strengthen quality and safety across provincial practice.

Conclusions

This provincial survey revealed substantial variation in re-irradiation policy, workflow, data access, and planning approaches across Ontario’s cancer centres. These inconsistencies, spanning policy to practice, pose risks to treatment quality and safety, particularly as re-irradiation becomes more prevalent, complex, and geographically distributed.

While advances in technology have enhanced the ability to plan and deliver re-irradiation, these capabilities must be supported by clear processes, shared tools, and collaborative decision-making. Establishing standardized guidelines, improving access to historical treatment data, and strengthening interprofessional approaches are essential next steps.

Ongoing efforts, such as the thoracic re-irradiation pilot, will inform future training and infrastructure needs. Sustained collaboration across RCCs is key to closing practice gaps, ensuring a high-quality, coordinated, and person-centred approach to re-irradiation in Ontario.
