OVATE family protein 6 interacts with CONSTANS-LIKE 3 to regulate bolting by transcriptional control of FLOWERING LOCUS T/TERMINAL FLOWER 1 family genes in lettuce
Authors: Guanghui An, Ruoqing Liu, Xinhui Zhao, Yueji Wang, Chenxi Li, Lei Zhu, Luming Yang, Peinan Sun, Yong Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAs one of the most globally cultivated vegetables, lettuce’s commercial yield is severely compromised by premature bolting, an undesirable agronomic trait that diminishes crop quality and market value. In this study, we genetically cloned the OVATE family protein 6 (LsOFP6) controlling bolting time in lettuce. LsOFP6 had a nonsense mutation in the late bolting parent. CRISPR/Cas9 knockout and complementary tests confirmed that LsOFP6 positively regulates bolting in lettuce. LsOFP6 physically interacts with the CONSTANS-LIKE 3 (LsCOL3). Genetic analyses suggest that LsOFP6 regulates bolting through an LsCOL3-dependent pathway in the Lsblh2 Lskn1 genotype background, where we have previously established that LsBLH2 and LsKN1, the positive and negative transcriptional regulators of bolting, respectively, both interact with LsOFP6 and exert their functions via the gibberellin pathway. LsCOL3 represses bolting development by directly binding to the promoters of TWIN SISTER OF FLOWERING LOCUS T (LsTSF1) and TERMINAL FLOWER 1 (LsTFL1), downregulating the former while upregulating the latter. These regulations are antagonized by LsOFP6, which promotes bolting by compromising the effects of LsCOL3 on LsTSF1 and LsTFL1. Therefore, loss of function of Lsofp6 decreases LsTSF1 and increases LsTFL1 expression, resulting in late bolting. In summary, the LsOFP6–LsCOL3 module regulates lettuce bolting development by controlling the transcript levels of LsTSF1 and LsTFL1 genes. This study deepens the molecular understanding of bolting development and provides valuable genetic resources and novel strategies for developing lettuce with improved resistance to premature bolting, highlighting the power of natural variation to address agricultural challenges.

Introduction

Lettuce (Lactuca sativa) is a globally significant vegetable crop and is primarily cultivated for its edible rosette leaves. Lettuce has also been genetically modified to serve as a bioreactor for producing protein-based therapeutics, including high-value pharmaceutical proteins and edible vaccines [1]. Premature bolting, an undesirable agronomic trait, severely limits vegetative biomass accumulation, impairs crop quality, and leads to yield reduction, thereby causing considerable economic losses in lettuce production [2, 3]. As a major challenge facing both growers and the vegetable industry, elucidating the genetic components and regulatory networks underlying bolting development is essential for breeding lettuce varieties with enhanced resistance to this phenomenon.

OVATE family proteins (OFPs) function as plant-specific transcriptional regulators distinguished by a conserved C-terminal OVATE domain. They play pivotal roles in diverse aspects of plant growth and development process, such as plant architecture, organ size, and fruit shape [4–9]. For instance, AtOFP1 overexpression suppresses cell elongation, resulting in rounded seeds and curled leaves in Arabidopsis [10]; OsOFP8/9/14 integrate multiple hormonal pathways to regulate key traits in rice by interacting with partners like Grain Shape Gene on Chromosome 9 (GS9) or DWARF AND LOW-TILLERING (DLT) [6, 11]. OFPs interact with TONNEAU1 Recruiting Motif proteins (TRMs) to impact organ shape along both the mediolateral and proximo-distal axes of tomato growth [9]. A central mechanism underlying OFP function is their interaction with three-amino-acid-loop-extension homeodomain proteins (TALEs) [including BELL-like homeodomain (BLHs) and KNOTTED1-like homeobox (KNOXs) families], forming a critical regulatory network for plant development [12]. Our earlier work established that LsOFP6 affects bolting via the LsBLH2- and LsKN1-mediated transcriptional control of gibberellin (GA) biosynthesis and signaling genes [2, 3]. However, it remains unclear whether LsOFP6 regulates bolting solely through the LsBLH2- and LsKN1-mediated GA pathway. Additionally, if LsOFP6 influences bolting via alternative mechanisms, the underlying genetic and molecular basis of such pathways has yet to be elucidated.

The CCT (CONSTANS, CO-like, and TOC1) gene family represents a crucial class of transcriptional regulators in plants, playing multifaceted roles in the control of flowering time, growth and development, and responses to biotic and abiotic stresses [13–16]. Among these, the CO-like genes (COLs) form a highly conserved subfamily across both monocots and dicots, serving as central regulators of bolting and flowering [17, 18]. COLs are distinguished by the presence of one or two B-box zinc finger domains at their N-terminus, which facilitate interactions between proteins, and a C-terminal CCT domain implicated in both protein interaction and nuclear localization, with potential DNA-binding activity [18, 19]. COLs integrate environmental cues, particularly photoperiod, into developmental programs, thereby optimizing bolting and flowering time [20]. Their conserved structure and functional significance across plant species underscore their importance in adaptation and crop improvement. However, COLs have not yet been characterized in lettuce, and the precise biochemical mechanisms through which COLs control the expression of their target genes are still not well understood.

The FLOWERING LOCUS T/TERMINAL FLOWER 1 (FT/TFL1) family genes, encoding phosphatidylethanolamine-binding proteins (PEBPs), serve as integrators for signals from various flowering pathways and play a crucial role in the transition from vegetative growth to reproductive development in many crop species, exhibiting substantial functional diversity in flowering regulation [21–23]. In Arabidopsis, the FT/TFL1 family includes both flowering promoters, such as FT, TWIN SISTER OF FT (TSF), and MOTHER OF FT AND TFL1 (MFT), and flowering repressors like TFL1, Arabidopsis thaliana CENTRORADIALIS HOMOLOG (ATC), and BROTHER OF FT AND TFL1 (BFT). FT, the well-characterized ‘florigen’, is synthesized in leaves under photoperiodic induction and translocated to the shoot apical meristem (SAM) to promote flowering [24]. In contrast, TFL1 acts as a local floral inhibitor expressed in the SAM [25]. Interestingly, the functions of FT and TFL1 have diverged evolutionarily, leading to species-specific roles in regulating flowering and bolting processes and other biological development processes [26, 27]. Despite this broad understanding, the FT/TFL1 gene family has not yet been fully characterized in lettuce, and its related mechanisms remain largely unclear in this crop.

In angiosperms, the floral transition and the molecular mechanisms driving the bolting–flowering process are coordinated by a sophisticated regulatory network that integrates diverse endogenous and environmental signals to ensure optimal reproductive timing [20, 28–30]. Despite extensive research in model plants, which has identified over 100 genes involved in bolting and flowering development control [28, 31–33], the key genetic factors, their precise functions, and the overarching regulatory network governing bolting and flowering in lettuce are still largely elusive. Limited numbers of genes have been identified to play a role in controlling bolting and flowering in lettuce. For instance, A DELLA protein LsRGL1 decreases the expression of LsGA3ox and LsYUC4 and thus negatively regulates bolting and flowering in lettuce [34]. Auxin response factor 3 upregulates LsCO to mediate thermally induced transition of vegetative-to-reproductive phase in lettuce [35]. Moreover, in our previous study, we characterized two key genes (LsBLH2 and LsKN1) regulating bolting time in lettuce, and demonstrated that LsBLH2 and LsKN1 transcription factors act as positive and negative regulators, respectively, forming a core module that coordinately controls bolting development through the gibberellin biosynthesis and signaling pathway [2, 3]. However, to date, only these two genes, LsBLH2 and LsKN1, have been identified through forward genetics and are known to carry natural variations suitable for breeding bolting resistance. These limited genetic resources remain far from sufficient for effective bolting-resistance breeding in lettuce. Therefore, it is essential to clone additional bolting-regulatory genes harboring natural variations and to elucidate their precise regulatory mechanisms and molecular foundations governing the entire bolting process.

Results

Construction of a segregating population for the bolting time

To investigate the genetic basis of bolting regulation in lettuce, we generated an F2 population through hybridization between an early- and a late-bolting cultivar (Fig. S1A, B). This F2 population, consisting of 201 individuals, exhibited a continuous variation in bolting time, confirming that bolting time is a quantitative trait in lettuce (Fig. S1C). To reduce the trait complexity within the F2 population and to facilitate the genetic cloning of the key gene governing bolting time, F2 plants were self-pollinated for three to four generations, resulting in the establishment of F4:5 and F5:6 families. We assessed the bolting time, measured as the number of days after sowing (DAS) until bolting, in individuals derived from these F4:5 and F5:6 families, and an F5:6 family named LB356 showed either early (59–68 DAS) or late (79–91 DAS) bolting, with no intermediate phenotypes (Fig. 1A; Table S1). Within the LB356 family, comprising 196 individuals, 141 exhibited early bolting, while 55 displayed late bolting (Fig. 1A–C; Table S1). This distribution of bolting time followed a 3:1 ratio, consistent with Mendelian inheritance (χ2 = 0.98, P > 0.05), indicating that bolting time in this family is governed by a single locus and that the allele responsible for late bolting is recessive.

We applied BSR-seq to pinpoint the genetic region responsible for bolting time variation in the LB356 family. Analysis of the sequencing data identified a distinct peak on chromosome 2, which we designated as Lettuce Bolting Locus 3 (LBL3) (Fig. 1D). A linked marker, LB3M07, was developed in the vicinity of LBL3 and showed complete co-segregation with the bolting time phenotype across all 196 progenies in the LB356 family (Fig. 1E, Table S2). Consequently, we established a population in which the late/early bolting time trait is governed solely by the LBL3.

Fine-mapping and cloning of the candidate gene for the bolting time trait

For the genetic fine-mapping and cloning of the LBL3 gene, we screened 1600 progenies from the LB356 family using two flanking markers, LB3M03 (94.42 Mb) and LB3M06 (135.84 Mb), and identified 75 recombinants (Fig. 1E). By genotyping these recombinants with multiple markers derived from the candidate region, the candidate gene was narrowed down to a 966-Kb interval on chromosome 2, located between LB3M22 (119.384 Mb) and LB3M29 (120.350 Mb) (Fig. 1E). Within this candidate interval, 13 genes were predicted (Fig. 1F). Analysis of function, sequence variation, and transcript level were subsequently conducted for these 13 genes (Table S3). The result indicated that LG2191590, an OVATE family protein 6 ortholog, has a single nucleotide polymorphism in the coding sequence between the alleles from two parents (Fig. 1G). We named LG2191590 LsOFP6. Compared to the LsOFP6 allele from the early bolting parent, the allele from the late bolting parent had a guanine (G) to thymine (T) mutation at the 352nd base in the coding sequence, forming a stop codon ‘TAA’, which led to premature termination of translation of LsOFP6 and a truncated protein with an impaired OVATE domain. We named the mutant allele Lsofp6 (Fig. 1G). The nonsense mutation in Lsofp6 co-segregated with the bolting time trait across all 1600 progenies of the LB356 family. Interestingly, our prior research has demonstrated that LsOFP6 participates in regulating lettuce bolting development by interacting with LsBLH2 (BELL1-like homeobox protein) and LsKN1 (knotted-1-like homeobox family protein) [2, 3]. Thus, LsOFP6 represents a strong candidate gene responsible for regulating bolting time in the LB356 family.

The expression levels of the LsOFP6 gene were measured across the rosette stage, vegetative-to-reproductive transition stage, bolting stage, and flowering stage to characterize its temporal expression profile in LB3-early plants (LsOFP6/LsOFP6). The expression level of LsOFP6 showed an increasing trend from the transition stage, peaked at the bolting stage, and subsequently declined during the flowering stage (Fig. 1H). To characterize the spatial expression of LsOFP6, its transcript levels were examined across leaf, shoot apex, root, seed, stem, and flower tissues. The leaf, apex, and root tissues were from the rosette stage, and the stem and flower tissues were from the bolting and flowering stage. The results indicated that LsOFP6 was predominantly expressed in leaves, stems, and apexes while showing minimal expression in flowers, seeds, and roots (Fig. 1H). Subsequently, we investigated the subcellular localization of LsOFP6 by introducing an LsOFP6-GFP fusion gene into lettuce. The result confirmed that LsOFP6 localizes to the nucleus in lettuce cells (Fig. 1I).

Knockout and complementation tests verified that LsOFP6 positively controls bolting in lettuce

A specific sgRNA targeting LsOFP6 was designed within the coding sequence of its OVATE domain to disrupt this domain in LB3-early plants using the CRISPR/Cas9 (Fig. 2A). We obtained two knockout alleles: LsOFP6-KO#5, which carries a 17-nucleotide deletion, and LsOFP6-KO#6, containing an 11-nucleotide deletion (Fig. 2A). These deletions in LsOFP6-KO#5 and #6 result in premature stop codons at amino acid residues 127 and 129, respectively, producing truncated LsOFP6 proteins with an impaired OVATE domain (Fig. 2B). As anticipated, the phenotype of the homozygous knockout shifted from early to late bolting (Fig. 2C and D). This confirms that LsOFP6 promotes bolting and that disruption of its OVATE domain leads to delayed bolting, as observed in LB3-late plants.

The full LsOFP6 allele, comprising a 2000-bp promoter, coding sequence, and a 500-bp downstream sequence, was also introduced into the LB3-late plants. Two positive transformants, designated LsOFP6-COM#5 and #6, were obtained (Fig. 2E). Both complementary lines exhibited earlier bolting compared to the recipient LB3-late plants (Fig. 2D and E). We also overexpressed LsOFP6 in the LB3-late plants, and the LsOFP6-OX#1 line also exhibited earlier bolting compared to the recipient (Fig. 2D–F). These results further support the role of LsOFP6 in promoting the transition from vegetative growth to reproductive development in lettuce.

LsOFP6-mediated bolting regulation is independent of GA in the Lsblh2 Lskn1 background

Based on our previous findings that LsOFP6 regulates lettuce bolting by interacting with LsBLH2 and LsKN1 to modulate gibberellin (GA) biosynthesis, we genotyped the LB356 family for these two genes. The results revealed that the LB356 family fixed a homozygous loss-of-function allele for LsBLH2 (Lsblh2) and a homozygous inactive allele for LsKN1 (Lskn1). This genetic analysis indicates that LsOFP6 regulates bolting in an LsBLH2- and LsKN1-independent manner in the LB356 family with genetic background Lsblh2 Lskn1.

Furthermore, we quantified GA levels at multiple developmental stages in both LB3-early (LsOFP6) and LB3-late (Lsofp6) plants. No significant difference in GA content was detected between the LB3-early and LB3-late plants at any stage (Fig. S2A–H). Consistent with these results, the expression of key GA biosynthesis/catabolism genes (LsGA20ox1, LsGA2ox8, and LsGA2ox4) also showed no significant variation across the stages assayed (Fig. S2I–T). These results collectively demonstrated that LsOFP6 regulates bolting through a GA-independent pathway in the LB356 family with genetic background Lsblh2 Lskn1.

LsOFP6 physically interacts with LsCOL3

We then explored whether LsOFP6 regulates bolting development through interacting with other proteins in lettuce. To identify potential interacting partners, a yeast two-hybrid (Y2H) screening was conducted with a lettuce cDNA library. This screen identified 20 candidate proteins (Table S4), including a CONSTANS-like (COL) protein (gene identifier: LG5518041), which belongs to the known flowering time regulator family, CCT (CONSTANS, CO-like, and TOC1) family. The lettuce genome contains 14 COL genes, while Arabidopsis has 17 [17]. A phylogenetic tree was constructed for all 31 COL proteins from lettuce and Arabidopsis, using BBX20 (AT4G39070) as an outgroup for comparison (Figs 3A and S3). According to the phylogenetic tree, LG2255123 is orthologous to AtCO (AT5G15840), AtCOL1 (AT5G15850), and AtCOL2 (AT3G02380); LG5518041, LG8728102, and LG5487786 are orthologous to AtCOL3/4 (AT2G24790, AT5G24930); LG3290364 and LG7632711 are orthologous to AtCOL6/16 (AT1G68520, AT1G25440); LG9775456, LG5497722, and LG7619064 are orthologous to AtCOL9/10 (AT3G07650, AT5G48250); LG4403319 is orthologous to AtCOL11/12 (AT4G15250, AT3G21880); LG9797271 is orthologous to AtCOL13 (AT2G47890); and LG8721313 is orthologous to AtCOL14/15 (AT2G33500, AT1G28050) (Figs 3A and S3). For convenience, we hereafter refer to LG5518041 as LsCOL3.

We next examined the spatial expression pattern of the LsCOL3 gene by measuring the transcript levels of the LsCOL3 gene in different tissues from LB3-early, LB3-late, and LsOFP6-KO plants. The results revealed no significant difference in LsCOL3 expression among the LB3-early (LsOFP6), LB3-late (Lsofp6), and LsOFP6-KO plants, indicating that LsOFP6 does not affect the expression of LsCOL3 (Fig. 3B). LsCOL3 was predominantly expressed in leaves, apexes, and stems, with minimal expression observed in other tissues, indicating that LsCOL3 has a similar expression pattern to LsOFP6 (Figs 3B and 1H). We also examined the temporal expression pattern of LsCOL3, and the result showed that LsCOL3 has a similar temporal expression pattern to LsOFP6 (Fig. 3C and 1H). Then, we identified the subcellular localization of LsCOL3 through transiently expressing the fusion gene LsCOL3-GFP in Nicotiana benthamiana leaves using Agrobacterium-mediated transformation. The result showed that LsCOL3 is co-localized with LsOFP6 at the nuclei (Fig. 3D and 1I).

We used the Y2H system to further confirm the interaction between LsOFP6 and LsCOL3 (Fig. 3E). Then, we applied the BiFC assay by co-expressing 35S:LsOFP6-eYFPC155 and 35S:LsCOL3-eYFPN173 in N. benthamiana leaf cells, and the result confirmed the LsOFP6–LsCOL3 protein interaction in vivo (Fig. 3F). The results from both Y2H and BiFC assays consistently demonstrated a physical interaction between LsOFP6 and LsCOL3, suggesting that LsOFP6 may regulate lettuce bolting through its association with LsCOL3.

LsOFP6 and LsCOL3 synergistically regulate bolting development

We therefore investigated the role of LsCOL3 in regulating bolting development, as well as the genetic relationship between LsOFP6 and LsCOL3 in this process. We first knocked out LsCOL3 in LB3-early (LsOFP6 LsCOL3) and LB3-late (Lsofp6 LsCOL3) plants to create LsOFP6 Lscol3 and Lsofp6 Lscol3 genotype plants, respectively (Fig. 3G). Interestingly, the results showed that the bolting time has no significant difference between LsOFP6 Lscol3 and Lsofp6 Lscol3 (both show early bolting), but the bolting time in Lsofp6 LsCOL3 plants is significantly later than that in LsOFP6 LsCOL3 plants (Fig. 3G and H). Therefore, LsOFP6 positively regulates bolting in a LsCOL3-dependent manner. In addition, the bolting time has no significant difference between LsOFP6 LsCOL3 and LsOFP6 Lscol3 (both show early bolting), while the bolting time in Lsofp6 LsCOL3 plants is significantly later than that in Lsofp6 Lscol3 plants (Fig. 3G and H). Therefore, LsCOL3 negatively regulates bolting and relies on LsOFP6 in a dominant epistatic manner.

The above genetic analysis results indicate that LsOFP6 positively regulates bolting in a LsCOL3-dependent manner (i.e. LsOFP6 relies on LsCOL3 in a recessive epistatic manner), while LsCOL3 negatively regulates bolting, but this effect requires dysfunctional LsOFP6 (i.e. LsCOL3 relies on LsOFP6 in a dominant epistatic manner).

LsCOL3 downregulates LsTSF1 and upregulates LsTFL1 genes to repress bolting development

To explore the molecular mechanisms through which LsCOL3 regulates bolting development, DNA affinity purification sequencing (DAP-seq) was performed to identify genomic regions directly bound by the LsCOL3 transcription factor. Preliminary data analysis revealed LG2192922 and LG2241145 as candidate genes potentially regulated by the LsCOL3 transcription factor in a direct manner. Both LG2192922 and LG2241145 encode FT/TFL1 family genes (Fig. 4A). Further analysis showed that both the lettuce and the Arabidopsis genomes encode six FT/TFL1 family genes. A phylogenetic tree was constructed by using the 12 FT/TFL1 proteins encoded by the lettuce and Arabidopsis genomes, with the STEPMOTHER OF FT AND TFL1 (SMFT, AT5G01300) as the outgroup (Fig. 4A). According to the phylogenetic tree, LG2192922 is orthologous to FT (AT1G65480) and TSF (AT4G20370); LG2241145 and LG8762796 are orthologous to TFL1 (AT5G03840) (Fig. 4A). LG2192922 exhibits 63.8% amino acid identity to FT and 73.7% to TSF (Figs S4 and S5). We refer to LG2192922 as LsTSF1 and LG2241145 as LsTFL1.

Notably, COLs act as transcriptional regulators of FT/TFL1 family genes by binding to their promoters, as demonstrated in previous studies [36–38]. Thus, we propose that LsCOL3 interacts with the promoters of LsTSF1 and LsTFL1, modulating their expression to regulate bolting in lettuce. To evaluate this, yeast one-hybrid (Y1H) assay and electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA) were conducted. Both assays demonstrated that LsCOL3 binds to the promoter regions of LsTSF1 and LsTFL1 genes (Fig. 4B–E). Indeed, the sequences of P2-b and P4-a probes from the LsTSF1 and LsTFL1 promoter, respectively, contain the ‘CCACA’ and ‘CCAAT’ motifs, which are potential binding motifs of COLs (Fig. 4B and C) [36, 37].

We further employed a dual-luciferase assay to examine how LsCOL3 influences the regulation of LsTSF1 and LsTFL1 (Fig. S6). The data revealed that the LUC enzyme activity in the combination ‘35S:LsCOL3 + LsTSF1-Pro:LUC’ was significantly reduced compared to that in the control ‘35S:GFP + LsTSF1-Pro:LUC’ (Fig. 4F). Conversely, the LUC enzyme activity in the combination ‘35S:LsCOL3 + LsTFL1-Pro:LUC’ was significantly increased compared to that in the control ‘35S:GFP + LsTFL1-Pro:LUC’ (Fig. 4G). Furthermore, in the Lsofp6 genotype background, the transcript level of LsTSF1 in LsCOL3 plants was significantly lower than that in Lscol3 plants, and the transcript level of LsTFL1 in LsCOL3 plants was significantly higher than that in Lscol3 plants (Fig. 4H, I). These data indicated that LsCOL3 directly binds the promoter of LsTSF1 and LsTFL1 and downregulates and upregulates their expression, respectively, to delay bolting in lettuce.

LsOFP6 attenuates the effects of LsCOL3 on LsTSF1 and LsTFL1

To investigate whether LsOFP6 influences the LsCOL3-mediated regulation of LsTSF1 and LsTFL1 expression, and to clarify the molecular mechanism through which the LsOFP6–LsCOL3 module controls bolting development in lettuce, we first measured the transcript levels of LsTSF1 in plants with different genotypic combinations: LsOFP6 LsCOL3, Lsofp6 LsCOL3, LsOFP6 Lscol3, and Lsofp6 Lscol3. The results showed that the transcript level of LsTSF1 in the Lsofp6 LsCOL3 genotype was significantly lower than that in other genotypes (Fig. 5A). Interestingly, no significant difference in the transcript level of LsTSF1 was observed among LsOFP6 LsCOL3, LsOFP6 Lscol3, and Lsofp6 Lscol3 plants (Fig. 5A). We also performed dual-luciferase assays to further investigate the combinatorial effects of LsOFP6 and LsCOL3 on the transcriptional regulation of LsTSF1 (Figs 5B and S6). The LUC gene is driven by the LsTSF1 promoter, and the result revealed that the LUC activity in group #2 (co-expressing Lsofp6 and LsCOL3) is significantly lower than that in other groups (Fig. 5B). Additionally, there was no significant difference in LUC activity observed among groups #1, #3, and #4 (Fig. 5B).

We also quantified the transcript level of LsTFL1 in the four genotypes. The results revealed that the transcript level of LsTFL1 in Lsofp6 LsCOL3 genotype was significantly higher than that in other genotypes, and no significant difference in the transcript level of LsTFL1 was observed among LsOFP6 LsCOL3, LsOFP6 Lscol3, and Lsofp6 Lscol3 plants (Fig. 5C). The dual-luciferase assays showed that the LUC activity in group #6 is significantly higher than that in other groups, and the LUC activity has no obvious changes among groups #5, #7, and #8 (Fig. 5D).

The above results indicated that LsOFP6 compromises the effects of LsCOL3 on downregulating LsTSF1 and upregulating LsTFL1; the transcriptional regulation of LsCOL3 on LsTSF1 and LsTFL1 requires the nonfunctional Lsofp6; LsOFP6 regulates the expression of LsTSF1 and LsTFL1 genes in a LsCOL3-dependent manner. Therefore, we hypothesized that the loss of function of Lsofp6 downregulates LsTSF1 and upregulates LsTFL1 to delay the transition of the vegetative growth to reproductive growth in lettuce.

To test this hypothesis, we first overexpressed LsTSF1 in the LB3-late plants (genotype: Lsofp6 LsCOL3) (Fig. 5E). All three LsTSF1-OX plants showed early bolting compared with the recipients LB3-late plants (Fig. 5E–G). Then, the specific sgRNA for LsTSF1 was designed in the coding sequence to knock out LsTSF1 in LB3-early plants (genotype: LsOFP6 LsCOL3) using the CRISPR/Cas9 system. The LsTSF1-KO plants lost the ability to transition from vegetative to reproductive growth (Fig. 5H). The LsTFL1 gene was also overexpressed in the LB3-early plants (Fig. 5I). The LsTFL1-OX plants showed late bolting compared with the recipients (Fig. 5I–K).

Therefore, LsOFP6 compromises the effects of LsCOL3 on downregulating LsTSF1 and upregulating LsTFL1, leading to activated LsTSF1 and suppressed LsTFL1, and consequently, early bolting (Fig. 6). The late bolting phenotype in Lsofp6 mutant results from the low expression of LsTSF1 and high expression of LsTFL1, which are suppressed and activated by LsCOL3, respectively (Fig. 6).

LsOFP6 and LsCOL3 synergistically regulate bolting time by transcriptional control of FT/TFL1 family genes in lettuce.

Discussion

LsOFP6 regulates bolting development by modulating multiple biological pathways

The regulation of bolting time is a pivotal agronomic determinant in lettuce production, as premature bolting compromises both nutritional quality and textural attributes, thereby undermining market competitiveness. However, the genetic architecture and molecular mechanisms governing bolting time in lettuce remain insufficiently elucidated, which hampers progress in precision breeding aimed at this trait. Currently available genetic resources for bolting-related genes are limited, and regulatory pathways may vary across cultivars, collectively posing significant challenges to genetic improvement.

In our study, an OVATE protein family gene, LsOFP6, was genetically cloned (Fig. 1D–G). Although members of the OFP family are involved in transcriptional regulation, they do not possess typical DNA-binding domains. Instead, they act through protein–protein interactions with transcription factors to mediate specific biological processes [7]. Indeed, our EMSA assay showed that LsOFP6 protein alone did not cause any mobility shift (Fig. 4E), confirming that LsOFP6 lacks intrinsic DNA-binding activity and does not directly target these promoters. OFPs exhibit a close functional association with TALEs, including BLHs and KNOXs. The OFP–TALE complexes play a critical role in plant developmental processes [12]. Indeed, our previous work established that LsOFP6 enhances the effects of LsBLH2 on upregulating LsGA20ox1 and downregulating LsGA2ox8, and LsOFP6 attenuates the effects of LsKN1 on upregulating LsRGA1 and downregulating LsGA20ox1 [2, 3].

Interestingly, the mapping population (LB356 family) fixed a homozygous loss-of-function allele for LsBLH2 (Lsblh2) and a homozygous inactive allele for LsKN1 (Lskn1), indicating that LsOFP6 regulates bolting in an LsBLH2- and LsKN1-independent manner in the LB356 family with genetic background Lsblh2 Lskn1. Further analysis showed that LsOFP6 physically interacts with LsCOL3 to control the expression of FT/TFL1 family genes, and LsOFP6 regulates bolting development in a LsCOL3-dependent manner in the LB356 family. LsOFP6 compromised the effects of LsCOL3 on downregulating the expression of LsTSF1 and upregulating the expression of LsTFL1, resulting in early bolting (Fig. 6). In the late bolting parent, the Lsofp6 allele has a nonsense mutation and encodes a truncated protein with an impaired OVATE domain (Fig. 1G). Compared to LsOFP6 plants, Lsofp6 plants exhibit lower LsTSF1 expression and higher LsTFL1 expression, which collectively contribute to delayed bolting.

These results indicated that LsOFP6 regulates bolting development by modulating multiple biological pathways and regulating different downstream genes, such as LsGA20ox1, LsGA2ox8, LsTSF1, and LsTFL1. Interestingly, the bolting time did not differ between LsOFP6 and Lsofp6 plants in the Lscol3 Lsblh2 Lskn1 triple mutant background (Fig. 3G–H), suggesting that LsOFP6 regulates bolting mainly through interacting with LsCOL3, LsBLH2, and LsKN1. Building on our previous genetic analyses [2, 3], we propose a model where the lettuce bolting phenotype is determined by the specific protein partners available to LsOFP6. In the Lsblh2 Lskn1 genotype background, where LsBLH2 and LsKN1 are nonfunctional, the LsOFP6–LsCOL3 module appears to be the predominant pathway regulating bolting, independently of GA signaling. Conversely, in genetic backgrounds where LsBLH2 and/or LsKN1 are functional, LsOFP6 may form complexes that primarily modulate the GA pathway, which could play a more dominant role in controlling bolting time in those contexts. This partitioning model suggests that LsOFP6 acts as a molecular hub, integrating signals from different pathways to fine-tune the bolting time in a genotype-dependent manner.

FT/TFL1 family genes play important roles in the transition of the vegetative-to-reproductive phase in horticultural crops

Bolting and flowering constitute a pivotal developmental transition in horticultural crops, involving a series of biologically coordinated processes such as floral induction, bud differentiation, and anthesis [28]. From an applied perspective, this phase not only determines reproductive success but also fundamentally influences yield formation, quality traits, and market supply scheduling. A deeper understanding of how environmental factors, including photoperiod, temperature, and abiotic stress, regulate the floral transition through interconnected signaling networks holds substantial theoretical and practical importance [29]. Such insights can identify key targets for precise flowering manipulation, enabling the development of cultivars tailored to diverse ecological regions and cultivation systems through molecular breeding. Moreover, by integrating environmental simulation and growth regulation technologies, season-independent production can be achieved, thereby enhancing product marketability, reducing energy and production costs, and ensuring a stable year-round supply of fresh horticultural produce, offering new frameworks for the sustainable optimization of horticultural production systems [39, 40].

In horticultural crops, genes within the FT/TFL1 family exhibit conserved roles in regulating the floral transition. For instance, both in Chrysanthemum seticuspe (Asteraceae, the largest flowering plant family) and Phalaenopsis (Orchidaceae, the second largest family of flowering plants), three FT-like genes have been identified as flowering inducers, and their heterologous overexpression causes an early flowering phenotype, while several TFL1 homologs act as flowering inhibitors [41–43]. In tomato (Solanum lycopersicum), the timing of flowering is co-regulated by the floral activator SINGLE FLOWER TRUSS (SFT) and the repressor SELF PRUNING (SP) [44]. Functioning as an FT homolog, SFT is produced in mature leaves and acts systemically to stimulate flowering. Conversely, SP, an ortholog of TFL1, accumulates in young leaves and shoots where it functions to inhibit the floral transition [45].

In this study, six FT/TFL1 gene family members were identified in the lettuce genome, including an FT/TSF homolog, an ATC homolog, two TFL1 homologs, and other members (Fig. 4A). We demonstrated that FT/TFL1 family genes function as key regulators of bolting development in lettuce (Fig. 5E–K). Indeed, overexpressing LsTSF1 in LB3-late plants changes the late bolting to early bolting, while overexpressing LsTFL1 in LB3-early plants changes the early bolting to late bolting (Fig. 5E–K). Notably, LsTSF1 knock-out plants lost the ability of transition of vegetative-to-reproductive phase (Fig. 5H). Therefore, FT/TFL1 gene family members play important and different roles in lettuce bolting development. In addition, our study identifies LsOFP6–LsCOL3 as a novel protein-level interaction module that acts as a critical switch upstream of these FT/TFL1 gene family members, adding a significant new layer to the understanding of how bolting time is precisely controlled in lettuce. This represents a conceptual advance beyond the canonical transcriptional activation of FT by CO.

LsOFP6 and LsCOL3 synergistically regulate bolting development

We clarified the genetic relationship between LsOFP6 and LsCOL3, and demonstrated that the LsOFP6–LsCOL3 module regulates bolting by transcriptional control of LsTSF1 or LsTFL1 genes in lettuce (Fig. 6). Interestingly, no significant difference in bolting time was observed between LsOFP6 Lscol3 and Lsofp6 Lscol3 plants (Fig. 3G and H). Similarly, the expression levels of LsTSF1 and LsTFL1 did not exhibit notable variation between the two genotypes (Fig. 5A and C). Moreover, the expression of the LUC reporter gene, driven by either the LsTSF1 or LsTFL1 promoter, did not differ significantly between the group co-expressing LsOFP6 and Lscol3 and the group co-expressing Lsofp6 and Lscol3 (Fig. 5B and D). These data suggest that Lscol3 is recessive epistatic to LsOFP6, and LsOFP6 regulates bolting time in a LsCOL3-dependent manner.

Furthermore, bolting time remained unchanged between LsOFP6 LsCOL3 and LsOFP6 Lscol3 plants, whereas Lsofp6 LsCOL3 plants exhibited significantly later bolting compared to Lsofp6 Lscol3 plants (Fig. 3G and H). Similarly, the expression of LsTSF1/LsTFL1 showed no significant difference between LsOFP6 LsCOL3 and LsOFP6 Lscol3 plants, but was markedly different between Lsofp6 LsCOL3 and Lsofp6 Lscol3 plants (Fig. 5A and C). These results demonstrate that the regulation of LsCOL3 on bolting and the expression of LsTSF1/LsTFL1 require dysfunctional LsOFP6 (i.e. LsCOL3 relies on LsOFP6 in a dominant epistatic manner).

We further examined the transcript levels of LsOFP6 and LsCOL3 across a lettuce natural population, comprising wild lettuce accessions (Lactuca serriola, Lactuca saligna, and Lactuca virosa) and 198 cultivated varieties (Fig. S7A; Table S5). The results revealed a negative correlation between the expression levels of LsOFP6 and LsCOL3 (P = 7.6 × 10−6), supporting our conclusion that LsOFP6 and LsCOL3 exert antagonistic effects on the bolting development regulation in lettuce (Fig. S7B). In addition, the expression of LsCOL3 varies among different lettuce cultivars, suggesting that its expression may be a key factor in cultivar domestication (Fig. S7A). Indeed, different cultivars require distinct bolting times. For example, stem lettuce (a stem-consuming type) needs earlier bolting, while heading lettuce requires later bolting to accumulate more biomass.

In addition, some members of the COL family act as positive regulators of flowering while others function as repressors. This divergence in function does not appear to be strictly correlated with subfamily classification, as even closely related COL members can exhibit opposite regulatory roles [19]. Such functional complexity within the COL family underscores how plants utilize a limited genetic toolkit to achieve precise developmental outcomes through elaborated regulatory networks [14]. By integrating endogenous and exogenous stimuli into differential transcriptional programs, COLs collectively ensure that flowering occurs at the optimal time, thereby enhancing reproductive success [15]. From a horticultural perspective, this regulatory flexibility offers promising avenues for crop improvement [39].

In this study, we elucidate the molecular mechanism whereby the LsOFP6–LsCOL3 transcriptional module coordinately regulates FT/TFL1 family genes to control bolting time in lettuce (Fig. 6). The role of LsOFP6 in regulating bolting time was not anticipated from studies in Arabidopsis and other model crops, underscoring the importance of forward genetics in diverse species. From a practical breeding perspective, our findings strongly support that manipulating the LsOFP6–LsCOL3 module or its downstream targets is a viable strategy for breeding next-generation lettuce cultivars with delayed bolting and improved bolt tolerance.

Materials and methods

BSR-seq

Bulked segregant analysis integrated with RNA sequencing (BSR-seq) was employed to identify genomic regions associated with bolting time in lettuce [46, 47]. For this purpose, two DNA bulks were constructed: an ‘early bolting’ pool and a ‘late bolting’ pool, each formed by combining equal quantities of tissue samples from 20 individuals exhibiting distinct bolting phenotypes. Total RNA was isolated from each pooled sample. Sequencing was carried out on the DNBSEQ-T7 platform (Smartgenomics, China), yielding ~6 Gb of high-quality reads per pool. Bioinformatics processing and data interpretation followed the methodology outlined [48]. The primers utilized in genetic mapping are listed in Table S6.

Plant materials

Two cultivars of lettuce (L. sativa), one exhibiting late bolting and the other early bolting, were selected for investigating the genetic basis of bolting time. These parental lines were crossed to generate an F₂ population suitable for segregation analysis, and the subfamily designated LB356 was utilized in subsequent map-based cloning of LBL3. From this family, individuals with extreme phenotypes, classified as LB3-early and LB3-late, were identified and used for further analysis. All plant materials were grown under controlled greenhouse conditions at Henan Agricultural University in Zhengzhou, China, with a 16-hour light/8-hour dark photoperiod maintained at a constant temperature of 26°C. Evaluation of the bolting phenotype under standardized controlled environment conditions revealed high heritability and stability across independent cultivation cycles.

RT-qPCR and quantification of GA

Total RNA was isolated using RNAiso Plus (Takara, Japan) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. cDNA was generated from equal amounts of RNA using the TransScript cDNA Synthesis SuperMix (TransGen Biotech, China) with an Oligo-dT18 primer to ensure enrichment of polyadenylated transcripts. Reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) was carried out following previously established protocols [49], with amplification efficiency and specificity confirmed through melting curve analysis. The expression levels were normalized to the constitutively expressed reference gene Ubiquitin (LsUBQ4, LG4362429). The corresponding tissues during the rosette and bolting/flowering stages were used for RT-qPCR. All experiments included four independent biological replicates, each assayed in technical replicates, to ensure statistical reliability and reproducibility of the data. The shoot apex tissues at rosette and bolting/flowering stages were used for GA quantification, following a previously published method [50].

Plasmid construction

To generate complementation constructs, the full-length LsOFP6 gene was amplified by PCR from cDNA of the early bolting plants. The resulting PCR fragments were cloned into the SacI-digested pl2b-GFP vector. For CRISPR/Cas9-mediated knockout experiments, sgRNAs specifically targeting LsOFP6, LsCOL3, or LsTSF1 were designed and inserted into the corresponding CRISPR/Cas9 binary vectors. In the overexpression assay, the coding sequences (CDS) of LsOFP6, LsTSF1, and LsTFL1 were individually amplified and integrated into the StuI-digested pl2b-GFP vector under the control of the LsUBQ4 promoter to ensure constitutive expression. A GFP-tag was added in-frame at the 3′ end of each target gene to generate C-terminal fluorescent fusion proteins. The Agrobacterium (strain EHA105) transformants carrying the correct constructs were selected and used for subsequent plant transformation assays.

Promoter activity analysis

The dual-luciferase reporter assay was executed following the manufacturer’s protocol (Promega). We PCR-amplified promoter regions (~2000 bp) of the LsTSF1 and LsTFL1 genes and individually cloned each fragment into the pGreenII0800-LUC vector upstream of the firefly luciferase gene (LUC) open reading frame. Within the same vector system, the Renilla luciferase reporter (REN), under the control of the constitutive CaMV 35S promoter, served as the internal transfection control. These recombinant constructs were transiently delivered into N. benthamiana leaves via Agrobacterium infiltration. The luciferase activity data in this study are based on at least four independent biological replicates. Fluorescence intensities for both LUC and REN were quantified 48-hour postinfiltration using a Tecan Infinite 200 PRO luminometer.

Bimolecular fluorescence complementation

Bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC) assays were conducted according to established methods [48]. The full-length coding sequences of LsCOL3 and LsOFP6 were inserted into the pSPYNE(R)173 and pSPYCE(M) vectors, respectively. The constructs 35S:LsOFP6-eYFPC155 and 35S:LsCOL3-eYFPN173 were co-expressed in N. benthamiana leaves for 48 hours, after which eYFP fluorescence was detected using a laser-scanning confocal microscope (Leica TCS SP5).

Y1H and Y2H

For yeast one-hybrid (Y1H) assays, DNA fragments corresponding to the P1, P2, and P3 regions of the LsTSF1 promoter, and the P4, P5, and P6 regions of the LsTFL1 promoter, were individually amplified and inserted into the XhoI-linearized bait vector (pAbAi). The coding sequence of LsCOL3 was cloned into the EcoRI/BamHI-digested prey vector (pGADT7). The bait vectors were linearized with BbsI and co-transformed with the prey construct into Y1H Gold yeast cells. Transformants were first selected on SD/−Leu/−Ura medium, followed by screening on SD/−Leu/−Ura/AbA plates containing 500 ng/ml aureobasidin A. Negative controls consisted of yeast co-transformed with p53-AbAi and empty pGADT7, while positive controls contained p53-AbAi and pGADT7-p53.

For yeast two-hybrid (Y2H) analysis, the coding sequence of LsOFP6 was cloned into the bait vector pGBKT7, and that of LsCOL3 into the prey vector pGADT7. The two constructs were co-introduced into Y2H yeast, and interactions were selected on SD/−Leu/−Trp/−Ade/−His medium. All primers for Y1H and Y2H experiments are listed in Table S6.

Electrophoretic mobility shift assay

The electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA) was carried out following the manufacturer’s guidelines (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The MBP-LsOFP6 fusion protein was expressed and purified for the assay. For binding reactions, 3 μl of purified MBP-LsOFP6 protein was incubated with 20 pmol of FAM-labeled probes. Probes derived from the LsTSF1 promoter included P2-a, P2-b, and P2-c; probes from the LsTFL1 promoter included P4-a, P4-b, and P4-c. The MBP protein alone was used as a negative control. Fifty-fold unlabeled probe relative to the labeled probe was used for the competition assays. The probe sequences are listed in Table S6.
