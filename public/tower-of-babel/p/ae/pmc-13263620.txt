Time Course and Determinants of Acute Dysgeusia in Patients Receiving Head and Neck Radiation Therapy
Authors: Sreenija Yarlagadda, Arun Maharaj, Tiffany Nong, Andrea V Gonzalez, Naima-Sajoni C Guerra, Dylan M Paterniti-Ruscin, Elysse Castro-Hall, Nicolas Carvallo, Lorein Gonzalez, Muni Rubens, Adeel Kaiser, Noah S Kalman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeDysgeusia is a common toxicity experienced by patients with head and neck cancer (HNC) receiving radiation therapy (RT). In this study, we assessed the timescale of onset, progression, and recovery of dysgeusia using an objective measure.Methods and MaterialsPatients receiving RT for HNC to ≥ 50 Gy were included in this prospective observational study. The Waterless Empirical Taste Test (WETT) was used to objectively measure dysgeusia for the 5 basic tastes: sweet, sour, salty, bitter, and umami. Patients were assessed at baseline (pre-RT), 2 and 4 weeks mid-treatment, end of treatment (EOT), and 12 weeks post-RT. Changes in scores at different time points relative to baseline scores were computed. The significance of trends over time was tested using linear regression. Associations of taste scores with clinical and dosimetric variables were tested using Pearson correlation.ResultsOf the 50 patients who underwent baseline taste testing, 32 completed 12-week post-RT follow-up assessments. The most common primary tumor site was the oropharynx (66%). The median WETT scores were 20.7 at baseline, 16.2 at 2 weeks, 17.7 at 4 weeks, 17.1 at EOT, and 19 at follow-up. Baseline and EOT comparisons revealed a decline in total score (−17.3%), with the highest relative changes in umami (−62.5%) and salty (−30.9%). The relative total score change between baseline and follow-up was −8.2% (P = .011), demonstrating a slight improvement versus EOT, with umami (−50%) and salty (−9.5%) remaining most affected. Pearson correlations showed a moderately strong association between the EOT relative change and mean tongue dose (r: −0.62; P < .003), and oral cavity mean dose (r: −0.58; P = .005).ConclusionsThis study confirms significant alterations in taste perception in patients with HNC during/after RT. Taste changes correlated with mean tongue and oral cavity doses. Future studies and longer follow-up are warranted to define dose-response relationships in dysgeusia.

Introduction

Dysgeusia, or an altered sense of taste, is a common symptom experienced by patients with head and neck cancer (HNC), which can be further aggravated by chemotherapy and radiation therapy (RT).1, 2, 3 Dysgeusia has been associated with profound implications, including anorexia and weight loss.4, 5, 6 Previous studies using conventional RT techniques showed associations between dysgeusia and volume of tongue irradiated or RT dose.2,7,8 However, it is not clear what clinical and/or treatment factors have the greatest impact on dysgeusia in the era of highly conformal radiation techniques, including intensity modulated RT and proton therapy.

In a preliminary report, we demonstrated the feasibility of using the commercially available Waterless Empirical Taste Test (WETT) test in HNC RT patients.9 The current study expands on this report, characterizing the chronology of dysgeusia during and after HNC RT using highly conformal RT techniques. We further aimed to determine disease-related and treatment-related factors associated with dysgeusia.

Methods and Materials

Study design

This was a prospective observational study designed to evaluate taste perception in patients with histologically confirmed HNC receiving curative intent RT. The study protocol was approved by the institutional ethics review board (2020-SPEC-ROS-001). All patients provided written informed consent before enrollment.

Clinical assessment of taste acuity

Patients were assessed at 5 time points as part of the study: baseline (within 28 days before the initiation of RT), during RT (2 and 4 weeks mid-treatment), end of treatment (EOT), and 12 weeks post-RT. The WETT test assessed the 5 basic tastes: sweet (sucrose), sour (citric acid), salty (sodium chloride), bitter (caffeine), and umami (monosodium glutamate).10 Demographic details, tumor characteristics (primary site), treatment parameters (photon or proton therapy, use of concurrent chemotherapy, and ipsilateral or bilateral elective nodal coverage), and dose parameters (mean dose to oral cavity, tongue, and salivary glands) were recorded.

During the course of the study, some ambiguity in labeling taste strips led to the exclusion of some data points from the final analysis. To ensure consistent within-patient comparisons, the authors settled on removing the lost data points across all time points. After removing these data points, scores for the 5 tastes under investigation were recalculated (8 sweet, 8 umami, 6 salty, 6 bitter, 4 sour, 9 blank strips), yielding a total score of 41. Patients served as their own controls for each taste, and all analyses were run relative to their own baseline/previous time point score.

Statistical analysis

Patient demographics and clinical characteristics were summarized using descriptive statistics. Mean taste scores at baseline, 2 and 4 weeks mid-treatment, EOT, and 12 weeks post-RT were reported by clinical characteristics and compared between different time points using paired sample t-tests or repeated measures analysis of variance (ANOVA). Similarly, individual taste scores were compared between different time points using paired sample t-tests or repeated measures ANOVA. As a sensitivity analysis, we additionally fit linear mixed-effects models to evaluate the robustness of the primary findings. For each outcome, time was modeled as a categorical fixed effect with baseline as the reference level, and a subject-specific random intercept was included to account for within-participant correlation across repeated measurements. Models were estimated using restricted maximum likelihood. This approach allowed inclusion of all available observations and did not require complete data at all time points. Results from the mixed-effects models were compared with those from the repeated measures ANOVA to assess consistency in the direction, magnitude, and statistical significance of time effects. The association between continuous variables was evaluated using Pearson correlation. To evaluate the significance of trends over time, linear regression was employed. All analyses were done using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Inc). All tests were 2-sided, and statistical significance was set at P ≤ .05.

Trends during RT

The mean score changes observed during RT and FU are illustrated in Fig. 1a (total score) and Fig. 1b (individual taste scores). The biweekly assessment during RT revealed a statistically significant decline in taste function with a nadir at 2 weeks after the start of RT (relative total score change = -21.7%, P = .021; Table 4). The relative change in total score at EOT compared to baseline was −17% (P = .012). The magnitude of decline varied across different tastes, with umami showing the greatest reduction (−62%, P = .001) followed by salty (−31%, P = .001) at EOT relative to baseline (Table 2). The trends for different primary sites, use of concurrent chemotherapy, treatment techniques, and treatment laterality are illustrated in Table 3 and Fig. 2.

Posttreatment recovery

At the 12-week post-RT FU visit (n = 32), an improvement in total score was observed compared to EOT (relative score change from EOT to FU = 11%, P = .032), but did not return to baseline (relative score change from baseline to FU: −8%, P = .011). Partial recovery between EOT and FU was consistent across all tastes, with the highest improvements seen in umami (33%, P = .009) followed by salty (31%, P = .010). However, these scores remained below baseline levels with a relative score change of −50% for umami (P = .001) and a −9.5% for salty (P = .011), while other tastes had a narrow range from reduction to improvement (Table 4). Sensitivity analysis results were consistent with the primary analysis, with no meaningful differences in effect estimates or statistical significance (Tables E1 and E2).

Determinants of dysgeusia

As summarized in Table 3, patients receiving RT alone had greater dysgeusia during treatment compared to those receiving CRT (relative total score change from baseline to EOT = −42% vs −10%, P = .011) but also had a greater recovery posttherapy (relative total score change from EOT to FU = 53% vs 4%, P < .001). Similar trends were also noted on comparing photon with proton RT (relative total score change, baseline to EOT = -34% vs −15%, P = .021; EOT to FU = 51% vs 3%, P < .001) and ipsilateral versus bilateral neck treatment (relative total score change, baseline to EOT = -41% vs −6%, P = .011; EOT to FU = 27% vs 4%, P = .021). At FU, the highest decline relative to baseline was noted for oral cavity primaries (−32%, P = .004), followed by nasopharynx (−18%, P = .018).

Among the dosimetric variables, mean dose to tongue (r = -0.62, P = .003) and oral cavity (r = -0.58, P = .003) were found to have significant, moderately strong negative correlation with total score, and also across all individual tastes (Table 5).

Discussion

Our study presents the temporal course of acute dysgeusia in patients with HNC, presenting the overall and individual trajectories of the 5 basic tastes during RT using an objective assessment. As expected, alteration in taste perception occurred during RT in patients with HNC (Fig. 1), consistent with previous studies.8,11 At 12 weeks post-RT, there was significant but incomplete improvement compared to EOT across all tastes (Tables 2 and 4). Regarding individual tastes, sour and bitter taste discrimination did not show a statistically significant difference from baseline to FU, possibly due to not having the full 8 testing strip samples for these tastes. Umami showed the greatest decline and least recovery among the tastes, which could be related to difficulty in discrimination between umami and other tastes.12 No difference in individual taste scores was seen by the cell signaling mechanism.13

During treatment, we observed nadirs in total taste scores at 2 weeks after the start of RT, while the individual scores of sweet, salty, and umami further worsened at 4 weeks into RT. Limited taste changes occurred between 4 weeks and EOT (Table 4). Not all patients complied with intratreatment or EOT testing. Acute treatment toxicity and the time required for the taste assessment may have limited patient compliance, so it is possible that scores at these time points oversampled those patients with decreased overall toxicity.

Our study demonstrated significant associations between EOT dysgeusia and mean dose to tongue and oral cavity, which were consistent across all tastes (Table 5). There was little difference in effect whether the tongue itself or the oral cavity was evaluated, consistent with reports investigating tongue surface versus oral cavity dose and taste changes.14 These results build on other reports demonstrating that increased oral cavity dose increases dysgeusia.15 Salty taste had a moderately strong negative correlation with the anterior quadrants of the tongue; however, the current study found no significant correlations of dysgeusia with mean dose to tongue quadrants, parotids, or submandibular glands, akin to other studies (Table 5).16

Differences in mean oral cavity dose likely accounted for the increased acute taste changes seen in patients who received RT alone (median 35.9 Gy) versus those who received CRT (median 25.8 Gy) (Fig. 2b), and for patients who received photon (median 43.9 Gy) versus proton (median 24.2 Gy) RT (Fig. 2c). That said, some differences in score drops across treatment factors did not correlate with differences in mean oral cavity doses. Patients who received ipsilateral neck treatment had increased acute taste changes compared to patients with bilateral neck treatment despite similar oral cavity doses (median 27.4 Gy vs 26.3 Gy, respectively) (Fig. 2d); ipsilateral patients had higher baseline taste scores, which could have accounted for the larger percentage change. Larger score drops also occurred in nasopharynx patients (median oral cavity mean dose 19.0 Gy) (Fig. 2a); olfactory dysfunction can diminish taste perception,17, 18, 19 which could have accounted for this effect despite low oral cavity mean doses.

A major study limitation is the loss of certain data points. While these scores were consistent for within-patient comparisons across different time points for the same taste, they could not be compared across different tastes due to differences in the number of taste strips used for each taste. Because baseline scores varied between patient groupings, relative changes could have also overestimated or underestimated treatment effects. Absolute score differences were small, as seen in other analyses using the WETT test,10 so small random score changes could increase result variability. Nonetheless, with differential scores for each taste, the relative change in scores at different time points allowed for individualized tracking of changes. Overall, the current study followed a rigorous workflow in the longitudinal assessment of dysgeusia using an objective taste score. With additional FU and larger patient numbers, our results may help build out normal tissue complication probability models for dysgeusia.

Conclusion

Patients with HNC experienced dysgeusia during and after RT as measured using objective taste testing. RT-induced taste changes were transient, with good recovery at 12 weeks of FU, consistent across all tastes. Umami was the most affected taste at baseline and throughout treatment. Mean dose to the tongue and oral cavity was associated with dysgeusia across all tastes. Long-term determinants of dysgeusia will be determined in future studies.
