Prevalence of anemia and its association with asthma control in school-aged children: a cross-sectional study in Karachi, Pakistan (2025)
Authors: Burhanuddin Shabbir, Muhammad Osama Sheikh, Aneela Pasha, Shahiryar Khan, Ana Parveen, Fyezah Jehan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAsthma is a frequently encountered paediatric health problem, with various factors influencing disease control, including anemia. Anemia, regardless of the cause, is as an independent risk factor for lower respiratory tract infections but its association with asthma has not been thoroughly explored. Hence, we conducted this study to determine the frequency of anemia in asthmatic children and its effect, if any, on asthma control.MethodsWe conducted a cross-sectional study in children of 6–15 years attending public or private schools in Karachi from February to March 2025. A non-probability convenience sampling technique was used to include the schools and asthmatic children. Asthmatic children were identified through physician-based diagnosis or self/parent-reported symptoms of asthma. Control of asthma was assessed using the GINA symptom control assessment. Anemia was identified using the HemoCue device with age appropriate haemoglobin cut-offs.ResultsAmong 198 asthmatic children studied, anemia was present in 46.0% of participants. Male asthmatics were associated with lower odds of anemia (aOR = 0.41, 95% CI: 0.23–0.75, p = 0.003) and partly controlled asthma (aOR = 2.43, 95% CI: 1.25–4.70, p = 0.009) was significantly associated with higher odds of anemia. Children exposed to cigarette smoke were over twice as likely to be anemic (aOR = 2.05, 95% CI: 1.03–4.10, p = 0.042). No significant associations were found between anemia and clinical symptoms of asthma, school category, or maternal education level.ConclusionWe found a high prevalence of anemia in asthmatic children and the study highlights a notable association with anemic children who are more likely to have partly controlled asthma. These findings suggest that anemia may be an under-recognized factor associated with asthma control, prompting routine screening and management of anemia in asthmatic children.Keywords: Anemia, Asthma, Control of asthma, Karachi

Background

Asthma is a commonly encountered disease in the paediatric population. It is characterized by chronic inflammation in response to airway hyperresponsiveness, manifested clinically with reversible episodes of chest tightness, cough, shortness of breath and wheeze [1]. An interplay of multiple genetic and environmental factors comes into play, putting individuals at risk for asthma development and disease manifestation [2]. While disease manifestation can vary from mild symptoms to severe acute exacerbations, paediatric asthma remains a major cause of morbidity and mortality in the paediatric age group [3]. Among the many known variables influencing the control of asthma, anemia has been recently proposed as a contributing factor [4].

Anemia remains a global health challenge, affecting approximately one-fourth of the population [5]. In 2019, 1.76 billion people were affected by anemia worldwide (prevalence of 23.67%) with pronounced disparities across ages, gender and regions [6]. Similarly, according to the analysis of a national survey data, anemia remains a concerning public health problem among the children in South Asia [7]. In developing countries, the prevalence of anemia is higher because of shared risk factors, including malnutrition, a high burden of infectious diseases, and poor health infrastructure, which together increase the risk of anemia. Anemia in children is generally defined as a hemoglobin concentration less than the 5th percentile for age [8]. Anemia in children may develop due to a defect in either the quantity or the functionality of the red blood cells or haemoglobin, leading to a compromise in the oxygen carrying capacity of the blood. It then manifests as easy fatigability and shortness of breath [9]. These symptoms also overlap with uncontrolled asthma highlighting the difficulty in differentiating between the two when present together.

The etiology of anemia is multifaceted, and iron deficiency is the most common cause [10]. Iron is an integral component of haemoglobin and myoglobin, both of which facilitate oxygen transport and storage, respectively [11]. Moreover, it is also involved in mitochondrial energy production through its role in key enzymes [12]. Iron also supports innate and adaptive immune responses by promoting the proliferation and activity of neutrophils, macrophages and lymphocytes, and by generating reactive oxygen species [13]. In asthma, iron deficiency may compromise respiratory muscle strength, airway immune surveillance and the ability to resolve infections. It may perpetuate chronic inflammation, thereby contributing to disease severity and exacerbation frequency. Anemia of inflammation is another common cause, and studies suggest that it, either alone or in combination with other forms of anemia, accounts for up to 40% of all anemia cases worldwide [8]. The pathophysiology arises from chronic inflammation and cytokine dysregulation, driven by the disruption of immune-mediated regulation of hepcidin and other cytokines that play key roles in iron homeostasis, accelerate red blood cell (RBC) turnover, and impair erythropoietin (EPO) activity, ultimately leading to anemia [14]. Since asthma is considered a chronic inflammatory disease driven by immune response from eosinophils, mast cells and T-helper cells through the release of cytokines and other mediators, it is possible that asthma could contribute to the anemia of inflammation [15].

It has been reported previously that anemia, regardless of the cause, is an independent risk factor for the development of lower respiratory tract infections [16] but there have been only a few reports regarding the association of anemia and childhood asthma. Uncontrolled asthma leads to multiple visits to health facilities and contributes to increased school absenteeism and compromised daily activities. Similarly, anemia, particularly iron deficiency anemia, has adverse effects on cognitive development, attention, behavior, school performance, and physical activity in children [17]. Given that both asthma and anemia are relatively common pediatric conditions, we conducted this study to determine the prevalence of anemia among children with asthma and to explore its association with asthma control. This investigation aims to fill a gap in the medical literature by examining whether anemia influences asthma control in affected children.

Materials and methods

We conducted a cross-sectional study in children of ages 6–15 years who attended public or private schools in Karachi, Pakistan, from February 2025 to March 2025. The sample size was calculated using OpenEpi, based on an estimated anemia prevalence of 14.47% among children with uncontrolled asthma [8], with a 95% confidence level and a design effect of 1, resulting in a required sample size of 191 participants. The study began after obtaining approvals from the AKU Ethical Review Committee, relevant school authorities and the Primary and Secondary District Health Officers. After obtaining parental consent and assent before the initiation of the study, all children with physician-diagnosed asthma, self-reported or parent-reported asthma symptoms, particularly those with a history of wheezing, were included. Children with a history of chronic illness, for example congenital heart disease, chronic kidney disease and chronic infections, or those who were on iron supplementation medications were excluded from the study.

Asthma control was assessed according to the GINA (Global Initiative for Asthma) guidelines. According to GINA asthma control assessment, asthma control is defined by the frequency of daytime asthma symptoms more than twice/week, any night-time awakenings, use of SABA for symptomatic relief more than twice/week, and any limitation of activity from asthma. All four of these parameters were assessed over the past 4 weeks and classified as well-controlled if none were present, partly controlled if 1–2 were present, and poorly controlled if 3–4 were present (Table 1).

The study team approached schools and informed them about the study. Schools that were willing to participate notified parents about the research. Parents and children who expressed interest in participating were provided with detailed information about the study, and informed consent/assent was obtained in accordance with WHO guidelines. A structured interview was conducted with each child in a private space in the school, in the presence of a parent or a teacher, as per the family’s preference. The structured questionnaire took approximately 15 min to complete and included questions on asthma symptoms and lifestyle factors. To ensure confidentiality, each participant was assigned a unique identification number, and all data were collected and recorded using Google Forms.

Following the interviews, every child was tested for anemia using the HemoCue® Hb 301 device which has a sensitivity and specificity of 83% and 86%, respectively, compared to the laboratory analyzer [18]. A trained phlebotomist measured each child’s hemoglobin level using the HemoCue® Hb 301 device, which requires only a single drop of capillary blood obtained via finger prick [19]. To avoid dilution by interstitial fluid, the first drop of blood was discarded. A single phlebotomist visited each school with our data collection teams to ensure standardization of the blood sample collection method. The device was calibrated daily according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and quality control checks were performed using standard control cuvettes to ensure the accuracy and reliability of the readings. Samples were measured during school hours under the supervision of the school authorities. Anemia was defined as a hemoglobin level of < 11.5 g/dl in 5–11 years, < 12 g/dl in 12–14 years, and < 13 g/dl in children 15 years or older [20].

Statistical analysis

Data were entered into a secure electronic database using Google Forms, and subsequently cleaned, categorized, and analyzed using Stata version 17.0 (Stata Corp, College Station, TX, USA). Quantitative variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation for normally distributed data, and as median with interquartile range (IQR) for non-normally distributed data. Categorical variables were summarized using frequencies and percentages. The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. For comparisons between two groups, the Student’s t-test was used for normally distributed variables, and the Wilcoxon rank-sum test was applied for non-normally distributed variables. For comparisons involving more than two groups, the Kruskal-Wallis test was employed. Associations between categorical variables were evaluated using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Logistic regression analysis was conducted to examine the association between anemia and asthma control. Univariate logistic regression was performed to calculate odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for potential risk factors. Variables with p-values < 0.20 in the univariate analysis were considered for inclusion in the multivariate model, which was built using a stepwise backward elimination approach. A p-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant throughout the analysis.

A statistically significant association was observed between anemia status and asthma control (p = 0.029), with children having partly controlled asthma exhibiting the highest proportion of anemia (56.0%), followed by those with well-controlled (23.1%) and poorly controlled asthma (20.9%). Children with well-controlled asthma had lower odds of anemia compared to those with partly controlled asthma, who had over 2-fold higher odds of anemia, (Table 4; aOR = 2.43, 95% CI: 1.25–4.70, p = 0.009).

Discussion

We conducted a study aimed at determining the frequency of anemia in asthmatic children aged 6 to 15 years and its effect on asthma control. We found that the anemia prevalence was 46.0%, and the majority of asthmatic anemic children had partly controlled disease (56.0%). We also looked at risk factors and found that asthmatic children who were exposed to cigarette smoke were more likely to be anemic and had a statistically significant difference from those who were not exposed to cigarette smoke. While physical activity and maternal education showed no statistically significant differences, trends do indicate possible correlations.

There is a dearth of data suggesting an association between asthma and anemia [17, 21, 22]. According to this study, the prevalence of anemia in asthmatic children was 46%, which is higher than the prevalence of anemia in general population, which is 23.67% [6]. In a South Korean study [8], the prevalence of anemia in uncontrolled asthma was 14.47%, while in our population, the prevalence of anemia in asthmatic children, regardless of asthma control, was 46%. The substantially higher prevalence observed in our study likely reflects contextual differences between the Korean population and our study setting in Karachi, Pakistan, where childhood anemia remains highly prevalent. This finding aligns with existing literature suggesting a potential link between chronic inflammatory conditions like asthma and altered hematological profiles, including anemia [8]. The high prevalence of anemia in our population is further attributed to poor nutritional status and access to quality healthcare for children of all socioeconomic groups [23].

Based on the available literature, this is among the few studies to examine the relationship between anemia and asthma symptom control as outlined in the GINA guidelines. Asthmatic children with well controlled asthma were less likely to have anemia (40.2% vs. 23.1%). Children with partly controlled asthma were twice as likely to have anemia than children with well controlled asthma (OR = 2.43, 95% CI: 1.25–4.70, p = 0.009), which is consistent with the findings of a study conducted in South Korea, which showed that uncontrolled asthma has a 2.64 times higher prevalence of anemia [8]. Unlike our study in which we stratified control of asthma into three cohorts, Ji-Eun Chang et al. described anemia in only 2 cohorts: well controlled and uncontrolled asthma. Although not statistically significant, children with poorly controlled asthma also showed increased odds of anemia (OR = 1.85, 95% CI: 0.82–4.17, P = 0.136). The finding that anemia was more prevalent and statistically significant in children with partly controlled asthma than poorly controlled asthma should be interpreted with caution, as the smaller number of children in the poorly controlled group may have reduced the statistical power to establish a stronger association. Also, from a pathophysiological perspective, while anemia exacerbates the symptoms of dyspnea, fatigue and activity limitation by reducing the oxygen carrying capacity of haemoglobin, it does not directly worsen airway inflammation and bronchial hyperresponsiveness to produce frequent nocturnal symptoms or bronchodilator use associated with poorly controlled asthma. These findings indicate that effective asthma control may play a crucial role in reducing the risk of anemia in asthmatic children and vice versa but further longitudinal studies are needed to establish the causality.

In our cohort, males had lower odds of being anemic as compared to their female counterparts. This indirectly corelates with findings from another study conducted in Ethiopia in which anemia was observed to be more prevalent in asthmatic girls [24] Menstrual blood losses, especially, when paired with inadequate dietary iron intake, predispose the female population to a greater likelihood of developing anemia. Additionally, females exhibit heightened asthma severity and type-2 inflammation, mediated by estrogen, which can induce anemia of chronic disease via cytokine-driven iron dysregulation [25]. We did not find any significant association between anemia and the clinical manifestations of asthma, such as wheeze, or with factors defining its severity, such as nighttime awakenings and speech impairment. A study conducted at Ain Shams University, Egypt, included asthmatic children from 6 to 16 years of age and showed that anemic asthmatic children have reduced lung functions (FEV1, FVC), which could potentially contribute to increased episodes of wheezing, nighttime symptoms, exercise-induced breathing difficulty and asthma exacerbations [26]. As there is very limited paediatric data linking anemia to asthma symptoms and severity, this remains an area of research before any definite associations and meaningful conclusions can be drawn. Although not statistically significant (p = 0.08), our findings show a trend between maternal education and anemia in children, as mothers of anemic children had only primary education, whereas mothers of non-anemic children had completed secondary education. This correlates with the results of a study conducted in school-aged children of Korea, where a higher prevalence of anemia was found in children whose mothers had lower education [27]. Another study conducted by our team in school-aged children of Karachi to estimate the prevalence of asthma and its risk factors also found that children whose mothers had a college/university or tertiary education were significantly less likely to have asthma. These observations highlight the importance of maternal education in shaping child health by improving care-seeking behaviours, health literacy, and dietary practices. In our study, we found that exposure to cigarette smoke was associated with a significantly higher prevalence of anemia among asthmatic children (27.5% vs. 15.9%, p = 0.04). This is supported by evidence from literature, as passive smoking can lead to systemic inflammation and raised levels of Interleukin-6, thereby inhibiting iron absorption through upregulation of hepcidin levels and leading to anemia [28]. Tobacco smoke also shortens the lifespan of healthy red blood cells through oxidative stress and impairs their oxygen-carrying capacity by elevating carbon monoxide levels [29, 30]. Although these findings suggest an association between anemia and asthma, our study design does not allow for causal inference.

The study had several limitations. Firstly, we did not use any objective tests for the confirmation of asthma and assessing its control like pulmonary function testing and relied solely on patient/parent-reported symptoms or physician based diagnosis. As patient/parent-reported symptoms are subject to recall, it may have introduced a misclassification bias. Secondly, we only tested for anemia and did not extend testing to different blood indices like MCV to further ascertain the type and cause of anemia, like iron deficiency anemia vs. anemia of chronic disease and thalassemia. The HemoCue device used in the study to measure the haemoglobin levels is a widely used and validated point-of-care device; however, in comparison to venous blood sampling, it may exhibit some measurement variability potentially leading to misclassification of anemia status. Additionally, the study did not include a non-asthmatic control group, which limits the ability to establish a baseline prevalence of anemia among school-aged children of Karachi and to directly compare anemia burden between asthmatic and non-asthmatic populations. Lastly, we used a cross-sectional study design that did not allow us to determine causality between anemia and asthma. The strength of this study lies in being the first study in our setting to explore the effects of anemia on asthma control. Further, we extended our investigation to school-aged children from both public and private schools, thus ensuring representation of all socioeconomic strata. Our findings highlight the potential value of integrating nutritional interventions, particularly anemia control strategies, into childhood asthma management programs. Policy measures such as routine anemia screening in children with asthma, food fortification, and school-based nutrition programs may be considered as they may have a dual impact by reducing anemia prevalence and improving asthma control. However, future longitudinal and interventional studies are warranted to establish causality and inform evidence-based guidelines.

In conclusion, our study highlights a notable association between anemia and asthma control in children. Anemic asthmatic children were more likely to have partly controlled disease as compared to non-anemic asthmatic children, who predominantly had well-controlled disease. These findings suggest that anemia may be an under-recognized factor associated with asthma control but further longitudinal research is warranted to explore the causal relationship and underlying mechanisms between anemia and asthma control following which routine screening and management of anemia in asthmatic children may be considered as it could serve as an important adjunct to optimizing asthma outcomes.
