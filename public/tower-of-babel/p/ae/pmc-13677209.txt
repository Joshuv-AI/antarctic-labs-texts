Systemic LPS exposure suppresses pulsatile LH secretion and impairs hepatic function in goats, accompanied by immune activation and stress responses
Authors: Wenhao CUI, Misato KIKUZAWA, Tomotsugu TAKAHASHI, Keiichi KARIYA, Junyou LI, Sho NAKAMURA, Satoshi OHKURA, Josef Johann GROSS, Hiroko TSUKAMURA, Fuko MATSUDA, Fumie MAGATA
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractUterine inflammatory diseases are a major cause of subfertility in both humans and domestic animals. Lipopolysaccharide (LPS) is a key mediator of uterine inflammation and reproductive dysfunction. Although the acute suppressive effects of LPS on the hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal axis are well documented, its sustained effects under persistent inflammation remain unclear. This study investigated the effects of systemic LPS exposure on reproductive and hepatic function, immune activation, and stress responses in estradiol-treated ovariectomized goats. Animals received either a single intravenous (i.v.) bolus injection of LPS or a continuous i.v. infusion. Serial blood samples were collected for 8 h to evaluate plasma concentrations of LPS, interleukin (IL)-1β, cortisol, and liver enzymes, as well as pulsatile luteinizing hormone (LH) secretion. Rectal temperature increased during the early phase after LPS administration. Plasma LPS concentrations increased several hours after LPS administration, with a biphasic elevation observed during continuous LPS infusion. IL-1β and cortisol increased within 1 h of LPS administration, coinciding with LH pulse suppression and preceding the rise in plasma LPS. Gamma-glutamyltransferase (GGT) increased within 3 h and remained elevated after LPS administration. Aspartate transaminase (AST) increased at the end of the continuous LPS infusion experiment. Systemic LPS administration significantly suppressed pulsatile LH secretion compared with saline treatment. These findings suggest that LPS-driven systemic immune activation and/or stress-related endocrine responses may contribute to suppression of pulsatile LH secretion, and that progressive hepatic dysfunction during continuous LPS exposure may contribute to sustained circulating LPS and prolonged reproductive dysfunction.Keywords: Goats, Immune activation, Lipopolysaccharide, Luteinizing hormone pulsatility, Stress-related endocrine response

Inflammatory diseases of the reproductive tract, such as endometritis, are considered major causes of subfertility in both humans and domestic animals. In women undergoing in vitro fertilization, untreated endometritis has been shown to reduce the clinical pregnancy rate by nearly 50% [1]. A recent meta-analysis further reported a strong association between chronic endometritis, infertility, and recurrent pregnancy loss [2]. In high-producing dairy cows, uterine inflammatory disease is also a significant concern, affecting approximately half of the population and markedly impairing reproductive performance and milk production [3]. In postpartum dairy cows, endometritis decreases the pregnancy rate at first artificial insemination to below 30%, primarily by increasing the early embryonic loss and disrupting the estrous cyclicity and ovulation [4]. Furthermore, cows that have experienced uterine inflammation often exhibit prolonged calving-to-conception intervals and decreased pregnancy rates, even after apparent clinical recovery [5]. Therefore, elucidating the mechanisms underlying both the immediate and long-term impacts of uterine inflammation on reproductive function is essential for developing effective strategies to restore fertility and improve the reproductive performance in affected animals.

Uterine inflammatory disease is primarily caused by bacterial infections, with Escherichia coli being the most prevalent pathogen isolated from affected patients [6] and cows [7]. The pathogenic effects of E.coli are primarily mediated by lipopolysaccharide (LPS), an endotoxin derived from the pathogenic membrane component of gram-negative bacteria [8]. LPS is released from the surface of replicating and dying E. coli, provoking both local and systemic inflammatory responses. Similar systemic inflammatory responses accompanied by increased circulating LPS and pro-inflammatory cytokines have also been reported in mastitis, another major infectious disease in dairy cows [9]. These observations suggest that infection-associated systemic inflammation may broadly influence reproductive physiology beyond uterine disease [10]. Elevated LPS concentrations have been detected in the plasma [11] and follicular fluid [12, 13] of dairy cows with uterine inflammation, suggesting that circulating LPS may impair reproductive function, particularly through its effect on ovarian activity [14]. Indeed, previous studies have demonstrated that LPS directly disrupts ovarian function by inhibiting steroidogenesis [15, 16] and reducing follicular cell responsiveness to gonadotropins [17] and the developmental competence of oocytes [18, 19].

In ruminants, as in other animals, reproductive function is regulated by the hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal (HPG) axis. Gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH), synthesized and released by hypothalamic GnRH neurons, stimulates the gonadotropin secretion from the anterior pituitary, which in turn controls ovarian function. In females, GnRH is secreted in pulsatile and surge patterns, generating corresponding luteinizing hormone (LH) pulses and the preovulatory LH surge, respectively [20]. Previous studies found that the LH pulses regulate the ovarian activity by promoting androgen synthesis in theca cells during the follicular phase and maintaining progesterone production during the luteal phase [21,22,23], while LH surge is known to induce ovulation [24]. Kisspeptin neurons located in the hypothalamic arcuate nucleus (ARC) are well recognized as the GnRH pulse generators [25,26,27,28], while the neurons in the anteroventral periventricular nucleus (AVPV) in rodents [29] or the preoptic area (POA) in ruminants [30] are responsible for generating the GnRH/LH surge.

Previous studies have demonstrated that systemic LPS administration suppresses pulsatile LH release in rats [31, 32] and ewes [33]. This suppression is thought to result from LPS-induced impairment of the hypothalamus and pituitary [31], including downregulation of hypothalamic ARC and AVPV kisspeptin gene (Kiss1) expression [31, 32, 34]. Notably, in most of these studies, the acute inflammatory response induced by LPS intravenous (i.v.) or intraperitoneal (i.p.) bolus injection appears to be transient, as LH pulsatility has been reported to recover within 2 h after LPS administration [35, 36], which may not accurately reflect the inflammatory status in clinical cases. Therefore, alternative experimental models of systemic LPS exposure may be warranted to elucidate the mechanisms underlying long-term reproductive dysfunction. Moreover, the inhibitory effects of LPS may diminish as it is metabolized and cleared from circulation; thus, the kinetics of LPS metabolism and clearance during uterine inflammatory diseases should be further investigated. In addition to the direct effects of LPS, pro-inflammatory cytokines such as interleukin (IL)-1β and stress-related hormones have also been implicated as potential mediators of the LPS-induced suppression of HPG axis [20].

This study, therefore, aimed to investigate the suppressive effects of systemic LPS exposure on pulsatile LH secretion under distinct exposure conditions, including acute and continuous exposure, and to characterize the associated systemic responses in female goats. To this end, LH pulse profiles, rectal temperature, and plasma concentrations of LPS, IL-1β, and cortisol were monitored in estradiol (E2)-treated ovariectomized (OVX) goats to assess reproductive function, systemic immune activation, and stress-related endocrine responses following acute or continuous systemic LPS administration. In addition, plasma liver enzymes were measured to evaluate hepatic function and assess the metabolism and clearance of circulating LPS under inflammatory conditions.

Materials and Methods

Animals

Three adult non-lactating female Shiba goats (Capra hircus) weighing 23.6–24.2 kg were used. Goats received the ovariectomy surgery at ages ranging from 78–89 months. Each goat was subcutaneously implanted with a silicone tube (inner diameter, 3 mm; outer diameter, 5 mm; length, 30 mm) filled with crystalline E2 (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) two weeks after the ovariectomy surgery. This hormonal treatment was based on a previously described protocol [37] designed to maintain plasma E2 concentrations within the luteal-phase range (4–8 pg/ml) in goats. The goats were transferred to the room for further experiment and were loosely tied to a specialized stanchion at least three days before the beginning of experiment to acclimate the goats to the surroundings. The room was maintained under controlled conditions with 12-h light/dark cycle at 23 ± 2 °C temperature. The goats were fed with a standard pelleted diet and hay once daily, with free access to water and supplemental minerals. All experimental protocols were approved by the Committee for the Proper Care and Use of Laboratory Animals, Graduate School of Agricultural and Life Sciences, the University of Tokyo (P20-017).

Effects of bolus i.v. injection of LPS on pulsatile LH secretion, liver function, and inflammatory response

The experimental procedure of the bolus i.v. injection is illustrated in Fig.1a. The goats (n = 3) were fitted with unilateral 18-gauge jugular catheters (NUKP-1830-S; Nipro Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) for blood sampling and saline/LPS i.v. injection 1 day before the experiment. On day 0, serial blood samples were collected without i.v. treatment for the data analysis of LH secretion. Immediately after blood sampling at 1100 h on day 1, the 10 ml physiological saline (0.9% NaCl; Otsuka Pharmaceutical Factory, Tokyo, Japan) was bolus i.v. injected through the jugular catheter. Immediately after blood sampling at 1100 h on day 2, the 10 ml physiological saline containing LPS (E. coli O111: B4, 400 ng/kg BW; Sigma-Aldrich) was bolus i.v. injected through the jugular catheter. This dosage was chosen based on the previous studies [38,39,40]. An inflammation model in ewes has been reported in which i.v. bolus injection of LPS at 400 ng/kg increased plasma cortisol and suppressed GnRH concentration in cerebrospinal fluid. On day 0, 1, and 2, the goats underwent serial blood sampling every 6 min from 1100 h to 1900 h for a total duration of 8 h. These blood samples were used for the LH assay. During the experiment on day 1 and 2, the additional blood samples were also collected every hour for the measurement of the plasma concentrations of LPS, IL-1β, cortisol, non-esterified fatty acid (NEFA), aspartate transaminase (AST), and gamma-glutamyltransferase (GGT). The volume of each blood sample, including those collected at 6-min intervals for LH measurement and those collected hourly, was 0.8 ml. Because AST and GGT are hepatic enzymes released into circulation following hepatic cell damage or necrosis, they are monitored to assess the progression of liver dysfunction. Meanwhile, the rectal temperature of each goat was recorded hourly over the experimental period for 8 h. Blood samples were collected into heparinized sampling tubes, containing 10 U heparin (Yoshindo Inc., Toyama, Japan) and kept on ice immediately after collection. Then, the blood samples were centrifuged at 4°C to obtain plasma, which was stored at –20°C until further analysis.

Effects of i.v. infusion of LPS on pulsatile LH secretion, liver function, and inflammatory response

The experimental procedure of the continuous i.v. LPS infusion is illustrated in Fig.1b. The i.v. infusion of LPS (n = 3) was conducted at least 3 months after the i.v. bolus injection of LPS for goats’ recovery and to prevent the residual effects of LPS. One month before the LPS infusion experiment, new E2 silicone tubes were implanted in each goat to maintain plasma E2 levels at 4–8 pg/ml, comparable to those during the luteal phase. One day before the experiment, the goats were fitted with bilateral 18-gauge jugular catheters, one for serial blood sampling and the other for continuous saline/LPS i.v. infusion. On day 0, serial blood samples were collected without i.v. treatment for the data analysis of LH secretion. Immediately after blood sampling at 1100 h on day 1, the continuous i.v. infusion of physiological saline was initiated at a rate of 10 ml/h using the peristaltic pump (Masterflex 7554-80; Cole-Parmer, Vernon Hills, IL, USA) and continued until the end of the 8 h blood sampling period. Immediately after blood sampling at 1100 h on day 2, LPS dissolved in sterile saline was i.v. infused at a rate of 600 ng/kg BW per hour (10 ml/h) using the same peristaltic pump, continuing until the end of the 8 h blood sampling period. The LPS dosage was chosen based on the previous studies [41,42,43,44]. A previous study in ewes indicated that an i.v. infusion of LPS at 600 ng/kg BW per hour successfully established the ovine endotoxemia model, causing the elevations in plasma cortisol and tumor necrosis factor α concentrations. The preliminary experiments indicated that this LPS dosage induced transient sickness behaviors, including fever, labored respiration, and signs of depression. The procedures for serial blood sampling for the LH assay and for measuring plasma concentrations of LPS, IL-1β, cortisol, NEFA, AST, and GGT were performed using the same protocol described above.

Enzyme immunoassay of plasma IL-1β and cortisol

The plasma concentration of IL-1β was determined using a commercial kit (Bovine IL-1beta ELISA Kit; ab273202, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), following the manufacturer’s guidelines. The range of the standards was 0.85–200 pg/ml, and the intra- and inter-assay coefficients of variation were 2.81% and 5.16%, respectively. The plasma concentration of cortisol was determined using a commercial kit (Cortisol Enzyme Immunoassay Kit; K003-H1, Arbor Assays, Ann Arbor, MI, USA) according to the manufacturer’s guidelines. The range of the standards was 45.4–3200 pg/ml, and the intra- and inter-assay coefficients of variation were 6.11% and 3.12%, respectively.

Determination of plasma concentrations of NEFA, AST, GGT, and LPS

The plasma concentrations of NEFA, AST, and GGT were determined using a clinical automated chemistry analyzer (TBA120FR; Toshiba Medical Systems Co., Ltd., Tochigi, Japan). The plasma concentration of LPS was measured by Limulus Color KY Test (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corporation, Osaka, Japan), following the manufacturer’s instructions. Firstly, plasma samples were diluted with endotoxin-free distilled water and tested for non-specific limulus amebocyte lysate (LAL) inhibition by comparing samples with and without a spike of a known LPS concentration and the dilution ratio of samples was determined based on the result of recovery ratio. Proteinase K digestion was performed as previously [17] to avoid inhibition of the LAL reaction. Then, the samples were mixed with KY reagent and measured in an endotoxin-free 96-wells plate. The range of standard was 0.01–10 endotoxin units (EU)/ml. The internal recovery measured using positively spiked samples was > 80%.

Radioimmunoassay of LH

Plasma LH concentrations were measured by double-antibody radioimmunoassay for ovine LH as previously described [45]. Each plasma sample was incubated with rabbit anti-ovine LH serum (YM#18, 1:80000) at 4°C for 46 h. The sample was then added with 125I-labeled ovine LH of 6000 cpm and incubated at 4°C for 46 h. After incubation, anti-rabbit goat globulin (ARGG, 1:10 in 0.05 M PBS-0.05 M EDTA 2Na-3% polyethylene glycol) was added to each sample, followed by 4°C incubation for 46 h. Lastly, samples were centrifuged at 2140 × g for 30 min. After the aspiration of supernatant, the radioactivity of sedimentation was measured by a gamma counter (2470 WIZARD, PerkinElmer, Waltham, MA, USA). The lowest detectable concentration was 0.098 ng/ml for 50 μl plasma sample. The intra- and inter-assay coefficients of variation were 8.01% and 8.13%, respectively.

Data analysis

Rectal temperatures and the plasma concentrations of IL-1β, cortisol, NEFA, AST, GGT, and LPS at each time point between the saline- and LPS-treated groups were compared using the Mann-Whitney U test. In both experiments, peaks of LH pulses were identified, and parameters of LH pulses, such as mean LH, mean baseline LH, amplitude, and pulse frequency, were calculated by the PULSAR computer program [46]. Because pulsatile LH secretion exhibits substantial physiological variation among individual goats, it is widely recommended to evaluate treatment effects using relative changes from each animal’s untreated baseline [27, 47]. This approach minimizes the influence of inter-individual variability and unequal variance in LH pulse parameters, allowing for more robust physiological interpretation. Furthermore, using change ratios aligns with principles of animal welfare by enabling an accurate assessment of endocrine responses without requiring additional animals to increase statistical power. Therefore, LH pulse parameters for the saline and LPS treatments were expressed as the change ratio relative to each animal’s untreated values on day 0. No direct statistical comparison was performed between the bolus injection and continuous infusion experiments because they were conducted under different experimental conditions, including differences in total LPS dose, administration kinetics, and experimental timing. The significant differences between the saline- and LPS-treated groups were analyzed using the Mann-Whitney U test. All statistical analyses were conducted with the SPSS software (IBM, Inc. Endicott, NC, USA). All analyses were considered statistically significant at P < 0.05.

Results

LPS administration elevated rectal temperature in female goats

Figure 2 shows changes in rectal temperature in OVX + E2 goats in saline- and LPS-treated groups during the bolus injection and continuous infusion experiments. Following LPS injection, rectal temperature was significantly higher at 1, 3, and 4 h (Fig. 2a, P < 0.05), peaking at 3 h compared with the saline group. The elevated rectal temperature persisted for approximately 2 h before returning to baseline levels. Under the LPS infusion condition, rectal temperature showed a transient elevation at 1 h after the onset of infusion (Fig. 2b, P < 0.05), with no significant differences between the LPS- and saline-infused groups thereafter. Overall, rectal temperature increased during the early phase after LPS administration and subsequently returned toward baseline during the observation period.

LPS administration increased plasma IL-1β, cortisol, and LPS levels

Following LPS injection, plasma LPS concentrations peaked significantly only 7 h after the injection (Fig. 3a, P < 0.05). Plasma levels of IL-1β and cortisol significantly surged at 1 h after injection and remained elevated until the end of the observation period compared with the saline-injected control group (Figs. 3b and c, P < 0.05). Under the infusion condition, plasma LPS levels rose transiently at 3 h after the onset of LPS infusion, followed by a second significant elevation at 6 h, which plateaued until the end of the observation period (Fig. 3d, P < 0.05). Plasma levels of IL-1β and cortisol increased significantly 1 h after the onset of LPS infusion and remained elevated throughout the observation period (Figs. 3e and f, P < 0.05). Overall, plasma IL-1β and cortisol showed rapid and sustained elevations, whereas circulating LPS exhibited delayed increases after administration.

LPS administration altered plasma GGT, NEFA, and AST profiles

Following LPS injection, there was no significant difference in plasma AST concentrations between the saline- and LPS-injected groups throughout the experiment (Fig. 4a). In contrast, plasma GGT concentration exhibited a significant upward trend from 3 h after LPS injection until the end of the observation period (Fig. 4b, P < 0.05). Plasma NEFA concentration also rose significantly at 1 h after injection but returned to levels by 4 h post-injection (Fig. 4c, P < 0.05). Subsequently, plasma NEFA concentration rose again at 6 h after injection and fluctuated within a narrow range until the end of the observation period. Under the infusion condition, plasma AST concentration surged only at 8 h after the initiation of LPS infusion (Fig. 4d, P < 0.05). In addition, plasma GGT concentration increased at 3 h after the onset of infusion and remained elevated until the end of the observation period (Fig. 4e, P < 0.05). Plasma NEFA concentration rose at 1 h after the onset of LPS infusion but returned to the baseline level at 3 h (Fig. 4f, P < 0.05). Subsequently, plasma NEFA concentration re-elevated at 6 h after the onset of LPS infusion and remained elevated until the end of the observation period. Overall, plasma GGT showed a sustained elevation, NEFA displayed a biphasic pattern with a secondary elevation in the later phase, and AST increased during the late phase of systemic LPS exposure.

LPS administration suppressed the pulsatile LH secretion in goats

Plasma LH profiles in representative goats from the untreated, saline-injected, and LPS-injected goats are shown in Fig. 5a. The levels and patterns of LH pulses were similar between the untreated and saline-injected groups. However, in the LPS-injected group, the apparent LH pulses almost disappeared from 4 h post-injection until the end of the observation period. The mean and mean baseline LH as well as the amplitude and frequency of LH pulses were significantly lower in the LPS injection group compared with the saline injection group (Fig. 5b, P < 0.05). Plasma LH profiles in representative goats from the untreated, saline-infused, and LPS-infused groups are shown in Fig. 5c. The levels and patterns of LH pulses were similar in the untreated and saline-infused groups. However, in the LPS-infused group, the apparent LH pulses almost disappeared from 2 h after the onset of LPS infusion until the end of the observation period. The mean and mean baseline LH as well as the amplitude and frequency of LH pulses were significantly lower in the LPS-infused group compared with the saline-infused control group (Fig. 5d, P < 0.05). Overall, pulsatile LH secretion was rapidly suppressed after LPS administration and remained suppressed throughout the observation period.

Discussion

The present study demonstrates that systemic LPS exposure suppresses the HPG axis in female goats, accompanied by immune- and stress-related responses, as well as impaired hepatic function. This was evidenced by the disruption of LH pulses together with increases in IL-1β, cortisol, and liver enzymes. Notably, plasma LPS concentrations did not increase immediately after either administration, but increased hours later, showing a biphasic pattern during infusion. This distinct temporal profile suggests complex regulation of circulating LPS dynamics during systemic LPS exposure. While plasma LPS kinetics following bolus injection have been described in cattle and mice [48, 49], no study has evaluated LPS kinetics during continuous infusion, which may more closely reflect the physiological inflammatory status observed in clinical cases. Therefore, by examining systemic LPS exposure using experimental administration models, the present study provides the first interpretation of the association between the suppression of pulsatile LH secretion and LPS metabolism in circulation. This delayed increase in plasma LPS following LPS administration, together with elevated liver enzymes, suggests a progressive reduction in hepatic clearance capacity during continuous LPS exposure. Furthermore, plasma IL-1β and cortisol levels increased earlier than the rise in LPS and coincided with LH pulse suppression. These findings suggest that systemic immune activation and HPA-axis activation may contribute to the LPS-induced inhibition of LH pulses rather than the direct action of LPS alone. To date, no study has simultaneously examined LPS kinetics alongside the temporal profiles of cytokines, cortisol, and hepatic enzyme profiles during experimental systemic LPS exposure. The present study, therefore, offers an integrated perspective on how endocrine stress responses, inflammatory signaling, and hepatic detoxification interact during systemic LPS exposure.

The immediate inhibitory effects of systemic LPS exposure on pulsatile LH release observed in the present study are consistent with previous reports in other species, including rats [31, 32, 36, 50, 51], ewes [33, 41, 52], and cattle [53]. The primary effects of LPS were reflected in the suppression of plasma LH concentrations and pulse frequency, indicating disruption of the HPG axis at both the hypothalamic and pituitary levels. Firstly, LPS may impair the hypothalamic GnRH pulse generator, namely arcuate kisspeptin neurons, as evidenced by decreased MUA volley frequency in the ARC following LPS treatment in rats [54] and goats [38]. Another potential mechanism is the inhibition of hypothalamic GnRH release via suppression of GnRH gene expression in the POA [55, 56], resulting in reduced GnRH levels in the hypophyseal portal blood [52, 57]. An additional mechanism involves the pituitary, where LPS has been reported in ewes to reduce gonadotroph responsiveness to GnRH by downregulating GnRH receptor gene expression [55] and to suppress LH synthesis through decreased LHβ subunit expression [58].

The present findings should also be interpreted in the broader context of LPS-mediated reproductive suppression reported across mammalian species. Previous studies in rodents [31, 32], ewes [33], cattle [53], and goats [38] have consistently shown that systemic LPS exposure suppresses pulsatile LH secretion, suggesting that inflammatory inhibition of the HPG axis is a conserved neuroendocrine response across species. Experimental evidence further indicates that this suppression is mediated, at least in part, by inhibition of hypothalamic arcuate kisspeptin neurons together with activation of cytokine- and stress-related pathways [59,60,61]. In this context, the present goat model extends previous findings by providing an integrated temporal characterization of LH suppression together with circulating LPS dynamics, immune activation, endocrine stress responses, and hepatic functional changes. As a well-established and manageable ruminant model, goats also offer practical advantages for repeated serial blood sampling and simultaneous physiological assessment in conscious animals, making them particularly valuable for translational studies of inflammation-associated reproductive dysfunction in domestic ruminants. This broader physiological perspective may be particularly relevant to bovine inflammatory diseases, such as endometritis and mastitis, in which persistent endotoxemia and impaired hepatic detoxification may contribute to prolonged reproductive dysfunction.

Systemic LPS exposure induced inflammatory responses that were accompanied by suppression of pulsatile LH secretion, suggesting a close association between inflammatory activation and HPG-axis dysfunction. Understanding these responses may facilitate interpretation of reproductive dysfunction under clinically relevant inflammatory conditions. Consistent with previous studies [62, 63], rectal temperature increased during the early phase following LPS bolus injection. In contrast, LPS infusion caused only a transient increase in rectal temperature at 1 h, suggesting a milder and more physiological inflammatory response. While acute suppression of the HPG axis after LPS bolus injection has been well documented, little is known about LH secretion under sustained LPS exposure, which more closely reflects clinical cases in cows. In sheep, daily injections with increasing doses of LPS for one week significantly decreased LH pulse frequency and the number of kisspeptin-immunopositive cells in the ARC [64]. On the other hand, repeated LPS injection over six weeks increased plasma LH level in rats [65]. Such discrepancies might be attributed to LPS tolerance induced by repeated exposure [66] and compensatory feedback regulation of LH secretion. Therefore, alternative LPS administration methods, such as continuous LPS infusion, which minimize LPS tolerance effects and accurately mimic physiological or chronic inflammation, are needed to advance understanding of reproductive dysfunction in inflammatory conditions. In the present study, continuous LPS infusion was associated with suppression of LH pulsatility, biphasic changes in circulating LPS, and late elevation of AST, indicating coordinated endocrine and hepatic responses during sustained systemic LPS exposure. Both LPS bolus injection and continuous infusion induced apparent physiological responses, despite the approximately 12-fold greater total LPS dose administered during infusion. This may reflect the fundamentally different exposure profiles produced by the two administration paradigms. Whereas the bolus injection model likely generated a transient exposure with a relatively high peak concentration, the continuous infusion model was expected to produce more prolonged systemic exposure and greater cumulative AUC. It is therefore possible that suppression of pulsatile LH secretion and the associated inflammatory and endocrine responses had already approached a near-maximal level under the bolus condition. However, we cannot exclude the possibility that differences in temporal response patterns or in other inflammatory indices may have become evident if additional parameters had been examined or if the observation period had been extended. These findings support the utility of continuous LPS infusion as an experimental approach for investigating reproductive dysfunction under prolonged inflammatory conditions in ruminants.

Pro-inflammatory cytokines and stress-related hormones may mediate the detrimental effect of systemic LPS on the HPG axis, because plasma IL-1β, an inflammatory cytokine, and cortisol concentrations were elevated within the first hour after systemic LPS administration. Notably, these increases coincided with LH pulse suppression and preceded a detectable rise in circulating LPS, suggesting that the inflammatory response to peripheral LPS and HPA axis activation may contribute to the disruption of pulsatile LH secretion prior to the direct action of LPS in the hypothalamic level. IL-1β is a potent pro-inflammatory cytokine primarily produced by monocytes and macrophages [67]. When LPS enters the circulation, LPS binds to LPS-binding protein (LBP) and activates the NF-κB pathway in these cells, thereby enhancing the release of IL-1β into the bloodstream [68]. It is hypothesized that peripheral IL-1β can access hypothalamic GnRH neurons either through the circumventricular organs or by directly crossing the blood-brain barrier (BBB), thereby affecting hypothalamic kisspeptin and/or GnRH neuronal activity in turn [69]. Indeed, a previous study using female mice demonstrated that peripheral IL-1β disrupted pulsatile LH secretion by suppressing ARC kisspeptin neuron activity [70]. Furthermore, central administration of IL-1β decreased GnRH gene expression and lowered GnRH levels in cerebrospinal fluid in ewes [71]. These findings suggest that IL-1β mediates LPS-induced suppression of LH pulses by inhibiting hypothalamic kisspeptin neurons and consequently reducing GnRH release. Furthermore, upregulation of HPA axis [72] may be triggered by circulating LPS-induced cytokines, acting on brain endothelium to stimulate prostaglandin E2 (PGE2) production, which, in turn, stimulates corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH) neurons [61]. Since several studies have already demonstrated the inhibitory effects of CRH on GnRH neurons [73] and kisspeptin neurons [31], the elevated plasma cortisol concentration observed in this study suggests that the hypothalamic CRH may mediate the LPS-induced suppression of LH pulses. Taken together, these results suggest that systemic immune activation and stress responses, reflected by increased IL-1β and cortisol, may mediate LPS-induced suppression of pulsatile LH secretion.

The present findings indicate that systemic exposure to LPS may progressively impair hepatic detoxification, thereby contributing to the sustained presence of LPS that underlies its long-lasting (carry-over) effects on reproductive function. Clinical observation showed that LPS can persist in dairy cows with metritis for several weeks postpartum [12]. Notably, the present study showed that plasma LPS levels did not increase immediately after administration but rose at 7 h in the LPS-injected group and at 3 h in the LPS-infused group. The absence of an immediate increase in plasma LPS concentration following administration was unexpected. One possible explanation is the rapid hepatic uptake and detoxification of circulating LPS, which may prevent an early rise in measurable plasma LPS levels. As systemic inflammatory responses developed, hepatic clearance capacity may have been transiently compromised, resulting in a delayed increase in circulating LPS. In addition, the LAL assay used in the present study may not fully reflect circulating LPS levels, as binding of LPS to plasma components could reduce its detectability, particularly during the early phase following administration. Given the limited available data on circulating LPS kinetics in ruminants, further studies are required to clarify the mechanisms underlying these observations. In the infused group, plasma LPS concentrations showed a secondary rise at 6 h, followed by sustained elevation, suggesting impaired clearance under continuous exposure. The accompanying changes in liver enzymes may support this interpretation. Plasma GGT, a sensitive marker of early hepatobiliary dysfunction [74], increased within 3 h and remained elevated in both treatment groups, consistent with LPS-induced cholestasis arising from rapid activation of hepatic Kupffer cells [75]. In contrast, AST, reflecting more severe hepatocellular damage and necrosis [76], increased only at the end of the observation period during LPS infusion, implying progression to severe hepatocyte injury under continuous LPS challenge. Alterations in plasma NEFA, whose circulating levels are known to be regulated by hepatic lipid metabolism [77], provide additional evidence of compromised liver function, particularly the immediate rise and re-elevation at 6 h after LPS exposure. A previous study in heifers further supports this interpretation, showing that plasma LPS disappeared within 30 min in healthy heifers but persisted for more than 6 h in heifers with liver dysfunction following LPS administration, suggesting a strong association between plasma LPS persistence and liver function [49]. Taken together, the temporal patterns of plasma LPS and liver enzymes may reflect progressive hepatic impairment, which could reduce LPS clearance capacity and contribute to the sustained elevation of circulating LPS and its carry-over effects on reproductive dysfunction.

In conclusion, this study provides an integrative characterization of how systemic LPS exposure perturbs reproductive and hepatic function in ruminants. Our findings indicate that systemic immune activation and the stress-related endocrine response may be involved in the suppression of pulsatile LH secretion. The study further elucidates the kinetics of circulating LPS and its association with progressive hepatic dysfunction. Collectively, these findings advance our understanding of LPS-induced disruption of the HPG axis and provide a foundation for future studies investigating the mechanisms underlying the impaired reproductive performance following infection-induced inflammation. These insights may provide a basis for developing strategies to alleviate inflammation-associated infertility in ruminant livestock.
