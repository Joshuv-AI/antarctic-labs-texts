Identification of Clinical Characteristics and Risk Factors of Invasive Pulmonary Aspergillosis in Patients with Acquired Immunodeficiency Syndrome: A Retrospective Study
Authors: Weipeng Zhang, Yanrui Zhang, Yao Tang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionInvasive pulmonary aspergillosis (IPA) is a severe opportunistic infection in patients with acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS), characterized by nonspecific symptoms, delayed diagnosis, and high mortality. This study aimed to investigate the clinical characteristics and risk factors of IPA in AIDS patients.Patients and MethodsA retrospective analysis was conducted on 49 AIDS patients treated at the Xi’an Eighth Hospital between January 2022 and May 2024. The patients were divided into an IPA group (n=24) and a non-IPA group (n=25). The non-IPA group was used as the control group. Demographic information, medical records, laboratory features, and imaging findings were collected. The risk factors for IPA infection in AIDS patients were analyzed using multivariate logistic regression analysis.ResultsAmong AIDS patients with IPA, the predominant clinical manifestations included chest tightness, dyspnea, cough, expectoration, fatigue, and fever. The proportion of patients with a history of smoking and concomitant antibiotic use was higher in the IPA group than that in the control group. In contrast, the peripheral blood white cell (WBC) count, CD4+T lymphocyte count, and serum albumin (ALB) level were significantly lower in the IPA group than in the non-IPA group (P<0.05). Multivariate logistic regression analysis identified concomitant use of antibiotics, reduced CD4+T lymphocyte count, and decreased ALB levels as independent risk factors for IPA in AIDS patients (P<0.05).ConclusionConcomitant use of antibiotics, reduced CD4+T lymphocyte count, and decreased ALB levels are independent risk factors for IPA in patients with AIDS. Early identification of these risk factors and timely initiation of appropriate clinical interventions are essential to reduce the incidence and improve the prognosis of IPA in AIDS patients.

Introduction

Acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS) is a communicable disease caused by infection with the human immunodeficiency virus (HIV), which targets CD4 + T lymphocytes and progressively impairs immune function, ultimately predisposing patients to a wide spectrum of opportunistic infections and malignancies.1,2 As viral replication advances and immune destruction reaches a critical threshold, profound immunodeficiency develops, rendering individuals highly susceptible to bacterial, viral, and fungal infection. Among fungal infections, invasive pulmonary aspergillosis (IPA) is particularly insidious, often presenting with nonspecific clinical manifestations, leading to delayed diagnosis, high mortality, and poor clinical outcomes.3 Aspergillus fumigatus is the predominant pathogenic species responsible for IPA in AIDS patients, accounting for 75–90% of severe cases, followed by Aspergillus flavus.4 Clinical diagnosis of IPA is challenging due to nonspecific manifestations (e.g., dyspnea, chest tightness) that overlap with other opportunistic infections. Culture-based methods have low positivity rates and long turnaround times, while molecular techniques (e.g., metagenomic sequencing) are required for species-level identification.5

HIV invades immune cells by targeting CD4 receptors on the surface of T lymphocytes, mononuclear macrophages, and dendritic cells, disrupting intracellular structures and abrogating their immune protective functions, which further increases the susceptibility to opportunistic fungi including Pneumocystis, Talaromyces marneffei, and Aspergillus.6,7 With the gradual increase in the global AIDS population in recent years, the incidence of IPA has risen significantly, yet research on IPA in AIDS patients remains relatively limited both domestically and internationally, with gaps in the understanding of its clinical characteristics and independent risk factors.

To improve understanding of its epidemiology and clinical characteristics, the present study conducts a retrospective analysis of AIDS patients, with the aim of elucidating the clinical features and risk factors associated with AIDS-related IPA, thereby providing evidence to support timely diagnosis and optimized clinical management of this life-threatening infection.

Materials and Methods

Study Plan

We retrospectively collected data from patients diagnosed with AIDS between January 2022 and May 2024 at Xi’an Eighth Hospital. A total of eligible 49 patients were included in this study. Based on their aspergillosis infection status, the 49 patients were divided into IPA (n=24) and non-IPA (n=25) groups. This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of The Eighth Hospital of Xi’an (approval no.20251108). Given the retrospective study design and the use of anonymized patient data, the requirement for informed consent was waived.

Inclusion Criteria

The diagnostic criteria for IPA in patients with HIV were based on Infectious Diseases Society of America guidelines of “Practice Guidelines for the Diagnosis and Management of Aspergillosis” in the USA.8 All patients were over 18 years old, had complete clinical and laboratory data available, and were in the advanced stage of HIV infection or the presence of AIDS-defining illnesses.

Exclusion Criteria

Patients were excluded if they did not meet the diagnostic criteria for AIDS or IPA, had a history of long-term systemic glucocorticoid use (prednisone or equivalent for >2 weeks), or had severe hepatic or renal dysfunctions. Individuals with hematological malignancies or a history of solid organ transplantation were also excluded.

Data Collection

Clinical data were extracted from the hospital’s Health Information System (HIS). Baseline demographic and clinical variables included age, sex, body mass index (BMI), clinical symptoms, history of highly active antiretroviral therapy (HAART), routes of transmission, antibacterial therapy, smoking history, lymphadenopathy, comorbid tuberculosis, and history of alcohol consumption. Imaging data consisted of chest computed tomography (CT) findings, which were reviewed to assess characteristic radiological features, including pulmonary consolidation, nodular lesions, cavity formation, and the presence of the air crescent (meniscus) sign. The laboratory parameters collected included white blood cell (WBC) count, CD4 + T-lymphocyte count, serum albumin (ALB) levels, prothrombin time (PT), and plasma D-dimer concentration. IPA diagnosis was based on a combination of clinical manifestations, chest CT findings, and metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) of bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF). BALF samples (5 mL) were collected via bronchoscopy, and metagenomic sequencing was performed using the MetaCAP platform (KingMed Group, Guangzhou, China) to detect Aspergillus. Concomitant antibiotic use included the administration of broad-spectrum antibiotics for >72 hours during hospitalization; the above using antibiotic patients were all included in the subsequent statistical analysis. Antifungal susceptibility testing was not performed in the present study.

Statistical Analysis

SPSS 25.0 (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA) was used for statistical analysis. Categorical variables were expressed as numbers or percentages, and the chi-square (χ2) test was used for comparison. Results

Clinical Characteristics and Imaging Findings of AIDS Patients with IPA

As shown in Table 1, among the 24 patients co-infected with AIDS and IPA, the main clinical manifestations included dyspnea, chest tightness, cough, expectoration, fatigue, fever, weight loss, and dizziness. Dyspnea and chest tightness accounted for more than 50% of the symptoms, whereas the symptoms of weight loss and dizziness were relatively mild. The main CT finding in AIDS patients with IPA was patchy exudation shadows, which accounted for 54.17%. The probability of cavities, ground-glass opacity (GGO), and air crescent signs in the chest can also be observed (Table 1 and Figure 1). Among IPA patients, mNGS analysis identified Aspergillus fumigatus as the predominant pathogen (15/24, 62.5%), followed by Aspergillus flavus (6/24, 25.0%). Multivariate Analysis of Risk Factors for AIDS Patients Complicated with IPA

Smoking history, concomitant antibiotic use, peripheral WBC count, CD4+T-lymphocyte levels, and ALB levels were included in multivariate logistic regression analysis. As shown in Table 3, concomitant use of antibiotics, decreased CD4+T-lymphocyte levels, and reduced ALB levels were identified as independent risk factors for IPA infection in AIDS patients (P < 0.05). The goodness-of-fit of the final logistic regression model was assessed by the Nagelkerke pseudo-R2 (0.567), indicating acceptable model fit. Multicollinearity was evaluated using the variance inflation factor (VIF); all VIF values were <2.0, indicating no significant multicollinearity. As shown in Figure 2, Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis identified an optimal CD4+T-lymphocyte count cutoff of 96 cells/μL for predicting IPA risk, with a sensitivity of 62.5%, specificity of 88.0%, and area under the curve (AUC) of 0.783 (95% CI: 0.655–0.912). Discussion

This study conducted a retrospective analysis of the clinical cases of AIDS complicated with IPA infection, aiming to identify and validate concomitant use of antibiotics, decreased CD4+T-lymphocyte levels, and reduced ALB levels as key high-risk factors, and thereby provide a scientific and theoretical basis for early intervention targeting these Aspergillus infection-associated risk factors, ultimately reducing the incidence and mortality of IPA infection in the AIDS/HIV population.

A total of 49 AIDS patients were included in this study, of whom 24 had IPA infection. Most early diagnoses were based on the detection of Aspergillus spp. in patients’ BALF samples. In the AIDS patients with IPA in our study, respiratory symptoms including dyspnea, chest tightness, cough, and sputum production were common, which was consistent with previous studies.9–11 Systematic analyses confirm that invasive pulmonary aspergillosis in HIV-positive patients is characterized predominantly by respiratory symptoms such as cough and dyspnea and remains associated with substantial mortality due to delayed diagnosis.12 The main reason may be that severe cellular immune deficiency caused by HIV infection leads to uncontrolled invasion and proliferation of Aspergillus spp. in the lung parenchyma, resulting in extensive inflammatory infiltration, tissue necrosis, and impaired gas exchange, thereby triggering prominent respiratory manifestations.12–14 Thus, when encountering patients with these symptoms clinically, clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for IPA, identify those at high risk, and implement targeted early prevention and treatment in a timely manner.

CD4+T lymphocytes are the primary target cells of HIV infection.15 Following HIV infection, the virus specifically recognizes and enters the host cells with the help of its receptors, followed by completing transcription, translation and replication, ultimately leading to a progressive reduction of CD4+T lymphocytes, subsequently causing various opportunistic infections in patients.16–18 Therefore, CD4+T lymphocytes counting is an important indicator for evaluating the immune-inflammatory status and therapeutic response in AIDS patients. In a case series including CD4 counts in 140 patients, of whom 95% had a CD4 count <100 cells/μL.19 Furthermore, a global analysis pooling 39 studies and 454 HIV‑infected patients with aspergillosis reported that 98.2% had a CD4 count <100 cells/μL.12 In our study, 66.7% patients in the IPA group had a CD4+ T‑lymphocyte count <100 cells/μL, which was consistent with the aforementioned findings. The median CD4+T lymphocyte count in the IPA group was 41.5 cells/μL, significantly lower than that in the non-IPA group. ROC curve analysis demonstrated that the AUC of CD4+ T‑lymphocyte count was 0.783. At a cutoff value of 96 cells/μL, the sensitivity and specificity for predicting IPA were 62.5% and 88.0%, respectively, indicating relatively high predictive performance and good clinical reference value.

ALB can be synthesized exclusively by the liver and accounting for 50% to 60% of the total plasma protein.20 It plays multiple physiological functions, including regulating fluid balance between the intravascular and extravascular spaces, prevents tissue edema by maintaining colloid osmotic pressure, mitigating oxidative damage through free radical binding, and participating in tissue repair.21,22 Moreover, studies have shown that ALB plays a regulatory role in various inflammatory responses and influences development of diseases.18,23 In healthy individuals, the liver normally produces the albumin needed by the human body. However, in AIDS patients, the destruction of the immune system leads to impaired hepatic function, reducing the ability to synthesize ALB and lowering its concentration.24 In this study, the median ALB level in the IPA group was 29.6 (23.7–34.1) g/L, compared with 36.2 (32.2–43.7) g/L in the non-IPA group (P<0.001). It means that patients with AIDS and IPA exhibited significantly decreased serum ALB levels. This observation aligns with previous evidence: the risk of 12-week mortality was significantly reduced by 70% in the middle serum albumin group (28–34 g/L) and by 40% in the high serum albumin group (≥34 g/L), compared with the low albumin group (<28 g/L).25 It demonstrates that hypoalbuminemia is a consistent biomarker and independent risk factor for invasive fungal infections in immunocompromised HIV/AIDS patients.26

Among the three independent risk factors, concomitant use of ≥2 antibacterial agents during hospitalization showed the highest OR, indicating that AIDS patients exposed to multiple antibacterial combination therapy had a >19‑fold higher risk of Aspergillus infection than those without. In a research of ICU patients with sepsis, using of ≥3 antibacterial agents was associated with an adjusted odds ratio of approximately 2.3 for invasive fungal infection compared with a single antibacterial agent.27 This may be attributed to the disruption of human commensal microbiota caused by frequent antibiotic adjustments or unnecessary combination therapy. Once microbial dysregulation exceeds a critical threshold, opportunistic fungal infections may subsequently occur.28–30

Several methodological limitations should be acknowledged. First, this retrospective single‑center study may carry selection bias and restrict causal inference. Second, although mNGS improved microbiological diagnosis, no universal standard exists for interpreting mNGS results in pulmonary aspergillosis, since quantitative parameters including read counts, relative abundance, and genome coverage remain unstandardized. Finally, the clinical management of pulmonary aspergillosis in AIDS patients are complicated by low CD4+T-lymphocyte count, hypoalbuminemia, and concomitant use of antibiotics, all of which may affect disease susceptibility, diagnostic accuracy, and outcomes. These factors should be carefully considered when interpreting our findings and applying them to clinical practice.

Conclusion

In conclusion, IPA in AIDS patients is insidious with nonspecific clinical manifestations. Concomitant use of antibiotics, decreased CD4+T-lymphocyte levels, and reduced ALB levels are independent risk factors for IPA. These findings provide practical biomarkers for clinical risk stratification and support early intervention in high-risk patients.
