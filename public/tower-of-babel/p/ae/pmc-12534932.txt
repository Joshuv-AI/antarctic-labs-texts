Minimizing transaortic valve gradient in a young female with a large body habitus and small annulus: a case report
Authors: Shuyang Lu, Takahiro Kitsuka, Ran Huo, Paul C. Tang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAortic valve replacement in the setting of a small aortic anulus and a large body habitus is challenging. A surgical aortic valve replacement (AVR) implanted circumferentially onto this native annulus will predictably result in patient-prosthesis mismatch (PPM). We discuss the decision making process for the management of this patient.Case presentationWe report our management strategy for a younger patient (55 years old) with a very large body habitus (body mass index = 63.3 kg/m2). She had severe aortic stenosis in a small aortic annulus (diameter = 18 mm) and moderate-severe mitral stenosis. She underwent double mechanical valve replacement which included a Y-incision root enlargement to reduce obstruction to the left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT) by displacing the structural components of the On-X valve into the enlarged root away from the LVOT.ConclusionsA Y-incision root enlargement is an effective strategy to maximize use of the native LVOT to avoid PPM. In the setting of a mechanical AVR, this is expected to result in favorable long-term outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13019-025-03597-2.

Introduction

A small aortic anulus with a large body habitus presents a unique challenge. Surgical aortic valve replacement (SAVR) often further reduces the native effective orifice area (EOA) leading to patient-prosthesis mismatch (PPM) [1] often as a result of the prosthetic valve frame occupying the annular diameter and narrowing the outflow tract. There are various root enlargements techniques [2] but the Y-incision with rectangular patch technique have gained recent popularity [3]. The manner in which it achieves higher EOA are frequently debated, and the ease of performing a transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) in this setting remains to be characterized.

Case

55-year-old woman with body mass index of 63.3 kg/m2 and body surface area of 2.85 cm2 had progressively worsening heart failure symptoms. She has hypertension, diabetes, lymphedema, gastroesophageal reflux, and obstructive sleep apnea. Echocardiogram showed left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) 68%, severe aortic stenosis (mean gradient 47 mmHg, area of 0.77 cm2) with moderate aortic insufficiency, and moderate-severe mitral stenosis (mean gradient 9 mmHg, and area of 1.5cm [2] Supplementary Video 1). There was no coronary artery disease, pulmonary artery pressure was 67/24 mmHg, and pulmonary capillary wedge pressure was 22 mmHg. Aortic annulus diameter was 18 mm while the sinotubular junction was 24 mm on imaging (Fig. 1). She preferred a mechanical prosthesis.

Operative technique

Following median sternotomy and initiation of cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) with cardioplegia administration, the ascending aorta was transected down toward the left-noncoronary commissure. The calcified bicuspid aortic valve with left-right cusp fusion was excised, and annulus diameter was confirmed to be 18 mm using hagar dilator assessment. This annulus tightly fits a 19 mm On-X sizer which will likely cause PPM. The aortotomy was extended as a Y-incision towards the left-non-commissure [3] with incisions extended toward the left and noncoronary side just underneath the aortic annulus. A generous rectangular bovine pericardial patch was then sewn with running 5/0 prolene to the annulus and cut edge of the aorta for root enlargement to allow placement of at least a 23 mm aortic On-X prosthesis. Through a trans-septal approach, the calcified anterior mitral leaflet was removed with partial excision of the posterior leaflet and chords for annular continuity followed by mitral 25/33 On-X mechanical valve implantation.

We then returned to the enlarged aortic root which sized to a 23 mm On-X mechanical valve. We prefer a “standard” On-X cuff as the wider and more rigid cuff tends to lift the valve leaflets away from the underlying patch for free movement. Turning the leaflets can finetune leaflet placement to prevent restriction from underlying abutting structures. After marking the pericardial patch where the prosthesis rides, pledgeted horizontal mattress stitches were used on the native annulus, and on the patch and the valve secured with CorKnot device. Aortic incision was closed by incorporating the patch. Discharge echocardiography demonstrated LVEF of 65%, mean prosthetic aortic valve gradient was 10 mmHg with EOA of 2.07 cm2, and mean mitral gradient was 3 mmHg with EOA of 2.27 cm2 (Supplementary Video 2). Postoperative course was uneventful with discharge to a rehabilitation center followed by dismissal to home 2 weeks later. At 6 months postoperatively, she denied dyspnea and had significantly improved functional status.

Discussion

Lifetime management for valvular disease has led to an emphasis on surgical root/annular enlargements. Even for mechanical valves with low reintervention rates, PPM has a negative impact on survival [1]. While the Y-incision root enlargement proposed by Yang et al.3 does not enlarge the annulus or left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT), it maximizes access to the native annulus area by shifting space occupying valve structural components into the noncoronary sinus (Fig. 2). The majority of adults should have a native aortic annular EOA that is adequate without generating a significant resting gradient. Outflow restriction arises when a smaller native aortic annulus that is just adequate relative to body size experiences a further reduction in EOA. This occurs with circumferential annular implantation of a SAVR where the valve frame occupies luminal space. The Y-incision technique displaces valve structural components into the noncoronary sinus which preserves more of the native LVOT area.

It is important to note that the prosthetic valve frame is displaced from the native annulus toward the noncoronary sinus which is augmented with a rectangular patch subtending the prosthesis. It is likely that “right-sizing” the enlargement so the prosthetic valve frame is juxtaposed or close to the edge of the native aortic annulus in the area of the patch augmentation will facilitate future TAVR.

Mechanical aortic valve replacement is an excellent use of the Yang technique given its durability and relative simplicity that allows maximal use of the native annular EOA. There are also no transcatheter intervention anatomic considerations in the setting of a mechanical SAVR. It has worked very well in this physically large and younger patient with a very small aortic annulus. While a “Commando” procedure is an alternative, we feel the current approach is efficient for reaching our surgical objectives of achieving a low transaortic valve gradient to avoid PPM and to minimize operative insult in a challenging patient. While the LVOT is not enlarged, this approach allows prosthesis structures to be displaced into the enlarged root to increase access to the native EOA to avoid PPM.
