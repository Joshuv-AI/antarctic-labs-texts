Caregivers with poorer health are less likely to engage in an intervention to prepare for their own health care emergency
Authors: Kris F Wain, Andrea E Daddato, Blythe Dollar, James Lagrotteria, Heather M Young, Anna Satake, Chan Zeng, Rebecca S Boxer, Joseph E Gaugler
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and ObjectivesPreparation for an unexpected caregiver health event may improve outcomes for spouses with dementia. Using the patient activation framework, this study examined characteristics of spousal caregivers who did and did not accept an offer for an emergency preparedness toolkit (EPT), a low-touch intervention designed to help caregivers prepare for their own unexpected health event.Research Design and MethodsCaregivers were linked to spouses with dementia and invited to request the EPT. Pre-exposure characteristics were measured during the 12-months preceding the EPT invitation date; post-exposure measures were captured during the 6-months after invitation. Multivariable logistic regression estimated associations between caregiver characteristics and EPT request, reported as marginal effects expressed in percentage point (pp) differences. Chi-square tests compared post-exposure measures by EPT request.ResultsWe identified 1,062 eligible caregivers; 489 (46%) requested the EPT and 573 (54%) did not. Each additional chronic condition in the caregiver was associated with a 2.7pp decrease in EPT request. A caregiver hospitalization was associated with a 12.2pp decrease. Post-exposure analyses estimated caregivers not requesting the EPT were 1.9pp more likely to be hospitalized and discharged to a skilled-nursing-facility, and 1.9pp more likely to die during follow-up.Discussion and ImplicationsSpousal caregivers with poorer health were less likely to respond to an offer to prepare for their own health event. Yet, caregivers at higher risk for health events may be most in need of emergency preparedness. As reliance on spousal caregivers grows, interventions that better balance caregivers’ self-care with responsibilities for a spouse with dementia may strengthen preparedness and improve health outcomes for both partners.Keywords: Dementia, Hospitalizations, Spousal Dyads

Spouses of people with dementia are often the primary caregiver and serve as a vital support in sustaining their partner’s quality of life (Kasper et al., 2015). In 2023, more than 11 million family members and other unpaid caregivers provided an estimated 18.4 billion hours of care to individuals with dementia (Alzheimer’s Association, 2024). Compared to other types of family caregivers, spouses providing care to people with dementia are more likely to suffer from poor physical health and psychological morbidity (Brodaty & Donkin, 2009). Notably, more than half of spousal caregivers received no assistance from other family members or friends when providing care to their spouse with dementia during the last years of life (Ornstein et al., 2019). If the spousal caregiver becomes ill or injured, substitute caregiver options may be required, but these substitutes may lack the knowledge to properly care for the person with dementia (National Research Summit on Care, Services, and Supports for Persons with Dementia and Their Caregivers, 2018). Recent evidence suggests that if a caregiver is hospitalized, the risk of hospitalization also increases for the spouse with dementia (Boxer et al., 2023). Identification and preparation of a substitute caregiver that can immediately step in for the primary caregiver may reduce the risk of adverse outcomes for the spouse with dementia.

Although many interventions have focused on psychosocial support for family caregivers of people with dementia (Teahan et al., 2020), few have been designed to ensure continuity of care for spouses with dementia when caregivers face temporary interruptions due to their own health challenges. This topic has garnered greater attention among grandparent caregivers and parents who care for children with developmental or intellectual disabilities (Lee & Burke, 2020; Taylor et al., 2019). Additionally, factors influencing caregivers’ decision to establish a backup plan remain poorly understood. Although limited, existing evidence suggests that healthier caregivers are more engaged and better able to provide consistent care for people with dementia (Palaza et al., 2024).

This study was guided by the Patient Activation conceptual model that reflects a range of readiness on the part of individuals to engage in health-seeking behaviors, including preventive measures. Those on the lower end of readiness lack confidence, may be overwhelmed, and believe health is largely out of their control, while those on the higher end of readiness are more able to take action and adopt behaviors that advance their health. Extensive research has established that higher patient activation is associated with better clinical and utilization outcomes (Janamian et al., 2022). Clinicians can influence activation by increasing skills and mastery, facilitating peer support, providing education and coaching, and encouraging shared decision making (Hibbard & Gilburt, 2014). In this study, we conceptualized a caregiver’s decision to request an emergency planning toolkit as an indicator of a high level of patient activation. Caregivers who are more engaged in health-seeking behaviors may be more likely to maintain up-to-date contingency plans and prepare for potential care transitions. However, it is also possible that highly engaged caregivers have already established care transition plans and therefore do not perceive a need for additional planning tools. In contrast, less engaged caregivers may be less likely to anticipate the implications of their own illness and may forgo emergency planning. We explored the role of predisposing characteristics including demographics, utilization, chronic conditions, and socioeconomic status (SES) on the choice to request the emergency preparedness toolkit (EPT).

The primary aim of this study was to examine differences in characteristics between spousal caregivers who requested or did not request the EPT. The EPT was offered through a low-touch intervention and was designed to help a caregiver prepare a substitute caregiver to immediately step in and care for the person with dementia if the spousal caregiver experienced an unexpected health event. Identifying caregiver characteristics that were associated with requesting the EPT can inform the design of future interventions aimed at strengthening caregiver preparedness for their own health emergency.

Research design and methods

Study setting and data

This study was conducted within Kaiser Permanente region of Colorado (KPCO), an integrated healthcare system serving as both provider and insurer with an enrolled population of approximately 550,000 members. Electronic health record (EHR) and administrative data were obtained from the KPCO Virtual Data Warehouse (VDW), a standardized data model containing information on diagnostic and procedural codes, healthcare utilization, enrollment, demographics, death, and SES (Ross et al., 2014). Data were collected for both the spousal caregiver and the person with dementia during the 12-month period prior to the date the EPT request form was sent, and for the 6-months afterward. All study data were managed using REDCap electronic data capture tools hosted by KPCO (Harris et al., 2009, 2019). Study protocol and human subjects protections were reviewed and approved by the KPCO Institutional Review Board (#1768361).

Participants

People with dementia aged 55 and older were identified using International Classification of Disease Version 10 (ICD-10) diagnostic codes as described in Supplementary Table S1. To form the dyad, the person with dementia was linked to an individual who self-identified as a spouse or domestic partner using membership-level data and mailing address; this methodology has been described previously (Boxer et al., 2023). Both the person with dementia and caregiver were required to be enrolled in the KPCO health system as of January 19, 2023, the earliest date the EPT request form was distributed to caregivers. Both members of the dyad were excluded if the person with dementia’s home address was listed as a nursing home or assisted living facility, if the caregiver participated in a prior focus group that contributed to the EPT design, or the caregiver did not speak English.

Intervention

Eligible spousal caregivers were invited to request and complete the EPT, a fillable document organized into multiple domains as shown in Supplementary Figure S1. The EPT begins with an overview of its purpose, followed by sections outlining the person with dementia’s routines and behaviors, instructions for managing medical equipment and chronic medications, guidance for handling medical emergencies, information on advance care planning, and contact information for family members and relevant healthcare professionals. The completed EPT was intended to provide instructions for a substitute caregiver in the event of the primary caregiver’s hospitalization or illness. Delivered as a low-touch, asynchronous intervention requiring no in-person visits, the EPT was sent to caregivers via their preferred method, either traditional mail or email. It was designed to be scalable and adaptable across health systems and was developed with input from key stakeholders, including spousal caregivers of persons with dementia, clinicians, and community advocates.

Leveraging EHR data, 1,130 individuals were identified as potentially eligible caregivers of people with dementia (Figure 1). Between January and June 2023, a request form was distributed to potentially eligible caregivers to confirm eligibility and assess their interest in receiving the EPT as shown in Supplementary Figure S2. To limit the impact of digital access we used a multistep approach to assure that caregivers received the intervention invitation. Those with an email address in the EHR were emailed the request form on January 19, 2023 (n = 1,016); if no response was received, they were emailed up to two additional times before being mailed a paper copy. Those without an email address listed in their EHR were mailed a copy of the request form on February 3, 2023 (n = 114). The date the request form was first sent was designated as the index date, regardless of whether the caregiver requested the EPT. Among all individuals contacted, 15 did not receive the request form because both the email and residential address listed in the EHR were incorrect. Of the 542 caregivers who requested the EPT, 489 were confirmed as eligible based upon self-reported responses in the request form, while 53 were deemed to be ineligible and excluded from the study sample (Figure 2). However, the EPT was provided to all caregivers who requested it, irrespective of eligibility. An additional 573 caregivers were identified as potentially eligible using EHR data but did not request the EPT.

Covariates

Pre-exposure characteristics for both the caregiver and the person with dementia were calculated during the 12-month period prior to the index date and included data on demographics, utilization, chronic conditions, and SES. Age at index date was included as a continuous measure. Self-reported racial categories were mutually exclusive and included Asian, Black, White, Unknown, and other minority races comprising Native Hawaiian/Pacific Islander, American Indian/Alaska Native, and Multiracial. A separate binary flag identified individuals who self-reported as Hispanic. Comorbidity status was calculated using the Elixhauser Coding Algorithm, the number of unique chronic conditions grouped into categories of 0, 1 or 2, 3 to 5, and ≥6 (Quan et al., 2005). Among people with dementia, we created an additional binary variable to identify severe dementia diagnosis with behavioral disorders using ICD-10 codes described in Supplementary Table S1. Binary indicators were used to capture healthcare utilization by care type, including hospitalizations, emergency department visits, urgent care, primary care, and specialty care. SES was identified by mapping the caregiver’s residential address to census-tract-level data. Because the caregiver and person with dementia were required to live at the same residential address, census-based measures of SES were shared for the dyad. The average family income was divided into quintiles at the census-tract level, 1 representing the least affluent census tracts, and 5 the most affluent. Similarly, the percentage of individuals in a census-tract with an associate’s degree or greater was split into quintiles, 1 representing least educated, and 5 most educated.

Post-exposure measures for both the spousal caregiver and person with dementia were calculated during the 6-month period after the index date. The 6-month follow-up period was chosen based on our previous work examining health outcome relationships between the members of the dyad (Boxer et al., 2023). Hospitalizations were extracted from VDW data and evaluated as a binary indicator for use (yes/no). In addition, we separately examined the subset of hospitalizations that involved a skilled nursing facility stay within 30 days following discharge. Hospice care and death were identified from VDW data and assessed as binary variables.

Statistical analysis

Pre-exposure characteristics between caregivers who requested the EPT and caregivers who did not request the EPT were compared using chi-square tests for categorical variables and two sample t-tests for continuous variables (Satterthwaite, 1946). Similarly, we compared pre-exposure characteristics between groups for people with dementia.

We estimated a logistic regression model to better understand the caregiver’s pre-exposure characteristics associated with requesting the EPT. Marginal effects (ME) were reported for each caregiver characteristic, holding all other covariates at their mean values. In this study, the ME is interpreted as a percentage point (pp) difference between caregivers who did and did not request the EPT. Because of small sample sizes for races other than White, we chose to group race into White and non-White for regression modeling. Emergency department and urgent care utilization were combined into a single category because they were highly correlated and likely explained a similar effect; similarly, outpatient primary and specialty care services were combined.

Due to the limited post-exposure measures available during the 6-month follow-up period, we were unable to estimate logistic regression models. Instead, we compared post-exposure measures for both caregivers and people with dementia by EPT request status using chi-square tests for categorical variables. To minimize the risk of patient re-identification from small cell sizes, we chose to only report post-exposure effect sizes within the text and did not include a detailed table with counts.

Sensitivity analysis

Our primary analysis excluded 53 dyads in which the caregiver requested the EPT but was determined ineligible based on their self-reported survey responses. In contrast, for caregivers who did not request the EPT, study eligibility was determined solely using EHR data. To assess whether the additional exclusion criteria for EPT requesters affected our findings, we re-estimated our logistic regression model and included the 53 dyads excluded from our primary analysis. All analyses were performed using SAS software version 9.04 for SAS Studio (SAS Institute, Inc., Cary, NC) and STATA® Statistical Software version 18.0 (StataCorp. 2019. Stata Statistical Software: Release 16. College Station, TX: StataCorp LLC).

Results

A cohort of 1,062 eligible dyads were offered the EPT (Figure 1). There were 489 (46%) caregivers who requested the EPT and 573 (54%) caregivers who did not request the EPT. Pre-exposure characteristics for caregivers by whether they requested the EPT are shown in Table 1. Caregivers less likely to request the EPT were male as compared to female caregivers (Requested EPT: 38.5% male vs 61.5% female, Did Not Request EPT: 45.6% male vs 54.5% female; p = .02), had more diagnosed chronic conditions (Requested EPT: mean of 4.4 chronic conditions, Did Not Request EPT: mean of 4.2 chronic conditions; p < .001), and had a hospitalization (Requested EPT: 13.4%, Did Not Request EPT: 7.0%; p < .001). Characteristics of people with dementia by whether the EPT was requested showed that caregivers were less likely to request the EPT when the person with dementia was slightly younger (Requested EPT: 79.4, Did Not Request EPT: 78.4 years; p = .03), Hispanic (Requested EPT: 10.0%, Did Not Request EPT: 15.2%; p = .01), or had a hospitalization (Requested EPT: 15.1%, Did Not Request EPT: 20.2%; p = .03).

Logistic regression estimates that evaluated pre-exposure caregiver characteristics associated with EPT request are presented in Figure 3. Female caregivers were 8.8pp more likely to request the EPT than male (ME = 8.8; 95% CI, 2.4 to 15.1). Caregivers who utilized outpatient primary or specialty care in the 12-month pre-exposure period were 16.3pp more likely to request than EPT than caregivers who did not (ME = 16.3; 95% CI, 2.9 to 29.7). Conversely, caregivers with a hospitalization in the pre-exposure period were 12.2pp less likely to request the EPT than caregivers with no hospitalization (ME = −12.2; 95% CI, −23.1 to −1.3). For each additional chronic condition diagnosed, caregivers were 2.7pp less likely to request the EPT (ME = −2.7; 95% CI, −4.2 to −1.2). Sensitivity analyses that included the 53 caregivers who requested the EPT but were ineligible based on their self-reported survey responses found similar results as shown in Figure 4. However, sensitivity also estimated that for each additional year of caregiver age, the likelihood of requesting the EPT increased by 0.5pp (ME = 0.5; 95% CI, 0.1 to 0.9).

Post-exposure measures that occurred during the 6-month period after the index date showed caregivers who did not request the EPT were 1.9pp more likely to be hospitalized and discharged to a skilled nursing facility (p < .01) and 1.9pp more likely to die (p = .01) (results not reported in table format to minimize risk of patient re-identification due to small cell sizes). For the people with dementia, there were no statistically significant differences in hospitalization or death between groups.

Discussion and implications

Among a cohort of spousal caregiver and person with dementia dyads, 54% did not request the EPT, indicating a relatively low level of activation among this population, compared to the 25–40% of other populations engaged in health behaviors (Hibbard & Cunningham, 2008). Further, we found that caregivers who had indications of increased health care needs and utilization were less likely to request the offered EPT. This suggests that caregivers who have the most need for emergency preparedness may be the least likely to engage when it is offered. The increased burden caregivers face from higher acuity health conditions likely leads to difficulties engaging in caregiver responsibilities (Navaie-Waliser et al., 2002). Caregivers may not see their own health as a priority and might not attend to their health care needs in a timely fashion (Tatangelo et al., 2018; Tingey et al., 2020). Engaging caregivers that have more complex healthcare needs may therefore require higher-touch, tailored strategies that address the needs of both the caregiver and the person with dementia, such as in-home visits, counseling, and coordinated care management (Elliott et al., 2010; Hibbard & Gilburt, 2014).

In this study, we found no difference in EPT requests based on whether the person with dementia had behavioral disorders or higher comorbidity scores, suggesting that the functional status of the person with dementia did not influence the caregiver’s decision to request the EPT, although our data were limited. In contrast, a recent study reported that caregivers of people with dementia with higher functional status were more likely to decline offers of supportive services (Zwingmann et al., 2020). Understanding how spousal caregivers engage with available support resources, and how this engagement relates to the health of both the caregiver and the person with dementia, is important for determining where to focus interventions and allocate limited resources.

Caring for a person with dementia can span many years and coincide with the emergence of chronic diseases in both parties, creating a dual trajectory of health and illness. Research has established that while caregiving has positive effects on well-being and purpose, there are risks for compromised health particularly with complex and lengthy caregiving (Quinn & Toms, 2019; Roth et al., 2015). Caregiving is associated with a number of health risks including cardiovascular disease (Haley et al., 2010), depression (Capistrant et al., 2014), cognitive decline (Vitaliano et al., 2011), and frailty (Cai et al., 2024). Caregivers with physical limitations or mental strain experience higher stress and increased risk of mortality (Bruce et al., 2005; Schulz & Beach, 1999). Researchers have demonstrated that caregivers of people with dementia at the end of life experienced increased frailty compared to non-dementia caregivers (Dassel & Carr, 2016). Healthcare providers should emphasize identifying patients who serve as caregivers for people with dementia. Providing supportive services and attending to caregivers’ health needs can reduce their risk of adverse health outcomes while also improving the well-being of the person with dementia (Gray et al., 2022).

We found that male caregivers were less likely to request the EPT. Prior research indicates that male caregivers are more likely to use formal resources, while female caregivers are more likely to use informal support from family and friends (Sun et al., 2008; Zodikoff, 2007). Additionally, the risk of adverse health outcomes differs by caregiver gender, with male caregivers more likely to experience greater mental/emotional strain and an elevated risk of stroke (Bakker et al., 2013; Haley et al., 2010). These findings suggest that future interventions should incorporate gender-sensitive support preferences (formal vs informal) to enhance engagement and incorporate gender-specific health monitoring to proactively identify and mitigate adverse health risks, potentially reducing the need for substitute caregivers.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. Although we were able to observe many clinical factors associated with requesting the EPT, we could not assess non-clinical factors that may have influenced this decision, such as whether the caregiver had already established a care transition plan prior to the EPT request, the quality of the caregiver–person with dementia relationship, the level of informal support available from family and friends, or whether a substitute caregiver has been identified. Distribution of the EPT was thoroughly tracked in our data; however, due to study design, we could not observe the extent to which it was completed or whether caregivers found it useful. Therefore, we were unable to evaluate its relationship to health outcomes or examine differences among caregivers who requested the EPT based on completion status. Additionally, sparse data during the 6-month follow-up period limited our ability to detect post-exposure differences in health outcomes based on the EPT request. SES was measured at the census tract level; individual-level measures may have revealed associations not detected using area-based measures. Health literacy would also be an important individual-level measure to include in future study. Our study was limited to spousal caregivers, so our findings may not extend to family caregivers who are not the spouse, nor to professional caregivers. Both members of the dyad were insured within the same integrated healthcare system at the time of outreach. Uninsured spousal caregivers, those in fee-for-service plans, and caregivers who receive care in a different healthcare system than their spouse with dementia may face additional barriers which may impact their decision to request the EPT.

Conclusions

The demand for family caregivers is growing rapidly due to the aging of the population. Supporting caregivers is instrumental to preventing poor outcomes for people with dementia. Spousal caregivers of people with dementia may be especially challenged due to advanced age and chronic comorbidity. Interventions to improve both the physical and mental health of caregivers have resulted in lower burden and greater ability to continue their role. Recognition of the intertwined health trajectories of the dyad provides a lens to promote and support the health of both the caregiver and the person with dementia. Given the shared risk for compromises in health, family level planning is vital to promote continuity of caregiving. Understanding the needs of caregivers as they navigate self-care and care for the person with dementia is essential for designing interventions that optimize health outcomes for both the caregiver and the person with dementia.
