Rapid neuronal labeling and functional imaging in the developing mouse brain with AAV-PHP.eB
Authors: Weiyi Ye, Mao Deng, Sijing Zhao, Xiaodong Chen, Simin Su, Xiaoqin Lian, Bowen Ren, Chuchu Qi, Jialong Li, Mei Fu, Tongtong Gao, Zhihan Bai, Minghan Li, Dihan Lu, Na Zhou, Shiqian Shen, Wenting Wang, Qian Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryNeuronal labeling in the developing brain is essential for studying the formation and maturation of neural circuits. Among labeling techniques, adeno-associated virus (AAV)-based gene delivery offers high efficiency and versatility. However, whether AAV can effectively label neurons in the developing brain has not been fully determined. Here, we demonstrate that administration of AAV-PHP.eB in neonatal mice results in neuronal transduction as early as 3 days after injection. By titrating the AAVs, we achieve sparse neuronal labeling and facilitate high-resolution reconstruction of dendritic morphology during the critical developmental period. Then, we extend this method to functional imaging by delivering GCaMP, allowing longitudinal in vivo two-photon calcium imaging in the immature brain. Our findings show that AAV-PHP.eB-mediated early postnatal delivery offers a rapid, efficient, and minimally invasive approach for neuronal labeling and functional imaging in the developing brain, providing opportunities to investigate neural circuit development at both structural and functional levels.Keywords: developing brain, AAV-PHP.eB, single-neuron labeling, calcium imaging, GCaMP

Introduction

The formation of functional neural circuits during early brain development underlies the emergence of cognition, perception, and behavior.1 At the cellular level, this process involves a series of dynamic and tightly regulated events—including dendritic growth, synapse formation, and activity-dependent refinement—that unfold over days to weeks. Capturing these events in vivo requires tools that allow for robust, high-resolution labeling and functional imaging of individual neurons throughout development. However, despite considerable technological advances, visualizing neuronal morphology and activity during early postnatal stages remains technically challenging. Viral-mediated gene delivery, particularly using adeno-associated viruses (AAVs), has emerged as a powerful strategy for neuronal labeling and manipulation. Among these, the engineered AAV-PHP.eB serotype has demonstrated efficient central nervous system (CNS) transduction via systemic injection in adult mice, crossing the blood-brain barrier (BBB) and enabling widespread labeling.2 We previously showed that retro-orbital injection of AAV-PHP.eB in adults enables brain-wide neuronal labeling suitable for high-resolution morphological reconstruction.3 However, whether this approach can be applied to the developing brain—where neurons differ markedly from their mature counterparts—remains unknown, as no study has systematically assessed the feasibility of AAV-PHP.eB for high-resolution morphological and functional imaging during early postnatal circuit maturation.

Here, we investigated whether AAV-PHP.eB-mediated systemic delivery could serve as a rapid and efficient method for neuronal labeling in the immature brain. We delivered AAV-PHP.eB vectors encoding a membrane-targeted EGFP construct via intravenous injection into neonatal mice and observed robust expression in diverse neuronal populations as early as 3 days post-injection. The labeling was sufficiently bright and consistent to support single-neuron analysis and dendritic arbor quantification. We further applied this method to functional imaging by combining genetically encoded calcium indicators with in vivo two-photon imaging to monitor cortical activity during early postnatal development. Using this approach, we successfully captured spontaneous neuronal activity at both population and single-neuron levels throughout the critical developmental period, observing the transition from synchronized to desynchronized activity that reflects circuit formation and refinement. In addition, we confirmed that this approach supports long-term tracing of neuronal structure and function from early development into adulthood without inducing detectable structural abnormalities or disrupting cortical network dynamics.

Results

AAV-PHP.eB enables rapid and efficient neuronal labeling in neonatal mouse brains following systemic delivery

AAV-PHP.eB has been widely used for efficient gene delivery across the BBB in adult mice, enabling transduction of neurons throughout the CNS.2 To evaluate the AAV-PHP.eB expression in the developing brain, we performed intravenous injections of AAVPHP.eB-hSyn-EGFP-P2A-EGFPf into the superficial temporal vein of neonatal mice at postnatal day 0 (P0) (Figures 1A–1C). The hSyn-driven construct co-expresses cytosolic and membrane-targeted EGFP reporters to facilitate visualization of fine neuronal morphology, including dendritic spines (Figure 1B).4,5 Surprisingly, EGFP expression was detectable at P3, much earlier than the typical 1–2 week lag often observed following AAV-mediated gene delivery in adult brains6 (Figures 1D and S1A). EGFP signal was widespread across the brain, with strong expression in layers 2–5 of the motor cortex and the olfactory bulb, known to undergo early postnatal maturation,7,8 but sparse labeling in the somatosensory cortex (S1) and most thalamic nuclei except the anterior group. In the cerebellum, robust Purkinje cell labeling was prominent in lobule X (nodulus) (Figure S1A).9 We then examined the EGFP expression 7, 14, and 21 days post-injection and found the overall spatial patterns largely preserved (Figure 1D). Quantification of EGFP/NeuN co-localization in multiple regions confirmed >95% neuronal specificity across stages, with a modest increase in labeling efficiency from P3 to P21 (Figures 1E–1G and S1B).10,11

To further examine whether the rapid onset of AAV-PHP.eB expression is specific to developmental stages, the same virus was intravenously delivered to adult mice (Figure S2). In adults, EGFP expression was sparse at 3 days and required approximately 7 days to achieve comparable structural labeling at the same viral dose, indicating slower onset than in neonates. Regional distribution also differed: neonatal injection showed enrichment in the hippocampus and brainstem with lobule X-restricted cerebellar labeling, whereas adult injection produced higher thalamic expression and more uniform cerebellar distribution (Figure S3). Together, these findings demonstrate that systemic delivery of AAV-PHP.eB enables rapid expression in both neonatal and adult mice, with significantly greater efficiency in neonates.

Cell-type-specific neuronal labeling enabled by promoter selection in neonatal brains

To assess promoter-dependent cell-type specificity, we delivered AAV-PHP.eB vectors expressing EGFP-EGFPf under CaMKIIα or Dlx5/6 promoters, targeting excitatory projection neurons12 and inhibitory interneurons,13 respectively (Figures 2A–2D). Both constructs showed detectable expression by P3. CaMKIIα-driven labeling was widespread in cortical and hippocampal regions, whereas Dlx5/6-driven expression was more restricted, prominently in the hippocampus, olfactory bulb, cortex, and striatum.14 Immunostaining confirmed high specificity, with >95% of CaMKIIα-labeled cells co-expressing CaMKIIα (Figures 2B and 2C) and approximately 80% of Dlx5/6-labeled cells co-localizing with GAD67 (Figures 2E and 2F). Notably, this specificity was consistent across developmental stages (P3, P7, and P14), indicating that promoter-driven cell-type-specific labeling remains stable throughout early postnatal maturation. Together, these results demonstrate that we can achieve cell-type-specific labeling in the developing brain using different promoters.

Establishing sparse single-neuron labeling for morphological reconstruction during early brain development

The rapid expression of AAV-PHP.eB in neonatal mice offers a great opportunity to label the neuronal morphology in the developing brains. To utilize this property, we aimed to establish a protocol for single neuron labeling across developmental stages. We systemically delivered AAV-PHP.eB-CaMKIIα-EGFP-P2A-EGFPf at varying doses at P0 and assessed labeling density at 7 and 14 days post-injection (Figures 3A and 3B). High titers (2 × 1010 vg/mouse) produced dense labeling that obscured individual arbors, whereas lower titers (6 × 108 vg/mouse) yielded well-isolated neurons with complete morphology (Figures 3C and 3D). Through this titration series, we identified an optimal dose of approximately 6 × 108 vg/mouse, which yielded clear and complete labeling of single neurons without excessive background in different regions. We applied the optimized protocol across several developmental stages (P3, P7, P14, and P21) and found it consistently produced sparse, high-contrast labeling of excitatory neurons across all time points in different brain regions (Figure S4). Based on these analyses, we now explicitly define a practical criterion for sparse labeling. A viral titer in the range of ∼5–10 × 108 vg/pup typically produces approximately 5–10 labeled neurons per ∼0.6 mm2 field of view (20× objective), providing sufficient separation for reliable single-neuron reconstruction. Together, these results establish a simple and reliable method for high-resolution single-neuron labeling during early post-natal development, achieved by leveraging the rapid transduction of AAV-PHP.eB and precise viral dosage control.

Distinct developmental trajectories of neuronal morphology across brain regions

To investigate neuronal maturation across brain regions and cell types, we applied the systematic AAVPHP.eB-CaMKIIα-EGFP-P2A-EGFPf sparse-labeling method into neonatal mice, followed by immunofluorescent staining and morphological reconstruction at P3, P7, P14, P21, and P60 across four distinct forebrain neuronal populations. S1 Layer 2/3 (L2/3) pyramidal neurons exhibited a biphasic pattern of dendritic complexity, with both basal and apical dendritic complexity (total length and Sholl area under the curve [AUC]) increasing from P3 to P21 before declining in adulthood (Figures 4A–4E). CA1 pyramidal neurons displayed compartment-specific developmental trajectories: basal dendrites peaked at P14 (AUC: 1745.50 ± 71.21) before regressing, while apical dendrites showed a continuous increase in complexity, reaching maximum values at P60 (AUC: 1,848.33 ± 229.82) (Figures 4F–4J). Dentate gyrus (DG) granule cells exhibited a more gradual, monotonic increase in dendritic complexity from P3 to P60, without a clear peak at juvenile stages (Figures 4K–4P). Similar to L2/3 pyramidal neurons in S1, medium spiny neurons (MSNs) in the striatum followed a biphasic trajectory, with rapid increases in dendritic complexity and length from P3 to P14, peaking at P14 (AUC: 1,827.20 ± 68.86; length: 2,359.33 ± 88.71 μm), and a gradual decline at P21 (Figures 4Q–4V).

Together, these data reveal remarkable diversity in the morphological maturation of forebrain neurons. These differences likely reflect region- and cell type-specific schedules of synaptogenesis and circuit refinement.

Rapid AAV-PHP.eB expression enables functional imaging of developing cortical circuits from neonatal stages

To complement these findings and gain insight into the functional organization of developing circuits, we sought to perform in vivo two-photon calcium imaging of neuronal activity using AAVPHP.eB-CaMKIIα-GCaMP6f. Although the systemic AAV-PHP.eB injection at P0 was sufficient to label neurons for morphological reconstruction, this approach was found to produce insufficient GCaMP expression for calcium imaging (Figure S5A). This phenomenon is likely due to a combination of factors including low spontaneous neuronal activity, immature calcium dynamics, limited GCaMP protein accumulation, insufficient viral dosage reaching critical brain regions and limited sensitivity of GCaMP6f. We then adopted an intracerebroventricular (ICV) injection at P0, which directly delivers the above virus (8 × 1010 vg/pup) into the cerebrospinal fluid (Figures 5A and 5B).15 As expected, ICV injection at P0 led to visible GCaMP expression in S1 L2/3 by P3 (Figure 5C). However, clear calcium transients were not observed. To verify whether GCaMP was functionally expressed, tail-pinch or tactile stimulation elicited robust, cortex-wide GCaMP fluorescence changes (5×; Figures 5D and S5D).16,17,18 However, high-magnification imaging (25×) revealed these fluctuations occurred predominantly in dendritic processes rather than the soma, suggesting sensory-evoked, dendrite-dominant calcium responses at this stage (Figures S5B and S5C).19,20 Notably, increasing scanning laser intensity triggered unexpected robust calcium transients (Figures 5E and S5E; Video S1). This incidentally confirmed that GCaMP expression was sufficient to report neuronal dynamics by P3 and that the sparse spontaneous activity observed reflects immature network states rather than low reporter levels.

Furthermore, we longitudinally recorded spontaneous neuronal activity from P3 to P24 and analyzed both temporal and spatial correlations to characterize the maturation of population dynamics (Figures 5F, S5F, and S5G). Spontaneous activity progressed from sporadic, infrequent calcium transients at P3 to robust, highly synchronized network-wide firing by P7, a coordinated state that persisted through P10 (Figure 5H).21,22,23,24 Strikingly, between P10 and P12, a rapid and dramatic transition was observed: the previously global synchrony gave way to a desynchronized activity pattern, reflected by a sharp decline in both pairwise correlation coefficients and spatial clustering (Figures 5F–5I, S5F, and S5G).25 In addition, event duration underwent a sharp decrease between P10 and P11, with a continuously increasing event frequency during this critical period (Figures 5J and 5K). This desynchronized state remained stable from P16 to P24, suggesting that the P10-P12 window constitutes a key transition phase in cortical circuit refinement and functional reorganization.26 Together, these results demonstrate that neonatal ICV injection of AAVPHP.eB-CaMKIIα-GCaMP6f enables high-resolution in vivo imaging, starting as early as P3.

Single-neuron tracking reveals heterogeneous activity trajectories during the critical period of network reorganization

To investigate how individual neurons contribute to population-level shift, we performed longitudinal two-photon calcium imaging of the same cortical region. Specifically, we focused on the P11-P15 window and tracked 122 excitatory neurons from three mice in the S1 L2/3 (Figure 6A; Video S2). We first examined the persistence of neuronal activity and found that most neurons (54.10 ± 17.05%) remained active across both time points, indicating overall stability of GCaMP expression and cellular viability. A subset of neurons displayed dynamic shifts in activity: 27.87 ± 11.23% transitioned from silent to active, while 6.56 ± 0.96% became silent by P15, and 11.48 ± 8.80% remained inactive throughout the period (Figures 6B and 6C). Correlation analysis of 122 neurons confirmed that most remained active during this phase, revealing a distance-independent desynchronization trend from P11 to P15 (Figures 6D–6F).

Next, we asked whether these neurons exhibited consistent changes in their functional coupling to the surrounding network. Interestingly, neurons segregated into three distinct groups based on their correlation trajectories: 61.48 ± 2.20% showed an initial high correlation with other neurons at P11, followed by a decline at P13 and a rebound at P15; 11.48 ± 3.42% showed a sustained decrease in correlation across time; and 27.05 ± 1.49% showed a gradual increase in correlation from P11 to P15 (Figures 6G–6I and S6).

To further characterize how neuronal activity evolves during the critical period at the single-cell level, we analyzed the temporal properties of calcium transients in the same population of tracked neurons (Figures 6J–6L). We found that the duration of calcium events gradually decreased from P11 to P15, suggesting a developmental refinement of spike timing and faster intracellular calcium dynamics as neurons mature (Figure 6K). In contrast, the frequency of calcium spikes progressively increased over the same period (Figure 6L), indicating enhanced firing rates or increased responsiveness to synaptic inputs. These changes reflect a developmental shift toward more temporally precise and frequent activity patterns, consistent with the maturation of ion channel expression and synaptic connectivity in cortical circuits.

To characterize inhibitory interneuron activity during the network desynchronization transition, we performed longitudinal calcium imaging from P10 to P15 using AAV-PHP.eB-Dlx5/6-GCaMP6f within the same cortical region. Similar to excitatory neurons, inhibitory interneurons exhibited a marked transition in activity during this developmental window (Figures S7A–S7E). Specifically, the frequency of calcium events increased significantly from P10 to P13 and then stabilized after P13 (Figures S7A and S7B). In contrast, event duration decreased significantly from P10 to P12, while calcium event amplitude remained relatively stable throughout this period (Figures S7C and S7D). Notably, a key difference between excitatory and inhibitory neurons emerged in the synchronization-desynchronization process (Figure S7E). While excitatory neurons displayed a pronounced switch from synchronized to desynchronized activity between P10 and P12 (Figures 5I and S7E), inhibitory interneurons remained largely synchronized throughout the critical period (Figure S7E). These results suggest that inhibitory interneurons follow a distinct developmental trajectory.

Together, these results reveal that during the critical period, neurons do not simply undergo changes in overall activity levels, but also exhibit refined temporal dynamics, which contribute to the functional reorganization of cortical networks observed at the population level.

Long-term expression and functional integrity of GCaMP-labeled neurons

Since long-term expression of GCaMP could potentially interfere with calcium signaling and neuronal physiology,27,28,29,30 we next sought to test whether AAV-PHP.eB-based labeling strategy was compatible with chronic functional imaging in adult animals. We performed functional imaging at P30 in mice that received ICV injection of AAV-PHP.eB-GCaMP at P0. Despite relatively low fluorescence in somatic compartments, we observed strong expression along the apical dendrites of S1 neurons (Figure 7A), suggesting sustained suitability for calcium imaging. Correspondingly, spontaneous calcium transients were readily detected in these neurons in the absence of sensory stimulation (Figure 7B).

By revisiting the same cortical regions, we successfully tracked individual neurons from P12 through P140, confirming they remained identifiable by position and morphology and maintained spontaneous activity despite substantial brain growth (Figures 7C, 7D, and S7F–S7I). Importantly, most of these neurons continued to exhibit spontaneous activity in adulthood, demonstrating that our approach enables robust, lifelong imaging, and functional tracking of individual neurons across developmental stages.

Finally, to assess whether long-term GCaMP expression affects neuronal responsiveness in adult mice, we performed the experiment in adult mice by delivering mechanical stimulation to both sides of the whisker pad using an automated von Frey system. Neurons in S1 L2/3 responded more robustly to contralateral stimulation than to ipsilateral stimulation (Figures 7E–7I), with ∼17.56 ± 3.87% of neurons showing significant stimulus-evoked activity, consistent with previous reports estimating 10%–20% of responsive neurons in this paradigm.31 These findings demonstrate that chronic GCaMP expression preserves neuronal viability and sensory responsiveness, establishing a proof-of-concept for life-long imaging of identified neurons to study development and function.

Discussion

A major challenge in developmental neuroscience is to monitor neuronal structure and activity with high spatial and temporal resolution throughout early postnatal stages. Traditional approaches, such as Golgi staining or in utero electroporation, have provided important insights but are limited by lack of cell-type specificity, incompatibility with longitudinal imaging, or technical complexity. Genetically modified mouse lines allow developmental labeling but require extensive breeding and are often inflexible. In this study, we establish an AAV-PHP.eB-mediated strategy that overcomes these limitations. By optimizing delivery routes and viral titers in neonatal mice, we achieved rapid, cell-type-specific, and stable gene expression using AAV-PHP.eB. This enabled high-resolution morphological labeling as early as P3 and functional calcium imaging across key windows of circuit maturation. Compared with transgenic approaches, our method is minimally invasive, broadly applicable across brain regions, and avoids lengthy breeding schemes, providing a practical platform for longitudinal studies of neuronal development.

Using this approach, we systematically reconstructed dendritic morphology across multiple neuronal types and brain regions. We observed distinct developmental trajectories. Pyramidal neurons in the S1 L2/3 and MSNs in the striatum exhibited a biphasic pattern of dendritic growth, followed by pruning, likely reflecting synaptic refinement. In contrast, CA1 pyramidal neurons showed compartment-specific maturation, with basal dendrites peaking early and apical dendrites continuing to grow, indicating distinct input integration strategies. These findings highlight the diverse maturation patterns across brain regions, with sensory cortex and striatum undergoing early development to establish sensorimotor circuits, while hippocampal neurons retain plasticity for learning and memory.32,33

While systemic delivery was highly effective for anatomical labeling, it proved less suitable for functional imaging with GCaMP. Using this strategy, we captured a shift from globally synchronized calcium events to more spatially decorrelated activity in cortical region. This change aligns with prior electrophysiological and imaging studies, which show that early network synchrony diminishes as inhibitory tone strengthens, synapses refine, and sensory experience emerges.26,34 To further examine the cellular basis of these dynamics, we longitudinally tracked individual neurons from P11 to P15. Pairwise correlation analysis revealed heterogeneous patterns that likely reflect differences in synaptic integration and functional specialization. Calcium events also became shorter and more frequent, consistent with developmental increases in synaptic drive, intrinsic excitability, and action potential fidelity.23,25,26,35 In addition, our approach enabled long-term imaging of the same cortical regions and neurons into adulthood, demonstrating the feasibility of life-long imaging of identified neurons.

Despite these advantages, our study has several limitations and opportunities for optimization. First, although AAV-PHP.eB achieves rapid and widespread expression in neonatal neurons, the precise mechanism underlying this rapid labeling is unclear. We speculate that it exploits the immature BBB and elevated cellular permissiveness of the neonatal brain.6 Understanding this mechanism could guide the design of other viral vectors for accelerated and efficient neuronal labeling. Second, while ICV injection improves early GCaMP expression, it is more invasive and less practical than systemic delivery. Future studies could explore self-complementary AAV (scAAV) to accelerate transgene expression and enable early imaging via systemic routes.36,37 Third, although GCaMP imaging captures population and single-cell calcium dynamics, low baseline activity in P3 neurons limits interpretability at the earliest stages. Incorporating a constitutive reporter such as tdTomato co-expressed via P2A could distinguish true inactivity from insufficient expression. Finally, our current approach focuses on calcium dynamics and structural labeling but does not capture other physiological parameters, such as membrane potential or subthreshold activity. Combining this method with genetically encoded voltage indicators or optogenetic tools would provide a more comprehensive picture of circuit development.38

In conclusion, we provide a versatile and accessible platform for in vivo studies of neuronal development. It enables sparse labeling for detailed morphological analysis, supports population and single-cell calcium imaging across developmental windows, and permits long-term tracking of individual neurons into adulthood. This strategy opens avenues for investigating how circuits emerge, reorganize, and mature in both healthy brains and models of neurodevelopmental disorders.

Limitations of the study

Although this method enables rapid neuronal labeling and functional imaging in neonatal mice, several limitations should be considered. For detailed morphological reconstruction, thick brain sections typically require prolonged immunofluorescent staining to enhance signal intensity and continuity of dendritic processes. In addition, viral transduction efficiency may vary across brain regions, developmental stages, and mouse strains due to differences in AAV tropism. Therefore, experimental conditions such as viral dose and labeling strategy may require optimization depending on the neuronal population and specific experimental objectives.

Experimental model and study participant details

Mouse strains and conditions

All experimental procedures were conducted in accordance with the guidelines established by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC-P2-2026-03-CQ-01) of the Zhongshan Institute for Drug Discovery (ZIDD). The C57BL/6 wild type mice used in this study were purchased from Zhuhai BesTest Bio-Tech Company (China). The mice were housed under controlled conditions at a constant temperature of 23°C and subjected to a 12-h light/dark cycle (lights on at 7:00 and off at 19:00), with ad libitum access to food and water. For viral injections, P0 neonatal mice weighed approximately 1.4 g, whereas adult mice (8–10 weeks old) weighed 22–26 g. Age-matched male and female mice were included in all tests, ensuring proportional representation across groups. The ZIDD Committee on Animal Care reviewed and approved all experimental procedures.

Method details

P0 intravenous injection and ICV injection

P0 intravenous injection procedure was performed following an established protocol.15 In brief, P0 pups were induced with hypothermic anesthesia by placement on ice. Anesthesia was confirmed by squeezing of paw and cessation of movement before injections. Intravenous injections were performed using 1 mL insulin syringes. Viral solutions were diluted in 1×PBS with fast green dye (1 mg/mL, Cat# F7258, Sigma, USA) and injected as 100 μL/pup. The facial vein was visualized by a dissection microscope and the virus was slowly injected into the vein. After removing the needle, pressure was applied to the injection site using gauze until the bleeding ceased. Injected pups were placed on a warming pad before returning them to their home cage.

For P0 ICV injections, anesthesia and recovery were the same as intravenous injections. Approximately 1–2 μL of AAV solution was loaded into a 1.4 mm diameter glass electrode syringe and injected at 2/5 of the distance from the lambda suture to each eye, at a depth of 1–2 mm.15,40

AAV vectors and virus production

To make pAAV-CaMKIIα-EGFP-P2A-EGFPf and pAAV-Dlx5/6-EGFP-P2A-EGFPf constructs, CaMKIIα and Dlx5/6 promoters were obtained from pAAV-CaMKIIα-GCaMP6f and pAAV-Dlx5/6-GCaMP6f constructs39 and replaced the human Synapsin I promoter in pAAV-hSyn-EGFP-P2A-EGFPf (Addgene, #74513). All constructs were confirmed with Sanger sequencing.

The AAV production method was used as described in detail previously.41 Briefly, HEK293T cells were co-transfected with the AAV transfer plasmid containing the transgene, the pAdDeltaF6 helper plasmid (Addgene, #112867), and the pAAV-PHP.eB capsid plasmid (Addgene, #103005) using polyethylenimine (23966-1; Polysciences, USA). Cells and culture media were harvested 72 h post-transfection. The cell pellet and the virus precipitated from the media were combined and resuspended in digestion buffer. Benzonase nuclease (10 KU, Novoprotein, China) was added, and the mixture was incubated in a 37°C water bath for 1 h. After centrifugation at 2,000 g for 15 min, the supernatant was layered onto a discontinuous iodixanol gradient (15%, 25%, 40%, and 60%) in a 39 mL ultracentrifuge tube (No. 344326, Beckman, USA) and centrifuged at 350,000 g for 2.5 h at 18°C. The 5 mL fraction corresponding to the 40% layer and the 40%/60% interface was collected. This fraction was then buffer-exchanged and concentrated using a 100 kDa cut-off ultrafiltration unit (UFC910096-1, 15 mL, Millipore, USA) by four rounds of centrifugation with 1×PBS containing 0.001% Pluronic F-68. AAV titers were determined by real-time quantitative PCR (qPCR) using primers specific to the ITR region (Forward: 5′-GGAACCCCTAGTGATGGAGTT-3'; Reverse: 5′-CGGCCTCAGTGAGCGA-3′). Purified viruses were aliquoted and stored at −80°C until use.

Perfusion and Immunofluorescence staining

All adult and neonatal mice were anesthetized with isoflurane (RWD, China) and transcardially perfused with ice-cold 1×PBS and then with ice-cold 4% paraformaldehyde42 in 1×PBS. The brains were removed and post-fixed in 4% PFA at 4°C overnight for adult mice, 5 days, 4 days, 3 days, and 2 days for P3, P7, P14, and P21 mice, respectively. Sagittal slices were prepared using Leica VT1000S Vibratome at either 50 μm or 200 μm thickness depending on the application. The thickness of brain slices was 50 μm in Figures 1, 2, and 3, and S1–S4, and 200 μm in Figure 4 for single neuron labeling.

Immunofluorescence staining was performed on 50 μm or 200 μm vibratome brain sections. For 50 μm sections, slices were permeabilized in 0.5% Triton X-100 in PBS, blocked in 15% normal goat serum and 5% BSA, and incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C in 0.2% Triton X-100-containing blocking buffer. After washing, sections were incubated with appropriate Alexa Fluor-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:1000, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) for 1 h at room temperature, followed by Hoechst counterstaining (1:1000, ab228551, Abcam, UK). For GAD67 staining, sections were processed in 0.1 M Tris buffer (pH 7.6) and incubated with mouse anti-GAD67 (1:1000, MAB5406, Millipore, USA) followed by donkey anti-mouse Alexa Fluor 555 (1:1000, A-31570, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA).

For 200 μm sections used for morphological reconstruction, slices were permeabilized in 0.2% Triton X-100 for 2 h, blocked as above, and incubated with rabbit anti-GFP (1:1000, ab6556, Abcam, UK) for 48 h at 4°C. After extensive washing, sections were incubated with donkey anti-rabbit Alexa Fluor 488 (1:500, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) overnight at 4°C. All sections were mounted using Fluoromount-G (SouthernBiotech, USA).

Sparse neuronal labeling and morphological analyses

To sparsely label neurons in different brain regions, we injected 100 μL AAVPHP.eB-CaMKIIα-EGFP-P2A-EGFPf through P0 facial vein injection (injected dose was 6×108 vg/mouse). The mice were euthanized and perfused in P3, P7, P14, P21, or P60. PFA-fixed 200-μm-thick sagittal brain slices were sectioned using Vibratome as mentioned above. Anti-GFP antibody was used to visualize the EGFP-P2A-EGFPf expression.

Whole brain images were captured with a 10×objective lens using a VS200 wild field microscope (Olympus, Japan). The Olympus FV3000 confocal microscope was used to image EGFP-labeled neurons. To acquire relatively intact dendrites, neurons located around the center in the depth of sections were imaged under 40× (NA 0.95) or 20× lens (NA 0.80) at 1.5 μm step size for z series capture, a bright z function was utilized to achieve better images. Each neuron was manually traced by using the filament module in Imaris software (v10.0.1, Oxford Instruments, USA) for 3D reconstruction and measurements. Surface module was used to denoise the non-specific fluorescence in the background. Total dendrite length, maximum branch depth, and number of Sholl analysis intersections were output as statistical values. AUC and maximum intersection were analyzed based on the data of Sholl analysis using customized MATLAB (MathWorks, USA) codes. Total dendrite length was defined as the sum of the length of all the dendrites in a selected neuron.

Cranial window surgery

Mice aged P3 to P7 were briefly anesthetized via hypothermia by placing them on crushed ice. Mice aged P10 to P24 were anesthetized with isoflurane (3% for induction, 1.5–2% for maintenance) and maintained on a heating pad throughout the surgical procedure. Preoperative analgesia was provided via subcutaneous injection of meloxicam (1 mg/kg). The surgical site was sterilized by alternating scrubs of 75% ethanol and 10% povidone-iodine. All surgical instruments and surfaces were sterilized prior to use.

A midline incision was made to expose the skull over S1. A custom-designed titanium head plate for neonatal mice was positioned over the skull and secured using cyanoacrylate adhesive (LOCTITE, Germany). P3-P7 mice were returned to the ice following head plate attachment to maintain anesthesia during the remaining procedure. A circular craniotomy (2–3 mm in diameter) was performed within the head plate aperture by carefully removing the skull using a #12 scalpel blade (RWD, China). After achieving hemostasis, a round glass coverslip (3 mm diameter, 0.17 mm thickness) was gently placed onto the brain surface and sealed in place using cyanoacrylate adhesive. Following surgery, pups were placed on a heating pad with sterile bedding and allowed to recover for at least 1 h before being returned to their home cage.

In vivo two photon calcium imaging

Briefly, recordings were performed on unanesthetized mouse pups of which the head was stabilized by attaching the head plate to a custom-made head fixation apparatus using screws. A FluoView FVMPE-RS multiphoton imaging system (Olympus, Japan) was used for detecting brain activity in mouse pups. A Mai Tai Deepsee Ti: Saphire laser (Spectra-physics, USA) was tuned to 910 nm for GCaMP6f excitation and the total laser power delivered to the brain was less than 60 mW. A 25× (1.05 NA) water immersion lens (Olympus, Japan) was used for imaging. Mouse pups were imaged between 130 and 230 μm below pia surface, targeting S1 L2/3. Imaging depths were adjusted as the animal matured to ensure that the same field was imaged across developmental stages.

For calcium imaging experiments, image frames with a field-of-view (FOV) of 509 × 509 μm (at a resolution of 512 × 512 pixels) were scanned using resonant scanning. We performed a 3-frame average of raw frames to remove fluorescence signal variations and obtained a final frame rate of 10 Hz for 3000 frames. For longitudinal experiments, the same FOV was located using anatomical landmarks across developmental stages to track the exact same group of neurons. Mice were returned to their home cage after each imaging session.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Quantification of colocalization and labeled neurons

Section locations were determined based on regional distribution as defined in the 4th Paxino and Franklin’s the Mouse Brain in Stereotaxic Coordinates, and from the same animal several brain sections were collected. On-target specificity of the specific promoter was calculated as the percent of EGFP+/endogenous marker+ co-expressing cells relative to the total number of EGFP+ cells, and on-target efficiency was calculated as the percent of EGFP+/endogenous marker+ co-expressing cells relative to the total number of endogenous marker+ expressing cells. For hSyn, CaMKIIα, and Dlx5/6 promoters, endogenous markers were NeuN, CaMKII, and GAD67, respectively.

To quantify the number of labeled neurons following different viral doses, EGFP-expressing cells, characterized by the corresponding signal above background fluorescence, were counted. Confocal microscope (10× objective, FV3000, Olympus, Japan) was used to image targeted brain regions, and positive cells in each image were counted as the number of neurons.

Two-photon imaging data analysis

The images were analyzed by using a custom program, and open-source routines, then written using MATLAB (MathWorks, USA). The package had been used elsewhere and is available via GitHub (https://github.com/cdeister/imageAnalysis_gui).39,42 Frames were collected as individual image files. For calcium analysis, 3000 frames were acquired per mouse at 10 Hz to assess spontaneous activity, while automated von Frey stimulus-evoked responses were captured by recording 10 frames before and 25 frames after stimulus onset. We applied rigid-body registration to align the imaging frames. All region of interests (ROIs) were identified manually, and binary masks of the segmented regions were created for subsequent analysis. Fluorescence values were then extracted from each ROI across all frames, and the mean fluorescence intensity for each cell was calculated.

To characterize calcium dynamics in individual neurons, we quantified two key parameters: event duration and event frequency. A calcium event was defined as a period during which the ΔF/F signal exceeded a threshold of 1.5–2 standard deviations (SD) above baseline for at least 0.2 s. Within each detected event, the onset was defined as the first frame in which ΔF/F rose above the cell’s average ΔF/F, and the offset was defined as the frame in which it fell back below this average. Event duration was measured as the time between onset and offset, and event frequency was calculated as the number of events per second (Hz). Both parameters were computed on a per-cell basis.

To assess coordinated network activity, pairwise Spearman correlation coefficients were calculated between all neurons within each field of view. The resulting correlation matrices were visualized as correlograms, where line colors indicate the magnitude of the correlation between neuron pairs.

To investigate developmental changes in network synchrony, we categorized neurons into three groups based on how their pairwise correlation patterns evolved over time (Figures 6G–6I; Figure S6). For each neuron, a threshold was defined as the average pairwise correlation coefficient across all analyzed days. Neurons were then classified based on whether their correlation coefficient on the first and last day of recording was above or below this threshold: Type 1 neurons had coefficients above the threshold on both days, Type 2 had a decrease from above to below threshold, and Type 3 increased from below to above threshold.

Utilizing an automated von Frey stimulation system, we analyzed both the ΔF/F traces and the number of neurons responsive to whisker pad stimulation. A neuron was considered responsive if calcium event onset occurred within 2 s of stimulus delivery. To quantify the overall response strength, ΔF/F traces were z-scored, and the area under the curve was calculated as the integrated area between the Z score trace and the baseline (z = 0) within a window from 1 s before to 2.5 s after stimulation.

For visualization purposes, calcium traces were normalized in two different ways depending on the figure: In Figures 5F, 6B, 6J, and S5, traces were normalized by the maximum ΔF/F value of each neuron. In Figures 7B–7D, S5C and S7A, traces were displayed using the same scaling factor and represent the raw ΔF/F values without normalization.

Statistics

All data were transferred to Prism 9.0 (GraphPad Software, USA) for statistical analysis and graphing. Data are presented as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). All error bars indicate the SEM. Statistical tests were chosen based on experimental design and sample size distribution as specified below. For the quantification of colocalization of promoter-driven EGFP and endogenous markers, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test was used. For quantification of the number of labeled neurons injected with different doses of virus and the area under the curve of automated von Frey stimulation, Welch’s t test was used. For Sholl analysis, two-way ANOVA was used. For total dendrite length analyzed during the development, One-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test was used. For event duration and frequency, One-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test was used and significant differences were labeled between P11 and other ages. The significance levels for all tests were set as ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001, and ∗∗∗∗p < 0.0001.
