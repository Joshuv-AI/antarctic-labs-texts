Comparative Evaluation of Microhardness and Solubility after Addition of Two Different Combinations of Double Antibiotic Powders Containing Metronidazole + Ciprofloxacin and Metronidazole + Amoxicillin Clavulanate to a Bulk-fill Alkasite Material, Cention-N: An In Vitro Study

Published: 2025

ABSTRACT

Introduction

“An ounce of prevention is worth a pound of cure.”—John Robert Colombo.

Dental caries is among the most widespread human diseases, caused by pathogenic microorganisms such as Streptococcus mutans and Lactobacillus species that adhere to tooth surfaces and initiate demineralization of dental hard tissues.1

The search for an ideal restorative material has been ongoing for decades. Since the 1900s, the development of newer materials has continued to evolve. In 1972, Alan Wilson and Brian Kent developed glass ionomer cement (GIC), which became widely accepted for its ability to bond chemically to teeth, provide fluoride release, and allow minimal cavity preparation.2

During the 1980s, composite resins emerged, followed by resin-modified GICs and compomers in the 1990s. More recently, bulk-fill composites were introduced as alternatives to dental amalgam for posterior restorations, offering superior properties over GICs and composites while requiring less manipulation time.3,4

Cention-N is a relatively new bulk-fill restorative material belonging to the “alkasite” subgroup of composite resins. It releases fluoride, hydroxide, and calcium ions, providing cariostatic benefits. This material can be used in both self-cure and light-cure modes and exhibits minimal polymerization shrinkage and microleakage compared with conventional composites and GICs.4

Localized antibacterial treatment of dentin reduces microbial load and helps prevent secondary caries. Previous studies have shown that adding antibiotics such as metronidazole, ciprofloxacin, and doxycycline to restorative materials enhances their antibacterial activity. Nevertheless, the effect of these additives on the mechanical properties of the materials remains unclear. Therefore, the present study aimed to evaluate and compare the microhardness and solubility of Cention-N when modified with two different combinations of antibiotic powders.

Methodology

This study was carried out in the Department of Pediatric & Preventive Dentistry, Maharishi Markandeshwar College of Dental Sciences and Research, in collaboration with the Departments of Mechanical Engineering, Chemistry, and the Central Research Laboratory at Maharishi Markandeshwar Deemed to be University, Mullana, Ambala, Haryana.

Metal molds (10 mm diameter × 6 mm thickness) were fabricated using a spot welder for disc preparation. The samples were assigned into three groups: Group I (control—Cention-N only), group II (Cention-N + metronidazole and ciprofloxacin), and group III (Cention-N + metronidazole and amoxicillin-clavulanate). Antibiotic powders for groups II and III were weighed in a 1:1 ratio using a precision analytical balance, mixed thoroughly, and incorporated into Cention-N at a concentration of 1.5% w/w. The powder and liquid were mixed according to manufacturer guidelines and placed into molds. To achieve a uniform surface, the material was compressed between glass slabs before undergoing light curing.

Each group was further subdivided into two subgroups based on storage medium: Neutral artificial saliva and acidic artificial saliva (pH adjusted to 5.5 using hydrochloric acid and monitored with a pH meter).

Ten specimens were prepared per group, mounted on acrylic discs, and polished with aluminum oxide abrasive discs. Microhardness was evaluated using Vickers indenter (200 gm load, 10 seconds dwell time), and mean values were obtained from three readings. Samples were then stored in the test solutions for 21 days at room temperature. Microhardness measurements were repeated after 24 hours, 72 hours, 7 days, and 21 days. Solutions were replaced every 24 hours.

For solubility testing, initial weight (m1) of each sample was obtained after conditioning in a desiccator at 37°C and 100% humidity until a stable mass was achieved (±0.005 gm). Samples were immersed in 5 mL of testing solution in an incubator at 37°C. Weights (m2) were recorded at 24 hours, 72 hours, 7 days, and 21 days after blot drying. At the end of 21 days, specimens were reconditioned in a desiccator for 24 hours to obtain final weight (m3). Volume of each disc was calculated using diameter and thickness measured with a vernier caliper, and solubility was expressed as (m1 – m3)/V (µg/mm³), based on the Oysaed and Ruyter formula.

Statistical Analysis

Data obtained were compiled and analyzed using SPSS version 17. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was employed for intergroup comparisons, and post hoc analysis was conducted for pairwise comparisons. A p value ≤0.05 was considered statistically significant (Figs 1 to 9).

Solubility

In acidic saliva, group I showed the highest mean solubility (19.51 ± 12.47), followed by group II (11.77 ± 3.21), and group III (6.56) (Table 1). The differences were not statistically significant (p = 0.065) (Table 2). In neutral saliva, mean solubility values were 10.23 ± 9.48 for group I, 10.76 ± 6.44 for group II, and 3.04 ± 8.79 for group III, with no significant differences (p = 0.300) (Fig. 10).

The differences were significant (p = 0.019) (Table 4). Over time, group I showed an increase by day 21 (49.87 ± 1.90), while groups II and III demonstrated reductions (Fig. 11). In neutral saliva, group III recorded the highest initial hardness (46.19 ± 2.14), followed by group II (44.25 ± 1.99) and group I (41.32 ± 1.92) (Fig. 12). By day 21, all groups showed an increase, with group I (50.59 ± 3.53) being the highest. Statistical differences were not observed (p = 0.633).

Discussion

Dental caries remains one of the most prevalent oral diseases worldwide. It is primarily a biofilm-mediated condition initiated by bacteria such as S. mutans, Streptococcus sobrinus, and Lactobacillu s spp., which demineralize tooth surfaces through acid production.5,6 These organisms exist within a structured microbial community, commonly referred to as dental plaque or oral biofilm, that attaches firmly to the tooth structure.

Caries developing adjacent to existing restorations are known as recurrent or secondary caries. Clinically, when such lesions are diagnosed, the restoration often requires replacement. It is estimated that nearly 60% of restorative procedures are performed to replace defective restorations, as highlighted by the National Institute of Dental and Craniofacial Research in 2009.7,8

Secondary caries is usually associated with microleakage or incomplete sealing at restoration margins, which permits colonization by cariogenic organisms. Species such as S. mutans, Lactobacilli, Veillonella, Prevotella, Neisseria, Actinomyces, and Fusobacterium are most frequently implicated in this process.9 This highlights the clinical importance of restorative materials possessing intrinsic antibacterial properties to reduce microbial persistence and prevent failure.

Cention-N, an alkasite-based bulk-fill restorative material, is relatively new and has limited evidence regarding its antibacterial capabilities. While antibiotics are routinely used in systemic and topical dental therapies, recent studies have explored their direct incorporation into restorative materials. One such study reported that supplementing Cention-N with antibiotics such as metronidazole and amoxicillin significantly enhanced antibacterial action.10

To improve efficacy and minimize bacterial resistance, combinations of antibiotics (double-antibiotic powders) such as metronidazole with ciprofloxacin or with amoxicillin have been advocated rather than using single agents.11 Incorporation of such antibiotic blends into restorative materials has been suggested to lower the viable bacterial load in carious lesions, thereby potentially improving clinical outcomes.12

Earlier investigations have also indicated that adding antibiotics or other active agents to restorative materials could compromise their mechanical properties. However, studies have shown that incorporation at concentrations of 1-1.5% w/w can yield satisfactory antibacterial activity without significant adverse effects on physical performance. For instance, addition of 1% metronidazole + ciprofloxacin to glass ionomer cement improved antibacterial potential without reducing shear strength.13 Similarly, incorporation of ciprofloxacin, metronidazole, and minocycline at 1.5% w/w enhanced antibacterial properties with minimal compromise of mechanical integrity.14 In line with these observations, the present study adopted a 1.5% w/w antibiotic concentration in Cention-N.

Because natural saliva is difficult to replicate due to its complex composition and variable properties, artificial saliva was employed in this study to simulate oral conditions. To represent cariogenic challenge, its pH was adjusted to 5.5, which corresponds to the critical level below which enamel demineralization occurs. Studies confirm that acidic environments enhance cariogenic bacterial activity and increase erosion of tooth tissues. Thus, testing in artificial saliva with a reduced pH allowed evaluation of restorative material stability under simulated clinical stress.

This investigation was conducted according to ISO 4049:2009 specifications, which recommend solubility limits of 7.5-40 µg/mm³ for polymer-based restorative materials.15 In our study, solubility values were generally higher in acidic artificial saliva compared with neutral saliva, consistent with previous reports that restorative cements show maximum stability at pH 7.16 Group I (Cention-N alone) demonstrated the highest solubility in both acidic and neutral media, while group III (Cention-N with metronidazole + amoxicillin) showed the lowest values. Although these differences were not statistically significant, the results suggest that antibiotic addition did not negatively influence solubility and may even improve it.

Hardness is defined as the resistance of a material to indentation or penetration and is often used as a predictor of wear resistance and durability against occlusal forces.17 The Vickers hardness test, used in this study, involves indenting the specimen with a diamond-shaped pyramid under a fixed load, and the depth and size of the indentation provide hardness values.18 This test is a reliable indicator of both surface properties and structural integrity of restorative materials.

In this study, group I showed the highest initial hardness values in acidic conditions, followed by group II, while group III exhibited the highest values in neutral saliva prior to immersion. After 21 days, microhardness in groups II and III decreased significantly in acidic medium, whereas group I displayed an increase, indicating that the base Cention-N material may better withstand acidic conditions. These findings are consistent with earlier studies reporting a reduction in composite hardness after exposure to acidic environments.19

The reduction in hardness under acidic conditions can be explained by water sorption and hydrolytic degradation of the polymer matrix. Authors such as Anseth et al. and Kloosterboer have suggested that absorption of fluids, along with the presence of voids and unreacted monomers, facilitates softening of composite surfaces.20,21 Since hardness testing is superficial, changes are primarily observed in the outermost layer of the material exposed to acidic media.

Conversely, when stored in neutral saliva, all groups in the present study demonstrated an increase in hardness values over 21 days. Group I recorded the highest values, followed by groups III and II. This trend supports previous findings that restorative materials show greater dimensional stability and surface integrity at neutral pH conditions.22 Furthermore, while oral fluids contain a wide variety of substances—such as acids, bases, salts, and alcohols—that may contribute to material degradation, water and neutral saliva are comparatively less aggressive and may facilitate maturation and strengthening of the polymer matrix over time.

Taken together, these findings suggest that although addition of antibiotics alters some physical characteristics, the overall performance of Cention-N is not significantly compromised. In fact, certain combinations, particularly metronidazole with amoxicillin, may improve solubility resistance while maintaining acceptable microhardness.
