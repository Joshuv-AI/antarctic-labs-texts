Validation of Coronary Wire for the Acquisition of Intracoronary Electrocardiogram in Swine
Authors: Dobrin Iotkov Vassilev, Niya Boykova Mileva, Teodora Yaneva‐Sirakova, Ranko Svetoslavov Georgiev, Ghassan Kassab
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundWe report the first in‐literature animal experiment to validate the intracoronary ECG signal acquired from a coronary wire compared with the direct signal from an epicardial electrode.MethodsAn animal model study was performed in a 40 kg pig. Acute myocardial ischemia was induced by intracoronary balloon inflation for 60 s. Simultaneous ECG recording and PCI guidewire electrical and motion measurements were obtained.ResultsThe first ischemic manifestation was myocardial kinetic disturbances, followed by ST‐elevation. The signals recorded from the PCI guidewire and the epicardial electrode ran simultaneously, from the induction of ischemia to complete recovery of signal.ConclusionThe intracoronary ECG signal acquired from a coronary wire was equivalent to the direct signal received from an epicardial electrode. Trial Registration: ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT04061525.

Introduction

The concept of the “ischemic cascade” is a fundamental part of the contemporary understanding of myocardial ischemic events [1]. However, to the best of our knowledge, there is no experimental or clinical study that captures myocardial mechanical and electrical events simultaneously in early ischemia in man. A coronary interventional guidewire may be used as a tool for recording of both epicardial and intracoronary electrocardiogram, providing a unique opportunity to assess mechanical and electrical events simultaneously. An intracoronary ECG was recorded using an interventional coronary guidewire in 1976 [2]. In 1985, it was revealed for the first time that a coronary wire can be used as an ECG unipolar lead to detect ongoing ischemia in humans, presuming that potential recorded from an intracoronary wire is coincident with epicardial potentials [3]. Intracoronary ECG remains a highly underused technique. Based on previously published data [4, 5, 6], icECG changes occur before the onset of LV pressure elevation, surface ECG changes, and chest pain. The icECG signal recorded from the PCI wire should be equivalent to that recorded from a direct epicardial electrode, a premise that has yet to be demonstrated. Based on multiple studies examining electrical and mechanical events during ischemia, the electrical and mechanical events follow identical appearances in time [7]. This is true mainly for ECG recordings made in experimental settings with electrodes placed directly on the epicardium or intramurally. In all human studies, icECG changes appeared within a few cardiac cycles after coronary flow was interrupted by the PTCA balloon, whereas surface ECG changes appeared much later, and in 20%–30% of cases, there were no changes detected [8, 9]. To our knowledge, no study has compared the intracoronary ECG signal obtained from a coronary wire with that obtained directly from an epicardial electrode.

Aim

The aim of the current study is to validate the ability of the PCI wire to acquire specific and accurate icECG signals equivalent to those obtained from an epicardial electrode.

Methods

An animal model experiment was performed using a 40‐kg swine. The animal was put under general anesthesia, and double artery access was obtained (right limb arteries). A 6 Fr guiding catheter was placed in the left coronary artery. Coronary angiography was performed in different projections to delineate the coronary artery movement. The projection with the best visible movement of the coronary artery was chosen as a working projection for further recordings. A thoracotomy was performed to acquire open surgical access to the heart. A custom‐made electrode (from decomposed 3M red dot electrode, connected with high conductive cable and crocodile clip at the end for linking with V‐connector of ECG machine) was positioned and glued (3M Vetbond glue) at the lateral heart wall, on epicardium at the area of the left circumflex artery (LCX). A 0.014″ PCI wire was positioned in the distal LCX as close as anatomically possible to the already placed epicardial electrode. The proximal end of the wire was connected to the unipolar V2 ECG lead. The epicardial electrode was connected to the V1‐lead. In addition, transesophageal echocardiography (TOE) was acquired during the whole experiment to visualize the myocardial kinetics. This is an additional assurance that there is no influence on the regional myocardial kinetics of the epicardial lead. A PCI balloon (with a diameter of 3 mm and a length of 15 mm) was inserted over the PCI wire. The balloon was inflated at the proximal third of the circumflex artery. After balloon inflation, a complete occlusion was performed for 60 s. Simultaneous continuous ECG recording from V1–V2 leads, fluoroscopic movement of the PCI wire tip, and echocardiographic changes in left ventricular kinetics were obtained at rest, during ischemia, and 5 min after ischemia.

Results and Discussion

After balloon inflation and induction of acute myocardial ischemia, the first manifestation was kinetic disturbances, which were recorded as reduced movement of the PCI wire and as hypo‐akinesia of the anterior wall and apex on the TOE. Eight to ten seconds after the mechanical disturbance, ST elevation was recorded on both the PCI wire and epicardial electrode signals. Shortly after balloon deflation, the ST elevation on both signals resolved and LV kinetics returned to normal. The signals recorded from the PCI wire and the epicardial electrode ran simultaneously—from the moment of ischemia induction to the complete recovery of the signal to the state before balloon inflation (Figures 1 and 2). There were no detectable kinetic disturbances in TOE caused by the epicardial electrode. The signal morphologies (icECG from PCI wire and epicardial electrode) were very similar but not identical, as they were recorded from close enough (3–5 mm) but not identical locations. However, we do not consider this to be significant because the changes in both recordings are identical.

Conclusion

We report the first animal study to validate the intracoronary ECG signal obtained from a coronary wire in comparison with the signal obtained directly from an epicardial electrode. After induction of acute myocardial ischemia, simultaneous ST‐segment elevation was observed in both the icECG and epicardial electrode signals (Figure 3).
