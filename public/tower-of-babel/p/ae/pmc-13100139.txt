Kibble-Zurek mechanism and beyond in a holographic superfluid disk
Authors: Chuan-Yin Xia, Hua-Bi Zeng, András Grabarits, Adolfo del Campo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCrossing a continuous phase transition in finite time induces spontaneous symmetry breaking and the formation of topological defects, such as domain walls and vortices. The AdS/CFT correspondence provides a framework for studying strongly coupled critical dynamics via a dual gravitational theory. We investigate the normal-to-superfluid phase-transition dynamics and the spontaneous vortex formation while crossing the critical temperature in a disk geometry by solving the Einstein-Abelian-Higgs model in an AdS4 black hole. For slow quenches, the vortex density follows the Kibble-Zurek mechanism (KZM) scaling with the cooling rate, whereas for fast quenches it scales universally with the final temperature, beyond the KZM predictions. Although vortex number distributions appear normal, the study of cumulants reveals non-normal features consistent with Poisson binomial statistics, confirmed by their scaling with the quench time and depth. Our results support a universal defect distribution that encompasses KZM scaling, its breakdown, and universal quench-depth scaling at fast quenches.Subject terms: Theoretical physics, Phase transitions and critical phenomena, Phase transitions and critical phenomena

Introduction

The description of strongly correlated quantum many-body systems poses a profound challenge, particularly in non-equilibrium settings where conventional perturbative methods fail. In this context, the gauge-gravity duality-also known as holography or the AdS/CFT correspondence-provides a powerful, non-perturbative framework. This approach maps the generally intractable strong-coupling problem in a quantum field theory to a more tractable calculation in a classical gravitational theory in a higher-dimensional space1–3. By leveraging this weak-coupling gravitational description, holographic techniques have already yielded significant insights into condensed matter physics, establishing the AdS/CMT correspondence as a paradigmatic theoretical tool4–8. In particular, it provides a setting in which the universal features of strongly correlated dynamics, both in equilibrium and in non-equilibrium, can be explored, features that simpler weakly coupled or free models cannot reliably reproduce.

An important application of the holographic setup is the description of strongly-coupled systems with broken continuous symmetries, such as a superconductor9–11. Such formulation not only sheds insights into the equilibrium state of superconductors and superfluids but also in the study of vortex dynamics12–19, and more generally, in the tenets of nonequilibrium statistical mechanics, such as the Kibble-Zurek mechanism (KZM)20–28. The KZM was initially proposed in cosmology and then applied in other fields, ranging from condensed matter physics to quantum computing29–35. It links the dynamics of continuous phase transitions to the equilibrium critical exponent, explains the generation of topological defects, and predicts that their density follows a universal power law that experiments and simulations can test. According to KZM, when a system is quenched by modulating a control parameter T across a critical point Tc in a finite quench time scale τQ, topological defects form as a result of the breaking of adiabaticity. The latter is unavoidable when crossing a continuous phase transition in the thermodynamic limit due to the divergence of the relaxation time, which is responsible for the critical slowing down. Specifically, KZM exploits the scaling relations for the relaxation time τ and the correlation length ξ, encoded in the power laws τ = τ0∣ϵ∣−zν and ξ = ξ0∣ϵ∣−ν, respectively. Here, ν is the correlation-length critical exponent, and z is known as the dynamic critical exponent. According to the adiabatic-impulse approximation, in the high-symmetry phase, the system adapts adiabatically to the modulation of the control parameter ϵ(T) until the time left until reaching the critical point is comparable with the instantaneous relaxation time. Its verification has motivated continuous research efforts over the decades. Early experiments focused on probing the spontaneous formation of defects in several systems, such as liquid crystals36–38, and superfluid helium39–42. Subsequent efforts focused on verifying the power-law scaling, using Josephson junctions43–46, thin film superconductors47,48, linear optical quantum simulators49, trapped ions50–52, quantum annealers53,54 and ultra-cold gases55–60.

In this work, we demonstrate the far-from-equilibrium universality in the defect number distribution both as a function of the quench time and the quench depth, that is, the final value of the control parameter. We study the critical dynamics using a holographic superfluid disk as a testbed. Our work largely transcends the scope of the celebrated KZM, focused on the mean density of topological defects as a function of the quench time35. Likewise, our findings extend the notion of the universality of critical dynamics with respect to previous works on beyond-KZM physics. Specifically, previous works reporting the universality of the defect number statistics in classical and quantum systems are restricted to the power-law scaling regime associated with slow quenches in which KZM holds, in theoretical24,27,28,61–65 and experimental studies53,54,66,67. Our manuscript establishes the universality of the defect number distribution at arbitrarily quench rates for the first time, from the slow to the sudden regimes. Specifically, we establish that the Poisson binomial distribution describes the universal statistics of spontaneously formed point-like topological defects in all regimes. It is a curious accident that previous studies have evaded the universal signatures of the defect number statistics in the power-law scaling regime for decades, possibly due to the lack of a theoretical framework, despite the signal being in the noise, often measured in the experiments. Once noticed, such a universal distribution is compatible with the observation that the KZM regime is restricted to slow quenches with very low defect densities. Our results are remarkable in that they establish the universality of the defect number distribution even at the highest density values achievable by spontaneous defect formation through sudden quenches that are associated with the saturation plateau and the breakdown of the KZM. These predictions can be experimentally tested by probing spontaneous vortex formation scenarios in newborn superfluids.

Results and background

Beyond KZM physics

Beyond KZM predictions, the fluctuations of the number of topological defects have been predicted to be universal. In one-dimensional systems, the point-like defect number distribution is well described by can usually be described by a binomial or Poisson-binomial distribution61,62. As a result, the universal scaling with the quench time extends beyond the KZM prediction to the variance and any other higher-order cumulant61,62, as confirmed by numerical simulations24,27,28,61–65 and experiments53,54,66,67. A complementary characterization based on spatial correlations between defects has also been reported68,69. KZM and beyond-KZM physics have also been established in strongly coupled systems described by holography16,20–28,70–72, showcasing their universality. Specifically, numerical simulations have been reported for one-dimensional21,23,24,27,28 and two-dimensional20,25,26 holographic superconductors and superfluids. But for some features20,21, all results are rather similar to those of the mean-field theory and verify the validity of KZM in holographic systems. This agreement reflects that the mean-field description is justified in these holographic settings, as the corresponding boundary 2D quantum field theory is defined at large rank, suppressing long-range fluctuations 11,20. Interestingly, no analogue of a thermal Berezinskii-Kosterlitz-Thouless (BKT) transition, expected in 2D superfluids, has been found in holography73,74. By contrast, in superfluids described by the 2D stochastic Gross-Pitaevskii equation, which supplements mean-field theory with noise and dissipation, the system exhibits both mean-field-dominated behavior and the BKT transition, depending on the temperature70,75,76.

Deviations from KZM physics and fast-quench universality

Naturally, the validity of the KZM scaling laws is limited by the onset of adiabaticity that occurs when ξ^ is comparable to the system size. There are also reports on the impact of finite size on KZM23,77,78. When the system size is close to the correlation length, the number of topological defects will deviate from the prediction of KZM. Numerical studies of finite-size effects23 show deviations of KZM universality, explaining the experimental findings in Ref. 78.

By contrast, when the quenching time is much shorter than the time scale in which the order parameter grows, the density of topological defects no longer satisfies the universal power law predicted by the KZM. Instead, it saturates at a constant value called the plateau. This phenomenon was found in experiments59,60,67,79 after it was observed in numerical simulations21–23,25,26,70,71,71,72,77,80,81. This universal breakdown of the KZM scaling in fast quenches is to be expected, since its derivation is restricted to slow quenches near the adiabatic regime33–35,82. The nature of the plateau, the value of the plateau density, and its onset have remained a matter of debate. Recently, its universal character was established, along with the fact that the plateau density is independent of the quench rate, being governed by the equilibrium relaxation time associated with the final value of the control parameters20,70. These findings have been further verified in a holographic setting that involves a structural phase transition72.

Role of boundary conditions

In addition to these advances, the possible breakdown of KZM and beyond-KZM predictions has been explored in various scenarios. This includes the effect of boundary conditions on KZM. Although open boundary conditions are natural in most experiments, numerical simulations are often facilitated by imposing periodic boundaries. In addition, it is known that the role of boundary conditions cannot be ignored, regardless of the size of the system. For example, the Poincaré-Hopf theorem dictates that the total topological charge of spontaneously formed defects must vanish identically in manifolds with zero Euler characteristic83. As a result, breaking the symmetry Z2 restricts the formation of defects to kink-antikink pairs on a ring topology84. Similarly, the thermal quench of a superconducting or superfluid ring leads to a vanishing winding number21,23,32,85–87. In the same way, breaking of U(1) associated with vortex formation is conditioned to vanishing total vorticity in samples with torus topology64,76. The results obtained in a lattice model indicate that even under extremely slow quenching, periodic, anti-periodic, and free boundary conditions will not suppress the scaling of the universal power law for the mean density, but may affect higher-order cumulants and the defect number distribution84. In studies of KZM physics, experimental setups often involved open boundaries39–42,47,48,55–60. However, two-dimensional systems with boundaries have not been numerically simulated in a holographic setting. In this article, we use the holographic superfluid model on an open disk geometry6 as a test bed for vortex formation and the validity of both KZM and beyond-KZM physics. As we use Neumann boundary conditions, the total vorticity is unrestricted. This geometry involves a boundary at the maximum radius, making it possible to test whether the finite-size boundary affects the universal finite-quench scaling behavior.

Universal statistics of vortices

The estimation of the low-order cumulants of the vortex number distribution is based on the assumption that the generation of vortices at different locations is governed by independent and equally distributed random events62–64,76. In our model, when the normal fluid undergoes a phase transition to a superfluid, it is partitioned into “protodomains” of size set by the correlation length ξ^, and there is a certain probability p of vortex generation at the interface between adjacent domains. Poisson-binomial vortex distribution

To date, studies of defect statistics beyond KZM have focused on defect formation in one spatial dimension62,63,65,84, see also refs. 53,54,53,61,66,91 in the quantum case. Studies in two spatial dimensions are scarce and have focused on other aspects, addressing only the normal features of the defect number distribution76. Only one study to date has characterized non-normal features in a two-dimensional system consisting of a holographic superconductor with periodic boundary conditions84. In such a setting, the vortex number distribution is confined to even outcomes; as such, it deviates from the binomial distribution and is best described as an even Poissonian model (a limiting case of the binomial distribution conditioned to even outcomes). Thus, evidence of the universality of the defect number distribution in two (and higher) spatial dimensions is lacking.

The number of defects in the superfluid disk with open boundary conditions is unrestricted and involves even and odd outcomes. The binomial distribution can accommodate the nonzero values of the third cumulant and its universal scaling; see Eqs. (14) and (15). Cumulant ratios depend solely on the defect-formation probability p in the binomial distribution. For the first three cumulants, these ratios are fixed as κ2/κ1 = 1 − p, κ3/κ1 = (1 − p)(1 − 2p). However, the fitted power laws are inconsistent with the binomial model, as they require values of p outside the physical range [0–1]; see “Methods”.

The best-fitted distribution is instead a generalization of the binomial distributions to independent, but not identically distributed, Bernoulli trials. This is known as the Poisson binomial distribution (PBD)92,93 and was predicted in Ref. 62 for the most general scenario of spontaneously formed point-like defects. Interestingly, the same distribution describes the defect number distribution in quantum systems61, as experimentally demonstrated under open and unitary quantum dynamics53,54,66.

The PBD generalizes the binomial model. To derive it in the context of beyond KZM physics, one can still invoke the notion of a random tessellation of the superfluids by assuming that the order parameter grows independently at different locations, forming protodomains. The spatial extension of such protodomains is determined by the KZM correlation length ξ^. We gloss over this pictorial description being hand-waving, as such protodomains are ill-defined in dimensions larger than one.

Topological defects, such as vortices, are then expected to form where adjacent domains meet with a given probability, in the spirit of the geodesic rule29. However, the number of domains that meet at a point varies, e.g., in a random tesselation. Two effects come into play in this picture. First, the frequency of occurrence of a boundary that merges different domains varies with the number of domains. Second, numerical simulations and experiments done by merging independent Bose-Einstein condensates show that the probability of vortex formation depends on the number of defects that meet94.

The total number of events for defect formation N can thus be split as N=∑mNm, where Nm=Nrm represents the number of events for defect formation as a result of merging of m protodomains. Here, the probability of finding m protodomains merging at a point is rm≤1, and thus ∑mrm = 1. We also consider the conditional probability pm that a defect will be formed when m protodomains meet.

Discussion

The KZM is an important paradigm in nonequilibrium statistical mechanics that utilizes equilibrium properties to predict the dynamics across a quantum phase transition. It predicts the spontaneous formation of topological defects with a density that scales universally as a function of the quench time. Recent progress has shown that additional universal features exist in the dynamics across a phase transition that can be described in terms of equilibrium properties, such as the statistics of topological defects. However, the validity of such predictions is limited to slow quenches where the density of defects is low. Faster quenches are no longer described by KZM and show a saturation of the density of defects with the quench time.

The recent observation that the mean defect density is also universal for fast quenches20,70 constitutes the starting point of our study. In this work, we have established that defect statistics is universal for arbitrary values of the quench time and quench depth. Specifically, we have shown that the defect number distribution follows a Poisson binomial distribution. This is the case both at slow quenches, for which KZM holds, and for fast quenches when KZM breaks down. A testable prediction of our analysis is the universal scaling of the cumulants of the defect statistics as a function of the quench time and the quench depth. While derived in a holographic setting, our results are expected to hold more broadly for continuous phase transitions. This opens the way for experimental tests in systems where the defect number statistics can be measured. Ultracold gases offer a relevant example in which the superfluid phase transition can be induced by quenching the temperature, chemical potential (via a dimple trap), or interatomic interactions. In such systems, direct imaging of the vortex patterns is possible, and the vortex number distribution can be analyzed56,60,67,79,90,95. Similar studies may be within reach in other systems, such as polaritonic condensates and quantum fluids of light96, colloidal monolayers97, and multiferroics98,99.

Methods

Scaling arguments

In the conventional KZM theory, the freeze-out time t^ is defined by equating the time elapsed after crossing the critical point to the instantaneous equilibrium relaxation time, i.e., as the solution to t^≡τ(t^). Using the power-law expression for the equilibrium relaxation time τ = τ0/∣ϵ∣zν and assuming a linear quench ε(t) = t/τQ, the solution to t^≡τ0/∣t^/τQ∣zν is the KZ expression t^=(τ0τQzν)11+zν. Note that this corresponds to ϵ(t^)=(τ0/τQ)11+zν. As ϵ(t) = ∣T(t) − Tc∣/Tc, all these KZ scaling formulae assume that the quench continues with no end, but experiments generally involve a finite value of Tf and tf. Thus, it is required to extend the quench protocol to a piecewise schedule in which the control parameter T(t), and similarly ε(t), remain constant after the time tf. This carries over the resulting expressions for t^, ϵ(t^), ξ(t^). When the variation of the control parameter occurs over a limited range of values, below the characteristic quench time τQcl in Eq. (9), one finds t^=τ(t^)=τ(tf). For τQ<τQc1, one finds t^=τ(tf), while for τQ>τQc1 one recovers the conventional KZM scaling t^~τQνz1+νz.

Numerical scheme for phase transition dynamics

Chebyshev spectral methods are used in the (u, r) direction for numerical simulation. Fourier spectral methods are used in the θ direction. In the time direction, we use the fourth-order Runge-Kutta method to simulate the evolution of the system and use boundary conditions at each step of the evolution to constrain the values of the boundaries in other directions. In addition, the initial configuration of each field needs to be specified. The specific scheme will be introduced in detail in the next section.

Before driving the system with a thermal quench, the normal fluid should be prepared above the critical temperature at equilibrium. Then, we added random noise to ψ in the whole bulk to simulate the thermal fluctuation of the system, that is, ψ = S(t, r, θ). In this way, the normal fluid that grows at the boundary also inherits random noise s(t, r, θ). The noise s satisfies the normal distribution, with a vanishing statistical average 〈s(t, r, θ)〉 = 0, and two-point correlations ⟨s(t,r,θ)s(t′,r′,θ′)⟩=hδ(t−t′,r−r′,θ−θ′), where the amplitude h = 10−4. After a little heating, the fluid disk is ready for quenching.

Numerical demonstration of the inadequacy of the binomial approximation

For both the fast and slow driving regimes, the binomial distribution fails to reproduce the observed scaling behavior of the first three cumulants. As shown in Fig. 8, even when the binomial success probability is optimized (by minimizing the relative squared differences of the cumulants independently for each value of τQ and ϵf), the resulting cumulants exhibit substantial deviations from those obtained in the holographic model.
