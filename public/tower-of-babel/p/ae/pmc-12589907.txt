Identifying psychosocial distress phenotypes in postoperative patients with breast cancer: A latent profile analysis
Authors: Wencan Cheng, Shihao Sun, Jia Fang, Ni Zhang, Yiheng Zhang, Baoyi Zhang, Ye Zhang, Meifen Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study aimed to identify psychosocial distress phenotypes in postoperative patients with breast cancer and explore influencing factors.MethodsA total of 210 patients were recruited from a general hospital in Guangzhou, China, between November 2022 and July 2023. Data were collected through a general information questionnaire, the Psychosocial Distress Questionnaire-Breast Cancer, and the Connor-Davidson Resilience Scale. Latent profile analysis was conducted to identify psychosocial distress phenotypes, followed by multinomial logistic regression to determine associated factors.ResultsThree distinct phenotypes were identified: “Resilient–Latent Anxious-Somatic” (Class 1, 27%), “Anxious-Somatic Dominant” (Class 2, 51%), and “Anxious-Somatic with Financial Toxicity” (Class 3, 22%). Low resilience level (odds ratio [OR] ​= ​14.230, P ​< ​0.001; OR ​= ​51.931, P ​< ​0.001) and not undergoing breast reconstruction surgery (OR ​= ​4.645, P ​= ​0.006; OR ​= ​4.175, P ​= ​0.041) were risk factors for Class 2 and Class 3. Compared with Class 1, lower income (OR ​= ​3.127, P ​= ​0.009) and urban residence (OR ​= ​3.162, P ​= ​0.025) were risk factors for Class 2. Factors significantly associated with membership in Class 3 included older age (OR ​= ​0.923, P ​= ​0.008), unemployment (OR ​= ​3.181, P ​= ​0.047), being married (OR ​= ​16.993, P ​= ​0.010), and living with others (OR ​= ​0.071, P ​= ​0.033).ConclusionsPsychosocial distress among postoperative patients with breast cancer is heterogeneous, manifesting in three phenotypes shaped by diverse factors. Early identification of patients by phenotype and implementation of group-level interventions tailored to phenotype-specific characteristics are essential for mitigating psychosocial distress.

Introduction

Breast cancer is the most prevalent malignancy threatening women's health worldwide.1 Recent estimates indicate approximately 2.3 million new cases of female breast cancer globally, with incidence projected to increase by 38% by 2050.2 In China, the incidence of breast cancer has risen sharply, accounting for nearly 19% of global cases and underscoring a pressing public health challenge.3 Advances in multimodal treatment have raised the 5-year survival rate of patients with breast cancer to over 80%.4 Nevertheless, extended treatment periods impose complex psychosocial burdens in addition to physical health challenges.5

Following surgery, patients with breast cancer often encounter complex psychosocial challenges that negatively affect their physical, psychological, and social well-being.6 Surgical procedures may cause body image dissatisfaction, lymphedema may impair limb function, and chemotherapy can induce side effects such as alopecia and sexual dysfunction.7, 8, 9 Financial strain and disruptions in occupational and family roles further exacerbate these difficulties.10 In response to such challenges, patients may experience a spectrum of emotional reactions, ranging from normal sadness and fear to debilitating conditions such as depression, anxiety, post-traumatic stress disorder, and other mental health disorders.11,12 This multifaceted emotional response is termed psychosocial distress, which significantly affects about one-third of patients with breast cancer.13 Previous studies have shown that women with breast cancer experiencing psychological distress face a 46% higher risk of mortality.14 Moreover, psychological distress can contribute to sleep disturbances and cancer-related fatigue, which in turn compromise quality of life.15 Therefore, assessing and addressing psychosocial distress in patients with breast cancer is of critical importance. Identifying factors influencing their distress can inform targeted interventions aimed at enhancing psychosocial well-being.

Previous studies have explored the impact of several factors on psychosocial distress in patients with breast cancer, such as age, symptom burden, financial strain, and social support.16, 17, 18 However, most of this research has concentrated on long-term psychosocial distress among patients receiving adjuvant therapies. This focus has overlooked the significant distress experienced by patients during the early postoperative period.19 According to the Stress Process Model,20 an individual's initial response to a major stressor not only directly affects their immediate psychological and physiological state but may also trigger secondary stressors, which together shape the adaptation trajectory. Building on this perspective, focusing on patients with breast cancer in the early postoperative period allows health care providers to promptly identify high-risk individuals and implement preventive interventions, which are essential for alleviating psychosocial distress and improving subsequent treatment outcomes and quality of life.

In addition, previous assessments of psychological distress in patients with breast cancer have largely relied on total scale scores to quantify its frequency and severity.16 However, this variable-centered approach may obscure substantial heterogeneity among patients. Individuals with identical total scores can exhibit markedly different distress profiles, characterized by distinct patterns of symptom distribution across multiple dimensions.21 This variability aligns with the classic Diathesis–Stress Model,22 which posits that psychological distress arises from the interaction between internal vulnerabilities (e.g., resilience) and external stressors (e.g., cancer diagnosis and treatment). Therefore, psychosocial distress in patients with breast cancer should be recognized as heterogeneous. Overlooking this heterogeneity and relying solely on summative scores renders current interventions imprecise, as they often fail to address the core issues of specific subgroups, resulting in limited effectiveness. To overcome this limitation, adopting a person-centered perspective is essential. Latent Profile Analysis (LPA) provides a suitable methodological approach, as it goes beyond total scores to identify distinct latent subgroups by clustering individuals with similar symptom patterns based on model fit.21 This approach not only uncovers the underlying heterogeneity within the population but also offers a scientific foundation for developing targeted nursing strategies tailored to the specific needs of each subgroup, thereby improving the effectiveness of interventions.

To address the overlooked heterogeneity of psychosocial distress, this study employed LPA to identify distress phenotypes and their associated factors among postoperative patients with breast cancer. Methods

Study design and setting

This cross-sectional study was conducted in the breast surgery ward of a university-affiliated, tertiary-level general hospital in Guangzhou, China. From November 2022 to July 2023, patients with breast cancer who met the eligibility criteria were enrolled using a convenience sampling method.

Participants

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age 18–75 years; (2) pathologically confirmed diagnosis of non-metastatic breast cancer; (3) inpatient status with surgical treatment within the past 3 days; (4) ability to understand and communicate in Chinese; (5) awareness of diagnosis; and (6) voluntary agreement to participate with provision of written informed consent. The exclusion criteria were: (1) recurrent or metastatic breast cancer; (2) history of other malignancies; or (3) documented history of severe mental illness or diagnosed cognitive impairment.

Sample size estimation

For LPA, prior simulation studies have shown that stable and reliable results can be achieved with a sample size of n ​= ​200.23,24 Additionally, general guidelines for multinomial logistic regression recommend at least 10 participants per predictor variable.25 In this study, 15 variables were included in the regression analysis, requiring a minimum of 150 participants to ensure adequate statistical power. With 210 participants enrolled, the sample size was deemed sufficient to satisfy the requirements for both analyses.

Measures

Sociodemographic and clinical characteristics

A general information questionnaire was used to collect sociodemographic characteristics (including age, employment status, marital status, ethnicity, living arrangement, whether having children, education level, monthly income per capita, and long-term residence) and disease-related information (including triple-negative breast cancer, surgical method, site of tumor, number of catheters, and breast reconstruction). Disease-related data were extracted from the patients’ electronic medical records.

Psychosocial Distress Questionnaire-Breast Cancer

The Psychosocial Distress Questionnaire-Breast Cancer (PDQ-BC) comprises 9 subscales with 35 items: trait anxiety (items 1–10), state anxiety (items 11–16), depressive symptoms (items 17–23), social support (item 24), social problems (items 25–27), body image (items 28–29), physical problems (items 30–33), sexual problems (item 34), and financial problems (item 35). All items are rated on a 4-point Likert scale, where 1 indicates “not at all” and 4 indicates “very much,” with higher scores reflecting greater psychosocial distress.5 In the original scale, Cronbach's α coefficients for the subscales ranged from 0.70 to 0.87.5 This study employed the 8-dimensional Chinese version of the PDQ-BC, which integrates the “social support” item into the “social problems” dimension.26 The Chinese version of the PDQ-BC demonstrated high internal consistency, with a Cronbach's α coefficient of 0.927.26

Connor-Davidson Resilience Scale

Connor-Davidson Resilience Scale (CD-RISC) is a 25-item questionnaire designed to assess resilience from five dimensions: the notion of personal competence, high standards, and tenacity; trust in one's instincts, tolerance of negative effect, and strengthening effects of stress; the positive acceptance of change, and secure relationships; control; and spiritual influences.27 Each item is rated on a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from “Never” to “Almost always”, with scores from 0 to 4. The total score ranges from 0 to 100, with higher scores indicating greater resilience. Scores ≤ 56 indicate low resilience, 57–70 indicate moderate resilience, and ≥ 71 indicate high resilience. The original English version demonstrated a Cronbach's α coefficient of 0.89.27 Following cross-cultural adaptation in 2007, the Chinese version of the CD-RISC was restructured into a 25-item scale with three dimensions: tenacity (13 items), strength (8 items), and optimism (4 items).28 The Chinese version of the CD-RISC demonstrated good internal consistency, with a Cronbach's α coefficient of 0.91.28

Data collection

Data for this study were collected during the first three days after surgery, when all patients were in the early postoperative inpatient recovery phase. A trained researcher introduced the study to eligible patients and obtained written informed consent. Clinical variables were retrieved from patients’ electronic medical records, while sociodemographic information and questionnaire responses were self-reported by patients. Participants could withdraw from the study at any time if they felt uncomfortable. Upon completion, the researcher checked each questionnaire for completeness and, if any items were missing, patients were politely reminded to complete them at their discretion.

Statistical analysis

LPA was performed using Mplus 8.3. The average scores of items from the eight dimensions of the PDQ-BC were used as indicator variables. The modeling process began with a single-class framework and progressively added classes to determine the optimal solution. Model fit was evaluated using the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), and adjusted BIC (aBIC), with lower values indicating better fit.29 The model selection process included both the Bootstrapped Likelihood Ratio Test (BLRT) and the Lo–Mendell–Rubin Likelihood Ratio Test (LMRT) to compare the k-class solution with the k–1 class alternative, with P ​< ​0.05 indicating significantly improved fit for the k-class model.30 Classification accuracy was assessed using entropy values (range: 0–1), with values ​> ​0.8 reflecting higher accuracy in LPA classification.31

Following the identification of the optimal latent profile solution, additional statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 26.0. Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages, while continuous variables were reported as mean ​± ​standard deviation. Multinomial logistic regression was conducted to identify factors associated with psychosocial distress profiles in patients with breast cancer. Statistical significance was defined as a two-tailed P ​< ​0.05.

Results

Characteristics of patients

A total of 210 patients with breast cancer were included, with a mean age of 47.84 ​± ​10.48 years. Most patients were married (87.6%) and lived in urban areas (80.5%). Over half reported a monthly per capita income below 6000 RMB (61.9%) and were currently employed (62.4%). Approximately one-third (34.3%) exhibited low resilience. Additionally, 66.2% underwent breast-conserving surgery, and 39.0% received breast reconstruction. Latent profile analysis of psychosocial distress

The eight dimensions of the psychosocial distress scale were used as observed indicators to fit models with 1–5 profiles, with fit indices for each model presented in Table 2. Values of AIC, BIC, and aBIC consistently decreased as the number of profiles increased, with improvement plateauing at the three-profile model, indicating progressively better fit from one to three profiles. Entropy values for all models exceeded 0.80, reflecting high classification accuracy. However, for the four- and five-profile models, the LMRT fit indices were not significant (P ​> ​0.05), suggesting that the three-profile model was superior. Considering the proportion of each class and the practical implications of the results, the three-profile model was deemed optimal and selected for subsequent analyses. Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first study to apply LPA to identify psychosocial distress phenotypes and their associated factors in patients with breast cancer during the early postoperative period. Our findings revealed heterogeneity in psychosocial distress at this critical stage, with most patients experiencing moderate to high levels, primarily driven by physical problems and state anxiety. These results underscore the importance of promptly identifying patients with different phenotypes during the early postoperative period and developing phenotype-specific interventions to alleviate psychosocial distress.

Phenotypes of psychosocial distress

This study identified three distinct psychosocial distress phenotypes: “Resilient–Latent Anxious-Somatic” (27%), “Anxious-Somatic Dominant” (51%), and “Anxious-Somatic with Financial Toxicity” (22%), indicating that most patients with breast cancer experience considerable psychosocial distress following surgery. This finding aligns with Liu et al.,32 who reported high distress scores in 21.3% of patients, and is further supported by evidence showing severe psychosocial maladjustment in 30.4% of young to middle-aged patients with breast cancer.33 Together, these findings underscore the substantial psychosocial challenges faced by patients with breast cancer and highlight the need for targeted interventions to improve psychosocial outcomes.

A notable commonality across all phenotypes was the prominence of state anxiety and physical problems, suggesting a shared anxious-somatic core regardless of overall distress levels. These findings highlight the universal need for interventions targeting anxiety reduction and physical symptom management in patients with breast cancer. The largest group, the “Anxious-Somatic Dominant” phenotype, represents the most typical distress profile, characterized primarily by this anxious-somatic axis. In contrast, the “Resilient–Latent Anxious-Somatic” phenotype, although generally well-adjusted, still exhibited residual anxious-somatic features, underscoring the importance of continued monitoring even in low-risk patients. For these two groups, interventions such as peer-led physical activity programs, which have been shown to improve both mood and physical functioning,34 may be particularly appropriate. The third phenotype, “Anxious-Somatic with Financial Toxicity,” reflects the most severe distress pattern. In addition to pronounced anxiety and physical problems, these patients face substantial financial strain. This phenotype suggests a reinforcing cycle in which psychological and socioeconomic stressors exacerbate each other. Unlike prior studies that examined these factors separately, this finding emphasizes the need for multidimensional interventions. For this high-risk group, an integrated approach combining psychological support and financial counseling may be essential to effectively address their complex challenges.

Factors influencing psychosocial distress phenotypes in patients with breast cancer

Our study demonstrates a significant association between resilience and psychosocial distress, consistent with the findings of Tao et al.35 This relationship can be understood within the framework of the Diathesis-Stress Model, in which resilience functions as an individual diathesis factor, whereas breast cancer diagnosis and surgical treatment represent major stressors. For patients with higher levels of resilience, such stressors can be effectively buffered through the mobilization of adaptive coping mechanisms,36 thereby significantly reducing psychological distress. In contrast, patients with lower resilience lack sufficient psychological resources, making disease-related stressors more likely to exceed their coping threshold, undermine adaptive processes, and ultimately result in more severe psychosocial distress. Consistent with this theoretical interpretation, patients with low resilience were more likely to be classified into the “Anxious-Somatic Dominant” and “Anxious-Somatic with Financial Toxicity” phenotypes, suggesting that resilience may influence not only the intensity but also the nature of psychological distress. This pattern highlights a potential mechanism by which resilience shapes patients' coping responses when facing stressors such as cancer diagnosis and surgical treatment. From a biological perspective, higher resilience has been linked to attenuated hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis activation under stress, resulting in more stable cortisol levels and reduced immunosuppressive effects,37 which may buffer against chronic stress–induced emotional dysregulation. Previous studies have shown that resilience is associated with multiple aspects of distress in patients with breast cancer, including lower anxiety and depression and higher body image satisfaction.38,39 Our findings extend this knowledge by showing that resilience is differentially distributed across distress phenotypes. This suggests that resilience may act as a phenotype-specific protective factor rather than a universal buffer, and its effect may depend on the interplay between various sources of psychosocial distress. This nuanced role aligns with Rutter's conceptualization of resilience as enhancing resistance to psychosocial adversities rather than guaranteeing positive outcomes.40 Therefore, interventions aimed at enhancing resilience, such as attention and interpretation therapy—which has been recognized as an optimal approach for boosting resilience in patients with cancer—should be implemented to improve psychosocial distress.41

In our study, patients who had not undergone breast reconstruction were more likely to be classified into the “Anxious-Somatic Dominant” and “Anxious-Somatic with Financial Toxicity” phenotypes compared with those who received reconstruction. This association suggests that reconstruction status may be an important indicator for identifying groups at high risk for psychosocial distress. Within the framework of the Diathesis-Stress Model, the absence of breast reconstruction introduces additional stressors related to body image changes,42 which compound the initial major stressor of breast cancer diagnosis and surgery. Among patients with low resilience or limited social support, this cumulative stress is more likely to exceed their coping capacity, thereby precipitating more severe psychosocial distress. However, interpreting the underlying mechanisms warrants considerable prudence. On one hand, as established by prior research, the absence of reconstruction can exert a direct, adverse impact on patients' psychological and somatic state through its effects on body image, functional limitations, and perceived social stigma.43,44 On the other hand, as clinical practice demonstrates, the decision to undergo reconstruction is itself governed by a confluence of complex factors.42 For instance, advanced cancer stage, inflammatory breast cancer, or insufficient financial support not only represent common contraindications for reconstruction but also constitute independent risk factors for distress.45 Consequently, the heightened psychosocial distress observed among patients without reconstruction is unlikely to stem directly from the absence of the reconstruction itself, but is rather a reflection of these more fundamental, underlying clinical and socioeconomic adversities. From a clinical perspective, incorporating structured discussions about reconstruction options into preoperative counseling, while fully considering potential contraindications and patients’ socioeconomic circumstances, can help clinicians look beyond reconstruction status itself to explore the underlying decision-making factors, thereby facilitating more targeted interventions that integrate both clinical and economic considerations.

Interestingly, our study found that older patients with breast cancer were less likely to be classified into the “Anxious-Somatic with Financial Toxicity” phenotype than younger patients, suggesting that younger patients face more complex psychosocial challenges. This age-related difference may reflect the greater psychosocial demands encountered by younger patients, including concerns related to work, education, finances, fertility, and social relationships,46,47 as well as heightened sensitivity to body image changes and age-specific stigma.48,49 While previous studies have documented similar trends in adjustment and emotional well-being, our findings provide additional nuance by linking age differences directly to phenotypic patterns of distress. Notably, younger patients in our cohort appear more vulnerable to the interaction of emotional, somatic, and financial stressors, which may explain their higher likelihood of being categorized into this high-risk distress phenotype. Conversely, older patients may draw upon accumulated life experience and established coping strategies, facilitating psychological adjustment and mitigating emotional burden. These insights underscore the need for age-sensitive interventions tailored to younger patients with breast cancer in this phenotype, particularly addressing body image, sexual functioning, and emotional regulation, to effectively manage their complex psychosocial distress.50

In this study, married patients with breast cancer were more likely to be classified into the “Anxious-Somatic with Financial Toxicity” phenotype. Patients often regard their partners as their primary source of emotional support; however, partners also experience stress related to the patient's diagnosis and treatment.51,52 This bidirectional stress may lead to greater adjustment challenges within the marital relationship, including impaired communication, emotional detachment, and imbalance in family roles, which can negatively affect patients' psychosocial adaptation and emotional well-being.18 In addition, married patients often worry about the potential adverse impact of their illness on their partner and family, further intensifying their psychological distress.53 Therefore, for married patients with breast cancer in this phenotype, dyadic interventions involving both the patient and the partner are essential. Evidence indicates that couple-based intimacy enhancement, coping skills training, and telephone-delivered dyadic psychosocial interventions are effective in reducing psychosocial distress in both partners.54

Our study found that unemployed patients with breast cancer were more likely to be classified into the “Anxious-Somatic with Financial Toxicity” phenotype. Previous research similarly identified full employment as a protective factor against psychological distress in this population.55 Unemployment often leads to financial hardship, which significantly contributes to psychological distress due to concerns over medical expenses and everyday living costs.45 Beyond providing financial stability, employment offers critical opportunities for social engagement and reinforces personal identity. The loss of these social roles and support systems due to unemployment can result in increased social isolation, lowered self-esteem, and weakened social networks, all of which are well-recognized factors that intensify psychological distress.56 For unemployed patients with breast cancer in this phenotype, incorporating practical support such as access to medical assistance programs, breast cancer–specific financial aid, and guidance in navigating and applying for these resources into conventional intervention plans may significantly enhance psychosocial adjustment and overall well-being.

In the present study, patients with breast cancer who live alone were more likely to be classified into the “Anxious-Somatic with Financial Toxicity” phenotype, consistent with findings from a previous study conducted during the COVID-19 pandemic.57 Living alone often entails a lack of emotional support from close family members or partners, which can heighten loneliness and increase vulnerability to anxiety and depression.58 Evidence indicates that family members play a protective role in mitigating psychological distress, whereas the absence of such bonds may reduce resilience.59 Furthermore, living alone limits access to practical assistance, such as reminders for medical visits, adherence to treatment, and daily care, and may increase the burden of treatment decision-making and self-care,60 thereby exacerbating psychosocial distress. Therefore, targeted nursing interventions, such as online or offline peer support groups,61 can provide supplementary emotional and caregiving support to patients with breast cancer who live alone in this phenotype, thereby alleviating psychosocial distress.

Our results indicated that patients with breast cancer who had a lower family monthly income were more likely to be classified into the “Anxious-Somatic Dominant” phenotype. Perry et al. also reported a negative association between financial strain and psychological well-being in this population.62 Financial hardship may limit patients’ ability to manage health-related crises and heighten concerns about treatment affordability. Following diagnosis, these patients often cope with financial strain by cutting living expenses, borrowing money, selling assets, or delaying treatment.63 These strategies can intensify family and workplace tensions, reduce social support, and exacerbate psychosocial distress. To address this, coordinated efforts from policymakers, insurance providers, and health care teams are essential to alleviate treatment-related financial burdens for low-income patients.64 Clinically, early identification, proactive communication, and shared decision-making should be emphasized to enhance the effectiveness of interventions for patients in this phenotype.

In the present study, patients with breast cancer who had long-term residence in urban areas demonstrated a notably higher likelihood of being classified into the “Anxious-Somatic Dominant” phenotype. This finding suggests that the residential environment may play a role in shaping patients’ psychosocial distress patterns, although the underlying mechanisms are complex and multidimensional. Inherent urban stressors such as noise, pollution, and a fast-paced lifestyle, combined with potentially weaker community ties and neighborhood support,55,56 offer plausible explanatory pathways. However, attributing this association solely to urban residence would be an oversimplification. Urban living often serves as a proxy for a constellation of other significant variables. For instance, systematic disparities exist between urban and rural areas in socioeconomic status, occupational types, lifestyle, educational attainment, and the structure of social support networks.65 Consequently, the observed association is likely a composite reflection of the interplay between these underlying social, economic, and individual factors and the residential environment. Future research requires more sophisticated designs to disentangle these interwoven factors, thereby precisely identifying the core drivers that elevate the risk of psychosocial distress in urban patients and informing the development of effective public health strategies.

Implications for nursing practice and research

Guided by the Diathesis–Stress Model, this study offers a coherent theoretical framework for interpreting the observed heterogeneity of psychosocial distress. The three identified phenotypes are conceptualized as distinct outcomes resulting from the interaction between internal vulnerabilities (diathesis) and multi-layered external stressors. Resilience is the core diathesis factor; a deficit in resilience appears to be a prerequisite for the emergence of more complex distress phenotypes, shaping the nature of distress rather than merely influencing its severity. The cancer diagnosis and surgical procedure function as universal stressors, accounting for the common anxious-somatic core observed across all phenotypes. More complex distress phenotypes arise when the core diathesis and universal stressors are further compounded by socioeconomic (e.g., low family income) and contextual stressors (e.g., breast reconstruction). This theory-driven understanding informs the formulation of a systematic and practical intervention framework. We propose a two-phase intervention framework based on the Diathesis–Stress Model. Phase one targets the diathesis by providing universal resilience-enhancing interventions for all phenotypes, such as attention and interpretation therapy, aimed at fortifying their defenses against internal vulnerabilities. Phase two employs stratified, phenotype-specific interventions targeting multidimensional stressors. For more complex distress phenotypes, interventions must be multi-component, addressing universal stressors (e.g., symptom management), socioeconomic stressors (e.g., proactive communication and shared decision-making), and contextual stressors (e.g., structured preoperative counseling on reconstruction). This framework provides a coherent, actionable, and theory-grounded pathway for delivering targeted psychosocial care.

Limitations

Although this study is the first to use LPA to uncover the heterogeneity of psychosocial distress among postoperative patients with breast cancer, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the study relied on a convenience sample from a single urban hospital, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Replication in more diverse settings, including rural and community hospitals, is necessary to confirm the existence and characteristics of these phenotypes across broader populations. Second, the cross-sectional design precludes conclusions regarding causality or temporal dynamics. It remains unclear whether the identified profiles are stable over time or whether patients transition between them during the course of their illness. Additionally, the present study did not incorporate comprehensive clinical variables that may influence psychosocial distress in patients with breast cancer, such as cancer stage or type of adjuvant therapy. The omission of these cancer-specific factors may limit the comprehensiveness of our findings. Future longitudinal studies with larger, multi-center samples and more comprehensive variables are warranted to address these questions and to identify predictive factors over time. Finally, the exclusive use of quantitative methods limits insight into the subjective meaning of psychosocial distress. A mixed-methods approach incorporating qualitative narratives would help elucidate the lived experiences underlying each phenotype. Such an approach is essential for moving beyond statistical categorization and for informing the development of more nuanced and culturally responsive interventions.

Conclusions

This study identified three psychosocial distress phenotypes among postoperative patients with breast cancer, each with distinct characteristics and influenced by a unique combination of factors. Prompt identification of patients in each phenotype, combined with group-level interventions tailored to the specific features of each phenotype, is essential for alleviating psychosocial distress.
