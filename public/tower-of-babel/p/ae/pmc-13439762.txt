Understanding door-to-needle delays in acute ischemic stroke: a workflow-based analysis of emergency department care processes
Authors: Wasin Rattanawichai, Nichapatr Phutthikhamin
Published: 2026

Background

Acute ischemic stroke remains a leading cause of death and long-term disability worldwide [1]. Intravenous thrombolysis is unequivocally time-dependent, with shorter door-to-needle (DTN) times consistently associated with reduced mortality and improved functional outcomes [2–3]. Accordingly, DTN has become a central performance metric in international stroke guidelines, which recommend treatment as rapidly as possible and ideally within 60 min of hospital arrival [3–5].

Large registry-based and systems-focused quality improvement studies consistently demonstrate that variability in DTN time is driven predominantly by modifiable workflow processes rather than patient characteristics alone [6–9]. Sustained reductions in delayed DTN have been achieved when hospitals standardize emergency department workflows, implement early pathway activation, and monitor interval-level performance rather than relying solely on aggregate time metrics [7–10]. These findings establish DTN performance as a function of system architecture rather than isolated clinical decision-making.

However, most evidence derives from tertiary comprehensive stroke centres in high-income countries with dedicated stroke teams and advanced imaging and laboratory infrastructure [6–9]. Secondary-level hospitals in middle-income settings operate within tiered referral networks and resource-variable emergency systems, where laboratory turnaround, interdepartmental coordination, and consent processes may influence delay differently [11, 12]. Despite their central role in regional stroke care, context-specific workflow analyses in such environments remain limited [11].

Prehospital system activation and emergency medical service coordination substantially influence downstream treatment timelines [13–16], and emergency department under triage has been associated with delayed diagnostic mobilization and treatment initiation in time-sensitive conditions [17]. Understanding how upstream activation interacts with downstream workflow intervals in secondary-level hospitals is therefore essential. This study addresses that gap by examining DTN delay through a structured emergency department workflow analysis in a Thai secondary-level setting. However, existing studies rarely integrate upstream system activation with interval-level workflow analysis within secondary-level settings, leaving a critical gap in understanding how delays propagate across sequential care processes.

Methods

Study design and setting

The study was conducted at Kalasin Hospital, a secondary-level provincial hospital in northeastern Thailand. The hospital serves as the primary referral centre for Kalasin province and surrounding districts, covering a predominantly mixed urban–rural population of approximately 800,000–960,000 residents. Within the Thai public health system, the hospital provides emergency stroke evaluation and intravenous thrombolysis but does not perform endovascular thrombectomy onsite. Patients requiring mechanical thrombectomy are transferred to tertiary comprehensive stroke centres. The hospital manages approximately 349 acute ischemic stroke admissions annually, with about 21.00% receiving intravenous thrombolysis.

The emergency department operates under a continuous 24-hour staffing model with on-site emergency physicians and nursing teams. Laboratory services and CT imaging are always available; however, stroke care coordination relies on locally defined operational protocols rather than dedicated stroke activation units typical of tertiary centres.

Prehospital transport in Thailand is provided through a nationally coordinated EMS system comprising hospital-based, public, and foundation-based units with varying stroke alert capability.

Between October 1, 2017, and February 28, 2022, acute ischemic stroke care was delivered through a standardized pathway incorporating triage-based activation, expedited neuroimaging, laboratory evaluation, and structured shared decision-making prior to thrombolysis.

Study population

All consecutive adult patients (≥ 18 years) with confirmed acute ischemic stroke who received intravenous thrombolysis in the emergency department between October 1, 2017, and February 28, 2022, were eligible. Patients were excluded only if essential timestamp data required to calculate DTN time were unavailable. No sampling was performed; the entire eligible cohort was analysed.

Outcome and performance metrics

The primary outcome was delayed intravenous thrombolysis, defined as DTN time greater than 60 min. The 60-minute threshold was selected in accordance with international guideline recommendations and its established use in quality benchmarking and stroke registry reporting [3–7]. DTN time was defined as the interval from emergency department arrival to initiation of intravenous thrombolysis. Onset-to-needle time was defined as the interval from symptom onset or last known well to treatment initiation.

Conceptual framework: emergency department workflow analysis

To align with a workflow-based quality improvement framework and to avoid circularity, DTN time was conceptualized as a downstream performance outcome resulting from a sequence of interrelated processes. Analyses were therefore structured into two components: upstream determinant analysis and process interval analysis. This workflow-based analytic structure reflects established systems-oriented stroke improvement models that prioritize process mapping and interval-level performance monitoring over isolated predictor analysis [8–10, 18].

Upstream determinant analysis

Upstream factors were defined as variables identifiable at or before initial emergency department assessment. These included patient baseline characteristics (age and sex as case mix), clinical presentation and stroke severity (NIHSS score on arrival and fluctuating neurological deficit), and prehospital access and early system activation factors. Prehospital variables comprised mode of arrival, prehospital stroke alert, and onset-to-door time. Stroke triage accuracy at presentation was also evaluated [8, 10, 18].

Process interval analysis

Emergency department workflow intervals were analysed descriptively as intermediate diagnostic and treatment processes contributing to DTN time, consistent with prior systems-based stroke workflow research [8, 10, 18]. These intervals included door-to-examination time, CT imaging turnaround time, waiting time for coagulation results, consultation time, pre-thrombolysis management time, final test-to-needle time, and patient or family decision time. Coagulation testing was obtained urgently in patients with known or suspected anticoagulant use, particularly warfarin therapy (e.g., atrial fibrillation), or when anticoagulation status was uncertain. It was not routinely required for all patients prior to thrombolysis. These intervals were not included as predictors in regression models.

Definition of variables

Stroke severity at presentation was assessed using the National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale at ED triage (NIHSS-T) [3]. Fluctuating neurological deficit was defined as a ≥ 2-point change between triage assessment and pre-thrombolysis evaluation. Triage misclassification referred specifically to false-negative activation of the stroke pathway among confirmed ischemic stroke cases. Detailed operational definitions of all study variables are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Statistical analysis

Prespecified upstream variables were entered into the multivariable model based on clinical relevance and prior evidence. Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIF < 5). Model performance was evaluated by discrimination (area under the receiver operating characteristic curve [AUC]) and calibration (Hosmer–Lemeshow test).

Median differences in workflow intervals between timely and delayed DTN groups were estimated using quantile regression at the 50th percentile, a distributionally robust method for modeling median effects in skewed time data [19]. Sensitivity analyses included: (i) covariate-adjusted quantile regression models controlling for age, sex, baseline NIHSS, prehospital alert status, and triage classification; (ii) exclusion of extreme interval values (upper 5th percentile); and (iii) alternative DTN thresholds (45 and 75 min) to assess robustness of interval-level findings. Stratified sensitivity analyses were performed by repeating quantile regression models according to prehospital stroke alert status.

Results

Study population and treatment timeliness

Between October 1, 2017, and February 28, 2022, 283 patients with acute ischemic stroke received intravenous thrombolysis through the emergency department FAST-Track pathway. Thirty-eight patients were excluded because of incomplete timestamp data, leaving 245 patients for analysis. Overall, 75 patients (30.60%) experienced DTN times exceeding 60 min, although the overall median DTN was 50 min (IQR 40–63), remaining below the recommended 60-minute threshold.

Baseline characteristics and stroke severity

Patient characteristics and upstream factors are summarized in Table 1. Most patients in both groups presented with moderate stroke severity (NIHSS 5–15) at initial assessment. Worsening neurological symptoms were more frequent among patients with delayed DTN compared with those treated within 60 min. Median onset-to-needle time was 158 min (IQR 128.80–203.00) in the non-delayed group and 179 min (IQR 141.00–210.00) in the delayed group. Correspondingly, median DTN time was 44 min (IQR 36.00–50.30) among non-delayed patients and 75 min (IQR 65.00–87.00) among delayed patients.

Upstream determinants of delayed door-to-needle time

In multivariable analysis restricted to upstream factors identifiable at or before initial emergency department assessment (Table 2), absence of prehospital stroke alert, triage misclassification, and fluctuating neurological symptoms were independently associated with delayed DTN. Absence of a prehospital stroke alert was associated with lower odds of timely treatment (adjusted OR 0.09, 95% CI 0.03–0.28). Triage misclassification was strongly associated with delayed DTN time (adjusted OR 21.17, 95% CI 2.44–183.38). Compared with patients whose neurological status worsened, those with unchanged or improving deficits had significantly lower odds of delay (adjusted OR 0.04, 95% CI 0.01–0.21). Age, sex, baseline NIHSS score, and onset-to-door time were not independently associated with delayed DTN.

Discussion

This study extends the existing door-to-needle literature by integrating upstream system activation determinants with interval-level workflow decomposition within a secondary-level hospital context. The findings demonstrate that DTN delay appears to arise from interactions between upstream system activation and downstream workflow intervals, with inefficiencies propagating cumulatively across sequential phases of care rather than reflecting a single dominant bottleneck. Although the cohort’s median DTN remained below 60 min, nearly one-third of patients experienced delayed DTN, underscoring substantial within-department variability not attributable to case mix alone.

Large registry-based analyses have demonstrated that improved clinical outcomes are associated not only with shorter median DTN times but also with reduced variability achieved through system-level optimisation [6–7]. However, most prior work derives from tertiary comprehensive stroke centres in high-income settings, where imaging turnaround and rapid team activation are emphasized as principal bottlenecks [8, 18]. In contrast, the present analysis suggests that in secondary-level and resource-variable environments, delay emerges as a cascading workflow phenomenon, in which early system activation shapes downstream efficiency and late-stage intervals amplify accumulated delay. Without prehospital notification or accurate triage activation, diagnostic mobilization and laboratory processing may proceed sequentially rather than in parallel, increasing cumulative delay and reinforcing the importance of early pathway activation as a determinant of downstream workflow efficiency.

By restricting regression modeling to determinants identifiable at or before initial emergency department assessment, this study clarifies how upstream activation influences subsequent workflow performance. Absence of prehospital stroke alert and triage misclassification were independently associated with delayed DTN. These factors should not be interpreted solely as statistical predictors but as structural modifiers of workflow trajectory. This interpretation aligns with prior evidence demonstrating that prehospital notification accelerates imaging and shortens overall treatment timelines [14–17]. The high discriminatory performance of the upstream model likely reflects the strong effect sizes of system-activation variables rather than overfitting, as only prespecified clinically relevant predictors were included.

Fluctuating neurological symptoms were independently associated with delayed DTN. Rather than reflecting intrinsic patient complexity alone, this association likely represents diagnostic uncertainty that slows triage categorization, imaging interpretation, and thrombolysis decision-making. Within a cascade framework, patient-level variability influences treatment timeliness through its interaction with system responsiveness rather than functioning as an isolated determinant of delay. Baseline NIHSS score itself was not independently associated with DTN time, in contrast to tertiary-centre studies in which higher stroke severity may expedite treatment through clearer clinical presentation [20]. This discrepancy likely reflects contextual differences in workflow architecture; in secondary-level hospitals, stroke severity alone may not automatically trigger parallelized diagnostic and preparatory processes. In transferred patients, repeat imaging may occasionally have been required when neurological deterioration occurred or when prior imaging was unavailable for immediate decision-making. In addition, discussion regarding possible transfer for thrombectomy in severe presentations may have contributed to treatment delay, although these factors were not directly measured.

Interval-level analysis further delineates where delay accumulates. Patients with delayed DTN experienced substantially prolonged waiting times for coagulation results, extended final test-to-needle intervals, and longer patient or family decision time. While upstream determinants shape the initial trajectory of care, the absolute magnitude of delay accumulated predominantly within these late-stage intervals, demonstrating temporal amplification across sequential workflow phases. Upstream and downstream contributors therefore function as interdependent components of a single propagation process rather than as competing explanations. This cascade-based conceptual model is illustrated in Fig. 1. Importantly, the persistence of interval-level effects across covariate-adjusted models and multiple sensitivity analyses supports the interpretation that these delays reflect structural workflow characteristics rather than statistical artifact or the influence of extreme observations.

Conceptual cascade model of DTN delay

Several tertiary-centre workflow studies identify imaging turnaround as the primary in-hospital bottleneck [8, 18]. In contrast, laboratory confirmation and shared decision-making emerged as dominant contributors in this secondary-level setting, highlighting contextual variation in bottleneck distribution across health system tiers. These findings reinforce the need for setting-specific workflow analysis rather than direct extrapolation from high-resource tertiary models.

The substantial contribution of patient and family decision time to delay warrants particular attention. Although shared decision-making is an essential ethical component of thrombolysis administration, unstructured consent discussions may introduce avoidable delay in time-sensitive contexts. Evidence supports the use of standardized communication frameworks and parallelized consent preparation to reduce decision latency during acute stroke evaluation [3, 21]. In resource-variable environments where laboratory turnaround cannot always be compressed technologically, structured communication pathways represent a pragmatic and scalable system-level leverage point.

From a systems leadership perspective, these findings support governance of workflow architecture rather than isolated technical modification. Potential strategies include strengthening EMS education and prehospital stroke alert activation, implementing standardized triage protocols with decision support tools, parallelizing laboratory and imaging workflows, introducing point-of-care coagulation testing where feasible, and deploying structured consent pathways. Sustained reductions in DTN variability are unlikely to result from thrombolytic agent substitution or incremental procedural adjustments alone without coordinated redesign of emergency department processes [6, 8].

The contextual setting further differentiates the contribution of this study. Secondary-level hospitals within the Thai public health system function as provincial referral centres without onsite thrombectomy capability and operate within tiered networks structurally distinct from tertiary academic stroke centres [11]. Understanding delay propagation within such environments is particularly relevant for middle-income health systems, where workflow optimisation may offer greater feasibility and sustainability than resource expansion. By integrating upstream activation determinants with interval-level decomposition, this analysis advances a context-sensitive systems framework to guide stroke pathway redesign across comparable emergency care settings.

Although conducted at a single site, the emphasis on mechanisms of delay propagation rather than effect-size estimation enhances conceptual transferability. The cascade model advanced here reframes DTN performance as a function of workflow architecture and interdependence, positioning delayed DTN as a systems phenomenon rather than an isolated operational lapse.

Compared with prior studies conducted in tertiary comprehensive stroke centres, where imaging turnaround and rapid team activation are consistently identified as the primary bottlenecks, the present study demonstrates a distinct delay pattern in a secondary-level setting. Specifically, late-stage workflow intervals, including coagulation processing and decision-making time, contributed more substantially to overall DTN delay than early diagnostic steps. This contrast highlights the context-dependent nature of workflow inefficiencies and suggests that extrapolation of tertiary-centre models to resource-variable settings may be insufficient without adaptation.

This study introduces a cascade-based conceptual model of DTN delay, demonstrating that treatment delay is not driven by a single bottleneck but by the cumulative interaction of upstream system activation and downstream workflow intervals. This extends existing stroke workflow literature beyond tertiary settings and provides a context-sensitive framework applicable to secondary-level and resource-variable health systems.

This study has several limitations. First, as a retrospective single-centre analysis conducted in a secondary-level hospital, the findings primarily reflect local operational characteristics and may have limited generalizability to tertiary comprehensive stroke centres or health systems with different organisational structures and resource availability. Second, the analysis relied on routinely recorded electronic timestamps, which may be subject to minor recording variability and cannot fully capture complex workflow interactions—such as informal communication, task overlap, or parallel processing—potentially leading to underestimation of real-time process dynamics. Third, although multivariable adjustment was performed, residual confounding cannot be excluded, as key contextual factors—including emergency department crowding, workload variability, and team experience—were not explicitly measured or modelled. Fourth, temporal variation in workflow performance was not directly examined. While the hospital operates a continuous 24-hour stroke pathway with relatively stable staffing, fluctuations in patient volume and service demand may still influence treatment timelines. Finally, the study focused on workflow architecture rather than patient-centred clinical outcomes; therefore, causal relationships between specific workflow intervals and functional outcomes cannot be established. Prospective, multicentre studies incorporating clinical endpoints are warranted to validate the observed associations and strengthen causal inference.

Future research should prospectively validate the cascade-based workflow model across different hospital settings. Multicentre studies are needed to enhance generalizability and examine whether similar delay patterns occur in diverse organisational contexts. Incorporating real-time workflow monitoring and key system-level variables—such as workload, crowding, and team experience—may help clarify mechanisms of delay propagation beyond routinely recorded timestamps. Additionally, integrating patient-centred outcomes will be essential to assess clinical impact and strengthen causal inference. Studies evaluating targeted system-level interventions—such as prehospital activation, triage decision support, parallel workflow processes, and structured consent pathways—are warranted.

The findings support a shift from single-interval optimisation toward coordinated workflow governance across sequential care processes. Prioritising structured consent pathways and parallel laboratory–treatment processes may be more effective in reducing DTN delay in secondary-level hospitals than focusing solely on imaging acceleration. Interval-level analysis identified modifiable leverage points, particularly coagulation result processing, final test-to-needle preparation, and patient or family decision-making—stages where cumulative delay becomes operationally apparent and amenable to redesign. Improving DTN performance therefore requires parallel and anticipatory workflows, including early pathway activation, concurrent laboratory testing with treatment preparation, and triage-based mobilisation. In settings with constrained laboratory turnaround, point-of-care coagulation testing may provide incremental benefit without major infrastructural expansion. The contribution of patient and family decision time also highlights structured communication as an important system-level intervention. Prescripted consent frameworks, early identification of decision-makers, and concurrent documentation preparation may reduce decision latency while maintaining ethical standards [3, 6]. Sustained improvement is unlikely to result from isolated technical refinements alone. Instead, governance of workflow architecture—including prehospital activation, triage accuracy, laboratory coordination, and communication standardisation—offers a scalable strategy for secondary-level and resource-variable settings, supported by pathway-based activation models [22].

Conclusions

In this study, delayed DTN was associated with both upstream system activation factors and prolonged downstream workflow intervals. The findings support a cascade-like pattern of delay accumulation across sequential emergency department processes. This workflow-based perspective provides a structured approach to identifying modifiable intervals for targeted quality improvement in secondary-level stroke care settings.
