Synthesis, Bioactivity and Molecular Modeling Studies on Benzimidazole Derivatives and Its Isosteres as Potent AChE/BChE and GSK3β Inhibitors for Alzheimer's Disease
Authors: Gözde Sayar, Sulunay Parlar, Ayse Hande Tarikogullari, Mumin Alper Erdoğan, Guliz Armagan, Vildan Alptuzun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTA series of benzimidazoles and its analogs (benzoxazoles and benzothiazoles) bearing a benzohydrazide‐hydrazones moiety at the 2‐position were designed and synthesized as potential acetylcholinesterase (AChE) inhibitors. Cholinesterase (ChE) inhibitory activity results showed that all compounds displayed good inhibition of AChE, whereas only some of the compounds were active against BChE. Among the title compounds, it was found that all benzimidazole series were selective towards AChE. Kinetic analysis and molecular modeling studies were conducted on the most active compounds, namely 1j for AChE and 3g for BChE inhibition. The kinetic results showed that the tested compounds exhibited a mixed‐type inhibition mechanism on both enzymes. In cell culture studies, all compounds were evaluated for their neuroprotective effects against hydrogen peroxide (H2O2)‐induced oxidative stress in the SH‐SY5Y human neuroblastoma cell line. Several compounds demonstrated more than 30% neuroprotection at a low concentration (1 μM) in the presence of H2O2. Selected compounds, chosen based on their EC50 values, were further examined for their effects on glycogen synthase kinase‐3 beta (GSK3β), an enzyme whose activity has been reported to be increased in the brains of patients with Alzheimer's disease (AD). The inhibition of GSK3β was assessed by measuring phosphorylated GSK3β (pGSK3β, Ser9) protein expression via Western Blot analysis under oxidative stress conditions. Among the tested compounds, 1l, 2b, 2j, and 3l significantly increased pGSK3β (Ser9) protein levels compared to cells treated with tideglusib, a known GSK3β inhibitor.Keywords: AChE/BChE inhibition, Alzheimer's disease, GSK3β inhibition, hydrazide‐hydrazone, molecular modeling, synthesis

Introduction

Alzheimer's disease (AD) is a complex neurodegenerative disorder associated with progressive cognitive dysfunction and memory impairment. Although the pathogenic mechanisms underlying AD have not yet been fully elucidated, several pathological hallmarks have been identified, including intracellular aggregation of tau protein, extracellular deposition of β‐amyloid (Aβ) plaques, degeneration of cholinergic neurons, and increased oxidative stress (Alan et al. 2023; Li et al. 2018).

Acetylcholine, a neurotransmitter that is released from cholinergic nerve terminals, is known to have an essential role in cognitive functions, particularly memory and learning. The deficiency of acetylcholine level implicates the deterioration of cognitive function. AChE is responsible for the enzymatic cleavage of acetylcholine through hydrolysis, resulting in the formation of choline and acetate at synaptic and neuromuscular junctions (Johnson and Moore 2006). Since acetylcholine level decreases in AD, the AChE inhibitors are used in order to enhance its level and thereby to improve cognitive function. Especially, AChE inhibitors which can be interacted simultaneously to both the catalytic and peripheral sites of the enzyme can also affect the aggregation of toxic β‐amyloid (Hardy and Selkoe 2002; Piazzi et al. 2003). Therefore dual‐binding AChE inhibitors can be more effective agents to combat AD.

Redox imbalance between reactive oxygen species (ROS) production and detoxification of the reactive intermediates by the antioxidant system leads to oxidative stress. Oxidative stress promotes cell death through the oxidation of cellular proteins and lipids. Brain which has high amounts of unsaturated fatty acids is prone to oxidative stress (Salim 2017). Therefore, oxidative stress also plays an important role in the pathogenesis and progression of AD (Cavalli et al. 2008).

Glycogen synthase kinase‐3 (GSK‐3) is a serine/threonine kinase with diverse biological functions and exists in two isoforms, α and β. GSK3 participates in various physiological processes of neurodegenerative diseases. Especially, aberrant activity of GSK3β is closely associated with the development and progression of Alzheimer's disease–related neuropathology (Takahashi‐Yanaga 2013; Gameiro et al. 2017; Bayraktar and Alptuzun 2024). GSK3β is over‐expressed in the brain of patients and contributes to neurofibrillary tangles formation, Aβ deposition, inflammation and apoptosis as well as oxidative stress‐induced neuronal cell death (Llorens‐Martín et al. 2014; Gameiro et al. 2017) It is known that oxidative stress is related to GSK3β activation (Lee et al. 2007) and the inhibition of GSK3β reduces neuronal cell death induced by oxidative stress (Schafer et al. 2004; Martinez et al. 2011). Thus, inhibition of GSK3β induced by oxidative stress is another promising target for the treatment of AD.

Heterocyclic systems have been widely employed as scaffolds for the development of novel AChE inhibitors (Dorababu 2019, 2022; Keri et al. 2013). In our previous studies, a series of benzimidazole derivatives were synthesized and evaluated for their inhibitory activity against AChE, revealing that compounds bearing a phenyl substituent at the 2‐position exhibited remarkable inhibitory potency (Alpan et al. 2013, 2017). On the other hand, we reported that most compounds bearing hydrazone or hydrazide hydrazone groups exhibited ChE inhibitory activity, and docking studies showed that the hydrazone moiety interacts with amino acid residues in AChE enzyme (Alpan et al. 2013; Keri et al. 2013; Parlar et al. 2016, 2019, 2023) (Figure 1).

Design of the target compounds as cholinesterase inhibitors.

Acoording to literature survey, various indole derivatives displayed GSK3β inhibitory activity. The derivatives bearing its isoster such as benzimidazole, benzoxazole possessed also GSK3β inhibition and it has been observed that some of these derivatives carry a hydrazone moiety (Ruiz and Eldar‐Finkelman 2022; Pandey and DeGrado 2016; Peat et al. 2004; Saraswati et al. 2018; Xu et al. 2019; Jouanne et al. 2017; Zhao et al. 2018; Saha et al. 2021) (Figure 2).

GSK3β inhibitors containing benzimidazole and its isosteres with hydrazone functional group.

In the light of mentioned above, in this study, we designed a series of 4‐(1H‐benzimidazol/benzoxazol/benzothiazol‐2‐yl)benzohydrazide‐hydrazone derivatives as potential AChE inhibitors. Benzimidazole ring bearing phenyl at 2‐position was chosen as main structure to be able to interact with one site of AChE and a second phenyl ring was combined through the hydrazide‐hydrazone functional group to the main structure to interact with another site of the enzyme. The derivatizations on this scaffold were realized from two terminals. Benzothiazole and benzoxazole rings instead of benzimidazole were substituted as isosteres at one terminal; substituted phenyl groups and some isosteres of phenyl were substituted at the other terminal. AChE and BChE inhibitory activities and neuroprotective capacity in the presence of H2O2 were evaluated in vitro. Besides, the effect of GSK3β inhibition on selected compounds was also tested in oxidative stress conditions. To gain insight into their binding behavior, molecular docking studies were performed on the most active compounds against AChE and BChE, and molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were subsequently conducted to evaluate the stability of the ligands within the enzyme active sites.

Results and Discussion

Chemistry

In this study, arylidenehydrazones of phenylhydrazide which substituted to 2‐ position of benzimidazole/benzothiazole/benzoxazole ring were synthesized according to method in the literatures (Özkay et al. 2010; Cho et al. 2013). The target compounds were synthesized through a three‐step synthetic route. Initially, 4‐formylbenzoate was reacted with o‐phenylenediamine, 2‐aminophenol, and 2‐aminothiophenol to afford methyl 4‐(1H‐benzimidazol‐2‐yl)benzoate, methyl 4‐(1H‐benzoxazol‐2‐yl)benzoate, and methyl 4‐(1H‐benzothiazol‐2‐yl)benzoate, respectively. Subsequently, these ester intermediates were then converted into the corresponding hydrazide derivatives by refluxing with hydrazine hydrate. In the final step, condensation of the hydrazide intermediates with diverse aldehydes furnished the target hydrazide–hydrazone derivatives (Scheme 1).

Synthesis pathway of the final compounds (1a–3l).

The structures of compounds 1a–3l were elucidated based on FT‐IR, 1H NMR, and ESI‐MS spectral data and their purity was assessed by elemental analysis (see Data S1–S4). The spectroscopic data obtained were in good agreement with the proposed molecular structures. In the IR spectra, the characteristic N–H stretching vibrations of the hydrazide moiety appeared in the range of 3483–3175 cm−1, while the carbonyl (C=O) stretching bands were observed between 1671 and 1632 cm−1. Analysis of 1H NMR chemical shifts demonstrated that both aromatic and aliphatic protons appeared within their characteristic regions with characteristic multiplicities, thereby confirming the proposed substitution patterns. According to the 1H NMR spectra, the N–H proton of the NH–N=CH moiety appeared as a sharp or broad singlet in the range of δ 12.28–11.56 ppm, whereas the N–H proton of the benzimidazole ring was observed as a singlet between δ 13.08–12.80 ppm. The other prominent signal in the spectrum belongs to the N=CH proton of the arylidene structure, observed as a singlet at δ 8.71–7.81 ppm.

In benzoxazole and benzothiazole derivatives H‐4′ and H‐7′ protons were observed either as two doublets or triplet signals, while the corresponding protons in benzimidazole derivatives were detected either as two doublets or broad singlet peaks due to 1,3‐tautomerization of NH on benzimidazole in a concentration‐dependent manner of the compound (Sridharan et al. 2005). On the other hand, in 1H NMR spectra of the compounds especially bearing N,N‐dimethylamino and N,N‐diethylamino substituents at the para position of the phenyl ring was detected isomerism at 1/10 ratio. 1H NMR spectrum of compound 2e was taken at 30°C, 50°C, and 80°C to verify isomerism. Depending on the increase in temperature, decrease and loss of the signals supported that an isomerism was formed (see Data S5). In hydrazide hydrazones, E/Z isomerism can be observed depending on the factors such as temperature, light, and solvent. In general, the thermodynamically more stable E‐isomer is reported to be predominant (Palla et al. 1986; Van Dijken et al. 2015). Accordingly, the compounds are likely to be present in the E isomeric form.

The molecular weights of the final compounds were verified by electrospray ionization mass spectrometry (ESI‐MS), with the observed m/z values of the molecular ion peaks showing excellent agreement with the calculated molecular weights. The detected [M]+ ions corresponded precisely to the expected molecular masses of the compounds. Elemental analysis (C, H, N) was employed to further assess the purity of the compounds, with the experimental values falling within ±0.4% of the calculated theoretical values.

Considering the benzimidazole series (1), among the derivatives bearing phenyl (1a), benzyl (1b), and phenethyl (1c) side chains, the best inhibitory potency was detected in compound 1b. Comparing the derivatives bearing cinnamyl (1d) and phenethyl (1c) side chains, both compounds exhibited similar AChE activity. According to this result, insertion of a double bond to the phenethyl group and thereby increasing conjugation did not cause a significant change in activity. Unlike the benzimidazole series, the insertion of a double bond into the phenethyl group led to a somewhat enhancement in AChE inhibition in series 2, whereas a slight decrease was observed in series 3. For the benzoxazole (2a, 2b, 2c) and benzothiazole series (3a, 3b, 3c), the activity decreased in series 2 and increased in series 3, with respect to the distance between the phenyl ring and the hydrazone group. On the other hand, particularly the substitution of dimethylamino or diethylamino groups to the para position of the phenyl ring in series 2 improved nearly two‐fold increase in inhibitory activity. When the phenyl ring was replaced with its ring isostere, pyridine, differences in activity were observed depending on the position of the nitrogen atom. Regarding the pyridine derivatives (1g, 1h, 1i) in the benzimidazole series, compound 1g exhibited AChE activity with an IC50 value of 2.61 μM, while compounds 1h and 1i showed activity with IC50 values of 8.10 and 10.07 μM, respectively. Similarly, among the pyridine derivatives in the benzoxazole (2g, 2h, 2i) and benzothiazole (3g, 3h, 3i) series, the highest activity was also detected in compounds 2g and 3g with IC50 values of 5.12 and 1.83 μM, respectively. To sum up, 2‐pyridinyl derivatives (1g, 2g, 3g) were found to be the most active compounds among the pyridine series. On the other hand, compounds 1j and 2j bearing the pyrrole ring, with the nitrogen at the 2‐position, displayed good AChE activity with IC50 values of 1.00 and 1.94 μM, respectively. Moreover, they were the most active derivatives against AChE in their own series. These results suggest that the position of the nitrogen atom in heterocyclic rings may be critical for AChE activity. Additionally, among the derivatives where the phenyl ring was replaced with its isoster, furan (1k, 2k, 3k) and thiophene (1l, 2l, 3l), a marked increase in AChE inhibitory activity was observed in the benzoxazole derivative bearing the furan ring (2k, IC50 = 3.83 μM) and in the benzothiazole derivative having the thiophene ring (3l, IC50 = 2.80 μM). Accordingly, no correlation was found between the type of isosteric ring substitution of the phenyl moiety and AChE activity.

Comparing BChE activity results, some of the compounds possessed good or moderate BChE inhibitory activity. Generally, benzothiazole compounds (3) were more active than benzoxazole compounds (2). All benzimidazole series were inactive, thereby selective towards AChE enzyme. With respect to the benzoxazole and benzothiazole series, among pyridine derivatives (2g, 2h, 2i, 3g, 3h, 3i) only 2‐pyridinyl derivatives (2g, 3g) displayed BChE inhibition. Moreover, compound 3g was the most active compound against BChE enzyme with an IC50 value of 1.81 μM. Replacement of the phenyl‐substituted side chain (2a, 3a) with a propylphenyl group (2c, 3c) resulted in reduced activity. BChE inhibition was markedly reduced in compounds 2c and 3c. In contrast, the introduction of a double bond into the propyl chain, as in compounds 2d and 3d, led to a significant enhancement of BChE inhibition. This observation suggests that conjugation contributes positively to the inhibitory activity. On the other hand, substitution of the phenyl ring (2a, 3a) with dimethylamino and diethylamino groups, affording compounds 2e, 3e, 2f, and 3j, resulted in a considerable decrease in BChE inhibitory activity.

The inhibition mechanisms were investigated using the most potent compounds, 1j for AChE and 3g for BChE. Lineweaver–Burk double‐reciprocal plots were constructed by measuring enzyme activity at three different inhibitor concentrations and four different concentrations of the corresponding substrate. The plots of the reciprocal initial velocity (1/V) versus the reciprocal substrate concentration (1/[S]) revealed a concentration‐dependent increase in both the slopes (indicative of a decrease in V max) and the intercepts (reflecting an increase in K m ) for both compounds. The steady‐state kinetic data and corresponding Lineweaver–Burk plots for compounds 1j and 3g are presented in Data S6. This characteristic pattern is indicative of a mixed‐type mode of inhibition for the interactions between AChE and 1j, as well as between BChE and 3g, suggesting that both compounds are capable of interacting with the catalytic active site (CAS) and the peripheral anionic site (PAS) of the respective enzymes. These findings are consistent with the results obtained from the molecular modeling studies of compounds 1j and 3g. The inhibition constant (Ki) of compound 1j was determined to be 0.96 μM for AChE and 2.50 μM for BChE (Table 2).

Molecular Modeling Studies

Docking studies of compound 1j and compound 3g, the most active compounds against TcAChE and eqBChE were conducted. Compound 1j is settled in the middle of the gorge by forming a H‐bond between the NH group of the benzimidazole moiety and Phe330 residue, as well as a π‐π interaction between the phenyl and Tyr334 which are the key residues (Figure 3A,B). Homology model of BChE was used because of the lack of crystal structure of eqBChE (Coban et al. 2016). Compound 3g is settled from the middle to the bottom of the gorge. Two terminal rings formed π‐π interactions with Tyr332 and Trp82 (Figure 4A,B).

(A) Interaction map of 3g in complex with BChE (B) Proposed binding mode of 3g in complex with BChE. 3g is colored as blue sticks while the involved residues are named using three letters code and colored as dark gray sticks.

Molecular dynamics simulation for 80 ns was carried out for compound 1j inside TcAChE and compound 3g inside homology BChE and the final data revealed that the ligands were conserved in the active site for the whole MD (see Data S7). In terms of compound 1j, the selected pose was conserved for about 7.5 ns, then the ligand moved slightly to the bottom of the gorge and interacted with Trp84 by conserving the other two interactions with Tyr334 and Phe330 and settled in that position throughout the whole simulation. The average RMSD value of compound 1j and TcAChE was ~3.91 Å and ~1.69 Å for the whole MD, respectively. Likewise, compound 3g was also stable inside the BChE enzyme during the 30 ns simulation. In the first 2 ns, the ligand first lost the H‐bond with Asp70, then moved slightly up from Trp82 and settled in the middle of the gorge where Tyr332 and Trp231 are located and kept its position throughout the rest of the simulation. The average RMSD value of the ligand is 5.26 Å due to the position change.

Neuroprotection Assessment

Based on these findings, we next investigated the neuroprotective potential of the synthesized compounds. Initially, the possible cytotoxic effects of these compounds were assessed in cells at high concentrations (100 μM). Synthesized compounds alone did not display cytotoxic effects at 100 μM concentrations on SH‐SY5Y cell line (data not shown). To evaluate neuroprotection by compounds, H2O2 was used to induce cellular death. These studies were carried out at four concentrations: 0.1, 1, 10, and 100 μM, in order to determine the (%) neuroprotection and the neuroprotective EC50 values. Meanwhile, NAC‐treated cells were used as a positive control group. Cell viability was found to be 87.21% in NAC + H2O2‐treated cells, which was significantly different from H2O2‐treated cells (39.74%). In the presence of H2O2, synthesized compounds significantly protected cells and reversed cellular damage induced by H2O2. Effect of Selected Compounds on GSK3β Inhibition

Treatment of H₂O₂‐exposed cells with the compounds at their respective EC50 values significantly enhanced the phosphorylation of GSK3β at the Ser9 residue, except for compounds 2g and 3g (Figure 5A). As anticipated, tideglusib, a potent GSK3β inhibitor, significantly elevated pGSK3β (Ser9) levels in the presence of H2O2 (1.87‐fold in tideglusib‐treated cells versus 0.77‐fold in H2O2‐only treated cells) (Figure 5B). Similarly, compounds 1b, 1g, 1j, 2l, 3b, and 3j markedly increased pGSK3β (Ser9) protein expression by 1.62‐, 1.59‐, 1.19‐, 1.07‐, 1.42‐, and 1.58‐fold, respectively, compared to cells treated only with H2O2 (0.77‐fold). Moreover, compounds 1l (2.48‐fold), 2b (2.93‐fold), 2j (2.23‐fold), and 3l (2.08‐fold) demonstrated significantly higher efficacy (p < 0.05) in inhibiting GSK3β than tideglusib (Figure 5B). The considerable elevation in pGSK3β (Ser9) levels suggests that these selected compounds potentially suppress GSK3β activity under conditions where oxidative stress induces enzyme overactivation. The observed increase in pGSK3β (Ser9) levels may reflect indirect pathway modulation rather than direct catalytic inhibition.

Materials and Methods

Chemistry

All reagents, solvents, and chemicals employed in the synthetic procedures were of analytical grade, sourced from commercial suppliers such as Merck, Alfa Aesar, Sigma‐Aldrich, Fluka, Chem‐Impex, and J.T. Baker, and were used as received without additional purification. The melting points of the compounds were measured with a capillary melting point apparatus (Stuart SMP30). Infrared spectra were obtained on a PerkinElmer Spectrum 100 FT‐IR spectrophotometer (PerkinElmer Ltd., Beaconsfield, United Kingdom) fitted with a diamond ATR accessory. NMR spectra were recorded at 400 MHz in the indicated deuterated solvents, with chemical shifts (δ) reported in parts per million relative to tetramethylsilane (TMS) as the internal standard. Coupling constants (J) are given in hertz (Hz). Spectra were obtained using an AS 400 Mercury Plus NMR spectrometer (Varian, Palo Alto, USA). The following abbreviations are used: s, singlet; d, doublet; t, triplet; quin, quintet; dd, doublet of doublets; m, multiplet; br, broad. Mass spectra were recorded using a Thermo MSQ Plus LC–MS system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, San Jose, CA, USA) equipped with an electrospray ionization (ESI) source. Elemental (C, H, N, S) analyses were performed using a Leco TruSpec Micro CHNS analyzer (Leco, St. Joseph, MI, USA), and the obtained values deviated by no more than ±0.4% from the theoretical calculations. Reaction progress was followed by TLC on pre‐coated silica gel aluminum plates (Kieselgel 60 F254, E. Merck, Germany), with spots detected under UV light (254 nm).

Synthesis

Synthesis of Methyl 4‐(1H ‐Benz[d]Imidazol‐2‐Yl)Benzoate

Methyl 4‐formylbenzoate (16.4 g, 100 mmol) and sodium disulfide (9.5 g, 50 mmol) were dissolved in 100 mL of 80% aqueous ethanol, and the resulting mixture was stirred at room temperature for 30 min. 20 mL more ethanol was added and stirred in an ice bath for 2 h. The resulting salt was filtered, washed with alcohol, and dried. This salt (21.44 g, 80 mmol) and o‐phenylenediamine (8.64 g, 80 mmol) were dissolved in 40 mL of N,N‐dimethylformamide (DMF) and refluxed at 130°C for 4 h on account of ring closure. The reaction mixture was subsequently poured into ice–water at room temperature. The resulting crude product was collected by filtration and recrystallized from ethanol, as previously reported (Alpan et al. 2013; Özkay et al. 2010).

Synthesis of Methyl 4‐(Benz[d]Oxazole‐2‐Yl)Benzoate

2‐Aminophenol (1.09 g, 10 mmol) and methyl 4‐formylbenzoate (1.64 g, 10 mmol) were refluxed in absolute ethanol (80 mL) at 80°C for 1 h under argon. After cooling to room temperature, the solvent was removed, and the resulting imine intermediate was crystallized from ethanol. The obtained imine (1.28 g, 0.50 mmol) was dissolved in DMF (20 mL) with NaCN (2.5 mg, 0.050 mmol) and stirred at room temperature for 5 h in an open flask to cyclize. Upon completion, the reaction was quenched with water, and the resulting white solid was collected by filtration, washed with water, and recrystallized from ethanol (Cho et al. 2013).

Synthesis of Methyl 4‐(Benzo[d]Thiazole‐2‐Yl)Benzoate

2‐aminothiophenol (250 mg, 2 mmol), methyl 4‐formylbenzoate (100 mg, 0.2 mmol), and molecular sieve (100 mg) were mixed in DMF (10 mL) and refluxed for 6 h at 60°C. After the mixture was cooled to room temperature, 10 mg of NaCN was added and continued to stir at room temperature. After the reaction was completed, 10 mL of water was added to the stirred mixture. It was extracted 3 times with 20 mL of chloroform. The chloroform layer was evaporated under reduced pressure. Subsequently, 15 mL of absolute ethanol was added, and the mixture was cooled at 0°C–4°C for 1 h. The white crude product was filtered and crystallized by ethanol (Cho et al. 2013).

Synthesis of Hydrazide Derivatives

Ester‐derived intermediates (40 mmol) were suspended in absolute ethanol (50 mL), followed by the addition of hydrazine hydrate (80%, 15 mL). The reaction mixture was refluxed for 21–24 h, then allowed to cool to room temperature. Subsequently, the solvent was removed under reduced pressure, and the resulting residue was washed with water and crystallized from ethanol to afford the corresponding hydrazide intermediates, as previously reported (Parlar et al. 2019, 2023; Özkay et al. 2010).

Synthesis of the Final Compounds

Hydrazide intermediate derivatives (2 mmol) were condensed with various aromatic aldehydes (2 mmol) in n‐butanol (25 mL) using a catalytic amount of acetic acid under reflux conditions at 100°C. After completion of the reaction, the mixture was cooled to room temperature, and the resulting precipitate was collected by filtration, washed with cold ethanol, and recrystallized from ethanol to yield the final hydrazide–hydrazone derivatives in accordance with previously reported procedures (Parlar et al. 2019, 2023; Özkay et al. 2010).

Among the final compounds, derivatives 1a, 1e, 1f, 1k, 1l, 3a, 3e, 3g, 3h, 3i, 3k, 3l were reported previouly in the literatüre as carbonic anhydrase inhibitory, α‐glucosidase inhibitory, anticancer and antimicrobial activities (El‐Hamouly et al. 2011; Özkay et al. 2010; Gupta and Shrivastava 2022; Kucukoglu et al. 2023; Mishra et al. 2020; Taha et al. 2015). Of the final compounds, 24 derivatives are novel, and the AChE, BChE and GSK3β inhibitions for Alzheimer's disease of all synthesized compounds are reported herein for the first time.

Biological Activity Assays

AChE and BChE Inhibition Assay

Acetylcholinesterase (AChE; EC 3.1.1.7, Type VI‐S, lyophilized powder, from Electrophorus electricus ) and butyrylcholinesterase (BChE; EC 3.1.1.8, from equine serum) were purchased from Sigma‐Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). Phosphate buffer components, 5,5′‐dithiobis(2‐nitrobenzoic acid) (DTNB), and acetylthiocholine iodide (ATC), used as the substrate for AChE, were obtained from Fluka (Buchs, Switzerland). S‐Butyrylthiocholine iodide (BTC), used as the substrate for BChE, and dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) were supplied by Sigma‐Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). Absorbance measurements were performed using a Shimadzu UV‐160A UV–Vis spectrophotometer.

The ChE inhibitory potencies of target compounds were measured using Ellman's colorimetric method with a slight modification (Parlar et al. 2023; Soyer et al. 2017). Galantamine and tacrine were employed as reference inhibitors. All measurements were carried out in triplicate, and inhibitory activity was expressed as the IC50 value, defined as the inhibitor concentration producing a 50% reduction in enzyme activity. The AChE and BChE inhibition profiles of the final compounds are outlined in Table 1.

Kinetic Studies of Inhibition on AChE and BChE

Kinetic studies of AChE and BChE were conducted using Ellman's method (Ellman et al. 1961) with eeAChE and eqBuChE as the enzyme sources. Double‐reciprocal Lineweaver–Burk plots (1/V vs. 1/[S]) were generated using varying concentrations of ATC and BTC for compounds 1j and 3g, respectively. Linear regression analysis was applied for the construction of the Lineweaver–Burk plots. Changes in absorbance were measured in the presence of different concentrations of inhibitors (1j: 0.5, 1, and 2 μM; 3g: 1, 2, and 4 μM), as well as in the absence of inhibitor. Seven different substrate concentrations were used in both assays. All experiments were performed in triplicate, and data analysis was carried out using SigmaPlot software (version 14.0).

Molecular Modeling Studies

Docking Studies

For molecular docking experiments, the two compounds exhibiting the highest biological activity, namely 1j and 3g, were selected for evaluation in complex with TcAChE and homology‐modeled BChE, respectively. The crystallographic structure of AChE co‐crystallized with donepezil (PDB ID: 1EVE, 2.5 Å resolution) was retrieved from the Protein Data Bank. All heteroatoms, the co‐crystallized ligand, and water molecules were removed, and hydrogen atoms were deleted and subsequently added to the protein using MOE 2014.09.1 (Molecular Operating Environment (MOE) 2014). The receptor structure was then protonated with the Protonate 3D procedure, followed by energy minimization employing the AMBER99 force field. Due to the absence of an experimental BChE crystal structure, the previously generated homology model of eqBChE reported by Coban et al. (Coban et al. 2016; Anil et al. 2022) was adopted for docking analysis.

Compounds 1j and 3g were modeled and protonated using the Protonate 3D module in MOE 2014.09.1, followed by energy minimization with the MMFF94 force field. Ligand docking was then performed in GOLD 5.2.1 under default parameter settings (Jones et al. 1997; Friesner et al. 2004). For compound 1j, the docking search space was defined as a 20 Å radius region surrounding the carbonyl moiety of Glu199, and a total of 100 conformations were generated. Binding poses were evaluated based on the Goldscore standard precision (sp) metric, and the most plausible orientation was identified through visual inspection. The pose with a Goldscore value of 63.6792 was selected for further interpretation (Figures 3 and 4). For compound 3g, the binding site was defined as a 20 Å radius around the backbone carbon of His438, with a limit of 50 generated conformations. Goldscore (sp) values were again computed, and the pose with the highest score (63.9245) was chosen for subsequent studies.

Molecular Dynamics Simulations

To gain further insight into the dynamic properties of the most potent inhibitors, molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were conducted for compound 1j (targeting AChE) and compound 3g (targeting BChE). Model preparation and simulation workflows were carried out with the AMBER20 software package (Case et al. 2020). Optimized ligand geometries for both 1j and 3g were assigned atom types and partial charges using Antechamber, and ligand parameters were generated according to the General AMBER Force Field (GAFF) via Parmchk (Wang et al. 2004). The AChE and homology‐modeled BChE receptors were protonated and parameterized using the ff99SB force field (Cornell et al. 1995). Complexes were constructed using the LEaP module. Charge neutralization was achieved by adding 10 K+ ions to the 1j–AChE system and 5 Cl− ions to the 3g–BChE model system. Solvent was represented using an octahedral TIP3P water box, containing approximately 14,548 and 13,911 water molecules for the respective complexes, and a solute‐to‐box boundary distance of 10 Å was maintained (Jorgensen et al. 1983). Structural relaxation and elimination of steric clashes were accomplished by energy minimization in Sander.

The minimized systems were gradually heated from 50 K to 300 K under 10 kcal mol−1 Å−2 positional restraints, enabling relaxation of the solvent and counterions. Short‐range non‐bonded forces were evaluated using a 9 Å cutoff, while long‐range electrostatic interactions were calculated via the Particle Mesh Ewald approach (Essmann et al. 1995). Hydrogen‐associated bond motions were constrained using the SETTLE algorithm (Miyamoto and Kollmann 1992). Restraints were progressively removed throughout a 2 ns equilibration period, followed by 80 ns of unrestrained production simulations. RMSD values were calculated for the protein Cα atoms and for the ligand relative to the protein backbone and are reported in Å. Trajectories were inspected and visualized in VMD, and simulation output plots were generated using XMGRACE (see Data S7) (Humphrey et al. 1996).

Cell Culture Studies

The human neuroblastoma‐derived SH‐SY5Y cell line (ATCC, Catalog #CRL‐2266) was cultured in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM, Gibco, Life Technologies) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Hyclone) and a penicillin/streptomycin mixture (100 U/mL, Invitrogen). Cells were incubated at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. Test compounds were dissolved in sterile DMSO to obtain 10 mM stock solutions, while tideglusib stocks were prepared at 1 mM. Prior to treatment, all stock solutions were freshly diluted with complete culture medium. N‐acetyl‐cysteine (NAC) was dissolved and diluted with Dulbecco's phosphate buffered saline (PBS).

Cell Viability Assay

H2O2‐induced toxicity and neuroprotection by compounds were determined as previously described (Loubidi et al. 2018). Cells were plated into 96‐well culture plates at a density of 2 × 103 cells per well and allowed to adhere for 24 h. Following this initial incubation, cells were exposed to the synthesized compounds at concentrations ranging from 0.1 to 100 μM, or a well‐known antioxidant, NAC (1 mM) for an additional 24 h. Subsequently, oxidative stress was induced by H2O2 (250 μM) prepared in sterile PBS for 1 h. At the end of this exposure period, the H2O2 solution was removed and replaced with pre‐warmed complete culture medium; cells were incubated for an additional 17 h. Cell viability was then assessed using the MTS assay. Effective concentration, EC50, of each compound for cell viability was calculated using AAT bioquest EC50 calculator (https://www.aatbio.com/tools/ec50‐calculator/).

Western Blot Analysis

For immunoblotting analyses, SH‐SY5Y cells were plated in 6‐well culture plates at a density of 0.5 × 106 cells/well and maintained under standard culture conditions for 24 h. Cells were then treated with selected compounds (10 μM) or tideglusib (2.5 μM), followed by H2O2 treatment as described earlier. At the end of the experimental period, cells were lysed and total protein concentrations were determined. Equal amounts of protein (30 μg) were resolved by SDS‐PAGE, and subsequently transferred onto PVDF membranes. Membranes were blocked and incubated overnight at 4°C with a rabbit monoclonal antibody against phosphorylated GSK3β (Ser9) (1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology). After washing, membranes were incubated with a mouse monoclonal anti‐β‐actin antibody (1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology) for 1 h at room temperature as a loading control. Horseradish peroxidase‐conjugated secondary antibodies were then applied for 1 h and bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence detection system.

Statistical Analysis

Data are presented as means ± standard error (SEM). For the normality, the data were analyzed by Kolmogorov–Smirnov and the Shapiro–Wilk normality test. The statistical significance was assessed by one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey's multiple comparison procedure where appropriate. The probability value of less than 0.05 was accepted as statistically significant.

Conclusions

In this study, a series of benzimidazole derivatives and their isosteres were synthesized and evaluated for cholinesterase inhibitory activities, neuroprotective effects and GSK3β inhibitions for the potential treatment of AD. According to the ChE activity results, generally the compounds were found to be more potent on AChE than BChE, especially benzimidazole derivatives showed selectivity on the AChE enzyme. Among the tested compounds, compound 1j exhibited the most potent inhibitory activity against AChE, whereas compound 3g demonstrated the highest inhibitory activity against BChE. The kinetic studies for AChE–1j and BChE–3g support a mixed‐type mechanism, suggesting that both compounds can bind to the CAS and PAS of their respective enzymes. This interpretation is further supported by the molecular modeling results for 1j and 3g.

In addition, all compounds were evaluated for neuroprotective capacity in the presence of H2O2 and the selected compounds were further investigated for their effects on GSK3β inhibition in oxidative stress conditions. In particular, compounds 1l, 2b, 2j, and 3l remarkably enhanced pGSK3β (Ser9) protein levels compared to reference compound tideglusib.

Taking together, all these results suggest that compounds 1j, 1l, 2b, 2j, 3g, and 3l may be considered as potential agents and may represent a new starting point for further development of AD.
