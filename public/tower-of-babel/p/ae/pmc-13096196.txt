Exploring the impact of Cenozoic climate change on diversification of the Australian endemic Eurepini crickets (Orthoptera: Gryllidae: Eneopterinae)
Authors: Shilin He, Gael J Kergoat, You Ning Su, Céline Bonillo, Andreas Zwick, Ben H Warren, Tony Robillard
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe Australian continent underwent a series of dramatic climate changes during the Cenozoic, which impacted the evolutionary history and distribution of many groups of organisms. Up to now, few studies have explored the drivers of diversification and the processes that generated and maintained biodiversity in Australia. Here, we used the Australian endemic Eurepini crickets as a model system to investigate the impact of paleoenvironmental changes on the diversification of Australian lineages with a series of state-of-the-art macroevolutionary models. We inferred an Early Eocene origin of Eurepini in current Northern Australia, with the majority of descendant lineages diversifying in situ. We detected a drastic drop in diversification rate at the beginning of Pleistocene. Diversification rates of Eurepini declined with increasing aridity and decreasing temperature. Diversification rate heterogeneity was linked to contrasting geographic distributions: the arid-adapted lineages diversified at a much lower rate than their mesic-distributed counterparts. The drastic environmental changes that occurred from the Miocene onwards were likely detrimental to dispersal of Eurepini lineages, slowing down their diversification during the Pleistocene. The tribe likely suffered substantial diversity losses in the arid zone during progressive aridification of Australia. Our study highlights the importance of paleoenvironmental changes in shaping diversification dynamics of Australian lineages.Subject terms: Evolutionary ecology, Phylogenetics

Introduction

A longstanding quest in evolutionary biology has been to investigate the processes governing species diversification and their links to shifts in geographic range1,2. Two distinct categories of hypotheses have been proposed to explain the underlying factors that generate and maintain biodiversity. The Red Queen hypothesis3 proposes that biotic or species-intrinsic factors play a major role in diversification, such as species interactions, ecology, and life history traits. In contrast, the Court Jester hypothesis4 proposes that diversification is driven by historical abiotic or extrinsic factors, involving large-scale abrupt perturbations of the physical environment, such as climate change or geological events occurring over millions of years. However, the factors involved in these two types of hypotheses need not be mutually exclusive and may act in concert or in succession5–7. Therefore, for any given group, disentangling particular historical processes from others and quantifying the relative contribution of each factor is key to understanding the assembly and maintenance of diversity. Over the last decade, methodological advances have enhanced the potential of using dated molecular phylogenies to investigate the relative contribution of biotic and abiotic factors to species diversification on a macroevolutionary scale8–16. Thanks to these approaches, evolutionary history and diversification dynamics of several groups of taxa have been studied at broad geographical scales17–22.

The biogeographic history of lineages with continent-wide distributions in Australia has been the focus of several studies23–30. However, studies that have applied methods to explore the effect of particular environmental factors on diversification of Australian lineages and that aim to provide insights into the processes that generated and maintained diversity on a continental scale remain relatively scarce (e.g., refs. 31–36), especially for lineages that are endemic to this isolated continent. The latter is important since the Australian continent underwent a series of dramatic climate fluctuations over the last 50 million years (Myrs)37,38, transitioning from a warm and humid landscape dominated by rainforests to a much cooler and drier one with an arid zone that now covers around 70% of the continent in west-central Australia37,39 (see Supplementary Note for detailed Cenozoic climate history of Australia). This gradual transition was the consequence of three major cooling and drying events that occurred in the last 34 Myrs40. It can be expected that these dramatic climate fluctuations had a profound impact on evolutionary history and geographic distribution of Australian lineages, driving some of them to extinction37,41,42.

The Australian continent experienced increasing aridification since the Middle Miocene, leading to fragmentation of habitats and contraction of refugia to small pockets in the arid zone. These isolated refugia may have contributed to allopatric speciation and have had two alternative consequences for a lineage: either fostering colonization to surrounding habitable areas or leading to extinction39. It is likely that different lineages may have distinct responses to these drastic climate changes. Several groups of taxa have been found to adapt to more arid environments and show increased diversification rates, such as the agamid lizards43, pygopodoid geckos32, scincid lizards44, and gall thrips45. Banksia and eucalyptus plants have increased diversification rates only at the start of the aridification, when rain forest was replaced by sclerophyllous vegetation, and they further diversified in non-arid (mesic) areas36,46,47. Diversification rates decreased as aridification increased for several groups, such as the Australian Anilios blindsnakes35, Cryptandra shrubs48, or the Myrtaceae tribe Chamelaucieae49. Thus, Australia’s paleoclimates likely played an important role in shaping the diversification of lineages and in their present-day distribution. Exploring how these complex historical processes impacted the diversification of lineages over time will help us better understand the evolutionary history of the Australian biota.

A prime example of a diverse and underexplored Australian radiation is the tribe Eurepini (Orthoptera: Gryllidae: Eneopterinae), a monophyletic group of Australian endemic crickets comprising 64 described species50–52. However, like most Australian crickets, this group has been understudied and the true species diversity is largely underestimated51. Eurepini crickets are distributed across the continent, including desert regions in the vast arid zone. They inhabit a wide range of habitats, such as leaf litter, tree trunks and bark, shrubs and grasses in forests or open areas (Fig. 1). While some species are widely distributed in large areas, many are endemic to restricted geographical regions of Australia and confined to specific habitats. Eurepini are also particularly diverse morphologically, including in terms of body size, male genitalia, forewing length, ovipositor and cerci length, and sound producing structure50,51. The frequencies of calling songs for these crickets are relatively low (4–8 kHz) compared with some crickets from other tribes in the same subfamily that have developed high-frequency calling songs (e.g., refs. 53,54).

The origin of Eurepini has been estimated to date back to the Late Paleocene ca. 58 Ma in Australia55, or the Early Eocene ca. 49.6 Ma56, which suggests that the group experienced a series of climate fluctuations in the last 50 Myrs, particularly a dramatic drop in global temperature at the end of Eocene (Eocene-Oligocene Climate Transition [EOCT], ref. 57), the temperature increase at the Middle Miocene Climatic Optimum (MMCO) ca. 17–15 Ma58, and increasing cooling and drying climate conditions from the Middle Miocene ca. 14 Ma to the present59,60. Therefore, the tribe Eurepini is an excellent model system for investigating processes involved in the generation and maintenance of species diversity during the Cenozoic. Recently, the systematics of Eurepini have been revised by He et al.61; hence, the stage is set for further comparative studies aimed to understand the processes underlying the evolutionary history of this diverse group of crickets.

In this study, we used genetic and geographic distribution data to infer the evolutionary history of crickets in the speciose tribe Eurepini endemic to Australia. Specifically, we first inferred the most densely-sampled species-level molecular phylogeny of the tribe, based on a combination of mitogenomes and nuclear genes, with comprehensive sampling of almost all described species and a number of undescribed species. We then estimated the divergence times of the tribe using primary fossil calibrations and inferred ancestral range evolution of this diverse group of crickets on the Australian continent. Using an array of state-of-the-art macroevolutionary models of diversification, we further investigated the tempo and mode of diversification of Eurepini, as well as the impact of paleoenvironmental changes on their diversification.

Results

Molecular phylogeny and divergence time estimation

We selected the best ML tree for each of the two datasets after comparing the log-likelihood scores of resulting trees generated from distinct values of perturbation strength. The two datasets yield highly congruent topologies (Supplementary Fig. 1), with most nodes being well supported, albeit with variable positions of Piestodactylus unicolor and a few lineages in Eurepella that radiated recently with short branches and low branch support. Both topologies are also largely congruent with that inferred in He et al.61. The congruence of the two tree topologies was further supported by the AU test (p = 0.681). Since the topology of the best tree from the dataset “1 + 2 + RNAs” is more congruent with the previous study, we used it as a starting tree for subsequent dating analysis.

Based on our dating analysis (Supplementary Fig. 2), we estimated an Early Eocene origin of the Australian Eurepini crickets ca. 49.6 Ma (95% HPD: 57.8–44.73 Ma), with the diversification of most lineages occurring since the Late Oligocene–Early Miocene. After diverging from all the other groups, the most recent common ancestor (MRCA) of the genus Salmanites Chopard originated in the Early Oligocene ca. 31.2 Ma (95% HPD: 37.02–26.66 Ma). The origin of the oldest clade, consisting of the genus Miripella Robillard, Tan and Su, was estimated to occur in the Late Eocene ca. 35.46 Ma (95% HPD: 41.74–30.47 Ma). The species-rich clade corresponding to the genus Napieria Baehr originated from an MRCA in the Early Oligocene ca. 28.65 Ma (95% HPD: 33.48–25.48 Ma). The second oldest clade, consisting of the genus Eurepa Walker, was also inferred to have a Late Eocene origin ca. 34.25 Ma (95% HPD: 39.8–30.3 Ma). The estimated crown age of the small clade Arrakis was also in the Early Oligocene ca. 29.65 Ma (95% HPD: 34.82–25.8 Ma). In contrast, the small clade corresponding to a part of the paraphyletic genus Piestodactylus Saussure (Piestodactylus A) had the youngest age estimate (4.85 Ma, 95% HPD: 8.54–2.19 Ma) compared with all the other groups. The age estimate for the MRCA of the relatively young clade formed by the genus Arilpa Otte and Alexander was found to be the end of Oligocene ca. 24.22 Ma (95% HPD: 28.21–21.16 Ma). The second Piestodactylus group (Piestodactylus B) was estimated to originate in the Early Oligocene ca. 30.9 Ma (95% HPD: 35.94–27.89 Ma). Finally, the divergence time of the most speciose clade Eurepella was dated to the Early Miocene ca. 22.94 Ma (95% HPD: 26.67–20.43 Ma).

Historical biogeography

Out of the two evaluated time-stratified models (without founder-event speciation; see the Discussion section for the rationale underlying this), the model postulating that barriers to dispersal increased through time (M1) had the best likelihood score (LnL of −240.09 vs. −244.69) and an AIC_wt of 0.98 (Supplementary Data 1). DEC was recovered as the best-fitting model for this analysis, with an AICc_wt of 1.0 (Supplementary Data 1). From the biogeographic inference based on the M1 DEC model, Northern Australia was inferred to be the most likely ancestral area of origin for Eurepini (Fig. 2). During the Eocene and Oligocene, three distinct lineages (corresponding to genera Miripella, Napieria, and Salmanites, respectively) mostly diversified in situ in Northern Australia, with a few range expansion and dispersal events in the Euronotian and Northern Desert during the Miocene. It is only in the last 10 Myrs that they finally reached the Eremaean and Southwest Australia. The remaining Eurepini lineages (corresponding to genera Arilpa, Arrakis, Eurepa, Eurepella, and Piestodactylus A and B) grouped as a large clade that was inferred to colonize the current Northern Desert area ca. 40 Ma. In this clade, most lineages diversified in situ during the Oligocene and it is only during the Miocene that several lineages colonized the Eremaean and Euronotian. For this clade, our results suggest that Southwestern Australia was independently colonized three times in the last 10 Myrs.

Diversification of Eurepini

BAMM did not detect a significant rate shift across the Eurepini phylogeny (Supplementary Fig. 3), but supported a single diversification regime with elevated speciation rate in an early stage of evolutionary history, followed by a progressive decline in speciation rate, and a constant low extinction rate since origin of the tribe to the present, leading to a general trend of net diversification rate similar to that of speciation rate (Supplementary Figs. 3 and 4). As with BAMM, RevBayes also found no distinct rate shift, while different species on the phylogeny displayed varying speciation rates (Supplementary Fig. 5).

The CoMET analysis indicated a relatively constant speciation rate (ca. 0.08 events/lineage/Myr) from the origin of the tribe to the Early Pleistocene ca. 2 Ma, followed by a sharp drop in speciation rates up to the present (Fig. 3a), which corresponds to the detected rate shift (2lnBF > 6; Supplementary Fig. 6a). Extinction rate remained rather low and constant throughout the history of the tribe (ca. 0.01 events/lineage/Myr; Fig. 3b). The combined effects of speciation and extinction dynamics resulted in a pattern of net diversification rates similar to that of speciation rate (Fig. 3c). As a consequence of a dramatic decline in speciation over the last 2 Myrs, the 95% confidence interval of net diversification over this period included the possibility of net diversity loss near the present (Fig. 3c). There was no strong evidence of mass extinction for the Eurepini (2lnBF < 2; Supplementary Fig. 6b, d).

a Speciation rate. b Extinction rate. c Net diversification rate.

The fit of four types of diversification models (i.e., constant-rate, time-dependent, temperature-dependent, and aridity-dependent models) was compared to determine which one best explains the diversification of Eurepini. Among the fitted models, the one with linear dependency of speciation on aridity and constant extinction based on the data inferred with Tiatragul et al.35 (derived from Scotese and Wright62) best fitted the Eurepini phylogeny (AICc = 676.496; Table 1, see Supplementary Data 2 for full set of fitted models). The best-fitting model suggests that speciation rate linearly declined with the increase of aridity (λ = 0.478, α = −0.0095; Fig. 4) and extinction rate remained constant. The second best-fitting model (AICc = 678.027) is a time-dependent model with a linear dependency; it is statistically supported because of a ΔAICc < 2. The latter model has an extinction rate at present that is superior to the speciation rate, which is consistent with a pattern of diversification slowdown63. Within the time-dependent series of models, the pure birth model with positive linear dependence of speciation on time performed better than the others (α = 0.0008, AICc = 685.421; Table 1), which is also congruent with the BAMM analysis in inferring declining speciation rate over time. Comparing the three sets of temperature-dependent models, we found that the model with a linear dependence of speciation and extinction on mean global temperature (α = 0.0646, AICc = 682.335; Table 1; see also Supplementary Fig. 7) provided a better fit to the Eurepini phylogeny than those inferred from Tiatragul et al.35, which refer to the Australia-specific temperature trend.

Discussion

Australia is characterized by its isolation64, high biodiversity, and endemism. Although the Australian continent has been relatively stable tectonically, it experienced a series of climate changes in the Cenozoic epoch, accompanied by dramatic landscape successions37,38. Although existing studies have explored the diversity and evolutionary history of numerous different groups, few attempts have been made to investigate the underlying processes and factors contributing to the diversity on the continent. Therefore, our study takes advantage of recent methodological advances to explore the macroevolutionary history of the Australian-endemic Eurepini crickets.

Ancestral range of origin and diversification patterns

With comprehensive sampling and three external fossil calibrations, we estimated an Early Eocene origin for Eurepini, which is slightly younger than the age inferred by Vicente et al.55, but close to the recent results of Le Flanchec et al.56, though the latter used different molecular markers, calibration method and taxonomic sampling from this study. Our results support the hypothesis that the tribe originated in Northern Australia (sensu Ebach et al.65) at a period in the Cenozoic when the region was warmest, with a greenhouse climate60,66, and dominated by rainforests across the continent37,38. Otte and Alexander50 hypothesized that Australian crickets (including Eurepini) colonized the continent from New Guinea via the Torres Straits, based on the proximity of New Guinea and similarity of the two faunas. Although this hypothesis is consistent with our finding of a Northern Australia origin for the tribe, it is worth highlighting that geological evidence indicates that New Guinea was largely submerged until the Early Pliocene and that it is only in the last 5 Myrs that most of its landmass emerged above sea level67,68. However, we cannot exclude the hypothesis that the New Guinean proto-archipelago, that started emerging in the Late Eocene/Oligocene when the Indo-Australian plate approached and collided with other plates69 acted as a potential dispersal route for Eurepini, as was inferred in several groups of organisms such as birds70,71, frogs72, or lizards73,74. An alternative hypothesis would be an out-of-Australia origin that could be related to ancient biogeographic events, when Australia and South America started breaking up from Antarctica ca. 90 Ma75,76. This hypothesis is supported by two studies on historical biogeography of Eneopterinae crickets55,56, in which analyses suggested that the subfamily originated in Australia, where Eurepini diversified, while the other eneopterine lineages underwent diversification by first colonizing South America via Antarctica ca. 75 Ma. While the Northern Australia origin we inferred for Eurepini does not really provide support for this scenario, the presence of a long branch separating crown Eurepini from the MRCA of Eneopterinae, makes it possible to envision a complex history where extinct stem Eurepini lineages originated in Antarctica or southern Australia before colonization of Northern Australia by crown Eurepini.

When examining the biogeographic history of Eurepini, we found major in situ diversification events in Northern Australia for Napieria, Piestodactylus A and Salmanites, in the Eremaean for Arilpa, and in Northern Australia and the Eremaean for Eurepella, mainly from the Late Oligocene to Early Miocene. In Napieria, starting from the Late Miocene, the inferred pattern is more dynamic with several lineages expanding their range southwards at a time when arid environments started expanding, which supports the hypothesis that arid-adapted Napieria lineages descended from mesic ancestors originating from Northern Australia. This pattern is consistent with the findings of Byrne et al.77, and was recovered, for example, in blindsnakes25,35, cicadas33, diving beetles31, and geckos23. The ancestor of Arilpa colonized the Eremaean and Northern Desert at a time when the continent was still warm and humid; the current distribution of this group of crickets is mainly confined to arid regions in Western Australia. A similar pattern, where more species are present in arid Western Australia than in Central and Eastern arid Australia, is also observed in several plant groups (e.g., refs. 78,79). One possible explanation is that this could be related to the presence of the Lake Eyre Basin, which may have prevented the diversification of arid-adapted lineages in what is now Central and Eastern arid Australia. Interestingly, the diversification of most Arilpa lineages occurred much later in the Middle Miocene, when aridification had already begun and the continent was experiencing a period of increasing cooling and drying climate conditions. The ancestor of Eurepella originated in the north (Northern Australia and Northern Desert) at the beginning of Miocene, where nearly half of extant lineages diversified in situ, and it is only in the Late Miocene ca. 8.5 Ma that several Eurepella lineages expanded their range southwards in the Eremaean, at a time when climate conditions were increasingly cooling and drying out. The diversification patterns of Miripella and Arrakis are less clear, as these genera have a low extant diversity (three and two species, respectively); at least for Arrakis, the inferred pattern is consistent with the general trends of colonization of more arid environments in the South of Australia.

The results of the analyses with founder-event speciation are presented in Supplementary Data 1. Consistent with our concerns, the corresponding analyses recover null (or almost null) extinction rates and biogeographic patterns in which founder-event speciation is prevalent (values of j between 0.29 and 0.38, compared with a dispersal parameter between 0.025 and 0.049), which translates into ancestral ranges in which Eurepini are almost absent from widespread areas, unlike their observed extant ranges. Such a pattern seems biologically implausible, especially considering the biogeographic context in which most areas are adjacent and not separated by clear biogeographic barriers. To be cautious, we have therefore only discussed the results of the analyses without founder-event speciation.

Impact of historical climate change on eurepini diversification

Exploring a correlation between diversification rates and historical climate change could provide a better understanding of the potential causes of diversification rate variation. Speciation rates for the tribe inferred with CoMET were relatively stable until the beginning of Pleistocene at ca. 2 Ma, followed by a sudden drop in speciation. Therefore, most of the diversification in the evolutionary history of the tribe occurred at a relatively constant rate of speciation. This period up to 2 Ma coincides with one of the three major cooling and drying events that occurred in Australia during the Cenozoic, considered to be the driest period leading to the formation of the current sandy and stony deserts80,81. Therefore, the sharp drop in speciation rates is likely due to the Pleistocene climate change, which contributed to the increase in aridity. However, given the history of climate fluctuations in Australia, it seems surprising that Eurepini crickets have only experienced one substantial decline in diversification rate in recent times. It could be that Eurepini were little impacted by the dramatic climate changes that occurred early in their history, or that they were able to cope with these changes, a scenario that seems consistent with the Red Queen hypothesis3. Similarly, declines in diversification rate have been found in diving beetles in the Pleistocene31 and in cicadas at the end of Pliocene33. A similar decline in diversification rates, associated with the progressive aridification of environments from the Late Miocene onwards, has also been found in Africa for several groups of Lepidoptera18,19,82.

The aridity data derived from Tiatragul et al.35 show that aridity on the Australian continent steadily rose from the origin of the tribe up to 30 Ma, followed by a decline up to 25 Ma and then a further gradual increase (Fig. 4a), following the separation of South America and Antarctica and the establishment of the Antarctic circumpolar current83. Although aridity was not severe before 10 Ma, it increased from the Late Miocene onwards and intensified from the beginning of the Pliocene ca. 5 Ma to the present day. Our best-fitting diversification model indicates that diversification of the Eurepini crickets declined linearly with increasing aridity (Fig. 4c). A number of studies have posited that speciation and mutation rates are correlated, and therefore that a decrease in mutation rate could lead to a decrease in diversification rate with increasing aridity84, as observed in Eurepini. Alternatively, increasing aridity might reduce the dispersal ability of Eurepini, thus diminishing the potential for speciation through colonization of new environments. Another possibility is that with increasing aridity, environmental conditions became inhospitable for Eurepini, especially for those inhabiting the current arid zone, leading to fragmentation of populations, lower rates of molecular evolution for smaller populations and a decline in speciation rate35. All three hypotheses are consistent with a decline in diversification rate with increasing aridity being due to decreasing speciation rate, as indicated in our CoMET analysis. On the other hand, decrease in diversification rate might also be related to increasing extinction rates. The Eurepini phylogeny exhibits long branches, which might reflect past extinction, considering our comprehensive sampling of the tribe. Extinction is expected to be elevated at times of climate fluctuations, accompanied by increasing aridity. Human-introduced predators are implicated in other Australian late Pleistocene extinctions85, and could conceivably have also been important for Eurepini. In the RPANDA analyses, the second-best-fitting model also indicates a slowdown in diversification rates driven by high extinction rates. By combining the two scenarios above, the consequence of a decreasing speciation rate and an increasing extinction rate would clearly lead to declining net diversification rates.

Although all temperature-dependent models were clearly outperformed by the two best-fitting models, we cannot rule out the effect of paleotemperature on diversification of the tribe, since development of continental aridification has been found to be associated with the Late Cenozoic global cooling, that initiated the Quaternary ice ages and increased aridity80. Diversification of the Eurepini crickets generally showed a positive correlation with past temperature fluctuations, and progressively declined to the present day as the climate cooled. The positive correlation between lineage diversification and paleotemperature variation has been observed in several groups on different continents, including Andean bellflowers86, Sesamiina stemborer moths18, Parnassiinae butterflies17, Oreocharis plants20, Bicyclus butterflies19, and lycaenid butterflies82.

Different taxa may have varied responses to the arid environment conditions on the Australian continent. It is hypothesized that aridification led to fragmentation of continuous habitats and isolated populations in local microhabitats and refugia, which may further foster allopatric speciation42. Alternatively, lineages may evolve particular traits under the selective pressure of aridification that enabled them to exploit new niches and stimulate diversification. The progressive development of aridification spurred adaptive radiation in lineages that are well-adapted to arid environments and experienced an increase in diversification rates over the course of aridification32,43–45. Other groups, such as Banksia and eucalypts only experienced a temporary increase in diversification rates when aridification intensified, and only thrived outside the arid zone, in mesic areas36,46,47. However, our HiSSE analysis revealed that the Eurepini lineages inhabiting the arid zone did not diversify at a high rate as did the lizards and gall thrips, but instead had much lower diversification rates than non-arid lineages. Some woody plants in the arid zone were also found to evolve at a lower rate than those in mesic regions84. The arid-adapted cicadas also diversified more slowly than their non-arid counterparts, though the differences were not significant33. Finally, in some groups, such as blindsnakes35 and several plant groups41,48,49, there is evidence that aridification led to a decrease in diversification rates. All these diverse responses indicate that the impact of aridification on diversification of Australian lineages is probably taxon-dependent.

Although the arid zone currently occupies ca. 70% of the continent, it is a relatively depauperate biome, providing habitats for ca. 40% of termites, ants, and nonmarine reptiles39. Similarly, only ca. 40% of Eurepini cricket species inhabit the arid zone, which may reflect both a real lack of diversity in this zone due to preferences for milder climate and environment conditions, and under sampling, given much fewer sampling localities in this zone than in mesic regions. Given the climate history of Australia in the last 10 Myrs, and the drastic decline in diversification in the Early Pleistocene, the phylogenetic branching pattern of the tribe and the number of survival arid-adapted lineages, we hypothesize that a large number of lineages went extinct in the arid zone, though there is no strong evidence of mass extinction. However, during the period of progressive cooling and intense aridification from the beginning of the Late Miocene, the ancestor of a group of Eurepella lineages dispersed to the current arid zone ca. 8.5 Ma. Our BAMM and RevBayes analyses indicated that these Eurepella lineages displayed particularly elevated diversification rates (Supplementary Figs. 3c and 5c), suggesting that they evolved traits that enabled them to cope with unfavorable climate conditions, such as more elongated ovipositors in females and more elongated faces. Therefore, responses to climate change may vary among lineages within Eurepini.

Study limitations

Undersampling constitutes an obvious limitation when conducting macroevolutionary analyses. Nonetheless, for this study, its impact is likely limited as we have very comprehensive taxonomic sampling (61 of the 64 described species sampled plus 33 undescribed species). Although we cannot exclude the possibility that new Eurepini species may be discovered in the future, it is worth highlighting here that the previous sampling effort for Eurepini, and for Australian Orthoptera in general, has been quite extensive in the past (e.g., see ref. 50). For this study, approximately 2000 Eurepini specimens were also studied, with good coverage of all Australian bioregions.

Another potential caveat is related to the nonidentifiability of extinction rates with birth-death models87,88. Several studies showed that this nonidentifiability issue can be mitigated (e.g., ref. 89). For instance, Kopperud et al.90 demonstrated that rapidly changing speciation and extinction rates can still be inferred in spite of nonidentifiability. Hypothesis-driven approaches, in which a limited set of alternative a priori models are considered, are not prone to nonidentifiability issues91–93. Furthermore, Legried and Terhorst94 showed that nonidentifiability issues can be solved given a sufficiently large extant timetree and some knowledge of the present-day population. All these recent studies highlight that, even if results of diversification analyses based on birth-death models should be viewed with caution, they can nonetheless be informative. In our study, the issue of non-identifiability remains, as our diversification analyses (BAMM, CoMET, and RPANDA) are based on the same birth-death process. We therefore maintain caution in interpreting the results when we hypothesize that the decrease in net diversification rates observed in these three analyses – either linked to a drop in speciation rates and/or an increase in extinction rates – is concurrent with the progressive increase of aridity in Australia.

Conclusions

Our study has revealed detailed insights on the evolutionary history of the Australian Eurepini crickets in the Cenozoic. The tribe originated in what is currently Northern Australia in the Early Eocene, and a large number of its descendants diversified in situ. Diversification rates of the tribe did not remain constant over time, the most notable change being a sharp drop at the beginning of the Pleistocene. Paleoaridity played a major role in Eurepini diversification, but we cannot rule out a role of paleotemperature. It is therefore possible that these two factors acted synchronously on the diversification of the tribe. Eurepini likely suffered a considerable diversity loss in the arid zone in the last 10 Myrs, while a small number of lineages evolved to tolerate the harsh climatic and environmental conditions. Our study highlights the importance of past climate change in shaping diversification of a tribe of Australian crickets. Although we may not have captured all the factors and processes that shaped Eurepini diversification on the Australian continent, our study provides strong arguments for the influence of the most likely factors on Eurepini diversification, and provides significant insights into the processes that generated and maintained extant biodiversity in Australia.

Methods

Taxon sampling and genetic data

We built on the study of He et al.61 with increased taxon sampling including 33 undescribed Eurepini species. We pooled all 12S rRNA sequences from type specimens and 90 newly generated sequences from undescribed species. These new samples were selected based on a taxonomic survey of approximately 2000 Eurepini specimens from several natural history collections (ANIC, MNHN, and AM) to compile a dataset for species delimitation. To approximate species boundaries using objective criteria, we applied the Assemble Species by Automatic Partitioning (ASAP, ref. 95) method with default settings. The choice of a distance-based molecular species delimitation method was favored because of its tendency for being conservative, limiting the degree of overestimation of putative species96–99. We used the best partition recommended as a reference, and, by combining it with morphological comparisons with type specimens and published descriptions, we identified 33 putative new species (Supplementary Data 4). To provide appropriate nodes for fossil calibrations in the subsequent dating analysis, we also included 31 relatively close outgroups in the same family, and 15 distant outgroups from other families in the superfamily Grylloidea, and one more distant outgroup from the Acrididae to root the tree. Our final dataset consisted of 94 Eurepini species (including 61 of the 64 described species) and 47 outgroups (Supplementary Data 4). The genes used in the dataset included all the mitochondrial genes (13 protein-coding genes [PCGs], two rRNA genes and 22 tRNA genes) extracted from mitogenomes with Geneious v.9.1.2 (Biomatters Ltd, Auckland, New Zealand) and three nuclear genes (Histone 3 “H3”, 18S, and 28S ribosomal DNA). The newly generated sequences were obtained following the protocols of He et al.61. Individual gene datasets of rRNA genes and 22 tRNA genes were aligned using MAFFT server v.7.511 with default settings100. Reading frames of PCGs were checked manually in MEGA v.7.0.26101. Finally, all gene alignments were concatenated into one supermatrix using SequenceMatrix102.

Phylogenetic analyses

Phylogenetic relationships within Eurepini were inferred under the maximum likelihood (ML) framework with IQ-TREE v.2.1.3103. The concatenated dataset was divided into 47 partitions, with one partition for each non-coding gene, three partitions (one for each codon position) for each of the 14 coding genes, and one partition for 22 concatenated tRNA genes. We configured two datasets: (1) “all nt” from the full dataset in 47 partitions, (2) “1 + 2 + RNAs” in which the 3rd codon positions in the 14 PCGs were removed, leading to remaining 33 partitions. The best-fitting partitioning scheme and nucleotide substitution model for each partition were estimated simultaneously using the greedy algorithm in ModelFinder104, based on the Bayesian information criterion (BIC). A maximum likelihood tree search in an analysis with four independent replicates was optimized by: (1) decreasing the perturbation strength for randomized NNI (nearest neighbour interchange) to 0.0 (-pers 0.0), (2) increasing the number of unsuccessful iterations before stopping tree optimization at 500 (-nstop 500), (3) performing more thorough hill-climbing NNI search (-allnni), (4) applying “GENE” resampling strategy for partitions (-sampling GENE), and (5) allowing the merging of partitions to increase model fit (-m MFP + MERGE). Branch support was estimated using 1,000 replicates for both ultrafast bootstraps (UFBoot, ref. 105) and Shimodaira-Hasegawa approximate likelihood ratio tests (SH-aLRT, ref. 106). Nodes both with SH-aLRT ≥ 80 and UFBoot ≥ 95 were considered strongly supported, moderately supported when SH-aLRT ≥ 80 or UFBoot ≥ 95, and weakly supported when both SH-aLRT ≤ 80 and UFBoot ≤ 95.

For each dataset, we ran a series of ML analyses with a range of distinct values of perturbation strength (0.0, 0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 0.8, and 1.0). The resulting tree with the best log-likelihood score was selected as the best tree for each dataset. We then conducted an approximately unbiased (AU) test107 with IQ-TREE to investigate whether the best ML trees resulting from the two distinct sets of IQTREE analyses (“all nt” and “1 + 2 + RNAs” analyses) were significantly incongruent. For this analysis the number of resampling of estimated log-likelihoods (RELL) replicates was set at 100,000.

Divergence time estimation

Since there are currently no described fossils for Eurepini or closely related tribes in the comprehensive Paleobiology Database (accessed on 21 January 2025), we relied on three external fossils to calibrate our dating analysis (following the recommendations of Sauquet et al.108), of which one is from the family Gryllidae and the remaining two from other families in Grylloidea. Starting from the root of the tree and the positions to place the fossils are listed as follows. The first is †Protogryllus grandis Zeuner (type specimen “NHML I 10530”), a female forewing and impression fossil from a Rhaetian marine horizon in the Lilstock Formation of the United Kingdom (age range: 208.5–201.3 Myrs). It is the oldest known Grylloidea fossil, and it was used to constrain the stem age of the Grylloidea with a uniform distribution prior bounded by a minimum age of 201.3 Myrs. The second is †Palaeonemobius occidentalis Laurent and Desutter-Grandcolas (type specimen “MNHN.F.A 71375”), an amber-preserved exoskeleton fossil from a Cenomanian terrestrial amber in France (age range: 99.6–93.5 Myrs). It is the oldest known Trigonidiidae fossil, and it was used to constrain the crown age of the Trigonidiidae with a uniform distribution prior bounded by a minimum age of 93.5 Myrs. The third, †Araripegryllus camposae Martins-Neto (type specimen “GP/1T 1478”), a male forewing and compression fossil from an Aptian lacustrine–large carbonate in the Crato Formation of Brazil (age range: 122.5–112.0 Myrs), is the oldest known Gryllidae fossil. It was used to constrain the crown age of the Gryllidae with a uniform distribution prior bounded by a minimum age of 112.0 Myrs. Based on the results of the study of Song et al.109, the median age of the suborder Ensifera (308 Ma) was used as the upper bound for fossil calibrations.

Divergence times were estimated using a Bayesian relaxed-clock approach taking rate variations across lineages into account110 under the uncorrelated lognormal (UCLN) distribution clock model as implemented in BEAST v.1.10.4111. The best partitioning scheme and substitution models were determined using PartitionFinder v.2.1.1112 with the greedy algorithm, branch lengths unlinked and models set as “beast”. The best-fitting model was selected based on the BIC. Substitution models and clock models for all partitions were unlinked. Since the birth–death process113 is a more realistic branching tree prior—accounting for extinction—than the Yule process for species-level dating analysis, we chose it as the tree speciation prior. The birth–death process was set with the following uniform priors: the mean growth rate ranged from 0 to 1 birth per lineage per million years with initial value at 0.1, and the relative death rate ranged from 0 to 1 death per lineage per million years with an initial value set at 0.5. To reduce the risk of over-parameterization of parameters, the best ML tree was used as a starting tree to constrain the tree topology, so only branch lengths were estimated by disabling the tree model operators. To minimize the risk of being trapped at local optima, we conducted 36 independent runs (with different random starting seeds) in BEAST for 50 million generations of Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC), each with model parameters and trees sampled every 5000 generations, generating a total of 10,000 trees in each run, of which the first 40% of samples were discarded as burn-in. Computation for dating analyses was accelerated using BEAGLE v.3.1.0114. Part of the dating analyses were run on the MNHN high-performance computer cluster (“Plateforme de Calcul Intensif et Algorithmique”, PCIA). We used LogCombiner v.1.10.4 (from the BEAST package) to combine tree and log files, respectively, and then used Tracer v.1.7.2115 to assess convergence of MCMC and to check effective sample size (ESS) of each parameter with the recommended threshold of 200. We retained nine (out of 36) distinct BEAST runs to ensure ESSs of all parameters over 200. These runs were combined after discarding 40% of samples as burnin, and then resampled every 20,000 generations. TreeAnnotator v.1.10.4 (from the BEAST package) was used to compute the maximum clade credibility (MCC) tree with median ages and the 95% highest posterior density (HPD) at each node. We then removed all outgroups, retaining only Eurepini crickets for subsequent analyses.

Historical biogeography

Historical biogeography analyses were carried out using parametric methods116 with the R package BioGeoBEARS v.1.1.3117. We implemented the following three models: (1) dispersal-vicariance analysis (DIVA, ref. 118), (2) Dispersal–Extinction–Cladogenesis (DEC, ref. 119), and (3) Bayesian inference of historical biogeography for discrete area (BayArea, ref. 120). For area categorization, we relied on the five biogeographic provinces (Northern Desert “D”, Euronotian “E”, Northern Australia “N”, Eremaean “R” and Southwest Australia “S”) defined by Ebach et al.65 for Australian plants, with slight modifications from Toussaint et al.34. These provinces were deemed important for the historical biogeography analyses, as they are defined based on Australian biotic affinities that have accumulated over deep time65. Furthermore, this choice was also deemed relevant due to the demonstrated correlation between the distribution of plant and insect diversity (e.g., refs. 121–123). It should also be noted that the current arid zone encompasses part of the Northern Desert, the Eremaean province, and the southern Euronotian. To reflect extant Eurepini distribution patterns, a maximum number of three areas was allowed (disjunct areas were also excluded).

We further defined two time-stratified models, which implemented a matrix of scaling factors for dispersal rates (DR) between areas. For the first model (hereafter called M1), our hypothesis is that drastic environmental changes, especially those from the Miocene onwards, acted as ever-increasing barriers to dispersal for groups that were originally adapted to more humid, forested environments42. Conforming with this hypothesis, in this model increased barriers to dispersals were implemented towards the present: (1) DR between adjacent areas were initially set to 0.5, then lowered to 0.25 during the Miocene and to 0.1 during the Pliocene and at the end of the Pleistocene, (2) DR between disjunct areas were initially set to 0.25, then lowered to 0.1 during the Miocene and to 0.01 during the Pliocene and at the end of the Pleistocene. The second model (M2) reflects the opposite hypothesis, i.e., that environmental changes and especially fragmentation of the rainforest and the appearance of more arid, open areas, favored dispersal events. To reflect this hypothesis: (1) DR between adjacent areas were initially set to 0.1, then increased to 0.25 during the Miocene and to 0.5 during the Pliocene and at the end of the Pleistocene, (2) DR between disjunct areas were initially set to 0.01, then increased to 0.1 during the Miocene and to 0.25 during the Pliocene and at the end of the Pleistocene.

The best-fitting models (DIVA, DEC, or BayArea) for each analysis and model selection between M1 and M2 were selected based on corrected Akaike information criterion weight (AICc_wt). Additional analyses were also carried out with founder-event speciation (j parameter in BioGeoBears, also referred to as jump dispersal speciation), which was initially designed for island clades124. Its statistical foundation has been debated (ref. 125, but see ref. 126), as well as the fact that analyses with a j parameter always recover null (or almost null) extinction rates127. Biologically, it makes sense when areas are separated by long distances with the presence of barriers, but it should not prevail when areas are adjacent and in the absence of barriers128; hence we posit that its implementation for our study is likely not optimal.

Diversification analyses

To explore the relative contribution of intrinsic biotic interactions and extrinsic abiotic historical factors, we fitted a series of macroevolutionary models to the MCC tree, with outgroups removed. For all these analyses, a global sampling fraction of 0.97 (94 out of 97 species sampled, including 33 undescribed species) was set to consider unsampled species. For the models that were fitted in the framework of RPANDA v.2.0129, we compared the fit of all models based on the corrected Akaike information criterion (AICc) to select the best-fitting model.

Time-dependent diversification

Eurepini diversification dynamics were inferred based on our time-calibrated phylogeny of Eurepini. We used Bayesian Analysis of Macroevolutionary Mixtures v.2.5.0 (BAMM, refs. 12,130) to estimate speciation, extinction, and net diversification rates over time and across branches, and to detect the presence of distinct rate shifts. BAMM uses reversible jump MCMC (rjMCMC) to explore parameter space and estimate the number of rate shifts. Prior distributions were estimated using the setBAMMPriors function in the R package BAMMtools v.2.1.10131 and the expected number of rate shifts was set to 1.0. BAMM comprised four independent MCMC runs and was run for 20 million generations and sampled every 2,000 generations. The R package coda v.0.19132 was used to assess MCMC convergence after discarding the first 20% of samples as burn-in to ensure the ESS of each parameter over 200. BAMMtools was then used to process the output files of MCMC. In addition, we also used RevBayes to estimate branch-specific diversification rate variation across the phylogeny16. The number of discrete rate categories was set to 6, 8, or 10, and to reflect our knowledge of the past three major cooling and drying events that occurred in Australia during the last 34 Myrs40, the number of expected rate shifts was set to 3. Parameters were estimated with two independent MCMC running for 4000 iterations. The remaining parameters were set as default following the program developers’ recommendations. Convergence of the MCMC was evaluated using Tracer v.1.7.2. Given concerns regarding the reliability of BAMM estimates (ref. 133, but see ref. 134), we complemented another set of time-dependent diversification analyses using the approach of Morlon et al.8. We fitted 10 diversification models implemented in the R package RPANDA to assess whether diversification rates varied over time as follows: (1) BCST, a null Yule model, where speciation rate is constant over time and extinction rate is null; (2) BCSTDCST, a constant birth-death model, where speciation rate and extinction rate are constant over time; (3) BTimeVar, where speciation rate varies over time and extinction rate is null; (4) BTimeVarDCST, where speciation rate varies over time and extinction rate is constant; (5) BCSTDTimeVar, where speciation rate is constant and extinction rate varies over time; (6) BTimeVarDTimeVar, where speciation rate and extinction rate both vary over time. Speciation (λ) and extinction (μ) rates were parameterized and set to vary exponentially over time: λ(t) = λ0 × eαt and μ(t) = μ0 × eβt, and λ(t) = λ0 + eαt and μ(t) = μ0 + eβt for linear dependency of rates on time, where λ0 and μ0 refer to speciation and extinction rates at the present, respectively. The variables α and β indicate the trend of rate variation of speciation and extinction over time t, respectively. Positive and negative values of α or β indicate positive and negative correlations of time with speciation or extinction, respectively.

Episodic birth–death diversification

To explore the effect of historical abiotic factors on diversification, such as abrupt changes in plate tectonics or climatic conditions (e.g., the EOCT ca. 33.9 Ma), or mass extinction events, we used the model of compound Poisson process on Mass-Extinction Times (CoMET, ref. 15) implemented in the R package TESS v.2.114. In the CoMET analysis, we set the number of extinction events and expected changes to three to account for the three major cooling and drying events described above. MCMC was run until the ESS of each parameter was ≥ 500. By default, the option of mass extinction (ME) is set to “FALSE”, but to test for the presence of an ME event, we set this option to “TRUE” with a survivor fraction set to the default value (5%). Model confidence was assessed by computing the Bayes factor (BF) between models15. We considered support for a rate shift or for a sudden extinction event significant if 2lnBF ≥ 2, strongly significant if 2lnBF ≥ 6 and decisive if 2lnBF ≥ 10135.

Paleoenvironment-dependent diversification

We tested the impact of past environmental changes on Eurepini diversification using paleoenvironment-dependent models, in which diversification rates may vary as a temporal function of the environmental factor11, as implemented in RPANDA. We used the paleoclimate data (aridity and mean temperature) specific to Australia recently derived by Tiatragul et al.35 with the two paleo-Köppen climate band approaches (i.e., topographic models) based on Scotese and Wright62 and Straume et al.136 to investigate how diversification rates varied with temperature and aridity over time. To explore the relationship between speciation and extinction rates and paleoenvironmental factors, we first fitted two standard models: (1) BCST, a Yule null model in which speciation rate is constant over time and extinction rate is null, (2) BCSTDCST, a constant birth-death model in which speciation and extinction rates are both constant over time; we then fitted four environment-dependent models as follows, (3) BEnvVar, where speciation rate varies with the environment and extinction rate is null, (4) BEnvVarDCST, where speciation rate varies with environment and extinction rate is constant, (5) BCSTDEnvVar, where speciation rate is constant and extinction rate varies with environment, and (6) BEnvVarDEnvVar, where speciation and extinction rates both vary with environment. We also fitted additional paleoenvironment-dependent models with the mean global paleotemperature dataset derived from the global Cenozoic deep sea oxygen isotope record59,60 for comparison with those fitted with data from Tiatragul et al.35. Within these environment-dependent models, speciation (λ) and extinction (μ) rates were set to vary exponentially with the environmental factor: λ(E) = λ0 × eαE and μ(E) = μ0 × eβE, and similarly, for linearly dependency of rates on environmental factor: λ(E) = λ0 + eαE and μ(E) = μ0 + eβE, where λ0 and μ0 refer to speciation rate and extinction rate for a given environmental factor for which the value is 0. In total, we fitted 10 models for each of the environmental factors. The variables α and β are coefficients that measure the strength and sign of the relationship with the environmental factor (e.g., α > 0 or β > 0 indicates that speciation rate or extinction rate increases with environmental factor, and vice versa).

Distribution-dependent diversification

Since the binary state-dependent speciation-extinction (BiSSE) model137 has been criticized for its susceptibility to type I error138, we tested whether lineage distribution had an impact on diversification rates under the Hidden State Speciation and Extinction (HiSSE) framework with the R package hisse v.2.1.1213. The HiSSE model is an extension of BiSSE that includes additional parameters to account for diversification rate heterogeneity that is not associated with the observed traits13. Thus, we used the HiSSE model to incorporate unmeasured factors (i.e., hidden states) that could affect diversification rates except for the observed focal trait13. Specifically, we investigated whether diversification rates of the arid-adapted lineages differed from those outside the arid zone. The boundaries of the arid zone were defined based on the dataset from Terrestrial Ecoregions of the World at the World Wildlife Fund (ref. 139, also see ref. 140), following previous studies specific to Australia (e.g., refs. 28,141 and Supplementary Fig. 8), including one that implemented the same approach with HiSSE35. We coded distributions of Eurepini species inside and outside the arid zone as two discrete observed states. In total, 39 out of the 94 sampled species were found to inhabit the arid zone (Supplementary Data 5). In the HiSSE model, we set two hidden states (A: hidden state absent, B: hidden state present) within each of the observed states (i.e., 0A, 1A, 0B, 1B). In addition, we also included two types of character-independent (CID) models, CID-2 and CID-4, which were used as the novel null model of BiSSE and HiSSE, respectively, assuming that the evolution of a trait is independent of the diversification process without forcing the diversification process to be constant across the entire tree13. The CID-2 and CID-4 models contain the same number of diversification process parameters as the BiSSE and HiSSE models, respectively. We fitted 26 models to the Eurepini distributional dataset (Supplementary Data 3): including six BiSSE-like models, four CID-2 models, five CID-4 models, 11 HiSSE models and one CID-4 model developed by Harrington and Reeder142. Within each of the four types of models, they varied mainly in the number of distinct transition rates (q), extinction fraction rates (ε = μ/λ), and net turnover rates (τ = μ + λ), and the most complex HiSSE model included four net turnover rates, four extinction fraction rates, and eight distinct transition rates. In addition, transition rates between both the observed and hidden states for dual transitions (i.e., q0A ↔ q1B, q1A ↔ q0B) were not allowed. All SSE models were compared using AIC, ΔAIC and Akaike weight to select the best-fitting model. We then estimated confidence intervals for relevant parameters and transformed extinction fraction ε and net turnover rate τ to obtain speciation (λ), extinction (μ), and net diversification rates (r = λ − μ) using the “SupportRegion” function in HiSSE13 from the best-fitting model.
