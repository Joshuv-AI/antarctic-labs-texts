Recrudescent severe malaria due to Plasmodium falciparum following treatment in a Taiwanese patient: a case report
Authors: Yu-Chuan Tseng, Meng-Yu Cheng, Peter Bor-Chian Lin, Shih-Fen Hsu, Jen-Jen Hsu, Yi-Cheng Shen, Mao-Wang Ho, Chiung-Tzu Hsiao, Po-Ren Hsueh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSevere malaria caused by Plasmodium falciparum remains a life-threatening disease, particularly in non-immune individuals returning from endemic regions. While prompt treatment can usually achieve parasite clearance, recrudescence after initial recovery is rare and presents unique diagnostic and management difficulties. Background

Severe malaria caused by Plasmodium falciparum remains a major global health challenge, particularly for non-immune individuals returning from endemic regions. The disease may rapidly progress to multi-organ failure and carries a high mortality risk if not diagnosed and treated promptly. In non-endemic areas, its rarity and diverse clinical manifestations further complicate timely diagnosis.

High parasitaemia (> 2% in non-immune patients) is a key diagnostic criterion for severe malaria and is associated with an increased risk of complications such as acute kidney injury, liver failure, and severe systemic inflammation. In P. falciparum malaria, the risk of vital organ dysfunction rises with parasite density. In low-transmission settings where individuals generally lack pre-existing immunity, mortality rises sharply when parasite density exceeds 100,000 parasites/μL—approximately 2% parasitaemia [1]. Current guidelines from the World Health Organization (WHO) and U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (U.S. CDC) recommend prompt parenteral artemisinin-based therapy, sometimes with adjunctive agents, as the mainstay of treatment [1, 2]. However, recrudescence remains a concern, particularly in patients with complicated courses, which may present additional therapeutic challenges [1].

This report describes a case of severe imported falciparum malaria in a non-immune traveller, complicated by multi-organ failure and recrudescence after standard treatment, highlighting the clinical challenges and management complexities in such cases.

Case presentation

The patient was a previously healthy 66-year-old Taiwanese man. He lived alone and independently joined an organized group tour to Africa for tourism, travelling through Angola, Congo, Zaire, Gabon, São Tomé and Príncipe, Cameroon, Equatorial Guinea, Chad, the Central African Republic, and Niger. Both rural and urban areas were included in his travel itinerary. He did not take any antimalarial prophylaxis prior to departure. Upon returning to Taiwan on March 21, 2025, he developed fatigue, vomiting, diarrhoea, and jaundice. He initially sought care at a local clinic, where he received only symptomatic treatment without a definitive diagnosis, and subsequently returned home to rest.

On the evening of March 25, his symptoms worsened with chills, oliguria, and generalized malaise. He was also noted to have a low-grade fever of 37.9 °C at 21:26, which resolved spontaneously later that day without intervention. The clinic recommended direct referral to the emergency department of China Medical University Hospital (CMUH). Upon arrival at the emergency department, initial clinical assessment revealed a body temperature of 36.6 °C, blood pressure of 129/89 mmHg, heart rate of 91 beats per minute, oxygen saturation of 95% on room air, and a body weight of 70 kg. Given the patient’s recent international travel and clinical presentation, a vector-borne infectious disease was suspected, and he was admitted to an isolation ward. Comprehensive laboratory investigations were promptly initiated, including tests for malaria, yellow fever, Ebola virus disease, hantavirus infection, and leptospirosis as part of the differential diagnosis. Laboratory results at presentation (March 25, 2025) included white blood cell count, haemoglobin, platelet count, aspartate aminotransferase, alanine aminotransferase, total bilirubin, direct bilirubin, blood urea nitrogen, creatinine, estimated glomerular filtration rate, lactate dehydrogenase, albumin, sodium, calcium, and high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (Table 1). In addition, severe thrombocytopenia and Plasmodium-infected erythrocytes were identified on blood smears. Collectively, these findings suggested a diagnosis of severe malaria complicated by liver failure, acute kidney injury, and thrombocytopenia, and indicated acute multi-organ failure.

On March 26, the patient was transferred to the intensive care unit due to respiratory distress. Early that morning, the Taiwan Centers for Disease Control (Taiwan CDC) confirmed a high parasitaemia level (20%) of P. falciparum. Representative microscopic images of ring forms and trophozoites from the initial episode are shown in Fig. 1A, B. Intravenous artesunate provided by the Taiwan CDC was administered immediately upon its arrival. At CMUH, all diagnostic and therapeutic steps were promptly conducted without delay in diagnosis, reporting, or initiation of treatment. On March 27, real-time PCR results confirmed P. falciparum infection without mixed-species involvement (sample 1, S1; Fig. 2A, B). Amplification of Internal Control (IC) and Pan-Plasmodium targets validated DNA extraction and Plasmodium spp. presence (Fig. 2A). Species-specific amplification confirmed P. falciparum (P.f. S1) infection, without detecting Plasmodium vivax, Plasmodium malariae, Plasmodium ovale, or Plasmodium knowlesi (Fig. 2B). The primers and probes used for Plasmodium spp. detection were provided by the Taiwan CDC and are listed in Table 2. Given the patient’s history of yellow fever vaccination and absence of severe bleeding symptoms, the Taiwan CDC did not perform testing for yellow fever or Ebola virus disease. Serologic antibody tests for hantavirus and leptospirosis, performed by the Taiwan CDC, were negative.

On March 26, Prior to the availability of artesunate from the Taiwan CDC, hydroxychloroquine was given as a bridging therapy (800 mg initially, followed by a second dose 6 h later). Artesunate treatment (168 mg intravenously, equivalent to 2.4 mg/kg for a 70- kilogram patient) was administered at 0, 12, and 24 h, then once Daily until parasitaemia fell below 1%, from March 26–28. After artesunate, oral artemether/lumefantrine (20/120 mg, 4 tablets every 12 h for 3 days) was administered from March 28 to the early morning of March 31 [1–3]. Both artesunate and artemether/lumefantrine were supplied by the Taiwan CDC. Antimicrobial management was adapted throughout the disease course. On March 25, prior to pathogen identification, empirical broad-spectrum antibiotics—doxycycline and cefotaxime—were initiated to cover potential atypical or bacterial infections. Following the confirmation of malaria, antimalarial treatment was prioritized. Later, when nosocomial pneumonia was clinically suspected, piperacillin/tazobactam was administered from March 28 to April 2. After the patient’s condition stabilized, de-escalation to ceftriaxone was implemented from April 2 to 15.

Due to acute kidney injury complicated by lactic acidosis and oliguria, the patient underwent renal replacement therapies as follows: continuous veno-venous haemofiltration from March 26 at 17:00 to March 30 at 21:00 (overlapping with the full course of intravenous artesunate); sustained low-efficiency Daily diafiltration daily from March 31 to April 14; and intermittent hemodialysis from April 16 to May 2. On March 31 at 04:28, a second febrile episode occurred with a peak temperature of 39.1 °C, which resolved spontaneously on the same day without modification of ongoing therapies. Plasma exchange was also administered in response to acute liver failure and suspected thrombotic microangiopathy, associated with rapidly worsening thrombocytopenia and elevated lactate dehydrogenase levels. A total of ten sessions were performed on March 31 and on April 1, 2, 3, 4, 9, 10, 12, 14, and 16.

Although the parasites were initially cleared after treatment, malaria recrudesced on April 25, presenting with fever, anemia (hemoglobin 6.5 g/dL), thrombocytopenia (platelet count 48,000/μL), and laboratory evidence of ongoing multi-organ dysfunction (Table 1). Blood smears on April 25 revealed the presence of P. falciparum ring forms, trophozoites, schizonts, and gametocytes, including both a macrogametocyte (female) and a microgametocyte (male), as shown in Fig. 1C–F. A blood sample (sample 2, S2) was also submitted to the Taiwan CDC for real-time PCR testing, which again confirmed P. falciparum mono-infection without evidence of mixed-species involvement (Fig. 2B), further supporting the diagnosis of recrudescence. Genetic analysis for P. falciparum Kelch 13 (pfk13) mutations revealed no known mutations associated with artemisinin resistance. The patient received intravenous artesunate (168 mg at 0, 12, and 24 h, then daily), combined with oral doxycycline (100 mg twice daily) and intravenous clindamycin (900 mg every 8 h) from April 25 to May 1 [1, 4]. Subsequent therapy included oral artemether/lumefantrine (May 2 to 5) alongside doxycycline and clindamycin. Although WHO guidelines recommend combining artesunate with either doxycycline or clindamycin—but not both simultaneously—both agents were used in this case as an empirical precautionary measure, due to concerns about possible multidrug resistance and the inability to promptly determine the resistance profile. Given these concerns about drug resistance [5–7], the therapeutic regimen was adjusted based on the treating physicians’ clinical judgment and precautionary principles aimed at maximizing antimicrobial coverage in the context of suspected multidrug resistance and treatment failure, despite the absence of molecular evidence of resistance in this case. Parasitaemia was cleared successfully by May 6, with no recrudescence or clinical recurrence observed during the subsequent 6-week follow-up period. The patient was routinely monitored according to Taiwan CDC guidelines, including scheduled blood examinations and self-monitoring for symptoms such as fever or chills. Throughout this period, the patient remained asymptomatic, demonstrating effective and stable therapeutic outcomes [8].

Discussion

According to international guidelines, high parasitaemia remains a crucial criterion for defining severe P. falciparum malaria. The WHO considers parasitaemia > 2% significant in non-immune individuals [1], whereas the U.S. CDC defines severe malaria as parasitaemia ≥ 5% [2] or the presence of one or more clinical features, including impaired consciousness, prostration, multiple convulsions, acidosis, hypoglycaemia, severe anaemia (hemoglobin < 7 g/dL), acute kidney injury, jaundice accompanied by at least one other severe feature, pulmonary oedema or acute respiratory distress syndrome, abnormal bleeding or disseminated intravascular coagulation, and circulatory collapse or shock [1, 2, 9–11]. In this case, the patient exhibited parasitaemia exceeding 20%, accompanied by acute hepatic and renal failure, respiratory distress, and impaired consciousness, thus fulfilling the diagnostic criteria for severe malaria.

According to data from the Taiwan CDC, sixteen imported cases of P. falciparum malaria were reported in Taiwan between 2020 and 2025, including two fatalities. Importantly, this case stands out as the sole documented instance of clinical recrudescence following standard therapy within this timeframe in Taiwan. During the same period, there were nine cases of P. vivax malaria (including two relapses), one case of P. malariae, one case of P. ovale, and one case of mixed P. falciparum and P. vivax infection [12]. This epidemiological landscape highlights the exceptional challenges presented by this rare case, reinforcing the critical need for heightened clinical awareness and vigilance, especially regarding imported malaria cases complicated by multi-organ involvement and the risk of recrudescence in severe presentations.

This case further underscores important deficiencies in pre-travel prophylaxis and initial clinical management. The patient did not receive any antimalarial prophylaxis despite visiting multiple endemic regions, representing a missed opportunity for prevention. Moreover, the initial management at a local clinic was limited to symptomatic treatment, without malaria-specific diagnostic evaluation or antimalarial therapy, potentially allowing unimpeded disease progression.

Haematologic abnormalities, particularly thrombocytopenia and anaemia, are well-recognized features of severe malaria, with previous studies reporting thrombocytopenia in over 70% and anemia in over 60% of P. falciparum cases [13, 14]. However, the initial presentation of profound thrombocytopenia in the absence of anemia, as observed in this patient, is rare. Such atypical presentations underscore the heterogeneity of haematologic responses in P. falciparum infection. This atypical haematologic profile broadens the known clinical spectrum of P. falciparum infection and suggests possible variability in host response or parasite virulence. Upon recrudescence, the patient developed both thrombocytopenia and marked anaemia, consistent with typical severe malaria manifestations described in the literature.

Serial blood smears documented the dynamic Changes in erythrocyte morphology throughout the disease course. On March 28, a mild increase in spherocytes, fragmented Red blood cells (RBCs), and burr cells indicated ongoing hemolysis. By March 30, the presence of hypochromia, anisocytosis, and nucleated RBCs reflected both destruction and regeneration. During recrudescence on April 25, smears again demonstrated anisocytosis, polychromasia, and fragmented RBCs, consistent with post-artesunate delayed haemolysis. These changes, aligned with the timing of treatment, suggest that the observed anaemia was primarily related to artesunate-induced delayed haemolysis [1, 3].

Parasitaemia decreased significantly from approximately 20% infected erythrocytes at admission to undetectable levels following initial therapy, followed by recrudescence characterized by parasitaemia rising to 7%, confirming parasite resurgence. Recrudescence is defined as the recurrence of parasitaemia resulting from incomplete clearance of blood-stage parasites, frequently associated with suboptimal therapy or pharmacokinetic factors. In P. falciparum malaria, infected erythrocytes expressing Plasmodium falciparum erythrocyte membrane protein 1 (PfEMP1) adhere to the postcapillary venule endothelium, facilitating sequestration of schizonts within the deep microvasculature. This sequestration impedes splenic clearance and renders parasites undetectable in peripheral blood smears, complicating assessment of treatment efficacy [8, 15–17]. In this case, detection of mature schizonts in peripheral blood during clinical deterioration served as a marker of both disease progression and the underlying sequestration mechanism [18]. Although mature schizonts were absent on initial blood smears, their subsequent appearance during recrudescence—accompanied by high parasitaemia and laboratory evidence of ongoing multi-organ dysfunction—indicates significant disease progression. This pattern suggests that early sequestration of schizonts within deep tissues or organ-associated venous plexuses may render them undetectable in peripheral circulation at the outset (see Fig. 1D), a phenomenon reported in previous studies [1, 19, 20].

In accordance with WHO guidelines, artemether-lumefantrine in combination with antibiotics was administered intravenously for seven days, alongside renal and hepatic replacement therapies for supportive care. The patient received a dose of 168 mg artemether-lumefantrine per administration, equivalent to 2.4 mg/kg based on his 70- kilogram body weight, consistent with WHO recommendations for severe malaria treatment [1]. This dosing regimen is widely regarded as adequate in current clinical practice, suggesting that underdosing was unlikely to have contributed to the observed recrudescence. However, a literature review reported a high rate of treatment failure with the standard 6-dose artemether-lumefantrine regimen in nonimmune men over 70 kg, likely due to insufficient dosing causing low lumefantrine levels and parasite recurrence [21]; although lumefantrine levels were not measured in the present case, it is suggested that adjusting the dose or extending the treatment duration might improve outcomes in similar cases. To fully understand the causes of recrudescence, other potential factors were investigated. Molecular analysis revealed no mutations in the pfk13 gene associated with artemisinin resistance, consistent with epidemiologic data indicating a low prevalence of such mutations in Central African regions. Moreover, documented pfk13-associated resistance cases have predominantly been reported in Uganda and Rwanda, supporting the conclusion that genetic resistance was not the primary driver in this case [22–24], supporting the conclusion that classical genetic resistance was not the main driver in this instance.

In addition, extracorporeal therapies, including continuous hemofiltration, dialysis, and therapeutic plasma exchange (TPE), although essential for organ support in critically ill patients, have unclear effects on the pharmacokinetics and clinical efficacy of artesunate and lumefantrine. Currently, this remains a clinical hypothesis supported by limited evidence, with potential drug concentration reductions due to poor absorption or extracorporeal therapies considered speculative and unconfirmed. TPE non-selectively removes drugs circulating in the plasma compartment, particularly those with high plasma protein binding and low volume of distribution [25]. Artesunate and its active metabolite dihydroartemisinin have short plasma half-lives and rapidly distribute into tissues [26]. As a result, most of the drug is no longer present in plasma by the time TPE is initiated in routine clinical practice. Existing pharmacokinetic studies and clinical data suggest that dose adjustment is generally not required for artesunate in patients undergoing plasma exchange. To minimize the risk of drug loss, clinicians may consider scheduling plasma exchange sessions 1–2 h after artesunate dosing, once plasma drug levels have declined [27]. Further accumulation of clinical cases, pharmacologic studies, and therapeutic drug monitoring are needed to clarify whether these factors affect treatment effectiveness and to assess the potential need for individualized adjustments in rare or exceptional cases.

This study has several limitations. First, the initial hemoglobin level was unexpectedly high (15.0 g/dL on March 25), and no repeat measurement was performed at that time. Subsequent monitoring showed a rapid decline (13.6 g/dL on March 26; 11.2 g/dL on March 27). The reason for the initial elevation remains unclear. Second, lumefantrine levels were not measured in this patient; however, previous reports suggest that dose adjustment or prolonged treatment may improve outcomes in similar cases. Third, only pfk13 mutation analysis was conducted to assess artemisinin resistance, and other potential genetic markers of resistance were not evaluated. Finally, the effect of extracorporeal therapies on antimalarial drug pharmacokinetics was not directly assessed.

Conclusion

This case highlights that even with prompt, guideline-based therapy and the absence of pfk13 mutations, severe P. falciparum malaria in non-immune individuals can still develop recrudescence. Enhanced clinical vigilance, laboratory monitoring, and multidisciplinary care are essential for effective management. Reporting such rare recrudescent cases is crucial for improving clinical awareness, informing best clinical practices, and strengthening public health preparedness in regions with low malaria incidence.
