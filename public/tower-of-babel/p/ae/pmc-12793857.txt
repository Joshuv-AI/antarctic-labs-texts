Sleep Health and Associations With Early Cardiovascular Risk in U.S. Collegiate Female Athletes
Authors: Austin J. Rim, J.T. Miller, Mekensie Jackson, Yi-An Ko, Neeya Patel, Sanchitha Rajesh, Heather DiGregorio, Rayan El Chami, Jennifer Mansour, Muhammad Owais, James Sherrer, Erin Simpson, Braxton Walker, Sophia Wolmer, Angelo Galante, Craig Clark, Aaron L. Baggish, Donald L. Bliwise, Jonathan H. Kim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSleep apnea is common among male competitive athletes and may be associated with early cardiovascular risk. Among female athletes, the prevalence and outcomes of sleep apnea have not been described.ObjectivesThe objective of the study was to comprehensively assess sleep health and associations with markers of cardiovascular risk in an observational cohort of U.S. collegiate female athletes.MethodsWe conducted a cross-sectional analysis of female collegiate athletes from 2 National Collegiate Athletic Association Division-I programs that included comprehensive measures of sleep health with home-based sleep studies and Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index surveys; anthropometrics; echocardiography; and vascular applanation tonometry. Prevalence of sleep apnea and associations between sleep apnea and anthropometric data, systolic and diastolic blood pressure, arterial stiffness, and measures of left ventricular structure and function were assessed.ResultsA total of 68 female collegiate athletes (18.00 [18.00, 20.00] years; 63% freshmen; 25% basketball, 41% softball, 34% volleyball) participated in this study. Mild sleep apnea (apnea-hypopnea index ≥5, N = 17, 25%) and poor sleep quality (Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index >5, N = 24, 36%) were common. Athletes with sleep apnea were older (19.0 [18.0, 21.0] vs 18.00 [18.00, 19.00] years, P = 0.03) and had higher lean mass (61.6 [58.5, 67.3] vs 54.2 [51.8, 59.3] kg, P < 0.01) and pulse wave velocity (5.3 [5.1, 6.0] vs 4.9 [4.5, 5.4] m/s, P = 0.01). Lean mass independently predicted sleep apnea (OR: 1.14; 95% CI: 1.04-1.28; P < 0.01) and higher apnea-hypopnea index was independently associated with higher arterial stiffness (β = 0.09; 95% CI: 0.04-0.15; P < 0.01).ConclusionsPoor sleep health, including sleep apnea, was common in this cohort of U.S. collegiate female athletes. Future longitudinal research, focused on sex-specific differences and cardiovascular outcomes, in competitive athletes with sleep-disordered breathing is warranted.

Obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) is highly prevalent in the general population and is associated with significant morbidity and mortality including numerous pathologic cardiovascular conditions.1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 Established risk factors for OSA and the development of cardiovascular disease include advanced age, male sex, and obesity,7, 8, 9 but the treatment of moderate to severe OSA among older individuals has not been shown to definitively improve cardiovascular outcomes.10, 11, 12 Emerging data have demonstrated that OSA may also be prevalent among more youthful populations,13 including competitive athletes,14, 15, 16, 17, 18 suggesting there are opportunities to characterize the epidemiology and corollary effects of OSA on cardiovascular physiology much earlier in life.

Interest in OSA epidemiology among young, competitive athletes has generally focused on men, particularly American-style football and rugby athletes,15, 16, 17, 18, 19 with an estimated prevalence ranging between 24% to 55% in separate observational cohorts.15,16,18,19 Among U.S. collegiate male American-style football athletes, prior data have also demonstrated that mild sleep apnea predicts early subclinical cardiovascular dysfunction.15 However, female athletes have not been included in prior athlete OSA studies, thus potential sex-specific differences in sleep apnea prevalence, risk factors, and associations with early cardiovascular risk are unknown.

We aimed to address this critical knowledge gap by providing the first comprehensive analysis of sleep health among competitive, multisport U.S. female collegiate athletes to characterize the epidemiology and risk factors for early sleep apnea and the relationship between sleep-disordered breathing with numerous key cardiovascular measurements. We hypothesized that sleep apnea would be common in this female cohort and that apnea-hypopnea index (AHI) would be associated with early markers of increased cardiovascular risk.

Methods

Georgia Institute of Technology, Furman University, and Emory University Student-Athlete Cardiovascular Registry (Study population and testing)

Since 2014, the primary investigative team, in collaboration with the U.S. National Collegiate Athletic Association Division-I athletic programs at Georgia Institute of Technology (Atlanta, Georgia) and Furman University (Greenville, South Carolina), and Division-III athletic program at Emory University (Atlanta, Georgia), has longitudinally enrolled healthy collegiate student-athletes in a prospective registry to examine cardiovascular health and physiology in competitive athletes. Beginning in 2024, this registry expanded to include comprehensive sleep health assessments that included portable home sleep tests for OSA (peripheral arterial tonometry [PAT], WatchPAT [Itamar Medical Ltd], described subsequently) and a validated sleep questionnaire (described subsequently). For this study, the initial cross-sectional results taken from female volleyball, basketball, and softball student-athletes at Georgia Tech and Furman, who were enrolled in this registry and who completed sleep assessments, are reported. Exclusion criteria included inherited or acquired cardiomyopathy leading to disqualification (eg, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, long QT syndrome, myocarditis), high-risk structural pathology (eg, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, bicuspid aortic valve with aortopathy, dilated or noncompaction cardiomyopathy, clinically significant valvular disease), pulmonary hypertension, or failed National Collegiate Athletic Association drug testing. All eligible student-athletes were healthy and free from underlying and clinically manifest cardiovascular disease. In addition to the comprehensive sleep analysis, demographics, clinical characteristics, anthropometric data, 2-dimensional transthoracic echocardiography, and vascular applanation tonometry were obtained and recorded at a single preseason or postseason time point. The Emory Institutional Review Board approved all aspects of this study, and subjects provided written informed consent.

Peripheral arterial tonometry-home sleep testing (WatchPAT)

PAT technology using the clinically validated20, 21, 22 WatchPAT device was used for home sleep assessments on all study participants. Participants were asked to complete 2 nights of home sleep monitoring anytime within a 4-week timeframe for this analysis. In brief, this is a forearm-mounted device with 2 finger probes, a pneumo-optical PAT sensor, and a pulse oximeter. Overnight while sleeping, the PAT signal, oxyhemoglobin saturation, and sleep-wake states were recorded. Sleep-time detection was determined by the total recording time minus invalid signals and the wake time and internal algorithms were used to differentiate respiratory events. We employed a 3-hour minimum WatchPAT recording in accordance with minimum durations for sleep study inclusions previously employed.23,24 AHI, a validated measure of sleep apnea with high correlation with in-lab polysomnography derived AHI,25,26 was calculated as the number of apneic and hypopneic events per hour of detected sleep and calculated based on oxygen-saturation data and indication of autonomic activation from PAT. Severity of sleep apnea was categorized based on standardized criteria using AHI as follows: mild (5 ≤ AHI<15), moderate (15 ≤ AHI<30), and severe (AHI ≥ 30).27,28 Respiratory events detected from the PAT signal employed an oxygen desaturation threshold of ≥3% to compute the AHI. All recorded and deidentified data were automatically uploaded to cloud storage and reviewed by a sleep specialist with expertise in interpreting PAT (D.L.B.) who was blinded to participant demographics and characteristics. One athlete, the only participant with severe sleep apnea, was an extreme outlier with disproportionate influence on model estimates and was thus excluded from analysis. The remainder of valid and sleep specialist approved studies were included in the final analysis. For participants who completed 2 nights of sleep analysis (N = 60/68), AHI was reported as the mean of both studies. Night-to-night consistency in AHI between studies was high (r = 0.51, P < 0.001).

Pittsburgh sleep quality index-sleep questionnaire

The Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI),29 a validated, self-reported questionnaire used to assess sleep quality over the preceding month, was administered to all study participants before home-sleep study analysis. The PSQI comprises 19 unique items/questions that evaluate multiple dimensions of sleep including subjective sleep quality, latency, duration, and disturbances. Additional questions regarding the use of sleep medications and daytime somnolence are also included in the PSQI. Responses were used to calculate a total unitless global score, ranging from 0 to 21, with higher scores indicating worse sleep quality. Poor sleep quality was indicated by a global PSQI score >5. Specific questions relating to the frequency of disturbances leading to poor sleep quality were dichotomized to either “yes” for frequency of the disturbance occurring at least once during the past month or “no” if no disturbances occurred over the past month.

Anthropometric data (Body composition and skin folds)

Percent body fat (BF%) was measured using the validated 7-site skinfold technique, which includes the measurement of subcutaneous fat thickness at the chest, abdomen, thigh, triceps, subscapular, suprailiac, and midaxillary regions.30,31 All measurements were taken or supervised by experienced exercise physiologists on the right-side using Lange Skinfold Calipers, with the average of 2 readings at each site recorded for analysis. Using the 7-site caliper measurements, BF% was calculated using the Siri equation, which incorporates a validated regression model.32 Lean mass was calculated by subtracting the estimated BF weight, derived from the Siri equation, from total body weight.

2-dimensional transthoracic echocardiography

Transthoracic echocardiography was performed using a commercially available system (Vivid-I, GE Healthcare). Two-dimensional and tissue-Doppler imaging from standard parasternal and apical positions were performed by experienced sonographers. Definitions of normality for cardiac structure and function were adopted from the most recent guidelines.33 Left ventricular (LV) mass was calculated using the area-length method (accounts for LV morphology in both the short and long axis) and indexed to body surface area. Frame rates were individualized per study for optimal image quality between 60 and 100 Hz. Comprehensive assessment of cardiac diastolic function using tissue-Doppler imaging was performed, and tissue velocities (E′, A′, and S′) were measured from color-coded images at the lateral and septal mitral annulus. E′ was then reported as the average value between the 2 measurements. Poststudy offline data analysis (EchoPAC, version 7; GE Healthcare) was performed by study investigators (A.J.R. and J.H.K.).

Vascular applanation tonometry

Arterial stiffness was measured using high-fidelity applanation tonometry (SphygmoCor, Atcor Medical). The primary measure of arterial function was the carotid–femoral pulse wave velocity (PWV), the gold standard index of arterial stiffness and an established independent risk factor for adverse cardiovascular outcomes.34,35 All applanation tonometry measurements were made with participants in the supine position. PWV was measured by acquisition of pressure waveforms within the carotid and femoral arteries. The distance between the carotid and femoral measurement sites was recorded manually using the “foot-to-foot method”.35,36 Pulse-wave analysis derivation >80% of the operator index and PWV with < 10% SD was required for quality control. Studies were performed by experienced device operators with the software programmed to acknowledge internal quality control.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are summarized as median (IQR) or mean (SD) and categorical variables using counts and percentages. Variables were compared using Mann-Whitney U test for continuous variables or chi-square for categorical variables. Kruskal-Wallis test followed by Dunn test with Holm correction was performed to evaluate for intersport differences in AHI between athletes. To test the association between AHI as a continuous variable and our primary cardiovascular outcome measures of systolic blood pressure, diastolic blood pressure, tissue-Doppler E′, PWV, and LV mass index, univariate and multivariable linear regression models, adjusted for age, self-identified race, BF%, and lean mass, were performed. Logistic regression was performed to test the association between anthropometric and clinical risk factors (age, BF%, body mass index, lean mass, PSQI scores) with the probability of having sleep apnea (ie, AHI ≥ 5). Each model included the predictor of interest and was adjusted for a consistent set of covariates: age, self-identified race, BF%, and lean mass where appropriate. All statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.3.1). A P value of ≤0.05 was considered significant.

Results

Female athlete characteristics

Of the 78 subjects eligible for the study, 10 did not complete a sleep study and were ineligible for analysis. A total of 68 female collegiate athletes (median age 18 [18, 20] years; 63% freshmen; softball [N = 28, 41%], volleyball [N = 23, 34%], basketball [N = 17, 25%]) completed comprehensive data acquisition, including home sleep testing and represented the final study cohort. A total of 38 (56%) athletes self-identified as White and 17 (25%) as Black.

There were significant differences in body composition comparing freshmen vs upper classmen with weight (69.9 [63.4, 74.9] vs 78.3 [74.2, 84.4] kg, P < 0.01) and lean mass (53.7 [51.3, 59.0] vs 59.4 [56.4, 65.1] kg, P < 0.01) significantly lower, respectively, among freshmen. BF% (22.9 [18.2, 25.2] vs 22.0 [20.0, 28.0], P = 0.85) did not differ between freshmen and upperclassmen, respectively.

Comparison of female athletes stratified by AHI

A total of 17 (25%) athletes had an abnormal AHI ≥5 (6.7 [5.9, 8.5]) with 1 athlete demonstrating AHI ≥15 consistent with moderate sleep apnea (Central Illustration). No athlete in this cohort was found to have severe sleep apnea. Compared to athletes without sleep apnea (AHI <5), those with at least mild sleep apnea were older (19.0 [18.0, 21.0] vs 18.00 [18.0, 19.0] years, P = 0.03), had higher lean mass (61.6 [58.5, 67.3] vs 54.2 [51.8, 59.3] kg, P < 0.01), weight (80.7 [74.2, 89.7] vs 70.8 [65.0, 77.6] kg, P < 0.01), PWV (5.3 [5.1, 6.0] vs 4.9 [4.5, 5.4] m/s, P = 0.01), and LV mass index (86.9 [79.4, 92.5] vs 78.1 [70.3, 86.5] g/m2, P = 0.03), respectively (Table 1). Discussion

The purpose of this study was to comprehensively assess sleep quality and health in a large cohort of U.S. collegiate female athletes. In addition, we believe this is also the first study to rigorously evaluate cardiovascular health metrics in accordance with sleep health among young female athletes. Our primary findings are as follows. First, we observed that mild, subclinical sleep apnea was common in this cohort. Second, independent of the tonometric evaluation of sleep health, sleep quality was relatively poor among these athletes based on numerous self-reported qualitative measures. Finally, higher severity of subclinical sleep apnea, as measured by AHI, was associated with higher PWV, the gold standard noninvasive marker of arterial stiffening, an established primary mechanistic determinant of hypertension, and an independent cardiovascular risk factor.35,37,38 In aggregate, poor sleep quality and mild sleep apnea are common among select female U.S. collegiate athletes and may be associated with acquired, early cardiovascular risk. These data emphasize the critical need to prioritize further longitudinal and prospective research efforts aimed to delineate potential sex-specific sleep apnea differences in young athletes and to understand the impact of impaired sleep quality on both general and cardiac health outcomes in young female athletes.

Our findings provide novel insights into the epidemiology of sleep apnea observed among young competitive athletes. In the general population, although more common among older men with prevalence estimates >30%,39, 40, 41 OSA is still prevalent among both older and younger sedentary women with estimates between ∼4% to 20%.13,40 Among young, competitive athletes, a prior study analyzing 40 male U.S. collegiate American-style football players with established OSA risk factors revealed ∼50% of the cohort had mild sleep apnea with AHI ≥5.15 In the present study, we observed a sex-specific difference with ∼25% of these ostensibly healthy, multisport female U.S. collegiate athletes demonstrating evidence of at least mild sleep apnea. Although our data also suggest there may be sport-specific differences in the prevalence of sleep apnea, further studies, inclusive of larger populations of multisport female athletes, are required to affirm the presence and degree of sport-specific sleep apnea risks.

In the general population, OSA is strongly associated with several common cardiovascular conditions including hypertension,3 atrial fibrillation,4 coronary artery disease,42 and stroke,1 but treatment with continuous positive airway pressure has not definitively led to improvements in cardiovascular outcomes,10,12 perhaps because outcomes have only been studied in older individuals with more severe forms of OSA and established cardiovascular disease. We previously reported that among collegiate American-style football players with subclinical OSA, higher AHI correlated with higher PWV and relative reductions in cardiac diastolic function,15 both markers of cardiovascular physiologic maladaptation and increased risk of cardiac pathology, including hypertension. We also observed that subclinical sleep apnea is present among select collegiate female athletes was associated with early arterial stiffening.

Proposed mechanisms of arterial stiffness as a consequence of sleep apnea include endothelial dysfunction and hypoxia-induced inflammation.43 Women may have increased sensitivity of the vascular endothelium to intermittent hypoxia compared to men.44 In the present study, we observed potential sex-specific differences in the cardiovascular risks of early sleep apnea as higher AHI was associated with higher PWV, but not impairments in cardiac diastolic function as previously suggested among male American-style football players.15 Whether this potential sex-specific difference is related to undefined sport-specific factors or indicative of an inherent biological difference and whether differences in cardiovascular outcomes exist between young male and female athletes with mild sleep apnea are unknown. Sex-specific and carefully controlled mechanistic and prospective outcomes analyses, inclusive of these same key cardiovascular measurements concomitant with comprehensive sleep assessments, are warranted to address these uncertainties.

Clinical and research implications

The comprehensive evaluation of sleep quality in these select collegiate female athletes provides additional important insights into general athlete health. First, sleep quality was relatively poor in this cohort as demonstrated by numerous dimensions of the PSQI.29 How our findings generalize to female athletes participating in other sporting disciplines and to male athletes represents a critical future research direction. Second, we observed that older female athlete age and higher lean mass were associated with higher AHI. Higher lean mass predictive of higher AHI is contrary to the presumed mechanistic paradigm present in male athletes, such as American-style football players, in which high BF% and larger neck circumference presumably increase the risk of sleep apnea.15,17 Although speculative, the combination of older (nonfreshmen) age and higher lean mass suggests that undefined, longitudinal training-related phenomena and/or other environmental stimuli (eg, academic pressure, or other collegiate life stressors) may have affected risks of sleep apnea observed among these female athletes. Understanding the true significance of the association between AHI and lean mass further underscores the need to prioritize sex-specific and longitudinal OSA work in athletes.

Study Limitations

We acknowledge several limitations in this study. First, this study was cross-sectional and therefore subject to potential unmeasured confounding variables, particularly in the analysis of factors associated with AHI and the potential effects of lean mass. Second, although this represents the largest study of female athletes inclusive of comprehensive sleep and cardiovascular measurements to date, there remains the possibility that we may have been underpowered to detect associations between AHI and other important cardiovascular variables such as blood pressure. Moreover, because this study was cross-sectional and the majority of study participants were freshmen, we were unable to establish longitudinal associations between AHI and blood pressure over time. However, arterial stiffness is an established mechanistic precursor to overt hypertension, which aligns with the results of this analysis. Third, 8/68 (12%) did not complete 2 home sleep assessments. Fourth, although dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry scans are the gold standard for BF% estimation, we used skinfold calipers for BF% estimation. However, skinfold measurements remain a validated method for accurate field measurements in younger, fit individuals, especially when performed by experienced operators, as in our study.31 Finally, beyond self-identified White and Black female athletes, we were limited in the capture of other self-identified racial and ethnic constructs.

Conclusions

In this cohort of U.S. collegiate female athletes, numerous measures of sleep quality were relatively poor and the presence of subclinical sleep apnea was common. Higher AHI was associated with early arterial stiffness suggesting that potential sleep apnea-associated cardiovascular risks may exist for this population. Future longitudinal studies examining sex-specific differences in sleep health outcomes among competitive athletes and underlying mechanisms of sleep-associated cardiovascular abnormalities in this population are warranted.PerspectivesCOMPETENCY IN MEDICAL KNOWLEDGE: Sleep apnea and poor sleep quality may be more common among young female athletes and associated with the development of arterial stiffness, an established cardiovascular risk factor.TRANSLATIONAL OUTLOOK: The high prevalence of mild sleep apnea and effects on cardiovascular function and risk factors among young female athletes warrant additional prospective and longitudinal analyses.
