Estimation of mortality and economic burden attributable to ground-level O3 in Central Iran using BenMAP CE
Authors: Zahra Safari, Reza Fouladi-Fard, Mohammad Hassan Mahmoudian, Ahmad Rahbar, Alireza Omidi Oskouei, Faramarz Azimi, Nayereh Rezaie Rahimi, Maria Fiore
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAir pollution remains one of the most significant environmental health threats in urban areas. Ground-level O3, a secondary pollutant formed through photochemical reactions of nitrogen oxides (NOx) and volatile organic compounds (VOCs) under sunlight, can lead to serious respiratory and cardiovascular effects. This study aimed to quantify the short-term mortality and economic burden attributable to O3 exposure in Qom, central Iran, during the summer of 2019, providing local evidence to support air quality management policies.MethodsHourly O3 concentration data were collected from the Qom Environmental Protection Agency for April to August 2019. After data cleaning and interpolation, the daily maximum 8-h average O3 concentration was calculated. Health impact and related economic burden were estimated using the U.S. EPA’s BenMAP-CE model (version 1.5.8.11). Relative risk (RR) and baseline incidence (BI) values for cardiovascular and respiratory mortality were obtained from previous epidemiological studies. The Value of Statistical Life (VSL) was adjusted for Iran’s gross domestic product (GDP) to estimate the economic cost. Descriptive statistics and concentration–response functions were applied to determine avoidable deaths and associated losses.ResultsThe mean 8-h maximum O3 concentration during the study period was 0.16 ppm, exceeding the EPA guideline (0.07 ppm) by 2.18 times. Short-term exposure at this level was linked to an estimated 239 premature deaths, including 190 from cardiovascular and 49 from respiratory diseases. The highest impact was observed in adults aged 30–39 years. The total economic loss attributed to these deaths was approximately 43.6 million USD, indicating a substantial burden on public health and the economy.ConclusionsThis study highlights the urgent need for effective air quality management in Qom. Reducing emissions of O3 precursors such as NOx and VOCs, especially during warm months, could yield considerable public health and economic benefits. Localized assessments using tools like BenMAP-CE can inform evidence-based policymaking and contribute to sustainable urban development.Keywords: Air pollution, BenMAP-CE, O3, Health risk assessment, Economic Costs

Background

Air pollution is one of the major environmental health challenges worldwide and has been associated with increased morbidity and mortality. Numerous epidemiological studies have demonstrated that both short and long-term exposure to ambient air pollution can adversely affect human health, particularly through respiratory and cardiovascular outcomes.[1–5]. Beside long-term effects of air pollution, air pollution is known as one of the causes of million deaths globally each year [6–9]. In addition to the mentioned risks of environmental exposure, the importance of workplace exposure for workers has been more noticed [10]. The main air pollutants include particulate matter with aerodynamic diameters of 10 µm (PM10) and 2.5 µm (PM2.5), sulfur dioxide (SO2), nitric dioxide (NO2), and ozone (O3) [11–14].

Among ambient air pollutants, ground-level O3 is of particular concern due to its strong oxidizing properties and its adverse effects on the respiratory and cardiovascular systems. O3 is a secondary pollutant formed through photochemical reactions involving nitrogen oxides (NOₓ) and volatile organic compounds (VOCs) under sunlight, and its concentrations typically peak during warm seasons [15–17]. Therefore, O3 concentrations generally peak during the warm season when meteorological conditions are favorable [18]. O3 is known as one of the criteria air pollutants and regulated by the US under the National Ambient Air Quality Standards (NAAQS) [19]. Long-term and short-term exposure to O3 can lead to morbidity and mortality [20]. O3 exposure is associated with premature mortality and can aggravate symptoms of respiratory diseases such as asthma and pneumonia, particularly among individuals with pre-existing conditions [18, 21]. Epidemiological studies have shown an association between O3 exposure and cardiopulmonary mortality [22–24].

Along with health effects, air pollution has resulted in enormous economic effects [25]. Previous studies have found that the cost of treatment, reduced labor productivity, morbidity-related losses (such as life years lost and diminished quality of life), and annual premature deaths due to air pollution can place a serious economic burden on communities [25, 26]. According to World Bank, air pollution has produced economic burden of 2.6 billion $ in terms of mortality and morbidity in Tehran in 2016 [25]. It should be mentioned that Environmental Benefits Mapping and Analysis Program-Community Edition (BenMAP-CE) has recently been utilized to estimate health and associated economic effects of reduction in PM2.5 and O3 pollution in ambient air [27–32].

BenMAP-CE is a GIS-based model developed by the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency to quantify the health and economic impacts of changes in air pollution levels. This model has been widely applied in international studies to estimate mortality and morbidity attributable to ambient air pollutants, including O3. However, despite its broad application worldwide, the use of BenMAP-CE for O3-specific health and economic assessments in Iran, particularly in central regions such as Qom, remains limited [33]. Iran is a developing country confronting air pollution problem due to the fact that many developing countries has sacrificed air pollution for economic progress [34]. In several cities of Iran, air pollution has reached hazardous levels, with the concentrations of pollutants such as PM2.5, PM10, NO₂, and O3 in megacities reported to be up to three times higher than the WHO Air Quality Guidelines [25]. It is necessary to quantify the effects of air pollution and the potential changes due to implementation of mitigation policies [35, 36]. Some studies have been conducted to survey effects of O3 worldwide [37]. Brajer, Hall, and Rahmatian [38] analyzed the health and economic impacts of air pollution in Tehran, one of Iran’s largest megacities. They found that particulate matter and other pollutants significantly increase mortality risk, contributing to an enormous economic burden. The study underscored the challenges posed by Tehran’s geographic and climatic conditions and high vehicular emissions, which limit air quality improvements. Their findings emphasize the urgent need for pollution control policies to reduce health risks and associated economic costs in Iranian urban centers, providing a relevant precedent for mortality and economic burden assessments of pollutant-specific impacts such as ground-level O3 in central Iran [38]. Soleimani et al. [39] evaluated the economic costs stemming from air pollution caused by motor vehicles in Isfahan, a major central Iranian city. Their research, focusing on PM2.5 emission data from 2018 to 2020, quantified the adverse health outcomes and calculated the resultant economic damages. The study demonstrated that motor vehicle emissions are a significant contributor to urban air pollution and impose substantial costs on public health and the economy. These results underline the critical importance of addressing vehicular pollution sources when estimating the economic and mortality burdens attributable to ground-level O3 and other pollutants in Central Iran [39]. Daneshipour, Naghipour, and Kaviani Rad [40] investigated the interactions between air pollution, warming trends, and public health in Tehran during 2015 to 2019. Their analysis revealed strong positive correlations between rising temperatures and elevated levels of O3 and particulate matter, linking climate change factors with increased air pollution exposure. These findings suggest that temperature increases exacerbate ground-level O3 pollution, which in turn can elevate mortality risks and economic burdens. Their study supports the inclusion of climate-related variables in models such as BenMAP CE to accurately assess O3 -related health and economic impacts in Iranian megacities [40].

However, in recent years studies have focused more on PM2.5 rather than O3 [41] due to high concentration of PM2.5 and its association with various adverse health endpoints [42].

Despite extensive research on the health impacts of PM₂.₅ in Iran, quantitative evidence on the short-term mortality and economic burden attributable to ground-level O₃ remains limited, particularly in central Iranian cities. To date, no study has specifically assessed O₃-related cardiovascular and respiratory mortality and its associated economic costs in Qom. Moreover, existing national and regional studies have rarely applied integrated health economic models such as BenMAP-CE to evaluate short term O₃ exposure. This lack of localized, O₃ specific evidence limits the ability of policymakers to design targeted air-quality control strategies. In this context, the present study provides several novel contributions: it represents the first O3 specific BenMAP-CE based health and economic burden assessment in Qom, focuses explicitly on short-term O3-attributable mortality rather than long-term or PM₂.₅ centered outcomes, and integrates locally measured pollutant data with district-level population distribution to generate spatially resolved estimates. Therefore, this study fills an important regional evidence gap and provides the first quantitative estimate of short term O₃ attributable mortality and economic burden in Qom using locally measured O₃ concentrations and the BenMAP-CE model.

Materials and methods

Data collection

Hourly O₃ concentration data were obtained from the Qom Province Environmental Protection Agency for the period April 2019 to March 2020 (Iranian calendar). Iran’s ambient air quality standard for O3 is consistent with the U.S. National Ambient Air Quality Standards (NAAQS), which define a maximum daily 8-h average of 70 ppb (0.07 ppm) [19]. After data cleaning and removal of outliers, missing values were interpolated using a linear method. Interpolation was applied only to short gaps in the hourly O3 time series to maintain data completeness above 75%, a threshold commonly used in air pollution studies. Linear interpolation has been widely applied for ambient O3 data when missing intervals are limited and does not substantially affect short-term exposure estimates. Daily maximum 8-h O₃ concentrations were calculated from the hourly measurements. During the cold season, maximum 8-h O3 levels were generally below the regulatory guideline; however, exceedances of 0.07 ppm were observed during three months of the warm season. Consequently, health and economic impact assessments were restricted to a warm-season trimester, from 13 May to 10 August 2019, corresponding to the warmest period of the year in Qom. The warm-season trimester was selected because ground-level O₃ formation is strongly temperature-dependent and reaches its annual peak during this period in Qom. Including colder months with consistently low concentrations would dilute exposure contrast and underestimate short-term health impacts [48]. Prior to health impact modeling, daily maximum 8-h O₃ concentrations were converted from parts per billion (ppb) to μg/m3 under standard conditions (25 °C and 1 atm) using the ideal gas law. This conversion was performed solely to ensure consistency with epidemiological concentration–response functions, as relative risk (RR) estimates used to calculate the exposure–response coefficient (β) are reported per 10 μg/m3 [49]. The same conversion procedure was applied to the control concentration (0.07 ppm). All unit conversions were completed during data preprocessing, before importing exposure data into BenMAP-CE. β represents the exposure–response coefficient derived from epidemiological analyses describing the relationship between pollutant concentration and health outcomes, while RR denotes the relative risk associated with a given increase in O3 concentration [50, 51]. Baseline incidence rates (BI), defined as the ratio of the number of deaths to the population size over a specified period, for cardiovascular and respiratory mortality attributable to short-term O3 exposure were obtained from published epidemiological studies [19]. Due to the absence of locally derived BI and RR estimates for Qom, values from comparable studies were adopted [49, 52, 53] and are summarized in Table 1. Given that O3 -related health effects are typically estimated using epidemiological coefficients for adults aged 30 years and older, population data were stratified accordingly. Detailed age-specific population data were obtained from the Qom Province Management and Planning Organization (accessed 24 January 2021) and used as model inputs in BenMAP-CE [27, 53]. Additionally, 3-hourly temperature data were obtained from the national meteorological data request system to support interpretation of O3 variability during the study period [30].

The selected RR values correspond to short-term O3 exposure and have been widely applied in previous Iranian and international BenMAP-CE–based assessments. Although locally derived cohort studies for Qom are not available, the adopted coefficients originate from populations with comparable urban air-pollution profiles and climatic conditions. Therefore, these estimates are considered transferable with acceptable uncertainty for short-term impact assessment purposes [54, 55].

Data completeness and interpolation sensitivity

Data completeness was maintained above 75%, a threshold commonly recommended in air quality time series studies. Linear interpolation was applied only to short temporal gaps (< 3 consecutive hours). Sensitivity checks comparing interpolated and non-interpolated days showed negligible minimal differences in average O₃ concentrations, suggesting limited influence on final health estimates [56].

Statistical analysis

Microsoft Excel was used to organize and prepare the O3 data for subsequent analysis. The dataset, which included O3 measurements along with temperature and humidity data, was further analyzed using R software. For this purpose, the ‘openair’ package developed by David Carslaw was applied. This package was used to process the O3 time series data and generate several visualization outputs, including the Calendar plot and Time Variation plot [57, 58] .

Health and economic impact assessment

A health impact assessment and an evaluation of the associated economic costs were conducted to estimate the health benefits of reducing daily maximum 8-h O3 concentrations to 0.07 ppm, corresponding to the U.S. EPA National Ambient Air Quality Standard [22]. The analysis was performed using BenMAP-CE, version 1.5.8.11, a geographic information system (GIS)-based model designed to quantify health and economic impacts attributable to changes in ambient air pollution levels [59]. In BenMAP-CE, the baseline scenario was defined as the observed daily maximum 8-h O₃ concentrations during the warm-season trimester of 2019, while the control scenario was set at a uniform concentration of 0.07 ppm. Concentration changes (ΔQ) between baseline and control scenarios were calculated on a daily basis and expressed in micrograms per cubic meter (µg/m3). Cardiovascular and respiratory mortality were selected as the health endpoints, and analyses were restricted to adults aged 30 years and older, consistent with the epidemiological evidence underlying the concentration–response (C-R) function [60]. Municipality districts were used as the spatial grid units in BenMAP-CE, and locally obtained age stratified population data were imported to replace the default datasets. Short-term O₃ concentration response functions were selected from the built-in BenMAP-CE database without applying custom functions, and a single-study approach was used rather than pooled estimates. Uncertainty was evaluated using the model’s default Monte Carlo simulation based on the 95% confidence intervals of the β coefficients [61].

Population data were stratified by age group and municipality district. Baseline incidence rates (BI) for cardiovascular and respiratory mortality, expressed as cases per 100,000 population per year, were obtained from officially reported mortality statistics and applied as model inputs within the BenMAP-CE framework. Relative risk (RR) estimates and corresponding concentration–response coefficients (β) were derived from published epidemiological studies embedded in the BenMAP-CE database. Health impacts of short-term O3 exposure were estimated using a log-linear concentration–response function, consistent with standard BenMAP-CE methodology, expressed as ΔY = Y₀ × (1—exp(−β × ΔC)) × Pop, where ΔY represents the change in health outcomes, Y₀ is the baseline incidence rate, β is the concentration–response coefficient, ΔC is the change in O3 concentration, and Pop is the exposed population. The β coefficients and their 95% confidence intervals for cardiovascular and respiratory mortality were obtained directly from the BenMAP-CE database. Cardiovascular and respiratory mortality were selected as health endpoints due to their well-established associations with short-term O3 exposure and their widespread use in previous BenMAP-CE based assessments. Hospital admissions and other morbidity endpoints were not included because of limited availability of reliable local incidence data and to reduce uncertainty, as mortality endpoints provide more robust and comparable estimates across studies [62].

Economic valuation of avoidable premature deaths was conducted using the Value of a Statistical Life (VSL) approach, adjusted for Iran based on gross domestic product (GDP) per capita and an income elasticity of 1.2 [63]. Uncertainty analysis was performed using the default BenMAP-CE framework, which incorporates uncertainty in concentration–response coefficients through Monte Carlo simulation based on the 95% confidence intervals of β. Additional sources of uncertainty were not explicitly modeled due to data limitations, consistent with previous BenMAP-CE–based studies.

Two commonly used approaches exist for estimating the economic cost of avoidable mortality: 1) the Value of a Statistical Life (VSL) and 2) the Value of a Life Year (VOLY). The VSL used in this study is based on willingness-to-pay (WTP) estimates derived from country-specific and regional studies, while the VOLY estimate reflects the WTP for gaining one additional year of life expectancy [27, 67] Because these estimates already reflect local income, demographic characteristics, and risk preferences, no additional benefit-transfer adjustment was applied to avoid double-scaling. Because these WTP-based valuations already incorporate local income levels, demographic characteristics, and population risk preferences, applying an additional benefit-transfer adjustment would result in double-scaling and was therefore not conducted. WTP captures both tangible and intangible components of health losses, including medical expenditures, lost leisure, and non-monetary suffering [51]. In order to investigate the economic cost of mortality attributable to O3 in the current study, the simplest approach, which is to multiply the predicted number of deaths by a locally valid estimate of VSL, was utilized. Cardiovascular and respiratory mortality were selected as health endpoints because they represent the most consistently reported and epidemiologically robust outcomes associated with short-term O3 exposure in previous studies and within the BenMAP-CE framework. Baseline incidence rates for each age group were obtained from officially reported mortality statistics used as inputs to the BenMAP-CE model and are consistent with those applied in similar national and international assessments [19].

For clarity and consistency, observed O3 concentrations are reported in ppb (or ppm) throughout the manuscript to facilitate comparison with air quality guidelines. In contrast, concentrations used for health impact assessment within the BenMAP-CE model are expressed in µg/m3, as required by the model. Baseline and control scenario concentrations represent population-weighted means of the daily maximum 8-h O3 concentrations over the study period and were converted from ppb to µg/m3 under standard temperature and pressure conditions.

Economic burden estimation

The total economic burden associated with O₃-attributable premature mortality during the warm season trimester of 2019 was estimated at 43.62 million USD. Cardiovascular mortality accounted for approximately 79% of the total economic loss (34.70 million USD), while respiratory mortality contributed 8.92 million USD (Table 4). This dominance of cardiovascular-related costs reflects both the higher number of ozone-attributable cardiovascular deaths and the larger baseline incidence of cardiovascular mortality in the adult population. The estimated economic burden represents the population’s willingness to pay to reduce the risk of premature death attributable to short-term ozone exposure and encompasses both tangible and intangible losses, including medical expenditures, productivity losses, and reduced quality of life. Although subject to uncertainty, these estimates provide a clear indication of the substantial economic consequences of elevated ozone levels in Qom and underscore the potential economic benefits of effective ozone mitigation strategies.

Discussion

The elevated O3 concentrations observed during the warm-season trimester in Qom reflect meteorological conditions that are highly favorable for photochemical O3 formation, including elevated temperatures and strong solar radiation. As a secondary pollutant, ground-level O3 is formed through complex reactions involving nitrogen oxides (NOx) and volatile organic compounds (VOCs), the emissions and reaction rates of which intensify during warmer months. The notable exceedance of air quality guidelines observed in this study therefore raises important public health concerns for a warm city such as Qom and underscores the need for greater attention and follow-up. Temporal variation analyses provide valuable insights into emission patterns and atmospheric processes influencing O3 formation. Variations across hours of the day and days of the week can reveal information about dominant sources and formation mechanisms, and such approaches have been widely used in air pollution studies [72]. In the present study, O3 concentrations were found to decrease during weekend days (Thursday and Friday), while the highest concentrations occurred on Wednesdays, a pattern that contrasts with findings reported for other Iranian cities [73]. This discrepancy may be explained by increased traffic activity during weekends, leading to higher particulate matter levels that attenuate solar radiation and consequently suppress photochemical O3 production [73]. The health impact assessment based on the BenMAP-CE framework indicates that higher O3 concentrations are associated with a substantial number of premature deaths and considerable economic costs. The underlying principle of BenMAP-CE is that health endpoints associated with higher mortality lead to greater attributable economic benefits when pollutant concentrations are reduced. Accordingly, the estimated reduction in O3 concentration between the baseline and control scenarios would translate into a meaningful decrease in premature mortality and associated economic losses. The consistency of the results obtained using both age-stratified and total population approaches further supports the robustness of the findings. Age-specific analysis suggests that individuals aged 30–39 years experience the greatest potential benefits from O₃ reduction, whereas those aged over 70 years appear to be less affected. These patterns likely reflect differences in population distribution, activity levels, and baseline health status across age groups. The higher number of avoidable deaths observed in the 30–39 age group does not necessarily indicate a higher individual risk compared with older adults; rather, it reflects the combined effects of population size, baseline incidence rates, and exposure patterns within the BenMAP-CE framework. Qom has a relatively young population structure, with the 30–39 age group constituting a substantial proportion of the exposed population. Moreover, working age adults are more likely to engage in outdoor activities and experience traffic-related exposure during peak O3 hours. Therefore, the observed pattern is primarily driven by demographic distribution and exposure frequency rather than biological susceptibility alone [74, 75]. The estimated economic benefit of O3 reduction, expressed in terms of willingness-to-pay (WTP), encompasses both tangible and intangible costs, including medical expenses, lost productivity, suffering, and loss of leisure time. The magnitude of the estimated health and economic burden is consistent with findings from other studies. In Tehran, reductions in O3 concentrations to the EPA standard level were associated with substantial decreases in avoidable all-cause mortality, with stronger associations observed for cardiovascular outcomes [19]. Similarly, a study in China reported increasing O3 -attributable mortality despite declining PM₂.₅ levels, particularly in densely populated regions [76]., In the United States, analyses of short-term O3 exposure in the Dallas-Fort Worth metropolitan area reported considerable economic costs attributable to O3 -related mortality [72]. Additional evidence from Iran indicates that air pollution-related mortality remains a significant public health concern across different exposure scenarios [77]. Despite the strengths of the present analysis, certain limitations should be acknowledged. The lack of access to daily cause-specific mortality data restricted the ability to examine direct short-term associations between O3 fluctuations and specific health outcomes. Nevertheless, this study represents the first localized assessment of short-term O3 -related mortality and economic costs in Qom, central Iran. Overall, the findings of this study indicate that O3 pollution imposes a substantial and preventable health and economic burden in Qom, underscoring the urgent need for targeted mitigation strategies aimed at reducing emissions of O3 precursors. These results provide locally relevant evidence to support air quality management and policy development in warm urban environments. In this study, uncertainty was partly addressed using Monte Carlo simulations in BenMAP-CE, which account only for variability in the concentration–response coefficients (β) derived from relative risk estimates. Other sources of uncertainty such as reliance on non-local relative risk and baseline incidence data, O3 measurement and interpolation methods, and population aggregation at the municipality level were not explicitly quantified. These factors may introduce some uncertainty in exposure and health impact estimates, but they are common in BenMAP-CE assessments conducted in data-limited settings. Despite the strengths of this analysis, several limitations should be noted. The lack of daily cause-specific mortality data restricted the evaluation of short-term associations between O₃ variations and specific health outcomes, and fixed-site monitoring may not fully reflect individual-level exposure. Using non-local baseline incidence and relative risk values may also add uncertainty to age-specific results. Nevertheless, BenMAP-CE offers a robust and widely applied framework for estimating population-level health impacts, and the consistency of results across scenarios supports their reliability. Future studies incorporating high-resolution exposure data and daily mortality records are recommended to improve accuracy.

Conclusions

This study provides a comprehensive assessment of short-term O₃ exposure and its associated health and economic impacts in Qom, a warm and rapidly urbanizing city in central Iran. The findings demonstrate that O₃ concentrations frequently exceed recommended air quality guidelines, resulting in a substantial burden of premature mortality and significant economic losses. Quantitatively, short-term exposure to elevated O₃ levels during the warm-season trimester of 2019 was associated with an estimated 239 premature deaths, including approximately 190 cardiovascular deaths and 49 respiratory deaths, corresponding to a total economic burden of about 43.6 million USD. Cardiovascular mortality accounted for nearly 79% of the total estimated economic loss, highlighting the dominant contribution of cardiovascular outcomes to the overall burden.

Temporal patterns indicate that O₃ formation is strongly influenced by seasonal and diurnal conditions characteristic of warm climates, with higher concentrations occurring during periods favorable to photochemical activity. By applying the BenMAP-CE model, this study offers the first localized, quantitative estimation of O₃-attributable health impacts and their associated economic costs in Qom, thereby filling an important knowledge gap in regional air quality research. The results underscore the importance of targeted, location-specific mitigation strategies aimed at reducing emissions of O₃ precursors, particularly during warm months when population exposure and health risks are greatest.

Overall, the findings suggest that O₃ pollution represents a largely preventable public health concern at the local level in Qom, with clearly measurable health and economic consequences. Although the results are specific to a single city and season, they provide robust quantitative evidence to support locally informed air quality management and health-oriented decision-making. Such evidence-based interventions have the potential to substantially reduce the avoidable mortality and economic burden associated with O₃ exposure in urban areas with similar climatic and environmental conditions, while broader generalization should be approached with caution.
