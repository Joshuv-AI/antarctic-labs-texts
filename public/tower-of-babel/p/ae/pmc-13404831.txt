Scoping review of barriers and facilitators to vasectomy uptake in Pakistan: a call for gender-equitable family planning
Authors: Jawaria Mukhtar Ahmed, Farina G Abrejo, Beenish Majlis Khan, Agha Xaher Gul
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo systematically map and synthesise available evidence on the barriers and facilitators influencing vasectomy uptake in Pakistan and to identify evidence gaps relevant to gender-equitable family planning policy, practice and future research.DesignA scoping review guided by the Joanna Briggs Institute methodology and the Arksey and O’Malley framework and reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR).Data sourcesPubMed, Scopus, CINAHL, Web of Science and PakMediNet were searched, along with grey literature sources including Google Scholar, government and non-governmental reports, policy documents, theses and dissertations. The initial search was conducted in October 2024 and updated on 15 January 2025. The search was not restricted by publication year, allowing a comprehensive review of available evidence on vasectomy in the Pakistani context.Eligibility criteriaEligible sources included peer-reviewed studies, reports, policy documents, theses, dissertations, opinion pieces and commentaries published in English that addressed vasectomy-related barriers, facilitators, attitudes, knowledge, beliefs, practices, services or programmatic factors in Pakistan or among Pakistani populations. Sources were excluded if they were not focused on vasectomy, did not include Pakistan-specific findings, or were not available in full text.Data extraction and synthesisData were charted using a standardised Microsoft Excel extraction tool. Extracted information included source details, study characteristics, population, setting, methods, key findings, barriers, facilitators and conclusions. Findings were synthesised descriptively and thematically using qualitative content analysis. The Socio-Ecological Model was used as a deductive framework to organise findings across intrapersonal, interpersonal, organisational/health system, and policy/programmatic levels.ResultsTwenty-nine sources were included, comprising 21 studies and 8 grey literature sources. The evidence base was methodologically diverse but largely observational and descriptive, with no intervention-based studies evaluating vasectomy promotion strategies in Pakistan. Barriers operated across multiple levels. Individual-level barriers included limited awareness and misconceptions equating vasectomy with castration, impotence, sexual weakness and loss of masculinity. Interpersonal barriers included religious doubts, spousal or family disapproval, peer pressure, stigma and reliance on informal advice. Health system and policy barriers included limited routine service availability, a shortage of trained providers, provider bias, poor follow-up mechanisms and a predominantly female-focused family planning system. Facilitators included economic motivation, concern for women’s health, completed family size, spousal communication, positive testimonials, community-based mobilisation and financial incentive schemes.ConclusionsThe underuse of vasectomy in Pakistan reflects mutually reinforcing sociocultural, interpersonal, health system and policy barriers rather than individual preference alone. Increasing uptake will require culturally appropriate communication, male-friendly counselling and services, trained providers, community engagement and policy reforms that recognise men as legitimate family planning clients. Future research should prioritise locally adapted intervention and implementation studies.Keywords: Scoping Review, Sexual Health, Public Health

Introduction

Global context: The Imperative for Male Engagement in Family Planning

The Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), especially Target 3.7, along with the broader FP2030 partnership, reaffirm the global commitment to ensuring universal access to sexual and reproductive healthcare services, including family planning.1,2 This vision is based on the principles of rights, equity and choice, first endorsed by 179 nations at the 1994 Cairo International Conference on Population and Development’s Programme of Action.3 A key element of this agenda is providing equitable and gender-responsive services that allow individuals and couples to make free, informed and voluntary choices from a wide range of contraceptive options.3 Importantly, reaching these goals requires the meaningful involvement of men, both as supportive partners and as contraceptive users, to challenge deep-seated gender disparities and redistribute the responsibility for fertility management.4

Despite this long-standing consensus, a significant gap persists between policy rhetoric and programmatic reality, especially concerning male contraception. Family planning remains largely synonymous with women, with programmatic efforts and service delivery overwhelmingly focused on female-controlled methods.5,6 This female-centric approach has resulted in a limited range of methods for men. By a female-centric approach, we refer to a programmatic model in which family planning information, counselling, outreach, and service delivery are designed primarily around women as clients, while men are engaged mainly as supportive partners rather than as direct contraceptive users. This places most of the physical, social and service navigation burden of contraception on women and contributes to a narrow male contraceptive method mix, largely limited to condoms and vasectomy. Globally, the only widely available options for male contraception are the male condom and vasectomy (male sterilisation).7 Vasectomy, a safe, highly effective, and permanent procedure, offers a compelling solution for couples who have completed their desired family size. It is a simpler, safer and quicker surgical procedure than its female equivalent, tubal ligation, with a lower risk of complications and a shorter recovery time.8 The World Health Organization’s (WHO’s) medical eligibility criteria confirm that vasectomy is a safe option for nearly all men.9 The procedure’s convenience and efficacy are reflected in its high prevalence in regions with high levels of socioeconomic development and greater gender equality, such as North America and Northern Europe.10

However, global vasectomy prevalence is highly variable and generally low. According to United Nations data, only 20 countries report vasectomy rates above 1%.11 South Korea leads globally at 16.8%, followed by Australia (14.0%), Bhutan (12.6%) and the United States (11.3%).12 Conversely, in 75 of 84 studies low-and middle-income countries (LMICs), prevalence remains below 1% and is less than 0.1% in 56 countries.8 This underutilisation is exacerbated by a notable decline in historically major programmes. China and India, once the largest contributors to global vasectomy totals, have seen a dramatic reduction, accounting for a significant portion of the global decline in users.13,14 This global landscape underscores a pervasive failure to integrate male methods equitably into family planning programmes.

Regional and National Focus: The Case of Pakistan

The situation in Pakistan starkly illustrates the global challenge of low male participation. With a total fertility rate of 3.14 births per woman, Pakistan faces significant demographic and public health challenges.15 According to the Pakistan Demographic and Health Survey 2017–2018, the contraceptive prevalence rate among married women is 34%, with only 25% using a modern method.16 The method mix is heavily skewed towards female-dependent options. Female sterilisation (9%) and male condoms (9%) are the most prevalent modern methods, while vasectomy accounts for a negligible 0.1% of contraceptive use.16 This disparity highlights a profound imbalance in contraceptive responsibility.

The minimal uptake of vasectomy in Pakistan is particularly alarming given that the procedure was introduced programmatically decades ago. The National Program for Family Planning and Primary Healthcare was launched in 1994, yet vasectomy has consistently failed to gain traction.17 This persistent low prevalence points to a complex web of barriers that have not been adequately addressed by the country’s health system or public awareness campaigns. The Pakistani family planning paradigm remains overwhelmingly female-focused, placing the physical and social burden of contraception almost exclusively on women, while offering limited options, information or services for men.

Barriers to Vasectomy Uptake: The Rationale for a Scoping Review

The underutilisation of vasectomy in Pakistan is not a matter of chance but a consequence of multifaceted barriers operating at various levels. Deep-rooted sociocultural norms and religious beliefs often frame fertility and contraception as a female domain, with male virility and masculinity closely tied to fathering children.18 Pervasive misconceptions about vasectomy—such as fears that it leads to sexual dysfunction, loss of physical strength or is religiously prohibited—further deter men from considering the procedure.4,19

At the health system level, significant gaps exist. There is a critical shortage of trained male service providers and dedicated, male-friendly service delivery points. Counselling services are primarily designed for women, with little or no provision for informed consent and counselling for men regarding permanent methods.3 Furthermore, programmatic and policy focus on vasectomy has been limited and inconsistent. Donor funding and government initiatives have predominantly prioritised female methods, reflecting and reinforcing a systemic bias that sidelines male involvement.5,20 This lack of gender-responsive programming contradicts the principles of equity and shared responsibility championed by the SDGs and FP2030.

A clear understanding of the barriers and facilitators to vasectomy in Pakistan is essential for effective intervention. However, existing evidence is fragmented across diverse sources. A systematic consolidation of this knowledge is urgently needed to inform policy and practice.

Methodology

This scoping review was conducted to systematically map and synthesise the available literature on the barriers and facilitators to vasectomy uptake in Pakistan. The methodology was guided by the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Manual for Evidence Synthesis and the framework proposed by Arksey and O’Malley.21,22 The review followed five key stages: (i) identifying the research question; (ii) identifying relevant studies; (iii) study selection; (iv) charting the data and (v) collating, summarising and reporting the results. The protocol was not preregistered; however, the methodology was predefined in accordance with the JBI and Arksey and O'Malley frameworks before the review commenced.

The primary objective of this scoping review is to map the breadth and nature of evidence concerning the low uptake of vasectomy in Pakistan. The following primary question guided the review:

What is known from the existing literature about the barriers to and facilitators of vasectomy uptake among men in Pakistan?

Step 2: Identifying Relevant Literature

A comprehensive search strategy was designed to identify both published and grey literature. An initial limited search was performed in PubMed and Google Scholar to identify relevant keywords and index terms. This informed the development of a full search strategy, which was then executed across five electronic databases: PubMed, Scopus, CINAHL, Web of Science and PakMediNet to ensure regional coverage. The search for grey literature included Google Scholar, government and non-governmental organisation (NGO) reports (eg, from the Ministry of National Health Services, Regulations and Coordination, Pakistan and the Population Council) and theses and dissertations.

The search strategy used a combination of keywords and Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms related to three concepts: (i) vasectomy, (ii) barriers and facilitators and (iii) Pakistan. Search terms included: "vasectomy", "male sterilisation", "male contraception", "family planning", "barriers", "facilitators", "acceptance", "utilization”, and "Pakistan". The initial search was conducted in October 2024 and updated on 15 January 2025. The search was not restricted by publication year or study design, allowing for a comprehensive review of all available evidence on vasectomy in the Pakistani context. English-language eligibility was applied during screening. The complete search strategies for PubMed, Scopus, CINAHL, Web of Science, PakMediNet and grey literature sources are provided in online supplemental file 1, including search terms, Boolean operators, filters, limits, dates searched and number of records retrieved.

Step 3: Study Selection

Following the search, all identified records were collated and uploaded to EndNote reference management software, and duplicates were removed. The study selection process was conducted according to the JBI scoping review methodology and the Arksey and O’Malley framework and is reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) checklist.23 Titles and abstracts were independently screened by two reviewers against the inclusion criteria. Potentially relevant sources were retrieved in full text and assessed in detail against the criteria by two independent reviewers. Any disagreements between the reviewers were resolved through discussion or with the assistance of a third reviewer.

Data from included sources were extracted and charted using a standardised data extraction tool developed in Microsoft Excel. The tool was piloted on a sample of studies and refined iteratively. Step 5: Data Synthesis and Thematic Mapping of Results

The extracted data were analysed to map the evidence in line with the scoping review questions. First, to answer Question 1, the types of evidence (eg, qualitative, quantitative) were compiled and summarised descriptively. For Questions 2 and 3, findings were synthesised thematically using qualitative content analysis and organised using the SEM framework. The SEM was used as a deductive coding framework to classify extracted findings according to the level at which each factor primarily operated. Intrapersonal-level codes captured individual knowledge, beliefs, perceived risks and benefits, misconceptions, fertility desires and personal motivations. Interpersonal-level codes captured spousal communication, family influence, peer pressure, social stigma, religious influence and community norms. Organisational and health-system-level codes captured service availability, provider attitudes, counselling quality, follow-up care, privacy, trained workforce and referral pathways. Policy and programmatic-level codes captured national strategy, financing, male inclusion in family planning programming, incentive schemes and monitoring indicators. Within each SEM level, related codes were compared and grouped inductively into themes and subthemes. When a finding operated across more than one SEM level, it was assigned to the level at which the main mechanism was described, while cross-level interactions were considered during interpretation. This approach enabled a systematic overview of the complex, multilevel factors influencing vasectomy uptake.24 Key themes, patterns and gaps in the literature were identified and are discussed in the results. Types and Scope of Available Evidence

The evidence on vasectomy in Pakistan is limited but methodologically diverse, comprising 21 peer-reviewed studies and 8 grey literature sources. Together, these sources provide a multifaceted but incomplete picture of barriers and facilitators to vasectomy uptake.

Methodological Distribution

The 21 studies comprised 10 qualitative studies exploring sociocultural norms and decision-making contexts; 8 quantitative studies reporting prevalence rates and demographic correlations, such as 47% awareness compared with 0.3% uptake, and 3 mixed-methods studies combining qualitative and quantitative approaches through triangulation.

Grey Literature

Technical reports from international organisations, including UNFPA, and WHO, as well as conference papers and books provided important programmatic context, policy analyses and field-based insights that were not captured in academic journals.

Critical Evidence Gap

All identified studies were observational and descriptive. No experimental or intervention-based studies evaluating strategies to promote vasectomy in Pakistan were identified. This highlights a critical need for research that tests potential solutions rather than solely describing the problem.

Documented Barriers to Vasectomy Uptake

Barriers to vasectomy uptake were identified across all levels of the Socio-Ecological Model (SEM) and were mutually reinforcing. Individual-level barriers included pervasive myths, limited awareness, misinformation and fertility desires. Interpersonal barriers included religious and cultural stigma, spousal or family disapproval, negative peer influence, and reliance on informal advice. Health-system and policy barriers included limited service availability, provider bias, weak follow-up mechanisms, shortages of trained providers, and female-focused programming. Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first scoping review to systematically map evidence on the barriers and facilitators influencing vasectomy uptake in Pakistan. It synthesises findings from 29 sources to address three key questions concerning the nature of the available evidence, documented barriers, and facilitators and promotional strategies. The findings reveal a complex interaction of factors across socio-ecological levels that collectively contribute to the profound underutilisation of vasectomy, while also identifying potential pathways for intervention.

Summary of Evidence and Critical Gaps

This scoping review provides the first systematic mapping of evidence on vasectomy in Pakistan. Our synthesis of 29 sources reveals a complex, multilevel system that drives its profound underutilisation, while also highlighting potentially actionable pathways for promotion.

The existing literature, although foundational, is primarily descriptive and includes substantial grey literature. It largely explains the ‘what’ and ‘why’ of low vasectomy uptake. A critical gap is the absence of intervention studies conducted in Pakistan. Although the barriers are well-documented, evidence concerning ‘what works’ is largely extrapolated from other LMICs, such as Iran and Rwanda25. This highlights an urgent need for locally conducted implementation and intervention research.

International evidence was used as a contextual comparison rather than as a substitute for Pakistan-specific findings. These comparisons are relevant because several LMIC settings share programmatic and socio-cultural characteristics that influence vasectomy uptake. These include low male sterilisation prevalence, gendered allocation of contraceptive responsibility, stigma surrounding male sterilisation, limited male-friendly services, and the influence of community, peer and religious norms on reproductive decision-making.

However, the transferability of international findings to Pakistan cannot be assumed. Differences in religious interpretation, fertility norms, provincial health governance, service organisation, and family planning history mean that international findings should be viewed as indications of plausible mechanisms and potential intervention models that require local adaptation and evaluation.

Multilevel Barriers: A System of Disincentives

Barriers to vasectomy uptake in Pakistan are deeply entrenched and mutually reinforcing across socio-ecological levels. At the individual and interpersonal levels, the findings are consistent with evidence from other LMICs, including Ghana, Ethiopia, and Uganda,26–28 where pervasive myths equate vasectomy with castration, impotence and loss of masculinity.6,29 These misconceptions are compounded by religious doubts and a cultural preference for large families linked to male identity.30

A severe lack of accurate information from formal sources forces many men to rely on misinformation circulated through informal networks, a pattern also observed in India.31 In Pakistan, this problem may be exacerbated further by limited open communication between spouses regarding contraception.

At the health system level, structural barriers are substantial. Female-focused programme design systematically excludes men as direct clients, a pattern observed in Nigeria32. This is compounded by limited service access, a critical shortage of trained providers, and reliance on temporary service-delivery camps. Provider-level barriers, including bias, negative attitudes, inadequate attention to male clients, and poor follow-up, have also been documented in Tanzania.25,33 These factors further erode trust in services. Without addressing such systemic failures, educational campaigns alone are unlikely to be sufficient.

Facilitators and Pathways for Promotion

Despite these challenges, the review identified several key facilitators and promising strategies that may inform future efforts to promote vasectomy.

At the client and interpersonal levels, economic motivation and concern for a spouse’s health may support acceptance, particularly among men who have completed their desired family size—a pattern also reported in studies from the United States.34,35 Open spousal communication and mutual decision-making may also improve satisfaction and acceptance.36

At the organisational and health-system levels, several strategic appproaches appear promising. Financial incentive schemes may encourage uptake when they are designed and implemented ethically.35 Community-based mobilisation through multifaceted programmes, such as Pakistan’s FALAH project, and initiatives undertaken in Iran and Rwanda may also support improvements in awareness and acceptance25. These approaches engage male mobilisers, religious leaders, community groups, and mass media, and may be particularly valuable for addressing prevailing social norms.

However, because these strategies have not been evaluated through intervention studies in Pakistan, their effectiveness in the local context remains uncertain.

Limitations of the Review

This scoping review has several limitations. The search was restricted to English-language sources focusing on Pakistan, potentially excluding relevant literature published in local languages and introducing language bias. Consistent with scoping review methodology, no formal quality assessment of the included sources was conducted. Therefore, the review maps the breadth and nature of the available evidence rather than evaluating its methodological rigour. The evidence base was small and predominantly descriptive, and several included sources addressed family planning broadly rather than vasectomy specifically. Consequently, although the review identifies important barriers and facilitators, the absence of intervention studies means that the question of “what works” in Pakistan remains unanswered.

Policy Implications and the Way Forward

Current family planning policy and programming in Pakistan frequently frame men as supporters of women’s contraceptive use rather than as clients in their own right. This contributes to the systemic neglect of vasectomy. A fundamental policy shift is therefore needed to recognise men as legitimate and independent family planning clients.

This shift requires concrete action, including revising national and provincial strategies to allocate dedicated funding for male-friendly services, training healthcare providers in male counselling and vasectomy procedures, and integrating male contraceptive methods into essential health-service packages.

Monitoring frameworks should also be updated to include indicators that measure male-method uptake, informed choice, service quality, follow-up, and client satisfaction.

Closing the policy–practice gap will require coordinated, multisectoral action that combines high-level political commitment with community-level strategies, including mobilising male health workers, community leaders, and religious leaders. Ultimately, achieving gender-equitable family planning and national development goals depends on empowering men to participate as informed and equal partners in reproductive health.

Conclusion

This review demonstrates that the underutilisation of vasectomy in Pakistan is not attributable to individual choice alone. Instead, it reflects deeply rooted socio-cultural, interpersonal, health-system, and policy barriers. These include widespread misconceptions, restrictive gender norms, limited service availability, provider bias, and a healthcare system that remains predominantly focused on women as family planning clients.

Nevertheless, identified facilitators, including economic considerations, concern for women’s health, completed family size, spousal support, positive testimonials, community mobilisation, and improved service availability, provide a foundation for future interventions.

A coordinated effort is required to address these interconnected barriers through culturally appropriate communication, inclusive and male-friendly health services, trained providers, community engagement, and decisive policy reform. Reframing vasectomy as a safe, responsible, and gender-equitable family planning option will be essential for advancing reproductive health and achieving national family planning objectives.
