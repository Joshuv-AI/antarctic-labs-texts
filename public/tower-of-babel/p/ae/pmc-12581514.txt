HROB is a novel prognostic biomarker correlated with immune cell infiltration and tumor progression in lung adenocarcinoma
Authors: Yongjie Zhu, Chaoqiang Wu, Qiwei Li, Yingxue Hu, Yue Li, Zuotao Wu, Honglin Lu, Ye jun Chen, Bo Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) is characterized by poor prognosis and limited treatment options. The homologous recombination factor with OB-fold (HROB) is reportedly associated with DNA repair, playing an essential role in a variety of cancers. However, the functional impact, clinical relevance, and underlying mechanisms of HROB in LUAD remain elusive, prompting this study to comprehensively characterize its expression profile and pathogenetic contributions.MethodsWe first systematically profiled HROB expression across pan-cancer contexts and multiple cell lines. To characterize its clinical relevance in LUAD, we employed receiver operator characteristic (ROC) curves, Kaplan–Meier survival analyses, and univariate and multivariate Cox regression modeling. Potential molecular mechanisms underlying HROB function were interrogated enrichment analyses and immune correlation studies. Using RT-PCR, we quantified HROB expression in different LUAD cell lines, followed by functional characterization through Cell Counting Kit-8(CCK-8), 5-Ethynyl-2’-deoxyuridine (EdU), wound-healing, and Transwell assays.ResultsHROB exhibited significant overexpression in LUAD tissues compared to normal, with high diagnostic accuracy. Elevated HROB expression was strongly associated with adverse clinical outcomes. Bioinformatics analysis revealed HROB’s involvement in modulating immune cell infiltration, regulating cell cycle progression and DNA repair pathways. Besides, BUB1B, BUB1, NUF2, CCNB2, CCNB1, KIF11, KIF2C, TPX2, DLGAP5, and CDCA8 were identified as potential hub genes within HROB-associated signaling modules. Knocking down HROB expression inhibited LUAD cell proliferation, migration, and invasion.ConclusionsHROB is upregulated in LUAD and represents a promising prognostic biomarker, driving tumor proliferation, migration, and invasion.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12957-025-04070-z.Keywords: HROB, Lung adenocarcinoma, Biomarker, Tumor microenvironment, Prognosis

Introduction

Lung cancer remains a formidable global health challenge as the most commonly diagnosed cancer and the leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide [1]. Non-small cell lung cancer, accounting for approximately 80%–85% of lung cancer cases, is characterized by genetic and cellular heterogeneity [2, 3]. Among non-small cell lung cancer subtypes, lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) is the most prevalent [3]. Despite advancements in therapeutic strategies, LUAD prognosis remains poor, with a five-year survival rate of < 15% [4]. This poor outcome is largely attributed to late-stage diagnoses and inherent resistance to conventional chemotherapy and radiotherapy [5]. The advent of targeted therapies has transformed treatment paradigms by focusing on specific molecular phenotypes, thereby improving outcomes for certain patients [6]. However, therapeutic resistance continues to be a major challenge [7]. Therefore, identifying novel biomarkers and therapeutic targets is crucial for improving survival rates and patient quality of life.

Homologous recombination factor with OB-fold (HROB) is a protein-coding gene on chromosome 17q21.3. It is predicted to enable single-stranded DNA binding activity [8]. Recent studies have highlighted the pivotal role of HROB in regulating homologous recombination and DNA repair. In particular, HROB interacts with the MCM8–MCM9 complex to facilitate DNA unwinding and repair synthesis at damage sites [9]. Notably, a deficiency in HROB results in significant meiotic defects, increased sensitivity to interstrand crosslinks, and impaired DNA recombination, underscoring its essential upstream function [10–12]. Furthermore, HROB is crucial for the proper localization of MCM8 at DNA repair foci, and its depletion impedes DNA replication fork progression and cellular proliferation [11]. Surprisingly, HROB has been implicated in chemoresistance, particularly to cisplatin and other cancer therapies, suggesting a potential oncogenic function in promoting cell survival under therapeutic stress [13]. Despite these findings, the role of HROB in LUAD remains underexplored. Considering its involvement in DNA repair and cell cycle regulation, further investigations into the function of HROB in LUAD may reveal its potential as a therapeutic target and its role in tumor progression and resistance mechanisms, thereby informing new strategies for improving LUAD diagnosis and treatment.

Herein we aimed to elucidate the expression level, clinical relevance, and underlying mechanisms of HROB across various human neoplasms, focusing on LUAD. We analyzed samples from public datasets to obtain a comprehensive overview of the expression of HROB and its clinical significance. Further, we explored the effects of HROB on the tumor microenvironment (TME), immune cell infiltration, and drug sensitivity in LUAD. To investigate its role in cell proliferation, migration, and invasion, small-interfering RNA (siRNA) was used to silence HROB in lung cancer cell lines, and a series of cellular assays were performed.

Materials and methods

Public data acquisition and data processing

Cancer Dependency Map (DepMap, https://depmap.org/portal/) is a publicly accessible resource that consolidates data from extensive multiomics screening initiatives, including gene essentiality information derived from large-scale loss-of-function screens employing CRISPR interference or RNA interference [14]. To assess the essentiality of HROB across diverse cancer cell lines, we utilized Chronos scores from DepMap. Chronos scores are a computational measure of gene essentiality, where a score of 0 signifies that HROB is non-essential for the respective cancer cell line; negative scores indicate increasing essentiality, with lower scores reflecting a higher dependence on HROB for cell survival [15]. We downloaded the 23Q4 version of CRISPR data for this analysis (Supplementary Table).

Pan-cancer expression, patient prognosis, and mutation annotation format (MAF) data were sourced from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA, https://www.portal.gdc.cancer.gov/) through UCSC Xena (http://xena.ucsc.edu/). HROB expression data for 33 cancer types were extracted and processed using a log2(x + 1) transformation to mitigate data skewness, approximating a normal distribution for subsequent statistical analyses. To further validate HROB expression patterns in pan-cancer research, nine LUAD datasets were retrieved from Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) and supplemented with additional RNA-seq data from normal lung tissues in the Genotype-Tissue Expression (https://gtexportal.org) database.

HROB mRNA expression and clinical significance analysis

We evaluated HROB expression in pan-carcer and multiple LUAD-GEO datasets. The ability of HROB expression to distinguish between tumor and control tissues was determined using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves based on sensitivity, specificity, and area under the curve (AUC) values, as well as summary ROC (sROC) curves.

Further, we analyzed the association between HROB expression and the survival outcomes of patients with LUAD by establishing Kaplan–Meier curves and performing univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses. Clinical indicators and prognostic information were obtained from UCSC Xena. The GSE30219 and Gene Expression Profiling Interactive Analysis [16] (GEPIA; http://gepia.cancer-pku.cn/) datasets were used to assess the association between the expression of HROB and prognosis of patients with LUAD.

Relationship between HROB expression and LUAD TME

The TME of malignant solid tumors includes not only tumor cells but also a variety of other cell types, such as stromal cells, fibroblasts, and immune cells [17, 18]. These diverse cellular components collectively form the TME, playing critical roles in tumor growth, disease progression, and drug resistance [19].

We utilized the ESTIMATE algorithm, which leverages mRNA expression profiling of tumors to assess stromal and immune cell content, for calculating ImmuneScore, StromalScore, and ESTIMATEScore [20]. In addition, we employed the single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) algorithm, based on the R package GSVA [21], to evaluate the abundance of 24 tumor-infiltrating immune cells in LUAD. Tumor Immune Estimation Resource (TIMER; https://cistrome.shinyapps.io/timer/) [22] was used to evaluate the relationship between HROB copy number variation and immune infiltration levels.

Analysis of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) and gene set enrichment analysis

Expression profiles were analyzed between cohorts with high and low HROB expression, stratified by the median, to identify DEGs using the DESeq2 [23] package. Significant differences were defined by absolute log2(fold change) >1 and adjusted P < 0.05. The “Most Similar Genes” module of the GEPIA dataset was utilized to identify the top 101 genes with expression patterns similar to those of HROB (including HROB itself). These genes were intersected with upregulated DEGs (up-DEGs). Subsequently, these intersected genes were subjected to gene ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses using ClusterProfiler [24] to elucidate potential signaling pathways involving HROB in LUAD.

Diagnostic value and survival analysis of hub genes

As mentioned earlier, genes with expression patterns most similar to those of HROB in LUAD were identified. STRING [25] (https://string-db.org/) was employed to construct a protein–protein interaction (PPI) network for the intersected genes, setting the minimum required interaction score to “high confidence (0.700)” while leaving other parameters at their default values. To identify key nodes and subnetworks within the PPI network, we used the Maximal Clique Centrality (MCC) algorithm within the CytoHubba plugin (v0.1) in Cytoscape [26, 27] (v3.9.1). Finally, 10 hub genes were identified within the PPI network. Subsequently, we comprehensively investigated the diagnostic and prognostic significance of these hub genes.We validated the protein expression levels of HROB and hub genes using the Human Protein Atlas [28] (HPA, https://www.proteinatlas.org/) database.

Cell culture

The LUAD cell lines A549 and H1299 were procured from the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). Cells were cultured under standard conditions at 37 °C with 5% CO₂. A549 cells were maintained in T25 culture flasks with DMEM (Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin mixture (Beyotime, China). H1299 cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (Gibco) under identical conditions.

Cell transfection

Si-RNAs targeting HROB and negative control (NC) were synthesized by Genesis Biotechnology Ltd (Nanning, China). A549 and H1299 cells were seeded into six-well plates and transfected with Lipo8000 (Beyotime, China) on reaching approximately 70% confluence, as per manufacturer instructions. Subsequent experiments were performed 48 h post-transfection. SiRNA sequences were as follows: si-HROB: 5’-GCCUGAACAAGACGAAUUUGA-3’ and si-NC: 5’-UUCUCCGAACGUGUCACGU-3’.

CCK-8 assay

A549 and H1299 cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 4,000 cells per well and incubated for 24 h to facilitate attachment. At 24, 48, and 72 h post-seeding, 10 µL CCK-8 reagent (Dojindo Laboratories, Inc.) was added to each well, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 2 h. After each incubation period, absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Inc.).

EdU assay

Forty-eight hours post-transfection, cells were harvested from the six-well plates. The culture medium was replaced with freshly prepared EdU solution (Beyotime, China), as recommended by the manufacturer, followed by incubation for 1.5 h at 37 °C to facilitate EdU incorporation. Subsequently, cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min at room temperature. They were then washed thrice with PBS, with each wash lasting 5 min. Cells were permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100 in PBS for 10 min at room temperature. Following permeabilization, they were incubated with the EdU reaction cocktail in the dark for 20 min, according to manufacturer instructions. Cell nuclei were stained with Hoechst 33,342 for 10 min to visualize DNA, and excess dye was removed by washing with PBS. Finally, stained cells were imaged using a fluorescence microscope equipped with appropriate filters for EdU and Hoechst.

Wound healing assay

A549 and H1299 cells were seeded into six-well plates and subjected to transfection. A sterile 200 µL pipette tip was used to create a precise vertical scratch on the cell monolayer. The wells were then washed thrice with PBS to eliminate any detached cells and debris. Initial images of the wound area were obtained using a light microscope (Nikon Corporation). To induce starvation, cells were incubated in serum-free media. The same wound area was re-imaged at 24 h post-wounding to evaluate the extent of wound closure.

Transwell invasion and migration assays

Migration and invasion assays were performed using Transwell chambers with 8 μm pore size (LabSelect; Beijing Lanjieke Technology Co., Ltd.). Cells were harvested 48 h post-transfection and resuspended in serum-free media (2 × 10⁵ cells/mL). For the invasion assay, the upper chamber of the Transwell was coated with diluted Matrigel (Biosciences, USA), followed by incubation at 37 °C for 2 h to allow gel solidification. Subsequently, 250 µL of cell suspension was added to the upper chamber, and 700 µL of 10% fetal bovine serum-containing medium was added to the lower chamber. The chambers were then incubated at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ atmosphere for 24 h. After incubation, non-migrated or non-invaded cells on the upper surface of the membrane were gently removed using PBS-moistened cotton swabs. Cells that had migrated to or invaded the lower surface were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min, rinsed with PBS, stained with 0.1% crystal violet (Beyotime, China) for 15 min, and then washed with PBS to remove excess stain. These stained cells were observed and counted under a light microscope (Nikon Corporation) from several random fields.

Immunotherapy response and drug sensitivity analyses

In this study, we comprehensively analyzed and visualized MAF data. We examined variations in tumor mutational burden (TMB) and immune checkpoint molecule expression between cohorts with high and low HROB expression. Furthermore, we utilized half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) data from the Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer database (https://www.cancerrxgene.org/) and employed the OncoPredict package to assess the potential efficacy of chemotherapy and targeted therapies in LUAD cases.

Statistical analysis

The Wilcoxon rank-sum test was employed to compare gene expression differences between the cancer and control groups. In cellular and molecular biology experiments, data from at least three independent replicates were analyzed. For comparisons involving two groups across multiple time points or doses, a two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed followed by Sidak’s post-hoc test for multiple comparisons. For comparisons of two groups under a single condition, a t-test was used. All these analyses were conducted in GraphPad Prism. The “meta” package in Stata was utilized to evaluate HROB expression levels between the LUAD and non-LUAD groups using standardized mean difference (SMD). Clinically significant results were indicated when 95% CI for SMD did not include 0, suggesting upregulated HROB expression in the LUAD group but not in the control group. Publication bias was assessed using Begg’s test, with P < 0.1 indicating significant bias. Unless otherwise stated, P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All correlation analyses were performed using Spearman’s correlation test. sROC curves were generated in Stata.

Results

Analysis of HROB expression and diagnostic performance in pan-cancer

HROB was essential for multiple malignancies, including lung cancer (Fig. 1A). Notably, compared to normal tissues, HROB mRNA was overexpressed in tumor tissues in 18 of 33 cancer types in TCGA database (Fig. 1B). ROC curve analysis revealed that in 23 of 24 cancer types, the AUC value was > 0.7, highlighting the effectiveness of HROB in differentiating between cancer and normal tissues in pan-cancer (Fig. 1C). sROC analysis further corroborated the robust diagnostic performance of HROB (sensitivity = 0.86, specificity = 0.92, AUC = 0.95) (Fig. 1D).

Analysis of HROB expression and diagnostic performance in LUAD

Using 10 datasets, we found that HROB mRNA was significantly overexpressed in LUAD (Fig. 2A). Integrating expression levels across all datasets, LUAD samples exhibited substantially higher HROB mRNA levels than non-cancer samples (SMD = 1.09; 95% CI [0.81–1.37]). Due to high heterogeneity (I2 = 80.3%), a random-effects model was used for meta-analysis (Fig. 2B). ROC curves showed HROB expression effectively discriminated LUAD from normal tissues (Table 1), with summary ROC (sROC) analysis demonstrating accurate distinction between LUAD and non-LUAD samples (AUC = 0.85) (Fig. 2C). Besides, dataset quality assessment via Begg’s test indicated no significant publication bias (P > 0.1) (Fig. 2D).

Prognostic significance of HROB in LUAD

We examined the associations between HROB expression, prognosis, and various clinical characteristics. In TCGA-LUAD cohort, univariate (Fig. 3A) and multivariate (Fig. 3B) Cox regression analyses revealed HROB expression as an independent predictor of adverse prognosis (HR > 1; P < 0.05). Kaplan–Meier survival analysis revealed a significant association between high HROB expression and poorer overall survival and disease-free survival in patients with LUAD (Fig. 3C-D). Furthermore, increased HROB expression in patients with LUAD was linked to advanced clinical T stage (Fig. 3E), lymph node metastasis (Fig. 3F), and more advanced pathological stage (Fig. 3G). These findings were validated through analyses of the GSE30219 (Fig. 3H–L) and GEPIA databases (Fig. 3M-N).

Role of HROB in immune evasion in LUAD

Using the ESTIMATE algorithm, we found that LUAD patients with low HROB expression exhibited significantly higher StromalScore, ESTIMATEScore, and ImmuneScore (Fig. 4A), indicative of more stromal and immune components within the TME. ssGSEA revealed that HROB expression was positively correlated with certain immune regulatory cells, such as T helper 2 cells (Th2 cells, R = 0.626, P < 0.05) and CD56dim natural killer cells (R = 0.225, P < 0.05) (Fig. 4B). On the contrary, HROB expression was negatively correlated with several key antitumor immune cells, including Eosinophils (R = − 0.356, P < 0.05), immature dendritic cells (R = − 0.390, P < 0.001) and CD8 + T cells (R = − 0.167, P < 0.05). Analysis of the TIMER database indicated that the arm-level deletion of HROB significantly reduced the infiltration of B cells, CD4 + T cells, macrophages, neutrophils, and dendritic cells (Fig. 4C). These findings highlight the complex role of HROB in modulating the immune landscape in LUAD, suggesting its significance in regulating immune response and tumor progression.

Potential signaling pathways and hub gene identification

To elucidate the potential biological functions of HROB in LUAD progression, we first performed a differential expression analysis between high- and low-HROB expression groups within the TCGA-LUAD cohort. This analysis identified 954 upregulated and 799 downregulated genes (Fig. 5A). Utilizing the GEPIA2 platform, we selected the top 101 HROB-related genes (including HROB). By intersecting the upregulated DEGs from the high-vs-low HROB comparison with genes obtained from the GEPIA2 database, we identified 65 co-expressed genes closely related to HROB (Fig. 5B). Enrichment analysis of these co-expressed genes revealed potential molecular mechanisms of HROB in LUAD (Fig. 5D).

GO enrichment analysis indicated that the biological functions of HROB were closely related to cell division and cell cycle regulation, encompassing terms such as “organelle fission,” “nuclear division,” “mitotic nuclear division” and “microtubule binding”.

KEGG pathway enrichment analysis revealed the involvement of HROB in several key signaling pathways, including “cell cycle,” “microRNAs in cancer” and “p53 signaling pathway”. These results indicated that HROB has significant functions in cell growth, development, DNA repair, and cancer progression (Fig. 5E). Through the PPI network created with the MCC algorithm, we identified 10 hub genes most likely to interact with HROB, collectively promoting LUAD development (Fig. 5C).

Comprehensive analysis of hub genes

We observed a significant upregulation of mRNA expression of the hub genes in tumor tissues compared to normal tissues (Fig. 6A). To evaluate the diagnostic potential of 10 hub genes, we generated diagnostic ROC curves. The ROC curves for each hub gene had an AUC value of > 0.9, indicating their strong diagnostic utility for LUAD (Fig. 6B). Furthermore, cox regression analysis identified these hub genes as risk factors for LUAD (Fig. 6C), and Kaplan–Meier survival analysis indicated their association with poor prognosis (Fig. 6D). Immunohistochemistry analysis based on the HPA database showed that tumor tissues exhibited higher staining intensity than normal tissues, confirming the overexpression of HROB, BUB1B, CCNB2, CCNB1, KIF11, KIF2C, TPX2, DLGAP5, and CDCA8 at the protein level in LUAD (Fig. 7).

HROB knockdown inhibited LUAD cell proliferation, migration, and invasion

Furthermore, to assess the functional roles of HROB in LUAD cell proliferation, migration, and invasion, we performed a series of cellular assays. RT-qPCR analysis confirmed successful knockdown, showing a marked decrease in HROB mRNA expression in both A549 and H1299 cells following si-HROB transfection (Fig. 8A). CCK-8 assay results indicated a notable, time-dependent decline in cell absorbance in the si-HROB group, suggesting that HROB inhibition hampered cell proliferation (Fig. 8B). EdU assay results supported this finding, which showed a significantly lower percentage of EdU-positive cells in the HROB-silenced group compared to that in the NC group (Fig. 8C).

HROB promotes LUAD cell proliferation, migration, and invasion. A HROB mRNA expression in A549 and H1299 cells after si-HROB transfection. B Cell proliferative capacity assessed by CCK-8 and (C) EdU assays after HROB knockdown. D Reduced wound closure (%) after si-HROB transfection. E Decreased LUAD cell migration and invasion after si-HROB transfection

Moreover, wound healing assay results demonstrated that compared to si-NC, si-HROB significantly decreased the wound closure rate in LUAD cells (Fig. 8D). Transwell assay findings demonstrated that HROB knockdown substantially reduced the number of invasive and migratory A549 and H1299 cells. These data indicated that HROB knockdown profoundly inhibited LUAD cell migration and invasion (Fig. 8E).

Immune response and drug potential of HROB in LUAD

TMB is intricately linked to the frequency of mutations in tumor cells [29]. Elevated TMB levels may lead to neoantigen generation, thereby activating more immune cells for recognition, which may be associated with the efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitors [30]. Figure 9A depicts the gene mutation profile for the high and low HROB expression groups. Analysis of the mutation profile revealed a distinct pattern between the groups. The TP53 mutation rate was significantly higher in the high-HROB group than in the low-HROB group (66% vs. 32%; Fig. 9A). Regarding MUC16, it was the most frequently mutated gene within the low-HROB group (34%), although its mutation rate was numerically higher in the high-HROB group (45%). Furthermore, the high HROB expression group exhibited increased TMB (Fig. 9B) and higher expression levels of immune checkpoint molecules (Fig. 9C).

Relationship between HROB and immunotherapy, targeted therapy, and chemotherapy in LUAD. A Gene mutation profiles, (B) tumor mutational burden (TMB), (C) immune checkpoint expression levels, and (D) sensitivity to first-line targeted therapy and chemotherapy between the high and low HROB expression groups

The drug sensitivity analysis showed that high HROB expression group exhibited lower IC50 than the low HROB expression group as for cisplatin, paclitaxel, docetaxel, afatinib, erlotinib, gefitinib, and osimertinib (Fig. 9D).

Discussion

As a leading cause of death worldwide, cancer critically threatens human health. Thus, investigating novel biomarkers to characterize disease status and predict prognosis in cancer patients is highly significant. Tumor cells typically exhibit high instability and rapid proliferation [31], necessitating efficient DNA repair mechanisms to sustain their survival and proliferative capacity [32, 33]. Previous studies have established the crucial role of HROB in homologous recombination and DNA repair processes [9, 11]. Additionally, Liu X et al. reported pan-cancer overexpression of HROB and proposed its potential as a promising diagnostic biomarker across multiple tumor types [34]. But this hypothesis remains experimentally unvalidated. To our knowledge, this study provides the first systematic functional characterization of HROB in LUAD through integrated experimental approaches.

In this study, we performed a comprehensive analysis using large-scale data from multicenter public databases. We found that HROB exhibits strong potential as a diagnostic biomarker for LUAD. Critically, upregulated HROB expression was significantly associated with poor clinical outcomes, establishing its prognostic value. These findings indicate that HROB may be a promising clinical biomarker for LUAD.

TME is a critical determinant of tumor progression and therapy response [35]. Patients with high HROB expression exhibited significantly lower StromalScore, ImmuneScore and ESTIMATEScore compared to those with low HROB expression, suggesting reduced stromal/immune constituents in their TME. This implicates HROB in promoting depletion of microenvironment components, may prevent stromal and immune cells from populating the tumor microenvironment, effectively starving the tumor of anti-tumor immunity. It was substantiated by ssGSEA. High HROB expression was negatively correlated with several key antitumor immune cells, including CD8 + T cells, immature dendritic cells, Th1 cells, B cells, and macrophages, which are crucial in orchestrating the antitumor immune response. For instance, CD8 + T cells, which identify and bind to antigens presented by major histocompatibility complex class I molecules on tumor cells, release perforin and granzymes to induce tumor cell apoptosis [36, 37]. Immature dendritic cells are essential for antigen uptake and presentation, initiating and regulating immune responses by presenting antigens to T cells [38].Th1 cells secrete interferon-gamma to activate macrophages and enhance cell-mediated immune responses [39]. B cells produce specific antibodies and present antigens to mediate tumor cell clearance [40], while macrophages phagocytose pathogens and apoptotic cells and regulate immune responses through cytokine secretion [41].

Further supporting the vital role of HROB in regulating immune cell infiltration, TIMER database analysis indicated that the arm-level deletion of HROB significantly reduced the infiltration of multiple immune cells, including B cells, CD4 + T cells, macrophages, neutrophils, and dendritic cells, indicating that HROB is essential for the recruitment and maintenance of immune cells within the TME.

Having established HROB’s significant association with an immunosuppressive TME, we then shifted our focus to elucidate its intrinsic molecular functions and signaling pathways that might underpin its oncogenic role in LUAD. GO enrichment analysis revealed that the biological functions of HROB were predominantly associated with cell division and cell cycle regulation, encompassing processes such as “organelle fission,” “nuclear division,” and “mitotic nuclear division.” These functions are essential for normal cell division and proliferation, with aberrant HROB expression potentially leading to cell cycle dysregulation, thus facilitating tumorigenesis and progression. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis highlighted the involvement of HROB in diverse critical pathways, including “cell cycle,” “homologous recombination,” and “p53 signaling pathway.” The role of HROB in cell cycle underscores its importance in maintaining cell proliferation and genomic stability. Its participation in the homologous recombination pathway further emphasizes its critical function in DNA damage repair, which is vital for rapidly proliferating cancer cells. Moreover, its involvement in the p53 signaling pathway suggests potential regulatory roles in cellular stress responses. As a tumor suppressor, p53 mitigates tumor formation by orchestrating cell cycle arrest and apoptosis [42]. HROB may modulate cancer cell survival and proliferation by influencing p53 function.

To further dissect the molecular network centered on HROB and identify potential key interactors, we constructed a PPI network and pinpointed hub genes. Our PPI network analysis led to the identification of 10 hub genes: BUB1B, BUB1, NUF2, CCNB2, CCNB1, KIF11, KIF2C, TPX2, DLGAP5, and CDCA8. Notably, we observed that each hub gene was overexpressed at both the mRNA and protein levels in tumor tissues, and correlating with poor prognosis. BUB1B, a kinase crucial to the mitotic spindle checkpoint, is consistently overexpressed in various cancers and emerges as a potential target for LUAD [43]. BUB1 regulates the spindle assembly checkpoint during mitosis and enhances TGF-β signaling in non-dividing cells [44]. This signaling promotes tumor invasion, metastasis, and immune evasion, contributing to epithelial–mesenchymal transition of LUAD cells. CCNB1 and CCNB2 encode cyclins integral to the G2/M phase transition, with their overexpression linked to the rapid proliferation of LUAD cells [45, 46]. KIF11 and KIF2C, part of the kinesin family, are essential for spindle formation and chromosome segregation [47, 48]. TPX2 is pivotal for spindle assembly, with recent findings suggesting that it remodels the LUAD microenvironment by influencing macrophage polarization, promoting tumor progression [49]. Zhu et al. reported that TPX2 is closely associated with immune function and malignancy [50], and its knockdown impairs LUAD cell proliferation and migration. Chen et al. found that DLGAP5 enhances LUAD cell proliferation by upregulating PLK1 expression, positioning DLGAP5 as a potential prognostic biomarker and therapeutic target [51]. CDCA8 has been identified as an oncogene in LUAD, regulated by miR-133b, which suppresses LUAD cell proliferation, invasion, and migration by targeting CDCA8 [52].

Collectively, the overexpression and prognostic association of these HROB-associated hub genes reinforce the potential oncogenic driver role of HROB. To functionally interrogate the oncogenic role of HROB in LUAD, we performed cellular experiments demonstrating that its knockdown significantly suppresses proliferation, migration, and invasion, thereby consolidating its critical involvement in LUAD pathogenesis.

As an established predictor of immunotherapy response in lung cancer, high TMB enhances treatment efficacy by promoting immunogenic tumor recognition [30, 53]. Our study revealed that patients with high HROB expression exhibited significantly higher TMB than those with low expression, suggesting an enhanced potential for tumor immunogenicity. Regarding the expression of immune checkpoint molecules, PDCD1 (PD-1), CD274 (PD-L1), LAG3, and IDO1 expression levels were significantly higher in the high HROB expression group than in the low HROB expression group, indicating a stronger immunosuppressive microenvironment that facilitates immune evasion [54–56]. Although elevated TMB suggests theoretical susceptibility to immunotherapy, patients with high HROB expression paradoxically demonstrate concomitant enrichment of immunosuppressive checkpoint molecules and reduced immune infiltration. This coexistence of high tumor antigenicity and a strongly immunosuppressive microenvironment presents a substantial challenge for single-agent immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI) efficacy.

Based on these findings, we propose that this distinct immunological profile establishes a rational basis for investigating combination therapeutic strategies in this patient population. Specifically, the simultaneous targeting of multiple immune inhibitory pathways (e.g., combining PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors with LAG3 or IDO1 blockade) or leveraging potential DNA repair deficiencies through PARP inhibition may represent promising approaches to overcome immunosuppression while capitalizing on the high antigenic potential in HROB-high tumors. Future experimental studies are warranted to validate these combinatorial strategies.

Beyond immunotherapy, we also explored the potential predictive value of HROB expression for the efficacy of conventional chemotherapy and targeted agents commonly used in LUAD. Through the OncoPredict analysis, we found that high HROB expression was associated with significantly lower predicted IC50 values for cisplatin, paclitaxel, docetaxel, afatinib, erlotinib, gefitinib and osimertinib, suggesting a potential increase in tumor cell sensitivity to these agents. This presents an interesting phenomenon where HROB overexpression, despite conferring a worse prognosis, may also mark an inherent vulnerability to therapies targeting DNA integrity and cell cycle progression. The platinum-based drug cisplatin inhibits DNA replication and transcription by forming cross-links with DNA [57]. Paclitaxel and docetaxel, as microtubule inhibitors, block cell division by promoting microtubule polymerization and inhibiting microtubule depolymerization [58, 59]. The association of high HROB expression with lower IC50 values for these drugs suggests that tumor cells in these patients are more vulnerable in terms of DNA repair and cell division, resulting in a better response to these chemotherapeutic agents. Afatinib [60], erlotinib [61], gefitinib [62], and osimertinib [63, 64] are targeted drugs for epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) mutations that inhibit EGFR tyrosine kinase activity, thereby blocking the EGFR signaling pathway to suppress tumor cell proliferation and survival. The high HROB expression group exhibited lower IC50 values for these EGFR inhibitors, possibly because of the role of HROB in DNA repair and cell cycle regulation. This could make LUAD cells more susceptible to lethal damage upon EGFR inhibition.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the retrospective design of the database analysis and the absence of in vivo validation limit the immediate clinical generalizability of our findings; thus, future prospective studies and animal models are needed. Second, our work focused on HROB function but did not address its upstream regulation or post-translational modifications. Finally, the mechanistic links between HROB’s DNA repair function and its proposed roles in cell cycle regulation and immune modulation remain unclear. The interrogation of these specific mechanisms will be the primary focus of our subsequent research, which will employ functional experiments to elucidate these functional relationships.

Conclusions

In summary, this study reveals the association between HROB and LUAD through bioinformatics analysis. Our results demonstrate that HROB is upregulated in LUAD, significantly correlates with poor prognosis, and shows potential as a diagnostic biomarker. Functionally, knockdown of HROB can effectively inhibit the proliferation, migration and invasion of LUAD cells. It is worth noting that HROB may promote immune escape by remodeling tumor immune microenvironment. Patients with high HROB expression may benefit from combination strategies such as immunotherapy combined with EGFR inhibitors. These findings establish HROB as a promising dual-role target for both prognosis prediction and therapy in LUAD patients.
