Genome association of carbohydrate metabolites provides new insights toward functional breeding in coffee
Authors: Estefania Tavares Flores, Robert Logan Spicer, Elaine M R Souza, Maria Amelia G Ferrão, João Felipe B Senra, Camila Azevedo, Patricio R Munoz, Denise Tieman, Cătălin Voiniciuc, Luis Felipe V Ferrão
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCoffee is one of the world's most widely consumed beverages, derived mainly from Coffea arabica, known for its superior flavor, and Coffea canephora (Robusta/Conilon), valued for its resilience and higher caffeine content. Functional breeding aims to develop cultivars that combine productivity and stress tolerance with improved health‐related traits and flavor quality. Because carbohydrate polymers account for more than 50% of green coffee bean dry weight and act as key precursors of aroma and bioactive compounds, we hypothesized that elucidating the genetic architecture underlying carbohydrate metabolites would create new opportunities for molecular breeding in coffee. To test this hypothesis, we integrated cell wall carbohydrate profiling with genomic analyses in a genetically diverse coffee collection encompassing both Coffea canephora and Coffea arabica. Genome‐wide association studies identified 14 major genomic loci associated with variation in cell wall polysaccharide composition. Using cup quality as a functional trait endpoint, we further linked carbohydrate profiles with sensory evaluations and propose a framework for implementing marker‐assisted selection to accelerate flavor improvement in coffee breeding programs.

INTRODUCTION

Coffee has a multifaceted importance in our society and is considered an integral part of our healthy lifestyles. For many countries, the commodity is a driver of economic activity through either export of the green beans or consumption of the final product (ICO, 2025). In 2025, the global coffee market was estimated around $170 billion, making the crop one of the top traded commodities in the world (ICO, 2025). As consumers become more sophisticated and knowledgeable (Bozzola et al., 2021), more aligned to the health benefits and flavor profiles available, the notion of functional coffee has gained prominence. This has catalyzed interest in breeding coffee cultivars that go beyond traditional goals of yield and resilience, offering enhanced health benefits and richer flavor profiles.

The drink consumed daily comes from two species: Coffea canephora, which is also known as Robusta (or Conilon), and Coffea arabica, known as Arabica coffee (Ferrão et al., 2023). Overall, Arabica beans represent around 56% of all coffee sold and are prized for their flavor and aroma. However, the Robusta market has experienced important growth, raising its global representation from 25% to 40% in the last three decades (ICO, 2025). The attention on C. canephora beans across soluble, roast/blend, and specialty coffee markets has been driven by two main factors: (i) higher profitability for farmers, since the species has the potential to yield more beans using fewer resources, and (ii) higher resilience to biotic and abiotic stresses (Ferrão et al., 2017). Therefore, with rising temperatures projected to reduce areas suitable for growing Arabica by 80% (Davis et al., 2021; Imbach et al., 2017), Robusta/Conilon coffee emerges as an alternative to developing climate‐smart cultivars for the coffee supply chain.

Among the factors driving coffee quality and its health benefits, cell wall components in green beans are important elements (Z. Li, Zhang, et al., 2021). Considered to be precursors of flavor and aroma, carbohydrate polymers presented can exceed 50% of the green bean dry weight (Redgwell et al., 2002). In C. arabica, polymers such as galactomannans serve as important energy reserves in mature seeds, with the polysaccharides playing crucial roles in the roasting chemistry when sugars are converted into aroma precursors (Joët et al., 2009). For example, furan—one of the most abundant groups of volatiles in coffee aroma—is formed through thermal degradation and rearrangement of carbohydrates (Sunarharum et al., 2014). Also relevant, there are several lines of evidence supporting the role of polysaccharides in the viscosity of the brew and improving its thickness (Arya & Rao, 2007; Buffo & Cardelli‐Freire, 2004). The so‐called “crema” foam stability presented in espresso‐style coffees has been associated with the diversity of carbohydrates in the beans and affects the visual acceptability, can trap the aromas, and affects consumer experiences (Illy & Navarini, 2011). Other biological activities relate to the benefits of coffee polysaccharides as dietary fibers and prebiotics, with galactomannans as the most promising plant glycans for the treatment of diabetes (Juhasz et al., 2023). Altogether, these examples support the importance of assessing the carbohydrate profile in coffee. However, a better understanding of its genetic basis is required to explore all this potential at the breeding level.

Inference on the genetic basis of complex traits in coffee has been addressed using different approaches. The use of “family‐based” methods, in the form of quantitative trait loci (QTL) mapping, is the most popular. In both C. arabica and C. canephora, several QTL were reported for yield (Leroy et al., 2011; Moncada et al., 2016), morphological traits (Michel et al., 2007), disease (de Almeida et al., 2021), and chemical traits (Leroy et al., 2011; Mérot‐L'Anthoëne et al., 2014).

More recently, genome‐wide association analyses (GWAS) have been explored with the possibility of investigating deeper recombination events in more diverse populations, a method that can result in higher resolution and power when compared with family mapping. (Ferrão et al., 2023; Jose Luis et al., 2022; Sant'Ana et al., 2018; Silva et al., 2024; Suela et al., 2023; de Faria Silva et al., 2022; Zaidan et al., 2025) reported promising markers associated with genomic regions controlling yield, disease resistance, and post‐harvest traits in C. canephora. In C. arabica, the use of GWAS analyses revealed candidate genes influencing lipids and diterpene contents contributing to cup quality (Sant'Ana et al., 2018). While GWAS addressing carbohydrate content was not described in the Coffea literature, some transcriptomic analyses have paved the way for mechanistic insights related to cell wall composition. It includes the description of putative genes associated with galactomannan biosynthesis (Joët et al., 2009; Pré et al., 2008), along with other major bean components (Cheng et al., 2018; Fu et al., 2023), and the construction of metabolic biosynthetic pathways across different Coffea species.

In this study, we tested the hypothesis that cell wall carbohydrate profiling could be introduced to support molecular breeding efforts toward functional coffee. To this end, we integrated chemical and genomic data in a coffee population of 143 individuals, including C. arabica cultivars (n = 18), C. canephora varieties cultivated in high altitude (n = 26), and a diverse C. canephora panel (n = 99). By comprehensibly quantifying neutral and acidic carbohydrates across these populations, we found evidence of large diversity across and within populations and candidate genes controlling polysaccharide expression. Altogether, for the first time in coffee literature, we present a comprehensive study gathering multi‐omic information to deliver tools and guide breeding for functional coffee.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Plant material

A diverse genetic panel maintained by the Instituto Capixaba de Pesquisa, Assistência Técnica e Extensão Rural (Incaper) in Espirito Santo (ES) Brazil, was used in this study. Here, we defined three “population” sets used to conduct sensory, genomic, and chemical analyses in coffee.

The first population was referred to as CARA (Coffea arabica cultivar population) and includes 18 Coffea arabica cultivars cultivated at the Fazenda Experimental do Venda Nova (FEVN), under an altitude of 720 m with latitude of 20° 385° S and longitude of 41° 189° W. In the same location, we evaluated a second population: CANEalt ­‐ Coffea canephora genotypes grown at high altitude, that comprises 27 C. canephora genotypes, including synthetic Conilon cultivars. This C. canephora population was grown at high altitude to study its adaptation and cup quality (or cupping) in alternative environments. The largest population was here called as CANE ­‐ Coffea canephora diverse germplasm panel, and it is composed of a diverse set of accessions maintained at the Incaper germplasm collection. Briefly, since 1988, plant accessions from different regions and origins have been collected and evaluated in an ex situ conservation site at the Fazenda Experimental de Bananal do Norte (FEB), with latitude 20,750°S and longitude 41,228°W. From the CANE collection, a total of 102 individuals were selected.

In summary, this study evaluated a total of 143 coffee genotypes from two species (C. canephora and C. arabica), structured into three populations: CARA (18 genotypes), CANEalt (26 genotypes), and CANE (99 genotypes). From each genotype, a sample of 2 kg ripened fruits (cherry stage) was harvested per genotype in the 2022 season, dried using a natural method, and depulped (getting rid of the skin and mucilage) to produce the green beans. Green beans were used for near‐infrared spectroscopy (NIR) and carbohydrate profiling. Individual leaves were collected from each genotype for DNA extraction and genotyping.

Plant material, carbohydrate isolation, and monosaccharide quantification

At least 30 green coffee beans per genotype, from two technical samples, were fresh frozen using liquid nitrogen and pulverized into fine powder using a Micro‐Mill II Grinder (Scienceware) for 5 min. Ground green coffee bean samples were stored in 50 mL tubes at −80°C until further processing. Alcohol insoluble residue (AIR) containing cell wall polymers was extracted according to a published protocol (Foster et al., 2010) with the following modifications. At least 10 mg of ground green coffee beans were transferred to 2‐mL safe lock tubes. Samples were sequentially washed using 1 mL of 70% ethanol, chloroform:methanol (1:1 v/v), and acetone (Foster et al., 2010) before being vortex mixed spun down for 3 min at 16,435 g to separate the pellet from the supernatant that is discarded. Before proceeding to the next solvent, wash steps were repeated until the supernatant became clear after centrifugation. After the final acetone wash, the pellet was gently resuspended in a portion of the residual solvent and carefully dried at 45°C using a Dri‐Block DB200/3 with sample concentrator (Techne).

Polysaccharides were hydrolyzed by weighing 1–2 mg of dry AIR in 2 mL screw‐cap tubes and incubating them with 300 µL of 2 M trifluoroacetic acid (TFA) in a heat block for 90 min at 120°C (Voiniciuc & Günl, 2016; Voiniciuc et al., 2015). After cooling on ice and centrifuging the samples, 150 µL of each supernatant was transferred to a new 2 mL screw‐cap tube, and the TFA was evaporated at 45°C with pressurized air. Blank and sugar standards were prepared via a 9‐sugar mix (fucose [Fuc], Rha, arabinose [Ara], galactose [Gal], glucose [Glc], mannose (Man), xylose [Xyl], galacturonic acid [GalA], and glucuronic acid [GlcA]) and dried under pressurized air at 45°C. Dried samples and standards were rehydrated with 300 µL of internal standard (30 µg/mL ribose), vortex mixed, and centrifuged for 3 min at 16,435 g. Before transferring 150 µL to a plastic ion chromatography (IC) vial with cap seal (Thermo Scientific), samples were diluted 1:1 with internal standard in a separate tube.

Monosaccharide analysis was conducted via High‐Performance Anion‐Exchange Chromatography with Pulsed Amperometric Detection (HPAEC‐PAS using a Metrohm 940 Professional IC Vario System equipped with Metrosep Carb 2–250/4.0 analytical and guard columns (Metrohm AG). Each sample and standard injected 10 µL of liquid through a 10 µL loop for analysis. A 60 min protocol suitable for separation of the nine‐sugar components included a 22 min isocratic 2 mM sodium hydroxide (NaOH) + 3.4 mM sodium acetate (NaAce) separation step, followed by 22 min rinse with 100 mM NaOH + 170 mM NaAce, before 14 min re‐equilibration with the starting eluents (Mielke et al., 2021). Generated peaks were automatically integrated (or manually) and calibrated using analytical standard curves using MagIC Net 4.0 software (Metrohm AG). Concentration for each genotype was calculated as the mean across its corresponding technical replicates.

Genotypic data

Genotyping was carried out by the LGC/RAPiD Genomics (Gainesville) company, using the sequencing capture methodology, targeting 10,000 probe regions designed for the sequence capture approach (Resende, 2016). Raw reads were first filtered by quality and trimmed. Filtered reads were mapped against the C. canephora genome assembly (Denoeud et al., 2014) using the BWA v.0.7.17 software (Li & Durbin, 2009). Single‐nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) were called using FREEBAYES v.1.0.1 (Garrison & Marth, 2012). Loci were also filtered by applying the following criteria: minimum mapping quality of 10, only biallelic locus, maximum missing data (call rate) of 40%, minor allele frequency of 1%, and minimum and maximum mean depth of 3 and 750, respectively. In total, 149,970 raw SNPs were originally reported. After following the quality control steps, a total of 43,279 markers were retained. More details regarding the SNP filtering procedure are provided by Ferrão et al. (2024) and Rocha et al. (2025), who applied similar filtering criteria as those adopted in previous studies.

Sensory analyses for cup quality

During this study, we illustrated the concept of functional coffee by linking the carbohydrate profile with sensory analyses. For the cupping quality, samples from both locations (FEVN and FEB) and three populations (CARA, CANEalt, and CANE) were first standardized by selecting beans with similar sizes. Beans with visual defects were removed, and 100 g of green coffee was collected per sample. For roasting, the selected beans were roasted within 24 h of cupping and allowed to rest for at least 8 h, following the protocol proposed by the Specialty Coffee Association (SCA). Evaluations were performed for, on average, four Q‐graders specialists who, using the same protocols, evaluated each coffee sample individually for flavor, acidity, balance, aftertaste, body, aroma, and overall liking (which are evaluated with scores from 6 to 10 points each). Attributes related to uniformity, sweetness, clean cup, and comments were also recorded. The final score was computed as the average between the panelists, and samples with a final score equal to or greater than 80 points were considered specialty coffees. Sensory attributes follow the protocols defined by the Coffee Quality Institute, in collaboration with the Uganda Coffee Development Authority. All samples were evaluated in the Incaper Institute, a coffee research center, in Brazil.

NIR data

NIR data were obtained from green coffee beans harvested in February 2022. To this end, around 2 kg of ripened fruits were dried and depulped (getting rid of the skin and mucilage) to obtain the green coffee beans. Each sample was ground to a fine powder. For each sample, three technical replicate reflectance data points were collected within the range of 906 to 1676 nm in a 6 nm step size, consisting of ∼125 wavelength data points. Spectra data were obtained using the VIAVI MicroNIR OnSite‐W device along with its corresponding software. To ensure data quality, we conducted checks for outliers and applied pretreatment techniques. The spectra data were normalized (centered and scaled), and their second derivative was computed using a Savitzky‐Golay filter with a window size of 15 data points, as implemented in the waves R package (Hershberger et al., 2021). Results from the NIR information and sensory were used for principal component analysis (PCA), as implemented by the FactoMineR package (Lê et al., 2008).

Genome‐wide association analyses

For SNP‐trait associations, two GWAS models were tested. First, analyses were conducted for each carbohydrate chemical using the classical Q + K linear mixed model (LMM) (Yu et al., 2006) implemented in the GWASpoly R package (Rosyara et al., 2016), as follows:

where y is the phenotypic vector, μ is the mean, 1 is a vector of the same dimension of y being all elements equal to 1, v represents the vector of effects for subpopulations and batch effects, Q is the matrix with the first five principal components, population (CARA, CANEalt, and CANE), and the batch information; u is the vector polygenic effects with u∼N(0,σu2K), where K is the genomic relationship matrix between individuals and σu2 is the additive genetic variance, Z is the matrix of incidence of random effects, xi is the additive effect of ith marker with incidence vector M and e is the vector of random errors, e∼N(0,Iσe2) and σe2 is the residual variance. The F‐test was used to compute the p‐value for each marker, and a Bonferroni‐type of multiple test correction was used to set the significance threshold.

Once significant SNPs were detected through GWAS analyses using additive gene action models, a genomic window of 100 kb on each side was defined to search for candidate genes. The Coffee Genome Hub database (Denoeud et al., 2014) was used to identify C. canephora genes located encompassing significant SNPs, as suggested in Ferrão et al. (2023).

Prediction analyses

Prediction analyses were carried out using LMMs by modeling variation on the cup quality as a function of either genomics, metabolomics, or NIR information. To this end, the same linear model reported in the Phenotypic Section was considered, with effects associated with batch and subpopulations effects, genetic effects, and the residual term.

To model the genetic effect, a variance and covariance structure connecting individuals was constructed using different omics data. For genomic selection, molecular markers were used as predictors, and a G matrix was computed as proposed by VanRaden (2008), using the AGHmatrix R package (Amadeu et al., 2023). For metabolomic selection, carbohydrates were used as predictors, and a so‐called K matrix was computed using Gaussian kernels, defined in the Euclidean space, as reported by Ferrao et al. (2022). The phenomic selection model was built based on NIR information. To this end, the so‐called P matrix was computed as SS′nw, where S is the centered and scaled matrix for each wavelength and nw is the number of wavelengths reflectance. All models were implemented in the Bayesian framework using the BLGR R package (Perez & de los Campos, 2014) with a total of 120,000 iterations, including a burn‐in period of 20,000 iterations and thinning every five iterations. The predictive performance was computed as the Person's correlation between predicted and the observed values, using a 10‐fold cross‐validation scheme.

Another predictive model was computed using the top significant hits identified via GWAS. Classified here as marker‐assisted selection (MAS), we used eight markers in multiple regression linear models to explain the cupping score. The coefficient of determination (R 2) from the regression model was used as proxy of the proportional variance estimated (PVE).

Response to selection

To project the response to selection, we used the so‐called Breeder's equation as follows (Falconer & Mackay, 1996): RL=iPA(σy)/L, where i refers to the selection intensity, PA is the predictive ability computed in a cross‐validation scheme using different methods (genomic selection, carbohydrate information, NIR, and MAS), σy is the phenotypic variability associated with cupping scores, and L is the length of the breeding cycle. In the case of MAS, the top six SNPs reported in the GWAS analyses (lowest p values) were used to predict and rank the genetic merits. For the heritability associated to cupping scores, we assumed a conservative value of 0.30, as recently reported in the coffee literature (Nadaleti et al., 2022). The genetic gains were expressed in percentage, after considering that a maximum cupping score using the SGA sensory protocol is 100 points. To this end, we considered a hypothetical population mean of 80 points, and computed the gains (in %) over this population mean.

Importantly, when projecting the genetic gains using the Breeder's equation, it is noteworthy that selection can be carried out on different phenological stages and biological materials. For example, the use of DNA information (either in the form of genomic selection or MAS) might be done in the first year for a new population, with information collected at the leaf stage. In contrast, NIR, carbohydrate, and cupping profiles require harvested beans and hence at least four years until new plants reach the first stable production of seeds. Such numbers were considered in the denominator of the equation (L) when reporting the RL values.

RESULTS

Carbohydrate diversity in a coffee panel

We evaluated the diversity in our genetic panel using both chemical and genomic data. Multivariate analyses showed that genomic information effectively separated the two species, with C. arabica cultivars clustering in a distinct quadrant from C. canephora plants (Figure 1a, top left). Focusing on the C. canephora samples, the genomic data revealed two additional subgroups within the original panel (Figure 1a, top right). In quadrants I and IV, PCA grouped genotypes belonging to the “Conilon” botanical group, while quadrants II and III contained hybrids between “Conilon” and “Robusta.” This botanical classification largely agreed with field notes and pedigree records collected by breeders (results not shown).

In parallel, we assessed diversity using the carbohydrate data collected in the green coffee beans. The carbohydrates evaluated in the cell wall were represented in the absolute amounts (µg sugar/mg of cell wall material) and the relative proportion of each monosaccharide (mol %) after acid hydrolysis of polymers. In total, nine carbohydrate components were quantified: Fuc, Rhamnose (Rha), Ara, Gal, Glc, Man, Xyl, GalA, and GlcA. When using the total amount, PCA did not reveal a clear separation between the three populations (Figure 1a, bottom left), likely due to the intrinsic challenges of weighing and transferring small amounts of many samples at the mg scale. However, when comparing relative proportions that eliminate weighing errors, C. arabica and C. canephora species separated into groups with more distinct carbohydrate profiles.

To quantify the main source of variations in the dataset, variance components associated within and across the population sets were estimated. We noticed that variations at the population level were larger than within‐population effects (Figure 1b), with nuances in specific carbohydrate (Figure 1c). For example, the C. arabica beans, on average, had more Glc‐rich polymers, while C. canephora cell walls contained a higher proportion of Gal. Within‐population, on the other hand, showed a large diversity, with the chemical distribution resembling a normal distribution (Figure 1d). Overall, the CANE population showed more extreme values.

GWAS reveals candidate loci related to glycobiology

GWAS was carried out to identify candidate genes related to absolute or relative cell wall monosaccharide levels using an LMM approach. In total, we identified 14 major genomic regions covering most of the chromosomes (Figure 2a). We then investigated the functional annotations of the 214 genes near the GWAS peaks and found some potential candidate genes controlling key pathways associated with coffee, flavor, and carbohydrate metabolism.

Functional coffee: Linking carbohydrate and cup quality

To explore the potential of cell wall analysis for functional coffee, we examined correlations between carbohydrate profiles and sensory attributes. While coffee quality arises from complex interactions among texture, sugars, acids, and aromas, our focus is on the predictive value of carbohydrates for cup quality.

Sensory quality was assessed using traditional cupping score (SCA's 0–100 scale). Pearson's correlation analysis revealed significant associations between certain glycosidic residues and sensory perception across three populations (Figure 3a). Specifically, Gal, Glc, and Ara exhibited positive correlations with overall cup quality (Figure 3b; Table S2).

Further insights were obtained through expert evaluations by certified Q‐graders. Although correlation does not imply causation, PCA indicated promising trends (Figure 3c). For instance, Gal was associated with descriptors such as “tropical fruits,” “floral,” “chocolate,” and notably “full‐bodied.” Conversely, lower total carbohydrate concentrations were linked to negative attributes like “no body,” suggesting a potential role in mouthfeel consistency. Common positive descriptors included “caramel,” “almonds,” and “sweet,” while negative terms comprised “strong bitter,” “herbal,” and “woody” (Figure 2d).

Comparative analysis across varieties revealed additional trends. Between two Arabica cultivars with distinct cupping scores (84 vs. 74), significant compositional differences were observed, including higher Gal% and Xyl% in the lower‐scoring sample. Moreover, certain C. canephora genotypes displayed similar chemical and sensory profiles to Arabica, highlighting the potential of Robusta/Conilon genotypes to contribute to cup quality. Similar patterns across C. canephora populations from different regions (CANEalt and CANE) further support the feasibility of specialty coffee production in diverse environments.

Connecting NIR, specific carbohydrate content, and related to cup quality

For benchmarking, we correlated the scores from cup quality with NIR, since this technique was previously used in coffee studies associated with quality and certification. For data collected on green beans, wavelengths ranging from 700 to 1700 were quantified and pre‐treated, as discussed by Adunola et al. (2024) (Figure 4a). After investigating each wavelength, a total of seven bands correlated with cupping quality were identified. The quantification was performed individually using Pearson's correlation with results ranging from −0.3 to 0.4 (Figure 4b). After selecting the wavelengths that correlated individually >30%, we carried out a multivariate analysis by integrating the three layers of information (chemical, sensory, and NIR). Notably, in Quadrant II, the cupping score (sensory analysis) clustered closely with three NIR wavelengths and two chemical components (Glc% and Man%). These five variables occupied a similar region in the biplot, suggesting a positive association with cupping score (Figure 3c). In short, our results support a large body of coffee research that has used NIR as a proxy for measuring quality.

When weighing the genetic gains per breeding cycle, genomic selection and MAS stand as the best alternatives, since early selection can be applied. Overall, we estimated a genetic gain of 5%–7% using genomic information, either in the form of MAS or whole‐genomic prediction, although it could be associated with higher implementation costs. From another perspective, flavor predictions could be viewed in terms of classification. For example, coffee samples with a cupping score >80 points are commonly classified as “high quality” and command higher prices on the specialty market. We used the same predictive models to measure the ability to classify a given sample as >80 points. Using NIR, chemical, genomic, and MAS approaches, we computed a predictive ability of 0.46, 0.49, 0.45, and 0.33, respectively.

DISCUSSION

Carbohydrate biosynthesis in coffee cell walls

In coffee beans, cell wall storage polysaccharides account for approximately 50% of the seed's dry mass (Redgwell & Fischer, 2006). The predominant components—galactomannans and arabinogalactans—are synthesized through a complex and dynamic process during fruit development (Redgwell & Fischer, 2006). As outlined by N. Li, Wang, et al. (2021), the composition and structure of these polysaccharides evolve across three distinct developmental phases.

The initial phase, marked by perisperm development, is characterized by high arabinogalactan accumulation (Redgwell & Fischer, 2006). This transitions in the second phase—endosperm development—to a predominant synthesis of branched galactomannans, accompanied by a reduction in arabinoxylans. In the final stage, associated with fruit ripening and pericarp maturation, galactomannan accumulation intensifies, contributing to endosperm hardening (N. Li, Wang, et al., 2021). The harvest of beans at full maturity explains the high Gal and Man abundance that are the primary constituents of galactomannan, which is also abundant in legume seeds and serves as an excellent gelling agent/stabilizer in numerous food products (Voiniciuc, 2022).

Throughout these developmental stages, a suite of genes and enzymes linked to ripening and polysaccharide biosynthesis has been identified in C. arabica (Joët et al., 2009; 2014; N. Li, Wang, et al., 2021). Although functional characterization of these genes remains in its early stages, recent advances in yeast‐based synthetic biology have begun to elucidate the specific role of key GT enzymes, including cellulose synthase‐like A (CSLA) and mannan synthesis‐related (MSR) proteins (Voiniciuc et al., 2018). Transcriptomic and metabolomic data suggest that polysaccharide accumulation is tightly regulated at the transcriptional level, particularly for the galactomannan biosynthetic pathway (Dussert et al., 2024), with modulation by soluble sugars such as sorbitol and raffinose.

While comprehensive overviews of carbohydrate biosynthesis in coffee have been provided previously (Joët et al., 2009; N. Li, Wang, et al., 2021), the present study advances this understanding by integrating phenotypic and genotypic data at the population level. To our knowledge, this is the first genome‐wide association study (GWAS) addressing cell wall composition in coffee. Accordingly, the discussion is organized around two key themes: (i) candidate genes for functional validation and (ii) the implications of these findings for functional breeding, with focus on flavor quality.

Candidate genes related to carbohydrate biosynthesis

In the coffee literature, GWAS studies have emerged as an important tool for modern genomics studies, enabling researchers to dissect the genetic architecture of a wide spectrum of economically and agronomically important traits in both species. Focusing on quality, for example, Sant'Ana et al. (2018) provided one of the earliest large‐scale demonstrations of GWAS potential in coffee by identifying candidate genes associated with lipid and diterpene. Building on this framework, Jose Luis et al. (2022) extended GWAS to a broader range of chemical attributes, confirming that SNP markers can reliably tag loci governing sensory and biochemical properties relevant to the coffee industry. Shifting attention to morphological, physiological, and yield‐related phenotypes, Suela et al. (2023), on the other hand, employed structural equation models within a GWAS framework to capture indirect genetic pathways. Complementing these studies, more recently, da Silva et al. (2024) and Ferrão et al. (2023) identified SNP markers and candidate genes for major agronomic traits. In parallel, disease resistance and plant architecture were also addressed by other authors (de Faria Silva et al., 2022; Zaidan et al., 2025). Herein, we extended the GWAS landscape in coffee by investigating carbohydrate traits.

Our first contribution is to report candidate genes within genomic windows surrounding significant SNPs. Herein, we focused our discussion on a small group of genes already reported in the plant literature, with functions related to cell wall biosynthesis. While examples of using gene editing in coffee are limited to a few traits, such as caffeine, for example (Prado et al., 2024), our goal here is to discuss a list of potential regions that could be used as a target for further functional studies.

The first genomic region is in chromosome 1, associated with the relative concentration of Glc. We could find a cluster of two candidate genes that might be related to cell‐wall biosynthesis. The first gene candidate is the α‐1,4‐glucan phosphorylase, mainly involved in the breakdown of starch. Well‐described in tomato (Di Giacomo et al., 2023; N. Li, Wang, et al., 2021; Slugina et al., 2019), this enzyme can influence the availability of activated Glc precursors for the synthesis of cell wall polysaccharides. A second candidate gene is related to the laccases, which are involved in the oxidation of phenolic compounds and can impact lignin polymerization to strengthen the plant cell wall (Schuetz et al., 2014). When connected to coffee flavor, this oxidation can reduce bitterness and enhance antioxidant properties in coffee (Fachniar et al., 2020).

A second important cluster contains four main genes reported in chromosome 7, associated with Man content. The first candidate gene is UDP‐Glc 6‐dehydrogenase 1 and encodes an enzyme that catalyzes the conversion of UDP‐Glc to UDP GlcA, which is a precursor for the synthesis of hemicellulose and pectin in the cell wall. In cotton, gene expression is linked to fiber development, indicating its importance in enhancing fiber quality and overall plant structure (Jia et al., 2021). In a transcriptome of C. arabica seeds, this gene was also related to the galactomannan synthetic machinery (Joët et al., 2014). The mapped chromosome 7 region also contains an α‐1,4‐glucan phosphorylase, plus putative GTs that could be involved in the formation and modification of cell walls (Ruprecht et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2023). These enzymes catalyze glycosidic bonds, facilitating the transfer of sugar monomers to form complex carbohydrates essential for cell wall structure and integrity, influencing properties such as growth, biomass recalcitrance, and fruit shelf life. Determining the biochemical activities of polysaccharide GT activities remains challenging (Amos & Mohnen, 2019) but is essential to modify cell wall composition and function to accelerate the breeding or editing of plants with improved commercial traits (Voiniciuc, 2023). GTs were also reported in a transcriptome analysis of C. arabica seeds (Joët et al., 2014). A fourth gene associated with the chromosome 7 region encode a hydroxyproline‐rich glycoprotein family protein (HRGP), which is an important component of the plant cell wall and could interact with non‐cellulosic polysaccharides to enhance their resilience (Hijazi et al., 2014; Josè & Puigdomènech, 1993). HRGPs have been modified to enhance biomass processability (Fang et al., 2020) and could be important to defend plants during host‐pathogen interactions (Deepak et al., 2010).

Molecular breeding in coffee: The flavor example

Recently, the industry has experienced an important shift toward craft experience, driven by a new generation of discerning consumers who prioritize quality and sustainability as fundamental principles (Bozzola et al., 2021). It makes flavor a central paradigm for modern breeding programs. Some preliminary studies have connected carbohydrate concentration with organoleptic properties of the coffee brew, including viscosity, mouthfell, and foam stability (Z. Li, Zhang, et al., 2021), with galactomannans as a neglected component for quality espresso (Illy & Navarini, 2011). In this study, multivariate analyses strengthen this relationship, revealing that samples with lower carbohydrate concentrations were consistently associated with lower cupping scores and intensity. Given that the carbohydrate profile serves as a reliable predictor of flavor, our key objective is to evaluate its effectiveness in applied breeding programs.

For selection, we evaluated different criteria for flavor prediction (NIR, GS, and MAS). NIR technology has recently been proposed as a promising alternative for predicting complex traits in coffee (Adunola et al., 2024; Mbebi et al., 2022). To predict cup quality, we computed a predictive ability of 0.16. In contrast to NIR collected in green beans, genomic prediction can be implemented at the seedling stage, thereby leveraging the genetic gains across generations. Utilizing genomic prediction, we reported a predictive ability of 0.12 and an estimated genetic gain of approximately 2.02% per breeding cycle. Finally, by selecting the six most significant loci identified via GWAS, we found that these loci collectively accounted for about 16% of the phenotypic variation (PVE). When translated into predictive ability, the MAS selection yielded a predictive ability of 0.11 and a genetic gain of approximately 1.85%. These values reported by genome‐based methods are more than twice the projected gains associated with NIR selection (0.67%). This fact highlights the advantages of conducting early selections, as estimated genomic breeding values can be predicted during the seedling stage within the first year.

We also reported the results in terms of genetic gains. In any plant breeding, resource allocation cannot be ignored. In our original analysis, we classified the cost and throughputs associated with each approach as high, medium, and low, with the best method aggregating (i) high genetic gains, (ii) low cost per samples, and (iii) high ability to process multiple samples simultaneously. Not surprisingly, the use of DNA information is the best alternative to maximize the gains and leverage the number of samples. However, DNA‐based genomic selection is still costly for coffee. Herein, we argue in favor of MAS, which emerges as an important alternative to guide future efforts for flavor selection.

From a different perspective, predictive results are still more promising when the final target is to classify brewed coffee samples. During sensory analyses, specialists classify a coffee sample as premium when the final cup score is >80, following the SCA protocols (Uganda Coffee Development Authority, 2012). The use of genomic prediction reported a projected genetic gain of 7.61% per cycle, while the use of MAS resulted in a project gain of 5.59%. These findings indicate that the classification of premium coffee genotypes can be effectively supported by DNA‐based information, even at early stages of breeding programs. Overall, the results suggest that incorporating genomic tools may enhance selection efficiency and maximize genetic gains. In a scenario where accessible genome‐wide markers to be used routinely are still a major barrier for most coffee breeding programs, the use of carbohydrate‐focused MAS emerges as a viable alternative to be implemented at the pace and scope of applied coffee programs. The integration of genomic selection and MAS into the C. canephora breeding pipeline was recently discussed by Ferrão et al. (2023).

It is important to note that the MAS approach employed in this study served as a preliminary assessment of the feasibility of using a limited set of markers to predict complex traits. The predictive ability reported for MAS was not derived from a cross‐validation framework, which may lead to overestimation due to potential overfitting. Future research should focus on developing KASP markers for genotyping independent populations. We believe that such efforts will provide a more precise evaluation of the effectiveness and practical impact of MAS in coffee breeding programs.

A note of caution is warranted when interpreting these findings in the context of flavor prediction. Coffee flavor is inherently complex, shaped by a multitude of compounds, including volatiles, acids, and alkaloids. While these have traditionally dominated flavor research, structural polysaccharides—particularly cell wall carbohydrates—have been relatively overlooked (Clarke, 2003; Toledo et al., 2016). Emerging data, however, suggests that these components may play a complementary role by influencing sensory attributes such as mouthfeel, perceived sweetness, and aroma retention (Illy & Navarini, 2011; Joët et al., 2009). By examining the contribution of carbohydrates to coffee quality, this study offers a novel perspective that broadens the current framework of flavor analysis and opens new opportunities for breeding and processing innovation. Another important observation is the role of genotype‐by‐environment interactions. Although the genotypes evaluated in this study were sampled from different environments, a more structured and replicated experimental design would be necessary to accurately quantify the effects of local and temporal variation on the expression of key carbohydrate traits.

Functional coffee: The importance of cell‐wall polysaccharides for the coffee research and industry

Although we illustrated the concept of functional breeding for coffee flavor, there are other potential fields that can benefit from understanding the genetic architecture of carbohydrates in coffee. In a recent review of the topic, Z. Li, Zhang, et al. (2021) highlighted its potential to leverage health‐promoting components, the use of coffee husks in biorefineries, and improving resilience to biotic and abiotic stresses.

The relationship between coffee polysaccharides and nutritional value is well‐established, highlighting opportunities to enhance bioaccessibility. Galactomannans, for example, are the best soluble fiber to lower blood sugar and treat diabetes. Considering that such polysaccharide can degrade more readily than arabinogalactans, modifying arabinogalactan structure through genetic engineering could improve digestibility (Gniechwitz et al., 2007; Z. Li, Zhang, et al., 2021). For example, a valid strategy includes reducing the units from the arabinogalactan component that are least degraded (the α‐(1,5)‐linked Ara) by targeting specific genes to generate a more linear, fermentable polymer (Gniechwitz et al., 2007). A second alternative is to manipulate genes related to lignin content that are presented in the coffee husks. While coffee husks contain a significant amount of cell wall polysaccharides, these compounds could be broken down into simple sugars and therefore converted into bioethanol or products for biorefineries (de Carvalho Oliveira et al., 2018; Z. Li, Zhang, et al., 2021). Several studies suggest that cell wall modifications can enhance resilience to environmental stresses. From a disease perspective, certain pathogens may lack the enzymatic repertoire required for effective cell wall degradation, potentially limiting infection. In response to heat stress, alterations in polysaccharide composition can influence cell wall remodeling, improving structural integrity and increasing tolerance to elevated temperatures (Miedes et al., 2014; Z. Li, Zhang, et al., 2021).

Altogether, research on cell wall biology in model plants has enabled biotechnological strategies for optimizing agricultural applications. While coffee is among the world's most valuable crops, the genetic architecture of cell wall properties has been primarily studied through biochemical approaches, with limited application in breeding programs. The perennial nature of the crop poses significant challenges for incorporating cell wall‐related traits into elite cultivars. However, recent advancements in genomics offer new opportunities to apply cell wall‐focused biotechnological strategies to improve coffee quality, stress resilience, and by‐product utilization. Although further research into functional validation and cell wall biosynthesis is required, here we presented a blueprint of potential genomic regions that could be explored toward the main goal of leveraging the flavor and sustainability of our daily coffee.

CONCLUSIONS

In this study, we evaluated a diverse set of 143 coffee genotypes from different species and investigated the genetic architecture of carbohydrate composition as a framework for functional breeding in coffee. Our results revealed meaningful variation in key carbohydrate components and identified positive corrections with cupping scores. By identifying candidate molecular markers and spectral regions associated with desirable quality traits, this study establishes a practical framework to support marker‐assisted and prediction‐based selection strategies in coffee. Overall, our work contributes foundational knowledge toward the development of more precise and efficient breeding approaches for quality improvement.
