Distinct Pathogenic Mechanisms of Neurodegeneration in Pachychoroid Pigment Epitheliopathy Versus Intermediate Age-Related Macular Degeneration
Authors: Pasquale Viggiano, Lorenzo Accurso Tagano, Roberta Binetti, Stefania De Leonardis, Marco Carella, Giulia Ribezzi, Alba Chiara Termite, Maria Grazia Pignataro, Stefano Dore, Federica Evangelista, Paolo Evangelista, Cristiana Iaculli, Giovanni Alessio, Francesco Boscia
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionTo investigate distinct neurodegeneration mechanisms in pachychoroid pigment epitheliopathy (PPE) versus age-related macular degeneration (AMD) using quantitative optical coherence tomography (OCT) with age-matched controls.MethodsThis cross-sectional study of 184 subjects included 50 age-matched controls (25 young: 47–63 years; 25 elderly: 67–83 years), 57 PPE patients (47–63 years), 39 AMD drusen patients (64–80 years), and 38 AMD reticular pseudodrusen patients (67–83 years). Primary outcomes included ganglion cell layer–inner plexiform layer (GCL-IPL) thickness, subfoveal choroidal thickness, and choroidal volume using swept-source OCT.ResultsAll pathologic conditions showed significant neurodegeneration versus age-matched controls (p < 0.001). PPE demonstrated choroidal thickening (410.5 ± 32.9 μm vs. 306.0 ± 10.8 μm controls) with inverse choroidal–neural correlations (r = −0.52 to −0.61, p < 0.001). AMD variants showed choroidal thinning (187–238 μm vs. 277 μm elderly controls) with positive correlations (drusen: r = +0.43, p = 0.006; RPD: r = +0.68, p < 0.001).ConclusionsTwo distinct pathways cause neurodegeneration: compressive (PPE: choroidal thickening → choriocapillaris compression → neurodegeneration) and ischemic (AMD: choroidal thinning → hypoperfusion → neurodegeneration).

Introduction

Neurodegeneration of the inner retinal layers, particularly the ganglion cell layer–inner plexiform layer (GCL-IPL) complex, represents a common endpoint in diverse macular pathologies, yet the underlying pathogenic mechanisms remain incompletely understood [1, 2]. While traditionally viewed as a secondary consequence of outer retinal damage, emerging evidence suggests that inner retinal neurodegeneration may follow distinct pathways depending on the primary vascular and structural alterations affecting the posterior pole [3].

Pachychoroid pigment epitheliopathy (PPE) and age-related macular degeneration (AMD) represent two fundamentally different disease processes that affect the choroidal–retinal interface [4, 5]. PPE, characterized by choroidal thickening and dilated pachyvessels, belongs to the pachychoroid spectrum diseases and typically affects younger patients [6]. In contrast, AMD variants, including conventional drusen and reticular pseudodrusen (RPD), are associated with choroidal thinning and vascular compromise, predominantly affecting elderly populations [7, 8].

The advent of enhanced-depth imaging optical coherence tomography (EDI-OCT) and swept-source OCT (SS-OCT) has enabled precise quantification of both choroidal architecture and inner retinal layer thickness, providing unprecedented insights into vascular–neural relationships [9, 10]. Recent studies have documented significant choroidal thickness differences between pachychoroid and AMD spectrum diseases, but the relationship between these vascular changes and neurodegeneration patterns remains unclear [11–14].

Traditional models suggest a positive correlation between choroidal health and neuronal preservation, with thicker choroids providing better vascular support for retinal function [15, 16]. However, this paradigm may not apply universally across all macular pathologies. The unique vascular architecture of pachychoroid diseases, characterized by dilated Haller layer vessels and compressed choriocapillaris, may paradoxically compromise rather than enhance retinal perfusion despite increased overall choroidal thickness [4].

Understanding the distinct pathogenic mechanisms underlying neurodegeneration in these conditions has critical implications for diagnosis, prognosis, and therapeutic intervention. The present study aims to elucidate the mechanistic basis of neurodegeneration across pachychoroid and AMD spectrum diseases through comprehensive OCT analysis with age-matched controls, establishing correlation-based biomarkers for diagnostic differentiation and therapeutic guidance.

Methods

Study Design and Participants

This retrospective cross-sectional investigation was conducted within the medical retina research unit of the Department of Translational Biomedicine and Neuroscience at the University of Bari Aldo Moro, located in Italy. The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional and national research committee and with the 1964 Helsinki Declaration and its later amendments or comparable ethical standards. In Italy, retrospective studies using anonymized data do not require specific patient consent or ethical committee approval. However, this study was reviewed and approved by the internal review boards of the University of Bari “Aldo Moro,” who confirmed that patient consent was not required due to the retrospective and anonymized nature of the data.

Our study comprised a comprehensive cohort of 184 eyes from 184 individual patients, stratified into five distinct groups based on age-matching and diagnostic criteria. The research period spanned from January 2023 to December 2024. Each participant underwent an exhaustive ophthalmologic assessment, which encompassed best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) measurements using Early Treatment Diabetic Retinopathy Study (ETDRS) charts, comprehensive dilated fundus examination, color fundus photography, and advanced SS-OCT utilizing the PLEX Elite 9000 imaging system (manufactured by Carl Zeiss Meditec, Inc., Dublin, CA, USA).

The study population was systematically organized into five groups: 25 young healthy controls (age range: 47–63 years), 25 elderly healthy controls (age range: 67–83 years), 57 patients diagnosed with PPE (age range: 47–63 years), 39 patients with AMD with conventional drusen (AMD-drusen, age range: 64–80 years), and 38 patients with AMD with reticular pseudodrusen (GCL-IPL, age range: 67–83 years).

Diagnostic Criteria and Patient Selection

Pachychoroid pigment epitheliopathy (PPE): The diagnosis of PPE was substantiated through comprehensive multimodal imaging analysis following established diagnostic criteria [6, 17]. On structural SS-OCT, we identified patients exhibiting characteristic vascular abnormalities, specifically including pathologically expanded vessels within Haller’s layer (pachyvessels) [4, 18]. Distinctive imaging features revealed significant attenuation of the inner choroidal layers, particularly affecting Sattler’s layer and the choriocapillaris [19]. The retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) demonstrated focal areas of irregular pigmentation and thickening. A distinctive feature was the presence of pachydrusen, characterized as isolated or scattered yellowish lesions with broader and more irregular configuration compared to typical drusen, predominantly located in the posterior pole and often corresponding to areas of choroidal thickening. Indocyanine green angiography (ICGA) was systematically performed in all PPE patients using the Spectralis HRA + OCT system (Heidelberg Engineering, Heidelberg, Germany) to evaluate choroidal vascular hyperpermeability [20].

Age-related macular degeneration with conventional drusen (AMD-Drusen): Patients in this group demonstrated the presence of conventional drusen (≥ 63 μm in diameter) on structural OCT, with characteristic sub-RPE deposits appearing as hyperreflective nodular elevations [21, 22]. The diagnosis required the absence of subretinal drusenoid deposits (reticular pseudodrusen) and the presence of choroidal thinning compared to age-matched controls. Additional criteria included typical RPE changes, including geographic atrophy in advanced cases, and the characteristic fundoscopic appearance of yellowish deposits beneath the neurosensory retina.

Age-related macular degeneration with reticular pseudodrusen (AMD-RPD): The diagnosis of AMD with reticular pseudodrusen was established based on the presence of subretinal drusenoid deposits, identified as hyperreflective lesions located between the RPE and neurosensory retina on structural OCT [8, 23]. These deposits characteristically appeared as confluent hyperreflective material in the subretinal space, often with a reticular or pseudodrusen pattern on en face imaging. The diagnosis required choroidal thinning and typical fundoscopic features, including a reticular pattern of yellowish deposits most prominent in the superior macula. Patients were classified as GCL-IPL based on the predominant presence of subretinal drusenoid deposits. While concurrent small conventional drusen (< 63 μm) were permitted, patients with both large conventional drusen (≥ 63 μm) and RPD were excluded to maintain distinct diagnostic categories and avoid mixed phenotypes.

Healthy Controls

Healthy control subjects were rigorously selected based on comprehensive inclusion and exclusion criteria. Inclusion criteria were as follows: individuals aged 45–85 years with BCVA of 20/25 or better, normal intraocular pressure (< 21 mmHg), clear ocular media, and absence of any retinal pathology on comprehensive fundus examination. Control subjects underwent the same imaging protocol as study patients to establish normative parameters for age-stratified analysis.

Exclusion Criteria

The study implemented comprehensive exclusion criteria to ensure data integrity and eliminate potential confounding variables. Patients were systematically excluded if they presented with current or previous history of subretinal fluid, choroidal neovascularization, central serous chorioretinopathy, or other chorioretinal disorders that could potentially influence choroidal thickness or retinal layer measurements. Additional exclusion criteria encompassed previous ocular surgery (including cataract surgery within 6 months), intravitreal treatments, significant media opacities affecting image quality, refractive errors exceeding ±6.00 diopters, and axial length measurements outside the range of 22–26 mm.

Systemic exclusion criteria included patients presenting with pathologies that could potentially interfere with retinal or choroidal vascular dynamics, including uncontrolled systemic hypertension (> 160/100 mmHg), diabetes mellitus, autoimmune disorders, and current use of systemic medications known to affect choroidal circulation. Additional measures were implemented to exclude normal-tension glaucoma as a potential cause of neurodegeneration. All participants underwent comprehensive optic disc evaluation during dilated fundus examination to identify signs of glaucomatous optic neuropathy, including cup-to-disc ratio > 0.7, rim thinning, and notching. Patients with visual field defects consistent with glaucomatous patterns were excluded during preliminary screening. Additionally, image datasets were rigorously screened, with those exhibiting suboptimal signal quality (defined as a signal strength index below 8/10) or demonstrating substantial motion-related artifacts being systematically removed from the analytical protocol.

Swept-Source OCT Imaging Protocol

All imaging procedures were performed using the PLEX Elite 9000 SS-OCT system (Carl Zeiss Meditec, Inc., Dublin, CA, USA), which operates at a central wavelength of 1060 nm with an acquisition speed of 100,000 A-scans per second. The enhanced penetration capability of this system enabled optimal visualization of choroidal structures and precise measurement of choroidal thickness parameters.

The standardized imaging protocol included high-resolution 6 × 6 mm macular cube scans centered on the fovea. Each scan was acquired in duplicate to ensure reproducibility, and only scans with a signal strength index ≥ 8/10 and absence of significant motion artifacts were included in the analysis. The scanning protocol was performed under standardized conditions, including consistent ambient lighting and patient positioning, with pupillary dilation achieved using tropicamide 1% and phenylephrine 2.5%.

Image Analysis and Measurements

Retinal layer segmentation: Automated retinal layer segmentation was performed using the integrated software of the PLEX Elite 9000 system, with systematic manual verification and correction when necessary [24, 25]. The GCL-IPL complex was automatically delineated, and thickness measurements were extracted for both average and minimum values within the standard ETDRS grid pattern [24]. Choroidal measurements: Subfoveal choroidal thickness was measured manually using the integrated caliper tool, defined as the perpendicular distance from the hyperreflective line corresponding to Bruch’s membrane to the hyperreflective line representing the choroidal–scleral interface [26]. Measurements were performed at the exact foveal center, identified as the point of maximum depression in the foveal contour. Choroidal volume was calculated using the automated volumetric analysis software, encompassing the entire 6 × 6 mm scanning area [27, 28].

Quality control and reproducibility: All measurements were performed by two independent (PV and GB) certified OCT technicians who were masked to patient diagnoses and clinical information. Inter-observer reproducibility was assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC), with values > 0.90 considered excellent agreement. In cases of measurement discrepancy exceeding 5%, a third observer (FB) performed the measurement, and the median value was utilized for statistical analysis.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 28.0 (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA) and R statistical software version 4.3.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). The distribution normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test for sample sizes < 50 and the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test for larger samples, supplemented by visual inspection of Q-Q plots and histograms.

For normally distributed variables, between-group comparisons were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by post hoc Tukey’s honestly significant difference (HSD) test for multiple pairwise comparisons. For non-normally distributed variables, the Kruskal–Wallis test was employed, followed by Dunn’s test with Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons.

Age-adjusted analyses were conducted using analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) to control for potential age-related confounding effects. Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated to assess linear relationships between choroidal parameters and neuronal layer measurements within each diagnostic group. The strength of correlations was interpreted according to Cohen’s conventions: small (r = 0.10–0.29), medium (r = 0.30–0.49), and large (r ≥ 0.50). Statistical significance was set at p< 0.05 for all analyses, with Bonferroni correction applied for multiple comparisons where appropriate.

Results

Patient demographics and clinical characteristics

Our study cohort comprised 184 eyes from 184 patients, systematically stratified into five groups based on age-matching criteria and diagnostic classifications. The age-matching strategy successfully achieved comparable age distributions between disease groups and their respective controls, with young healthy controls (55.9 ± 4.5 years) appropriately matched with PPE patients (55.5 ± 4.4 years, p = 0.71), and elderly healthy controls (75.4 ± 4.5 years) matched with AMD drusen patients (70.9 ± 4.3 years) and GCL-IPL patients (75.6 ± 4.4 years). Gender distribution was balanced across all groups, with no significant differences observed (p = 0.89). Best-corrected visual acuity demonstrated the expected pattern of progressive impairment from controls through PPE to AMD variants, with GCL-IPL patients showing the most significant visual compromise (0.24 ± 0.22 LogMAR) compared to young controls (0.02 ± 0.05 LogMAR, p < 0.001). Spherical equivalent measurements confirmed the absence of significant refractive differences between groups (p = 0.23), validating our exclusion criteria for high myopia and ensuring that choroidal thickness variations were not confounded by axial length differences(Table 1). Pachychoroid Pigment Epitheliopathy

PPE patients demonstrated a distinctive pattern of neurodegeneration occurring in the context of marked choroidal thickening. Average GCL-IPL thickness in PPE patients was significantly reduced to 83.6 ± 5.1 μm compared to age-matched young controls (95.0 ± 3.0 μm, p 0.001), representing a 12% reduction in neuronal layer thickness. This neurodegeneration was even more pronounced when examining minimum GCL-IPL thickness, which decreased to 73.7 ± 6.0 μm compared to controls (87.6 ± 2.1 μm, p < 0.001), indicating focal areas of severe neuronal loss. Paradoxically, these patients exhibited substantial choroidal thickening, with subfoveal measurements reaching 410.5 ± 32.9 μm, representing a 34% increase compared to young controls (306.0 ± 10.8 μm, p < 0.001). The choroidal volume similarly increased to 13.2 ± 1.6 mm3 compared to 11.5 ± 0.6 mm3 in controls (p < 0.001) (Table 2). Age-Related Macular Degeneration

AMD patients, regardless of subtype, demonstrated the expected pattern of neurodegeneration accompanied by choroidal thinning, but with important differences in severity between drusen and reticular pseudodrusen variants. AMD drusen patients showed average GCL-IPL thickness of 78.1 ± 3.4 μm, representing a 14% reduction compared to age-matched elderly controls (90.5 ± 2.8 μm, p < 0.001). Minimum GCL-IPL thickness was more severely affected at 65.0 ± 3.9 μm compared to controls (84.4 ± 2.5 μm, p< 0.001). Concurrent choroidal changes included subfoveal thickness reduction to 238.0 ± 21.3 μm, representing a 14% decrease from elderly controls (276.8 ± 12.3 μm, p 0.001), with corresponding volume reduction to 8.0 ± 0.8 mm3 (controls: 10.1 ± 0.6 mm3, p < 0.001) (Table 2).

GCL-IPL patients demonstrated the most severe manifestation of both neurodegeneration and choroidal compromise. Average GCL-IPL thickness decreased to 75.4 ± 4.0 μm, representing a 17% reduction from age-matched controls, while minimum GCL-IPL showed profound thinning to 62.8 ± 5.0 μm ( p< 0.001). The choroidal architecture in RPD patients revealed the most dramatic changes, with subfoveal thickness severely reduced to 187.2 ± 20.5 μm, representing a 32% decrease from elderly controls (p < 0.001). Choroidal volume was correspondingly diminished to 6.7 ± 0.8 mm3, indicating substantial vascular compromise (Table 2).

Discussion

The present study provides compelling evidence for fundamentally distinct pathogenic mechanisms underlying neurodegeneration in PPE versus age-related macular degeneration variants, challenging traditional paradigms of choroidal–retinal relationships. Our findings demonstrate that while all three pathologic conditions result in significant inner retinal neurodegeneration compared to age-matched controls, the underlying vascular–neural coupling patterns are diametrically opposed, suggesting entirely different disease mechanisms that require tailored diagnostic and therapeutic approaches [15, 16, 29, 30].

The most striking finding of our investigation was the identification of strong inverse correlations between choroidal thickness and neuronal preservation in PPE patients. This counterintuitive relationship directly contradicts the traditional assumption that increased choroidal thickness provides enhanced vascular support for retinal function [31, 32]. Instead, our data suggest that the pathological choroidal thickening characteristic of PPE, while representing increased overall choroidal volume, paradoxically compromises retinal perfusion through a compressive mechanism. This finding aligns with emerging concepts in pachychoroid pathophysiology proposed by Warrow et al. [5], who first described the pachychoroid concept and suggested that dilated Haller layer vessels could compress the overlying choriocapillaris, leading to compromised perfusion despite increased choroidal volume. This compression theory is further supported by indocyanine green angiography findings in our PPE cohort, which consistently demonstrated areas of choroidal hyperpermeability corresponding to regions of pachyvessels and pachydrusen [33]. The hyperpermeability likely reflects compromised vascular integrity due to increased hydrostatic pressure within the compressed choriocapillaris, leading to breakdown of the blood–retinal barrier and subsequent inflammatory responses that could contribute to neurodegeneration [34]. Our quantitative correlation analysis provides the first direct evidence supporting this compressive hypothesis by demonstrating that greater choroidal thickening in PPE correlates with more severe neurodegeneration.

In stark contrast to PPE, both AMD variants demonstrated positive correlations between choroidal thickness and neuronal preservation, consistent with traditional ischemic disease models. These findings align with the extensive literature documenting choroidal thinning as a hallmark of AMD progression [35, 36]. Manjunath et al. [9] first demonstrated, using enhanced depth imaging OCT, that AMD patients consistently exhibit choroidal thinning compared to age-matched controls, with more advanced disease stages showing greater choroidal compromise. Our correlation analysis extends these observations by demonstrating that the degree of choroidal thinning directly predicts the severity of neurodegeneration, supporting an ischemic pathogenic model wherein progressive choroidal atrophy leads to hypoperfusion and subsequent neuronal loss.

The particularly strong correlations observed in AMD RPD patients deserve special consideration, as this variant has been increasingly recognized as a more aggressive form of AMD, with accelerated progression to advanced disease stages. Zweifel et al.[8] demonstrated that reticular pseudodrusen are associated with more severe choroidal thinning and higher rates of progression to geographic atrophy compared to conventional drusen. This suggests that RPD represents a phenotype of AMD characterized by particularly severe vascular compromise, leading to pronounced ischemia and accelerated neurodegeneration [23, 37, 38]. The mechanistic basis for this enhanced vascular compromise in RPD likely involves the unique location of subretinal drusenoid deposits, which form between the neurosensory retina and RPE rather than beneath Bruch’s membrane as in conventional drusen. This anatomical difference may more directly compromise the metabolic support systems for photoreceptors and downstream neurons, explaining the enhanced neurodegeneration observed in this variant [39].

The age-matching strategy employed in our study was crucial for interpreting our findings, as both choroidal thickness and GCL-IPL thickness are known to decrease with normal aging. By comparing PPE patients to young controls and AMD patients to elderly controls, we were able to isolate the pathological changes from physiological age-related variations. This approach revealed that the neurodegeneration observed in all three pathologic conditions significantly exceeds normal age-related changes, confirming that these represent true disease processes rather than accelerated aging.

Despite these strengths, our study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. The retrospective design limits our ability to control for all potential confounding variables, and the cross-sectional nature precludes assessment of temporal relationships or disease progression patterns. While we excluded patients with high myopia and other conditions that might affect choroidal thickness, subtle variations in axial length or other unmeasured factors could still influence our measurements. The correlation analysis, while revealing distinct patterns, cannot definitively establish causality. Alternative explanations for the inverse correlations could include inflammatory mechanisms, altered metabolic demands, or other vascular regulatory factors that warrant future investigation. Moreover, while our ICC values (> 0.90) indicate excellent inter-observer agreement for choroidal thickness measurements, the manual nature of these measurements introduces inherent variability that could influence correlation analyses. Additionally, our study focused on structural OCT parameters and did not include functional assessments such as electrophysiology or microperimetry, which might provide additional insights into the relationship between structural changes and functional outcomes.

Future research directions emerging from this work include longitudinal studies to assess temporal relationships and disease progression patterns, functional studies to correlate structural changes with visual outcomes, and therapeutic trials targeting the specific mechanisms identified in each disease type. The development of artificial intelligence approaches for automated correlation analysis could facilitate the clinical implementation of these findings, while advanced imaging techniques such as OCT angiography might provide additional insights into the microvascular changes underlying our observed correlations.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study provides compelling evidence for distinct pathogenic mechanisms underlying neurodegeneration in PPE versus AMD variants. The identification of inverse correlations in PPE suggests a novel compressive mechanism wherein choroidal thickening paradoxically compromises retinal function, while positive correlations in AMD variants support traditional ischemic models of disease pathogenesis. These findings challenge conventional assumptions about choroidal–retinal relationships and provide new insights into disease mechanisms that could guide the development of targeted therapeutic approaches. The correlation patterns identified in our study represent potential biomarkers for disease classification, prognosis, and treatment monitoring, offering practical clinical applications beyond their mechanistic significance.
