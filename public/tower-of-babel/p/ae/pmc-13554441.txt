Mechanistic elucidation of Panax ginseng in improving atherosclerosis via multidimensional artificial intelligence computing coupled with experimental validation
Authors: Lei Gu, Heng Li, Yi Hu, Junde Fan, Lei Yan, Qingping Xiong, Liwei Li, Yifei Li, Lin Bo, Xuemei Sui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAtherosclerosis (AS) is the core pathological foundation of cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases, and its efficient prevention is pivotal to lowering related mortality and disability. Panax ginseng confers remarkable cardiovascular protection, whereas its anti-atherosclerotic efficacy and intrinsic mechanisms remain poorly elucidated, restricting its clinical translation. This study sought to decipher the mechanism underlying Panax ginseng mediated AS improvement via multidimensional artificial intelligence (AI) computation integrated with experimental validation.MethodsUPLC-Q-TOF-MS/MS was employed to characterize Panax ginseng chemical constituents. Multidimensional AI computation strategies, including differential expression analysis, weighted gene co-expression network analysis, machine learning, coupled with transcriptome and database mining were utilized to screen core anti-AS targets. Meanwhile, the molecular docking, molecular dynamics simulation, cellular thermal shift assay and in vivo experiments combined with multivariate statistical analysis were performed for target verification.Results55 compounds from Panax ginseng and 117 anti-AS potential targets were identified. The RXRA was recognized as the pivotal hub target via machine learning. The 14 components in Panax ginseng displayed stable binding with RXRA in molecular docking, dynamics simulations and cellular thermal shift assay. Ultimately, the experiments in vivo confirmed that Adenine, a representative compound of Panax ginseng, alleviated AS via regulating RXRA expression.ConclusionPanax ginseng exerts anti-atherosclerotic effects by targeting RXRA, with 14 active compounds (e.g., Adenine) serving as the key material basis for its pharmacological activity.Keywords: Panax ginseng, Atherosclerosis, Computational pharmacology, Mechanism of action

Introduction

Atherosclerosis (AS), the primary pathological basis underlying life-threatening cardiovascular and cerebrovascular events including myocardial infarction and stroke, represents a substantial global socioeconomic burden owing to its persistently high morbidity and mortality rates [1]. AS is a prototypical chronic inflammatory vascular disease, whose initiation and progression involve multiple intricate and interwoven pathological processes, such as lipid metabolic dysregulation [2], endothelial dysfunction [3], immune cell infiltration [4], and enhanced plaque vulnerability [5]. These processes are synergistically modulated by pro-inflammatory cytokines, inflammatory signaling cascades, bioactive lipids, and cellular adhesion molecules [6]. Lipid-lowering medicines, particularly statin-based regimens, are the cornerstone of clinical atherosclerosis prevention and treatment [7]. Nevertheless, their clinical applicability is severely constrained by prominent limitations including statin intolerance in a subset of patients, residual cardiovascular risk, and potential adverse reactions linked to long-term administration [8]. Accordingly, developing novel therapeutic strategies with multi-target regulatory properties to effectively intervene in the complex pathological network of AS has become a pivotal and urgent scientific challenge in contemporary cardiovascular research.

Given the multifactorial and complex pathogenesis of AS, traditional natural Chinese medicine products exhibit distinct benefits in intervening in such chronic refractory diseases, relying on the holistic regulatory theory of “multi-component, multi-target, multi-pathway” combination [9]. As a renowned and precious traditional natural Chinese medicine herb, Panax ginseng has attracted extensive research interest owing to its varied pharmacological properties. Contemporary pharmacological research has shown that Panax ginseng extracts and their principal active constituents (e.g., ginsenosides) provide preventive and therapeutic benefits against cardiovascular disorders, including ischemia-reperfusion injury, AS, and heart failure, primarily by enhancing energy metabolism, inhibiting excessive autophagy, demonstrating antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties, and facilitating exosome secretion [10]. For instance, Zhao et al. revealed that Panax notoginseng saponins, the pivotal active components of Panax ginseng, attenuate atherosclerosis by suppressing USP2-mediated Keap1 deubiquitination, accelerating Keap1 degradation, subsequently activating Nrf2 signaling, optimizing iron metabolism, and reducing lipid peroxidation [11]. Zheng et al. further verified that ginseng oligopeptide extract effectively reversed the decrease in cell viability and protected human aortic endothelial cells against nicotine-induced oxidative damage by inhibiting intracellular excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) production and preserving superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity, thereby exerting anti-atherosclerotic effects [12]. Despite accumulating phenotypic evidence, however, critical knowledge gaps remain regarding the exact pharmacodynamic material basis, core molecular targets, and systematic regulatory network of Panax ginseng against AS. Such ambiguity in fundamental mechanisms drastically impedes the modernization and precise clinical translation of Panax ginseng.

The conventional network pharmacology approach, which relies heavily on static databases, often struggles to identify core targets within intricate biological networks due to its limited dynamic and contextual analytical capabilities [13]. Moreover, even when potential targets are screened, traditional methods frequently lack robust and efficient validation strategies. Fortunately, the advancement of multi-dimensional artificial intelligence (AI) offers a promising solution by integrating transcriptomic analysis, machine learning, virtual screening and computer simulation verification. Multi-dimensional AI, as an evolving multidisciplinary area, integrates computational modelling with bioinformatics to swiftly and accurately identify core molecular targets and conduct subsequent validation, thereby markedly improving the precision and efficiency of network pharmacology research. For instance, Zheng et al. employed integrated computational virtual screening to identify three core genes (BTK, P2RX7, and PREPL) associated with mitochondrial function and AS, and screened nine compounds from Buyang Huanwu Decoction that alleviate AS by improving mitochondrial function [14]. Similarly, Li et al. utilized multi-dimensional AI to elucidate the toxicological mechanisms induced by nicotine exposure and identified antagonists derived from the active components of Dendrobium officinale [8]. These advancements underscore the transformative potential of multi-dimensional AI in overcoming the inherent constraints of traditional network pharmacology.

This study employed a multi-dimensional AI-driven computational pharmacology strategy integrated with experimental validation to systematically elucidate the mechanism by which key active constituents of Panax ginseng alleviate AS through the modulation of core molecular targets, as illustrated in Fig. 1. Specifically, comprehensive chemical profiling of Panax ginseng was first conducted using UPLC-Q-TOF-MS/MS, followed by the prediction of its compound-target interaction network. Subsequently, these compound-related targets were integrated with AS-associated genes derived from multi-omics analyses, and a multi-model machine learning framework was applied to precisely identify and prioritize the core therapeutic targets of Panax ginseng against AS from the extensive candidate pool. Molecular docking and dynamics simulations were then performed to screen and validate potential active components capable of effectively interacting with the prioritized core targets. Finally, the in vivo pharmacodynamic effects of the screened core active components were evaluated in an ApoE−/− mice model of AS to verify whether their anti-atherosclerotic efficacy was mediated through the regulation of the predicted core targets.

Experimental materials and methods

Materials and reagents

The Panax ginseng used in this study was purchased from Guilin Zhongnan (Bozhou) Pharmaceutical Technology Co., Ltd. The raw material was originally cultivated in Baishan, Jilin Province, China, and consisted of 4-year-old plants. The botanical identity and quality of the Panax ginseng were authenticated by Professor Qingping Xiong from Huai'an University. The sample was confirmed to meet the quality standards specified in the Chinese Pharmacopoeia (2025 edition). Atorvastatin (purity ≥98%) was provided by Pfizer Inc., while adenine was supplied by Shanghai Kanglang Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Assay kits for total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), and high-density lipoprotein (HDL) were provided by Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute. The hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining reagents was obtained from Nanjing Fuyi Technology Development Co., Ltd. All primary and secondary antibodies were sourced from Abcam Ltd. (Cambridge, UK). All other reagents utilized in the UPLC-Q-TOF-MS/MS investigations were of chromatographic quality.

Preparation of Panax ginseng powder

A test solution for UPLC-Q-TOF-MS/MS analysis was prepared to identify the chemical constituents of Panax ginseng for subsequent target prediction. The roots of Panax ginseng were pulverised into a fine powder and filtered through a 60-mesh sieve. The quality of the Panax ginseng powder was assessed by high performance liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry following a previously established method [15].

Chemical profiling and target prediction of Panax ginseng

UPLC-Q-TOF-MS/MS analytical conditions

Chromatographic separation was performed on a Waters Acquity HSS T3 column (2.1 mm × 150 mm, 1.8 μm) maintained at 35 °C, using a mobile phase consisting of water containing 0.1% formic acid and acetonitrile delivered at a rate of movement of 0.3 mL/min with an injection volume of 4 μL under the gradual elution program detailed in Table S1. A SCIEX Exion liquid chromatography system and an X500B Q-TOF mass spectrometer (AB Sciex, USA) with an electrospray ionisation (ESI) source that operates in both positive and negative ion modes were used to perform mass spectrometric analysis. The mass range was set at m/z 100-1250, and detailed parameters are provided in Table S2. SCIEX OS software was used for data processing and acquisition.

UPLC-Q-TOF-MS/MS analysis of Panax ginseng sample solution

Panax ginseng solution was prepared using the previously outlined procedures. In short, 0.1 g of precisely weighed Panax ginseng powder was put into an extraction vessel and extracted for 30 min using 10 mL of 70% methanol while being ultrasonicated. To guarantee thorough extraction, this process was carried out twice. After gathering the combined supernatants, 2 mL of the pooled supernatant was concentrated under a nitrogen stream till all methanol evaporated, and then it was regenerated in pure water. A C18 solid-phase extraction (SPE) column was then filled with 1 mL of the reconstituted solution. One millilitre of filtered water was used to wash the column, and the washing solution was thrown away. To guarantee full recovery, the target compounds were then eluted using 1.5-2.0 mL of methanol, and the eluate was gathered for additional processing. After centrifuging the collected eluate for 15 min at 13,000 r/min, 400 μL of the supernatant was moved to a clean tube and dried under nitrogen. The dried residue was reconstituted in 200 μL of 50% acetonitrile solution prior to LC-MS analysis, and any particle matter was then removed by additional centrifugation at 13,000 r/min for 10 min. The chemical components of Panax ginseng were then determined by injecting a suitable quantity of the supernatant into the LC-MS instrument for assessment.

Target prediction of Panax ginseng components

The potential targets of Panax ginseng components were predicted through a systematic computational approach. First, the chemical names of Panax ginseng constituents identified by mass spectrometry were searched in PubChem (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) to obtain their corresponding SMILES notations. These SMILES strings were sent to the SwissTargetPrediction site (https://www.swisstargetprediction.ch/) for target forecasting, with the species parameter set to “Homo sapiens.” Only targets with prediction probability exceeding 0 were selected as possible biological targets for the Panax ginseng components, ensuring a focused and relevant target pool for subsequent analysis. This computational strategy provided a comprehensive and evidence-based approach to identify the potential molecular targets of Panax ginseng's bioactive constituents.

Acquisition of AS-related targets

Identification of AS-related targets through differential expression analysis

In this study, the gene expression dataset GSE100927 related to AS was retrieved from the GEO database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/), which includes samples from both AS patients and healthy controls for subsequent differential expression analysis. The expression data were standardised via the avereps function from the limma package to remove technical batch effects and systematic variations between samples. Differential expression analysis was subsequently conducted with the limma package by first constructing a linear model that classified samples into control and treat groups, followed by setting up a contrast matrix to analyze gene expression differences between different groups. The model was further refined using the empirical Bayes adjustment via the eBayes function to enhance the statistical power of the analysis. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified based on the criteria of |logFC| > 0.5 and adj.P.Val <0.05, with the false discovery rate (FDR) approach utilized for hypothesis correction. Overall, the analysis employed a linear model combined with empirical Bayes moderation, and significant genes were screened using the dual criteria of logFC and adj.P.Val.

Identification of disease modules’ genes via WGCNA

The expression data were first standardised via the normalizeBetweenArrays function from the limma package to remove technical batch effects, and duplicate gene probes were averaged using the avereps function. The goodSamplesGenes function from the WGCNA package was employed to perform quality control on samples and genes, removing those with excessively low expression or excessive missing values. The qualified expression matrix was then transposed and subjected to hierarchical clustering to generate a sample clustering dendrogram. A scale-free weighted gene co-expression network was constructed using the WGCNA package, where the optimal soft thresholding power was selected from a series of candidate values via the pickSoftThreshold function. Using the chosen soft threshold, the adjacency matrix and topological overlap matrix (TOM) were calculated. Gene co-expression modules were initially identified using the dynamic tree cut technique and gene hierarchical clustering based on TOM dissimilarity (1-TOM). Subsequently, module eigengenes were computed, and highly correlated modules were merged based on the correlation of their eigengenes to simplify the network structure and enhance biological interpretability. Pearson correlation analysis was conducted between the clinical trait information and the module eigengenes to assess module-trait relationships and corresponding P-values, visualized via a heatmap to illustrate the association strength and statistical significance of each module with the AS phenotype. To identify key genes highly associated with AS, gene significance (GS) and module membership (MM) were computed. Genes exhibiting both high GS and high MM within the relevant modules were considered core members and were selected as potential key targets for AS.

Identification of AS-related targets via integration of different methods

The final collection of genes linked to AS was determined by combining genes identified through differential expression analysis with those acquired from WGCNA. This integration of different methods was achieved by means of R language (version 4.4.2) by merging the gene lists from both analyses, thereby consolidating the DEGs and the key co-expressed module genes into a comprehensive candidate gene set for subsequent functional enrichment and validation studies.

Investigation of core targets for Panax ginseng against AS

Identification of potential anti-atherosclerotic targets of Panax ginseng

The intersection of previously identified Panax ginseng-related targets and AS-associated targets was analyzed to find prospective targets of Panax ginseng against AS. This operation was achieved by means of R language (version 4.4.2) to compute the common gene set between the two groups. The resulting overlapping targets were considered as the potential anti-AS targets of Panax ginseng and were used for enrichment analysis and further screening.

Functional enrichment analysis of potential targets

The Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) and Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analyses were carried out through the clusterProfiler package in R language (version 4.4.2) to explore the biological roles and processes of the possible targets of Panax ginseng against AS. Potential target gene symbols were first converted to ENTREZID format via the org.Hs.eg.db database. GO enrichment analysis encompassed three ontology categories: biological process (BP), cellular component (CC) and molecular function (MF), while KEGG enrichment analysis was carried out through Homo sapiens as the model species. The statistically significant criteria were set as an adjusted P-value <0.05 and a Q-value <0.05. For visualization, the top 10 most significant terms from each GO category (P.adjust) and the top 10 pathways with the highest gene counts from the KEGG analysis were selected and merged into a combined data frame. A nested-axis dot plot was generated using ggplot2 and related extension packages, in which different ontology categories were distinguished by distinct color gradients, dot size represented the amount of concentrated genes, and color intensity indicated the P.adjust value.

Screening of core targets based on machine learning

Multiple machine learning methods were used for modelling and assessment for the purpose to fine the core targets of Panax ginseng against AS from the potential targets. First, the gene expression matrix of candidate targets was integrated with sample grouping information and arbitrarily divided into a training set and a test set. Model training and evaluation were conducted using the caret package in R language (version 4.4.2), with a random seed set to 123 to ensure reproducibility. A 5-fold repeated cross-validation strategy was performed to optimize model parameters. Seven classical machine learning models were constructed and compared, including Random Forest (RF), Support Vector Machine with a radial basis function kernel (SVM), Generalized Linear Model (GLM), K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN), Neural Network (NNET), Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO), and Decision Tree (DT). RF utilized the “rf” method from the caret package, constructing 500 decision trees (ntree = 500) and automatically optimizing the number of features selected per split (mtry) via cross-validation. SVM employed the radial basis kernel function (“svmRadial”) with its cost parameter (C) and kernel width (sigma) optimized through cross-validation, and probability prediction was enabled (prob.model = TRUE). GLM implemented logistic regression (“glm”) specified with a binomial distribution (family = “binomial”) for binary classification without hyperparameter tuning. KNN used the “knn” algorithm, where the optimal number of neighbors (k) was automatically selected via 5-fold cross-validation; NNET applied a single-hidden-layer feedforward network (“nnet”), with the number of hidden neurons (size) and weight decay (decay) optimized through cross-validation. LASSO regression employed the “glmnet” method with alpha = 1 for L1 regularization, and the regularization strength (lambda) was automatically optimized via cross-validation to generate a sparse model. And DT utilized the CART algorithm (“rpart”), with the complexity parameter (cp) optimized through cross-validation for automatic pruning. The DALEX package was used for interpretability analysis of each model, calculating variable importance to assess the contribution of each gene to model predictions. Model performance was comprehensively evaluated using residual distribution plots, boxplots, histograms, and Precision-Recall Curves generated by the model performance function. Additionally, the effectiveness of each model's classification was assessed by displaying receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and computing the area under the curve (AUC). Finally, based on variable importance rankings and model consistency, genes identified as key predictive factors across multiple models were selected as the final core targets of Panax ginseng against AS.

Virtual screening and validation of Panax ginseng-derived anti-atherosclerotic key active ingredients

Molecular docking for key active ingredients screening

The 2D models of Panax ginseng active compounds were collected from the PubChem (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) and subsequently submitted to energy minimization by Chem3D software (version 22.0). The 3D crystal models of the target proteins were acquired from the PDB database (https://www.rcsb.org/), and all water atoms, irrelevant ligands, and non-essential ions were eliminated using PyMOL software (version 4.6.0). The purified protein structures were then constructed with AutoDock Tools 1.5.7, which included adding hydrogen atoms, assigning charges, and setting atom types. The grid box was established within the known location of activity to cover key residues and ensure sufficient degrees of freedom for docking. Semi-flexible molecular docking was performed using AutoDock Vina, with each ligand-receptor pair subjected to 50 independent docking runs to thoroughly explore the conformational binding space. The search exhaustiveness was set to 32 to balance computational efficiency with result reliability. Ten output conformations were retained to ensure sufficient conformational diversity, and an energy range of 3 kcal/mol was applied to filter for meaningful binding poses. The conformation with the lowest binding energy was extracted and visualized in three dimensions using PyMOL 4.6.0 to analyze key interactions, such as hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic interactions, between the ligand and the target protein.

Molecular dynamics simulation for binding validation

Based on the molecular docking scores between the core targets and the active components of Panax ginseng, compounds with docking scores lower than −5 kcal/mol were selected as potential active ingredients of Panax ginseng against AS [16]. To validate the reliability of this screening, the compound with a docking score closest to −5 kcal/mol was subjected to molecular dynamics simulation for comprehensive evaluation of its anti-AS potential. Molecular dynamics simulations were conducted using GROMACS 2020.3. The model box was tuned to provide a distance larger than 1.0 nm among protein atoms and the box borders, and the system energy was lowered to reduce atomic collisions. The system was equilibrated under NVT and NPT ensembles, before performing by a production run of 100 ns. Temperature and pressure were adjusted using the V-rescale and Parrinello-Rahman techniques, accordingly. The Lennard-Jones function was used to compute van der Waals interactions using a non-bonded cutoff of 1.4 nm. Bond lengths for all atoms were restricted by the LINCS method. Electrostatic interactions were estimated using the Particle Mesh Ewald method with a Fourier spacing of 0.16 nm. The binding free energy was computed using the gmx_MMPBSA tool to fully characterize the changing instability of the complex, and integrated Gromacs analysis modules were used to evaluate system parameters like root mean square deviation (RMSD), solvent accessible surface area (SASA), root mean square fluctuation (RMSF), free energy landscape (FEL), and radius of gyration (Rg). The simulation system was considered stable and the binding conformation reliable when RMSD, SASA, and Rg values converged to stable plateaus and RMSF at the binding site showed significant reduction.

Cellular thermal shift assay (CETSA)

RAW264.7 foam cells were successfully constructed via ox-LDL induction [17]. The cell lysates of modeled foam cells were collected and divided into two groups: the control group received no drug treatment, while the experimental group was incubated with 5 μM adenine at room temperature for 1 h. Subsequently, the lysates from both groups were subjected to thermal denaturation at gradient temperatures of 42, 45, 48, 51, 54, and 57 °C for 5 min, followed by natural cooling at room temperature to terminate the reaction. After treatment, all samples were centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C, and the supernatants containing soluble proteins were harvested. The residual soluble RXRA protein levels in supernatants at different temperatures were determined by Western blot. The gray values of protein bands were quantified using Image J software. After internal reference calibration and normalization, the remaining percentages of soluble RXRA protein at each temperature point were calculated. The CETSA curves were plotted with temperature as the independent variable and the residual ratio of soluble RXRA protein as the dependent variable. The melting temperature (Tm) values of RXRA protein in the control and adenine-treated groups were obtained via nonlinear fitting, and the Tm difference (ΔTm) between the two groups was calculated to evaluate the binding capacity of adenine to RXRA protein.

In vivo animal experimentation

Establishment of the atherosclerotic mice model

36 female ApoE−/− mice (aged 7-8 weeks, body weight 18-22 g) were obtained from Jiangsu Jicui Yaokang Biotechnology Co., Ltd. and kept in a specific pathogen-free (SPF) animal facility. The study protocol received approval from the Animal Ethics Committee of Huai'an University (Approval No. 202419). The animals were kept in controlled environments with unlimited access to food and water. Mice were randomly assigned to either a model duplication group (MDG, n = 27) or a standard control group (SCG, n = 9) for AS induction after a week of acclimatisation. Throughout the modeling phase, SCG animals were provided with a standard chow diet, whereas MDG mice were fed a high-fat diet as described in our earlier work [18]. After 12 weeks of dietary induction, three mice from each of the SCG and MDG groups were randomly euthanized for hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining to verify successful AS lesion formation.

After validation, the remaining 24 mice from the MDG mice were distributed at random to four experimental groups: model group (MG, n = 6), adenine low-dose group (LG, n = 6), adenine high-dose group (HG, n = 6), and positive control group (PG, n = 6). The remaining mice from the SCG group were designated as the control group (CG, n = 6). Throughout the subsequent 12-week intervention phase, all groups continued to receive the same diets as during the model induction period. Daily oral gavage was administered at a volume of 10 mL/kg. Mice in the LG and HG respectively received adenine at doses of 19.5 μg/kg/day and 58.5 μg/kg/day (Calculations are made based on the clinical dosage and the Adenine content in Panax ginseng) [19]. The PG was treated with atorvastatin at 10 mg/kg/day [20], while both the CG and MG were given an equivalent volume of normal saline.

Histopathological sampling and analysis

Mice were put to sleep with diethyl ether 24 h after the last dose of medication. Retro-orbital punctures were used to obtain blood samples, and centrifugation was used to separate the serum. Serum levels of TC and HDL were determined using commercially available assay kits. Following blood collection, animals were euthanized via cervical dislocation. After meticulous dissection, the aortic arch tissue (around 1 cm upstream section) was frozen in 4% paraformaldehyde for at least 24 h. At the same time, a cryostat microtome was used to prepare serial cross-sections of the aortic sinus while tissue samples were immersed in OCT compound. These sections were coated with H&E for histological analysis. An optical microscope (Olympus BX53) and a polarised light microscope (Nikon Eclipse Ci-POL) were used to view and photograph each stained segment. Plaque components were quantified following previously established protocols from our laboratory [8]. ImageJ software (version 1.8.0) was used for statistical analysis. The atherosclerosis index and vulnerability index were computed via techniques previously reported by our study group so as to assess the interventional impact of adenine on AS [20,21].

Fluorescence staining of atherosclerotic vasculature

Aortic tissues from mice were collected, rinsed with pre-cooled PBS buffer, and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h at 4 °C. Following gradient dehydration (70%-100% ethanol), xylene clearing, and paraffin embedding, 5 μm-thick sections were prepared. Sections were deparaffinized in xylene, rehydrated using a graded ethanol series (100%-70%), and then washed with distilled water in preparation for immunofluorescence staining. After 20 min of antigen retrieval in citrate buffer (pH 6.0) at 95 °C, the mixture was naturally cooled to room temperature. To lessen non-specific binding, sections were blocked with PBS containing 5% BSA at room temperature for 1 h after being permeabilized with PBS containing 0.3% Triton X-100 for 15 min. The primary antibody targeting the core protein Retinoic acid receptor RXR-alpha (RXRA) was diluted 1:200 in blocking buffer and incubated with the sections in a conditioned room at 4 °C for one night. Sections were then incubated with an Alexa Fluor-conjugated secondary antibody at room temperature for 30 min in the shadows following three PBS washes of 5 min each. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI for 5 min in the dark after three additional PBS washes of 5 min each. Sections were fixed with an anti-fade mounting buffer and viewed with a Pannoramic MIDI scanning confocal microscope, with similar collection parameters (laser power, gain, exposure time) applied to all samples. Integrated fluorescence density was quantified by measuring the mean gray value within standardized regions of interest (ROIs) in the AS plaques using ImageJ software. Three ROIs per section and three sections per sample were analyzed [22].

Multivariate statistical analysis

This study used multivariate statistical techniques to thoroughly assess the inherent correlations in the experimental data. Protein expression and efficacy assessment data from various groups were first compared to those of the MG, normalized, and then thoroughly statistically analyzed. Intra-group relationships were investigated using hierarchical clustering analysis and stacked bar graphs. Furthermore, intergroup interactions and the correlations between drug efficacy and protein levels were compared using Pearson correlation analysis.

Data statistics and analysis

GraphPad Prism 9.0 (GraphPad Software, USA) was used for statistical analysis. The mean ± standard deviation (SD) is used to display measurement data. ANOVA, or one-way analysis of variance, was employed to compare several groups. The Pearson correlation coefficient was used to perform correlation analysis. P < 0.05 was deemed statistically significant in all two-sided statistical tests.

Results

The main active components of Panax ginseng and predicted potential targets

UPLC-Q-TOF-MS/MS analysis of Panax ginseng active components

The chemical profiling of Panax ginseng extract was systematically performed using UPLC-Q-TOF-MS/MS in both positive and negative ion modes. Based on characteristic fragment ions and specific cleavage patterns of diverse structural types, a total of 55 compounds were identified in the present study (Table S3). The base peak chromatograms illustrating the chemical complexity of the extract were presented in Fig. 2A and B. This comprehensive profile includes 33 triterpenoid saponins, which constitute the most characteristic and predominant chemical class represented by various ginsenosides and their derivatives, 7 flavonoids known for their antioxidant bioactivities, 6 water-soluble amino acids and nucleosides, and 9 other compounds such as saccharides and secondary metabolites (Fig. 2C and D).

Target prediction for Panax ginseng components

The SMILES of 55 identified compounds were retrieved from the PubChem database and subsequently submitted to the SwissTargetPrediction for target prediction. As shown in Fig. 2E and Table S4, the prediction yielded 836 high-confidence potential targets, each with a predicted probability score exceeding 0. These predicted targets encompass a broad spectrum of protein classes, including kinases, enzymes, and nuclear receptors, providing a foundational dataset for the subsequent screening of anti-AS targets.

Screening of AS-related targets

Identification of AS-related targets via differential expression analysis

Transcriptomic data from the GEO dataset (GSE100927) were analyzed to find genes associated with AS. Differential expression analysis between AS patients and healthy controls revealed 1616 genes that were significantly dysregulated (Table S5), with an adj.P.Val <0.05 and an |logFC| > 0.5. Fig. 3A illustrated the distinct expression patterns of these DEGs across samples, while Fig. 3B visually summarized the statistical significance versus magnitude of change, clearly distinguishing the 998 up-regulated and 618 down-regulated gene clusters.

Identification of AS-related targets via WGCNA

WGCNA was conducted to identify genes with coordinated expression patterns potentially involved in AS progression. After quality control of 30,558 genes across 63 samples, including the removal of outliers and low-expression genes, the scale-free topology criterion was applied to determine the optimal soft-thresholding power (β = 8) (Fig. 3D and E), which was used to construct the co-expression network. Hierarchical clustering based on TOM dissimilarity generated nine distinct gene modules, as color-coded in the dendrogram (Fig. 3F). Correlation analysis between module genes and clinical characteristics revealed that the MEturquoise module exhibited the most significant association with the AS phenotype (|cor| = 0.86, P = 3e-19) (Fig. 3G). Within this module, a strong positive correlation was observed between MM and GS (cor = 0.91, P < 1e-200), indicating that the module is highly enriched for genes functionally relevant to AS (Fig. 3H). A total of 888 genes were extracted from this key module for subsequent analysis (Table S6).

Identification result of AS-related targets via integration of different approaches

The study combined the results from differential expression analysis and WGCNA to obtain a more comprehensive and reliable set of AS-related genes. As illustrated in the Venn diagram (Fig. 3C), the union of the 1616 DEGs and the 888 MEturquoise module genes yielded a final candidate gene set comprising 1778 unique genes, which were designated as AS-related targets (Table S7). This integrated approach ensured the inclusion of both statistically significant DEGs and genes embedded in a biologically coherent co-expression network, thereby strengthening the reliability of subsequent target screening.

Identification of core targets for Panax ginseng against AS

Potential anti-atherosclerotic targets of Panax ginseng

Through intersection of 1778 AS-associated candidate genes and 836 computationally predicted targets of Panax ginseng's major bioactive compounds, this study identified 117 high-confidence candidate genes as potential anti-atherosclerotic targets (Table S8, Fig. 4A). Additionally, a protein-protein interaction (PPI) network that elucidated the functional connections between these potential targets was created through the STRING database. The resulting network (Fig. 4B) exhibited a high degree of connectivity, indicating that these targets are not isolated but likely function in coordinated biological pathways.

Functional enrichment analysis

GO and KEGG enrichment studies were carried out to clarify the biological roles of the 117 possible targets (Table S9 and Table S10). GO analysis indicated high enrichment of these targets in BP such as response to light stimulus, regulation of protein localization to cell periphery, and positive regulation of leukocyte mediated immunity. CC analysis revealed their enrichment in secretory granule membrane and focal adhesion, while MF analysis highlighted associations with protein-folding chaperone binding and phospholipid binding. KEGG pathway analysis further emphasized the significant involvement of these targets in key pathways including Lipid and atherosclerosis, MAPK signaling pathway, and PI3K-Akt signaling pathway (Fig. 4C). These results suggest that Panax ginseng may modulate AS-related processes through mechanisms involving immune regulation, lipid metabolism, and critical signal transduction pathways.

Identification of core targets via machine learning

A multi-model machine learning approach was employed to further refine the target list and identify the most critical core targets. To distinguish AS samples from controls, seven different classification models (RF, SVM, GLM, KNN, NNET, LASSO, and DT) were built using the expression profiles of the 117 possible targets. The specific screening results are shown in Table S11. The performance of these models was rigorously evaluated. The distribution of residuals and the precision-recall curves indicated that the RF, LASSO, and SVM models achieved the most robust and stable predictive performance (Fig. 4D–G). The variable importance analysis derived from the RF, SVM, and LASSO models ranked all candidate genes according to their contribution to classification performance. Among these models, RXRA consistently ranked at the top in feature importance and showed strong discriminative ability in distinguishing AS samples from controls. Although other models (GLM, KNN, NNET, and DT) exhibited relatively lower stability and predictive performance, the convergence of feature ranking across the three best-performing models supported the prioritization of RXRA as a core regulatory target in AS (Fig. 4H). The high discriminatory power of these models was further confirmed by ROC curves, with AUC values of 0.979 (RF), 1.000 (SVM), and 0.986 (LASSO) (Fig. 4I). Based on this comprehensive machine learning analysis, RXRA was identified as the primary core target for further investigation.

Identification and validation of key anti-atherosclerotic ingredients from Panax ginseng

Molecular docking screening

Molecular docking was performed between the core target RXRA (PDB ID: 5K13) and the 55 identified active components of Panax ginseng to screen for potential compounds capable of modulating RXRA activity. The docking scores for all compounds are visualized in a heatmap (Fig. 5A). Compounds with docking scores lower than −5 kcal/mol were selected as final candidates, yielding a total of 14 compounds (Table S12). Among these, Adenine exhibited a binding energy closest to the threshold (ΔG = −5.873 kcal/mol), indicating strong interaction potential, and was therefore selected for subsequent molecular dynamics simulation and experimental validation. This selection was based on its stable binding conformation within the RXRA active pocket, favorable docking score, and well-characterized chemical structure that facilitates biological interpretation. The stable docking conformation revealed by the 3D binding pose and 2D interaction diagram (Fig. 5B and C) shows that Adenine occupies a deep hydrophobic pocket within the ligand-binding domain of RXRA, forming specific interactions with surrounding residues such as PHE A:286, ILE A:273, ARG A:276, and LEU A:269, primarily mediated by hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic contacts. These results suggest that the screened compounds, including Adenine, may alleviate AS by binding to the core target RXRA.

Molecular dynamics simulation validation

After successful molecular docking, a 100 ns molecular dynamics simulation was performed to evaluate the stability of the RXRA–Adenine complex. The RMSD of the RXRA protein backbone was used to assess the overall structural stability of the protein during ligand binding. As shown in Fig. 5D, the RMSD of the RXRA backbone stabilized after approximately 40 ns, with an average value of 1.52 Å, indicating that the protein–ligand complex reached equilibrium and maintained structural stability.

The RMSF was calculated at the residue level of RXRA to evaluate the flexibility of individual amino acids during ligand binding. As shown in Fig. 5E, residue fluctuations ranged from 1.67 to 33.01 Å, with reduced fluctuations observed at key binding-site residues, suggesting that Adenine binding induced local conformational stabilization of RXRA.

The Rg of the RXRA backbone was further analyzed to evaluate the compactness of the protein structure. As shown in Fig. 5F, the Rg values remained stable between 18.00 and 18.48 Å throughout the simulation, indicating that the overall structural integrity of RXRA was maintained upon ligand binding.

Hydrogen bond analysis between RXRA and Adenine demonstrated dynamic interactions ranging from 0 to 3 hydrogen bonds during the simulation, with an average of 1.12 (Fig. 5G), consistent with the docking results and supporting stable ligand binding. Per-residue binding free energy decomposition analysis was further conducted to identify key residues contributing to the stability of the RXRA–Adenine complex. The results revealed a binding pattern dominated by polar interactions with complementary hydrophobic contributions. Ser287 (−1.3 kcal/mol) and Cys235 (−1.2 kcal/mol) were identified as the primary stabilizing residues, mainly contributing through strong polar interactions such as hydrogen bonding. Arg276 (−0.9 kcal/mol) further stabilized the ligand via electrostatic interactions. Meanwhile, Leu269 (−0.7 kcal/mol) and Phe286 (−0.4 kcal/mol) provided secondary stabilization through hydrophobic interactions and potential π–π stacking effects. These residues collectively formed a multi-point cooperative interaction network, ensuring stable ligand anchoring within the RXRA binding pocket.

In addition, FEL analysis revealed two low-energy basins, suggesting the presence of stable conformational states of the RXRA–Adenine complex (Fig. 5H and I). Collectively, these results confirm that Adenine binds stably to RXRA and maintains a favorable dynamic interaction profile over the simulation period.

Intracellular binding validation of RXRA and adenine

In order to complete the intracellular binding validation of RXRA and Adenine, CETSA data were displayed in Fig. 5 J. Across the tested temperature gradient from 42 °C to 57 °C, soluble RXRA protein abundance gradually decreased in both the Control and Adenine groups as temperature increased. However, the Adenine group exhibited a much slower rate of soluble protein loss relative to the Control, indicating improved thermal resistance of cellular RXRA upon ligand treatment. The Tm analysis revealed a distinct shift in thermal stability: the Control group exhibited a Tm value of 48.72 °C, whereas Adenine treatment increased the Tm of RXRA to 52.95 °C, yielding a prominent ΔTm of 4.23 °C. Such a substantial thermal shift strongly supports direct physical binding between Adenine and RXRA inside living cells, which experimentally verifies the target-ligand interaction predicted by molecular docking and molecular dynamics simulations.

In vivo validation of the core target and pharmacodynamic effects

As schematically illustrated in Fig. 6A, an AS model was created in ApoE−/− mice given a high-fat diet in order to confirm the pharmacologic effects of adenine and its regulation of the core target RXRA in vivo. Compared to the CG, the atherosclerosis index in the MG was significantly elevated by 7.85-fold (P < 0.01), and the vulnerability index increased markedly by 30.13-fold (P < 0.01). Furthermore, distinct lipid-rich plaques accompanied by pronounced swelling were clearly observed on the arterial walls of mice in the MG group (Fig. 6B–D). The ratio of area for plaque to lumen (RAP) of the MG was also higher than that of the CG (P < 0.01) (Fig. 6E), confirming successful induction of AS and plaque instability by the high-fat diet. However, following intervention with adenine and atorvastatin, these pathological abnormalities induced by a high-fat diet were all significantly improved compared to the MG group (P < 0.01). Notably, the intervention effect of adenine exhibited an obvious dose-dependent pattern, with the HG group showing significant reversal in metrics such as plaque development (Fig. 6B–F). These results confirm that adenine can markedly counteract the exacerbation of AS.

Immunofluorescence research demonstrated that RXRA protein expression within plaques was considerably elevated in the MG compared to CG (P < 0.01) (Fig. 6D and F), indicating its involvement in AS progression. Importantly, both Adenine treatment (especially at HG) and atorvastatin markedly downregulated RXRA expression in plaques (P < 0.01), indicating therapeutic efficacy for both adenine and atorvastatin. Fig. 6 further confirmed that all treatment groups showed a significant reduction in RAP compared to the MG (P < 0.01). These findings collectively imply that Adenine may prevent AS progression, at least in part, through downregulation of RXRA protein expression.

Multivariate statistical analysis of the pharmacodynamic data

A multivariate statistical analysis was conducted out with the goal to methodically assess the interventional effects of Adenine. For comparability, the relevant parameters of other groups were standardized with reference to MG.

The normalized stacked bar chart visually summarizes the overall improvement across all measured indicators for each treatment group. As illustrated in Fig. 6G, significant differences in parameter distributions were identified among groups. Relative to the CG, the MG exhibited abnormal increases and decreases in these parameters. Drug intervention significantly suppressed these adverse pathological trends, with Adenine demonstrating a dose-dependent effect. The coordinated alterations in the expression of proteins and medicinal characteristics across groups initially confirmed that the target RXRA is a core target in AS, and that the representative antagonist Adenine exerts its effects through modulation of this target.

Hierarchical cluster analysis further revealed different grouping patterns: LG, HG, and PG clustered together, CG clustered with them, and MG finally formed a cluster (Fig. 6H). This clustering trend clearly indicates that as drug concentration increased, all parameters determined gradually altered from a problematic state toward normal ones. In summary, these findings demonstrate that Adenine significantly inhibits pathological features and modulates core protein expression, adding to the possibility of therapeutic function in AS.

The Pearson correlation analysis and the Mantel test were employed to evaluate connections among key parameters in order to clarify the underlying mechanisms. As shown in Fig. 6I, under drug intervention, all analyzed parameters indicated substantial positive associations (all correlation coefficients >0.81), indicating that these parameters are co-regulated during AS pathogenesis and intervention. The Mantel test findings demonstrated a good association between the therapeutic benefits of the intervention and the modulation of core protein expression (Mantel's P < 0.05, correlation coefficient >0.90), suggesting that Adenine's efficacy is closely linked to its modulation of the core target (Fig. 6J).

Collectively, the multivariate statistical analysis provides robust evidence that Adenine exerts its anti-atherosclerotic effects through coordinated modulation of multiple pathological indicators, with the downregulation of RXRA expression serving as a central mechanistic component of its therapeutic action.

Discussion

In this study, we employed a multi-dimensional AI-enabled computational pharmacology strategy integrated with experimental validation to systematically elucidate the anti-atherosclerotic mechanism of Panax ginseng and identify its key active ingredients. Initially, comprehensive chemical profiling of Panax ginseng extract via UPLC-Q-TOF-MS/MS led to the characterization of 55 constituents, including ginsenosides, flavonoids, amino acids, and nucleosides, whose potential target network comprising 836 proteins was subsequently predicted. By integrating DEGs and co-expression modules from WGCNA, a consolidated set of 1778 AS-related targets was established. The intersection between these disease-associated targets and the Panax ginseng component-predicted targets yielded 117 overlapping genes, which were defined as potential anti-atherosclerotic targets of Panax ginseng. Subsequently, a multi-model machine learning framework was applied to prioritize these candidates, consistently identifying RXRA as the core therapeutic target. Molecular docking was performed on 55 identified active ingredients with RXRA, and 14 high-affinity compounds were screened as potential Panax ginseng-derived key active ingredients. Among them, adenine was selected for further validation through molecular dynamics simulation. Finally, in an ApoE−/− mouse model of AS, the in vivo pharmacodynamic efficacy of the prioritized active component was evaluated, confirming that its anti-atherosclerotic effects are mediated, at least in part, through the modulation of the predicted core target RXRA, thereby completing a closed-loop workflow from computational prediction to experimental verification.

The identification of 117 potential targets in this study vividly illustrated the holistic, “multi-component, multi-target” regulatory characteristic of Panax ginseng as a traditional Chinese herbal medicine. AS is a complex pathological process involving dysregulated lipid metabolism, chronic inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, and plaque instability, where interventions targeting a single node often yield suboptimal therapeutic outcomes [23]. As a natural product, Panax ginseng offers a favorable safety profile compared to synthetic drugs [24]. Its diverse array of active constituents may act synergistically on multiple pathological facets, thereby conferring a unique advantage in addressing disease complexity. For instance, ginsenoside Rb1 has been shown to alleviate atherosclerosis by inhibiting vascular cell proliferation through disruption of the CCDN1-CDK4 interaction [25], while Panax ginseng extracts can modulate atherosclerosis-related gene expression and restore endothelial function by altering lipid-associated gene profiles [19]. Our functional enrichment analysis of these 117 targets provided mechanistic support for this systemic action. KEGG pathway analysis revealed significant enrichment in core pathways intimately linked to AS pathogenesis, including Lipid and atherosclerosis, MAPK signaling pathway, and PI3K-Akt signaling pathway. These pathways are critically involved in key processes such as macrophage lipid accumulation [26], inflammation modulation and plaque stabilization via MAPK/NF-κB signaling [27], and regulation of cholesterol efflux and inflammatory responses through PI3K-AKT signaling [28]. Furthermore, GO analysis highlighted their potential roles in biological processes like positive regulation of leukocyte mediated immunity, exemplified by studies showing that deficiency in the T-cell regulator Casitas B-cell lymphoma-B exacerbates AS by inducing CD8+ T cell-mediated macrophage death [29]. Notably, most of the enriched pathways and functions were also linked to protein RXRA, indicating that RXRA can regulate these pathways and functions. Collectively, these findings suggest that the active components of Panax ginseng may exert a systemic intervention against AS by modulating an integrated signaling network encompassing lipid homeostasis, inflammatory responses, and cell survival/proliferation. Nevertheless, while enrichment analysis delineates a potential action network, pinpointing the most critical regulatory hub within this network is essential for achieving precise therapeutic intervention, which logically leads to our subsequent screening and validation of the core target.

A pivotal aspect of this study is the application of a multi-model machine learning strategy for target prioritization, which demonstrates several distinct advantages over traditional network pharmacology methods. Conventional network pharmacology often relies on single algorithms or static topological analyses, which may introduce model-specific bias and lead to inconsistent or less robust target rankings. In contrast, our multi-model framework leverages the complementary strengths of diverse algorithms to enhance predictive reliability and biological relevance. Among the numerous potential targets, RXRA was consistently identified as the most critical target by multiple machine learning models. As a key part of the nuclear receptor superfamily, RXRA serves as a pivotal transcription factor regulating lipid metabolism and inflammatory responses [30]. It frequently forms heterodimers with receptors such as PPARG to jointly modulate processes including endothelial dysfunction, fatty acid oxidation, and inflammatory mediator expression [31], thereby occupying a central position in the pathological network of AS. Relevant studies have demonstrated that the transcription factor RXRA inhibits the accumulation of TGs, CHOL, NEFAs, and lipid droplets in CS2 cells by promoting CD36 expression, thereby slowing the progression of atherosclerosis [32]. Consequently, the prioritization of RXRA through data-driven machine learning methods was grounded in solid biological rationale. Based on this core target, molecular docking screening of the 55 identified Panax ginseng components successfully revealed 14 candidate compounds with high affinity for RXRA (binding free energy < −5 kcal/mol). These compounds can be systematically categorized into four classes: flavonoids (Astragalin, Formononetin, Isoquercetin, Quercetin, Catechin, Naringenin), nucleosides (Guanosine, Adenosine, Adenine), phenolic acids and amino acids (p-Hydroxybenzoic acid, L-Tryptophan), and saccharides (Disaccharides ((2Methyl-Hex)-Pen), Sucrose, Dendrolasin). Notably, Adenine, with its binding energy closest to the screening threshold and well-defined structure, was selected as the representative molecule for focused investigation.

Flavonoids, widely present polyphenolic compounds in plants, have been extensively documented for their significant anti-atherosclerotic activity, primarily involving mechanisms such as improving endothelial function and regulating lipid metabolism [33]. For instance, isoquercetin and quercetin significantly reduce total cholesterol, low-density lipoprotein, and the atherosclerosis index in rats, thereby alleviating AS [34,35]. Naringenin, with its ability to lower LDL and triglycerides while inhibiting glucose uptake, increases HDL, suppresses macrophage inflammation and foam cell formation, and is considered to play a key role in promising anti-atherosclerotic effects [36]. Nucleosides, crucial molecules involved in cellular energy metabolism and signal transduction, have garnered increasing attention for their cardiovascular protective effects in recent years. Relevant studies indicated that blocking endothelial adenosine receptor 2A inhibits AS in vivo by suppressing CREB-ALK5-mediated endothelial-to-mesenchymal transition [37], while adenine exhibited atheroprotective effects by enhancing cholesterol efflux and lipid clearance [38]. p-Hydroxybenzoic acid, a simple phenolic acid, and L-tryptophan, an essential amino acid and precursor to bioactive molecules such as serotonin, demonstrate antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activities that may help alleviate vascular oxidative stress and inflammation [39]. Research by Sukka et al. showed that macrophage efferocytosis upregulates tryptophan transport and metabolism, with its metabolite kynurenine activating the aryl hydrocarbon receptor, thereby inducing the production of pro-resolving mediators such as IL-10. This forms a critical pathway linking macrophage efferocytosis, tryptophan metabolism, and inflammation resolution, whose activation plays an important role in AS regression [40]. Although direct anti-AS studies on these two classes of molecules are relatively limited, as active components of Panax ginseng, they may assist other major constituents in exerting synergistic protective effects by modulating redox balance and immunometabolism. The saccharide compounds identified include disaccharides, sucrose, and Dendrolasin. Conventionally, simple sugars are associated with increased AS risk. However, specific oligosaccharides or sugar derivatives from plants may exhibit distinct bioactivities. For example, certain plant polysaccharides demonstrate strong anti-inflammatory activity and potent inhibition of vascular smooth muscle cell proliferation, potentially serving as natural antioxidants, anti-inflammatory agents, and therapeutic candidates for cardiovascular diseases [41].

Molecular docking and molecular dynamics simulations can preliminarily predict the potential binding relationships between target proteins and small molecules; however, these computational results lack intuitive and reliable cellular experimental validation, which compromises the credibility of the proposed molecular mechanism and the overall conclusions of this study. To address this limitation and further validate the intracellular interaction between RXRA and adenine, we performed CETSA, a well-established technique that directly reflects ligand–protein binding in physiological cellular environments by detecting changes in protein thermal stability. In the present study, multiple temperature gradients (42, 45, 48, 51, 54, and 57 °C) were applied to quantify the remaining soluble RXRA protein levels with or without adenine treatment. The experimental results demonstrated that adenine markedly increased the thermal stability of cellular RXRA protein, with ΔTm of 4.23 °C (greater than the valid threshold of 2 °C). These findings provide direct evidence for the physical binding between adenine and RXRA in living cells, strongly corroborating the binding interactions predicted by molecular docking and molecular dynamics simulations. Collectively, the CETSA results supply solid in-cell experimental support for our proposed molecular mechanism and substantially strengthen the scientific rigor and reliability of our conclusions.

The binding of these components to RXRA suggested they may not merely be inert constituents but potential signaling molecules or modulators, warranting further investigation into their specific mechanisms of action. Molecular dynamics simulations confirmed the favorable binding stability of the adenine-RXRA complex, while in vivo experimental validation indicated that adenine may prevent AS by downregulating RXRA protein expression. Consequently, we posited that the other 13 compounds with superior binding energies may also exert preventive and therapeutic effects against AS. This series of discoveries shifts the understanding of Panax ginseng's anti-AS action from the vague concept of a “multi-target network” to a focused perspective on a specific hub target (RXRA) and a group of well-defined candidate compounds, thereby achieving a deeper and more concrete elucidation of the mechanistic underpinnings.

A significant methodological innovation of this study lies in its adoption of a multi-model machine learning framework and a multi-scale integrated validation strategy, which effectively addresses several limitations inherent in traditional network pharmacology [[42], [43], [44]]. Conventional approaches often rely on static databases, generating broad and difficult-to-prioritize target lists that can lead to imprecise mechanistic interpretations [45]. In contrast, our study first established a disease-relevant target set more reflective of the genuine biological state of AS by integrating differential expression analysis with WGCNA. Subsequently, a comparative strategy employing seven distinct machine learning models was applied to cross-validate and prioritize the 117 potential targets. This multi-model comparison surpasses the three traditional machine learning methods more commonly used for target selection, and can more objectively identify the most discriminative and stable core targets, thereby greatly reducing subjective bias and improving the reliability of discovery. More importantly, this research constructed a complete closed-loop workflow of “multi-dimensional AI-enabled computational pharmacology integrated with experimental validation.” The workflow commenced with the chemical profiling and target prediction of the herbal drug, progressed through disease target integration and machine learning-based prioritization, advanced to molecular docking and dynamics simulations for screening key components, and culminated in in vivo validation of both pharmacodynamic efficacy and target modulation in an animal model. This strategy seamlessly bridges computational prediction with experimental verification, establishing a novel, reproducible, more precise, and highly persuasive paradigm for deciphering the “multi-component, multi-target” mechanisms of complex natural products. This methodological framework is not only applicable to Panax ginseng but also provides a viable and generalizable path for the modernized investigation of other natural medicines or TCM formulations with intricate compositional systems.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study systematically elucidated the mechanism by which Panax ginseng exerts its anti-atherosclerotic effects through the core target RXRA and identified 14 major anti-atherosclerotic active ingredients, employing an integrated strategy of multi-dimensional AI-enabled computational pharmacology coupled with experimental validation. Despite these systematic discoveries, several limitations warrant acknowledgment and necessitate refinement in future work. Firstly, while UPLC-Q-TOF-MS/MS comprehensively characterized the predominant components, it may have overlooked certain trace or structurally unstable bioactive substances. Secondly, computational prediction tools like SwissTargetPrediction, which operate based on chemical similarity principles, inherently carry risks of false positives or false negatives. Thirdly, our machine learning models were trained on a specific public transcriptomic dataset, and the generalizability of their conclusions to other AS populations or different pathological stages requires further validation. Fourthly, although animal experiments have confirmed the efficacy of Adenine and its regulation of RXRA protein expression, and the CETSA performed in this study has further demonstrated direct intracellular binding between Adenine and RXRA, the precise downstream signaling mechanisms of RXRA remain incompletely elucidated at the cellular level. Future studies should therefore focus on clarifying how Adenine binding leads to RXRA downregulation and what signaling pathways are involved, leveraging the confirmed direct interaction as a foundation. Finally, while the ApoE−/− mouse model is classic, it does not fully replicate all features of human AS, particularly the complex clinical course. Building upon the current findings and addressing these limitations, future research should validate the regulatory effect of Adenine on the target protein RXRA at the cellular level. Clinically, analyzing RXRA expression levels in human atherosclerotic plaque tissues and correlating them with plaque stability and disease severity will be crucial to confirm its clinical value as a therapeutic target.
