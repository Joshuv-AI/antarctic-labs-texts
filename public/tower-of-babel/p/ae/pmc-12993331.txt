Mechanistic insights into gluten network alteration in Rumex hanus-fortified steamed bread: From molecular interactions to quality characteristics
Authors: Zhizhe Zheng, Ziru Yin, Yuhang Li, Meina Zhang, Zhigang Qiao, Xinyan Zhao, Yandong Li, Tao Zhou, Xiaofei Wu, Xue Han
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Steamed bread is a common staple with good taste and digestibility but low nutritional value. Rumex hanus by. (RHB) is rich in protein, fiber, and minerals, making it a promising fortifier. This study examined the effects of 0–10% RHB addition on the nutritional quality, texture, and gluten structure of steamed bread. RHB significantly increased protein, fiber, amino acids, and minerals but, at levels ≥4%, reduced sensory quality due to texture deterioration. Mechanistic analysis showed that RHB lowered gluten zeta potential, inhibited disulfide bond formation, and caused glutenin depolymerization, leading to smaller particles, reduced hydrophobicity, and more disordered protein structures. These changes weakened the gluten network, reducing dough elasticity and gas retention. Consequently, the specific volume decreased by 25.29%, and hardness rose by 217.05%. This work offers guidance for optimizing nutrition and quality in functional steamed bread.

Introduction

Steamed bread has been a staple food in China for over 2000 years and remains widely consumed in most parts of Asia (Cao et al., 2019). The process for making traditional steamed bread is very simple: wheat flour serves as the primary ingredient, mixed with water and yeast to form a dough, which is then allowed to ferment before steaming. Although traditional steamed bread is known for its appealing sensory and quality attributes, it is not nutritionally balanced, with low levels of dietary fiber, trace elements and other beneficial nutrients (Zhao et al., 2024). When made solely from wheat flour, steamed bread provides insufficient amounts of lysine, minerals, and vitamins, and therefore does not fully satisfy the body's nutritional requirements.

To address this nutritional gap, numerous studies have focused on developing nutritionally fortified steamed bread. For instance, Mashau et al. (2022) enriched steamed bread with groundnut flour, thereby enhancing its protein, ash, and crude fiber content. Similarly, Hu, Cheng, et al. (2023) fortified steamed bread with sweet potato leaf powder, which significantly increased its contents of protein, fat, dietary fiber, minerals, vitamin B2, and niacin.

Rumex hanus by. (RHB) is a plant of medicinal and edible value, belonging to the genus Rumex, and was officially recognized as a new food ingredient in October 2021. RHB is highly nutritious, containing 22.4%–35.8% total protein (Ma, 2023; Wang et al., 2023) with 17 types of amino acids, approximately 44.5% of which are essential, making it a high-quality protein source (Hu, Li, et al., 2023). Additionally, RHB is rich in dietary fiber, which promotes intestinal peristalsis, enhances calcium absorption and supports proper digestive function. Furthermore, RHB is abundant in polyphenols, flavonoids, vitamins, β-carotene, rhubarb phenol, superoxide dismutase (SOD), isoflavones and various minerals (including potassium, selenium, zinc, iron and calcium), which collectively contribute to its potential in preventing intestinal disorders, replenishing micronutrients, accelerating metabolism, combating aging, providing antioxidant effects, and boosting immunity.

Given its rich nutritional profile, incorporating RHB into steamed bread formulations offers a novel and promising strategy to enhance the health benefits of this traditional staple food. However, certain nutrient-fortified components in RHB, such as cellulose and protein, may influence key quality attributes, including texture, taste, gluten network formation and starch digestibility. For example, Wang et al. (2021) found that the addition of insoluble dietary fiber and ferulic acid can lead to the formation of a denser gluten network, which adversely affects steamed bread quality by increasing hardness and decreasing specific volume.

The purpose of this study is to investigate the effects of RHB on the nutritional attributes, textural properties, and gluten aggregation behavior of Chinese steamed bread. Specifically, the nutritional indicators (including amino acids, proteins, dietary fibers, and minerals) and quality-related characteristics (such as texture profile analysis, specific volume, microstructure, and sensory properties) of steamed bread formulated with different levels of RHB were evaluated. Additionally, the aggregation mechanisms of gluten proteins were systematically investigated at molecular, nanoscale, and macroscopic levels, including analyses of chemical bonds and non-covalent interactions. Through these analyses, this study aimed to elucidate the interactions between RHB and wheat-based food systems, thereby providing a scientific basis for developing functional cereal products with enhanced nutritional value and improved textural and sensory quality.

Materials and methods

Materials

Rumex hanus by. (RHB) powder (32.5% protein, 9.85% crude fiber, 2.3% moisture content) was obtained from Shijiazhuang Sancao Agricultural Technology Co., Ltd. (Hebei, China). Commercial wheat flour (9.6% protein, 73% carbohydrate) was purchased from Wu Deli Flour Group (Hebei, China). Instant dry yeast was acquired from Angel Yeast Co., Ltd. (Hubei, China). All other chemicals were of analytical grade.

Sample preparation

RHB powder was incorporated into wheat flour at levels of 0%, 2%, 4%, 6%, 8%, and 10% (w/w, on flour basis), and mixtures were labeled as S0 to S10, respectively. Doughs were prepared by blending flour with 0.6% yeast and deionized water (80% of water absorption capacity). After mixing for 6 min using a vertical mixer, the dough was sheeted 10 times, shaped into 100 g pieces, and proofed at 36 °C and 85% RH for 30 min. The fermented dough was steamed for 20 min, cooled at room temperature for 1 h, and stored for further analysis. Freeze-dried doughs and steamed bread were ground and sieved (100-mesh) for chemical measurements.

Nutritional analysis

Amino acids

Seventeen amino acids were quantified using an automatic amino acid analyzer (LA8080, Hitachi, Japan) after acid hydrolysis at 110 °C for 22 h, based on the method of Wang et al. (2022) with minor modifications. Briefly, the sample was hydrolyzed with 5 mL of 6 mol/L HCl at 110 ± 1 °C for 22 h. After cooling, the hydrolysate was filtered and made up to 10 mL with distilled water; an aliquot was dried under nitrogen, redissolved in 0.02 mol/L HCl, and filtered (0.22 μm) prior to analysis. Amino acids were separated on a sulfonic acid cation-exchange resin column using stepwise sodium citrate buffer elution, followed by post-column ninhydrin derivatization and detection at 570 nm (primary amino acids) and 440 nm (proline).

Protein content

Protein content was determined using the Kjeldahl method (AOAC 2001.11). Samples were digested with concentrated sulfuric acid, distilled using a fully automated nitrogen analyzer, and titrated with standard hydrochloric acid. Protein was calculated using a nitrogen-to-protein conversion factor of 6.25.

Minerals

The contents of Fe and Zn were measured using inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS, iCAP RQ, Thermo, USA) following microwave digestion (AOAC 985.35), while Ca and K were determined by inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry (ICP-OES, Optima 8000, PerkinElmer, USA) following AOAC 2013.06 protocols.

Polyphenols and flavonoids

Total phenolic content (TPC) and total flavonoid content (TFC) were analyzed using 80% ethanol extracts (1,20 w/v), subjected to ultrasonic treatment at 40 °C for 30 min, and centrifuged (10,000 rpm, 15 min). TPC and TFC were quantified following Pessanha et al. (2021) and expressed in mg/g.

Dietary Fiber

Dietary fiber was determined using the enzymatic-gravimetric method (AOAC 985.29). Briefly, samples were enzymatically hydrolyzed with α-amylase, protease, and glucosidase. The residue was precipitated in 95% ethanol, filtered, washed with ethanol and acetone, dried at 105 °C, and corrected for ash and protein content (measured by Kjeldahl and ashing at 525 °C).

Starch digestion

Starch digestion was evaluated using a simulated three-phase system (Wang et al., 2025), Samples (0.5 g) were sequentially treated with α-amylase (50 U/mL), pepsin (4 mg/mL), and a mixture of α-amylase (2 mg/mL) and glucosidase (28 U/mL) at 37 °C. Glucose release was measured at 540 nm (DNS method) over 180 min. Rapidly digestible starch (RDS), slowly digestible starch (SDS), and resistant starch (RS) contents were calculated, and hydrolysis kinetics were modeled using LOS and CPS equations (Sang et al., 2025).

Steamed bread quality evaluation

Specific volume

Steamed bread volume was determined using millet seed displacement. Specific volume was calculated as the volume-to-mass ratio (g/mL), following the method of Cao et al. (2019).

Texture profile

Texture profile analysis (TPA) was performed using a TA-XT2i analyzer (Stable Microsystems, UK). Bread slices (25 mm thickness) were compressed to 50% with a cylindrical probe (35 mm diameter) at a speed of 1 mm/s. Parameters including hardness, cohesiveness, springiness, and chewiness were recorded (Wang et al., 2021).

Sensory evaluation

The sensory evaluation protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Review Committee of the College of Food Science and Biology, Hebei University of Science and Technology. All procedures were conducted in full accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants after they were fully informed of the study objectives, procedures, and potential allergens (including gluten). Participation was entirely voluntary, and panelists were free to withdraw at any time without consequence. No personally identifiable information was collected, and all sensory data were anonymized. The official ethics approval document approved by the College of Food Science and Biology is provided as Fig. S1.

The evaluation involved twelve trained adult panelists (18–55 years old) recruited from the university community. All panelists were familiar with steamed bread and had prior experience with sensory assessment. Before the evaluation, they completed a brief training session to standardize their understanding of the sensory attributes and scoring procedures. The evaluation followed the Chinese national standard GB/T 21118-2007, with minor adaptations for steamed bread products (see Table S1 for detailed evaluation criteria). Samples were coded with random three-digit numbers and presented in a fully randomized order to minimize sequence bias. Panelists evaluated each sample for fluffiness, skin color, surface structure, internal structure, elasticity, stickiness, taste, and aroma using a 10-point structured scale, where higher scores indicated better sensory quality. Warm water was provided between evaluations to minimize carry-over effects.

Dynamic rheological properties

The dynamic viscoelastic properties of dough were determined using a rotational rheometer equipped with parallel plate geometry (25 mm diameter, 2 mm gap). A portion of freshly mixed dough was loaded onto the lower plate, and the upper plate was lowered to the specified gap. Excess dough protruding from the edges was carefully trimmed, and mineral oil was applied to the exposed edges to prevent moisture loss. The sample was allowed to rest for 5 min at room temperature before measurement. Frequency sweeps (0.1–10 Hz) were performed at 25 °C within the linear viscoelastic region (0.1% strain), and storage modulus (G′), loss modulus (G″), and tan δ were recorded (Peng et al., 2017).

Scanning Electron microscopy

The crumb microstructure was observed using SEM (S-4800, Hitachi, Japan). Samples (5 × 5 × 5 mm) were sputter-coated with gold and examined at 5.0 kV, with magnifications of ×30 and ×300.

Gluten protein characterization

Glutenin macropolymer (GMP)

Freeze-dried dough powder (2 g) was extracted with 1.5% SDS and centrifuged (17,000×g, 20 min, 25 °C). The residue was freeze-dried and analyzed for nitrogen content using the Kjeldahl method to estimate GMP content.

Ζ-Potential, dough pH and particle size

ζ-potential was measured using a Zetasizer Nano ZS (Malvern, UK) at 20 °C. Gluten dispersions were prepared in 0.5 M acetic acid and filtered prior to measurement to remove insoluble impurities.

The pH of the dough samples was determined following a modified AACC Method 02-52.01. Briefly, 10 g of dough was homogenized with 90 mL of distilled water using a homogenizer for 2 min to obtain a uniform slurry. The pH of the resulting suspension was measured with a calibrated pH meter after stabilization. All measurements were performed in triplicate.

Particle size was determined using a Zetasizer Nano ZS (Malvern, UK) at 20 °C. Gluten was dispersed in 0.5 M acetic acid and filtered prior to analysis.

Free sulfhydryl and disulfide bonds

Free sulfhydryl groups (—SH) and disulfide bond (S—S) contents were determined using 5,5′-dithiobis-(2-nitrobenzoic acid) (DTNB, Ellman's reagent) as described by Ramos Magalhães et al. (2023). Absorbance was read at 412 nm, and values were calculated with appropriate dilution factors.

FTIR analysis

FTIR spectra (400–4000 cm−1) were recorded using a KBr pellet method. The amide I region (1600–1700 cm−1) was deconvoluted using PeakFit software to estimate secondary structure elements, including α-helix, β-sheet, β-turn, and random coil contents (Welc et al., 2022).

Fluorescence spectroscopy

Fluorescence spectra of gluten protein solutions were recorded using a Spectra Max M2 (Molecular Devices, USA) at λex = 290 nm and λem = 360–410 nm. Fluorescence quenching was used to infer tertiary structural changes.

Surface hydrophobicity

Surface hydrophobicity was evaluated using ANS fluorescence probe method as described by Tang et al. (2021). Fluorescence intensity was measured at λex = 390 nm and λem = 470 nm, and the hydrophobicity index (H0) was calculated from the slope of fluorescence intensity versus protein concentration.

Correlation analysis

Correlation analysis was conducted using SPSS 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) to evaluate the relationships among measured parameters. Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated, and the significance levels were defined as p < 0.05 (*), p < 0.01(**), and p < 0.001 (***).

Statistical analysis

Each experiment was conducted in triplicate, and all results were presented as the mean ± standard deviation. Data were analyzed using SPSS 26.0 (IBM, Chicago, IL, USA). Descriptive analysis was conducted using analysis of variance (ANOVA), and Duncan's multiple range test was employed to assess statistical differences among samples, with a significance level set at α = 0.05.

Protein content and amino acid composition

Experimental results showed that the addition of RHB significantly improved the amino acid nutritional quality of steamed bread. The total amino acid content increased in a dose-dependent manner with rising levels of RHB, reaching a significant 24.12% increase in the 10% supplementation group compared to the control (Fig. 1A). It should be noted that standard acid hydrolysis may lead to partial degradation of sulfur-containing amino acids such as methionine (Met) and cysteine (Cys), potentially resulting in slight underestimation of their contents. Further analysis revealed that the enhancement was more pronounced for essential amino acids (EAAs), with significant increases observed in lysine, leucine, and phenylalanine contents (Fig. 1B). Lysine, the first limiting amino acid in wheat flour, is critical for protein synthesis, growth, and immune function (Meybodi et al., 2019). Supplementation with 10% RHB significantly improved the amino acid profile, increasing lysine and threonine contents by 89.47% and 54.55%, respectively. This supplementation effectively compensates for the deficiency of limiting amino acids in wheat flour, thereby significantly enhancing the amino acid quality of steamed bread.

The addition of RHB significantly increased the total protein content, with a 23.93% increase observed at a 10% inclusion level (Fig. 1C). This result confirms that RHB is an effective plant-based protein fortifier, consistent with previous studies reporting improvements in protein quality through the incorporation of quinoa, amaranth, and legumes into cereal matrices (Batool et al., 2025; Laugesen et al., 2022; Lawal et al., 2021). Overall, RHB demonstrates a dual function by enhancing EAA content and increasing total protein concentration, highlighting its great potential as a nutritional enhancer in wheat-based formulations. By effectively alleviating lysine deficiency and increasing protein density, RHB offers a practical approach to improving the protein quality of staple cereal products.

Mineral content and dietary fiber

As shown in Fig. 1D and E, the addition of RHB to steamed bread significantly increased its mineral content. At a 10% substitution level, the contents of calcium (Ca), iron (Fe), potassium (K) and zinc (Zn) increased by 4.62 times, 9.96 times, 5.9 times and 27.86%, respectively, compared to the control group. This enhancement is likely due to the intrinsic mineral accumulation capacity of RHB during plant growth, suggesting its potential as a bioavailable plant-based source of essential trace elements. Notably, the calcium content of steamed bread with 10% RHB addition reached 147.33 mg per 100 g, which exceeds that of whole milk (approximately 113 mg/100 g) reported in the USDA Food Database. This finding indicates that RHB-fortified products could serve as a viable non-dairy alternative for calcium intake, particularly suitable for individuals with lactose intolerance or those on restricted animal-based diets.

The substantial enrichment of iron and zinc carries important nutritional implications. These two minerals play key roles in immune function, cognitive development, and cellular metabolism, yet deficiencies remain widespread, especially among low-income populations (Cole et al., 2010). Such deficiencies, often referred to as “hidden hunger”, are common in cereal-based diets due to the inherently low bioavailability of trace elements in grains (Burchi et al., 2011). The incorporation of RHB into staple foods offers a practical and culturally acceptable strategy to address these gaps. By increasing the density of trace minerals in commonly consumed products, RHB may help alleviate the burden of potential micronutrient deficiencies without requiring major dietary changes. Overall, these findings highlight the dual nutritional role of RHB: improving the mineral composition of wheat-based foods and providing a sustainable, food-based solution to mitigate micronutrient deficiencies.

Phenolic compounds content

As illustrated in Fig. 1F, the incorporation of RHB into steamed bread led to a significant increase in both total phenolic content (TPC) and total flavonoid content (TFC). At a 10% substitution level, TPC increased by 72.30%, while TFC reached 6.89 times that of the control group. These findings demonstrate that RHB is an effective source of bioactive compounds and can markedly enhance the nutritional properties of wheat-based products. Polyphenols, particularly flavonoids, are well-documented for their strong antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects, which play a key role in mitigating oxidative stress—a major contributor to chronic diseases such as cardiovascular disorders, type 2 diabetes, and certain cancers (Shahidi & Ambigaipalan, 2015; Vauzour et al., 2010). Therefore, the observed enrichment in TPC and TFC not only signify improved in the nutritional composition but may also markedly enhance the product's health benefits.

Dietary fiber content

Fig. 1G presents the effect of RHB addition on the dietary fiber content of steamed bread. When the substitution level increased from 0% to 6%, dietary fiber content rose markedly by 181.4%, indicating effective fiber enrichment. However, further increasing the substitution level to 8% and 10% resulted in a decline in fiber content. This trend is primarily attributed to complex interactions between RHB dietary fiber and the dough matrix during processing. At moderate inclusion levels, RHB supplies abundant native fiber, effectively enhancing the nutritional density of the product. In contrast, excessive addition may interfere with gluten network formation and alter water distribution, thereby inhibiting fiber swelling and dispersion and reducing extractability (Zhou et al., 2021). Additionally, thermal treatments during steaming may induce fiber cross-linking, embedding within the matrix, or partial degradation, further affecting the measured fiber content.

Digestion of starch

As shown in Fig. 2A, all steamed bread samples (S0–S10) exhibited a typical biphasic starch digestion behavior, characterized by a rapid initial phase followed by a slow digestion phase. With increasing levels of RHB addition, especially beyond 6%, the digestion curves shifted upward, indicating an enhanced degree of starch hydrolysis. Fig. 2B presents the corresponding changes in starch fractions. With increasing levels of RHB addition, the contents of rapidly digestible starch (RDS) and slowly digestible starch (SDS) gradually increased, while the content of resistant starch (RS) decreased. These changes suggest a shift in starch composition toward more digestible forms. To gain deeper insights into the digestion curves, the Logarithm of Slope (LOS) model and the Combined Parallel and Sequential (CPS) model were applied for curve fitting (Fig. 2C). Both models achieved excellent fits (R2 > 0.99), further confirming the biphasic nature of starch digestion, characterized by clearly distinguishable rapid and slow digestion fractions, with partial overlap between the two kinetic phases (Dhital et al., 2017; Yu et al., 2021). These changes may be partially attributed to matrix effects. At low RHB levels (≤4%), dietary fiber may inhibit enzyme diffusion by forming a compact matrix or restricting starch accessibility within the gluten network. Conversely, excessive RHB disrupts the continuity of the gluten structure and dilutes the protein–starch matrix, reducing its structural integrity and thereby promoting enzymatic hydrolysis. Given RHB's negligible starch content (carbohydrates predominantly in the form of dietary fiber, per literature and novel food approvals), any contribution from RHB itself is minimal relative to wheat starch. The shift toward more digestible fractions at higher RHB levels (>6%) is thus primarily driven by matrix disruption from excessive insoluble fiber, which dilutes/interrupts the gluten network and enhances enzyme access. This aligns with biphasic kinetics in the LOS/CPS models (Fig. 2C) and similar observations in fiber-enriched breads where high insoluble fiber increases starch digestibility by disrupting gluten-starch interactions (Ronda et al., 2012; Güven & Şensoy, 2024).

Excessive incorporation of RHB negatively impacted the sensory qualities of steamed bread

Specific volume and texture

Specific volume is a key quality parameter reflecting the degree of fermentation and dough aeration, and it is critical to the quality of steamed bread products. The effect of RHB addition on the specific volume of steamed bread is shown in Fig. 3A. When 2% RHB was added, the specific volume slightly increased (2.58 mL/g), but the difference was not significant compared to the control group. However, as the RHB addition level increased from 2% to 10%, the specific volume significantly decreased to 1.95 mL/g, representing a 24.42% reduction. This decline may be attributed to the following mechanisms: 1) Cellulose in RHB competes with gluten for moisture, weakening the gas-holding capacity of the gluten network (Zhou et al., 2021); 2) Active ingredients in RHB may bind to gluten protein sulfhydryl groups, forming a denser gluten structure that limits gas retention (Girard & Awika, 2020); 3) Excessive RHB may reduce dough pH, thereby inhibiting yeast activity. Sensory evaluation

The sensory evaluation results of steamed bread with different levels of RHB addition are shown in Fig. 3B The figure lists scores for key sensory attributes, including fluffiness, surface structure, internal structure, resilience, viscosity, and texture parameters that directly reflect the product's mouthfeel and structural integrity. Steamed bread with 2% RHB addition (S2) achieved the highest overall sensory score, indicating the highest level of consumer acceptance at this substitution level. However, when the RHB content exceeded 4%, sensory scores for nearly all attributes declined significantly (p < 0.05). Specifically, samples containing 6% to 10% RHB exhibited characteristics such as coarse structure, low cohesiveness, darkened surface color, and pronounced odor.

These changes may be attributed to the dietary fiber in RHB, which disrupts the integrity of the dough matrix, negatively impacting the quality of the steamed bread. In addition, the high water-holding capacity and swelling properties of RHB particles during mixing and steaming may create physical barriers within the dough, limiting gluten expansion and weakening the gluten network (Ozyurt & Ötles, 2016). Such structural disruptions reduce the bread's resilience and cohesiveness, ultimately resulting in lower consumer acceptability.

Dynamic rheological properties

The mechanical behavior of dough under different frequencies was characterized through oscillatory shear tests. As shown in Fig. 3C, the viscoelasticity of the dough was evaluated using storage modulus (G′), loss modulus (G″), and the loss factor (tan δ = G″/G′). Within the tested frequency range of 0.1–10 Hz, both G′ and G″ increased with frequency, and G′ consistently remained higher than G″ (tan δ < 1), indicating that all dough samples exhibited solid-like behavior dominated by elasticity (Peng et al., 2017). With increasing levels of RHB addition, both G′ and G″ values rose significantly, suggesting enhanced dough rigidity and viscoelastic strength. Meanwhile, the gradual decrease in tan δ with increasing frequency indicated a stronger elasticity-dominated response at shorter timescales (i.e., higher frequencies), where molecular relaxation processes are increasingly limited. This frequency-dependent shift in viscoelastic behavior may be attributed to the rigid-filler effect of dietary fiber in RHB, which enhances packing density and constrains gluten chain mobility, thereby reducing the network's ability to relax under rapid deformation. Additionally, certain bioactive components in RHB may alter intermolecular interactions within the gluten matrix, thereby modifying its relaxation dynamics across different timescales. Together, these effects contribute to the observed decrease in tan δ and the enhanced elastic character under high-frequency oscillation.

Microstructure

Fig. 4 illustrates the effect of RHB addition on the microstructure of steamed bread. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images revealed pronounced structural differences in the internal structure of steamed bread samples with varying levels of RHB. Both the control sample (S0) and the bread containing 2% RHB (S2) exhibited a well-developed, continuous gluten network accompanied by uniformly distributed pores. This suggests that a low-level RHB addition contributes to the stabilization of the gluten matrix, promotes gas retention during fermentation, and ultimately improves porosity and texture. However, at 4% and 6% RHB substitution levels, the gluten structure became progressively looser and more discontinuous. Although pores were still present, their size distribution became irregular, indicating partial disruption of gluten aggregation and reduced gas-holding capacity. During fermentation, carbon dioxide produced by yeast causes the gluten–starch matrix to expand, but the weakened network is unable to effectively retain the gas bubbles, leading to coalescence and collapse—morphological features clearly visible in the SEM images (Yu et al., 2023).Fig. 4The effect of RHB on the microstructure of steamed bread observed through an electron microscope. At higher substitution levels (8% and 10%), the porous structure deteriorated severely. The crumb structure appeared denser, with fewer and more indistinct pores, reflecting extensive disruption of protein–protein interactions, which interfered with both gas formation and retention. These observed microstructural changes are consistent with the decrease in specific volume (Fig. 3A) and the decline in sensory scores (Fig. 3B), particularly for attributes related to internal structure, texture, and fluffiness.

RHB addition influenced the physicochemical characteristics of gluten

Glutenin macropolymer (GMP) content

During dough preparation, insoluble glutenin macropolymers (GMPs) play a pivotal role in the formation of the gluten network. A higher GMP content typically reflects greater protein cross-linking through the oxidation of free sulfhydryl groups into disulfide bonds (Liu et al., 2022). The effect of RHB addition on GMP content before and after fermentation is shown in Fig. 5A At 2% RHB, GMP content remained comparable to the control both before and after fermentation (p > 0.05), suggesting minimal disruption to gluten structure. However, as the RHB level increased from 2% to 10%, GMP content decreased markedly by 24.41% and 21.31% before and after fermentation, respectively (p < 0.01).This decline may be attributed to fiber components in RHB interfering with disulfide bond cross-linking, thereby promoting GMP depolymerization. Interestingly, the effect of fermentation on GMP stability also exhibited RHB concentration dependence. At low RHB levels, fermentation induced moderate GMP depolymerization, which is consistent with the typical gluten protein remodeling that occurs during dough fermentation. In contrast, at higher RHB levels, fermentation-induced GMP depolymerization was significantly suppressed (p < 0.05), suggesting that excessive RHB physically hinders the development and reassembly of gluten proteins.

ζ-Potential and its relationship with dough pH

As shown in Fig. 5B, the positive ζ-potential of the dough systems steadily declined as the level of RHB increased. This shift paralleled the drop in dough pH, reflecting a gradual loss of net surface charge. The reduction was more evident in the fermented samples (FD), where the accumulation of organic acids (mainly lactic and acetic acids) produced a further decrease in pH. When the RHB level reached 10%, the dough pH approached the isoelectric region of gluten proteins. At this point, charge neutralization became substantial, resulting in an 80.1% to 82.7% decrease in positive ζ-potential compared with the control.

The marked decline in ζ-potential can be explained by the interaction between protonated gluten proteins and the negatively charged constituents of RHB. Although acidification increases the protonation of lysine, histidine, and other basic residues, thereby raising the intrinsic positive charge of gluten, this effect is outweighed by the abundance of anionic groups provided by RHB. These include phenolic acids and flavonoids, protein derived carboxyl groups, and uronic acid containing polysaccharides, such as hemicelluloses. Their association with the positively charged gluten domains leads to strong electrostatic screening, and possibly additional hydrophobic contacts. Together, these interactions shield the surface charge near the slipping plane, resulting in a substantial reduction in the measured ζ-potential of the composite particles.

Particle size distribution

Fig. 5C shows the effect of different RHB concentrations on the particle size distribution of gluten proteins in unfermented dough. At low RHB levels (2–4%), the particle size distribution was similar to that of the control group, with the main peak located in the larger size range. At a moderate concentration (6%), the particle size distribution became narrower and shifted toward smaller sizes. This transition suggests the onset of gluten depolymerization, likely driven by electrostatic interactions or steric hindrance introduced by RHB components. This observation aligns with the ζ-potential reduction shown in Fig. 5B, supporting the hypothesis that charge neutralization weakens inter-protein interactions, thereby promoting disassembly into smaller units (Saurabh et al., 2024).

At higher RHB levels (8–10%), the particle size reduction became more pronounced, with a substantial increase in the proportion of gluten particles smaller than 200 nm. This phenomenon may be attributed to the polyphenols and acidic polysaccharides abundant in RHB, which adsorb onto the protein surface, forming protective shells that prevent reaggregation and maintain colloidal dispersion stability (Xiao et al., 2023).

Free sulfhydryl and disulfide bonds

As shown in Fig. 5D, RHB supplementation significantly affected the redox state of gluten proteins (p < 0.05). Increasing RHB levels resulted in a dose-dependent rise in free sulfhydryl content, accompanied by a corresponding decline in disulfide bond content. This effect may be attributed to reductive constituents in RHB, such as flavonoid polyphenols, which can competitively inhibit thiol oxidation or directly reduce disulfide bonds, thereby promoting sulfhydryl accumulation and disulfide bond depletion. Welc et al. (2022) reported that the introduction of selected hydroxycinnamic and hydroxybenzoic phenolic acids into gluten proteins resulted in an increase in sulfhydryl content and a decrease in disulfide bond content. In addition, dietary fibers or other bioactive components in RHB may alter the conformation of gluten proteins, thereby increasing steric hindrance that prevents sulfhydryl groups from approaching each other. Such a reduction in gluten network cross-linking could impair the gas-holding capacity of steamed bread, ultimately compromising its quality.

Compared with the control (S0), samples supplemented with RHB exhibited a new absorption peak at 2362.67 cm−1, which became most pronounced at higher supplementation levels (S10). According to Addadi et al. (2003) and considering the mineral-rich composition of RHB, this peak is likely attributed to the introduction of carbonate or oxalate species. This suggests that in addition to providing organic bioactive compounds, RHB may also alter the infrared absorption characteristics of the system through its inorganic constituents. In addition, with increasing levels of RHB, the Amide I band exhibited a slight red shift, indicating strengthened intermolecular hydrogen bonding or an increased proportion of β-sheet structures, consistent with the secondary structure analysis. Conversely, the Amide II band showed a slight blue shift, which typically reflects weakened hydrogen bonding or local conformational tightening, potentially due to enhanced hydrophobic interactions arising from protein–polysaccharide complex formation (Zhang et al., 2023). These results indicate that the incorporation of RHB not only modifies the secondary structure of gluten proteins but also regulates their hydrogen-bonding network and hydrophobic interactions, thereby influencing the spatial arrangement of the gluten network.

As shown in Fig. 6B, the addition of RHB markedly altered the secondary structure composition of gluten proteins in steamed bread. Compared with the control, the β-sheet content exhibited an initial increase followed by a slight decline with rising RHB levels, reaching a maximum of 43% at 4%, and remaining relatively high thereafter (36–40%). This indicates that moderate levels of RHB can promote the transition of gluten proteins toward β-sheet conformations, thereby enhancing intermolecular hydrogen bonding and structural stability. In contrast, α-helix content remained stable, suggesting insensitivity of this conformation to RHB incorporation. Meanwhile, the proportion of random coil structures decreased, reaching its lowest value at 4%. This suggests that the original ordered helical conformations and flexible chain segments were partially disrupted or rearranged, likely due to interactions between polyphenolic compounds in RHB and protein side chains through hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions, which competitively modify the protein hydrogen-bonding network. In addition, the β-turn content also declined during supplementation, decreasing from 36% in the control to 30–32%, indicating reduced chain bending and a more compact spatial conformation. These conformational changes have important implications for steamed bread quality. While the increase in β-sheet content may enhance the stability of the gluten network, the reduction in random coil structures could weaken its extensibility and gas-holding capacity, thereby impairing loaf volume and texture to some extent. Overall, low levels of RHB supplementation may induce favorable structural rearrangements, whereas excessive levels may reduce the flexibility of the gluten network, ultimately leading to quality deterioration.

Fluorescence spectroscopy

As shown in Fig. 6C, the intrinsic fluorescence intensity of both fermented (FD) and unfermented (PD) dough samples gradually decreased with increasing levels of RHB addition, indicating that RHB markedly influenced the fluorescence properties of gluten proteins. The intrinsic fluorescence of gluten proteins primarily originates from aromatic amino acids, such as tryptophan, tyrosine, and phenylalanine, whose fluorescence signals are highly sensitive to the polarity of their surrounding microenvironment and the spatial conformation of the protein. Bioactive constituents in RHB, including polyphenols and dietary fibers, may interact with gluten proteins through hydrophobic interactions, hydrogen bonding, or steric hindrance, thereby inducing conformational alterations. These interactions can increase the polarity of the environment around aromatic amino acids, leading to fluorescence quenching or masking of their signals. Consistent with this observation, Cao and Xiong (2015) reported that water-soluble phenolic acids enhance the polarity of the microenvironment surrounding tryptophan residues, exposing them to the aqueous phase and consequently reduced their fluorescence intensity.

Surface hydrophobicity

Fig. 6D illustrates the effects of different RHB addition levels on the surface hydrophobicity (H0) of gluten proteins in both fermented (FD) and non-fermented (PD) doughs. As RHB concentration increased, surface hydrophobicity significantly decreased in both samples (p < 0.05). Surface hydrophobicity primarily reflects the exposure of hydrophobic groups on protein surface, which is closely related to higher-order protein structures. Typically, hydrophobic residues are buried within the protein interior, and their surface exposure is governed by protein conformation and intermolecular interactions. Upon the introduction of RHB, its abundant hydrophilic components (e.g., polyphenols and dietary fibers) may interact with the hydrophobic sites of gluten proteins, thereby masking part of the hydrophobic groups and reducing their accessibility to fluorescent probes (Nam et al., 2025). In addition, RHB incorporation may alter protein–protein interactions, promoting partial folding or aggregation that relocates previously exposed hydrophobic residues into the protein interior to form hydrophobic cores, thereby further decreasing surface hydrophobicity. Collectively, RHB reduces the surface hydrophobicity of gluten proteins through multiple mechanisms, which in turn modulates protein aggregation and gluten network formation, ultimately affecting the textural quality of the final product.

These results indicate that active substances in RHB mediate redox reactions that cleave disulfide bonds into —SH, weakening intermolecular crosslinks. Simultaneously, dietary fiber (DF) in RHB imposes steric hindrance, physically impedes gluten chain alignment (Fig. 8). Negative correlations between DF and glutenin macropolymer content (GMP) (p < 0.001), and between GMP and hardness (p < 0.001), confirm that DF disrupts glutenin network formation and protein aggregation, ultimately deteriorating product texture. Furthermore, DF competes with gluten proteins for water molecules, as reflected by its negative correlations with surface hydrophobicity (H₀) (p < 0.05), GMP content, and S—S bonds (p < 0.001), alongside positive correlations with -SH groups. This water competition limits protein hydration, molecular unfolding, and crosslinking (Zhou et al., 2021), reducing gluten polymerization and network stability, which is further evidenced by the negative correlation between DF and springiness.

In summary, the combined effects of active substance-induced S—S bond cleavage and protein interactions, together with the steric hindrance and water competition caused by dietary fiber, synergistically damage the gluten network. This disruption results in starch leakage, gas escape, and ultimately manifests as decreased loaf volume, and increased crumb firmness.

Conclusions

This study systematically investigated the effects of varying levels of RHB addition on the nutritional composition, quality attributes, and gluten polymerization behavior in steamed bread. Results demonstrated that the addition of RHB significantly enhanced nutritional attributes of the steamed bread, including protein, essential amino acids, dietary fiber, minerals, and other bioactive compounds, demonstrating a clear nutritional fortification effect. However, excessive RHB addition (above 6%) adversely affected sensory characteristics and structural stability of the steamed bread. High RHB concentrations reduced electrostatic repulsion, disrupted disulfide bonds, and weakened hydrophobic interactions, thereby impairing gluten network cross-linking. This led to diminished network strength, reduced porosity, lower gas retention capacity, and decreased specific volume, ultimately resulting in deteriorated texture. In summary, an RHB addition level of 4%–6% offered the best balance between nutritional enhancement and product quality- significantly improving the nutritional value of steamed bread while maintaining desirable texture and sensory acceptance. These findings highlight the potential of RHB as a functional ingredient for nutritional improvement in staple foods and provide a scientific foundation for product innovation in the food industry.
