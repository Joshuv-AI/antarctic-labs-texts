Exploring the use of large language models for self-learning in preventive dentistry and their associations with academic performance: a cross-sectional pilot study among dental students
Authors: Jiayi Yang, Mingming Li, Shuhua Wang, Zhuojin Shi, Jinhua Fang, Pei Zheng, Zhiwu Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLarge language models (LLMs) are being gradually integrated into dental education, but more attention was paid in perspective of educators rather than students. Evidence regarding how students employ them for self-learning in Preventive Dentistry, and whether specific usage patterns are related to academic performance, remains scarce. This study examined dental students’ self-perceived LLM knowledge, attitudes, and behaviors; evaluated perceived usefulness across Preventive Dentistry modules; and explored associations between LLM usage (frequency, type, motivation) and academic performance.MethodsThis cross-sectional pilot study recruited all third-year dental undergraduates enrolled in a Preventive Dentistry course at a university in eastern China. A total of 117 students provided valid responses and completed final examination. Academic performance was assessed using final examination scores as a continuous outcome variable. Linear regression analysis was performed to examine associations between LLM usage and examination scores. The data were analyzed using SPSS 22.0, and the level of significance was set at 0.05.ResultsLLM use was reported by all participants, with eighty-four students (71.8%) considering themselves as proficient users. For self-learning in Preventive Dentistry, 49.6% of participants reported using LLMs one to three times per month and DeepSeek (99.1%) was most popular. Students perceived LLMs as more helpful for theoretical learning than for practical skill acquisition (p < 0.001). The most frequently reported motivation for LLM use was to broaden knowledge and facilitate comprehension (76.1%), compared with other motivations. Importantly, the linear regression model (adjusted R2 = 0.274) showed that an association was observed between the motivation “to broaden knowledge and facilitate comprehension” and academic performance (B = 5.321 (95%CI: 1.669 to 8.973), β = 0.233, p = 0.005), indicating a small-to-moderate positive effect size.ConclusionsIn this pilot sample, LLMs were widely reported to be used and were perceived by these students as more helpful for learning theoretical rather than for practical knowledge during self-learning in Preventive Dentistry. Using LLMs to broaden knowledge and facilitate comprehension was the most frequently reported motivation and showed a modest positive association with academic performance, although causality cannot be inferred due to the cross-sectional design. These findings provide preliminary insights into students’ use of LLMs and may help inform future research on their role in dental education.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12903-026-09345-9.

Background

Large language models (LLMs), a subset of artificial intelligence (AI) capable of generating coherent, context-aware responses in real time [1–3], offer accessible and personalized educational support that may address gaps in self-learning [4].Since the emergence of GPT in 2018 and the subsequent rise of DeepSeek in 2025, academic interest in LLMs has grown steadily [5, 6]. It was reported that nursing students widely employed LLMs and achieved improved learning outcomes [4]. In undergraduate dental education, tools such as ChatGPT have demonstrated considerable potential to support exam preparation and enhance conceptual understanding [7], and their applications have also been explored across multiple dental specialties, including periodontology, endodontics, restorative dentistry, pediatric dentistry, oral and maxillofacial surgery, and orthodontics [8]. However, evidence specifically examining how dental students utilize LLMs for self-learning in Preventive Dentistry, a discipline characterized by a unique integration of theoretical public health knowledge and clinical preventive skills, remains limited.

Preventive Dentistry in undergraduate dental education in China encompasses a broad range of topics, including oral epidemiology, the prevention of caries and periodontal diseases, function of fluoride for caries, pit and fissure sealant, toothbrushing, evidence-based dentistry, and infection control in dental practice, among others [9], reflecting a complex and integrated body of knowledge. Additionally, the predominant reliance on lecture-based teaching has resulted in limited student participation, insufficient practical training resources, and challenges in providing personalized instruction [10]. Consequently, fully achieving the core educational objective of Preventive Dentistry remains challenging: to cultivate prevention-oriented dental professionals who possess both public health awareness and practical skills, grounded in the integration of theoretical knowledge with application [9, 10]. In this context, LLMs may serve as supplementary tools to support students’ self-learning by providing accessible and flexible learning resources. Self-learning focuses on the process in which an individual learns independently, often without formal guidance from a teacher, lecturer, or institution [11]. Existing studies on LLMs in dental education have explored applications across multiple specialties, including periodontology [12], endodontics [13], restorative dentistry [14], orthodontics [15], and oral surgery [16], with a particular focus on assessment-related functions such as question generation, examination support, automated grading, and knowledge testing, often from the perspective of educators [17]. However, this teacher-centered perspective overlooks the role of students as active users of these tools in their self-learning processes. Consequently, little is known about the actual patterns of student-initiated LLM use in Preventive Dentistry, including which tools they choose, how frequently they engage with them, and for what purposes.

The present study is conceptually grounded in two complementary theoretical frameworks. First, Self-Regulated Learning (SRL) theory posits that learners who actively employ cognitive and metacognitive strategies to deepen understanding, such as seeking elaboration, connecting new knowledge with prior knowledge, and monitoring comprehension, tend to achieve better academic outcomes [18]. In the context of LLM use, this framework suggests that students’ usage patterns (e.g., frequency, types of tools, and purposes of use) may reflect different levels of SRL engagement. Second, Self-Determination Theory (SDT) distinguishes between autonomous motivation (engaging in an activity for its inherent interest or value) and controlled motivation (engaging due to external pressures) [19]. Within this framework, students’ motivation for using LLMs may be associated with academic performance, depending on whether their use is driven primarily by autonomous or controlled forms of motivation.

These theoretical perspectives directly inform the present investigation. In particular, empirical evidence regarding the relationship between specific LLM usage patterns, including frequency of use, types of LLMs employed, and underlying motivational orientations, and objective academic performance in Preventive Dentistry remains lacking. Furthermore, it is unclear whether students perceive LLMs as differentially helpful across the theoretical and practical modules that characterize this discipline.

Therefore, guided by SRL and SDT, the primary objective of this study was to examine the associations between patterns of LLM use in self-learning of Preventive Dentistry, including usage frequency, types of LLM used, and learning motivations, and academic performance among dental undergraduates. Secondary objectives were to describe students’ self- perceptions, attitudes, and general usage behaviors regarding LLMs, and to evaluate their perceived usefulness across different Preventive Dentistry content modules.

Methods

Design and participants

A cross-sectional pilot study was conducted among third-year dental undergraduates enrolled in a “Preventive Dentistry” course at Zhejiang Chinese Medical University (a single university in eastern China) during the spring semester of 2025, which followed the STROBE guidelines [20]. The STROBE checklist completed for this cross-sectional study is included in Supplementary Material 1. Consistent with the descriptive and exploratory function of a cross-sectional design, this pilot investigation was undertaken within a single institution and cohort to assess the current prevalence and patterns of LLM use in Preventive Dentistry self-learning at a single time point, and to explore their associations with academic performance [21, 22]. However, as a single-institution study, the findings may have limited generalizability to other educational settings or geographic regions.

Sample size was determined by established recommendations for pilot studies [21, 23]. According to the formula proposed by Viechtbauer et al., the required sample size for a pilot study can be estimated as: n = ln(1-γ)/ln(1-π) [23], where n is the required sample size, γ is the confidence level (set at 0.95), and π is the expected problem probability (set at 0.05 for questionnaire-based responses). This calculation yielded a minimum required sample size of 59. Additionally, multiple rules of thumb for pilot studies suggest that for binary outcomes, 60–100 participants per group are sufficient, while for continuous outcomes, 12–35 participants per group are adequate [21]. In this study, all students enrolled in the course were invited to participate. All 122 students enrolled in the course were eligible to participate. Of these, 120 students completed the online questionnaire, yielding a response rate of 98.4%. After excluding two invalid questionnaires and one student who did not participate in the final examination, 117 students were included in the final analysis, which exceeded sample size thresholds. Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of the Stomatology Hospital of Zhejiang Chinese Medical University (No. ZCMUHSIRB-2025031003).

Data collection

Data were collected using an online questionnaire that included sociodemographic variables (age, gender and ethnicity), as well as items assessing students’ self-perceived knowledge, attitudes, and behaviors regarding LLMs [24, 25]. This self-administered questionnaire was developed based on the study objectives and informed by a systematic framework for questionnaire design in educational research [26]. It was subsequently refined through iterative discussions within the research team to ensure relevance to the Preventive Dentistry curriculum and alignment with LLM tools commonly available in China (e.g., DeepSeek, Doubao, Kimi, ChatGPT) [27]. Two broad construct domains were defined: (I) general awareness, attitudes, and usage behaviors regarding LLM tools; and (II) the specific use of LLMs in Preventive Dentistry self-learning. Within Domain II, subdomains assessed included frequency of LLM use for this course, preferred tools, motivations for use (multiple-response), and perceived helpfulness across seven core curriculum modules (oral epidemiology; caries and periodontal disease prevention; fluoride function; pit and fissure sealants; toothbrushing; evidence-based dentistry; infection control).

Self-reported measures were used because several key constructs of interest, including students’ subjective perceptions, attitudes, and self-initiated learning behaviors, are inherently subjective and are most appropriately assessed through participant self-report [28]. The self-perceived knowledge items reflect subjective evaluation and should be interpreted as an indicator of perceived understanding rather than objective knowledge. Then, we examined the frequency, types and motivations for using LLMs in Preventive Dentistry in self-learning. Motivation was assessed using a multiple-response question, allowing participants to select one or more reasons for use. Each response option was subsequently coded as a binary variable for analysis. The content validity of the questionnaire was evaluated by six experts using the item-level content validity index (I-CVI) and the scale-level content validity index (S-CVI). The I-CVI values ranged from 0.833 to 1.00, and the S-CVI was 1.00, indicating excellent content validity [29]. The full version of the online questionnaire is available in Supplementary Material 2.

The internal consistency of the questionnaire was acceptable, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.742. The Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) measure was 0.704, and Bartlett’s test of sphericity was statistically significant (p < 0.001), indicating that the data were suitable for factor analysis and providing preliminary support for the construct validity of the questionnaire [30, 31].

The questionnaire was created and administered online via the survey platform Wenjuanxing (www.wjx.com). To ensure data completeness, all items were configured as required responses; therefore, participants could not submit the questionnaire with any missing entries. Participants were required to complete it between the conclusion of the Preventive Dentistry course and the final examination.

The final examination was a written theory test consisting of 30 multiple-choice questions, 3 short-answer questions, and 2 case analysis questions, covering all core modules of the standardized textbook Preventive Dentistry [12]. The examination was developed by the course teaching team, informed by the standardized textbook, and reviewed by two external subject experts to ensure content validity. Internal consistency reliability was acceptable (Cronbach’s α = 0.764). Detailed reliability analysis results of the final examination paper are provided in Additional file 3. The total score (range: 0-100) was used as a continuous measure of academic performance. To link survey responses with academic performance while protecting participant privacy, each student’s student ID number served as the unique identifier. Data linkage was performed exclusively by an independent teaching support staff member. Immediately after matching, student ID numbers were removed and replaced with newly assigned study codes, and only the de-identified coded dataset was made available to the research team for analysis. This process ensured accurate linkage of survey responses with final examination scores while maintaining participant confidentiality.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages. Participants rated the level of LLM-assisted learning for each Preventive Dentistry module using a 5-point Likert-type scale [17] (e.g., 1 = not used or no assistance at all; 5 = very helpful). The Likert-type scale items were treated as continuous data and were summarized using means and standard errors for descriptive clarity. However, inferential comparisons across modules were performed using non-parametric tests, which are appropriate for ordinal data [32]. Specifically, the Kruskal-Wallis test was used for comparisons across multiple modules, and the Mann-Whitney U test was used for comparisons between theoretical and practical modules.

Academic performance was treated as a continuous variable. To examine the factors associated with academic performance, a linear regression model was constructed. The adequacy of the sample size for linear regression was considered based on commonly recommended rules of thumb (10–20 observations per parameter) [33]. Given that categorical variables were represented using dummy coding, the effective number of parameters could increase substantially. Univariable linear regression analyses were first performed to examine the association between each independent variable and academic performance. Variables with p < 0.10 in the univariable analysis were included in the multivariable linear regression model [34, 35]. This relatively inclusive threshold was adopted in accordance with methodological recommendations for exploratory analyses, where more liberal univariable screening criteria may help reduce the risk of prematurely excluding potentially relevant predictors before multivariable adjustment [36, 37]. The model was adjusted for age and gender. Due to the pilot nature of this investigation and the practical constraints of data collection within a single course setting, several additional potential confounders, including prior academic performance, general study habits, and baseline digital literacy, were not measured, in order to minimize questionnaire burden and maximize response completeness within the cohort. The codes for all variables can be found in Table S1 (see Supplementary Material 3).

We reported unstandardized coefficients (B), 95% confidence intervals (95%CI), standardized coefficients (β) and p-values. Model fit was assessed using R2 and adjusted R2. Variance inflation factor (VIF) was examined to assess multicollinearity. All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics, version 22.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). A two-tailed p value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 120 third-year undergraduate dental students participated in the online survey. Two students were excluded due to invalid questionnaire responses, and one student was excluded because of an incomplete final examination record. Consequently, 117 students were included in the final analysis. The mean age of the participants was 20.97 ± 0.65 years, with a male-to-female ratio of 1:1.34. Students of Han ethnicity comprised 98.3% of the study population (Table 1).

The descriptive statistics on self-perceived knowledge, attitude and behavior of LLMs

Students’ attitudes toward LLMs were predominantly positive: over 90% agreed that LLMs could improve quality of life and had already benefited student learning. Nevertheless, concerns were also widely shared, with a clear majority of respondents agreeing that LLMs might inhibit independent thinking (73.5%) or raise issues such as privacy infringement and plagiarism (69.2%) (Table 2).

All participants in this single-institution cohort reported prior use of LLMs, while the majority (51.3%) reported using them three to six times per week (Table 2). The most commonly used LLMs were DeepSeek (99.1%), followed by Doubao (76.1%), ChatGPT (53.8%), suggesting a strong concentration of use in highly accessible Chinese-language platforms (Fig. 1, Table S2, Supplementary Material 3).

Notably, LLM use was less frequent in the specific context of Preventive Dentistry self-learning than for general use reported above. Nearly half of students (49.6%) reported using LLMs one to three times per month, suggesting that these tools were used more selectively for subject-specific learning (Fig. 2a). Despite this lower frequency, DeepSeek remained the most frequently used platform for this purpose (70.9%), significantly exceeding all other reported tools (p < 0.001; Fig. 2b).

Students were asked to rate the extent to which LLMs supported their learning across different content modules. The highest-rated module was the prevention of caries and periodontal diseases (3.983 ± 0.105). Whereas pit and fissure sealants (3.573 ± 0.133) and toothbrushing (3.547 ± 0.144) received the lower ratings (Fig. 3a). Notably, pit and fissure sealants and toothbrushing were primarily taught through hands-on practice, whereas the remaining five modules were delivered mainly through theoretical instruction. When modules were categorized accordingly, theoretical modules received significantly higher ratings than practical modules (p < 0.01), indicating that students perceived LLMs as more helpful for supporting theoretical knowledge acquisition than practical skill learning (Fig. 3b).

The most frequently endorsed motivation for LLM use was to broaden knowledge and facilitate comprehension (76.1%), which was significantly more common than other reported motivations (p < 0.01). Other commonly reported motivations included learning concepts (57.3%) and searching for relevant cases (54.7%) (Fig. 4). Overall, these findings suggest that students primarily used LLMs as supplementary tools to enhance understanding and expand subject knowledge rather than solely for task completion.

The academic performance of students who used LLMs with varying frequency, types and motivation

In univariable linear regression analysis, frequency of LLM use was not significantly associated with academic performance (p > 0.05). Compared with students who used DeepSeek (the reference category), use of LLMs categorized as “Others” (i.e., tools not listed in Table 3) showed a weak negative association with academic performance (B = -20.440 (95%CI: -33.963 to -6.916), β = -0.027, p = 0.003). Regarding motivation, using LLMs to broaden knowledge and facilitate comprehension had a significant association with academic performance, with a small-to-moderate positive effect size (B = 6.126 (95%CI: 2.065 to 10.187), β = 0.268, p = 0.003). In contrast, using LLMs to analyze data exhibited a weak negative association with academic performance (B = -3.842 (95%CI: -7.617 to -0.068), β = -0.185, p = 0.046) (Table 4).

Variables with p < 0.10 were included in the multivariable analysis [34, 35]. In the initial multivariable model, the predictors explained 15.6% of the variance in academic performance (adjusted R² = 0.156), indicating modest explanatory value [38]. No significant multicollinearity was observed (all VIF < 2). Using LLMs to broaden knowledge and facilitate comprehension remained positively associated with academic performance, with a small-to-moderate effect size (B = 6.328 (95%CI: 2.447 to 10.209), β = 0.277, p = 0.002). After adjustment for age and gender, the final model explained 27.4% of the variance in academic performance (adjusted R² = 0.274), indicating moderate explanatory value for an educational outcome influenced by multiple determinants. The motivation to broaden knowledge and facilitate comprehension remained modestly positively associated with academic performance (B = 5.321 (95%CI: 1.669 to 8.973), β = 0.233, p = 0.005), suggesting that learning-oriented use of LLMs may be more relevant to achievement than simple frequency of use. However, the association between using LLMs to analyze data and academic performance was no longer statistically significant after adjustment (B = -1.208 (95%CI: -4.631 to 2.215), β = -0.058, p = 0.486) (Tables 5 and 6).

Discussion

The use of LLMs among dental undergraduates may be becoming increasingly widespread. In the present study, all participants reported using LLMs in general learning contexts, with more than 65% engaging with these tools at least once per week, a pattern consistent with findings from other higher education settings [39, 40]. However, the 100% reported usage rate should be interpreted cautiously. It may reflect the specific digital learning environment of the study population, in which LLM tools were highly accessible and increasingly integrated into students’ learning practices [41]. In addition, the voluntary online survey format may have introduced selection bias, as students who were more interested in or familiar with digital technologies may have been more likely to participate. Therefore, this finding likely overestimates the true prevalence of LLM adoption in less digitally saturated educational contexts.

DeepSeek emerged as the most frequently used LLM. In contrast, use of “other LLMs” (i.e. LLMs not individually listed in the questionnaire) showed a weak negative association with academic performance compared with DeepSeek. This association may reflect differences in user motivation, learning strategies, and digital literacy, rather than the intrinsic effectiveness of the tools themselves [42]. Platform preferences are also likely to be context-dependent. In China, high visibility, free accessibility, and strong Chinese-language support may have contributed to the widespread use of DeepSeek without implying inherent superiority over alternative platforms [43].

The observed phenomenon of multi-tool usage (e.g., Doubao, ChatGPT, and Kimi) may reflect students’ ability to flexibly select among different LLMs to address diverse learning needs. This behavior is consistent with strategic digital learning, in which learners combine tools with different strengths rather than relying on a single platform. As AI tools become increasingly integrated into both educational and clinical environments, the ability to critically evaluate and appropriately select among available tools may represent a skill that may warrant explicit attention in dental curricula [44]. However, the present study did not assess whether students’ tool choices were guided by a critical understanding of each LLM’s strengths and limitations. Distinguishing adaptive multi-tool use from unstructured trial-and-error exploration represents an important direction for future research and curriculum-based digital literacy training [45] .

Assessment of LLM-related proficiency was based on self-reported measures and should be interpreted cautiously. Perceived competence may be influenced by overconfidence, limited self-awareness, or subjective response bias and may not accurately reflect actual prompting skills, critical evaluation ability, or effective academic use [46, 47]. Ethical considerations (AI misuse, academic integrity, dependency) surrounding LLM use in dental education also warrant closer attention. Potential misuse includes overreliance on AI-generated answers, submission of AI-assisted work without appropriate disclosure, and uncritical acceptance of inaccurate or fabricated information [48]. Such practices may undermine academic integrity and limit opportunities for independent reasoning. In dental education, where clinical reasoning requires active problem-solving, excessive reliance on LLMs for ready-made explanations during self-study could reduce the cognitive engagement necessary for deep learning [49]. In addition, students’ widespread recognition of privacy and plagiarism risks underscores the importance of incorporating responsible AI use into dental curricula, including guidance on data privacy and appropriate attribution when LLM outputs inform academic work [50]. Encouragingly, students generally expressed positive attitudes toward LLMs while also recognizing their potential risks, which may help students to adopt a critical approach to using LLMs. This combination of openness and caution may be beneficial, as effective educational use of LLMs requires both willingness to engage and the ability to critically appraise generated content.

Notably, the frequency of LLM use in Preventive Dentistry was significantly lower than that reported for general learning contexts, with nearly half of students reporting use only one to three times per month. This pattern suggests that broad familiarity with LLMs does not necessarily translate into deep integration within discipline-specific learning. One possible explanation is that students may remain cautious about the accuracy, reliability, or professional relevance of LLM-generated responses in clinically oriented subjects [51, 52]. Alternatively, students may perceive that specialized coursework requires resources beyond the current capabilities of general-purpose LLM systems. These findings indicate that adoption of LLMs in higher education may be uneven across disciplines and influenced by perceived task fit.

Students’ perceptions of the auxiliary effectiveness of LLMs varied according to the nature of the teaching content. Modules primarily based on theoretical instruction received higher ratings of perceived support, whereas hands-on practical modules received lower ratings. This pattern is educationally plausible. LLMs may be particularly useful for explaining complex concepts, organizing knowledge structures, generating examples, and supporting real-time question answering, all of which may facilitate comprehension and knowledge construction [53]. By contrast, in skill-based modules such as pit and fissure sealing, current LLM-based assistance formats (e.g., text explanations, static guidance, or instructional videos) cannot replicate tactile experience, psychomotor training, or immediate instructor feedback. This likely limits their perceived usefulness. More broadly, the findings suggest that current LLM systems may be better suited to supporting cognitive aspects of dental education than procedural skill acquisition. Future work should explore whether multimodal AI, simulation technologies, or supervised clinical integration could narrow this gap.

These findings also have important implications for curriculum design. Given that LLMs were perceived as more helpful for theoretical content, educators could consider integrating them as preparatory tools for knowledge acquisition before class, thereby allowing face-to-face time for hands-on practice where LLM support is limited. Students should also be guided to develop realistic expectations of LLM capabilities, recognizing that these tools may strengthen conceptual understanding but cannot substitute for the tactile experience and instructor feedback essential for clinical skill development. A blended approach that allocates LLM use to theoretical self-study while preserving instructor-led practical training may optimize the complementary strengths of AI tools and traditional dental instruction. Such targeted integration may be particularly relevant in prevention-focused areas of dental education, particularly in prevention-oriented domains that overlap with pediatric dentistry education.

The motivation for using LLMs showed a modest association with academic performance. Students who reported motivation for “broadening knowledge and facilitating comprehension” were more likely to adopt a learning-oriented approach and mastery goals, viewing these tools as partners in exploration and knowledge construction. In addition, this usage pattern may reflect a deep learning strategy, in which students may proactively leverage LLMs for conceptual expansion, connection building, and problem-solving, processes associated with the development of more robust and transferable knowledge systems [53, 54]. From a cognitive perspective, using LLMs to deepen understanding may support several processes central to effective self-learning. These include elaborating on new concepts by generating explanations, integrating new information with existing knowledge, and monitoring one’s own comprehension through interactive questioning [55]. Such processes are consistent with cognitive learning theories that emphasize active knowledge construction during self-directed study [56]. By contrast, using LLMs for more mechanical tasks, such as data processing or assignment completion, may not evoke the same depth of cognitive engagement [57]. This distinction may help explain why only the motivation to broaden knowledge and facilitate comprehension showed a positive association with academic performance in the regression models. Given the modest effect size, however, the practical significance of this association should not be overstated. The cross-sectional data can only suggest, rather than confirm, such cognitive mechanisms.

Several alternative explanations must be considered. First, reverse causality is plausible. Students with higher academic performance may already possess more effective learning strategies and may therefore be more inclined to use LLMs for conceptual deepening rather than superficial task completion [58]. Second, unmeasured confounders could simultaneously influence both LLM usage patterns and examination performance. These include prior academic achievement, intrinsic motivation, and baseline digital literacy [42, 59].

Academic performance was assessed using a single institutional final examination. Although the examination was based on a standardized textbook, externally reviewed for content validity, and demonstrated acceptable internal consistency, a single written assessment cannot fully capture broader academic competence, practical ability, critical thinking, or long-term retention.

Although several statistically significant associations were identified, the effect sizes were relatively modest. This suggests that LLM use is only one of many factors that may be relative to academic performance, which is likely shaped by multiple interacting factors including prior academic ability, study habits, motivation, and digital literacy. Reverse causality is also possible because of the cross-sectional design. It cannot be determined whether specific patterns of LLM use contributed to better academic performance, or whether students with higher academic performance may already possess more effective learning strategies and may therefore be more inclined to use LLMs for conceptual deepening rather than superficial task completion. Therefore, the observed associations should be understood as exploratory and hypothesis-generating, and as preliminary evidence of cross-sectional associations within a complex learning context rather than causal. As a pilot study, the present findings provide preliminary estimates of LLM use patterns, perceived usefulness, and associations with academic performance that may inform the design of future larger-scale studies. In particular, these results may assist in refining survey instruments, estimating sample sizes, and generating hypotheses for future longitudinal or interventional research aimed at clarifying the temporal directionality and underlying mechanisms of these relationships.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, as a cross-sectional study, the observed associations do not imply causality, and the directionality of the relationships cannot be determined. It is possible that students with higher academic performance are more likely to adopt more effective ways of using LLMs, reflecting potential reverse causation. Furthermore, this design cannot assess whether the observed usage patterns are stable over time or whether they predict longer-term academic outcomes or later clinical competence [60]. Longitudinal or experimental designs would be required to evaluate the temporal stability of these patterns and to determine whether specific LLM usage strategies can causally improve learning outcomes in Preventive Dentistry.

Second, the study may be subject to self-selection bias [61]. Although the response rate was high, students who were more engaged with digital tools or more interested in LLMs may have been more likely to participate, potentially overestimating the prevalence and patterns of LLM use. Several key variables, including LLM use frequency, motivations, and proficiency, were assessed using self-reported measures. These data are susceptible to recall inaccuracy and to systematic overestimation driven by social desirability bias. Students may tend to over-report usage behaviors perceived as academically desirable, such as using LLMs to broaden knowledge and facilitate comprehension, while under-reporting less socially approved uses. Similarly, self-assessed proficiency may substantially exceed actual competence, a well-documented tendency in digital literacy self-assessment [46, 62]. Such overestimation could inflate the reported prevalence of educationally oriented LLM use and the perceived level of LLM proficiency in this sample. Therefore, the accuracy of these measures cannot be fully assured, and the observed associations should be interpreted cautiously.

Third, although the regression models were adjusted for age and gender, residual confounding cannot be excluded. Several potentially important variables, including prior academic performance, learning habits, digital literacy, and socioeconomic background [42, 59, 63], were not measured or controlled for. These factors may have influenced both students’ patterns of LLM use and their examination scores, thereby affecting the validity of the observed associations. Accordingly, the findings should be interpreted cautiously.

Fourth, the study was conducted in a single institution within a specific digital environment in which LLMs are highly accessible. The sample was drawn from one university in eastern China, with relatively uniform digital infrastructure and a course-specific learning context. These factors inherently limit external validity. The observed usage patterns, including the near-universal adoption of DeepSeek, may not generalize to institutions with different technological infrastructures, cultural contexts, student demographics, or varying levels of LLM accessibility. Therefore, the generalizability of the findings to other educational contexts may be limited [29]. Multi-center studies with diverse institutional and regional settings are needed to determine the broader applicability of these exploratory findings.

Finally, this study did not include an objective evaluation of the quality and educational effectiveness of different LLM tools, which may vary substantially in their utility for learning [64]. Such measurement was beyond the scope of this pilot investigation, which relied primarily on self-reported perceptions rather than independent benchmarking of tool performance. Consequently, the observed patterns of tool preference and their associations with academic performance may partly reflect unmeasured differences in output quality, usability or interface design rather than student-related factors alone. These unmeasured tool-level differences may also have influenced how students engaged with LLMs and their potential learning value.

Conclusion

In this single-institution cross-sectional pilot study, LLM use was widely reported among the participating dental students, suggesting its growing presence in self-learning activities. However, these usage patterns were observed in one institution and may not represent broader dental student populations. Students perceived LLMs as more helpful for theoretical learning than for practical skill acquisition within Preventive Dentistry. The utilization of LLMs guided by deepening understanding may be modestly associated with academic performance, although this finding is preliminary and causality cannot be inferred. A practical takeaway for educators is that guiding students toward purposeful LLM use to support conceptual understanding during self-study may offer greater educational value than emphasizing frequency of use or tool selection alone. Further longitudinal, experimental, and multi-center studies with more comprehensive outcome measures are needed before these exploratory associations can inform wider educational practice.
