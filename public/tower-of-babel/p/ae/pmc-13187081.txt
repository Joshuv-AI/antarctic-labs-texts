The hidden players: LncRNA-Encoded micropeptides in cancer hallmarks
Authors: Xiaoyun Chen, Sijie Luo, Ruishu Chen, Ximo Zhang, Tiantian Yang, Qiwei Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Traditionally, long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) were considered non-protein-coding. However, recent studies reveal that their short open reading frames (sORFs) can translate functional micropeptides (10–100 amino acids), forming “bifunctional RNAs” with dual RNA and polypeptide functions. This review summarizes the mechanistic roles and clinical significance of lncRNA-encoded micropeptides in tumor development. By integrating ribosome footprinting sequencing (Ribo-seq), mass spectrometry, and bioinformatic prediction, researchers have identified numerous tumor-associated micropeptides (e.g., APPLE, RBRP, CIP2A-BP). These micropeptides drive malignant progression by regulating key oncogenic processes, including sustained proliferative signaling (e.g., enhancing translation initiation complex assembly), evasion of growth suppression (e.g., blocking tumor-suppressive pathways), and metabolic reprogramming (e.g., activating ATP synthase). Notably, micropeptide functions align with Hanahan & Weinberg’s hallmarks of cancer framework, highlighting their centrality in tumor biology. The expression levels of specific micropeptides (e.g., MIAC, SMIM30) correlate significantly with patient prognosis, demonstrating their potential as novel diagnostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets. Although challenges in stability and delivery exist for micropeptide-based drugs, their high specificity and low toxicity offer promising avenues for precision cancer therapy.

Introduction

Proteins currently represent the predominant class of disease biomarkers and drug targets. However, only about 2% of transcripts in the human genome are protein-coding mRNAs, while the remaining 98% are non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs) [1]. ncRNAs lack canonical open reading frames (ORFs) and comprise diverse types such as tRNA, rRNA, miRNA, circRNA, lncRNA, and piRNA [2]. Among these, long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) are defined as transcripts without protein-coding capacity [3]. lncRNAs account for approximately 70–80% of ncRNAs and play crucial roles in immune regulation, developmental differentiation, and disease progression [4–8]. Compared to the relatively limited repertoire of protein targets, lncRNAs offer immense diagnostic and therapeutic potential due to their vast number and functional diversity.

Traditionally, lncRNAs were regarded as non-translatable, owing to their low sequence conservation, absence of standard initiation codons, and the presence of only short ORFs (sORFs) typically < 300 nt in length [9–11]. However, recent evidence shows that sORFs within certain lncRNAs can encode functional short peptides with biological significance [12–18]. These active peptides endow the parent RNA with a dual capacity—functioning both as an RNA molecule and as a peptide-encoding transcript—thereby justifying their reclassification as bifunctional RNAs [19, 20].

ORF length is a central criterion for distinguishing lncRNAs from mRNAs. To avoid misclassification resulting from random short ORFs, a minimum ORF length threshold is commonly applied. Structurally, lncRNAs resemble mRNAs (featuring a 5′ cap, 3′ poly(A) tail, and primarily being synthesized by RNA polymerase II) [21], yet they exhibit lower sequence conservation, more complex secondary and tertiary structures, and highly tissue-, cell type-, and developmental stage-specific expression patterns. Research has firmly established lncRNAs as important functional RNA molecules.

lncRNAs are defined as RNA molecules longer than 200 nucleotides that lack protein-coding potential. They play significant roles in key biological processes such as the regulation of gene expression and cell differentiation. Their mechanisms of action primarily include: functioning as signaling molecules by recruiting transcription factors to modulate gene expression; serving as decoy molecules to sequester and inhibit proteins; acting as guide molecules to direct chromatin-modifying complexes to specific genomic loci (in cis or in trans); or serving as scaffold molecules to assemble multi-protein complexes involved in epigenetic regulation [22].

Recent studies have revealed that certain lncRNAs can directly encode functional micropeptides—typically 10–100 amino acids in length and translated from sORFs—which participate in processes such as calcium homeostasis, mitochondrial metabolism, and tumor initiation and progression [23]. This indicates that a subset of “non-coding RNAs” in fact possess coding potential, and the functions of their derived micropeptides may have been previously overlooked [24]. Systematically identifying these hidden functional elements is therefore crucial and holds significant promise for expanding the scope of disease diagnosis, therapy, and research. A representative example is the micropeptide APPLE (90 amino acids), which is encoded by the lncRNA ASH1L-AS1 and was identified through multi-omics integrative analysis [24]. APPLE localizes to the endoplasmic reticulum, where it enhances mRNA circularization and eIF4F complex assembly by promoting the interaction between PABPC1 and eIF4G, thereby driving an oncogenic translational program.

Characteristics and significance of lncRNA encoding micropeptides

Discovery and identification of lncRNA-Encoding microPeptides - A technology-driven paradigm shift

The discovery of micropeptides encoded by lncRNAs has challenged the traditional paradigm that “lncRNAs lack coding capacity,” representing a landmark breakthrough in the life sciences [18]. A central challenge now lies in accurately identifying short open reading frames (sORFs) with true translational potential from vast transcriptomic data and in functionally validating the resulting micropeptides [25, 26].

Bioinformatic prediction serves as the primary step for mining potentially functional sORFs. Early strategies focused on conserved sequence features: defining sORF boundaries by start and stop codons, applying length thresholds, evaluating codon usage bias (e.g., via the Codon Adaptation Index, CAI), analyzing cross-species evolutionary conservation (e.g., with PhyloCSF), and detecting translation initiation signals [4, 27]. Modern approaches, such as those integrated into platforms like sORFs.org, substantially enhance prediction accuracy by synergistically analyzing multi-dimensional omics data. Translatomics (Ribo-seq) provides direct evidence of active translation [28]; transcriptomics (RNA-seq) confirms tissue- or state-specific expression; and proteomics attempts to validate peptides via mass spectrometry (MS), though this still faces considerable technical hurdles. A cutting-edge trend involves employing machine learning or deep learning models (e.g., DeepSORF) to integrate these multi-source evidences for high-precision sORF prediction [29].

Experimental validation is the core step for confirming lncRNA-encoded micropeptides, encompassing three progressively advancing levels: (1) Translation Activity Verification: Relies on ribosome footprinting sequencing (Ribo-seq) [28], which directly identifies the active translation state of an sORF through its characteristic tri-nucleotide periodicity footprint pattern [30, 31]; (2) Direct Peptide Detection: Primarily challenges the limits of mass spectrometry (MS) technology. Strategies include targeted MS methods (PRM/SRM) for direct validation, optimizing untargeted strategies (DDA coupled with fractionation or DIA/SWATH-MS), and enhancing instrument robustness against interference [12, 13, 32, 33]; (3) Functional Confirmation: Core methods include CRISPR-mediated sORF knockout/regulation combined with phenotypic rescue, developing high-specificity antibodies (for detection, localization, and interacting protein identification), overexpression/RNA interference, and biophysical/biochemical analyses (e.g., ITC, SPR to study interactions) [34, 35]. These validation steps are interlinked, collectively building a comprehensive evidence chain from the potential coding capacity of lncRNAs to functional micropeptides.

lncRNAs encode the biosynthesis and regulatory mechanisms of micropeptides

The biosynthesis of lncRNA-encoded micropeptides centers on the identification of sORFs and the mechanisms of translation initiation [36]. The process typically begins with the bioinformatic prediction of potential sORFs, followed by experimental validation using Ribo-seq (to identify actively translated sites) and mass spectrometry (to detect the peptides themselves), often combined with immunoblotting or gene editing techniques (e.g., CRISPR) to confirm functionality. The mechanism of translation initiation critically determines the efficiency of micropeptide synthesis [37]. Canonical cap-dependent translation represents a common pathway, relying on the m⁷G cap and eukaryotic initiation factors (eIFs) and most frequently initiating at an AUG start codon. Importantly, non-canonical translation mechanisms also play crucial roles. These include Internal Ribosome Entry Sites (IRES) (enabling cap-independent initiation, often under stress), upstream ORFs (uORFs) (which can regulate translation of downstream primary sORFs), non-AUG initiation (typically with lower efficiency), and the rolling circle translation of circular RNAs [38].

The biosynthesis of lncRNA-encoded micropeptides is governed by a precise, multi-layered regulatory network. At the transcriptional level, lncRNA gene expression is regulated by transcription factors, DNA methylation, and histone modifications, exhibiting strict spatiotemporal specificity.Post-transcriptional regulation is highly diverse: the stability of lncRNAs, modulated by RNA-binding proteins, miRNAs, and RNA modifications such as N⁶-methyladenosine (m⁶A), determines template availability. m⁶A exerts a dual effect [39]; it can recruit the translation machinery via reader proteins like YTHDF1/3 to enhance sORF translation, or promote RNA decay via readers such as YTHDF2. Furthermore, nucleocytoplasmic distribution and alternative splicing affect sORF accessibility and integrity, ultimately influencing functional micropeptide output. At the translational level, sORF translation efficiency is controlled by factors including the initiation codon type (AUG or non-AUG), the use of IRES elements, the regulatory role of upstream ORFs (uORFs), the availability and activity of translation initiation factors (e.g., regulated by eIF2α phosphorylation), and the unique topology of circular RNAs [40, 41]. Finally, at the post-translational level, nascent micropeptides undergo post-translational modifications (PTMs) (e.g., phosphorylation, acetylation) that modulate their activity. Their cellular levels are tightly controlled by degradation systems, primarily the ubiquitin-proteasome pathway or lysosomal degradation. Degradation rates are regulated by specific E3 ubiquitin ligases, molecular chaperones, intrinsic degron sequences, and degradation-triggering modifications such as ubiquitination.

Dysregulation of micropeptide synthesis is associated with disease, but the understanding of their regulatory networks (e.g., specific RNA-binding proteins, modifying enzymes, degradation dynamics) remains limited. Future research necessitates the integration of spatial transcriptomics, multi-omics (translatomics/modificomics), structural resolution, and live-cell imaging for in-depth analysis, providing support for targeted therapies.

lncRNAs encode the role of micropeptides in tumors

Tumorigenesis and progression are precisely regulated by multi-layered and multi-factorial mechanisms. Recent studies have revealed that long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) play extensive regulatory roles in key processes of cancer cells, including proliferation, apoptosis, invasion, metastasis, metabolic reprogramming, and immune evasion. lncRNAs are closely associated with the development and progression of various cancers such as colorectal cancer, hepatocellular carcinoma, breast cancer, and glioma [42–45]. They function by regulating signaling pathways involved in proliferation and autophagy, inhibiting tumor metabolic reprogramming, and suppressing the epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) process, thereby constraining tumor initiation and progression. Micropeptides, primarily resulting from the translational capacity of lncRNAs, function as novel bioactive molecules. They have been found to directly participate in regulating oncogenic signaling pathways, influencing the function of key tumor-associated proteins, remodeling the tumor microenvironment, and even acting as drivers in the early stages of tumorigenesis. The micropeptides encoded by lncRNAs represent a class of long-overlooked bioactive molecules that can directly regulate key oncogenic signaling pathways, modulate the function of tumor-related proteins, reshape the tumor microenvironment, and may even act as drivers in early tumorigenesis. This discovery not only expands our understanding of lncRNA functional mechanisms but also raises a series of emerging questions and conceptual gaps that warrant further exploration: for example, how can we systematically distinguish between the RNA-level functions of lncRNAs and the protein-level functions of the micropeptides they encode? What are the fundamental differences between micropeptides and the products of canonical protein-coding genes in terms of their mechanisms of action, regulatory networks, and evolutionary characteristics? Why have these transcripts with coding potential been evolutionarily conserved yet largely missed by conventional annotation systems? Therefore, a deeper investigation into the expression dynamics, molecular mechanisms, and pathophysiological roles of lncRNA-encoded micropeptides in cancer is essential not only for a comprehensive understanding of tumor regulatory networks but also for opening new avenues to address “undruggable” targets, discover novel diagnostic biomarkers, and develop innovative micropeptide-based targeted therapies. Future research should move beyond descriptive correlations and focus on establishing standardized frameworks for micropeptide functional characterization, elucidating their context-specific regulatory logic across different tumor types, and exploring feasible pathways for clinical translation.

This section will review recent advances in the field, focusing on the functional mechanisms of micropeptides, their associations with malignant tumor phenotypes, and their potential and challenges as novel diagnostic and therapeutic targets. Maintain proliferation signaling: Enhance growth factor receptor, downstream kinase, or transcription factor activity

APPLE

In a study by Sun et al., integration of Ribo-seq, mass spectrometry (MS), and RNA-seq data identified the lincRNA ASH1L-AS1 as encoding a 90-amino-acid micropeptide, termed APPLE, which localizes to the endoplasmic reticulum and its ribosome-associated regions. APPLE is ribosome-enriched and promotes mRNA circularization and eIF4F initiation complex assembly by strengthening the interaction between PABPC1 and eIF4G, thereby elevating global translation efficiency to sustain high-level synthesis of oncoproteins. In multiple AML subtype (M2-M5) cells, suppression of APPLE expression exerted potent anti-leukemic effects, including proliferation inhibition, cell-cycle arrest, and apoptosis induction. Knockdown of APPLE in NB4 and MOLM-13 cells reversed differentiation blockade and impaired self-renewal capacity. Conversely, overexpression of functional APPLE induced opposite phenotypes, confirming the critical driver role of the APPLE protein in leukemogenesis [24].

C20orf204-189aa

In hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), Linc00176 (C20orf204) is transcriptionally activated and upregulated by Myc [48]. This lncRNA encodes a 189-amino-acid micropeptide, C20orf204-189AA, which promotes HCC progression by enhancing tumor cell proliferation. Mechanistically, the micropeptide forms a ternary complex with nucleolin and ribosomal RNA (rRNA), thereby augmenting rRNA transcription activity. Its oncogenic effect depends primarily on the peptide function, with the core mechanism involving the stabilization of key nucleolar proteins to accelerate cell growth. Clinical analyses show that expression levels of both C20orf204-189AA and nucleolin in HCC tumor tissues are significantly inversely correlated with patient survival time. These findings demonstrate that this micropeptide functions as a cancer-specific fine-tuner of HCC development [49].

LINC013026-68aa

Immunoblotting (Western blot) and immunohistochemical (IHC) analyses confirmed the endogenous expression of a 68-amino acid micropeptide encoded by Linc013026. This micropeptide primarily localizes to the perinuclear region and is expressed in specific subpopulations of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) cells, where it contributes to the regulation of cell proliferation. Functional studies show that the 68AA micropeptide promotes ribosomal RNA (rRNA) transcription, accelerates cellular proliferation, and thereby supports HCC tumor growth [44]. Notably, this 68AA micropeptide undergoes phosphorylation both under endogenous conditions and upon exogenous expression. Owing to its specific expression in HCC cells and its role in driving cell proliferation, the Linc013026-68AA micropeptide represents a promising therapeutic target for liver cancer.

RBRP

The micropeptide RBRP, encoded by LINC00266-1, functions as a regulatory subunit of the m⁶A RNA methylation reader complex. It enhances the recognition of m⁶A-modified RNA by binding to IGF2BP1, thereby increasing the mRNA stability and protein expression of the oncogene c-Myc [50]. In colorectal cancer (CRC), Zhu et al. reported that elevated RBRP expression correlated significantly with advanced clinical stage (p = 0.027). Genetic knockout of LINC00266-1 markedly suppressed CRC cell proliferation, clonogenicity, migration, and invasion; re-expression of its wild-type ORF—but not a mutant version—restored these malignant phenotypes, confirming that the RBRP peptide acts as a tumor driver. High RBRP levels were significantly associated with aggressive pathological features and shorter patient survival, indicating its potential as an independent predictor of poor prognosis, a prognostic biomarker, and a therapeutic target in CRC [51]. Interactome profiling of RBRP revealed that its key interacting partners are predominantly involved in RNA splicing regulation (e.g., hnRNP family members, splicing factors, and snRNPs), many of which are known to regulate cancer hallmarks. This suggests that RBRP may promote tumorigenesis by modulating pre-mRNA splicing, underscoring the importance of further dissecting the functions of its protein interactors [52].

SRSP

The long non-coding RNA LOC90024 encodes a 130-amino-acid micropeptide, SRSP. Mechanistically, SRSP enhances the binding affinity of the splicing factor SRSF3 for exon 3 of the transcription factor Sp4 pre-mRNA, thereby promoting the retention of this exon. This alternative splicing event increases the production of the pro-oncogenic long Sp4 isoform (L-Sp4 protein) while suppressing the generation of the short Sp4 isoform (S-Sp4 peptide), which lacks a transcriptional activation domain [53]. In colorectal cancer (CRC), expression of both the LOC90024 transcript and its encoded product SRSP is significantly upregulated. Functional studies demonstrate that the key driver of CRC tumorigenesis and progression is the SRSP protein itself, rather than the LOC90024 lncRNA; silencing SRSP effectively inhibits tumorigenesis. Clinical analyses revealed that SRSP expression in CRC tumor tissues is significantly higher than in paired adjacent tissues. Moreover, patients with high SRSP expression showed a significantly increased risk of cancer-related death. Thus, SRSP is aberrantly overexpressed in CRC and correlates with poor prognosis, confirming its oncogenic role [53].

Evasion of growth inhibition: Inhibition of the tumor suppressor pathway

CIP2A-BP

The transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β) signaling pathway plays crucial roles in normal development and homeostasis, while its aberrant activation promotes tumor initiation, invasion, and metastasis [54]. In breast cancer, TGF-β can induce epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) and enrich cancer-stem-cell populations; conversely, inhibiting this pathway enhances chemotherapy sensitivity in triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) [55].

Research has shown that LINC00665 encodes the micropeptide CIP2A-BP. CIP2A-BP directly binds the oncoprotein CIP2A, thereby activating protein phosphatase 2 A (PP2A) activity, which subsequently inhibits the PI3K/AKT pathway and its downstream effector NF-κB. Active NF-κB upregulates MMP2/MMP9 and Snail while downregulating E-cadherin. TGF-β signaling, via the Smad pathway, induces expression of the translation repressor 4E-BP1; 4E-BP1 then binds eIF4E and suppresses the translation of LINC00665, reducing CIP2A-BP levels. In a high-TGF-β milieu, LINC00665 translation becomes insensitive to mTOR regulation, further intensifying TGF-β-mediated translational repression. In TNBC, this mechanism—involving the CIP2A/PP2A axis and the PI3K/AKT/NF-κB cascade—leads to upregulation of MMP2/MMP9/Snail and loss of E-cadherin, thereby driving invasion and metastasis. Functional rescue experiments confirmed that either overexpression of CIP2A-BP or exogenous administration of the peptide effectively inhibits lung metastasis and prolongs survival in tumor-bearing mice [56]. Thus, the CIP2A-BP micropeptide, which suppresses metastasis by activating PP2A, represents a potential candidate therapeutic agent for treating TNBC metastasis.

KRASIM

The abundance of KRAS protein is subject to post-transcriptional regulation mediated by non-coding RNAs [57]. Studies have identified that the lncRNA NCBP2-AS2 encodes a 99-amino-acid micropeptide, termed KRASIM, which is differentially expressed between normal hepatocytes and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) cells and acts to inhibit HCC growth and proliferation. Functional evidence indicates that the anti-proliferative and cell-cycle-regulatory effects originate from the micropeptide itself, rather than from the lncRNA.

Mechanistically, KRASIM specifically interacts with and co-localizes with the KRAS protein in the cytoplasm of HCC cells. The expression level of KRASIM directly modulates KRAS protein stability: knockdown of KRASIM elevates KRAS protein levels, whereas its overexpression reduces them, without affecting KRAS mRNA abundance. In both HuH-7 and SK-HEP-1 cells, overexpression of wild-type KRASIM (but not a frameshift mutant) significantly suppresses proliferation; correspondingly, silencing or overexpressing KRASIM enhances or attenuates downstream ERK pathway activity, respectively. Together, these findings demonstrate that KRASIM overexpression inhibits HCC cell proliferation by negatively regulating the KRAS/ERK signaling axis [58].

MIAC

The micropeptide MIAC, encoded by the lncRNA AC025154.2, functions as a tumor suppressor and shows low expression in various cancers. Downregulation of MIAC promotes tumor proliferation. Mechanistically, MIAC exerts its effects through binding to the aquaporin protein AQP2 [59]. In head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC), this interaction inhibits the expression of Septin 2 (Sept 2) and integrin β4 (ITGB4), thereby regulating the actin cytoskeleton to suppress tumor growth and metastasis [60].

In renal cell carcinoma, the binding of MIAC to AQP2 attenuates epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) signaling, specifically by inhibiting the expression of the EGFR ligand EREG. This mediates its antitumor effects through suppression of the PI3K/AKT and MAPK signaling pathways [61].

PINT87aa

In senescent hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), expression of the micropeptide PINT87aa, encoded by LINC-PINT, is upregulated. PINT87aa inhibits the transcriptional activity of FOXM1 by binding to its DNA-binding domain, thereby inducing cellular senescence and proliferation arrest [62, 63]. Overexpression of PINT87aa suppresses HCC proliferation, promotes senescence, and reduces mitophagy; conversely, FOXM1 overexpression partially reverses the senescent phenotype and enhances autophagy. While PINT87aa does not alter FOXM1 protein levels, it downregulates a key downstream target gene of FOXM1, PHB2 (a factor involved in mitophagy and cancer progression [64, 65]). In senescent HCC, FOXM1 overexpression upregulates Ki67 and PHB2, counteracting the pro-senescent effects of PINT87aa. Thus, PINT87aa induces senescence by blocking FOXM1-mediated transcriptional activation of PHB2 [66], whereas FOXM1 in turn exerts epistatic regulation over PINT87aa by activating PHB2.

Resist cell death: Block apoptosis/senescence

AC15619-22aa

The lncRNA AC115619 encodes a 22-amino-acid micropeptide, AC115619-22aa, which inhibits hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) progression by directly binding to the WTAP protein and disrupting the assembly of the N⁶-methyladenosine (m⁶A) methyltransferase complex. This complex regulates the expression of multiple tumor-related genes, including SOCS2 and ATG14. Notably, AC115619 shows coordinated transcriptional behavior with its adjacent upstream coding gene, APOB. Under hypoxic conditions, activation of the HIF1A/HDAC3 and HNF4A signaling pathways mediates transcriptional downregulation of both APOB and AC115619. In vivo studies confirmed that overexpression of AC115619-22aa significantly reduces global m⁶A modification levels and effectively suppresses tumor growth in both animal models and patient-derived xenograft (PDX) models [67].

LINC02381-aa

Ferroptosis is an iron-dependent form of regulated cell death characterized by excessive lipid peroxide accumulation. It is closely linked to disease pathogenesis and represents a potential therapeutic strategy [68]. In glioblastoma (GBM), exosome-derived non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs) are abundant and stable and contribute to malignant progression; one of their key targets is the glucose transporter SLC2A10, which mediates substance transport across the blood–brain barrier [69]. The SLC2A10-associated lncRNA LINC02381 is highly expressed in GBM and localizes to both the cytoplasm and exosomes. It encodes the exosomal micropeptide LINC02381-aa, which modulates the ferroptosis pathway in GBM cells via SLC2A10 [70]. Thus, LINC02381 and its encoded peptide LINC02381-aa represent potential diagnostic biomarkers as well as prognostic and therapeutic targets for GBM.

PACMP

Human CtIP is a critical nuclear protein with multifaceted roles in DNA metabolism and genome maintenance, exhibiting context-dependent tumor-suppressive and pro-oncogenic functions in cancer [71].

Research has shown that the lncRNA CTD-2256P15.2 encodes the micropeptide PACMP, which protects the CtIP protein via two distinct mechanisms: (1) it interacts with the E3 ligase adaptor protein KLHL15, thereby inhibiting CtIP ubiquitination and degradation to maintain its stability; and (2) it directly binds damage-induced poly(ADP-ribose) (PAR) chains, enhancing PARP1-dependent PARylation and promoting DNA repair. In breast cancer, upregulation of CTD-2256P15.2 (lnc15.2) is associated with poor prognosis. The lnc15.2/PACMP axis influences tumor progression and therapy resistance by modulating CtIP abundance and PARylation levels. Functionally, targeting the PACMP micropeptide—rather than its parent RNA—inhibits tumor growth. This effect stems from the disruption of CtIP-protective pathways, leading to CtIP functional deficiency and establishing a synthetic lethal interaction with PARP inhibition. Depletion or inhibition of PACMP significantly sensitizes tumor cells to multiple therapies, including PARP inhibitors, ATR inhibitors, CDK4/6 inhibitors, ionizing radiation, and chemotherapeutic agents such as epirubicin and camptothecin [72].

PINT87aa

The circular RNA circPINTexon2, spliced from LINC-PINT, is translated independently in gliomas to produce the 87-amino acid, nucleus-localized micropeptide PINT87aa—a function not supported by its linear transcript counterpart. PINT87aa is essential for normal cell survival but is frequently lost in various cancer cells; its absence accelerates the cell cycle and promotes proliferation. Both circPINTexon2 and its encoded micropeptide are detectable in normal brain tissue but are markedly downregulated in brain tumors (mirroring the pattern of linear LINC-PINT). Knockdown of PINT87aa in normal cells significantly reduces cell viability. circPINTexon2 and PINT87aa are highly expressed in the brain, liver, kidney, and stomach, and expressed at low levels in tissues such as breast and intestine. Their tumor-suppressive activity depends on the micropeptide PINT87aa rather than on the circular RNA itself [42]. Molecularly, PINT87aa co-localizes with the PAF1 complex (PAF1c) in the nucleus and directly interacts with it. This binding specifically inhibits transcriptional elongation of multiple proto-oncogenes [73, 74]. The low expression of circPINTexon2/PINT87aa in gliomas is therefore likely to promote tumorigenesis by derepressing oncogene transcription.

Induce angiogenesis: Promote the production of factors such as VEGF

ASPRS

Bioinformatic and experimental studies have established that LINC00908 encodes the polypeptide ASRPS. Its expression is regulated by estrogen receptor α (ERα), which binds to its promoter, leading to specific expression in TNBC cells [45]. In TNBC models, either knocking out ASRPS or knocking down LINC00908 RNA accelerated tumor growth, but simultaneous knockdown of both did not produce an additive effect. Conversely, complementation with either full-length LINC00908 or the ASRPS peptide in ASRPS-knockout cells inhibited tumor growth. Mechanistically, ASRPS directly binds to STAT3, inhibiting its phosphorylation and transcriptional activity. This interaction blocks the IL-6-induced STAT3 activation pathway and, via the downstream STAT3/VEGF axis, inhibits tumor angiogenesis. Preclinical studies demonstrated that intratumoral injection of ASRPS prolongs survival in TNBC mouse models. Clinical analyses indicate that ASRPS exerts this anti-angiogenic effect through the same pathway in TNBC patients. In summary, ASRPS is an endogenous polypeptide that exerts anti-tumor effects by inhibiting angiogenesis via the STAT3/VEGF pathway [45].

XBP1SBM

Rapid tumor proliferation results in nutrient deprivation, such as glutamine (Gln) deficiency [75, 76], which can trigger endoplasmic reticulum stress (ERS) [77]. Cells respond via the unfolded protein response (UPR), which comprises the IRE1α-XBP1, ATF6, and PERK branches [77]. Among these, the IRE1α-XBP1 pathway critically regulates triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) growth, metastasis, and angiogenesis [78, 79]. Metabolomic profiling of TNBC tissues shows decreased Gln and elevated glutamate (Glu) levels, indicating heightened sensitivity of TNBC to Gln deprivation [79].

Research has identified that the lncRNA MLLT4-AS1 encodes the micropeptide XBP1SBM. XBP1SBM is expressed in various breast cancer cells but is significantly upregulated specifically in Gln-starved TNBC cells. Functional rescue experiments confirmed that in TNBC cells following knockout or knockdown of XBP1SBM/MLLT4-AS1, complementation with the XBP1SBM peptide or its coding sequence—but not the RNA alone—restores tumor growth, demonstrating that the pro-tumorigenic effect depends on the micropeptide. In TNBC, Gln deficiency activates XBP1 and upregulates XBP1SBM. XBP1SBM promotes the expression of pro-angiogenic factors such as VEGF, thereby driving tumor angiogenesis and metastasis, by stabilizing nuclear XBP1s. It may also contribute indirectly to maintaining Gln supply [80].

Activates invasive transfer: Mediates matrix remodeling

YY1BM

LINC00278 expression is downregulated in esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC), and its loss accelerates malignant progression. Overexpression of LINC00278 inhibits the interaction between the transcription factor YY1 and the androgen receptor (AR); conversely, disruption of YY1–AR binding upregulates LINC00278. This lncRNA encodes the micropeptide YY1BM, which regulates the downstream eEF2K/eEF2 pathway by inhibiting the transcriptional activities of both YY1 and AR. Notably, inactivation of eEF2 suppresses YY1BM translation, forming a LINC00278-YY1BM-YY1-AR-eEF2K/eEF2 negative-feedback loop. Environmental factors such as tobacco smoke upregulate the demethylase ALKBH5, which reduces m⁶A modification on LINC00278 and inhibits YY1BM translation, thereby promoting ESCC development [81]. Exogenous administration of the YY1BM micropeptide shows anti-tumor efficacy in xenograft models, indicating its therapeutic potential. In summary, the LINC00278/YY1BM axis integrates hormonal (AR), environmental (e.g., smoking), and genetic networks, representing a potential therapeutic target and prognostic biomarker for male ESCC.

Energy metabolism reprogramming: Alters glycolysis/oxidative phosphorylation/lipid metabolism

ASAP

The micropeptide encoded by LINC00467, a peptide rich in basic amino acids, is designated ATP Synthase-Associated Peptide (ASAP) [43]. ASAP is expressed at low levels in normal intestinal epithelial cells. Overexpression of ASAP in NCM460 cells [82]. significantly promotes proliferation and increases ATP synthesis, whereas its knockout did not induce substantial phenotypic changes. Subcellular localization studies confirmed that ASAP endogenously localizes to mitochondria. Mechanistically, ASAP directly binds to the α and γ subunits of ATP synthase (encoded by ATP5A and ATP5C, respectively), stabilizes the assembly of the enzyme complex, and enhances its activity and mitochondrial oxygen consumption, thereby driving the proliferation of colorectal cancer (CRC) cells. Conversely, ASAP deletion suppresses the growth of patient-derived tumor xenografts, accompanied by reduced ATP synthase activity and mitochondrial ATP production, indicating that ASAP influences CRC growth by regulating mitochondrial ATP synthesis [43]. Clinical data show that high expression of LINC00467/ASAP correlates with poor prognosis in CRC patients, and further studies have confirmed the functional role of ASAP in CRC [83]. Additionally, ASAP has been implicated in regulating the progression of leukemia 84, non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) [85], and esophageal cancer [86].

HOXB-AS3

Jin-Zhou Huang, Min Chen, and colleagues identified the endogenous micropeptide HOXB-AS3, which is encoded by the lncRNA HOXB-AS3. In colorectal cancer (CRC), this peptide acts as a tumor suppressor: it blocks hnRNP A1-mediated aberrant splicing of PKM pre-mRNA, thereby inhibiting production of the oncogenic isoform PKM2 and suppressing cancer-associated glucose metabolic reprogramming. HOXB-AS3 inhibits CRC cell proliferation, colony formation, migration, and invasion in vitro and suppresses tumorigenesis in vivo [13]. Loss of HOXB-AS3 expression correlates with aggressive pathological features and poor prognosis in CRC patients. HOXB-AS3 shows heterogeneous expression across cancer types. Its levels are elevated in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), where it promotes radiation-induced tumor growth by modulating the PI3K/AKT pathway [87]; it is also among the dysregulated genes in head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) [88]. In summary, the HOXB-AS3-encoded peptide plays a significant tumor-suppressive role.

MPM

The NAD⁺/NADH balance functions as a central hub linking cellular metabolism with signaling pathways [89, 90]. In hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), the mitochondrial micropeptide MPM, encoded by the lncRNA LINC00116, is significantly downregulated, and its low expression correlates with an elevated risk of metastasis and recurrence. Overexpression of MPM decreases the cellular NAD⁺/NADH ratio, inhibits cell migration and invasion in vitro, and suppresses metastasis in vivo; conversely, silencing MPM increases the NAD⁺/NADH ratio and promotes malignant phenotypes. The inhibitory effect of MPM on migration could be reversed by treatment with nicotinamide, an NAD⁺ precursor. The underlying mechanism involves several layers of regulation: miR-17-5p negatively regulates MPM expression by targeting its mRNA 3′ UTR. Functionally, MPM directly binds to the mitochondrial Complex I accessory subunit NDUFA7. Consequently, MPM overexpression inhibits Complex I activity (thereby reducing the NAD⁺/NADH ratio), whereas its silencing enhances Complex I activity (increasing the NAD⁺/NADH ratio). The enhanced Complex I activity induced by MPM silencing was attenuated upon knockout of NDUFA7 [91]. These findings establish a molecular pathway through which MPM suppresses HCC metastasis by targeting mitochondrial Complex I to regulate NAD⁺ metabolism, thereby revealing a potential target for therapeutic intervention in HCC progression.

Evade immune destruction: Inhibits immune recognition or promotes immunosuppressive microenvironment

MELOE-1/2/3

The lncRNA MELOE encodes three distinct micropeptides: MELOE-1 (39 aa), MELOE-2 (44 aa), and MELOE-3 (54 aa) [92–94]. The transcript contains a multicistronic structure, in which translation of MELOE-1 and MELOE-2 is driven by internal ribosome entry sites (IRES) [95], whereas MELOE-3 is produced via the canonical cap-dependent mechanism.Their expression patterns differ markedly: MELOE-1 and MELOE-2 are downregulated in melanoma tissues and are undetectable in normal melanocytes, whereas MELOE-3 is expressed at high levels in both melanoma and melanocytes. Furthermore, MELOE-3 displays low immunogenicity and may exhibit immune-tolerant properties. In contrast, MELOE-1 and MELOE-2 show high tumor-specific antigenicity in melanoma, and their expression correlates with T-cell-mediated immune surveillance. Together, the MELOE family polypeptides generated through IRES-dependent translation represent promising T-cell targets for melanoma immunotherapy [94].

Protein multifunctionality: Possessing multiple cancer biological characteristics

Proteins and micropeptides play diverse and critical roles in cellular processes, with particularly multifaceted regulatory functions in cancer biology. For instance, the micropeptide JunBP binds directly to the bZIP domain of c-Jun, enhances its JNK-dependent phosphorylation and activation, and thereby promotes its interaction with SMAD3. This forms a SMAD3/JunBP/c-Jun positive-feedback loop that drives the invasion and metastasis of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) [96]. Similarly, other micropeptides such as SMM30, encoded by LINCO0998, also promote HCC tumorigenesis [97]. These findings illustrate that micropeptides act not only as key regulatory nodes within signaling pathways but also contribute to multiple cancer hallmarks—including epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), cell migration, and invasion—by modulating transcriptional complex activity, chromatin architecture, and RNA-protein assemblies [98–100].

Moreover, the expression levels of many micropeptides are closely linked to clinical outcomes. For example, high JunBP expression is significantly associated with reduced overall survival (OS) and disease-free survival (DFS) in HCC patients and serves as an independent prognostic factor in multivariate analysis [96]. Notably, micropeptides encoded by ncRNAs can simultaneously impinge on several core cancer hallmarks, such as metabolic reprogramming, sustained proliferation, angiogenesis, invasion and metastasis, and resistance to cell death. This expands our understanding of cancer regulatory networks and, owing to their high specificity and low immunogenicity, positions them as a highly promising resource for the development of novel diagnostic markers, prognostic indicators, and peptide-targeted therapies [38, 101]. ACLY-BP(Maintain proliferation signaling and reprogram energy metabolism)

ATP-citrate lyase (ACLY) is a key enzyme that catalyzes the rate-limiting step of de novo lipid synthesis. It is frequently overexpressed or aberrantly activated in multiple cancers, driving lipid production and accelerating tumor progression [66, 102], [103]. The transcription factor GATA3, essential for kidney development and post-injury regeneration, is significantly downregulated in clear-cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC) [104, 105]. Studies have revealed that the transcription of LINC00887 is negatively regulated by GATA3. Consequently, the expression of ACLY-BP, a micropeptide encoded by LINC00887, is elevated under low-GATA3 conditions such as those in ccRCC. In this context, ACLY-BP enhances the stability of the ACLY protein by interfering with its SIRT2-mediated deacetylation. This leads to increased ACLY protein levels, promoting the production of its catalytic product acetyl-CoA and the accumulation of intracellular lipids. Functional studies demonstrate that ACLY-BP promotes tumor cell proliferation in both cellular assays and animal models [106]. Thus, targeting ACLY-BP may represent a potential therapeutic strategy to suppress tumor growth in ccRCC.

JUNBP(Evasion of growth inhibition activates invasive metastasis)

The multifunctional cytokine TGF-β plays a central role in chronic liver disease and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) [107–110]. In HCC, TGF-β stimulation upregulates the micropeptide JunBP, which is encoded by the LINC02551 gene. JunBP facilitates the TGF-β-induced nuclear interaction between SMAD3 and c-Jun, a key effector of the MAPK pathway [111, 112]. JunBP itself is transcriptionally activated by SMAD3 and functions by enhancing the phosphorylation-dependent activation of c-Jun, thereby increasing its affinity for SMAD3. The activated c-Jun binds SMAD3 more stably, recruiting additional SMAD3 to the LINC02551 promoter and establishing a JunBP–SMAD3–c-Jun positive-feedback loop. Functionally, JunBP promotes HCC metastasis by binding to and activating c-Jun: TGF-β treatment exacerbates intrahepatic and pulmonary metastasis, whereas knockdown of JunBP partially reverses this pro-metastatic effect [96]. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that JunBP mediates TGF-β signaling to drive epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), invasion, and metastasis in HCC [113].

SMIM26(Evasion of growth inhibitors energy metabolism reprogramming)

The microprotein SMIM26, encoded by LINC00493, localizes to the outer mitochondrial membrane. In clear-cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC), SMIM26 is downregulated, and its low expression correlates with poor patient prognosis. Functional studies demonstrate that SMIM26 acts as a tumor suppressor: by binding to acylglycerol kinase (AGK), it promotes the mitochondrial translocation of AGK, thereby inhibiting activation of the AKT signaling pathway [114]. SLC25A11, a core component of the malate-aspartate shuttle that regulates ATP production, is linked to metastatic paraganglioma when carrying loss-of-function mutations [115]. In ccRCC cells, the interaction of SMIM26 with SLC25A11 within mitochondria significantly elevates mitochondrial glutathione (GSH) levels, ATP content, and oxygen consumption rate (OCR). Notably, knockdown of SLC25A11 reverses the suppressive effects of SMIM26 overexpression on cancer cell migration and invasion [114]. Mechanistically, SMIM26 interacts with two key partners: SLC25A11, a regulator of glutathione transport, and AGK. The binding of SMIM26 to AGK drives AGK translocation to mitochondria, which blocks AGK-dependent AKT phosphorylation and subsequent AKT pathway activation.

SMIM30(Maintains proliferative signaling and activates invasion and metastasis)

The lncRNA LINC00998 encodes the endogenous micropeptide SMIM30, which drives hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) progression by promoting tumor cell proliferation and migration. Its elevated expression is significantly associated with poor patient prognosis [97]. Mechanistically, SMIM30 expression is regulated by the transcription factor c-Myc. The peptide then facilitates the translocation of the non-receptor tyrosine kinases SRC/YES1 to the plasma membrane [116, 117]. Activated SRC/YES1 subsequently activates the downstream MAPK signaling cascade, thereby mediating HCC proliferation and metastasis. Loss-of-function studies confirm that knockout of LINC00998/SMIM30 inhibits the cell cycle, proliferation, and migration in vitro, and impedes tumor growth and metastasis in vivo [118]. Its elevated levels correlate with shortened patient survival, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic target and a diagnostic/prognostic biomarker for HCC [97].

SPAR (Evasion of growth inhibitors energy metabolism reprogramming)

The micropeptide SPAR, encoded by LINC00961, is functionally conserved across mammals, yet its mechanisms of action are highly context-dependent, varying with cell type and pathological state. During skeletal muscle regeneration, SPAR localizes to the lysosomal membrane, where it binds to and stabilizes the v-ATPase–Ragulator–Rag GTPase supercomplex. This interaction specifically inhibits amino-acid-activated mTORC1, thereby negatively regulating muscle regenerative capacity. This finding first demonstrated that lncRNA-encoded peptides can precisely modulate tissue-specific signaling pathways [16].

In breast cancer, human SPAR (hSPAR) exerts tumor-suppressive activity through a distinct mechanism. Its C-terminal domain interacts with the E3 ubiquitin ligase TRIM21, blocking TRIM21-mediated ubiquitination and degradation of P27KIP1 to maintain its cytoplasmic stability. Simultaneously, hSPAR downregulates the glutamine transporter SLC38A2, reducing intracellular glutamine levels and promoting the translocation of P27KIP1 to the lysosomal membrane. There, P27KIP1 competitively binds the Ragulator scaffold protein LAMTOR1, disrupting mTORC1 assembly and ultimately inhibiting the mTOR signaling pathway, which suppresses tumor proliferation. Notably, the hSPAR-C domain alone retains tumor-suppressive activity, and the synthetic fusion peptide TAT–hSPAR-C significantly inhibits tumor growth in vivo [119].

Through a systematic review of the cases of lncRNA-encoded micropeptides discussed above, several common themes emerge. In terms of shared regulatory logic, the expression of many micropeptides is tightly controlled by core transcription factors (e.g., Myc, ERα) or upstream signaling pathways such as TGF-β [e.g., C20orf204-189aa, ASRPS, JUNBP, SMIM30]; concurrently, their translation is often dynamically regulated by RNA modifications including m⁶A, representing a key layer of post-transcriptional control [e.g., RBRP, YY1BM].Regarding convergent mechanisms of action, many micropeptides act as “molecular glues” or stabilizers that directly bind to and modulate the activity, stability, or assembly of key protein complexes—such as translation initiation factors, metabolic enzymes, and signaling proteins—thereby amplifying or suppressing specific cellular functions [e.g., APPLE, RBRP, ACLY-BP]. These activities depend on intrinsic structural features, including targeting signals that dictate subcellular localization (e.g., to mitochondria, endoplasmic reticulum, or nucleus) [e.g., APPLE, ASAP, MPM, SMIM26], as well as conformational motifs or short linear sequences that mediate specific protein-protein interactions (though most such motifs remain uncharacterized) [e.g., CIP2A-BP, KRASIM, PINT87aa].From the perspective of shared signaling pathways, micropeptide functions frequently converge on classic oncogenic cascades such as MAPK/ERK [e.g., KRASIM, SMIM30], PI3K/AKT [e.g., CIP2A-BP, MIAC, SMIM26], and TGF-β/SMAD [e.g., CIP2A-BP, JUNBP]. They also widely participate in tumor metabolic reprogramming, influencing energy metabolism (e.g., ATP synthesis, NAD⁺/NADH balance) and lipid synthesis [e.g., ASAP, MPM, ACLY-BP, SMIM26]. The identification of these common features indicates that lncRNA-encoded micropeptides intervene in core tumor-biological processes through convergent molecular logic.

The potential impact of lncRNA-encoded micropeptide research on tumor clinical practice

Micropeptides offer considerable promise for disease diagnosis and prognosis assessment. In oncology, low expression of MIAC, a micropeptide encoded by the lncRNA AC025154.2, correlates with better overall survival in patients with head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) [60]. Conversely, in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), low expression of SMIM30, encoded by LINC00998, is associated with poor patient survival, marking it as a potential diagnostic biomarker and therapeutic target [97]. In interstitial cystitis/bladder pain syndrome (IC/BPS), urinary peptidomic profiling revealed alterations in 71 peptides, five of which reliably distinguish patients from healthy controls [120]. Mechanistically, micropeptides participate in central pathological processes. For example, the micropeptide PACMP protects CtIP by inhibiting its ubiquitination and promoting PARP1 activity [72]. During tumor development, micropeptides regulate signaling cascades, metabolic reprogramming, and epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT). The tumor suppressor SMIM26, encoded by LINC00493, is downregulated in renal cancer, where its loss correlates with reduced survival and leads to inactivation of AKT signaling through binding to AGK [114]. In contrast, ACLY-BP, encoded by LINC00887, is upregulated in clear-cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC) and promotes tumor proliferation by inhibiting ACLY deacetylation, thereby increasing acetyl-CoA production and lipid deposition [106]. Targeting such micropeptides represents a promising frontier in therapeutic development. While challenges—including short half-life and relatively low binding affinity—remain, their druggability can be enhanced through peptide engineering strategies such as cyclization or glycosylation [23, 121].

Compared with the development of novel therapeutic micropeptides, the discovery and identification of cancer-associated micropeptides as biomarkers is relatively more feasible. Current research typically progresses from the initial discovery of a micropeptide to the investigation of its specific targets and functional roles within tumors. This approach refines the functional characterization and molecular targets of newly identified micropeptides, ultimately influencing the regulation of the tumor microenvironment. The identification of functional micropeptides has revealed an additional layer of exquisitely precise, efficient, and widespread regulatory systems in biology. This advance challenges the previous notion that lncRNA-encoded micropeptides are “undruggable.” Despite their small size, these peptides exhibit potent biological activity. They can transmit critical intercellular signals at very low concentrations and broadly participate in regulating nearly all life processes—including development, growth, immune defense, stress responses, metabolic homeostasis, and even behavioral patterns in animals and plants [23, 42–45].This represents not only a significant expansion of our understanding of life’ s regulatory networks but also a profound step forward in comprehending biological complexity and adaptability. It opens promising new avenues for the discovery of novel therapeutic targets, diagnostic tools, and treatment strategies for human diseases.

Today, leveraging their advantages of high specificity, low toxicity, and engineerability [122], micropeptides are transitioning from fundamental laboratory research towards clinical application. Functioning as bridging molecules linking the genome and proteome, micropeptides can integrate static genomic information with the dynamic functions of the proteome, enabling the exploration of their complex and previously unknown regulatory mechanisms. Future research will be critically important in elucidating the regulation of micropeptide generation by non-canonical translation initiation mechanisms (e.g., IRES, uORFs), deciphering their causal roles in disease pathogenesis, and unraveling their regulatory mechanisms within the tumor microenvironment (such as metabolic reprogramming and immune evasion). While micropeptides offer theoretical advantages as therapeutic agents—such as high specificity, low immunogenicity, and ease of engineering—and show promise in modulating key signaling pathways (e.g., STAT3, MAPK), metabolic enzymes (e.g., SQLE), or even acting as tumor-specific antigens (e.g., MELOE peptides) in immunotherapy, their clinical translation faces considerable challenges. First, stability and delivery efficiency remain major bottlenecks: micropeptides typically have a short in vivo half-life, are prone to proteolytic degradation, and their small size complicates both mass-spectrometric detection and drug development. Moreover, achieving efficient, tumor-specific delivery while crossing cellular membrane barriers remains a key unsolved problem. Second, immunogenicity risks and poorly defined pharmacokinetic profiles pose additional hurdles. Although many micropeptides are considered low-immunogenicity, they may still elicit unintended immune responses as exogenous agents. Systematic data on their absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion in vivo are still lacking. Further limitations include potential off-target effects, challenges in scalable manufacturing, and quality-control issues. To overcome these barriers, future efforts should prioritize enhancing peptide stability through protein engineering and chemical modifications (e.g., cyclization, PEGylation); designing smart, tumor-targeted delivery systems; and elucidating functional networks within the tumor microenvironment using single-cell multi-omics. Concurrently, systematic preclinical pharmacokinetic/toxicological evaluation and exploration of synergies with conventional therapies will be essential to advance micropeptides from “peptide discovery” toward “personalized therapeutics.”
