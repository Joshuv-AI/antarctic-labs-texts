A Quality Improvement Project to Limit Perioperative Transfusion During Craniofacial Surgery in Infants and Children
Authors: Grace Parizek, Annie Drapeau, Jonathan Pindrik, Gregory Pearson, Joseph D. Tobias, Ashley Smith
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Introduction:Open surgery for craniosynostosis in infants and children is frequently associated with significant intraoperative blood loss and the need for allogeneic transfusion therapy. A lack of consensus at our institution led to inconsistent transfusion therapy during open cranial vault remodeling. We sought to address this through a multidisciplinary quality improvement approach to decrease variability in transfusion practices.Methods:A streamlined intraoperative transfusion algorithm, from the moment the patient was evaluated in the preoperative holding area on the day of surgery through their departure from the postanesthesia care unit, was implemented during quarter 1 of 2021. We aimed to decrease the percentage of intraoperative transfusions administered at hemoglobin (Hgb) levels greater than 7.5 g/dL in stable patients without massive bleeding undergoing open craniosynostosis surgery from 40% in January 2021 to 0% by December 2023.Results:From January 2018 to December 2023, 118 pediatric patients underwent open cranial vault remodeling for craniosynostosis. Before the implementation of the protocol in quarter 1 of 2021, open cranial vault reconstruction (CVR) intraoperative transfusion rates in cases where the threshold was not met (Hgb >7.5 g/dL) were 32%. After protocol implementation, open CVR intraoperative transfusion rates that did not meet the threshold averaged 5%.Conclusions:In this quality improvement project, we decreased transfusion rates for patients with Hgb greater than 7.5 g/dL from 32% to 5% during 3 years. We have successfully developed and implemented an intraoperative blood transfusion algorithm that is generalizable and requires minimal human and material resources.

INTRODUCTION

Craniosynostosis is the premature fusion of 1 or more cranial sutures, leading to abnormal bone growth and compensatory changes that give rise to a misshapen skull.1 It has an incidence of approximately 1 per 2000 live births.2 If left untreated, craniosynostosis can lead to adverse effects, including developmental delay and increased intracranial pressure.2,3 Surgical intervention is recommended within the first year of life.2,4

The primary objectives of surgical intervention include providing ample space within the skull to allow normal brain development and creating a normocephalic appearance.5,6 Multiple surgical techniques exist and broadly fall into open cranial vault remodeling or minimally invasive craniectomy approaches. The minimally invasive approach, often performed with endoscopic assistance, is preferred in infants aged 2.5–4 months to take advantage of the accelerated cranial growth that occurs in those early months of life. Its benefits include quick recovery, minimal blood loss, and shorter operative times. Unfortunately, not all patients are candidates for minimally invasive approaches, and it may not be successful in older infants. Open cranial vault remodeling is preferred after 6 months of age because the cranial bones are thicker, head growth has slowed, and patients typically present with more established craniofacial abnormalities.6

Open craniosynostosis surgery allows for wider remodeling of the skull, but in infants and children, it is frequently associated with significant intraoperative blood loss. D’Amore et al2 found that the median intraoperative blood loss accounted for 58% of total blood volume. Because of this, transfusion of blood products is often required and has been reported to approach rates ranging from 78% to 100%.7–10 Although significant blood loss contributes to morbidity in invasive craniofacial procedures, the use of blood transfusions is independently associated with increased risk as well.11–13

The lack of an intraoperative transfusion protocol is one of multiple clinical factors independently associated with an increased risk for transfusion during cranial vault surgery.8 Although rare, transfusion-related complications include hemolytic and nonhemolytic reactions, immunomodulation, allergic reactions, exposure to infections, transfusion-related acute lung injury, and longer intensive care unit (ICU) stays.12–14 Multiple institutions have published their approaches and techniques to decrease blood transfusions, with many requiring the use of complex and expensive adjuncts such as preoperative erythropoietin or intraoperative autologous blood recovery (cell saver) techniques.15,16

At Nationwide Children’s Hospital (NCH), a lack of consensus among providers led to an inconsistent approach to transfusion during craniosynostosis surgery. We used quality improvement (QI) methodology to decrease the variability in transfusion practices. We aimed to decrease the percentage of intraoperative transfusions administered at hemoglobin (Hgb) levels greater than 7.5 g/dL in stable patients without massive bleeding undergoing open craniosynostosis surgery from 40% in January 2021 to 0% by December 2023.

METHODS

The NCH institutional review board determined this project did not meet the criteria for “human subjects research” and was exempt from formal institutional review board review. QI methodology, adapted from the Institute for Healthcare Improvement model, was used to decrease the variability in transfusion practices and to decrease the rate of blood transfusions in stable patients undergoing open cranial vault craniosynostosis surgery.17

The study time frame was January 2018 to December 2023, with intervention beginning in January 2021. An average of 40–50 pediatric patients per year receive craniosynostosis surgery at NCH, with approximately half using an open cranial vault remodeling approach. Because transfusion rates in minimally invasive craniectomy approached zero, these cases were excluded from this project. The primary outcome was the total intraoperative packed red blood cell transfusion rate in patients presenting for open cranial vault remodeling for craniosynostosis. Balancing measures included hospital length of stay, operative time, ICU length of stay, and lowest postoperative Hgb to monitor for unintended consequences of reduced intraoperative transfusion rates. The project’s team members involved the Center for Complex Craniofacial Disorders surgeons (2 plastic surgeons and 1 neurosurgeon), 2 pediatric anesthesiologists, and 2 certified registered nurse anesthetists.

We conducted a preliminary survey of anesthesiologists, neurosurgeons, plastic surgeons, and certified registered nurse anesthetists. Based on survey results, the identified main factors driving the decision to give a blood transfusion during these cases included: (1) age and weight of the patient; (2) type of surgical approach; (3) Hgb and hematocrit (Hct) levels in relation to timing/step during surgery; (4) fluid resuscitation; (5) hemodynamic status; and (6) team consensus. Our key driver diagram illustrates the components influencing intraoperative transfusion decisions, incorporating major factors identified through a staff survey. We grouped these factors into patient-related considerations, induction of anesthesia, general anesthesia management, and surgical drivers (Fig. 1). We consulted the blood bank manager and director of transfusion services while developing the intraoperative transfusion algorithm. Project support was provided by the pediatric neurosurgery section’s senior QI consultant.

Anesthetic Management

The anesthetic technique included inhalation induction with increasing inspired sevoflurane concentration. Following anesthetic induction, a nondepolarizing neuromuscular blocking agent (rocuronium) was administered to facilitate endotracheal intubation with a cuffed endotracheal tube. Two peripheral intravenous catheters and an arterial cannula were placed in addition to standard American Society of Anesthesiologists’ monitors with a temperature-monitoring indwelling urinary catheter. Cefazolin was administered for surgical site infection prophylaxis, unless contraindicated due to allergies, in which case clindamycin was used. Maintenance anesthesia included an inhalational agent (sevoflurane or desflurane), incremental doses of fentanyl, and a titrated opioid infusion (remifentanil or sufentanil). Intermittent rocuronium was administered for ongoing neuromuscular blockade. All patients received tranexamic acid, as it can reduce blood loss and transfusion requirements during craniosynostosis surgery.18 Tranexamic acid was administered as a 50-mg/kg bolus before surgical incision, followed by a continuous infusion at 5 mg/kg/h. All patients were started on isotonic crystalloid infusions at maintenance rates.

After anesthetic induction and patient positioning, the surgeon infiltrated the surgical site with bupivacaine 0.125% and epinephrine 1:400,000 based upon the patient’s weight (1.25 mg/kg). After procedure completion, residual neuromuscular blockade was reversed with sugammadex, and the patient was extubated awake. Incremental doses of fentanyl or morphine were administered to supplement postoperative analgesia. The patient was transported to the postanesthesia care unit (PACU) for immediate postoperative care, then transferred to the ICU.

Intraoperative Pathway to Limit Allogeneic Transfusion Therapy

A streamlined intraoperative transfusion algorithm, from preoperative holding area arrival to departure from the postanesthesia recovery unit (PACU), was proposed (Fig. 2). Anesthesia completed a preoperative assessment before surgery (Fig. 2A). Guidance was provided regarding the timing of intraoperative bloodwork (Fig. 2B). Baseline Hgb, Hct, and point-of-care arterial blood gases were obtained at the time of arterial line placement. The Hgb/Hct results from the laboratory complete blood count were compared with the point-of-care Hgb/Hct values from the arterial blood gas for correlation. Point-of-care laboratories were repeated once all bone drilling and craniotomy cuts were completed and once again in the PACU. At any time, the anesthesia team and/or the surgeons could request a repeat Hgb/Hct measurement.

Guidance on intraoperative fluid replacement (Fig. 2C) was provided based on the step of the surgical procedure, Hgb/Hct results, and the patient’s hemodynamic status. For this project, hemodynamic stability was defined as a Hgb level greater than 7.5 g/dL in the absence of ongoing hemorrhage or physiological instability (eg, persistent hypotension, tachycardia requiring intervention). Recommendations on the type and quantity of crystalloid and colloid were also provided. Finally, recommendations regarding blood product administration based on the surgical procedure step, Hgb/Hct results, hemodynamic status, fluid resuscitation, and potential surgical complications were provided (Fig. 2D, Table 1).

The final decision to transfuse packed red blood cells required consensus from the anesthesia team and surgeons, and transfusion data were recorded from the intraoperative period through discharge from the hospital. The use of the algorithm was documented in the neurosurgeon’s operative report. No modifications were made to the type of general anesthesia or to the surgical technique.

Data Collection and Statistical Analysis

Data were collected from January 2018 to December 2023 through chart review of patients with a diagnosis of craniosynostosis who underwent open cranial vault remodeling. The International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision code Q75.0 was used to diagnose craniosynostosis. Craniosynostosis surgery was determined by one of the following Common Procedural Technology codes: 61550, 61552, 61557, 61559, with or without 20692. Those undergoing a minimal invasive (or endoscopic) craniectomy approach were excluded. The included patients were cross-referenced with the neurosurgeon’s surgical case log to verify accuracy and completeness.

The data were collected by the Quality Improvement Services team and presented in an automated monthly spreadsheet (Microsoft, Redmond, WA). Outcome measures were displayed in statistical process control charts, allowing the team to study process changes over time and identify common cause and special cause variations. For the statistical process control charts, we used the American Society for Quality criteria to adjust the centerline and control limits, and we evaluated each of our outcomes at monthly and quarterly intervals.19

RESULTS

The cohort included 118 patients: 57 during the baseline period of January 2018 to December 2020 and 61 during the intervention period of January 2021 to December 2023, with an average age of 10.17 months (interquartile range 0–80 mo). The transfusion rates had approached 100% (2018), 75% (2019), and 63.3% (2020), with NCH’s total transfusion rate for open cranial vault remodeling starting at 71.9% (Fig. 3). After protocol implementation, 61 patients underwent open cranial vault remodeling, and NCH’s total transfusion rate dropped to 62.3% (Fig. 3). Before protocol implementation, the rate of blood transfusion in stable patients undergoing open cranial vault remodeling, defined as a Hgb value greater than 7.5 g/dL, was 30%–35% (Fig. 4). We excluded 2 patients from the analysis due to their Jehovah’s Witness faith (surgeries in January 2019 and April 2020). One patient was removed from the study because their surgery was a revision only, and another was removed from analysis due to case complexity (surgeries in June 2019 and April 2022).

After implementation, transfusions given to stable patients with a Hgb greater than 7.5 g/dL decreased from a baseline of 32%–5% (Fig. 4). Three of 61 craniosynostosis patients received blood transfusions despite being hemodynamically stable. These patients had an average surgical time of 188 minutes, a length of stay of 4 days, and an ICU length of stay of 1 day. The hemodynamically stable patients who did not receive intraoperative transfusions also did not receive a transfusion in the postoperative period. Among 61 craniosynostosis patients, 35 received blood transfusions because their Hgb was greater than 7.5 g/dL. Of those 35 blood transfusions, 10 were transfused during the postoperative period in the ICU, 24 were transfused intraoperatively, and 1 patient received both an intraoperative and postoperative blood transfusion.

For the 38 total patients who received blood transfusions, the average length of operation was 246 ± 69 minutes, the average length of hospital stay was 4.9 ± 2.7 days, and the average length of ICU stay was 1.4 ± 1.2 days. For the 23 patients who did not receive blood transfusions, the average length of operation was 184 ± 49 minutes, the average length of hospital stay was 3.5 ± 0.7 days, and the average length of ICU stay was 1 ± 0.1 days. Although the average length of operation was slightly longer in the transfused cohort, the length of hospital and ICU stay was comparable between the transfused and nontransfused cohorts, including when the primary neurosurgeon changed at the end of 2022.

All postoperative transfusions given to patients with a Hgb 7.5 g/dL or lower occurred within 1 day after the open cranial vault remodeling operation. No transfusions were required beyond postoperative day 1. No complications occurred in patients who did not receive blood transfusions. No blood transfusion–related complications were reported. The only intraoperative complication occurred before protocol implementation (January 2018), consisting of a small dural tear with venous hemorrhage. The patient’s lowest intraoperative Hgb was 6.1 g/dL, for which they received an intraoperative blood transfusion (18 mL/kg), and were stabilized with a Hgb of 11.5 g/dL. Using QI methodology, this project significantly reduced overall blood transfusion rates, with the rate of intraoperative transfusions given to patients undergoing open cranial vault surgery with a Hgb greater than 7.5 g/dL decreasing from 32% to 5% (P < 0.01). DISCUSSION

Our application of the intraoperative blood transfusion algorithm was associated with a 26.4% reduction in transfusions, with the 2023 transfusion rate of 5.17% in patients with a Hgb greater than 7.5 g/dL (Fig. 4). This successful initiative highlights the benefits of having a multidisciplinary Center for Complex Craniofacial Disorders, creating consistency and efficiency among the surgical team during craniofacial surgeries. Beethe et al20 performed a 5-year QI project consisting of stepwise changes in the perioperative protocol for cranial vault remodeling for craniosynostosis to reduce total transfusion rates. Our protocol was similar in that all patients received preincisional local and intraoperative tranexamic acid. Our study’s blood transfusion protocol focused on minimizing intraoperative transfusions in patients with a Hgb greater than 7.5 g/dL, whereas the protocol by Beethe et al focused on minimizing postoperative blood transfusions in patients with a Hgb greater than 7 g/dL. By the end of their 5-year project, their total allogenic transfusion rate decreased from 100% (2013) to 22.7% (2018), but all patients received autotransfusions via cell saver.20 Comparatively, our total transfusion rate decreased from 66.7% (2018) to 8.3% (2023). In our project, the cell saver was not used, and patient blood loss was supplemented by transfusions only if the patient met the intraoperative transfusion criteria. Our decision to focus on a slightly higher Hgb threshold (>7.5 g/dL) was mainly in an abundance of caution for the safety of the patients and to allow an easier adaptation to a new protocol. Also, we did not use an autologous blood recovery system due to the associated costs, the need to involve a perfusionist to operate the machine, and its incompatibility with the use of flowable gelatin-based hemostatic matrix agents21 (eg, Surgiflo), an extremely useful hemostatic agent used routinely by the surgeons. Overall, the similarity in surgical time and length of hospital stay between transfused and nontransfused patients suggests that the reduction in transfusion rates did not negatively impact operative efficiency or postoperative recovery, supporting the safety of the intraoperative blood transfusion protocol.

Another study conducted by Krajewski et al22 focused on craniosynostosis repair during the infant’s physiological nadir of Hgb (3–6 mo old) and decreasing intraoperative blood transfusion rates. Patients were divided into a study group, which received recombinant human erythropoietin (Procrit) preoperatively and cell saver intraoperatively, and a control group.22 Although our studies are comparable in surgery performed, our quality improvement project did not use Procrit or cell saver, and our patient population averaged about 10 months old, whereas theirs averaged 5–6 months old. When comparing our rate of 8.3% blood transfusions to the 22.7% reported by Beethe et al20 with cell saver and the 31% reported by Krajewski et al22 without cell saver, our project demonstrates substantially lower total transfusion rates. Although both studies highlight the variability in blood transfusion protocols for craniosynostosis surgeries and the need for standardization, our quality improvement project is distinct in that it achieves results without relying on costly adjuncts, making it broadly applicable across institutions with varying resource levels.

Nonsyndromic craniosynostosis cranial vault remodeling has been associated with fewer intraoperative complications and blood transfusions than syndromic craniosynostosis.23 Our project found there was no statistically significant difference between intraoperative transfusions given to syndromic (eg, Apert or Crouzon) versus nonsyndromic patients during open cranial vault remodeling. (See table S2, Supplemental Digital Content 2, which displays the intraoperative transfusion rates for syndromic and nonsyndromic children undergoing open cranial vault remodeling before and after intraoperative blood transfusion algorithm implementation, https://links.lww.com/PQ9/A727.) One limitation of these findings is that one of the excluded patients (surgery April 2022) was excluded due to case complexity. This patient, only 24 days old, had extensive multisuture craniosynostosis, obstructive sleep apnea, and Pfeiffer syndrome. Due to these factors, the standard blood transfusion protocol was not used.

One limitation of this quality improvement project includes the size of the study population after protocol implementation. A smaller postprotocol sample size can limit the generalizability of the data to all craniosynostosis surgeries and reduces the power of the project to identify statistically significant differences between subsets of the population. As the primary neurosurgeon leading the quality improvement project also served as the neurosurgeon for open cranial vault remodeling, there may have been an inherent bias toward accepting lower thresholds for blood loss and intraoperative Hgb levels, influencing transfusion decisions.20 Although the intraoperative transfusion algorithm encourages discussion between the anesthesiologist and surgical team, taking into account the hemodynamic status of the patient, the primary decider on whether or not to transfuse blood products was the Hgb value. There were no standardized values defined for vital signs or other indicators of hemodynamic stability that served as a threshold for blood transfusion. This lack of standardized thresholds for hemodynamic stability can reduce the consistency and generalizability of the study, as providers may use different criteria to assess stability. In October 2022, a change in team members occurred with the departure and replacement of one of the neurosurgeons (Fig. 4). There was an increase in intraoperative transfusion rates for patients with a Hgb greater than 7.5 g/dL during quarter 4 of 2022 and quarter 1 of 2023 (n = 3; Supplemental Digital Content 1, https://links.lww.com/PQ9/A726). The new primary neurosurgeon initiated blood transfusion following a discussion between the surgical and anesthesiology teams due to a downward Hgb trend (n = 1) and a low starting Hgb and Hct with further blood loss anticipated based on the stage of surgery (n = 2). During the transition of the surgical team, a temporary decrease in full protocol compliance was expected as new members became familiar with and fully adopted the established protocol. Even with this transition, there was a statistically significant improvement in transfusion rates (Supplemental Digital Content 1, https://links.lww.com/PQ9/A726). Future directions include implementing quarterly or biannual meetings with the project team to ensure adherence to the blood transfusion algorithm.

Our study demonstrated a statistically significant reduction in intraoperative blood transfusions for patients with Hgb greater than 7.5 g/dL undergoing open craniosynostosis surgery. These findings suggest that a standardized intraoperative blood transfusion algorithm can effectively reduce variability in transfusion practices. Because this algorithm requires minimal human or material resources, it could easily be adopted by other institutions with multidisciplinary teams performing open cranial vault remodeling for craniosynostosis.
