Distinct hyaluronan content and hierarchical organization of collagen-elastic fiber assemblies in subcutaneous fascia
Authors: Natsuki Maeda, Kenshi Togashi, Yongchol Shin, Yasutada Imamura
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Previous studies have demonstrated that the subcutaneous fascia is a continuous connective tissue layer consisting of morphologically distinct “loose regions” and “dense regions”. However, the molecular basis of this regional heterogeneity remains unclear. This study aimed to analyze the biochemical and compositional differences between these two regions to elucidate the factors responsible for their functional and structural distinctions. Loose and dense fascia were isolated from chicken subcutaneous fascia and subjected to collagen composition analysis using SDS-PAGE, hyaluronan (HA) quantification, and fluorescence and electron microscopy combined with various biochemical treatments.The results showed no significant differences in the composition ratios of major fibrillar collagens (types I, III, and V) or in total collagen content per dry weight between the two regions. In contrast, significant differences were found in non-collagenous matrix components; specifically, loose fascia contained approximately twice as much HA as dense fascia (loose: 0.31%, dense: 0.15%). Collagen hybridizing peptide (CHP) staining, which detects denatured/disrupted collagen chains, showed strong signals at the bundle boundaries in dense fascia, suggesting region-specific differences in supramolecular organization and locally increased collagen triple-helix dynamics/instability at bundle peripheries. In addition, elastase treatment led to loosening/disruption of collagen bundles, an observation consistent with a contribution of elastic-fiber components to maintaining higher-order bundle integrity, although enzyme purity and secondary mechanical effects cannot be fully excluded.These results suggest that the regional mechanical behaviors are not fully explained by differences in major collagen composition and are more strongly associated with differences in hyaluronan abundance and supramolecular organization. These findings provide a molecular and ultrastructural basis that may underlie the distinct mechanical behaviors previously reported for loose and dense regions of the subcutaneous fascia.

Introduction

Subcutaneous fascia is a fibrous connective tissue layer within the subcutaneous tissue, located immediately beneath the dermis, that contributes to force transmission, mechanical support, and interlayer gliding between adjacent tissue layers [[1], [2], [3], [4]]. Within the subcutaneous fascia, regional heterogeneity is commonly described as morphologically distinct loose and dense regions, which have been associated with differences in architecture and mechanical behavior [5]. Although these regional features have been characterized largely through morphological observations, the molecular and biochemical basis underlying this heterogeneity remains incompletely understood.

The subcutaneous fascia is distributed widely across the body surface and is a critical structure for facilitating gliding between the skin and the underlying deep fascia and muscle tissue during movement. Recent research has revealed that the subcutaneous fascia is not a homogeneous layer but comprises loose and dense regions with distinct mechanical properties [5]. Loose regions are characterized by sparse collagen fibers and abundant adipocytes, whereas dense regions feature densely aligned fiber bundles. In addition, prior studies have suggested that elastic fibers may contribute to fiber-bundle formation in dense regions.

However, these prior findings have been derived largely from morphological and macroscopic observations, and the molecular and biochemical basis underlying the formation of loose and dense regions remains to be fully elucidated. Although previous studies have suggested potential contributions of elastic fibers, a systematic and integrated analysis of region-specific molecular differences relevant to tissue mechanics remains limited, including fibrillar collagen composition and supramolecular organization, extracellular matrix components such as hyaluronan (HA), and elastic-fiber–associated constituents.

A clearer understanding of the molecular composition and biochemical organization of the subcutaneous fascia will deepen insight into regional tissue mechanics and may inform clinically relevant conditions in which fascial properties are implicated, including aging, obesity, impaired gliding, and pain.

Against this background, we aimed to characterize the molecular composition and supramolecular organization of the loose and dense regions of the subcutaneous fascia and to determine how these features differ between regions.

Materials and methods

Materials

Chicken subcutaneous fascia specimens were obtained from commercially available broiler chickens intended for food consumption (post-mortem tissues) purchased from a local retailer in Hachioji, Tokyo, Japan. No live-animal procedures were performed by the authors.

Ethics statement

As this study used commercially obtained post-mortem tissues and did not involve live-animal experimentation, ethical approval for animal experiments was not required according to institutional guidelines.

Preparation of the subcutaneous fascia samples

The subcutaneous fascia samples were collected according to a previously reported method [5]. The membranous layer between the skin and muscle was peeled from commercially available chicken breasts, and extensibility was confirmed by gentle traction with tweezers. Areas that extended well were defined as “loose fascia,” and areas that were difficult to extend were defined as “dense fascia.” Collected samples were immersed in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4) and stored at −30 °C.

Biochemical treatment of fascia samples

Defatting treatment

Lipid removal was performed according to a previous report [6]. Fascia was immersed in an ethanol:diethyl ether (1:1) solution, shaken for 10 min, and then allowed to stand for 40 min; this operation was repeated four times. The samples were then lyophilized to obtain defatted samples. Defatting was performed on 6 specimens each for loose and dense fascia, and lipid content was calculated from the weight change before and after treatment.

Sequential biochemical treatment (Urea → Guanidine-HCl → elastase → pepsin)

Defatted samples (10 mg) were treated in the following order:1.Urea Extraction: Samples were immersed in PBS containing 6 M urea and stirred overnight at 4 °C. The extract was collected, and the residue was used as the urea-treated sample.2.Guanidine-HCl Treatment: After washing with PBS, samples were immersed in 1 M guanidine-HCl/PBS for 3 min on ice. The extract was collected, and the residue was used as the guanidine-treated sample.3.Elastase Treatment: After washing guanidine-treated samples with PBS, they were reacted with elastase (536-24291; EPC, MO, USA; 0.2 U/mL in 0.1 M Tris–HCl) at 37 °C for 2 h or 2 days [7,8]. The extract was collected, and the residue was used as the elastase-treated sample.4.Pepsin Extraction: Elastase-treated samples were treated with pepsin (PM; Funakoshi, Tokyo, Japan; 1.0 mg/mL in 0.5 M acetic acid, 4 °C, overnight), and the supernatant was collected. The residue was re-extracted under the same conditions up to three times.

Extracts were dialyzed against PBS at 4 °C and then subjected to composition analysis. Pepsin extracts were neutralized with 2 M Tris aqueous solution. Three samples each for loose and dense fascia were used for each condition.

Collagen composition analysis

SDS-PAGE

According to the Laemmli method [9], dialyzed extracts and neutralized pepsin extracts were subjected to SDS-PAGE and visualized by Coomassie Brilliant Blue (CBB) staining.

Type III collagen analysis (sykes method)

Analysis of type III collagen followed the Sykes method [10], utilizing both reducing and non-reducing SDS-PAGE. The total collagen band intensity, including α1(I), α2(I), α1(III), α1(V), α2(V), and β/γ components, was taken as 100%, and the relative ratio of each α chain and total collagen content were calculated.

Stains-all staining

Gels after SDS-PAGE were stained with Stains-all (E9379-1G; Sigma, MO, USA) [11] to visualize carbohydrates and glycoproteins. HA (HA-LQH; Kewpie Corp., Tokyo, Japan) was used as a standard, and HA content was determined by densitometry of band intensity. Three samples each for loose/dense were analyzed.

Carbohydrate analysis

Carbohydrate content of fascia samples (untreated or defatted lyophilized products) was measured by the phenol-sulfuric acid method [[12], [13], [14]]. 5% phenol (1.0 mL) was added to the sample and mixed, followed by the dropwise addition of concentrated sulfuric acid (5.0 mL). After reacting for 10 min at room temperature, the mixture was cooled, and absorbance at 490 nm was measured. Glucose was used as a standard. Measurements were performed 6 times each for loose/dense samples.

Fluorescence imaging

Fixation and Autofluorescence Quenching

Untreated or biochemically treated fascia were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min and washed with PBS. Samples were immersed in 50 mM ammonium acetate containing 10 mM CuSO4 for 1 h to remove red autofluorescence [15]. Subsequently, green autofluorescence was suppressed using the TrueVIEW Autofluorescence Quenching kit (SP-8400; Vector Laboratories/Funakoshi) for 5 min at room temperature, according to the manufacturer's instructions. For calcein staining, the CuSO4-based quenching step was omitted to avoid potential interference with Ca2+-dependent calcein labeling.

Elastin immunofluorescence

No blocking or permeabilization was performed for immunofluorescence staining; omitting these steps did not compromise staining quality (signal specificity/background) under our conditions. Samples were incubated with elastin monoclonal antibody BA-4 (MA1-27129, Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA, USA; dilution 1: 100 in T-PBS containing 0.1% BSA) for 1 h at room temperature, washed three times for 5 min each with T-PBS (0.1% Tween-20 in PBS), and then reacted with Alexa Fluor 594–conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (A11032; Thermo Fisher; dilution 1:1000 in T-PBS containing 0.1% BSA) for 1 h at room temperature [16].

Collagen hybridizing peptide (CHP) staining

According to the manufacturer's protocol, 20 μM CHP (FLU60; 3Helix/Funakoshi) dissolved in PBS was heated at 80 °C for 5 min, rapidly cooled in an ice bath, and centrifuged; the supernatant was then used without further dilution [[17], [18], [19]]. Samples were incubated in CHP solution at 4 °C overnight under light-protected conditions, washed three times for 5 min with PBS, and mounted with Permafluor. For each condition, 2 samples each of loose/dense fascia were stained, and 3 distinct areas per sample were observed. The experiment was repeated 5 times.

Double staining with calcein and elastin

After elastin immunostaining, samples were incubated with 10 mM CaCl2 for 5 min followed by 10 μM calcein for 10 min, according to a previous report [20]. As calcein (Dojindo Molecular Technologies Inc., Kumamoto, Japan) selectively binds to fibrillin-rich elastic fibers in a calcium-dependent manner [20], this method accurately visualizes the elastic fiber distribution. Excess calcein was removed by briefly rinsing with deionized water (1 min) followed by 10 mM CaCl2 (5 min). Some samples were observed with a high-resolution fluorescence microscope (IX73; Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM)

Untreated or biochemically treated fascia were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min and subjected to graded ethanol dehydration. Subsequently, samples were immersed in 100% ethanol:t-butanol (1:1) (30 min) followed by 100% t-butanol (30 min) and lyophilized overnight. After lyophilization, samples were mounted on Si substrates and imaged immediately by SEM to minimize moisture absorption. Observations were performed in charge-reduction mode without metal coating at an acceleration voltage of 15 kV (TM3000; Hitachi High-Tech, Tokyo, Japan). Three samples were observed for each condition, with at least three areas observed per sample.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using one-way ANOVA and post hoc Tukey's HSD test. Data are presented as mean ± SD, and p < 0.05 was considered significant.

Results

Extraction and quantification of glycosaminoglycans (GAGs) in the subcutaneous fascia

GAGs extracted from the loose and dense fascia were separated by SDS-PAGE and detected by CBB staining and Stains-all staining. In Stains-all staining, hyaluronan (HA) stains blue, proteoglycans purple, and weakly acidic proteins red [21]. Analysis revealed that extracts from both fascia types showed a blue band corresponding to HA, appearing as a concentrated band in the stacking gel and a smear in the separating gel at the same position as the HA standard (Fig. 1A). This band was commonly detected after urea, elastase, and pepsin treatments, indicating that high molecular weight HA exists entangled in the fiber network and is solubilized by biochemical treatments. In the urea-treated extracts, type VI collagen was not detectable by CBB staining but was detected by Western blotting (data not shown). CHP staining specifically stained collagen fibers. In the loose fascia samples, areas corresponding to fibers in phase-contrast images were stained in a fibrous manner, forming a net-like structure overall (Fig. 3Ab). In the dense fascia samples, CHP staining was observed in the direction of the collagen fiber bundle orientation shown in phase-contrast microscopy (Fig. 3Ba). The parts stained in a streak-like pattern by CHP corresponded to the dark areas in the phase-contrast image. The CHP staining signal was relatively stronger at the bundle boundaries of the fiber bundles rather than the entire bundle (Fig. 3Bb). Additionally, there were fibers stained in directions seemingly unrelated to that orientation.

In the loose fascia samples, elastin fibers stained the entire sample in a network pattern. Some parts stained along collagen fibers, while others did not co-localize at all (Fig. 3Ab). Elastin fibers in the dense fascia appeared more linear compared to the loose fascia (Fig. 3Bc). Some parts were stained in streaks, corresponding to the spaces between fiber bundles. Neither urea nor guanidine-HCl treatment caused major changes in fiber structure in either sample, but there was a tendency for elastin staining to be enhanced (Fig. 3Bf). Quantitative analysis of fiber diameter confirmed this observation (Supplementary Fig. 1). In the loose fascia, elastin fiber diameter showed no significant change across treatments (approx. 2.3 - 2.6 μm). In contrast, in the dense fascia, the diameter increased significantly from 1.2 ± 0.20 μm in controls to 2.1 ± 0.40 μm after urea treatment, 2.0 ± 0.25 μm after guanidine-HCl treatment, and 2.5 ± 0.41 μm after 2-h treatment (all p = 0.001). In phase-contrast images of the dense fascia samples, guanidine-HCl treatment tended to clarify the gaps between fibers (Fig. 3Bg). The guanidine-HCl treatment was short (3 min). Long-term treatment (data not shown) resulted in sample clearing. Even short-term treatment promoted subsequent collagen extraction by pepsin (data not shown).

In the loose fascia samples, collagen fiber structure was clearly confirmed after elastase treatment (Fig. 3Aj, k). Most elastin fibers had disappeared, but remaining elastin fibers co-localized with collagen fibers (Fig. 3Ak). In the dense fascia samples, disruption of fiber bundles was observed in phase-contrast images (Fig. 3Bj). Elastin immunoreactivity was redistributed after elastase treatment, showing locally increased intensity in transverse and punctate patterns relative to collagen bundle orientation (Fig. 3Bn). When elastase treatment was performed for 2 days, elastin staining almost disappeared, and collagen fibers were dispersed (Fig. 3Bop). Because this prolonged incubation is non-physiological and may amplify cumulative secondary effects, it is presented descriptively and is not used as primary evidence for the proposed model.

Differences in localization of elastin and fibrillin

Fibrillin staining with calcein and elastin staining were compared (Fig. 5). Although they partially co-localized, there was a clear difference in distribution. In the loose fascia, both were distributed in a network. In the dense fascia, fibrillin showed an orientation tendency similar to collagen fiber bundles. It localized mainly on the surface of fiber bundles, and staining was relatively weak in the gaps between bundles. Elastin existed in the gaps between collagen fiber bundles, but transverse staining across fiber bundles became stronger with urea or guanidine-HCl treatment. Elastin disappeared with 2-h elastase treatment, but fibrillin staining was retained. With 2-day treatment, collagen fibers dispersed and fibrillin disappeared. Discussion

In this study, we identified clear differences in the organization of collagen–elastic fiber assemblies and extracellular matrix composition between loose and dense regions of the subcutaneous fascia. These regional features are consistent with, and may contribute to, previously reported differences in extensibility and rigidity between the two regions. The major findings are that while there were no significant differences in the composition ratios of type I, III, and V collagens or in the total collagen content per dry weight between the regions, there were remarkable regional differences in the quantitative distribution of hyaluronan (HA) and the mode of supramolecular organization of collagen–elastic fiber assemblies. In particular, the higher HA abundance in loose fascia may contribute to hydration-dependent tissue rheology and low-friction gliding, whereas the prominent hierarchical bundle architecture in dense fascia is consistent with greater load-bearing capacity and elastic recoil. Together, these findings extend previous morphological observations [5,20] by providing molecular and ultrastructural evidence for region-specific ECM environments and higher-order fiber organization.

We interpret these observations in the context of a composite fiber bundle model, in which elastic-fiber–associated components contribute to maintaining the hierarchical integrity of collagen bundles in dense fascia. Previous studies have suggested that elastic fibers may contribute to the formation and stabilization of fiber bundles in dense regions, and our immunostaining (elastin and fibrillin) and SEM observations further support a distinct organization of elastic-fiber–associated structures at the bundle scale. Importantly, in the dense fascia subjected to elastase treatment, loosening and disruption/dispersion of collagen bundles became prominent, an observation consistent with the idea that modification of elastic-fiber–associated elements reduces constraints that help maintain higher-order bundle structure. However, this interpretation should be made cautiously: although we used a high-purity elastase preparation validated in our prior fascia work [20], we cannot completely exclude trace contaminating activities or secondary mechanical dispersion during post-treatment handling. Accordingly, we view the elastase-associated bundle changes as supportive of, but not definitive proof for, a structural contribution of elastic-fiber components to composite bundle integrity.

As an alternative (and still untested) possibility, the punctate and/or thickened appearance of elastin/fibrillin signals after elastase treatment may reflect local release of pre-existing tensile stress and subsequent redistribution/aggregation of residual elastic-fiber–associated structures following partial cleavage. This interpretation is consistent with prior observations that elastase treatment can induce macroscopic stretching/expansion of fascia sheets, suggesting elastic-fiber–derived tensile pre-stress. In our previous study [20], the same high-purity porcine pancreatic elastase (PPE) preparation selectively affected calcium-associated, fibrillin-rich elastic fibers without obvious nonspecific disruption of collagen architecture at the light-microscopy level under comparable incubation conditions, supporting the plausibility of an elastic-fiber–centered interpretation.

While the theoretical possibility of trace protease contaminants in commercial preparations cannot be fully excluded, the phenotype observed here—bundle dispersion into finer filaments rather than complete disappearance of collagen—appears more consistent with weakening of elastic-fiber–associated constraints than with direct collagenolysis. The bundle loosening observed even during short-term (2-h) incubation is also consistent with elastic-fiber components contributing to the maintenance of hierarchical bundle organization.

Consistent with our biochemical analyses, the relative ratios of type I, III, and V collagens were nearly equivalent in both regions, and no significant difference was observed in total collagen content. This suggests that the regional structural differences are not explained by collagen type composition alone, but are more likely associated with differences in the degree of fiber assembly and supramolecular organization [[22], [23], [24]]. Consistent with this view, multiple pepsin treatments were required to recover collagen from dense fascia, suggesting that collagen in dense regions is assembled into bundles that are relatively resistant to enzymatic extraction.

Collagen hybridizing peptide (CHP) staining provided additional insight into regional differences in collagen structural integrity/organization. Because CHP detects denatured/disrupted collagen chains, the observed staining pattern may reflect localized collagen disruption and/or increased triple-helix dynamics/instability. While CHP staining was distributed throughout the network in loose fascia, signals in dense fascia were concentrated at bundle boundaries and inter-bundle regions. This boundary enrichment was further supported by binarization and line-profile overlap analyses using matched phase-contrast and fluorescence images (Supplementary Fig. 2). This pattern is consistent with a hierarchical bundle architecture in dense fascia in which the bundle interior is more densely assembled, whereas boundary regions may be more susceptible to local structural fluctuation or micro-disruption.

Among non-collagenous ECM components, HA was a particularly important factor distinguishing the two regions. HA content per dry weight was approximately twice as high in loose fascia as in dense fascia, suggesting that in loose regions an HA-rich matrix may form a hydrated layer between fiber bundles. HA is known to exhibit high water-retention capacity and to contribute to tissue viscosity and lubrication, thereby facilitating fiber gliding [25,26]. Given HA's exceptional hydration capacity, even the observed ∼0.1% (w/w) difference in HA content may translate into substantial differences in local water retention and the inter-bundle microenvironment. Accordingly, the gliding ability and deformation tolerance of loose fascia may be strongly influenced by, or partly attributable to, this HA-rich hydration environment. On the other hand, dense fascia contained relatively less HA, and spaces between fiber bundles were enriched with other ECM components such as type VI collagen and proteoglycans. The appearance of distinct gaps between bundles after urea/guanidine-HCl treatment is consistent with the possibility that these non-collagenous components contribute to inter-bundle cohesion, although the specific molecular interactions remain to be established.

Integrating these results, the subcutaneous fascia appears to exhibit a hierarchical organization in which [1]: assemblies composed of collagen and elastic-fiber–associated components constitute a primary structural unit [2]; these assemblies bundle together to form higher-order fiber bundles; and [3] fiber bundles are interconnected by non-collagenous ECM components, including HA, type VI collagen, and proteoglycans. In loose regions, composite bundles are sparsely distributed within an HA-rich matrix, an arrangement consistent with increased inter-bundle mobility and deformation permissiveness. In dense regions, composite bundles are more tightly interconnected by non-collagenous ECM, a configuration consistent with higher resistance to deformation and previously reported load-bearing characteristics.

This study has several limitations. First, the analysis was limited to chicken subcutaneous fascia; therefore, extrapolation to other species, anatomical locations, ages, or pathological conditions should be made with caution. Second, the present study focused primarily on compositional and structural analyses and did not include direct mechanical testing or quantitative modeling. Future studies integrating the structural features identified here with independently obtained mechanical measurements will be necessary to test the mechanical implications of the composite bundle framework across multiple length scales.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that the regional heterogeneity of the subcutaneous fascia is not fully explained by differences in major fibrillar collagen composition ratios or total collagen content alone, and is more strongly associated with variations in non-collagenous extracellular matrix components and the supramolecular organization of collagen–elastic fiber assemblies. Loose fascia contains a higher amount of hyaluronan (HA) and exhibits a relatively sparse organization of composite fiber bundles, whereas dense fascia is characterized by tightly interconnected composite fiber bundles with lower HA content. These structural and compositional differences provide a molecular and ultrastructural framework for understanding the distinct mechanical behaviors previously reported for loose and dense regions of the subcutaneous fascia.
