Exploring the multifaceted influencers on intrinsic capacity among community-dwelling older adults: a scoping review
Authors: Zihan Li, Lei Liu, Fengjie Tao, Hongyuan Li, Jiayu Wang, Yu Tian, Xinyu Zhu, Xiaohui Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIn the context of healthy ageing, older adults living in communities, as a high-risk group for declining intrinsic capacities, have received widespread attention. However, the existing literature on the factors influencing the decline in intrinsic capacities among this group is fragmented, and comprehensive reviews are lacking.MethodsA scoping review was conducted from July to August 2025. Firstly, one researcher, with the assistance of two librarians, developed a search strategy and systematically searched the PubMed, Web of Science, CINAHL, and Embase databases. The search period was limited to the period from the establishment of the databases until August 7, 2025. Then, two researchers independently screened and included the literature, extracted key information, and discussed any discrepancies with the involvement of another two researchers. The entire research process strictly followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses Extension for Scoping Reviews framework to ensure the research quality.ResultsThis study included a total of 25 articles published between 2019 and 2025, mainly from China. The intrinsic capacities of community-dwelling older adults were found to be influenced by five major factors: personal characteristics, social environmental factors, lifestyle patterns, health and functional status, physiological and metabolic indicators.ConclusionsThis review establishes that the intrinsic capabilities of community-dwelling older adults are shaped by the complex interplay of factors across five key dimensions. Sustaining intrinsic capabilities requires a holistic approach that translates evidence on its multidimensional determinants into comprehensive strategies for empowering older adults, thereby optimising.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-026-07197-0.

Background

The global population is aging at an unprecedented rate. The World Health Organization (WHO) projects that the number of people aged 60 years and older worldwide will increase from 1 billion in 2020 to 2.1 billion by 2050 [1]. In China, by the end of 2024, the population aged 60 and above had reached 310 million, accounting for 22.0% of the total population; meanwhile, the population aged 65 and above reached 220 million, constituting 15.6% [2]. These figures indicate that China has entered a deeply aging society and has become the country with the fastest growth and largest increase in its older adult population globally [2]. Therefore, it is of significant practical importance to delve into health issues within the context of population aging in China.

Among the older adult population, approximately 90% choose to age in community settings, while another 7% rely on various community support services [3]. This substantial community-dwelling older adult population faces multidimensional health challenges during the aging process. In the domain of movement, aging can lead to reduced muscle mass, decreased muscle strength, and diminished balance capacity in older adults [4]. Regarding vitality, aging may lead to a decline in chewing and swallowing functions, ultimately resulting in malnutrition [5]. In terms of sensation, age-related declines in vision and hearing are common [6]. Concerning cognition, the cognitive function of older adults also tends to decline with advancing age [7]. Furthermore, in the psychological domain, age-associated transitions in social roles, the shrinking of social networks, and the resulting feelings of loneliness and psychological stress further exacerbate the mental health burden on older adults [8, 9]. These changes collectively lead to declines in capacity across multiple dimensions—including movement, vitality, sensation, cognition, and psychology—significantly affecting their well-being and functional ability to live independently in later life [10].

In response to the aforementioned challenges, the WHO proposed the Healthy Aging Framework in 2015. This framework comprises three key components: the functional ability of older adults, their intrinsic capacity, and their surrounding environment [11]. Among these, intrinsic capacity is defined as the composite of an individual's physical and mental capacities, encompassing five key domains: movement, vitality, sensation, cognition, and psychology [12]. Maintaining and enhancing intrinsic capacity is of paramount importance for delaying functional ability decline, reducing the risk of disability, and improving quality of life among older adults [11]. For community-dwelling older adults, whose daily lives heavily rely on interactions within their community environment, intrinsic capacity directly influences their social participation, independent living, and health outcomes [13]. Current evidence on the influencing factors of intrinsic capacity among community-dwelling older adults remains significantly fragmented, primarily in two aspects. Firstly, there is a lack of unified classification standards. Research explores factors from individual, environmental, and health dimensions but lacks a standardized framework. For instance, Tang et al. pointed out that the distinction between "sociodemographic" and "physio-biological" factors is often blurred, with the same concept assigned different meanings across studies, leading to non-comparable data [14]. Secondly, there is a separation of dimensions. Most studies focus on a single factor or domain, lacking exploration into the interactive mechanisms among multiple factors. This fragmented status quo severely limits the understanding of the mechanisms behind intrinsic capacity decline and hinders the identification of high-risk groups and the development of targeted interventions [15].Therefore, this study aims to systematically collate and synthesize the multifaceted factors influencing the intrinsic capacity of community-dwelling older adults using a scoping review methodology. The findings are expected to provide an evidence base for optimizing community-based health services for older adults. Future researchers can utilize this evidence to formulate interventions aimed at preventing or mitigating the negative impacts associated with declining intrinsic capacity in community-dwelling older adults, thereby contributing to the goal of healthy aging.

“Influencing factors” in this scoping review refer to all variables that demonstrate a causal association or a significant predictive relationship with the intrinsic capacity of community-dwelling older adults. These variables are identified through various research designs—including cross-sectional studies, cohort studies, and qualitative research—and are analyzed using both qualitative methods (e.g., expert interviews, Delphi technique) and quantitative methods (e.g., regression analysis, analysis of variance). To be included, variables must be explicitly reported in the source literature as independent predictors, risk factors, or protective factors. They encompass characteristics and contexts at the individual, environmental, and societal levels.

Methods

This scoping review aims to synthesize the factors influencing the intrinsic capacity of community-dwelling older adults and to identify research gaps in the current literature. Scoping reviews can be utilized to map, integrate, and summarize evidence in areas that have not been fully explored, enabling researchers to gain a clear overview of the breadth and depth of existing findings, thereby identifying areas where knowledge remains limited [16].This review strictly adheres to the PRISMA-ScR reporting framework recommended by the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) for evidence-based healthcare [17]. This framework has evolved from the five-stage scoping review framework originally proposed by Arksey and O’Malley [18].

Research questions

This scoping review aims to employ the scoping review methodology to explore which factors influence the intrinsic capacity of community-dwelling older adults. The present study addresses the following research question: What factors influence the intrinsic capacity of community-dwelling older adults?

Literature search strategy

The literature search was conducted by the researchers under the guidance and supervision of the corresponding author. For grey literature, manual examination of bibliographies was employed for identification. During the literature review process, the retrieved records were first imported into EndNote 21 (Clarivate Analytics, PA, USA) to remove duplicates. Subsequently, relevant records were identified by screening titles and abstracts, and all records were filtered according to pre-defined inclusion and exclusion criteria. This process was completed independently by two researchers. If a record was judged as "potentially relevant", two additional researchers were involved in a discussion until a consensus was reached. Finally, the preliminarily selected records were imported into Zotero 7.0.16 for full-text review to determine the final list of included studies.

In the initial phase of this study, a search strategy was developed with the assistance of two medical librarians. Following their recommendations, a preliminary search was conducted using PubMed, and the effectiveness of the search strategy was evaluated through an analysis of the retrieved literature. Subsequently, the first author conducted a systematic search across four databases—PubMed, Web of Science Core Collection, CINAHL, and Embase—using the established search strategy. See Appendix 1 for details. The search time span ranges from the database's establishment to 7 August 2025, and the research language is English. Additionally, no restrictions were imposed on outcome measures or study design types during the search process to ensure the comprehensiveness of the results. Furthermore, the researchers employed a snowball sampling method to screen the references in the retrieved literature to ensure the representatives of the final results.

The first author is responsible for literature retrieval and screening, consulting with the corresponding authors throughout. For grey literature, manual verification of catalogues is employed for identification. During the literature review process, retrieved documents are first imported into EndNote 21 to eliminate duplicates. Relevant topics are then identified through titles and abstracts, with all literature screened against predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. This process is conducted independently by the first and fourth authors. Where a publication was deemed ‘potentially relevant’, the fifth and sixth authors were consulted to reach consensus. Finally, the preliminarily screened literature was imported into Zotero 7.0.16 for full-text review, determining the final included publications.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria for this study were based on the Participant-Concept-Context (PCC) framework approach recommended by the JBI [19]. (1) Participants: Older adults aged ≥ 60 years. (2) Concept: Outcome measures of any influencing factors (including predictive factors, risk factors, or protective factors) may not include the intrinsic capacity domain but must include at least one of the five dimensions (cognitive, locomotor, vitality, psychological, and sensation) to meet the definition of intrinsic capacity [12]. (3) Context: Living in a community setting. (4) Study type: This study only includes peer-reviewed English-language literature published in international journals, which must be original literature. Exclusion criteria: (1) Duplicate publications. (2) Literature for which the full text is unavailable. (3) Case reports, conference proceedings, reviews, and theses.

Data extraction

Data integration was performed on the final included literature. Two researchers jointly developed a standardised data extraction form to extract literature data, standardising terminology to account for cultural differences between Eastern and Western contexts. All authors participated in the design and refinement of the table format. Extracted data included author/year, country, study design, target population, sample size, and influencing factors. Microsoft Excel was utilised to construct the tables. The entire process was conducted under the guidance of the corresponding author. In instances where discrepancies arose during the charting and extraction process, two additional researchers were invited to conduct a comparative analysis until a consensus was reached. The results were then reviewed by a team member possessing an international clinical nursing perspective and experience, along with proficient English skills, to mitigate potential misinterpretations or ambiguous expressions arising from cultural differences between Eastern and Western contexts.

Results

Search results and screening process

A preliminary literature search yielded a total of 478 articles, with one additional article obtained through snowballing in Google search, bringing the total to 479 articles. After excluding 252 duplicate articles, 227 articles entered the initial screening phase, with all relevant information accessible for retrieval. Following a review of article titles and abstracts, 152 articles were ultimately excluded, leaving 75 articles selected for full-text reading. Ultimately, 50 articles were excluded for failing to meet inclusion criteria, resulting in the retention of 25 articles. The literature screening process for this study is shown in Fig. 1. It is worth noting that during the preliminary search, we also searched the Cochrane Library, yielding 47 articles. However, these articles were not accessible in that database and all originated from the aforementioned databases that had already been searched. Personal characteristics

Among the 25 studies ultimately included, 16 studies discussed the impact of age on IC in community-dwelling older adults [24–27, 30, 32–36, 38, 39, 42, 44]. Nine studies explored the impact of gender on IC [21, 22, 26, 32, 34, 38, 42–44]. Eight studies examined the impact of educational attainment on IC [22, 24, 26, 27, 32, 37, 38, 42]. Additionally, eight studies explored the influence of marital status on IC [24–27, 32, 36, 38, 44]. Two studies analyzed the relationship between living conditions and IC [34, 40]. Two studies analyzed the impact of place of residence on IC. One study explored the correlation between body mass index (BMI) and IC [21, 22, 24]. Seven studies examined the correlation between comorbidity and IC [21, 22, 25, 33, 36, 42, 43]. One study explored the correlation between number of children and IC [39].

Social environmental factors

Among the 25 studies ultimately included, 1 study validated the relationship between social isolation and IC [22]. Three studies explored the impact of social networks on IC [32, 36, 38]. One study analyzed the impact of social participation on IC [43]. One study analyzed the impact of social distance on IC [31]. Two studies examined the association between economic status and IC [24, 32]. Two studies demonstrated the correlation between health insurance and IC [32, 37]. Two studies examined the impact of healthcare accessibility on IC [37, 43]. Two studies examined the relationship between external environment and IC [36, 37]. One study validated the impact of family function and occupational status on IC [35].

Lifestyle behaviour patterns

Among the 25 studies ultimately included, four studies examined the impact of dietary patterns on IC [23, 28, 33, 43]. One study validated the impact of meat intake on IC [24]. One study validated the impact of fruit and vegetable intake on IC [38]. Five studies explored the relationship between physical activity and IC [24, 27, 41, 43, 44]. One study validated the impact of sedentary time on IC [41]. One study explored the correlation between leisure activities and IC [42]. One study examined the relationship between sleep duration and IC [34]. One study simultaneously validated the effects of smoking and alcohol consumption on IC [27, 32].

Health and functional status

Among the 25 studies ultimately included, three studies demonstrated the association between chronic diseases and IC [24, 30, 32], with Ma also demonstrating the impact of gait slowness on IC in their study [24]. Three studies analyzed the impact of disability on IC [26, 30, 39]. Two studies validated the detrimental effects of fall risk on IC [35, 39]. Two studies validated that grip strength, as one of the indicators of IC, can also serve as a precursor to IC [40, 43]. Two studies validated the impact of nutritional status on IC [35, 40]. One study explored the relationship between pain severity and hospitalization history with IC [43]. Another study examined the impact of self-perceived fatigue and appetite on IC [40].

Physiological and metabolic indicators

Among the 25 studies ultimately included, one study examined the impact of LGI on IC [20]. Two studies validated the relationship between Hcy and IC [20, 27]. One study discussed the correlation between IL-6, TNFR-1, GDF-15, and IC [29].

Discussion

With the accelerating pace of population aging, the intrinsic capacity of older adults is progressively declining, which severely affects their well-being. Consequently, the timely identification and management of factors influencing intrinsic capacity can benefit every older individual [45]. The community environment can either exacerbate or mitigate the age-related decline in intrinsic capacity [46]. Therefore, numerous scholars worldwide have turned their focus to community-dwelling older adults, producing a substantial body of academic work on the management of their intrinsic capacity. This study aims to synthesize the existing evidence on factors influencing the intrinsic capacity of community-dwelling older adults, thereby providing a basis for developing future related interventions. Based on the 25 studies selected through the final screening process, we have categorized 43 influencing factors into five major groups: basic personal characteristics, socio-environmental factors, lifestyle and behavioral patterns, health and functional status, and physiological and metabolic indicators.

Personal characteristics

Personal basic characteristics are fundamental factors shaping the intrinsic capacity of community-dwelling older adults. Individual-level variables such as age, sex, socioeconomic status, marital and living arrangements, and health status significantly influence the level of intrinsic capacity. Firstly, increasing age is a pervasive and largely irreversible core risk factor for declining intrinsic capacity. Multiple studies consistently indicate this [24–27, 30, 32–36, 38, 39, 42, 44], primarily attributed to the age-associated decline in physical and mental capacity. Research by Yu et al. showed that [26], the prevalence of impaired intrinsic capacity significantly increased from 76.9% in the 60–64 age group to 92% in those aged 85 and above.Secondly, the influence of sex on intrinsic capacity presents a complex and multidimensional pattern. Overall, women may face a higher risk of diminished intrinsic capacity, potentially due to post-menopausal physiological changes and more common burdens associated with social caregiving roles [21, 22, 26, 32, 34, 38, 42–44]. However, sex differences are not consistent across all domains of intrinsic capacity. Evidence suggests that women may have advantages in the auditory and psychological domains [26], while men might perform better in the cognitive, visual, and locomotor domains, with no significant sex difference observed in the vitality domain. This indicates that the impact of sex on intrinsic capacity is not unidirectional but rather results from interactions with specific capacity domains and socio-cultural contexts. Thirdly, socioeconomic resources and social support are key protective factors for maintaining intrinsic capacity. Multiple studies confirm that community-dwelling older adults with higher educational attainment [22, 24, 26, 27, 32, 37, 38, 42], those who are married [24–27, 32, 36, 38, 44], and those not living alone tend to have better intrinsic capacity [40]. The underlying mechanisms likely involve higher educational levels typically correlating with better health literacy and greater access to health resources, enabling individuals to adopt healthier behaviors more effectively [47]. Being married and not living alone provides continuous emotional support, practical assistance, and social interaction, which directly benefits the maintenance of capacity in the psychological and vitality domains and may foster a positive psychological state through enhanced feelings of "love and sense of responsibility" [48]. Conversely, living alone, being unmarried, or lacking adequate support from children increases the risk of impaired intrinsic capacity due to the absence of these resources [34, 39, 40]. Furthermore, the broader residential environment indirectly shapes intrinsic capacity through resource accessibility. Older adults residing in urban areas [21] or regions with better economic development [24] generally exhibit higher levels of intrinsic capacity. Superior living environments typically offer more comprehensive healthcare, richer recreational facilities, and more convenient transportation. These external resources can compensate for declines in individual function, thereby helping to maintain and enhance intrinsic capacity [49]. This aligns with the concept within the healthy aging framework that the environment can influence the intrinsic capacity and functional ability of older adults. Finally, direct health status is one of the most closely related factors affecting intrinsic capacity. A higher body mass index (BMI) [22] and the presence of multimorbidity [21, 22, 25, 33, 36, 42, 43] are established risk factors for diminished intrinsic capacity. The burden of chronic diseases may directly erode the physiological and mental capacity of older adults through multiple pathways, including pathological damage, activity limitations, and side effects from polypharmacy.

Social environmental factors

The social environment is a key exogenous factor shaping the intrinsic capacity of community-dwelling older adults. Dimensions such as social connectedness, socioeconomic resources, and environmental support significantly influence intrinsic capacity. Firstly, the quality and breadth of social connections are central to maintaining intrinsic capacity. A cohort study from Japan indicated that social frailty negatively affects the intrinsic capacity of older adults [22]. Conversely, active social participation [43] and extensive social networks serve as important protective factors for maintaining intrinsic capacity by providing cognitive stimulation, emotional support, and oversight of health behaviors [32, 36, 38]. Notably, robust family functioning is also crucial. A study from Mexico found that family dysfunction increased the risk of intrinsic capacity deterioration in older adults by 5.7 times compared to those without such dysfunction [35]. Research conducted during the global COVID-19 pandemic showed that social distancing eroded the movement and cognitive capacity of older adults [31], highlighting the acute harms of social isolation during special periods, whose long-term effects warrant further investigation in the post-pandemic era. Secondly, a stable socioeconomic foundation and accessible healthcare resources constitute the supportive pillars for preserving intrinsic capacity. Stable economic income [24, 32] and comprehensive health insurance [32, 37] are directly associated with better intrinsic capacity. The core mechanism lies in their role in alleviating the financial burden of healthcare and facilitating the access to and utilization of health resources, thereby laying the groundwork for maintaining intrinsic capacity. Meanwhile, greater accessibility to healthcare services has also been shown to be conducive to maintaining intrinsic capacity [37, 43]. It is important to note, however, that the specific effects of health insurance systems vary significantly across countries [50] and need to be considered within the context of national policies. Finally, supportive physical and policy environments can further empower intrinsic capacity. Barrier-free facilities, convenient transportation, and inclusive policies can slow the decline of intrinsic capacity by compensating for functional limitations and promoting active social participation [36, 37]. This reaffirms the supportive role of the environment on intrinsic capacity. Occupational status, as a comprehensive reflection of socioeconomic position and daily activity, has also been shown to promote better intrinsic capacity, likely related to the higher social participation and better economic security of employed individuals [35].

Lifestyle patterns

Lifestyle and behavioral patterns are crucial modifiable factors that influence the intrinsic capacity of community-dwelling older adults. Dietary habits, physical activity, sleep, and tobacco and alcohol use significantly affect the level of intrinsic capacity. Regarding diet, overall dietary quality and the intake of specific foods play an important role in intrinsic capacity. Healthy dietary patterns, such as the Mediterranean Diet, the Okinawan Diet, and diets scoring high on the International Diet Quality Index (DQI-I), are associated with higher levels of intrinsic capacity [28, 33]. Although some controversy exists—for instance, older adults are often advised to consume more plant-based foods rich in protein and possessing antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects to delay aging, Japanese researcher Huang contends that [23] foods rich in animal protein are more beneficial for the intrinsic capacity of older adults [23]. While the impact of different protein sources on intrinsic capacity remains debated, evidence regarding specific intake quantities is more indicative. Consuming meat less than twice per week is an independent risk factor for declining intrinsic capacity, whereas each additional daily serving of fruits and vegetables significantly reduces the risk of rapid intrinsic capacity decline [24, 38]. This review posits that, compared with animal-based diets, plant-based diets that are higher in dietary fiber and lower in cholesterol are more suitable for community-dwelling older adults. Regarding physical activity, intensity is paramount and must be coupled with reducing sedentary behavior. Regular physical activity, particularly moderate-to-vigorous physical activity (MVPA), demonstrates significant benefits for improving the psychological capacity of older adults [24, 27, 41, 43, 44]. One study showed that for every 15-min increase in MVPA, intrinsic capacity could improve by 0.63% [41]. Conversely, sedentary behavior and light physical activity are associated with a significant decline in intrinsic capacity, with every 15-min increase in sedentary time leading to a 0.29% decrease in intrinsic capacity [41]. Active participation in various leisure activities has also been confirmed to positively affect intrinsic capacity [42]. Furthermore, a healthy lifestyle forms the cornerstone for maintaining intrinsic capacity. Ensuring adequate sleep of 6 to 8 h per night is an important protective factor, reducing the risk of intrinsic capacity decline by 23% [34]. Simultaneously, avoiding tobacco use and excessive alcohol consumption are key protective behaviors, the harms of which have been confirmed in multiple studies [27, 32].

Health and functional status

The health and functional status of older adults constitute the most direct and core dimension influencing their intrinsic capacity. Pre-existing diseases, functional limitations, and observable clinical indicators collectively form risk factors for the decline in intrinsic capacity. Firstly, chronic diseases and geriatric syndromes provide the pathological basis for impairment of intrinsic capacity. Research confirms that specific chronic conditions (e.g., COPD, osteoarthritis) and geriatric syndromes (e.g., insomnia, urinary incontinence) are significantly associated with a decline in intrinsic capacity [24, 30, 32]. The cumulative effect of multimorbidity is particularly noteworthy. The prevalence of declining intrinsic capacity among older adults with five or more chronic conditions (62.5%) is more than double that of those without multimorbidity (30.9%) [24]. Multimorbidity not only contributes through direct pathological damage but also systematically accelerates the decline in intrinsic capacity by reducing the functional ability in activities of daily living/instrumental activities of daily living (ADL/IADL) [26, 30, 39]. Secondly, functional limitations and their ensuing adverse consequences create a vicious cycle. Slow movement and decreased muscle strength directly undermine the locomotor capacity of older adults. More seriously, the increased fall risk associated with these limitations can lead to fear of falling, resulting in further reduced activity levels. This accelerates muscle atrophy and joint stiffness, thereby exacerbating the decline in intrinsic capacity [35, 39]. Finally, observable physical functional states can serve as early warning signs for identifying declining intrinsic capacity in older adults. These states include objective physiological signs, such as reduced handgrip strength [40, 43] and malnutrition [35, 40]; health status information, such as high levels of pain and frequent hospitalization history [43]; and self-reported symptoms, such as perceived fatigue and loss of appetite [40]. They are not merely clinical manifestations of declining intrinsic capacity but are involved throughout the entire trajectory of its change. For example, malnutrition can simultaneously lead to muscle strength loss and oxidative stress in the central nervous system, directly impairing movement and cognitive capacity [35, 40]; whereas severe pain can indirectly accelerate the decline in intrinsic capacity by limiting physical activity and social participation [43]. Some studies further substantiate these associations; for instance, older adults experiencing self-perceived fatigue have a significantly higher risk of declining intrinsic capacity [40].

Physiological and metabolic indicators

Physiological and metabolic indicators serve as crucial markers of the decline in intrinsic capacity associated with biological aging. Systemic low-grade inflammation is a potential pathophysiological basis accelerating intrinsic capacity decline. In a five-year follow-up study, French researcher Giudici et al. found that [20] older adults in a state of low-grade inflammation experienced a significantly greater decline in intrinsic capacity compared to those without inflammation. The declines in scores for the cognitive, psychological, and movement domains were 0.10, 0.52, and 0.38 points greater, respectively, in the inflammation group than in the non-inflammation group (P > 0.05). This effect may be mediated by various inflammatory mediators. For instance, elevated levels of interleukin-6 (IL-6) and tumor necrosis factor receptor-1 (TNFR-1) have both been confirmed to increase the risk of decline in multiple domains of intrinsic capacity [29]. Growth differentiation factor-15 (GDF-15), as a stress response marker, shows a particularly significant association between its elevation and an increased risk of intrinsic capacity decline, suggesting it may be a strong biological indicator for assessing the risk of intrinsic capacity deterioration [29]. Furthermore, hyperhomocysteinemia (Hcy concentration > 15 μmol/L) is an independent risk factor for declining intrinsic capacity [20]. Lin et al. further proposed that even mildly elevated Hcy levels (≥ 8.53 μmol/L) are associated with an increased risk of intrinsic capacity decline [27], indicating that Hcy may be a continuous risk factor negatively affecting the cognitive, movement, and vitality domains of intrinsic capacity [20].

Research gaps and future directions

Following the systematic synthesis of the five categories of factors influencing the intrinsic capacity of community-dwelling older adults, this scoping review reveals both progress and limitations in the accumulation of evidence in this field. While existing studies have begun to outline the contours of factors influencing intrinsic capacity, several critical gaps remain, constraining a deeper understanding of the underlying mechanisms and the formulation of effective intervention strategies. Firstly, there are deficiencies in study design and the evidence base. Current evidence predominantly originates from cross-sectional studies, lacking long-term longitudinal data. This makes it difficult to elucidate the dynamic trajectory of intrinsic capacity decline and establish causal mechanisms. Furthermore, there is insufficient geographical and cultural diversity in study samples. Most research is concentrated in specific regions (e.g., mainland China), limiting the generalizability and cross-cultural applicability of the findings. Secondly, the examination of environmental factors, particularly the macro and physical environments, remains relatively weak. For instance, while variables like place of residence are mentioned, there is a lack of systematic and in-depth assessment of community physical environments (e.g., accessibility features, public spaces) and macro-level policy support (e.g., elderly care service systems). Only a few studies have attempted to incorporate environmental dimensions [44], yet they have not thoroughly dissected the specific pathways through which community environments and individuals interact to influence intrinsic capacity. This limits the understanding of the potential for modifiable environmental interventions. Finally, the exploration of the deep-seated biological mechanisms underlying intrinsic capacity decline is still in its infancy. Although some studies have focused on physiological indicators, such as inflammatory markers [20], research systematically revealing the biological basis of intrinsic capacity from molecular, genomic, or metabolomic perspectives is largely lacking. Most evidence remains at the level of macro-influencing factor analysis, hindering the discovery of precise prevention and intervention targets based on pathophysiological mechanisms.

Based on these gaps, future research should focus on the following directions. First, priority should be given to conducting large-scale longitudinal cohort studies to delineate the natural progression of intrinsic capacity and identify its critical turning points and predictive factors. Second, research should expand sample diversity and cultural contexts by undertaking more multi-center, cross-cultural comparative studies. This will enhance the generalizability of conclusions and help identify culture-specific factors. Third, empirical research on environmental factors should be deepened. Theoretical models incorporating physical environments and policy factors should be developed and tested to clarify their mediating and moderating pathways that affect intrinsic capacity. Fourth, interdisciplinary research on mechanisms should be promoted. Integrating methods from epidemiology, clinical medicine, and molecular biology (e.g., genomics, metabolomics) is essential to deeply analyze the biological basis of intrinsic capacity maintenance and decline.

By addressing these evidence gaps, future research will provide a more robust scientific foundation for developing multi-level, personalized, and culturally appropriate intervention strategies. This will effectively contribute to maintaining and enhancing the intrinsic capacity of community-dwelling older adults.

Limitations

Although this review extensively searched relevant literature and identified the factors influencing the intrinsic capacities of community-dwelling older adults, it is still necessary to address the limitations of the studies. First, there are differences in the methodological approaches (cross-sectional studies, longitudinal studies, and cohort studies) and assessment criteria used in the included studies, which may affect the generalisability of the conclusions. Secondly, most relevant studies rely on quantitative analysis rather than qualitative comparison, with insufficient consideration given to patients' subjective experiences. Thirdly, assessment tools and classification criteria for intrinsic capabilities remain inconsistent across studies, necessitating further validation of conclusions' reliability. Fourth, the primary aim of a scoping review is to map the existing knowledge in a field and identify evidence gaps, not to appraise the methodological quality of included studies or to evaluate the effect sizes of influencing factors. Fifth, this review was limited to English-language publications. This may have omitted relevant studies published in non-English languages (e.g., Chinese), thereby affecting the cross-cultural generalizability of the findings. Furthermore, among the 25 studies ultimately included, 12 originated from China. While this may reflect recent scholarly activity in the field of intrinsic capacity among community-dwelling older adults in China, it also results in an imbalanced geographical distribution of the sample. Consequently, while this study comprehensively presents the breadth of influencing factors, it cannot definitively identify which factors hold greater potential for intervention. Subsequent research may focus on conducting systematic reviews to assess the efficacy of key factors, designing multicentre, standardised longitudinal cohorts to clarify causal pathways, and undertaking in-depth qualitative or interdisciplinary studies to more fully elucidate the dynamic interaction mechanisms between social environments and IC.

Conclusions

We systematically synthesized 25 relevant studies and the latest supplementary evidence, clarifying that the intrinsic capacity (IC) of community-dwelling older adults is comprehensively influenced by five key dimensions: personal characteristics, social environmental factors, lifestyle patterns, health and functional status, physiological and metabolic indicators. This confirms that maintaining intrinsic capacity is a complex process involving multilevel interactions, which necessitates an integrative perspective for understanding and intervention. Based on these findings, and to advance the goal of healthy aging, we recommend that both researchers and community practitioners integrate the assessment of intrinsic capacity into routine community health services, providing targeted support based on individual risk profiles. At the community level, efforts should be made to actively develop age-friendly environments and promote healthy lifestyles. For the research community, further long-term longitudinal studies should be conducted to develop practical screening tools and evaluate the effectiveness of comprehensive interventions. By shifting the health focus from "treating disease" to "maintaining capacity," we can better support older adults to live autonomously and with dignity within their communities. This is not only the core objective of healthy aging, but also a direction society as a whole should strive towards.
