Association between regional anaesthesia strategies, postoperative pain and same-day discharge following knee arthroplasty: a single-centre service evaluation
Authors: D. Yildiz, N. Kader, F. Kashif, S. Radha, K. Mrozek
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundKnee arthroplasty is an established treatment for degenerative and inflammatory knee disorders. Effective perioperative analgesia is essential for early mobilisation and same-day discharge. This study evaluated the relationship between different regional anaesthesia techniques, postoperative pain, and same-day discharge rates in knee arthroplasty patients.MethodsWe evaluated 100 consecutive patients as part of a service evaluation of our established knee arthroplasty pathway. Four regional anaesthesia strategies were used: adductor canal block (ACB) only, ACB + genicular, ACB + IPACK (infiltration of popliteal artery and capsule of the knee), and no block. Pain was assessed using a verbal rating scale (0–3) at 2, 6, 12, and 24 h postoperatively. Patient demographics, anaesthetic technique, surgical type and discharge status were recorded. Group comparisons were statistically analysed and predictors of same-day discharge were assessed adjusting for age, gender, BMI, and mean 24-h pain score and surgical type.ResultsMean 24-h pain score for the cohort was 1.05 ± 0.81, with women reporting higher scores than men (p-value 0.023). Higher pain scores were independently associated with reduced odds of same-day discharge (OR 0.32, p-value 0.002). Across the four block modalities, mean pain scores did not differ significantly (p-value 0.968). Using no block as the reference, ACB + genicular was associated with higher odds of same-day discharge (OR 5.29, p-value 0.035). Younger age (p-value 0.022) and higher BMI (p-value 0.044) were also significant predictors; gender and ASA grade were not.ConclusionWhile overall pain scores were similar between block types, higher pain was independently associated with lower likelihood of same-day discharge. In this observational cohort, ACB + genicular was independently associated with same-day discharge following adjustment for confounders. Further work is needed to determine whether these associations are reproducible across centres and patient populations.

Introduction

Knee arthroplasty is a well-established treatment modality for the treatment of degenerative and inflammatory conditions of the knee. High patient satisfaction with pain relief and improvement in mobility is well documented [1, 2]. The average age for TKA is 65 years and the optimal age with regard to patient reported outcome measures is said to be approximately 70 years [3]. The increasing number of younger patients requiring surgery, combined with an ageing population, is likely to result in a significant increase in the incidence of TKA in the future [4]. Pain management and early mobilisation are essential for optimising functional recovery, facilitating timely discharge and improving overall patient outcomes following surgery. For many patients this can be life changing. However, many knee arthroplasty patients complain of high pain levels in the early hours following surgery and this can often inhibit early mobilisation and discharge. Therefore, early management of pain is crucial for these patients in order to expedite mobilisation [5]. Various techniques are routinely used such as Spinal Anaesthesia (SA), which typically provides good analgesia for 2–3 h postoperatively and intrathecal adjuvants can prolong this duration [6]. In addition, newer modalities such as local blocks can offer additional analgesia which will outlast the spinal block and provide good local analgesia promoting earlier mobilisation and discharge [7]. In this service evaluation, we examined a retrospective group of 100 patients undergoing knee arthroplasty surgery, some of which had additional local blocks and examined how this impacted their pain scores and discharge rates.

Materials and methods

This was a service evaluation of the established knee arthroplasty pathway at the South West London Elective Orthopaedic Centre, Epsom & St Helier University Hospitals NHS trust. Data were obtained exclusively from routinely collected care records; no alterations to standard care, randomisation, or research procedures were undertaken.

We carried out an evaluation of 100 consecutive patients undergoing knee arthroplasty surgery, including Total Knee Replacements (TKR), Unicondylar Knee Replacement (UKR), 1 Patellofemoral replacement (PFR), 1 Patella resurfacing (PR) and 5 revision TKR (RTKR).

Pain scores were assessed using Verbal Pain Rating Score [8] where 0 is no pain, 1 is mild pain, 2 is moderate pain and 3 is severe pain.

Anaesthetic technique, including intrathecal and intraoperative opioid administration, was at the discretion of the attending anaesthetist as part of standard clinical practice. Specific details regarding intrathecal opioid dosing and intraoperative opioid use were not consistently recorded within the routinely collected dataset and were therefore not available for analysis.

Regional anaesthesia strategies included adductor canal block (ACB), ACB + genicular block, ACB + IPACK block, or no block. All peripheral nerve blocks were performed under ultrasound guidance. In addition to the regional techniques, all patients had surgical infiltration prior to surgical closure using Ropivacaine 0.75% + Ketorolac + Adrenaline 1:1000 at doses ranging from 100–150 ml. For regional blocks, either bupivacaine or ropivacaine was used at varying concentrations and volumes at clinician discretion. Postoperative analgesia followed the established institutional knee arthroplasty pathway, consisting of regular paracetamol and non-steroidal anti-inflammatory medication unless contraindicated, with oral opioids administered as required for breakthrough pain. This background multimodal regimen was applied consistently across patients irrespective of regional anaesthesia technique.

Continuous variables were summarised as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Variables meeting normality assumptions (e.g. BMI) were compared between groups using the independent t-test. Variables that were ordinal (e.g. ASA grade) or non-normally distributed (e.g. age & pain scores) were compared using the Man-Whitney U test, and where appropriate, the Kruskal–Wallis test for comparisons across more than two groups. Positive predictors of same-day discharge were assessed using multivariable logistic regression, adjusting for age, gender, BMI, and mean 24-h pain score, block modality and type of surgery (TKR versus other knee procedures). Block modality was included in the multivariable logistic regression model as a categorical variable, with ‘no block’ specified as the reference category. Odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) and p-values were reported.

Pain assessment

Using the Verbal Rating Scale patients pain scores were assessed at 2, 6, 12 and 24 h and the total mean value obtained was 1.05 ± 0.81 (female 1.19 ± 0.85 and male 0.81 ± 0.67). There was a statistically significant difference in 24 mean pain scores between males and females, with females reporting higher pain on average (p-value 0.023, U-statistic 1441.0- Mann–Whitney U test).

For patients that were discharged on the same day of surgery, the mean pain score was lower than those who stayed longer (0.62 vs 1.2). Using logistic regression, higher mean pain scores was associated with a reduced likelihood of same day discharge (OR = 0.32, 95% CI 0.15–0.66, p- value 0.002). Multivariable predictive values for same day discharge

Regional blocks

In a multivariable logistic regression model adjusting for age, gender, BMI, mean 24-h pain score and type of surgery, block modality was analysed using no block as the reference category. Compared with no block, ACB + genicular was independently associated with a significantly higher likelihood of same-day discharge (OR 5.29, 95% CI 1.13–24.83, p = 0.035). ACB alone (OR 1.41, 95% CI 0.32–6.18, p = 0.650) and ACB + IPACK (OR 4.51, 95% CI 0.63–32.50, p = 0.135) were not statistically significant predictors.

Importantly, the association between ACB + genicular and same-day discharge persisted after adjustment for mean 24-h pain score, indicating that this relationship was not solely explained by differences in average postoperative pain.

Age, gender, BMI, ASA grade

Using a binary regression model, age, gender, BMI and ASA was recorded to analyse positive predictive factors for same day discharge.

Age was found to be a positive predictive factor with each additional year of age being associated with a 6% lower odds of same day discharge. (OR 0.94, 95% CI 0.889–0.991, p-value 0.022).

Males had more than twice the odds of same day discharge compared to females but the association was not statistically significant (OR 2.22, 95% CI 0.890–5.525, p-value 0.087).

Unusually, our study found that for each 1 kg/m2 increase in BMI, the odds of same day discharge increased by approximately 8.7% and was statistically significant (OR 1.09, 95% CI 1.002–1.178, p-value 0.044).

Higher ASA was associated with lower odds of same day discharge but was not statistically significant (OR 0.72, 95% CI 0.256–2.019, p-value 0.531).

Sensitivity analysis: primary TKR cohort

A sensitivity analysis restricted to patients undergoing primary total knee replacement (n = 81) was performed to further address procedural heterogeneity. Within the TKR-only cohort, ACB + genicular remained independently associated with higher odds of same-day discharge compared with no block (OR 8.13, 95% CI 1.05–62.86, p = 0.045). Higher mean 24-h pain score remained significantly associated with reduced likelihood of same-day discharge (OR 0.35, 95% CI 0.13–0.93, p = 0.036). BMI was also independently associated with increased likelihood of same-day discharge (OR 1.17 per 1 kg/m2 increase, 95% CI 1.03–1.32, p = 0.013). These findings demonstrate consistency of effect within a more homogeneous surgical subgroup.

Discussion

In this service evaluation of 100 patients undergoing knee arthroplasty, we found that significant predictors of same-day discharge were younger patient age and higher BMI. Male gender and lower ASA trended towards earlier discharge but were not statistically significant. A study carried out on preoperative predictors of same-day discharge after TKA featured 2266 patients (mean age 65.8 ± 8.5 year, BMI 31.9 ± 5.6 kg/m2), of which 205 (9%) were discharged on the day of surgery found that younger age, male gender, lower BMI and an ASA ≤ 3 were statistically significant factors for same day discharge [10]. A systematic review analysing reasons and risk factors for same-day discharge following arthroplasty also cited older age, female gender, obesity and ASA ≥ 3 as primary significant predictors of failed same-day discharge [11]. Our study higher ASA was associated with lower rates of same-day discharge but was not statistically significant. The association between higher BMI and same-day discharge should be interpreted cautiously. This finding may reflect selection bias within the fast-track pathway or unmeasured confounders rather than a direct physiological effect. However, a study assessing same day discharge rates on 3,897 patients who underwent fast-track uni-compartmental knee arthroplasty same-day discharge was achieved in 26% of patients (n = 922) with no statistically significant difference between BMI groups [12].

A significant finding of this study was that higher mean 24-h pain scores were strongly associated with a reduced likelihood of same day discharge, highlighting the importance of effective postoperative analgesia. Our results found that women had a significantly higher mean 24-pain score than men. This has also been noted in a study assessing sex differences and postoperative pain in patients undergoing TKA. Compared to males, females were found to have higher acute postoperative pain scores during the first two weeks after TKA in an age matched cohort [13]. A study on reasons and risk factors for failed same day discharge after primary TKA reported that a post operative pain score of > 8 (VAS Visual Analogue Scale) was a statistically significant risk factor for failed same-day discharge. Notably, this study cited failure to meet ambulation goals as the leading reason for failed same-day discharge [14].

In our study the choice of regional anaesthesia strategy did not significantly impact mean 24-h post operative pain scores, finding no statistical difference between using ACB alone, ACB + genicular, ACB + IPACK or no block. This is in line with trials comparing genicular nerve block and local infiltration, which show analgesic non-inferiority and similar functional outcomes. Mu et al. found that ACB combined with genicular provided non-inferior analgesia compared to ACB with local infiltration on post-operative day 1 after TKA and lower local anaesthetic use [15]. Cuñat et al. concluded that a genicular block provided non-inferior analgesia compared with local infiltration and also demonstrated a considerable reduction in local anaesthetic use [16]. Concurrently, a recent prospective, double blind RCT comparing ultrasound-guided adductor canal block with surgeon-performed peri-articular infiltration for pain management after total knee arthroplasty found lower pain scores only in the first 12 h post-op, with no significant difference in pain scores or range of motion beyond the post-operative day 1 [17].

The choice of regional anaesthesia technique may not always be the determining factor for mobilisation success. This is supported by Koczian et al., who reported comparable early mobilisation outcomes between different peripheral nerve block combinations (IPACK + ACB vs FNB + PSB groups) despite variations in pain scores, with IPACK + ACB having higher VAS pain scores and greater opioid requirements. These finding suggest that multiple regional techniques can be equally effective for mobilisation, even if pain scores between modality groups differ [18]. In our study, when assessing same-day discharge, no significant predictors were identified when comparing each of the four modalities independently. However, when ‘no block’ was used as the reference group, the use of ACB + genicular was associated with a significantly higher likelihood of same day discharge than ACB alone or ACB + IPACK. This aligns with the findings demonstrated by Zhao et al. highlighting that specific block combinations, such as ACB + genicular, were associated with superior early functional outcomes compared to ACB alone or ACB + IPACK [19]. Similarly, Akesen et al., compared Genicular versus IPACK (control being no block) after TKA and found shorter length of hospital stay, greater early ambulation distance and greater knee flexion in the genicular group [20]. Kukreja et al. also reported that the addition of genicular to ACB resulted in lower average pain scores, greater postoperative day-1 ambulation and shorter length of stay compared with ACB alone [21]. Taken together, these findings indicate that while some studies suggest equivalence between techniques, others demonstrate measurable advantages in early recovery when using specific block combinations such as ACB + genicular.

Conclusion

Across the four regional anaesthesia modalities (ACB only, ACB + genicular, ACB + IPACK, and no block), 24-h mean pain scores were comparable. Higher 24-h pain was independently associated with lower odds of same-day discharge, with women reporting higher pain scores than men.

When no block was used as the reference category, ACB + genicular was independently associated with a higher likelihood of same-day discharge after adjustment for age, BMI, mean 24-h pain score and surgical type. This suggests that factors beyond average pain score alone may contribute to discharge readiness.

Among patient factors, younger age and higher BMI were independently associated with same-day discharge, whereas gender and ASA grade were not statistically significant predictors.

Given the observational design, unequal subgroup sizes and heterogeneity in surgical procedures, these findings should be interpreted with appropriate caution. Further prospective and adequately powered studies are warranted to clarify the role of regional anaesthesia strategy in facilitating early discharge following knee arthroplasty.

Limitations

This study has limitations that should be considered. The sample size of 100 patients from a single centre limits generalisability. Block allocation was not randomised and no blinding was employed.

Regional anaesthesia subgroups were unequal and, in some cases, small, which may reduce statistical power and contribute to wide confidence intervals in regression analyses.

Although surgical type was adjusted for and a TKR-only sensitivity analysis was performed, procedural heterogeneity remains inherent to real-world service evaluation. Approximately one third of patients underwent second-side knee surgery, which has been associated with increased postoperative pain and opioid requirements in prior studies [22, 23] Individual patient-level laterality data were not consistently available for inclusion in multivariable modelling and therefore second-side surgery could not be adjusted for, representing potential residual confounding.

Local anaesthetic type, concentration and volume varied at clinician discretion in routine practice. This pharmacological variability may represent an additional source of residual confounding when interpreting associations between block strategy and discharge outcomes.
