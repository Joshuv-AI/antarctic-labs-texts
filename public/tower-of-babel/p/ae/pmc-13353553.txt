Triggers, Responses, and Outcomes for Patient Related Violence and/or Aggression Events During Acute Hospitalisation: A Documentary Analysis
Authors: Debra Kerr, Alison M Hutchinson, Elisa Ilarda, Louise Alexander, Susan Perlen, Nicole Davies, Trish Mant, Angela Skazlic, Joanna Petrunic, Rachel Mrhar, Adam Searby
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimTo examine triggers, responses, and outcomes for patient‐related violence and/or aggression events during acute hospitalisation.DesignThis was a descriptive observational study undertaken at two healthcare organisations.MethodsPre‐existing data were extracted from organisational incident reports and individual medical records during a retrospective period (1/1/2023 to 30/6/2023) and a prospective period (7/6/2024 to 16/11/2024). Violence and/or aggression events requiring an organisational response that involved patients hospitalised in general ward areas at a metropolitan (Site A) and a regional (Site B) site were included. Data were analysed using descriptive statistics and content analysis.ResultsThe sample included 100 retrospective cases and 42 prospective cases. The most prevalent causes for hospitalisation related to a medical or mental health condition and dementia. Confusion and irritability were the most common forms of behaviour of concern prior to the event. Physical restraint was utilised more frequently in the prospective period compared with the retrospective period. Chemical restraint was used in approximately half of the cases in both study periods. A form of physical violence was the most prevalent behavioural symptom in both periods, followed by verbal aggression and inability to be re‐directed.ConclusionPatients with a pre‐existing medical condition, confusion and/or dementia are frequently involved in violent and/or aggressive events in ward settings. Physical and chemical restraints are commonly used to manage violence and aggression.Implications for the ProfessionAlternative strategies are needed to manage occupational violence and aggression to minimise the need for physical and/or chemical restraint.ImpactThis study addresses a gap in evidence regarding triggers, responses and outcomes for patients exhibiting violence and aggression in ward settings during hospitalisation. Patients with dementia, confusion and irritability frequently exhibit behaviours of concern, exposing healthcare workers to potential physical and psychological harm.Reporting MethodSTROBE checklist.No Patient or Public ContributionThis study did not include patient or public involvement in its design, conduct, or reporting.Keywords: aggression, healthcare professional, hospital, response, violence, ward

Introduction

Healthcare workers, including nurses, are frequently exposed to violent, aggressive, abusive or threatening behaviour, exhibited by patients or visitors, in healthcare settings. It has been estimated in a systematic review of 253 studies examining violence against healthcare workers globally that 61.9% had experienced some form of verbal or physical violence, and 24.4% had experienced physical violence (Liu et al. 2019). Safe Work Australia (2024) have defined workplace violence as an event in which ‘a person is abused, threatened or assaulted at the workplace or while they are working’. Safe Work Australia (2021) describe that workplace violence and aggression can include actions and behaviours such as physical assault (e.g., biting, scratching, hitting, kicking, pushing, grabbing or throwing objects), intentionally coughing or spitting on someone, sexual assault or any other form of indecent physical contact, harassment or aggressive behaviour that creates a fear of violence (e.g., stalking, sexual harassment, verbal threats and abuse, or yelling and swearing), hazing or initiation practices for new or young workers, gendered violence, and/or violence from a family or domestic relationship when this occurs at the workplace.

Aggression and violence have been implicated as substantial issues in workforce sustainability, leading to higher rates of staff turnover, decreased job satisfaction and intention to leave, and increased costs in managing physical and psychological workplace injuries, staff training and replacement (Rosenthal et al. 2018).

The financial costs of patient aggression and violence are difficult to quantify but extend to loss of workdays for healthcare staff, workforce sustainability issues and staff replacement costs, workplace injury claims and staff replacement for unintended sick leave. A survey study of Tasmanian (Australia) nurses (N = 2407) found that verbal or physical aggression was not only associated with these issues but influenced productivity and clinical errors (Farrell et al. 2006). Whilst costs related to staff absence and injury were not assessed in their study, hence there was no evidence of cause and effect, there was likely a financial organisational impact from nurses' experiences of aggression.

Although little financial data on the true cost of aggression and violence and healthcare exist in Australian settings, the American Hospital Association (2017) estimated the cost of community violence to hospitals and health systems in the United States during 2016 to be 2.7 billion USD. The Victorian Auditor General's (2015) report into ‘Occupational Violence Against Healthcare Workers’ shows that 12% of overall occupational violence claims came from the healthcare sector, with 94% of these claims coming from public hospitals. Their analysis also revealed that registered nurses, mental health nurses and personal care assistants are among the top 10 occupations exposed to occupational violence. Safe Work Australia (2024) also report that healthcare workers have the highest proportion of serious compensation claims.

The impact on healthcare staff goes beyond financial implications, with occupational violence implicated in experiences of fear and depressive symptoms (Fu et al. 2023), and role dissatisfaction for staff who have been subject to verbal and/or physical aggression or violence (Zhang et al. 2021). This is exacerbated by inadequate support after violent incidents. In a qualitative study by Dafny et al. (2022) in a regional Queensland hospital, it was identified that nurses often felt unsupported after an occupational violence incident, with formal support mechanisms described as inadequate.

Violence and harassment are one of the most common occupational hazards in the health sector. In Australia, Safe Work Australia (2021) mandates that all businesses are required by law to eliminate risks to health and safety for all employees, so far as is reasonably practicable. Where risks cannot be eliminated, there is an expectation that mitigation strategies should be implemented to minimise the impact of those risks on healthcare workers. Risk management involves the following four steps (Safe Work Australia 2021): (1) Identifying the hazards, (2) Assessing the associated risks, (3) Implementing control measures to eliminate or minimise the risks, and (4) Regularly reviewing control measures to ensure they are effective. One means of controlling occupational violence and aggression is the establishment of communication and alarm systems, including Code Grey responses.

Background

In Australian healthcare settings, a Code Grey is typically described as an organisation‐level response to actual or potential violent, aggressive, abusive or threatening behaviour, exhibited by patients or visitors, towards others or themselves, which creates a risk to health and safety (State of Victoria 2011). Most healthcare services in Victoria have a Code Grey emergency system, sometimes referred to as an unplanned Code Grey, initiated as a response to aggression and violence from healthcare consumers, family members, and other visitors. The Department of Health and Human Services (DHHS 2017) (Victoria, Australia) introduced the Code Grey Standards following the ‘Inquiry into violence and security arrangements in Victorian hospitals and, in particular, emergency departments’ (State of Victoria 2011). That inquiry report highlighted the ongoing problem of violence in Victorian hospitals, placing nurses most at risk.

More recently, healthcare organisations, including the settings in which this study was conducted, have introduced a planned Code Grey response, which is initiated by staff following a risk‐based assessment for anticipated assistance with a scheduled event (e.g., anticipated difficult medication administration or patient appointment, anticipated challenging emergency admission) (DHHS 2017). For planned and unplanned Code Grey incidents, a team comprising of designated staff respond to the events. It is also a government requirement that the Code Grey team have specially trained staff members available for incidents both during and after business hours (DHHS 2017).

The prevalence of unplanned Code Grey incidents in Australian hospitals is difficult to define due to differences in reporting practices, lack of a uniform definition of violence and aggression, inconsistency in measurement, significant under‐reporting, and different healthcare contexts. Also, most international research is concentrated in mental health and emergency department (ED) (Hou et al. 2024; Littlechild 2015) settings. For example, Littlechild (2015) found that 69% of assaults reported by National Health Service staff occurred in mental health and learning disability settings. In a meta‐analysis of 65 studies, Li et al. (2020) found that one in five healthcare professionals experience workplace physical violence perpetrated by patients or visitors annually. The highest prevalence was for nurses employed in mental health settings. The global incidence of occupational violence is unclear due to recognised under‐reporting (Veronesi et al. 2023). It is also acknowledged that staff in EDs perceive violence as an inherent occupational risk (Nikathil et al. 2017). Estimates reveal that one in every three ED medical trainees has experienced violence from a patient or carer (Australasian College for Emergency Medicine 2024).

The composition of aggressive incidents in healthcare differs by area. In the ED, long waiting times, poorly understood triage systems, overcrowding, barriers to effective communication (e.g., burn‐out and compassion fatigue), confusion, and alcohol intoxication are contributing factors (Australasian College for Emergency Medicine 2024). In contrast, risk factors for aggression in mental health inpatient wards have been categorised as patient‐related, staff‐related, and ward‐related contributing factors (Weltens et al. 2021). Patient‐related factors include psychotic disorder or bipolar disorder, substance use, a history of aggression, and younger age (Weltens et al. 2021). Staff‐related factors include male gender, unqualified or temporary staff, job strain, dissatisfaction with the job or management, burn‐out and poor interpersonal communication between patients and staff (Weltens et al. 2021). Ward‐related factors include higher bed occupancy, a busy and unsafe ward, a restrictive environment, lack of structure in the day, inability to smoke and lack of privacy (Weltens et al. 2021).

There is limited evidence regarding prevalence and factors associated with Code Grey incidents in acute hospital ward settings in Australia. In a retrospective 24‐month audit of medical records in a Victorian intensive care unit, Pol et al. (2019) found that female patients who had experienced drug overdose or head trauma were most likely to be involved in Code Grey incidents. In an analysis of patient factors associated with aggression in inpatient ward settings in Victoria (Australia), Williamson et al. (2014) found delirium and dementia, male sex, and older age (mean of 68 years) increased the risk for Code Grey incidents. An audit of electronic records by Lim et al. (2022) revealed that increased age, male sex, and cognitive or neurological impairment were risk factors for Code Greys in a private hospital setting. To date, there has been little examination of triggers and responses for Code Grey incidents in hospital ward settings. This study was designed to address this gap.

The Study

Aim and Research Questions

The aim of this study was to examine triggers, responses, and outcomes for Code Grey incidents in hospital ward settings. The research questions guiding this project were: What factors contribute to Code Grey incidents? What responses are applied during Code Grey incidents? What are the outcomes of Code Grey incidents? The methods of this study are detailed in line with the STROBE checklist for reporting observational studies (von Elm et al. 2007).

Methods

Design

This was an observational study of unplanned Code Grey incidents for two healthcare organisations during two study periods: retrospective (1/1/2023 to 30/6/2023) and prospective (7/6/2024 to 16/11/2024). Secondary analysis of data was undertaken by an audit of unplanned Code Grey incident reports and individual medical records. Currently, public hospitals in the State of Victoria generally use an online database for the reporting of all clinical (e.g., medication errors) and non‐clinical (e.g., violent events) incidents, with Code Grey incidents categorised as non‐clinical incidents. Usually, these reports include brief patient demographics and a narrative account of the event. Code Grey incident reports were used as the primary data source. To gather additional information about the Code Grey event, data were also extracted from the individual medical record for each case. For the prospective period, data were also obtained directly from ward staff regarding the Code Grey event.

Study Setting and Sampling

Data were collected in general ward areas at two hospitals in a metropolitan health service (Site A) and a regional health service (Site B) in Victoria, Australia. General wards included acute medical‐surgical wards, geriatric evaluation wards, and rehabilitation wards.

Site A is a large public hospital located in the Western regions of metropolitan Melbourne, Australia. A range of acute and sub‐acute clinical services are provided at the hospital, including mental health and wellbeing, emergency medicine, intensive care, medical and surgical services, women's and children's services, and drug health and addiction medicine. The organisation employs more than 11,000 staff and is the major healthcare provider to one of the fastest growing and most diverse regions of Australia. The catchment population is almost 900,000 and community members speak more than 150 different languages and dialects.

Site B is the largest provider of regional healthcare in Victoria and employs over 9000 staff. It has a primary catchment population of 350,000 that extends to 500,000 for some tertiary services. A comprehensive range of health services is provided at the hospital including acute care (medicine, surgery, women's health, paediatrics, emergency, and specialist clinics), mental health, alcohol and other drugs services, rehabilitation, geriatric medicine, palliative care and hospital in the home.

The study was not designed to test a specific hypothesis; therefore, a formal sample size calculation was not performed. A target sample of 100 cases was sought for the retrospective period, whereas the sample for the prospective period was limited by funds, researcher availability, and presence at the time of the Code Grey event.

Inclusion and/or Exclusion Criteria

To be included in the retrospective period, an incident report must have been completed for hospitalised patients during a ward admission within the study period (1/1/2023 to 30/6/2023). The unique record (UR) hospital number for patients with a reported unplanned Code Grey event within the 6‐month period was obtained from the Security Manager of each organisation, in the form of a Microsoft Excel spreadsheet. Eligible incident reports included the first 50 unplanned Code Grey incidents at each site. Once 50 cases were identified at each hospital, no further data extraction was undertaken, limiting the sample to a total of 100 cases across both sites.

For recruitment during the prospective period, one research assistant was employed at each site for 1 day per week. They were notified of an unplanned Code Grey event via the respective hospital notification systems. Due to security restrictions at one site, two different processes were followed. At Site A, the researcher attended the Code Grey site approximately 30 min following the event to review the medical records and to ask staff for more detail about the Code Grey event. At Site B, the researcher attended the site immediately on notification of the Code Grey event to observe the response, review the medical records and to ask staff for more detail about the Code Grey event. Data were collected during the period 7/6/2024 to 16/11/2024. Staff were given an information form which provided details about the study and their potential involvement.

For individual patients, only the first Code Grey event in the study period was included as a case in this study. Data were not collected from EDs, critical care units, or mental health units. However, patients hospitalised in medical wards who were involved in Code Grey incidents in common areas such as foyer areas, waiting areas or cafes were included.

Data Collection

Unique record numbers were used to identify the unplanned Code Grey event in the online incident system. Data related to each Code Grey event were extracted from the incident report and documented on an Audit Tool created for this study. Following data extraction from the incident report, information was extracted from the individual medical record for up to 48 h prior to the Code Grey event on the Audit Tool. As previously described, following notification of the unplanned Code Grey event for the prospective period, the research assistants attended the incident location to obtain additional information regarding the Code Grey event.

The Code Grey incidents were scrutinised for details about triggers, responses, and outcomes. Regarding triggers, the reason for the Code Grey event and documented behaviours of concern observed prior to the event were recorded.

Responses following initiation of a Code Grey event included strategies used to manage the event, healthcare professional and security attendance, and physical and chemical restraint. Outcomes related to the Code Grey event included transfer/hospital discharge and adverse events concerning staff or patients (e.g., injuries). Additional data extracted included demographic information (e.g., age, sex), date/time, duration, location (e.g., ward type), hospital site, prior Code Grey incidents, primary language, need for an interpreter, weapons, evidence of drugs and/or alcohol, admission reason, past medical history, and history of cognitive impairment.

At both sites, the Broset Violence Checklist (BVC) is utilised as a short‐term prediction tool (Hvidhjelm et al. 2023). Six behaviours of concern (e.g., confusion, irritability, boisterousness, verbal threats, physical threats, and attacks on objects) are recorded as either absent or present, then the patient is rated as low, moderate, or high risk for exhibiting violence during hospitalisation. At the time of the study, completion of BVC forms followed a different process at each site. For Site A, completion of this form was mandatory for all patients on ward admission. In contrast, the BVC form was completed by ED staff at Site B for patients exhibiting concerning behaviours.

Data Analysis

Data were analysed to examine trends for triggers, responses and outcomes using descriptive statistical analysis (median (Mn), inter‐quartile range (IQR), number, percentage). Analyses were performed using SPSS version 29. Data files are stored in a secure hospital server, only accessible to researchers named in this protocol.

Condensing descriptions of the Code Grey incidents, as documented in the incident report and medical record, was undertaken using an inductive content analysis approach, a process aimed to systematically transform a large amount of content into organised key findings (Elo and Kyngäsm 2008). Data relating to triggers and behaviours were extracted from the Microsoft Excel spreadsheet, which contained the audit data, and transferred to a Microsoft Word document. Then, the Microsoft Word files were uploaded into NVivo to assist with organisation of the data and analyses. Two researchers independently analysed the data to decipher the potential triggers leading to the event and the nature of behaviour exhibited by the patient. Actions and behaviours typically conformed to Safe Work Australia (2021) criteria, as previously described (e.g., physical assault, intentionally coughing or spitting). This was the definition adopted in this study to inform data collection and analysis.

The data were read and re‐read to gain an understanding of the Code Grey event. Following an inductive content analysis approach, text was labelled with codes related to potential triggers leading to the event and the nature of behaviour exhibited by the patient. When all data were analysed, codes were grouped into categories to reflect similar meaning. This was a reflective process, involving review of the original data once initial categories were created to confirm that the meaning of codes and categories were adequately refined and reflective of a deep understanding of the data. Once completed, the two researchers compared their interpretations of the data and reached consensus regarding the categories for triggers leading to the event and the nature of behaviour exhibited by the patient.

Ethical Considerations

Prior to commencement of data collection, ethical approval was obtained from the two healthcare organisations (HREC/101563/VICBH‐2023‐404,603(v5); 23/177) and the academic institution (2024‐146‐240502). As no identifying information was collected, a waiver of consent was obtained for retrospective documentary data extraction. Unique record numbers were documented on a coding sheet to enable data linkage between sources of data (e.g., incident reports, individual medical records). Informed consent, in verbal form, was obtained from nurses who participated in the prospective period.

Results

Characteristics of the Sample

For the retrospective period, a total of 2048 unplanned Code Grey incidents were initiated during 1/1/2023 to 30/6/2023 across both sites as shown in Figure 1. Following exclusion of Code Grey events in ED and mental health settings, at Site A there were 271 incidents in ward settings which involved 107 individuals; and at Site B there were 786 incidents in ward settings which involved 181 individuals. Records were audited for the first 50 patients who met the inclusion criteria at both sites, reaching a final sample of 100 cases. A total of 42 incidents, 21 at each site, were audited for the prospective period.

Patient characteristics also differed across the sites, with 86% of patients at Site B using English as their primary language compared with 52% at Site A in the retrospective period, and 95.2% at Site B compared with 57.1% at Site A in the prospective period. There was a roughly even distribution of Code Grey incidents across the morning (37%), afternoon (39%), and night shifts (24%) in the retrospective period. In contrast, 61.9% of incidents occurred in the morning shift for the prospective period, likely due to the process of data collection during the business week.

Code Grey incidents occurred most frequently in the patient's room (46.5%) and the ward corridor (27.3%). Similar rates of weapon use were observed across the study periods (e.g., retrospective—15%; prospective—16.7%), including furniture, mobilisation frames, personal items (e.g., phone, bag, hot food), and intravenous cannulae.

For both study periods, the most prevalent cause for hospitalisation in the ward settings related to a medical (55%) or mental health condition (51%), as shown in Table 2. Dementia was a prominent medical condition, warranting hospital admission for 37% of patients in the retrospective period and 26.2% of patients in the prospective period.

Responses and Outcomes for the Code Grey Event

Physical restraint was reportedly higher in the prospective period (57.1%) compared with the retrospective period (30%). Differences were observed across the two sites for physical restraint use in the prospective period with 47.6% at Site A and 66.7% at Site B. The types of physical restraint were not documented in the existing reports. Chemical restraint was used for 55% of Code Grey incidents in both study periods. Slightly higher rates of chemical restraint use were observed at Site A for the retrospective and prospective studies (64%, 52.4%) compared with Site B (46%, 42.9%), respectively. Medications administered in response to the Code Grey event in the retrospective period included: Olanzapine (N = 11, 11%), Haloperidol (n = 9, 9%), Midazolam (n = 9, 9%), Quetiapine (n = 9, 9%), Lorazepam (n = 6, 6%), Oxazepam (n = 5, 5%), Diazepam (n = 4, 4%), Droperidol (n = 1, 1%) and Risperidone (n = 1, 1%). For the prospective period, medications administered included Olanzapine (N = 10, 23.8%), Quetiapine (n = 9, 9%), Midazolam (n = 3, 7.1%), Lorazepam (n = 3, 7.1%), Haloperidol (n = 2, 4.8%), Oxazepam (n = 1, 2.4%) and Risperidone (n = 1, 2.4%).

In the retrospective and prospective study periods, respectively, nurses (82%, 100%) and security staff (95%, 97.6%) were more likely to be present during the Code Grey event, in contrast to medical doctors (50%, 54.8%). Few patient injuries (n = 7) were reported for the Code Grey incidents, with 5 (10%) minor injuries in the retrospective period for Site A, and one (2.1%) minor injury in each study period (n = 2) for Site B. There were five minor staff injuries in total, three (3%) in the retrospective period and two (4.8%) in the prospective period.

Following the event, most patients remained in their allocated ward in the retrospective period (98%). However, in the prospective period, patients were more likely to be transferred to another ward or discharged from hospital following the Code Grey event. At Site A, two (9.5%) patients were discharged from hospital; and at Site B, two (9.5%) patients were moved to a single room, three (14.2%) patients were discharged, and one (4.8%) patient was transferred to another ward.

Triggers Leading to the Event

Potential triggers of the Code Grey incidents were categorised following content analysis of the extracted audit data, as shown in Table 4. A single patient may have had multiple documented triggers. There were some observed differences across the study periods. In the retrospective period, the most common trigger was agitation (48%), followed by wanting to leave (37%) and wandering (37%). In contrast, in the prospective period, wanting to leave (78.6%) was the most prevalent trigger for the incident, followed by agitation (69%), confusion (66.7%), and wandering (47.6%).

Discussion

This study was conducted to address the limited evidence regarding prevalence and factors associated with Code Grey incidents in acute hospital ward settings in Australian settings. We found that healthcare workers are frequently exposed to behaviours of concern, particularly by older patients and patients with dementia, confusion and irritability. Increasing awareness of situational factors and patient characteristics associated with violence and aggression in hospital ward settings can inform organisational strategies to prevent harm and reduce future occurrence. Despite the dearth of evidence in the literature, Code Grey incidents in ward settings occur as frequently as in ED and mental health settings in the organisations in which we conducted this study. Also, patients for whom a Code Grey response is called are likely to experience multiple Code Grey incidents across one hospitalisation period. We also identified that physical and/or chemical restraint was commonly used in response to violent and/or aggressive patient behaviours.

The Code Grey Standards were introduced in Victoria, Australia (DHHS 2017) to ensure health services implemented a standard and consistent escalation process to any actual or potential threat to (1) reduce the risk of injury to staff, patients and visitors, and (2) ensure patients are treated safely and with dignity. Despite the high rate of Code Grey incidents for both organisations in this current study, there were few reported injuries to patients and/or staff. Also, no patient or staff member experienced a serious injury. It is possible that the low number of injuries evident in this study was due to staff awareness of organisational procedures to manage violent and/or aggressive situations, including prompt Code Grey responses. However, management of the Code Grey incident with physical and/or chemical restraint was common. Physical restraint was utilised more frequently in the prospective period (57.1%) compared with the retrospective period (30%), and chemical restraint was used in just over one half (55%) of all Code Grey incidents. In slightly more than half of cases (Retrospective: 54.8%, Prospective: 50%), a medical doctor was in attendance, which reflects the proportion of Code Grey events in which medication was prescribed. For Site A, the senior clinical lead, most often the nurse‐in‐charge of the shift, holds responsibility for allocating specific responsibilities and roles as required and to request medical attendance if deemed necessary. In contrast, at Site B, hospital procedure requires medical doctors to respond to Code Grey events. Therefore, the rate of medical attendance for Site B Code Grey events (50%) reflects moderate adherence to the organisational procedure for responding to Code Grey events. Larger observational studies are needed to measure adherence to the organisational procedure for responding to Code Grey events.

There is widespread opinion that restrictive practices can, and should be, reduced in health care settings (Department of Health 2013). It is therefore surprising that the rates of physical and chemical restraint increased from 2023 to 2024 as observed in this study. Whilst experts acknowledge that less is known about Code Grey events in acute ward settings compared with mental health and emergency settings (Department of Health 2013), research indicates that strategies can be tailored to address organisational needs and OVA characteristics. Initiatives shown to reduce the use of restrictive interventions include psychosocial models of care, treatment plans, assessment tools, physical environments, sensory experiences, engaging/debriefing/monitoring consumers, communication of behaviour standards, and physical activity (Cuskelly and Nicola 2024). Our findings suggest that more work is needed to reduce restrictive practices to manage behaviours of concern in hospital ward settings. Development of future strategies for the two organisations involved in this study might focus on the identification of triggers and behavioural characteristics identified in this study, and related de‐escalation training for healthcare professionals and security staff.

When first developed, it was originally intended that a Code Grey response should be initiated for actual or potential violent, aggressive, abusive or threatening behaviour, exhibited by patients, towards others or themselves, which creates a risk to health and safety (DHHS 2017). It has been identified in this study that a moderate proportion of Code Grey incidents are initiated in response to staff inability to re‐direct patients and cognitively impaired patients trying to leave the ward and/or hospital. When BVC were completed, a large proportion of patients were either confused (67.1%) or irritable (42.9%). Largely, these Code Grey incidents involved patients with dementia who were experiencing confusion and/or irritability. Although not legally restrained in acute medical settings, staff restrict patients from leaving their room and/or ward and/or hospital setting to maintain their and others' safety. During 2022–2023 in Australia, there were approximately 26,300 dementia‐related hospitalisations, and the rate increased by 24% compared with 2016–17 data (Australian Institute for Health and Welfare 2024). Individuals with dementia are at risk of adverse healthcare outcomes during hospital admission such as falls, poor nutrition and hydration, infections, and delirium (Bail et al. 2015). Whilst training in dementia care as a single strategy has been found to be effective in improving staff knowledge of dementia and confidence in providing effective, safe and dignified care for people living with dementia, evidence suggests that staff require leadership support and resources to improve care practices (Handley et al. 2017). This study was not designed to explore staff competence for providing nursing care to people with dementia in hospital ward settings.

Across the sites, there was a significant difference in the rate of BVC form completion: Site A: 90%–95% vs. Site B: 50%–29%. Processes were different at each respective organisation at the time of the study regarding assessment for behaviour of concerns using the BVC form. Nursing staff were required to complete a BVC form for all newly admitted patients to ward settings at Site A, whereas, at Site B, it was ED clinicians' responsibility to complete BVC forms for all patients subsequently admitted to the organisation. Few (28.6%) forms were completed for patients who subsequently were involved in a Code Grey incident at Site B in the prospective period of the study. However, a moderate proportion (n = 4, 66.7%) of these patients subsequently exhibited behaviours of concern during hospitalisation. This places staff at risk and prohibits the timely implementation of behaviour care plans and other strategies to prevent the occurrence of occupational violence.

It is feasible that the differences observed in this study across the two periods were affected by the method of data extraction. Data for the retrospective period were extracted from incident reports and individual medical records. In contrast, data collected in the prospective period were collected from incident reports and medical records, and through participant reflection immediately following the incident. The researchers observed that more in‐depth information in relation to the triggers, responses and outcomes for the Code Grey incident was obtained in conversations with clinicians who were involved in the event. This suggests that there may be deficits in documentation related to Code Grey incidents in incident reports and individual medical records. There is widespread recognition that implementation of systematic reporting systems is scant and inconsistent across geographical sites (Rossi et al. 2023). Veronesi et al. (2023) reported that prior to 2020, Australia and California were the only regions with established violence monitoring programmes. To address the under‐reporting of workplace violence, up to 73%, they implemented a systematic workplace violence surveillance system in two public referral hospitals in Italy, which resulted in significant reductions in under‐reporting. Our study also highlights that incident reports may lack important details about the OVA incident. For example, it is possible that more staff were injured in our study periods. Specifically, it was identified that more patients had injuries related to the Code Grey event compared with the staff. It is also feasible that staff did not report minor injuries. This is likely to hinder efforts to develop effective strategies, which rely on in‐depth and accurate reporting of organisational workplace violence.

Finally, we identified that many patients are involved in multiple Code Grey events across one hospitalisation. Hence, there is potential that healthcare workers, mainly nurses, are being exposed to OVA on a frequent basis if they are employed in one specific ward. Violence prevalence has been estimated at 78.9% for healthcare workers in a recent meta‐analysis by Rossi et al. (2023). Their report did not include a breakdown of OVA prevalence for different departments and/or ward settings, aside from describing that healthcare workers employed in psychiatric wards are most impacted. As previously mentioned, single strategies have been found to be less effective than multicomponent interventions (Somani et al. 2021), which might include education and training, post‐incident debriefing, multidisciplinary rapid response teams, and environmental modifications. Data relating to nurses' experiences of Code Grey events in ward settings will be reported in a separate future manuscript. However, we can report that both organisations offer education to all healthcare staff in the form of compulsory annual online training and utilise a multidisciplinary rapid response team for all Code Grey incidents. At the time of the study neither organisation had a formal post‐incident debriefing process for ward‐based Code Grey events, nor were staff monitored for psychosocial health outcomes, such as anxiety, stress, poor sleep and burn‐out. This may need consideration as staff employed in ward areas are likely to be exposed to recurrent violence and aggression in their workplace, which may result in adverse mental health outcomes and potential injury for staff.

Strengths and Limitations of the Work

This study has some limitations. Data were collected from two hospital sites in Victoria (Australia). Hence, findings may not be applicable to other settings. Documentation in incident reports and medical records about the Code Grey incidents was sparse, particularly related to triggers leading to the event and the type of adverse behaviour. Data were obtained across multiple forms of medical record documentation to gain an understanding of the event. It was observed by the researchers at each organisation that more information was obtained by interviewing clinicians following the event, in contrast to reliance on documentation in the medical record alone. Potential measurement bias may also affect the reliability of study findings associated with retrospectively reviewing incident reports and medical record documentation.

Limited resources restricted the researcher's capacity to recruit participants for all Code Grey incidents in the prospective study period. The researchers collecting the data for the prospective period were employed one weekday per week, during business hours, and attempted to capture data for Code Grey incidents occurring during this time period only. This may have introduced some bias. Future researchers might consider observation of behaviour in patients who are involved in recurring Code Grey incidents to scrutinise triggers and utilisation of strategies in more depth.

Recommendations for Further Research

To prevent ongoing mental health effects from exposure to frequent Code Grey events, healthcare organisations might consider the development, implementation and evaluation of debriefing and/or reflection following Code Grey incidents. Most evidence, however, relates to post‐cardiac arrest clinical scenarios, rather than OVA. Further research is needed to consider the ongoing effect of violence and aggression in ward settings on healthcare workers' mental health, coping, resilience and retention.

Implications for Policy and Practice

As many patients involved in Code Grey incidents in this study were older and had pre‐existing medical conditions and/or dementia, the design of future ward environments may need consideration regarding appropriate lighting, single bed layout, and security. Identified risk factors for the initiation of Code Grey responses in this study included older age, confusion, agitation, delirium, and dementia. Although physical violence was the prominent behaviour of concern observed in this study, staff also seemed to be challenged by wandering patients. Alternative strategies may be required to manage situations in which patients are resisting care rather than exhibiting violence and/or aggression. Provision of focused in‐person training initiatives for management of behaviours of concern in patients with dementia, including de‐escalation techniques, may need to be prioritised for ward settings.

Conclusion

This study has identified that a large proportion of Code Grey incidents occur in hospital ward settings, often involving recurring events with older patients resisting care. Consistent utilisation of behaviour of concern assessment tools to identify patients at risk of exhibiting violence and aggression is needed. Healthcare staff may need focused training to increase their confidence when dealing with patients who have dementia or are confused, including distraction techniques.
