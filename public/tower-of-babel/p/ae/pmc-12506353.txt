LIMCH1 inhibits antitumor immunity by upregulating PDL1 in triple-negative breast cancer
Authors: Minghan Qiu, Zhanhua Gao, Mengran Tian, Yuya Liu, Jinpu Liu, Qiaonan Zhang, Ruxue Liu, Yayun Wang, Xiangqian Zheng, Jie Hao, Zhen Yang, Ming Gao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBreast cancer is one of the most common malignancies globally. Despite advancements in treatment, efficacy for certain subtypes such as triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) remains limited. Immunotherapy has provided new hope for advanced breast cancer, particularly triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), though its effectiveness varies by tumor type and patient differences. LIMCH1, a relatively understudied LIM-domain protein, has been linked to cytoskeletal regulation and various cancers, including lung, cervical, and renal carcinoma. This study aims to investigate LIMCH1 expression and its impact in breast cancer.MethodsThe prognostic role of LIMCH1 in multiple cancers was analyzed using the TCGA database, and its expression in breast cancer was validated through immunohistochemistry (IHC) on breast cancer tissue microarrays. LIMCH1 overexpression and knockdown TNBC cell lines were constructed, and CCK-8, colony formation, transwell migration, and wound healing assays were performed to assess the effect of LIMCH1 on cell proliferation and migration. PD-L1 expression and selected immune-related proteins, such as CD3, CD4, CD8, and p-ERK1/2, were evaluated by flow cytometry, immunofluorescence, or Western blotting. A mouse allograft model was established to evaluate LIMCH1’s role in tumor growth and T-cell infiltration within the TIME.ResultsLIMCH1 was highly expressed in breast cancer and associated with poor prognosis. Bioinformatics analysis revealed that high LIMCH1 expression correlates with an immunosuppressive state. Functional assays showed that LIMCH1 overexpression promoted proliferation and migration, while knockdown inhibited these phenotypes. Mechanistic studies indicated that LIMCH1 upregulated PD-L1 via MAPK pathway activation, suppressed CD4 + and CD8 + T-cell infiltration, and led to an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment. High LIMCH1 expression was also linked to low response rates to anti-PD-1 therapy, suggesting it may influence immunotherapy efficacy.ConclusionLIMCH1 promotes tumor activity in breast cancer by activating the MAPK pathway, upregulating PD-L1, and enhancing immune evasion. LIMCH1 may serve as a potential therapeutic target for breast cancer immunotherapy. This study provides new evidence for the role of LIMCH1 in breast cancer and offers new insights and directions for precision therapy of breast cancer.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12964-025-02387-6.Keywords: Breast cancer, TNBC, LIMCH1, PD-L1, Immunotherapy

Introduction

Cancer remains one of the leading causes of morbidity and mortality worldwide, with an estimated 20 million new cases and 10 million deaths reported in 2022 [1, 2]. Breast cancer is the second most common cancer worldwide, with approximately 11.6% of all new cancer cases [1]. By 2040, it is projected that over 3 million new cases and 1 million deaths will occur due to population growth and aging [3]. Triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), which comprises 15% of cases, is primarily treated with chemotherapy as it does not respond to endocrine or HER-2 therapies. However, the efficacy of chemotherapy is limited, resulting in advanced TNBC patients surviving only 13–18 months [3, 4]. The poor treatment outcomes for TNBC are attributed to multiple factors, including high tumor microenvironment (TME) heterogeneity and a lack of biomarkers [5]. Consequently, further investigation of novel approaches is necessary to enhance the clinical prognosis of these patients.

In recent years, significant progress has been made in cancer treatment owing to a deeper understanding of tumor biology [6]. Immunotherapy, particularly immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) targeting PD-1/PD-L1, has brought substantial and durable clinical benefits across multiple malignancies [6, 7]. TNBC exhibits higher immune activity compared to other breast cancer types, supporting the utilization of ICIs [8]. However, ICIs monotherapy in TNBC benefits only about 20% of PD-L1-positive patients [9]. Research indicates that the combination of ICIs with neoadjuvant chemotherapy in early-stage TNBC achieves a high pathological complete response (pCR) rate, irrespective of PD-L1 status, suggesting that PD-L1 alone is an insufficient predictor of ICIs efficacy [10, 11]. At present, there is a lack of reliable immune biomarkers to identify TNBC patients who will derive benefit from ICIs, underscoring the necessity to investigate TNBC immune evasion mechanisms to enhance immunotherapy outcomes.

LIMCH1 is a minimally investigated LIM domain protein that has been identified as interacting with the actin cytoskeleton [12, 13]. Recent studies have demonstrated LIMCH1’s potential as a significant regulator in various cancers, including lung, renal, and cervical carcinoma [14–17]. In breast cancer, LIMCH1 appears to exhibit oncogenic properties, as it is upregulated in PI3KCA-mutant breast cancer and increases the risk of distant metastasis [18, 19]. However, the current understanding of LIMCH1 in tumors remains limited, and there is a paucity of research into its functional mechanisms. Furthermore, LIMCH1 levels are elevated in cervical cancer with chronic HPV infection, suggesting a potential role as a negative regulator of innate immunity [20], yet its impact on the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) remains to be elucidated. Given the potential roles LIMCH1 has demonstrated in multiple cancers, further investigations are necessary to elucidate its specific functions and mechanisms, particularly in breast tumors.

In our study, we demonstrated that LIMCH1 is overexpressed in breast cancer and is an independent factor linked to poor prognosis in patients. Functionally, LIMCH1 promotes proliferation and metastasis of TNBC, while reducing T-cell infiltration in the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME), partly through the activation of the MAPK pathway and increased PD-L1 expression. These findings deepen our understanding of LIMCH1’s role in TNBC immune evasion and offer new insights for future TNBC immunotherapy research.

Methods and materials

Survival analysis

The TCGA Pan-Cancer data and its clinical and survival information were downloaded from the UCSC Xena database (https://xenabrowser.net/datapages/). Survival analysis was conducted using the TPM from TCGA pan-Cancer data along with corresponding survival data. Analysis was performed using R software (version 4.3.1). Statistical analyses were conducted using the survival and coxphf packages, with LIMCH1 expression dichotomized by the optimal cutoff for the log-rank test and the median cutoff for Cox regression. Visualization was performed using the survminer, forestplot, and ggplot2 packages. The association between LIMCH1 protein levels and prognosis in breast cancer was obtained from the KM-plotter database (https://www.kmplot.com/). The nomogram was constructed and analyzed using the rms package.

Analysis of the correlation between LIMCH1 and immunological features

TCGA pan-cancer immune infiltration-related scores were obtained from the TIMER 2.0 database [21] (http://timer.cistrome.org/), estimated using the CIBERSORT, CIBERSORT-ABS, QUANTISEQ, MCP-counter, Xcell, TIMER, and EPIC algorithms. Immunogenicity indicators for TCGA samples were sourced from prior study [22]. Immune step activity scores were sourced from the TIP database (http://biocc.hrbmu.edu.cn/TIP/index.jsp/). MSI scores were downloaded using cBioPortalData package. Somatic mutation data were downloaded using the TCGAbiolinks package and subsequently utilized to calculate TMB. Analysis was performed using R software (version 4.3.1). Spearman correlation analysis was performed with the stat package. The ggplot2 package and Adobe Illustrator software were used for visualization.

LIMCH1 expression in response to immunotherapy was obtained from the ROC Plotter database (https://rocplot.org/) as well as the KM-plotter database.

Analysis of LIMCH1 expression levels

The breast cancer tissue microarray (array number HBreD090PG01) was obtained from Shanghai Outdo Biotech Company (Shanghai, China). The samples were ethically reviewed and approved by the committee of Shanghai Outdo Biotech Company (approval number SHYJS-CP-1910006). Tissues were fixed in formalin, and tissue microarrays were arrayed with a tissue diameter of 1.5 mm. The array consisted of 67 cases of invasive breast cancer tissues and 20 cases of normal breast tissues, with 16 pairs of cancer and adjacent normal tissues. The anti-LIMCH1 antibody used for IHC was obtained from Abcam (ab96178), while the IHC results for PDL1 were provided by Shanghai Outdo Biotech Company. Staining intensity and percentage of positive cells were scored by pathologists using a semi-quantitative scale, and final scores were calculated as the product of the two parameters. Single-cell RNAseq data (SCAR_Atlas_1144) and their analysis were obtained from the SCAR database (http://8.142.154.29/SCAR2023/).

Cell lines and cultivation

The mouse TNBC cell lines 4T1 and EMT6 were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, USA). Cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium (Gibco, New Zealand) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, ZETA-life, USA) and incubated at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ incubator.

Cell transfection and lentiviral infection

The mouse LIMCH1 gene sequence was cloned into the PLVX-ires-neo vector for overexpression experiments, while shRNA sequences targeting LIMCH1 were cloned into the pSUPER-ires-puro vector for gene knockdown experiments. The target plasmid and packaging plasmids (psPAX2, pMD2.G) were co-transfected into HEK293T cells, and viral supernatants were collected and filtered after 48 h. The virus was used to infect 4T1 and EMT6 cells, and 48 h post-infection, appropriate antibiotics were used to select stable LIMCH1 overexpression or knockdown cell lines. The LIMCH1 shRNA sequences are as follows: Forward: CCTGAACTGTAATGATTGCTA, Reverse: TAGCAATCATTACAGTTCAGG.

RNA sequencing

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA) and quantified with NanoDrop ND-1000. RNA integrity (RIN > 7.0) was assessed using Bioanalyzer 2100 and confirmed via denaturing agarose gel electrophoresis. Poly(A) RNA was purified from 1 µg total RNA using Dynabeads Oligo(dT)25 (Thermo Fisher, USA) in two rounds and fragmented with Magnesium RNA Fragmentation Module (NEB, USA). The fragments were reverse-transcribed into cDNA using SuperScript™ II (Invitrogen, USA), followed by synthesis of U-labeled second-strand DNA. After adapter ligation and size selection with AMPure XP beads, PCR amplification was performed, generating cDNA libraries with an average insert size of 300 ± 50 bp. Libraries were sequenced using 2× 150 bp paired-end reads on Illumina NovaSeq 6000 (LC-Bio, China).

Raw data were processed with fastp for quality control, and reads were aligned to the Homo sapiens GRCh38 reference genome using HISAT2. Transcript assembly and expression quantification were performed with StringTie, and comprehensive transcriptomes were reconstructed using gffcompare. Differentially expressed mRNAs (fold change > 2 or < 0.5, p < 0.05) were identified using edgeR based on FPKM values.

Western blotting

Total protein was extracted using RIPA lysis buffer containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors, and protein concentration was determined by the BCA method. After SDS-PAGE separation, proteins were transferred to PVDF membranes and blocked with 5% skim milk or BSA for 1 h. The membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies (detailed in Supplement Table 1), followed by incubation with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies. ECL detection reagent was used for chemiluminescence.

Real-time polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted from cells using Trizol reagent, and cDNA was reverse transcribed with kit and was used during PCR on an LightCycler480 system. The primers used are as follows: mLIMCH1-F: GAATGAGGTGCCAGAACA; mLIMCH1-R: GGAGAATCCGCTTGAGAA; mACTB-F: CTGTCCCTGTATGCCTCTG; mACTB-R: GCTCGTTGCCAATAGTGAT.

CCK-8 and colony formation experiments

Cells (5 × 102 per well) were seeded in a 96-well plate, and cell proliferation was assessed by measuring absorbance at 450 nm using the CCK-8 reagent at specified time points. Cells (1 × 10³ per well) were seeded in a 6-well plate, cultured for 10–14 days, fixed with methanol, and stained with crystal violet. Colonies containing more than 50 cells were counted.

Transwell migration assay

Transwell chambers with an 8 μm pore size were used. Serum-free cell suspension was added to the upper chamber, while medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum was added to the lower chamber as a chemoattractant. After 24 h of incubation, cells that migrated to the bottom of the chamber were fixed with methanol and stained with crystal violet, and the number of migrated cells was counted under a microscope.

Flow cytometry

Apoptosis was evaluated using Annexin V-FITC/PI dual staining (ZETA-life, USA), following the manufacturer’s protocol. To quantify PD-L1 expression on the cell membrane, cells were incubated with an anti-CD274 antibody for 30 min at 4 °C. For T cell quantification in mouse tumor samples, CD4⁺ T cells were identified as CD3⁺CD4⁺, and CD8⁺ T cells were identified as CD3⁺CD8⁺. The antibodies used, including mouse anti-CD274, mouse anti-CD3, mouse anti-CD4, and mouse anti-CD8, are detailed in Supplementary Table 1. Fc receptor blockers (anti-CD16/CD32) were included in all surface staining mixtures to minimize non-specific binding. Cells were incubated with the designated antibody combinations for 45 min at 4 °C to target specific antigens, followed by two washes. Flow cytometry analysis was performed using a BD Fortessa flow cytometer (BD Biosciences), and data were processed using FlowJo software.

Immunofluorescence (IF)

Cells were seeded on coverslips, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, and permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100. After blocking with 5% BSA, primary antibodies were added and incubated overnight at 4 °C. The following day, fluorescently labeled secondary antibodies were added and incubated at room temperature in the dark for 1 h. After mounting with DAPI, images were captured under a fluorescence microscope. The list of antibodies used for IF is provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Syngeneic tumor mouse model

The experimental protocol on mice was approved by the Ethics Committee of Ethics Committee of the Affiliated People’s Hospital of Nankai University. All procedures involving animals and their care were conducted in conformity with institutional guidelines in compliance with national and international laws and policies. Four-week-old BALB/c wild-type female mice purchased from Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology (Beijing, China). Each mouse was injected with 100 µL of a cell suspension containing 3 × 10⁵ cells into the mammary fat pad. Sixteen mice were randomly divided into two groups: control group and overexpressed LIMCH1 group. After 7 days, the growth of tumors was observed and measured every two days until 17 days. Finally, the subcutaneous tumors were harvested and weighed.

Mouse lung metastasis model

Twelve 6-week-old BALB/c wild-type female mice were randomly divided into two groups. The lung metastasis model was constructed by injecting control or overexpressed LIMCH1 4T1 cells (1 × 10⁵) into the tail vein, and mouse lung tissues were harvested after 12 days and HE staining was performed.

Multiplexed immunohistochemistry

Deparaffinized slides were incubated with anti- CD3 antibody (#ab225820, Abcam), for 30 min and then treated with anti-rabbit/mouse horseradish peroxidase-conjugated (HRP) secondary antibody (abs50015-02, Absinbio, Shanghai) for 10 min. Then labelling was developed for a strictly observed 10 min, using TSA 520 per manufacturer’s direction. Slides were washed in TBST buffer and then transferred to preheated citrate solution (90 °C) before being heat-treated using a microwave set at 20% of maximum power for 15 min. Slides were cooled in the same solution to room temperature. Between all steps, the slides were washed with tris buffer. The same process was repeated for the following antibodies/fluorescent dyes, in order: anti-CD4, anti-CD8. Each slide was then treated with 2 drops of DAPI (abs47047616, Absinbio, Shanghai), washed in distilled water, and manually cover slipped. Slides were air dried, and take pictures with Pannoramic MIDI II (3DHISTECH). Images was analyzed using Indica Halo software. The antibodies information is summarized in the Supplement Table 1.

IHC of mouse tumor tissues

Tissue sections are meticulously processed for immunohistochemical staining following established procedures. The immunological score was performed by a professional pathologist, employing a scoring system that assigns 0 for negative, 1 for weakly positive, 2 for strongly positive, and 3 for very strongly positive based on staining intensity; The percentage of positively stained cells is also scored, with ≤ 25%, 26%−50%, 51%−75%, and > 75% corresponding to 1, 2, 3, and 4 points, respectively. The final score is calculated by multiplying the two scores. The list of antibodies used is provided in Supplementary Table 1.

fStatistical analysis

All data were analyzed with GraphPad Prism Ver. 9.0. The data are presented as the mean ± SD of at least three independent experiments. Two-way ANOVA was used to compare multiple groups. All data were analyzed with Student’s t-test (***p < 0.001, **p < 0.01, *p < 0.05).

Results

LIMCH1 is a potential prognostic marker in various cancers, with higher LIMCH1 levels associated with poorer prognosis in breast cancer

To investigate the role of LIMCH1 in tumors, we analyzed the prognostic impact of LIMCH1 across cancers using the TCGA database. Using the Cox proportional hazards model analysis, we observed that that high LIMCH1 expression was significantly associated with poor prognosis in BRCA, and correlated with better prognosis in KIRC, LGG, and SARC (Fig. 1A, Supplementary File 1). Furthermore, we performed a pan-cancer analysis of the relationship between LIMCH1 at the optimal cutoff and OS, DSS, DFI, and PFI using Log-rank tests, showing that LIMCH1 was associated with worse prognosis in BRCA, KIRP, and UVM, but with better prognosis in BLCA, KIRC, LUAD, and PAAD (Fig. 1B, Supplementary File 1). These results suggest the potential role of LIMCH1 in cancers.

Reports on the role of LIMCH1 in breast cancer are still limited. Therefore, this study subsequently focused on the function of LIMCH1 in breast cancer. Survival curves showed that breast cancer patients with high LIMCH1 expression had lower OS, DSS, and PFI (Fig. 1C-E). Similarly, patients with elevated LIMCH1 protein levels exhibited poorer prognoses (Figure S1A). Notably, LIMCH1 was identified as an independent prognostic factor for predicting overall survival in breast cancer patients (Figure S1B). In addition, we established a clinical prognostic nomogram to predict OS in BRCA patients by balancing clinical characteristics with LIMCH1 expression (Figure S1C). These findings suggest that LIMCH1 is a potential biomarker for survival prognosis in breast cancer patients.

LIMCH1 negatively regulates the TIME

Evidence has suggested that LIMCH1 may be involved in immune processes. To explore its impact on the TIME, we used multiple immune infiltration algorithms to analyze the correlation between LIMCH1 and immune cell infiltration at the TCGA pan-cancer level. The results showed that LIMCH1 was significantly negatively correlated with CD8 + T cell infiltration levels and positively correlated with neutrophils, which promote an immunosuppressive state (Fig. 2A). Using data from previous study [22], we found that LIMCH1 expression was inversely linked to tumor immunogenicity indicators such as SNV neoantigens, TIL fraction, and homologous recombination deficiency (HRD) score, especially in BRCA (Fig. 2B).

Tumor mutational burden (TMB) and microsatellite instability (MSI) are important indicators reflecting the TIME. We found that LIMCH1 was negatively correlated with both MSI and TMB in BRCA (Figure S2A-B), further suggesting its potential role in regulating the immune microenvironment in breast cancer. Furthermore, Analysis of the relationship between LIMCH1 and the BRCA immune cycle revealed significant associations between LIMCH1 and key immune molecules across various steps of the immune cycle, notably during immune cell activation and tumor cell recognition stages (Fig. 2C). Additionally, LIMCH1 expression was found to have a positive correlation with multiple inhibitory immune checkpoint molecules, including PD-1, PD-L1, CTLA-4, and TIGIT (Fig. 2D). These observations suggest that LIMCH1 plays a regulatory role in immune evasion within breast tumors.

LIMCH1 is overexpressed in breast cancer and can enhance the proliferation and migration of TNBC cells

Using human breast cancer tissue microarrays, we employed IHC to reveal that LIMCH1 expression was significantly higher in breast cancer tumor tissues compared to adjacent non-cancerous tissues (Fig. 3A). Western blot analysis corroborated these results, demonstrating significantly elevated LIMCH1 levels in breast cancer tumor tissues (T) relative to adjacent normal tissues (N) (Fig. 3B). LIMCH1 was also more highly expressed in breast cancer cell lines compared to human normal mammary epithelial cells MCF-10 A (Fig. 3C). Moreover, Single-cell transcriptome analysis identified that LIMCH1 was predominantly expressed in cancer cells, fibroblasts, and endothelial cells (Figure S3).

Furthermore, we established stable LIMCH1 overexpression and knockdown cell lines in mouse TNBC cells 4T1 and EMT6 (Fig. 3D). CCK8 and colony formation assays showed that LIMCH1 enhances the proliferation of TNBC cells (Fig. 3E-F). Moreover, LIMCH1 overexpression markedly increased cell migration, with significantly more migrating cells observed compared to controls (Fig. 3G). Scratch assays further confirmed this result, as the cell migration speed in the LIMCH1 overexpression group was significantly faster than in the control group (Fig. 3H).

To further verify the role of LIMCH1 in breast cancer cells, we established stable cell lines with LIMCH1 knockdown in these cells (Fig. 4A-B). As expected, LIMCH1 knockdown significantly reduced the proliferation and colony formation abilities of TNBC cells (Fig. 4C-E). Furthermore, cells with LIMCH1 knockdown demonstrated reduced migration capacity (Fig. 4F-G). These findings are consistent with the previous analysis, indicating that LIMCH1 promotes malignant behaviors in breast cancer.

LIMCH1 activates the MAPK pathway and increases PDL1 expression in TNBC

To elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying LIMCH1 regulation, RNAseq was performed on 4T1 cells with stable LIMCH1 overexpression and their empty vector controls. A total of 750 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified, including 347 upregulated and 403 downregulated genes (Fig. 5A). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis showed that the DEGs were mainly enriched in immune and inflammation-related pathways, such as the NOD-like receptor signaling pathway, Toll-like receptor signaling pathway, cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction and antigen processing and presentation (Fig. 5B).

LIMCH1 activates MAPK pathways. A Volcano plot of the RNA-seq analysis of 4T1 cells with overexpressing LIMCH1 (|log2FC|≥1.0 p.adj < 0.05). B Representative KEGG pathway analysis of up-regulated genes after transfection with overexpressing LIMCH1 (p < 0.05). C-D Western blot detected the protein expression and phosphorylation of pivotal molecules (PI3K, AKT, p65, MEK1/2, E1/2, p38, JNK) in toll-like receptor signaling pathway

The TLR signaling pathway is crucial in regulating invasion, metastasis, and the immune microenvironment of breast cancer cells, primarily involving downstream pathways such as NF-κB, MAPK (ERK, JNK, p38) and PI3K/AKT [23]. Western blot results showed that overexpression or knockout of LIMCH1 did not consistently affect the activation of p-PI3K, p-Akt, and p-p65 (Fig. 5C), suggesting that PI3K/AKT and NF-κB may not be the main targets regulated by LIMCH1. In contrast, LIMCH1 significantly affected key molecules in the MAPK pathway: LIMCH1 overexpression markedly increased the phosphorylation levels of ERK1/2 and p38 in the MAPK pathway, including the activation of p-Mek1/2, p-Erk1/2, and p-p38, while LIMCH1 knockdown significantly inhibited the activation of these molecules (Fig. 5D). These findings indicate that LIMCH1 promotes the malignant biological behavior of TNBC cells by regulating the MAPK pathway.

As previously described, LIMCH1 can mediate antitumor immune tolerance in breast cancer, and shows a negative correlation with the key immune checkpoint protein PD-L1 in the TCGA-BRCA database (Fig. 6A). Additionally, the MAPK pathway is a primary pathway involved in regulating PDL1 expression in tumor cells [24]. Thus, we explored whether LIMCH1 can regulate PD-L1 expression. Western blot results demonstrated that LIMCH1 overexpression significantly increased PD-L1 expression in 4T1 and EMT6 cells, whereas LIMCH1 knockdown markedly reduced PD-L1 expression (Fig. 6B). Flow cytometry results further validated these findings, showing that LIMCH1 overexpression significantly increased the proportion of PD-L1 positive cells, whereas LIMCH1 knockdown had the opposite effect (Fig. 6C). Immunofluorescence consistently demonstrated that PD-L1 expression was significantly elevated in the LIMCH1 overexpression group (Fig. 6D). Previous studies have shown that the AP-1 complex, a downstream effector of the MAPK/ERK pathway, can transcriptionally activate PD-L1 in various cancers, including TNBC [25, 26]. Our findings showed that LIMCH1 overexpression significantly upregulated the expression of AP-1 components c-Jun and ATF3, while LIMCH1 knockdown exerted the opposite effects (Fig. 6E). RT-PCR further showed that LIMCH1 upregulated PD-L1 mRNA levels (Figure S4A). These were further validated in the patient tissue microarrays, where we found that LIMCH1 IHC scores were significantly higher in the PD-L1 high-expression group than in the PD-L1 low-expression group (Fig. 6F-G).

Moreover, analysis using the ROC-plotter database revealed that LIMCH1 expression was significantly elevated in non-responders to anti-PD-1 therapy (Figure S4B-C). The KM-plotter database findings were consistent: in patients treated with anti-PD-1 therapy, the high LIMCH1 expression group had a significantly lower survival rate compared to the low expression group, indicating that high LIMCH1 expression may correlate with poor outcomes in anti-PD-1 therapy (Figure S4D-F). These experiments indicate that LIMCH1 activates the MAPK pathway and upregulates PD-L1 expression, which may be one of the mechanisms through which it plays a key role in immune evasion in breast cancer.

LIMCH1 promotes proliferation and metastasis of TNBC tumors and suppresses T-cell infiltration in the TIME in vivo

To assess the effects of LIMCH1 on breast cancer invasion and the TME, we initially implanted TNBC cell 4T1 with LIMCH1 overexpression and their control cells into the mammary fat pads of BALB/c wild-type mice. The findings indicated that LIMCH1 significantly enhanced tumor growth in vivo. In comparison to the control group, both the tumor volume and weight were notably higher in the LIMCH1 overexpression group (Fig. 7A-C).

To further evaluate the impact of LIMCH1 on metastatic potential, LIMCH1 overexpressing cells and control cells were injected into mice through the tail vein. The results showed that LIMCH1 overexpression significantly enhanced the metastatic ability of TNBC tumors. The number of lung metastatic foci and the relative tumor area of metastases in the LIMCH1 overexpression group were significantly higher than those in the control group (Fig. 7D-G).

In the previous section, we found that LIMCH1 promotes PD-L1 expression and suppresses the immune microenvironment in breast cancer. Therefore, we performed multiplex immunofluorescence (mIHC) detection on tumor tissue sections to determine the effect of LIMCH1 on the TIME. The results revealed that LIMCH1 overexpression significantly reduced the infiltration of total T cells, as well as CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T cell subsets within the TME (Fig. 8A-B). Flow cytometry analysis of the transplanted tumor tissue also showed a consistent result (Fig. 8C-D, Figure S5A-B). IHC confirmed elevated LIMCH1, PD-L1, and p-Erk1/2 levels in the LIMCH1 overexpression group (Fig. 8E). In addition, we conducted in vivo experiments using LIMCH1-knockdown 4T1 cells. The results showed that LIMCH1 silencing significantly inhibited tumor growth (Figure S5C-E) and enhanced the infiltration of CD3⁺, CD3⁺CD4⁺, and CD3⁺CD8⁺ cells within the TME (Figure S5F-G). These experiments further confirmed that LIMCH1 regulates PD-L1, altering the TIME and promoting immune evasion in TNBC.

Discussion

The LIM domain is a zinc-finger motif that facilitates protein-protein interactions and serves as a crucial domain for many proteins’ biological functions, with LIM-domain-containing proteins frequently playing important roles in diverse physiological processes [27, 28]. As a member of the LIM-domain protein family, LIMCH1 can bind and regulate the actin cytoskeleton in cells [13]. Recent studies have preliminarily indicated that LIMCH1 has potential functions in tumor development and progression. For instance, in lung cancer, LIMCH1 is downregulated in tumor tissues and, by interacting with the LRIG protein, increases patients’ prognostic risk [29]. In renal cancer, two studies utilized public databases to develop renal cancer risk scores, identifying LIMCH1 as a core gene in these systems, with high expression linked to improved prognosis. However, research on LIMCH1 remains limited [17, 30]. Table 1 summarizes the current research on LIMCH1 in the field of oncology.

Overview of LIMCH1-related studies in oncology

Breast cancer diagnosis and treatment have been a research focus in recent years. With the progress in molecular oncology and therapeutics, the survival rate for breast cancer has been comprehensively improved. Nevertheless, delayed diagnosis, therapeutic resistance, and metastasis still result in poor prognoses for some patients [37]. Considerable progress has been made in identifying cancer prognostic biomarkers, although many findings remain to be clinically validated [38–41]. Therefore, there remains an urgent need to identify new molecular targets to improve treatment efficacy and support personalized prediction of response and prognosis in breast cancer.

Previous studies have shown that LIMCH1 exhibits oncogenic properties in breast cancer: LIMCH1 is upregulated in PI3KCA-mutant breast cancer [18] and is linked to a higher risk of distant metastasis [19]. In our study, we found that LIMCH1 is highly expressed in TNBC and significantly promotes tumor cell proliferation and metastasis. More importantly, we found that LIMCH1 is an independent factor affecting OS in breast cancer, suggesting that LIMCH1 plays an important role in breast cancer development and progression and can serve as a potential prognostic factor for breast cancer.

In breast cancer, triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) is the subtype with the poorest prognosis. Due to the lack of estrogen receptors, progesterone receptors, and HER2 receptors, TNBC has limited responsiveness to endocrine therapy and anti-HER2 targeted therapies, leaving few treatment options for advanced-stage patients [42]. Notably, immunotherapy, particularly immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) targeting PD-1/PD-L1, has emerged as a promising treatment direction for TNBC and has shown some efficacy [8]. Programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) is commonly expressed in tumor cells, where it binds to PD-1 receptors on activated T cells, suppressing anti-tumor immune responses and counteracting T-cell activation signals [43]. However, only a subset of PD-L1-positive patients show a good response to immunotherapy, suggesting that PD-L1 may be insufficient as a reliable predictive marker [44, 45].

The Mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathway is one of the most well-defined pathways in cancer biology, with its hyperactivation being pivotal for tumor cell proliferation and metastasis [46]. Our study showed that LIMCH1 can significantly upregulates key molecules of the MAPK signaling pathway, suggesting that LIMCH1 may enhance tumor cell proliferation and migration by promoting sustained activation of the MAPK pathway. PD-L1 expression in tumors is regulated by the MAPK signaling pathway. Previous studies have shown that MAPK-ERK signaling regulates PD-L1 expression in TNBC via STAT1 phosphorylation induced by IFN-γ [47]. Another MAPK signaling cascade, p38 MAPK, also positively regulates PD-L1 expression [43]. In this study, we discovered that LIMCH1 upregulates PD-L1 by activating the MAPK pathway, which contributing to the existing understanding of PD-L1 regulation. mIHC showed that high LIMCH1 expression significantly reduced CD4 + and CD8 + T-cell infiltration in tumor tissues, further confirming the inhibitory effect of LIMCH1 on the tumor immune microenvironment. Furthermore, we observed that high LIMCH1 expression was associated with poorer prognosis after PD-1 inhibitor treatment, validating the effect of LIMCH1 on the PD-1/PD-L1 axis and suggesting that LIMCH1 could be a potential predictive marker for the efficacy of immunotherapy in patients.

Another notable finding is that our data indicate LIMCH1 might suppress anti-tumor immune activity by modulating other immunosuppressive factors like TGF-β and IL-10, suggesting that LIMCH1 could be involved in regulating multiple aspects of the tumor immune microenvironment. Further experimental validation is needed, but these findings imply that LIMCH1 plays a significant role in the immune evasion mechanisms of TNBC.

While this study provides novel insights into the role of LIMCH1 in promoting immune evasion and tumor progression in TNBC, several limitations should be noted. Firstly, our experiments were mainly based on in vitro models and syngeneic mouse tumors, which may not fully reflect the complexity of human tumors [48]. Future studies using human cell lines and patient-derived xenograft models are needed to confirm the translational relevance. Secondly, while LIMCH1 was shown to activate the MAPK pathway and upregulate PD-L1, its direct interactions with MAPK components remain unclear. Lastly, whether LIMCH1 can serve as a viable therapeutic target to enhance PD-1/PD-L1 blockade efficacy remains unexplored in this study, and the potential of LIMCH1 inhibition to reverse immune suppression represents an important avenue for future research. In summary, this study revealed that LIMCH1 is overexpressed in breast cancer and serves as an independent factor associated with poor prognosis in breast cancer patients. Functionally, LIMCH1 enhances TNBC proliferation and metastasis, while suppressing T-cell infiltration in the TIME, with one mechanism involving activation of the MAPK pathway and upregulation of PD-L1 expression (Fig. 9). Our findings suggest that LIMCH1 might be a potential therapeutic target, providing new directions for targeted and immunotherapy for TNBC. Future research should be performed to explore LIMCH1’s role in immunotherapy and its potential as a therapeutic target to deliver more effective personalized treatment strategies for TNBC patients.
