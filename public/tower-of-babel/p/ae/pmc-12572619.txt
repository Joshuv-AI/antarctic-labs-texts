Management of Primary Adrenal Insufficiency and Overt Hypothyroidism in an HIV‐Positive Patient in a Resource‐Limited Setting: A Case Report
Authors: Majani Edward, Witness John, Dominick M Raphael
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIn resource‐limited settings, clinicians must maintain a high index of suspicion for primary adrenal insufficiency and hypothyroidism in HIV‐positive patients presenting with non‐specific symptoms. Relying on clinical judgment and response to treatment is crucial when confirmatory tests are unavailable. Early recognition and intervention can prevent life‐threatening complications.Keywords: adrenal insufficiency, HIV, hypothyroidism, resource limited setting

Introduction

Adrenal insufficiency (AI) is a clinical syndrome characterized by the inadequate production of adrenal hormones, most notably cortisol and aldosterone, which are essential for regulating the stress response, electrolyte balance, and blood pressure [1]. AI can be classified into three types: primary, secondary, and tertiary [2]. Primary adrenal insufficiency, also known as Addison's disease, results from direct damage to the adrenal glands, leading to insufficient hormone production [3]. This condition is often attributed to autoimmune disorders, infections, or other destructive processes, such as tuberculosis. Secondary and tertiary AI occur when there is a disruption in the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis, typically due to pituitary or hypothalamic dysfunction resulting from either the presence of a pituitary adenoma or pituitary apoplexy with hormone loss.

The clinical manifestation of AI is diverse and can range from fatigue, weight loss, and hypotension to more severe symptoms like electrolyte imbalances, hypoglycemia, and even adrenal crisis, a life‐threatening condition [4]. Because these symptoms overlap with those of other common diseases, diagnosing AI can be challenging, particularly in patients with complex or chronic health conditions. In low‐resource settings, where chronic diseases such as HIV and tuberculosis are prevalent, AI can be underdiagnosed or misdiagnosed due to the shared clinical features [5]. The presence of these chronic diseases further complicates the diagnostic process, as they may exacerbate or mimic the symptoms of AI, leading to delayed or inappropriate treatment [6].

In such settings, early recognition of AI is critical, as prompt and accurate diagnosis followed by appropriate treatment can prevent complications, including adrenal crisis. Treatment typically involves hormone replacement therapy, which requires careful tailoring to the individual's needs [7]. The management of AI in patients with coexisting chronic diseases requires a multidisciplinary approach to address the complexities of their care and ensure optimal health outcomes [8]. This case report aims to raise awareness among healthcare providers about the challenges in diagnosing and managing AI, particularly in patients with chronic diseases common in resource‐limited settings.

Case Presentation

A 78‐year‐old woman presented with an 8‐month history of progressive skin hyperpigmentation, joint pain, fatigue, weight loss, and decreased appetite. She had been managed for HIV for 8 years and was treated with Abacavir, Lamivudine, and Dolutegravir (ABC + 3TC + DTG). She also had heart failure with reduced left ventricular ejection fraction (HFrEF) and was on guideline‐directed medical therapy (GDMT) with Sacubitril/valsartan 25 mg twice daily, Bisoprolol 2.5 mg once daily, Spironolactone 25 mg once daily, and Dapagliflozin 10 mg once daily.

Based on her clinical presentation of hyperpigmentation, hypotension, low serum sodium, and high potassium, a diagnosis of primary adrenal insufficiency secondary to HIV was made. The diagnosis could not be confirmed with a cosyntropin stimulation test as it was unavailable in our setting. In addition, she was diagnosed with primary overt hypothyroidism, as evidenced by a markedly elevated TSH level at 85 μIU/mL and a very low Free T4 level. Due to resource limitations, specific tests such as thyroid antibodies (e.g., anti‐TPO, anti‐thyroglobulin) or imaging studies of the adrenal and thyroid glands were not performed.

Methods (Differential Diagnosis, Investigations, and Treatment)

Differential Diagnosis: The differential diagnoses for this patient's symptoms included chronic heart failure decompensation, a variety of endocrine disorders (such as primary hypothyroidism and primary adrenal insufficiency), and chronic infections like tuberculosis. The combination of hyperpigmentation, hypotension, and electrolyte imbalances strongly pointed towards primary adrenal insufficiency.

Investigations: As per Table 1, key investigations included serum electrolytes (sodium, potassium), creatinine, TSH, and Free T4. These were chosen based on the patient's symptoms and were the only tests available.

Treatment: Initial management included intravenous hydrocortisone 200 mg stat, followed by 100 mg every 4 h for 24 h, along with liberal salt intake. After 48 h, the patient's blood pressure improved, serum sodium levels slightly increased, and potassium levels decreased to the high‐normal range. Initially, she was prescribed fludrocortisone 0.1 mg once daily and prednisolone 5 mg at bedtime. However, due to financial constraints, she was unable to afford fludrocortisone. We transitioned her to hydrocortisone 20 mg daily, administered in divided doses (10 mg upon awakening, 5 mg in the early afternoon, and 5 mg in the late afternoon), along with encouragement of liberal salt intake. In addition, Sacubitril/valsartan and spironolactone were discontinued. For the management of primary overt hypothyroidism, she was started on levothyroxine replacement therapy at a dose of 1.6 μg/kg/day in the morning, 30 min before a meal.

Outcome and Follow‐Up

At follow‐up visits at 4 and 8 weeks, the patient demonstrated significant clinical improvement, with normalization of blood pressure and serum electrolyte levels. However, residual hyperpigmentation persisted in some areas of her skin. The patient provided written consent for the publication of this case report, but she did not consent to the use of photographs documenting the hyperpigmentation.

Discussion

Adrenal insufficiency (AI) is a potentially life‐threatening condition that can present with a wide range of symptoms, often mimicking other illnesses, particularly in patients with complex and chronic health conditions [9]. This case presented highlights the challenges of diagnosing and managing AI in patients with multisystem involvement, particularly in resource‐limited settings where diseases like HIV, tuberculosis, and chronic kidney disease (CKD) are prevalent [5]. Early recognition, appropriate testing, and tailored management are crucial in preventing complications such as adrenal crisis, which can lead to severe morbidity and mortality if not promptly treated.

A 78‐year‐old woman with a history of HIV, TB, hypertension, and heart failure presented with progressive skin hyperpigmentation, joint pain, fatigue, weight loss, and decreased appetite. These symptoms are characteristic of adrenal insufficiency, particularly primary AI, as seen in Addison's disease [3]. Skin hyperpigmentation, due to increased levels of adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH), is a hallmark feature of primary adrenal insufficiency, particularly when the ACTH secretion becomes elevated in response to low cortisol levels [10]. The patient's hypotension, generalized edema, and fatigue further support the diagnosis of AI, compounded by the potential thyroid dysfunction indicated by the markedly elevated TSH levels [2].

The presence of HIV and multiple comorbidities in this patient complicates the clinical picture. HIV itself, particularly if poorly controlled, is associated with a higher incidence of opportunistic infections and autoimmune diseases that may affect the adrenal glands. Moreover, patients with advanced HIV may be predisposed to endocrine dysfunction, including adrenal insufficiency, due to both direct viral effects and the side effects of antiretroviral therapy (ART) [11]. The patient's ART regimen (ABC + 3TC + DTG) was not specifically linked to AI in the literature, but ART regimens in general can sometimes cause drug‐induced adrenal dysfunction, either through direct toxicity or by modifying the immune system, which may contribute to the autoimmune process [12].

In this patient, her management with intravenous hydrocortisone, normal saline, liberal salt intake, and levothyroxine followed by oral replacement therapies for both adrenal and thyroid hormones was effective [7]. This case underscores the importance of recognizing adrenal and thyroid dysfunction in patients with multi‐system involvement, particularly those with chronic conditions such as HIV and heart failure, who may present with atypical or overlapping symptoms [11]. The improvement observed in this case highlights the need for a high level of suspicion in elderly patients with chronic diseases, where AI might be mistaken for other conditions like generalized fatigue or cardiac decompensation.

Primary hypothyroidism, characterized by elevated TSH and low free T4, results from impaired thyroid hormone production, commonly due to autoimmune thyroiditis (Hashimoto's thyroiditis), iodine deficiency, or thyroidectomy [13]. Symptoms include fatigue, weight gain, cold intolerance, bradycardia, and myxedema. The condition is commonly encountered in older individuals and those with chronic illnesses, making early diagnosis and management crucial for preventing complications such as myxedema coma. The co‐occurrence of primary adrenal insufficiency and primary hypothyroidism is well documented in conditions such as polyglandular autoimmune syndromes (PAS) [10]. While this patient was not diagnosed with PAS Type 1 or Type 2, the presence of both endocrine disorders raises concerns about potential autoimmune mechanisms. Adrenal insufficiency should be addressed first before initiating thyroid hormone replacement to avoid precipitating adrenal crisis [5]. Thyroid hormone replacement increases metabolic demand, which can worsen cortisol deficiency if not corrected beforehand. In this case, the patient was appropriately treated with hydrocortisone replacement before the initiation of levothyroxine. Previous case reports have highlighted the co‐occurrence of adrenal insufficiency and hypothyroidism in HIV‐positive patients. For instance, a case reported by Nassoro et al. described a patient with advanced HIV who developed both conditions, emphasizing the diagnostic challenge [11]. Similarly, Mathavan et al. discussed various endocrine abnormalities in HIV, including AI and hypothyroidism [14]. Our case aligns with these findings by demonstrating the diagnostic complexity and the importance of clinical suspicion. Regarding treatment, while fludrocortisone is the standard for mineralocorticoid replacement in primary AI, several studies and case reports described the use of higher doses of hydrocortisone in divided doses to provide both glucocorticoid and sufficient mineralocorticoid activity, especially in settings where fludrocortisone is unavailable or unaffordable [10, 11, 14].

The interplay between HIV, primary adrenal insufficiency, and primary hypothyroidism underscores the need for careful endocrine evaluation in HIV‐positive patients presenting with nonspecific symptoms such as fatigue, weight loss, or hypotension [11]. Given the high burden of HIV‐related endocrine disorders, routine screening for adrenal and thyroid dysfunction should be considered in symptomatic patients.

This approach, while not ideal for long‐term management due to potential glucocorticoid excess, can be a pragmatic solution in resource‐limited settings. Our patient's clinical improvement with hydrocortisone alone supports the feasibility of this alternative when fludrocortisone is inaccessible. The daily hydrocortisone dose of 20 mg, divided into 10 mg in the morning, 5 mg in the early afternoon, and 5 mg in the evening, provided adequate adrenal replacement. The levothyroxine dosage of 1.6 μg/kg/day for primary hypothyroidism is a standard starting dose and was adjusted based on clinical response and TSH levels. One of the critical lessons from this case is the importance of a thorough clinical evaluation and a high index of suspicion for adrenal and thyroid dysfunction in patients with chronic diseases. Healthcare providers must be vigilant in recognizing signs and symptoms that might indicate adrenal insufficiency, including unexplained fatigue, weight loss, hypotension, electrolyte imbalances, and skin hyperpigmentation. Early detection and treatment of AI and hypothyroidism can prevent life‐threatening complications. The approach to treatment should prioritize stabilizing adrenal function before initiating thyroid hormone replacement. This case also emphasizes the need for improved access to essential diagnostic and therapeutic resources in low‐resource settings to ensure better patient outcomes.

Conclusion

This case highlights the diagnostic and management challenges of coexisting primary adrenal insufficiency and hypothyroidism in an HIV‐positive patient. It underscores the importance of a multidisciplinary approach in managing complex endocrine disorders, particularly in patients with chronic illnesses. Improved access to essential endocrine diagnostics and treatments in resource‐limited settings remains crucial for better patient care. While routine screening for primary AI in all HIV patients is not universally recommended, a high index of clinical suspicion and targeted screening using tests like the cosyntropin test should be considered in symptomatic patients, as adrenal insufficiency can present as an emergency condition in such individuals and if untreated can lead to poor outcomes.
