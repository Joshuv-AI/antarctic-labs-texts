Learning Curve of Robot-Assisted Total Hip Arthroplasty: Cumulative Sum Analysis
Authors: Hyun-Min Lee, Seong-Jun Kim, Yong-Chan Ha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRobot-assisted total hip arthroplasty (RA-THA) has been developed to enhance implant placement accuracy and minimize surgical variability. Although potential benefits have been demonstrated, adoption is accompanied by a learning curve. This study aimed to evaluate the learning curve associated with RA-THA using a single robotic platform.MethodsIn this prospective design and retrospective review study, 40 consecutive RA-THA procedures were performed between March and April 2025, marking the initiation of a robotic hip program. All procedures utilized the MAKO robotic system (Stryker). Step-specific operative times, including pelvic array fixation, dissection and bone preparation, landmark registration, and acetabular reaming and impaction, were prospectively recorded by an independent observer. The learning curve was analyzed using cumulative sum (CUSUM) analysis.ResultsCUSUM analysis identified 2 distinct phases: a learning phase (first 11 cases) followed by a proficiency phase. Significant reductions in operative times were observed for robotic-specific steps, particularly pelvic array fixation and landmark registration, during the proficiency phase (p < 0.05). There was no significant difference between the 2 phases in terms of acetabular cup positioning. The mean inclination angle was 38.9° ± 1.5° (range, 37–41), with all cases within the Lewinnek safe zone. Anteversion remained within 3° of the planned target, and combined anteversion was maintained within the safe range of 25°–50°.ConclusionsThe learning curve for RA-THA using the MAKO system reached a plateau after 11 cases, with improved efficiency in robotic-specific steps. Accurate and reproducible cup positioning was maintained throughout the series, suggesting that the technical benefits of robotic assistance are realized early in the learning process.Keywords: Learning curve, Robotic-assisted surgery, Total hip arthroplasty

Conventional total hip arthroplasty (THA) has demonstrated excellent and durable long-term outcomes. However, implant design, surgical technique, and patient characteristics can affect both short- and long-term results. During THA, accurate positioning of the acetabular cup and femoral stem is critical for success. Malpositioning of the acetabular cup and inappropriate femoral stem insertion are major causes of hip dislocation, leg-length discrepancy, and femoral stem subsidence, all of which may lead to revision surgery. Robot-assisted total hip arthroplasty (RA-THA) has been increasingly adopted in clinical practice.1,2,3,4,5) Prior studies demonstrate that RA-THA improves the accuracy of implant placement, enhances leg-length equalization, and restores hip offset, and may reduce hospital length of stay and cost.6,7,8)

The semi-active MAKO robotic system (Stryker) utilizes computed tomography (CT) and 3-dimensional reconstruction to assist surgeons in achieving precise component alignment and biomechanics. Since its introduction in the United States in 2014, MAKO RA-THA has gained global adoption and was first introduced in South Korea in 2018. Despite growing clinical use domestically, no study has yet examined the learning curve of RA-THA using the MAKO platform in South Korea.

The purpose of this study was to assess the learning curve of a single surgeon performing RA-THA. The secondary aim was to evaluate accuracy of cup positioning, restoration of biomechanics, correction of leg-length discrepancy, and perioperative complications. We hypothesized that cumulative experience would decrease operative time, whereas accuracy of component positioning would remain consistent from the outset.

METHODS

The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Seoul Bumin Hospital (IRB No. 2025-09-005), and the requirement for obtaining informed consent from the participants was waived because of the retrospective nature of the study.

This prospective design and retrospective review study was conducted at a single center and included 40 consecutive RA-THA procedures performed between March and April 2025. All patients were diagnosed with osteonecrosis of the femoral head (ONFH) (ARCO stage III or IV) or hip osteoarthritis.9) Patients were excluded if they had a femoral neck fracture, were undergoing bilateral THA, or had a history of prior surgery to the affected hip.

Preoperative Preparation

All patients underwent preoperative CT scans of the pelvis and proximal femur to create patient-specific 3-dimensional models. These models were utilized with the MAKO software (Stryker) to guide implant positioning. The acetabular cup inclination was consistently set at 40°. Acetabular cup anteversion was determined intraoperatively after measurement of femoral anteversion.

Surgical Technique

All operative procedures were performed by a single surgeon (HML) with hip arthroplasty fellowship training, who had no prior experience with robot-assisted systems for THA, under the direct supervision of the senior author (YCH) with 30 years of THA experience and 4 years of robotic THA experience. The posterolateral approach in the lateral decubitus position was used for all patients. All procedures utilized the MAKO robotic hip system (Stryker) to guide acetabular bone reaming and acetabular cup positioning according to the preoperative surgical plan.

The initial surgical step involved marking a small dot (approximately the size of the probe tip) with a surgical marker at the lateral pole of the patella. This mark served as a fixed reference point for real-time display of leg-length changes. Subsequently, 3 pins were fixated at the most prominent part of the anterior superior iliac spine to install the pelvic array. After skin incision and soft-tissue dissection of the fascia lata and gluteal musculature, a proximal checkpoint was inserted at the lateral portion of the greater trochanter. Both the proximal checkpoint and the distal reference point were registered with the probe before further dissection. The “femur-first” approach was then commenced with femoral canal broaching. After broaching, femoral anteversion was measured using a goniometer to determine the angle between the head-neck axis of the final broach and the lower leg. The target acetabular cup anteversion was then decided, aiming for a usual combined anteversion target of 38°, and the MAKO Product Specialist was requested to update the surgical plan accordingly.10,11) Once femoral preparation was complete, the acetabulum was exposed, and the labrum and ligamentum teres were trimmed prior to landmark registration. Landmark registration involved 32 points both inside and outside the acetabulum, complemented by 8 verification points. Following successful registration, acetabular reaming was performed under the guidance of the robotic-assisted arm at the planned target position. When the surgeon placed the cup into the prepared acetabulum, the robotic-assisted arm was locked at the target angles during impaction until the surgeon was satisfied with the new center of rotation. Final cup position was confirmed by selecting 5 different points in the plane of the cup with the probe. Subsequently, the surgeon performed a trial reduction, and portable radiographs were obtained to double-check cup position and leg- length discrepancy. After acetabular cup position, limb length, and offset were confirmed via radiography, the femoral stem was implanted manually, and hip stability was tested through the full range of motion.

Outcome Measures

An independent observer recorded step-specific operative times. “Pelvic array fixation” was timed from making the stab incisions for the pins until the completion of pelvic array installation. “Dissection and bone preparation” encompassed skin incision for the main procedure, softtissue dissection, proximal checkpoint insertion, femoral neck cutting, broaching, and acetabular exposure. “Landmark registration” time primarily accounted for checking registration points inside and outside the acetabulum. “Acetabular reaming and impaction” were timed from the point the robot-assisted arm entered the operative field until confirmation of the cup position with the probe. “Total operative time” was recorded from the start of pelvic array fixation until operative site closure. The final cup position was additionally verified using intraoperative probe measurements after acetabular cup fixation. Follow-up visits were scheduled at 4 weeks, 2 months, 3 months, and 6 months. Patients who did not regularly return for their scheduled visits were contacted by telephone interview.

Statistical Analysis

Cumulative sum (CUSUM) analysis was utilized to assess the learning curve for RA-THA. CUSUM provides a visual representation of learning trends by accumulating deviations between the observed value and a target value (defined as the mean duration of operation in this study).12,13) The results were presented in a chart with the sequence of operation on the x-axis and the relative CUSUM value on the y-axis. The CUSUM value increased when surgical time exceeded the average and decreased when operative time was shorter than the average. An inflection point, where the slope of the curve changed from positive to negative, indicated the end of the learning curve, signifying a transition from a learning phase to a proficiency phase.14) The learning phase refers to the early period during which operative times exceed the mean, reflecting the surgeon’s progressive familiarization with the procedure. The proficiency phase refers to the subsequent period in which operative times fall at or below the mean, indicating more consistent and efficient technical performance. Continuous variables are reported as means and standard deviations and were compared between groups using independent t-tests. Categorical data were compared using the chi-square test. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS software version 19.0 (IBM Corp.).

RESULTS

Demographics

A total of 40 patients who underwent primary unilateral THA were enrolled. Twenty patients were diagnosed with osteoarthritis and 20 with ONFH. CUSUM analysis identified an inflection point at the 11th case (Fig. 1). Consequently, the first 11 cases were categorized as the learning phase, and the subsequent 29 cases were categorized as the proficiency phase. Operative Time

Step-specific surgical times and total operative time were compared between the learning and proficiency phases (Table 2). Total operative time averaged 81.8 ± 9.0 minutes in the learning phase and 73.3 ± 5.8 minutes in the proficiency phase, representing a significant reduction of 8.5 minutes (p = 0.01). The most substantial improvements were observed in robotic-specific steps. Pelvic array fixation time decreased from 4.2 ± 1.3 minutes to 2.5 ± 0.9 minutes (p < 0.001), and landmark registration time decreased from 5.1 ± 1.2 minutes to 2.7 ± 1.6 minutes (p < 0.001). The time for acetabular reaming and impaction did not differ significantly between the 2 groups (6.9 ± 2.5 minutes vs. 7.8 ± 2.6 minutes, p = 0.33).

Component Positioning

No statistically significant differences in component positioning were observed between the learning and proficiency phases (Table 3). The mean acetabular inclination angle was 39.1° ± 1.2° in the learning phase and 38.8° ± 1.6° in the proficiency phase (p = 0.63), with all cups placed within the Lewinnek safe zone (30°–50°).15) Mean combined anteversion was 38.9° ± 4.7° during the learning phase and 38.4° ± 3.7° during the proficiency phase (p = 0.35), and all values fell within the safe range of 25°–50° suggested by Dorr.16) The final cup position deviated from the preoperative plan by no more than 3° in all cases (Fig. 2). The mean discrepancy between planned and final inclination was 1.3° ± 0.8° in the learning phase and 1.1° ± 1.1° in the proficiency phase (p = 0.37). For anteversion, the mean discrepancy was 1.2° ± 1.0° and 1.0° ± 1.0°, respectively (p = 0.50).

Complications

No intraoperative or postoperative complications were observed, including loosening of the pelvic array, loss of pelvic checkpoints, or inadvertent pelvic checkpoint reaming. All patients initiated ambulation on postoperative day 1. There were no cases of dislocation, periprosthetic fracture, surgical site infection, or wound complications at the primary incision site or pelvic array pin sites during the follow-up period.

DISCUSSION

This study evaluated the learning curve associated with RA-THA in a single-surgeon cohort. Our findings demonstrate that an initial period of 11 cases was required to optimize operative times and refine robotic-specific surgical steps. This finding is consistent with previous studies that reported similar learning curve durations between 12 and 14 cases.17,18,19,20) This learning phase primarily involved increased efficiency in pelvic array fixation and landmark registration, suggesting an adaptation to the system’s setup and anatomical referencing procedures. However, the time dedicated to acetabular reaming and impaction remained consistent from the outset, indicating that the surgeon’s proficiency in these critical steps, even with robotic guidance, was sustained throughout the learning period.

Acceptable acetabular component positioning appeared to be maintained consistently across both phases of the learning curve. Specifically, no statistically significant differences in mean acetabular inclination or anteversion were observed between planned and final cup positions when comparing the learning phase to the proficiency phase. These findings suggest that the robotic system’s guidance may help maintain consistent component positioning from the outset, potentially mitigating the positioning-related inaccuracies typically associated with the early surgical learning curve. If confirmed in larger studies, this early accuracy may have meaningful implications for patient outcomes, including a reduced risk of complications such as dislocation and premature wear associated with malpositioned components. These results are consistent with previous reports highlighting the early accuracy benefits of robotic assistance in THA.21,22,23,24)

The findings of this study are particularly relevant in the Korean context. ONFH is quite prevalent in Korea, which leads to a demographic of younger patients undergoing THA compared to many other countries. Since these younger, often more active patients will live longer with their implants, accurate implant positioning is paramount for long-term success and for minimizing the need for revision surgery. Furthermore, ceramic-on-ceramic implants are frequently used in Korea because of their longevity. The most critical complication of this bearing surface is a ceramic fracture due to cup malpositioning, but RA-THA is particularly beneficial, as its enhanced precision can help mitigate this specific risk, offering an additional layer of safety for patients receiving these implants.

Furthermore, the absence of intraoperative or postoperative complications across both phases indicates that the introduction of robotic assistance did not introduce new acute risks to the patient, even during the surgeon’s initial period of adaptation. This supports the safety profile of the technology during its integration into surgical practice.

This study contributes to the literature by providing data on the learning curve of RA-THA with the MAKO system in a previously unexamined population. While learning curve lengths can vary, our detailed analysis of step-specific operative times and consistent component accuracy reinforces that while efficiency may require an adaptation period, the precision benefits are often attained from the commencement of robotic utilization.

This study has several limitations. First, limitations of this study include its single-surgeon design and a relatively modest sample size, particularly of the learning phase. While this approach provides focused insight into individual surgeon adaptation, the generalizability of these findings to broader surgical populations or varied institutional settings may be limited. Future multi-center investigations with larger cohorts are warranted to corroborate these findings and to investigate the influence of surgeon experience and training protocols on the learning curve. Second, the relatively short follow-up precludes assessment of long-term outcomes such as implant survivorship and functional results. Third, the learning curve in this study was defined primarily based on operative time using CUSUM analysis. Although a reduction in operative time was observed after 11 cases, other clinically relevant parameters such as component positioning and complications might not demonstrate a similar learning effect. However, satisfactory acetabular cup positioning was confirmed from the first case onward. Finally, the MAKO robotic system officially supports only Stryker prostheses. In this study, however, RA-THA was performed using a prosthesis from an alternative manufacturer capable of ceramic articulation. Although this may have influenced clinical outcomes, this did not appear to significantly affect the surgical process or overall results.

RA-THA is associated with a learning curve of approximately 11 cases, primarily reflected in operative efficiency rather than component accuracy. There is no learning curve effect for the accuracy of cup positioning or the restoration of hip biomechanics, as high precision is achieved from the outset. These findings support the safe and efficient integration of robot-assisted systems into clinical practice, with rapid attainment of operative proficiency and consistent accuracy in component placement.
