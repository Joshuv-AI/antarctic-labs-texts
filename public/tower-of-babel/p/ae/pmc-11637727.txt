In the SARS‐CoV‐2 era, do we need to be afraid of obinutuzumab?
Authors: Joleen Choy, Katharine L Lewis
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn their paper, Pinto et al. report findings from the Italian URBAN study; an ambispective, observational, multicentre study evaluating the safety and efficacy of obinutuzumab‐based therapy for advanced stage follicular lymphoma (FL) in routine practice. The URBAN substudy reported here examined severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS‐CoV‐2) outcomes in 299 patients with treatment‐naïve advanced stage FL, treated with obinutuzumab‐based chemoimmunotherapy and maintenance. The study began enrolling in September 2019, continuing enrolment throughout the pandemic, with cut‐off for the current analysis of 31 January 2022; thus, it provides unique insights into various pandemic phases, including the impact of administration of SARS‐CoV‐2 vaccination.Commentary on: Pinto et al. Exposure to obinutuzumab does not affect outcomes of SARS‐CoV‐2 infection in vaccinated patients with newly diagnosed advanced‐stage follicular lymphoma. Br J Haematol 2024; 205:2219‐2227.Keywords: advanced stage follicular lymphoma, obinutuzumab‐based therapy, SARS‐CoV‐2

Since the beginning of the severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS‐CoV‐2) pandemic in 2020, there have been over 772 million cases and nearly 7 million deaths globally to the end of 2023. 1 There have been many valuable studies evaluating severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS‐CoV‐2) outcomes in patients with haematological malignancies, including large meta‐analyses. 2 A key strength of the URBAN study is the uniform histological diagnosis of participants and relative uniformity of treatment. Additionally, the population has comparable baseline characteristics to the randomised GALLIUM trial population (n = 1202), 3 suggestive that it is representative of a FL first‐line treated population.

Prior to the availability of vaccination, 100‐day SARS‐CoV‐2 mortality for patients with indolent non‐Hodgkin lymphoma (iNHL) was as high as 21%. 4 The URBAN study reports an even higher mortality rate from this period (8/21; 38.1%). The resultant profound and prolonged B‐cell depletion from anti‐CD20 monoclonal antibodies led to concerns among lymphoma clinicians regarding the risk of increased severity of SARS‐CoV‐2 infections in the anti‐CD20‐treated population, including hospitalisation, intensive care admissions and death and impaired ability to mount an adequate humoral response to vaccination. The detrimental effects of anti‐CD20 therapy on SARS‐CoV‐2 outcomes and antibody responses to vaccination during and up to 12 months beyond completion of anti‐CD20 therapy are well documented. 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 Furthermore, the use of bendamustine which is frequently utilised in combination with anti‐CD20 in iNHL treatment was of concern due to the T‐cell depletion expected with this agent, potentially further increasing the risk of severe SARS‐CoV‐2 infection due to impaired T‐cell response. Twenty‐three URBAN patients had lymphocyte subsets tested as part of routine clinical care, revealing B‐cell depletion and preservation of T cells at completion of induction therapy. However, chemotherapy received with obinutuzumab, and vaccination status of these specific patients is not reported or able to be correlated.

In the prospective UK PROSECO study, 8 221 patients with indolent B‐cell non‐Hodgkin lymphoma, including 93 with FL, were evaluated for cellular and antibody responses to SARS‐CoV‐2 vaccination. The authors observed marked impairment to antibody response in those treated with anti‐CD20‐based therapy, with 60% of those who had received anti‐CD20 therapy within 12 months showing undetectable antibodies despite full vaccination. However, T‐cell response to vaccination was frequently preserved, indicative of protective benefit of vaccination even in this patient group.

During the pandemic, many clinicians, therefore, considered deferring initiation of follicular lymphoma (FL) treatment or initiation of maintenance anti‐CD20 after completion of induction therapy. The effect of this approach on treatment efficacy is unknown, in particular the impact of delay or omission of maintenance on early progression events (POD24), which are prognostic for inferior survival in FL. 9

In the URBAN study, all patients received bendamustine (n = 142, 47.5%), CHOP (cyclophosphamide, vincristine, prednisolone and doxorubicin; n = 139, 46.5%) or CVP (cyclophosphamide, vincristine, prednisolone; n = 18, 6%) chemotherapy in combination with obinutuzumab as first‐line therapy for FL, according to treating physician choice. The reasons for treatment selection were not recorded. In contrast to previous studies, 10 , 11 , 12 there were no statistically significant differences in the incidence of SARS‐CoV‐2 infections, hospitalizations and deaths in patients who received obinutuzumab in combination with bendamustine versus CHOP or CVP induction. Similarly, there was no statistically significant difference in incidence of SARS‐CoV‐2 infections between patients who initiated obinutuzumab maintenance (266/299; 88.9%) compared to those that did not. Only a minority (10.4%) have completed maintenance at the time of data cut‐off. Hospitalizations due to SARS‐CoV‐2 infections were similar between patients who completed obinutuzumab maintenance in comparison to those who did not receive it (37.5% vs. 50%, respectively, p = 0.888). Likewise, there was no difference in SARS‐CoV‐2‐related mortality between those who completed maintenance versus those who did not receive it (0% vs. 25%, respectively, p = 0.394). Despite the majority of patients not yet having completed maintenance, 65% patients have already required modifications to their maintenance regimen; higher than in previous studies, 3 further underscoring the impact of the pandemic. It is possible that these modifications facilitated more effective vaccination or recovery from SARS‐CoV‐2 infections; however, granular details on schedule modification are not available to provide further insight. This substudy was not designed to evaluate lymphoma treatment efficacy, however efficacy data at the end of induction therapy are available for 193/299 (64.6%), with an overall response rate of 90.7%; comparable with obinutuzumab‐treated patients in the pre‐pandemic randomised GALLIUM study. However, no data are available from this substudy on progression‐free survival or POD24 events, and the impact of treatment delays or modifications on lymphoma outcomes currently remains unknown.

The URBAN study was not designed to elucidate the univariate impact of obinutuzumab administration on the risk of SARS‐Cov‐2 adverse sequelae nor the case report forms designed to capture granular details of SARS‐Cov‐2 infection episodes or vaccination history. Alongside receipt of obinutuzumab, frequency of SARS‐Cov‐2‐related hospitalizations and deaths is influenced by many uncaptured variables, including heterogeneity of SARS‐Cov‐2 prophylaxis and treatment, the strain implicated in infection and it's virulence and availability and administration of other supportive care measures such as convalescent plasma or adjunctive immunoglobulin replacement therapy. Tixagevimab and cilgavimab (Evusheld) and nirmatrelvir/ritonavir, which both have efficacy against some SARS‐CoV‐2 strains, 13 , 14 were not available in Italy during the study period. The overall incidence of SARS‐CoV‐2 infection (48/299) is lower than expected if the study had covered later phases of the pandemic, where the highly infectious omicron variant was dominant (November 2021 onwards), as suggested by the exponential rise in SARS‐CoV‐2 infections in Italy in the 2 months before data cut‐off.

Additionally, the efficacy of vaccination in reducing adverse‐related COVID‐19 sequelae is potentially underestimated in this study due to low recruitment numbers during the first wave, prior to the availability of vaccination, which may impact upon the incidence of SARS‐CoV‐2 infection and the number of hospitalizations in the pre‐vaccinated era in comparison to the post‐vaccinated phase. Only around half of enrolled patients (55.1%) had received three vaccines, with patients not having opportunity by the time of data cut‐off for the additional protective effect of booster vaccination, reported of crucial importance to the development of humoral immunity in the PROSECO study. 8 As the authors note, further microbiological testing to determine the implicated SARS‐CoV‐2 strain would enable the correlation of virulence and impact upon SARS‐CoV‐2‐related morbidity and mortality and efficacy of vaccinations in reducing these adverse outcomes. Details on many aspects of SARS‐CoV‐2 infective episodes are unavailable. Although the overall study involves a large cohort, the small number of total infections reported (n = 48) means we cannot definitively conclude that obinutuzumab maintenance has no impact on SARS‐Cov‐2 outcomes.

The retrospective design of the majority of studies conducted to examine the impact of treatments on SARS‐CoV‐2‐related outcomes leads inherently to heterogeneity and confounding, presenting challenges in interpreting reported outcomes and delineating the impacts of individual factors, and applicability of the results to other patient populations. Compilations of data sets such as the URBAN study continue to shed further light on the effect of anti‐CD20 administration in the SARS‐CoV‐2 era and the impact of vaccination. Data are still lacking to draw conclusions regarding the optimal approach to the administration of maintenance, with the impact of specific schedule modification on vaccine efficacy, incidence and severity of SARS‐CoV‐2 infections and lymphoma therapy efficacy remaining unknown.

While the URBAN study provides valuable data to support the ongoing use of obinutuzumab in the SARS‐CoV‐2 era, a considered, individually tailored approach towards FL therapy, including careful assessment of a patient's risks and benefit profile for proceeding with obinutuzumab‐based therapy (including maintenance) and willingness to receive vaccinations, including booster vaccinations, continues to be essential.
