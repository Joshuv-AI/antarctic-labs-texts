Enhanced prediction of coronary heart disease risk in diabetic patients via Machine learning incorporating multiple inflammatory and metabolic indices: A study with Dual-Cohort validation
Authors: Pinpeng Xie, Xiangzhen Ran, Weichun Zhu, Yunfang Xie, Youran Dong, Zhiyuan Wang, Chungang Zhai, Lei Qiao, Jianmin Yang, Wenqiang Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstractKeywords: Coronary heart disease, Diabetes mellitus, Inflammatory and metabolic indices, Machine learning, Dual-Cohort Validation

Introduction

Coronary heart disease (CHD), a chronic ischemic disorder primarily resulting from coronary artery narrowing, constitutes one of the leading global causes of morbidity and mortality [1]. The pathophysiology of CHD involves the accumulation of lipids and fibrous tissue within arterial walls, proliferation of smooth muscle cells, and both local and systemic inflammatory responses [2]. Chronic inflammation has been implicated as a pivotal mechanism in the development of CHD, influencing multiple stages such as atherosclerotic plaque formation, plaque rupture, ischemia–reperfusion damage, and subsequent ventricular remodeling [3]. Despite substantial advancements in therapeutic approaches, CHD incidence continues to rise, driven by global aging trends and increasingly prevalent unhealthy lifestyles [4]. In the United States, CHD accounts for approximately one-fourth of annual deaths [5]. DM is recognized as a critical predictor for CHD, with diabetic individuals often presenting with more extensive coronary artery lesions, leading to more severe vascular injury[6]. Although CHD represents a common and lethal complication in diabetic patients, the underlying pathogenic mechanisms linking these conditions remain incompletely understood [7]. Therefore, precise identification and assessment of CHD risk in diabetic populations is clinically essential for reducing adverse cardiovascular outcomes.

In recent years, various novel composite indices have emerged, combining inflammatory markers and metabolic indices to improve disease risk assessment. Some biomarkers such as the visceral adiposity index (VAI) and lipid accumulation product (LAP), have demonstrated promising potential for evaluating abdominal obesity, fat distribution, and cardiometabolic risk[8]. Neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), systemic immune-inflammation index (SII), neutrophil-monocyte-to-lymphocyte ratio (NMLR), systemic inflammation response index (SIRI), monocyte-to-lymphocyte ratio (MLR), and other inflammation-related indices derived from whole blood parameters, have increasingly been adopted as prognostic markers for mortality and clinical outcomes across metabolic disorders, cardiovascular diseases (CVD), and cancer [9], [10], [11]. Furthermore, the aggregate index of systemic inflammation (AISI)—comprising platelet, neutrophil, monocyte, and lymphocyte counts—provides a comprehensive reflection of systemic inflammatory status, with potential implications for predicting depression [12]. Similarly, the platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR) has been validated as a rapid and reliable indicator for detecting acute heart failure, suggesting potential diagnostic utility in CHD [13]. Another notable inflammatory biomarker, the neutrophil-to-albumin ratio (NPAR)—combining serum albumin concentrations and neutrophil counts—has demonstrated efficacy as a predictor of mortality across various cardiovascular conditions, such as acute myocardial infarction (AMI) [14]. Notably, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) notably exerts protective cardiovascular effects through its anti-inflammatory actions, promotion of cholesterol efflux, and inhibition of low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) oxidation, thereby mitigating endothelial damage[15]. Novel HDL-based inflammatory ratios, including neutrophil-to-HDL ratio (NHR), platelet-to-HDL ratio (PHR), monocyte-to-HDL ratio (MHR), lymphocyte-to-HDL ratio (LHR), and uric acid-to-HDL ratio (UHR), are attracting increasing attention due to their potential for comprehensive evaluation of oxidative stress, inflammation, and cardiovascular prognosis[16], [17]. Furthermore, the triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index, a straightforward and trustworthy proxy for insulin resistance (IR), has demonstrated strong correlations with cardiovascular events, and diabetes [18]. Recent findings further suggest that combining TyG with anthropometric parameters enhances its prognostic capacity compared to using TyG alone, thereby providing more precise risk stratification for clinical outcomes [19]. The strengths of these novel composite indices, which integrate inflammation and metabolic parameters, stem not only from the straightforward and simplicity calculation methods—rendering them extremely useful in standard clinical situations [20] —but also from their potential superiority in disease assessment compared to traditional single-marker serological tests[21]. Nevertheless, systematic investigations regarding their effectiveness in predicting CHD specifically in diabetic populations remain relatively scarce.

Machine learning (ML), an innovative artificial intelligence approach, has increasingly become indispensable in improving predictive accuracy in clinical settings and is extensively utilized for medical data analysis. ML methodologies offer distinct advantages by capturing intricate clinical patterns often missed by conventional statistical models [22]. Recent evidence supports the enhanced predictive performance of ML-based predictive models compared to traditional statistical techniques[23]. Considering the intricate relationship between DM and CVD such as CHD, it is imperative to achieve early and accurate CHD risk stratification among diabetic patients to mitigate adverse cardiovascular outcomes. Given this context, our study seeks to establish a robust ML-based model to predict CHD risk in diabetic patients utilizing cross-sectional data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) and external data from Qilu Hospital of Shandong University from 2024 to 2025. The main goal of this research is to create a practical and highly accurate predictive tool to enable clinicians to recognize high-risk individuals early, thereby facilitating timely and targeted prevention and management strategies. Ultimately, this approach aims to enhance patient outcomes by providing clinicians with novel insights into risk stratification and prognosis optimization for diabetic patients at risk of CHD.

Methods

Study design and population

NHANES is a cross-sectional research program designed to collect nutritional and health-related information from the common people in the US. Conducted in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki, this study was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Review Board of the National Center for Health Statistics. Prior to inclusion, every participant provided written informed consent. Participants in this study were derived from NHANES 1999–2018. The following exclusion criteria were applied: 1) participants aged < 20 years; 2) participants without diabetes at baseline; 3) participants missing data for calculating inflammatory and metabolic indices; and 4) participants with missing outcome data for CHD. For missing data on covariates, we used multiple imputation to retain the largest possible sample size. Specifically, the random forest algorithm was used to impute covariates with less than 10% missing data. Ultimately, this study included 3,379 participants.

Data Collection and Definitions

We collected data on participants' demographics, lifestyle, laboratory testing, and medical conditions. The study population comprised individuals with diabetes, defined as follows: 1) self-reported diabetes diagnosed by a physician; 2) fasting plasma glucose (FPG) ≥ 126 mg/dL; 3) glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) > 6.5%; or 4) present use of insulin or glucose-lowering drugs. Eight inflammatory composite indices (SIRI, SII, NLR, PLR, NMLR, MLR, AISI, NPAR) and fourteen metabolic composite indices (TyG-BMI, TyG-WHtR, TyG-WC, CMI, UHR, AIP, MHR, NHR, LHR, PHR, TyG, BRI, LAP, VAI) were analyzed, with detailed calculation methods offered on Table S1. CHD, defined as fulfilling any of the following criteria: 1) self-reported physician-diagnosed CHD; 2) self-reported physician-diagnosed angina/angina pectoris; or 3) self-reported heart attack reported by a physician. This study accounted for multiple covariates: chronological age, sex, ethnicity, education, family poverty − income ratio (PIR), marital status (not single, single), body mass index (BMI), smoking status (yes, no), hypertension (yes, no), and hyperlipidemia (yes, no). Current smoking is defined as having smoked at least 100 cigarettes during one’s lifetime and continuing to smoke at the time of the survey. Hypertension refers to self-reported physician-diagnosed hypertension, antihypertensive drug use, systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mmHg or diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mmHg. Hyperlipidemia refers to self-reported physician-diagnosed hyperlipidemia, use of lipid-lowering drugs, or total cholesterol > 240 mg/dL.

Feature selection

For feature screening, multiple statistical methods were applied. Initially, the variance inflation factor (VIF) was employed to figure out multicollinearity among variables and perform preliminary variable selection. Multicollinearity refers to the condition in which two or more independent variables are highly correlated. In fact, the dependent variable is typically influenced jointly by multiple correlated predictors. The presence of multicollinearity thus reduces the reliability of estimated coefficients, impacting model stability and performance. Given the non-normal distributions of the unique inflammatory and metabolic composite indices included in the statistical models, Spearman's correlation analysis was used to assess the correlations between the independent variables. To further clarify the intercorrelations among the selected indices, a Spearman correlation heatmap was constructed using the original dataset. Generally, a VIF exceeding 10 indicates severe multicollinearity between a given variable and other predictors [24]. Consequently, variables with VIF values greater than 10 were directly removed from the dataset.

Three different feature selection techniques were thoroughly integrated to identify the final set of variables: the Boruta algorithm, least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression, and the univariate logistic regression. LASSO is particularly advantageous for managing multicollinearity and enhancing model interpretability, as it penalizes coefficients, effectively shrinking less informative predictors towards zero [25]. A two-step robust feature selection process was implemented: first, to get the ideal regularization parameter (lambda), suitable predictors with nonzero coefficients were first chosen using LASSO with 10-fold cross-validation. Subsequently, the stability of variable selection was further assessed through Bootstrap-LASSO stability selection, involving 1000 bootstrap replications, whereby variables with selection frequencies ≥ 85% were retained as stable predictors. The Boruta algorithm is a feature selection method that uses an assessment of variable relevance. Critical variables are specifically identified by contrasting the Z-scores of actual features with those of synthetic “shadow features.” While the Z-scores of shadow features are obtained by randomly permuting the values of the real features, the relevance (Z-score) of each genuine feature is determined iteratively using a random forest model. Features proven consistently less significant than their shadow counterparts across multiple bootstrap iterations are iteratively excluded. Consequently, only those features demonstrating Z-scores consistently higher than the maximum Z-score are retained [26]. Ultimately, the final feature set for the creation of the predictive model was the intersection of variables chosen by these three approaches. After this, we used restricted cubic splines (RCS) analysis to investigate the dose–response relationship between five inflammatory and metabolic indices and the risk of CHD.

Construction and development of Machine learning models

To guarantee data integrity and usefulness for further analyses, the dataset was rigorously preprocessed during the early phases of model design and development. Given the class imbalance observed in the original binary outcome labels, the Synthetic Minority Oversampling Technique (SMOTE) was implemented. SMOTE is particularly effective in mitigating class imbalance and preventing model overfitting by synthesizing novel minority class instances based on k-nearest neighbors [26]. The processed dataset was then split into training and validation subsets at random in a 7:3 ratio. This partition ratio was deliberately chosen for several reasons: first, it ensures a balanced distribution between training and validation datasets, providing adequate data volume in the training subset to effectively capture predictive features, while maintaining sufficient observations in the validation subset to reliably assess model performance. Second, this ratio helps minimize overfitting and enhances the generalizability of the developed models. Selected final variables from prior feature screening and meaningful baseline features were then utilized for machine learning modeling. Nine different ML algorithms were created and assessed, including Support vector machine (SVM), random forest (RF), the univariate logistic regression, extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost), gradient boosting machine (GBM), adaptive boosting (AdaBoost), neural network, categorical boosting (CatBoost), and light gradient boosting machine (LightGBM). Model robustness and stability were rigorously validated using 5-fold cross-validation repeated three times (5-fold repeated 3 times cross-validation).

Model performance evaluation and Interpretation

Meaningful baseline features and selected indices were utilized in a methodical manner in order to fully evaluate the predictive performance of the ML models for CHD. The main indicator was the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC AUC), which shows how well a model can distinguish between those with and without CHD. A higher AUC signifies superior discriminatory performance. F1-score, sensitivity (Sen), specificity (Spec), and accuracy (Acc) were additional measurements. The F1-score offers a fair overview of both recall and precision, sensitivity shows the model's ability to identify those with CHD, specificity measures the ability to correctly recognize individuals without CHD, and accuracy indicates proportion of correctly classified observations. Values approaching 1 for these metrics denote better model performance. Predictive reliability was ensured by assessing the consistency between expected probabilities and observed outcomes using calibration curves with Brier scores. To determine the prediction model's clinical usefulness and practical applicability in clinical decision-making scenarios, decision curve analysis (DCA) was also carried out. Finally, the best model was evaluated on the external set to determine its clinical utility and how well it performs in various contexts.

To further interpret the best-performing ML model, Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) were employed[27]. Unlike traditional linear models where coefficients directly represent feature importance, ML models require specialized methods to enhance interpretability due to their inherent “black-box” characteristics. SHAP quantify each feature's contribution toward individual predictions, clearly illustrating how specific variables influence predicted CHD risk. This method significantly enhances model transparency, providing valuable clinical insights and facilitating deeper understanding of the predictive model for CHD risk among American adults with diabetes. The detailed process is illustrated in Fig. S1 and S2.

External validation

To rigorously appraise the model’s performance across heterogeneous settings and its clinical utility, we conducted external validation in a cohort of DM inpatients treated at Qilu Hospital of Shandong University between 2024 and 2025. Inclusion and exclusion criteria mirrored those of the original model group to ensure comparability of samples and credibility of inferences. Testing the algorithm in an independent population enabled evaluation of transportability across differing data distributions, supporting its robustness and suitability for real-world clinical deployment.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.2.2). Data preprocessing steps aimed to ensure cleanliness, structural integrity, and suitability for subsequent ML modeling. Using the Shapiro-Wilk test, normality was evaluated for continuous variables. Normal distribution variables were described as means ± standard deviations, while non-normal variables were summarized as medians with interquartile ranges (IQR), which provide reliable measurements of central tendency and dispersion when outliers are present. The nonparametric Mann-Whitney U test was used for group comparisons because all of the continuous variables in this study had non-normal distributions. The Chi-square test or Fisher's exact test were used to evaluate categorical data, and the results were summarized as frequencies with percentages. Specifically, when anticipated frequencies were poor, Fisher's exact test was applied, providing an accurate assessment of statistical significance by precisely calculating the probability of observing given frequency distributions, thus particularly suitable for small sample sizes. For every analysis, a p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Spearman correlation heatmaps that illustrate the pairwise relationships among the five inflammation- and metabolism-related indices exhibit moderate correlations for the majority of these composite indices (Fig. S4). RCS analyses demonstrated linear associations of UHR, MHR, NLR, and AIP with CHD (all P for overall < 0.05; all P for nonlinear > 0.05), whereas NPAR showed a significant nonlinear association with CHD (P for overall < 0.001; P for nonlinear = 0.018) (Fig. S5).

To mitigate bias arising from data imbalance and provide a more comprehensive evaluation, additional performance metrics—including F1 score, Acc, Spec, and Sen—were calculated and are summarized in Table 3 and S2. The RF algorithm yielded outstanding values across all metrics, achieving 0.916 (Acc), 0.920 (Sen), 0.912 (Spec), and 0.927 (F1 score) in the training, 0.761, 0.798, 0.716, and 0.788, respectively, in the internal validation, and 0.692, 0.729, 0.614, 0.762, respectively, in the external validation. DCA based on training set (Fig. S8A) demonstrated that the RF model offered the best net clinical benefit among all ML methods, reinforcing its practical utility. This finding was further validated in the internal validation set (Fig. S8B) and external validation set (Fig. S10B), where RF continued to deliver substantial net benefit. With its calibration curves nearly matching the optimal 45° reference line, the RF model demonstrated a strong relationship between the anticipated and measured CHD probabilities in both datasets, according to calibration curves (Fig. S9 and S10A). In contrast, other models showed marked calibration deviation.

Brier scores were computed to further evaluate the discriminative ability of the model. Competing with all other models, the RF model produced the training set's lowest Brier score (0.103) (Table S3). Similarly, it achieved the lowest Brier score in the internal validation set (0.166) and in the external set (0.193), further confirming its superior predictive reliability. Taken together, these comprehensive evaluations consistently demonstrate that the RF-based model achieved the best—and nearly optimal—performance among the ML algorithms tested.

To further examine the impact of the ten parameters on the RF model's predictions of CHD risk, we also used SHAP dependency plots. Discussion

In this study, we developed and externally validated an interpretable ML model to predict the risk of CHD among adults with diabetes. By integrating data from the NHANES database and an independent hospital-based validation cohort, the model demonstrated a certain degree of generalizability and clinical applicability. Notably, one of the key findings of this study was the promising potential of the five composite indices—UHR, MHR, NLR, NPAR, and AIP in ML-based CHD risk prediction. These indices are calculated from routine clinical and laboratory measurements, providing readily accessible tools for cardiovascular risk stratification in diabetic populations.

UHR, an emerging biomarker integrating inflammatory and metabolic components, exhibited substantial predictive value for cardiovascular events in this study. Elevated Uric acid (UA) levels, considered an independent cardiovascular risk factor, may impair endothelial function and inhibit vascular repair mechanisms [28]. On the other hand, HDL has anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and anti thrombotic properties that work together to reduce oxidative stress and vascular inflammation [29]. Elevated UHR levels have been increasingly recognized as a predictor of adverse cardiovascular outcomes, encompassing congestive heart failure (CHF), and CHD [30]. Monocytes contribute to initiate and advance atherosclerosis by promoting endothelial dysfunction, arterial wall injury, and chronic inflammation within coronary vessels [31]. MHR has been strongly associated with chronic heart failure [32], and Cardiovascular-Kidney-Metabolic (CKM) syndrome [33]. Moreover, MHR serves as an reliable indicator of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACEs) and long-term mortality[34], and has been implicated in obesity-related cardiac dysfunction. Similarly, both NLR and NPAR, as representative markers of systemic inflammation, demonstrated a significant association with CHD development. NLR is a well-known inflammatory indice that has been connected to a variety of metabolic and cardiovascular diseases, such as DM, hypertension, and obesity [35]. Likewise, elevated NPAR levels have been associated with unfavorable cardiovascular outcomes, including heart failure [36].

One of the study's main advantages is the utilization of a large-scale, nationally representative cross-sectional dataset and externally validated in an independent hospital-based cohort. Most prediction models are developed and validated only in internal datasets, leading to overestimation of performance and limited generalizability. Our dual‑cohort design provides more robust evidence for potential clinical translation. In addition, the selected indices are derived from routinely available laboratory tests, enabling low‑cost, wide‑scale deployment without additional invasive examinations. From a modeling perspective, the favorable performance of the random forest algorithm may be attributable to its ability to capture nonlinear associations and complex interactions among demographic characteristics, clinical variables, and composite laboratory indices without relying on strict parametric assumptions. This feature is particularly relevant in diabetic populations, in whom CHD risk is shaped by multiple interrelated metabolic and inflammatory processes. Among the nine models tested, the RF algorithm consistently outperformed the other models, achieving the highest AUC in both internal (0.852) and external (0.713) validations, supporting its potential utility for identifying high-risk individuals who may benefit from early preventive interventions. Importantly, model performance was assessed using a multidimensional framework that extended beyond discrimination alone to include DCA, calibration curves, and Brier scores, thereby allowing a more comprehensive evaluation of predictive performance and potential clinical utility. To translate these findings into clinical utility, we envision the integration of the RF model into electronic health record systems and decision support platforms. By leveraging routinely collected anthropometric and laboratory data, the model could generate individualized, real-time risk predictions during standard outpatient encounters. This would enable clinicians to stratify patients according to CHD risk and implement timely, personalized preventive interventions. Furthermore, embedding the model within remote monitoring infrastructures could facilitate continuous risk surveillance, allowing early detection of dynamic changes in cardiovascular risk and triggering appropriate medical responses. Crucially, by demonstrating how various variables contribute to risk prediction, the SHAP improves model transparency and, consequently, clinical interpretability, reliability, and user acceptance.

Nevertheless, addressing class imbalance remains a critical methodological challenge in predictive modeling, particularly when employing resampling techniques such as SMOTE. While SMOTE-based approaches can enhance classification accuracy by artificially balancing minority and majority classes, emerging evidence suggests they may compromise the reliability of predicted probabilities, potentially leading to poor model calibration and suboptimal clinical decisions. In our study, we implemented SMOTE to mitigate class imbalance; however, we remained cognizant of its limitations regarding calibration. We used calibration curves, intercepts, and slope evaluations to check and correct for this, making sure that forecast probabilities closely matched observed occurrence rates. The use of resampling techniques in clinical prediction models is still controversial in spite of these remedial initiatives. To increase calibration fidelity and generalizability, future studies should examine different approaches, such as hybrid ensemble techniques or cost-sensitive learning, and validate model performance across diverse populations.

There are various limitations to this study that warrant consideration. First, causal relationships between detected risk indicators and the development of CHD cannot be conclusively proven since the NHANES dataset is cross-sectional. To validate these correlations and clarify the chronological order of events, longitudinal cohort studies are required. Second, although the model showed acceptable discrimination in the external validation set (AUC = 0.713), its specificity was modest (0.614), implying a notable false-positive rate. A likely explanation is the gap between the training and external cohorts. The training data (NHANES) reflect a large-scale, nationally representative cross-sectional population, whereas the external cohort was hospital-based and drawn from a single region in eastern China. These differences include race, genetic background, health behaviors, and access to care. In addition, the external cohort had a relatively small sample size (n = 902), which may increase the variability of performance estimates, including specificity. Together, these factors likely produced a shift between the internal and external validation datasets, limiting generalizability. Notably, a decrease in AUC from internal to external validation is common in clinical prediction studies, especially across diverse populations, consistent with findings by Chen et al. [37]. Therefore, replication studies in ethnically and geographically diverse cohorts are essential to ensure broader generalizability. Third, even though multiple models of ML were employed to assess the predictive power of particular composite indices, issues like class imbalance and sample size restrictions may still have an impact on how well they work. Future investigations should consider using larger, more representative datasets, improved resampling strategies, and enhanced calibration techniques to strengthen model robustness and predictive reliability.

In summary, this work uses novel composite biomarkers and ML techniques to present an easy-to-use and effective method for predicting the risk of CHD in persons with diabetes. Such predictive models may improve risk classification, facilitate prompt preventive measures, and ultimately lead to better cardiovascular outcomes in these high-risk individuals if they are incorporated into standard clinical practice.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we developed and dual-cohort validated a random forest–based machine learning model for CHD risk prediction in diabetic patients. This predictive tool holds promise for clinical implementation to improve early identification of high-risk diabetic patients and optimize cardiovascular prevention strategies.
