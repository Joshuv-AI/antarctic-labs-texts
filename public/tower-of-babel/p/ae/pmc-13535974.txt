Diffusing Capacity of the Lung for Carbon Monoxide and the Risk of All‐Cause Mortality in Patients With Pulmonary Hypertension Caused by Fibrosing Mediastinitis: A Multicenter Cohort Study
Authors: Mengyu Wu, Hongling Su, Xuechun Sun, Sugang Gong, Wei Ding, Yuhong Li, Yuting Li, Wenjie Dong, Bowen Jin, Liming Zhao, Qian Jiang, Xincao Tao, Ou Xu, Wande Yu, Jie Cheng, Xintian Cai, Yunshan Cao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPulmonary hypertension caused by fibrosing mediastinitis (PH‐FM) is a high‐risk condition with limited validated tools for prognostic assessment. Although diffusing capacity of the lung for carbon monoxide (DLCO) predicts outcomes in several cardiopulmonary diseases, its prognostic value in PH‐FM remains unclear. In this multicenter retrospective cohort study, we included 106 patients with confirmed PH‐FM from 14 centers across China. The primary endpoint was all‐cause mortality. Cox proportional hazards models were used to assess the association between baseline percent‐predicted DLCO and mortality, with sequential adjustment for clinical, hemodynamic, and spirometric variables. The final cohort included 106 patients, of whom 60 had DLCO ≥ 60% predicted and 46 had DLCO < 60% predicted. Over a median follow‐up of 36 months (interquartile range [IQR], 14.0–50.8), 27 deaths occurred. Across Cox models, each 10‐percentage‐point increase in percent‐predicted DLCO was associated with a 30% lower risk of all‐cause mortality. DLCO < 60% predicted was associated with higher all‐cause mortality compared with DLCO ≥ 60% predicted, with hazard ratios ranging from 6.90 to 7.80 across models. Lower baseline percent‐predicted DLCO was independently associated with a higher risk of all‐cause mortality in patients with PH‐FM. DLCO may provide a simple, noninvasive measure to complement current risk assessment in this population.Keywords: all‐cause mortality, cohort study, diffusing capacity of the lung for carbon monoxide, fibrosing mediastinitis, pulmonary hypertension

Introduction

Fibrosing mediastinitis (FM) is a rare fibroproliferative disorder characterized by excessive fibrotic tissue within the mediastinum. Although nonmalignant, FM can be clinically severe because the fibrotic tissue may encase and compress vital intrathoracic structures, including the pulmonary arteries, pulmonary veins, and central airways [1]. The etiology of FM is commonly associated with an aberrant granulomatous immune response to infection. In North America, FM is most frequently linked to Histoplasma capsulatum, whereas Mycobacterium tuberculosis is more frequently implicated in endemic regions such as China [2]. Pulmonary hypertension (PH) represents one of the major clinical consequences of FM and is mainly driven by extrinsic compression of the pulmonary vasculature. This process increases pulmonary vascular resistance and pulmonary arterial pressure, which may promote right ventricular dysfunction and contribute to adverse clinical outcomes [3]. PH caused by FM (PH‐FM) is categorized as World Health Organization (WHO) Group 5 PH, reflecting its heterogeneous and incompletely understood pathophysiology [4]. Despite its substantial clinical burden, validated noninvasive tools for routine risk stratification in PH‐FM remain limited. Identifying readily available physiological markers may therefore help refine risk assessment and support clinical management in this rare but high‐risk population.

Diffusing capacity of the lung for carbon monoxide (DLCO) is a noninvasive pulmonary function measure that evaluates the efficiency of gas transfer across the alveolar‐capillary interface. It reflects the combined effects of alveolar‐capillary membrane conductance, effective gas‐exchange surface area, and pulmonary capillary blood volume [5]. Accordingly, DLCO is widely used in the clinical evaluation of both parenchymal and pulmonary vascular diseases [6]. Beyond its diagnostic role, reduced DLCO has been associated with adverse outcomes in a broad range of cardiopulmonary disorders [7, 8, 9]. In chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), DLCO below 60% of the predicted value has been linked to increased all‐cause and respiratory mortality, independent of other pulmonary function measures [9]. In interstitial lung diseases, lower percent‐predicted DLCO is consistently associated with a higher risk of death or lung transplantation [10]. In pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH), DLCO has also been recognized as an important prognostic marker [7]. Similar observations have been reported in other forms of PH, suggesting that DLCO may capture aspects of disease severity that are not fully reflected by conventional hemodynamic parameters [11, 12, 13]. Taken together, the available evidence indicates that DLCO may serve as an integrative physiological marker of disease severity and prognosis across cardiopulmonary conditions.

However, the prognostic value of DLCO in PH‐FM has not been systematically evaluated. Unlike pulmonary hypertension driven by intrinsic pulmonary arteriopathy or primary parenchymal fibrosis, PH‐FM is largely caused by extrinsic compression of the pulmonary arteries and/or veins. This distinct pathophysiological feature may impair gas exchange through several mechanisms, including reduced effective pulmonary capillary blood volume, secondary microvascular dysfunction or distal capillary bed loss, and ventilation‐perfusion mismatch resulting from heterogeneous pulmonary perfusion. In this context, DLCO may serve as a simple, noninvasive physiological marker of prognostic risk in patients with PH‐FM, potentially helping to identify those at heightened mortality risk who may benefit from closer surveillance or more intensive therapeutic management.

Therefore, using a large multicenter cohort, we evaluated the association between baseline percent‐predicted DLCO and all‐cause mortality in patients with confirmed PH‐FM.

Methods

Study Design

To address the limited evidence on prognostic assessment in PH‐FM, we conducted a multicenter retrospective cohort study coordinated by Sichuan Provincial People's Hospital. The study was conducted in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines and included patients recruited from 14 centers across 10 provinces in China between May 1, 2017, and August 31, 2025.

Both incident and prevalent cases of PH‐FM were included. Potentially eligible patients were retrospectively identified from electronic health records and institutional databases at each participating center. PH‐FM was diagnosed by computed tomography pulmonary angiography (CTPA), based on characteristic infiltrative fibrotic changes within the mediastinum with compression of major vascular or airway structures [1, 3]. PH was defined according to current hemodynamic criteria as a mean pulmonary arterial pressure (mPAP) > 20 mmHg measured by right heart catheterization (RHC) [14]. Eligibility required age ≥ 18 years, confirmed PH‐FM as defined above, and at least one objectively measured DLCO value obtained under clinically stable conditions within 30 days after PH‐FM diagnosis. Exclusion criteria included incomplete core data, alternative mediastinal or vascular pathologies, significant concomitant parenchymal lung disease, active infection or respiratory instability at the time of DLCO assessment, or less than 3 months of follow‐up.

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee for Basic and Clinical Research of Sichuan Provincial People's Hospital (approval No. 2025‐559), as well as the ethics committees of the participating centers. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Data Collection and Clinical Assessment

At each participating center, baseline information was retrieved from electronic health records and entered into a standardized electronic case report form. Because PH‐FM can be difficult to diagnose on imaging, all computed tomography pulmonary angiography (CTPA) scans were independently assessed by two experienced thoracic radiologists who had no knowledge of clinical outcomes. The reviewers confirmed the presence of infiltrative mediastinal fibrosis and recorded the involvement of major structures, including the pulmonary arteries, pulmonary veins, and central airways. Baseline was defined as the date of diagnostic RHC. Baseline variables included age, sex, body mass index, smoking status, comorbidities, WHO functional class, and symptoms reported by patients or documented by physicians, including dyspnea, cough, hemoptysis, chest pain, and syncope. Smoking status was recorded as never, former, or current smoking. Functional exercise capacity was evaluated with the 6‐min walk distance (6MWD) conducted per European Respiratory Society/American Thoracic Society (ERS/ATS) technical standards [15]. Laboratory variables included N‐terminal pro‐brain natriuretic peptide (NT‐proBNP) levels and arterial blood gas measurements.

Pulmonary function testing was performed within 30 days of RHC in accordance with international standards and included spirometry, lung volumes, and hemoglobin‐corrected single‐breath DLCO [16, 17, 18]. Forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1), forced vital capacity (FVC), total lung capacity (TLC), residual volume (RV), and DLCO were reported as percent‐predicted values according to appropriate reference equations. Baseline hemodynamic data were obtained during RHC under clinically stable conditions. Recorded variables included mPAP, pulmonary artery wedge pressure (PAWP), cardiac output (CO) measured by thermodilution or the Fick method, cardiac index, mixed venous oxygen saturation (SvO2), and calculated pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR). Treatment decisions were made by local multidisciplinary teams and were not dictated by the study protocol.

Outcome Ascertainment and Follow‐Up

We targeted all‐cause mortality as the primary endpoint. To reduce the risk of informative censoring, vital status was ascertained using two complementary sources: electronic health records at participating centers and structured telephone follow‐up conducted by trained personnel. Patients were contacted directly whenever possible; when patients could not be reached, designated family members were interviewed. Follow‐up time was calculated from diagnostic right heart catheterization to death or administrative censoring on August 31, 2025, whichever occurred first.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous data were described as mean ± standard deviation or as median with interquartile range [IQR], depending on normality assessed using the Shapiro‐Wilk test. Categorical data were reported as frequencies and percentages. Patients lacking essential information were not included in the analytic dataset, and complete‐case analyses were performed. Comparisons of baseline characteristics across DLCO groups were made using one‐way analysis of variance, the Kruskal‐Wallis test, or the chi‐square or Fisher's exact test, as appropriate.

Time‐to‐event outcomes were described using Kaplan‐Meier estimates, and survival differences between DLCO groups were compared with the log‐rank test. The proportional hazards assumption was checked by examining Schoenfeld residuals. The relationship between DLCO and all‐cause mortality was assessed with Cox proportional hazards regression in both univariable and multivariable analyses. DLCO was entered into the models as a continuous variable per 10‐percentage‐point increase in percent‐predicted value and as a binary variable defined by the 60% predicted threshold. Effect estimates are presented as hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Exploratory analyses were performed using alternative DLCO thresholds of 45%, 50%, 55%, and 60% predicted, and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was additionally performed using DLCO as a continuous variable to evaluate its discriminative ability.

Four prespecified models were used to examine the robustness of the association. The first model was unadjusted. The second model included sex, age, 6MWD, WHO functional class, and NT‐proBNP. The third model included sex, age, mPAP, SvO2, and PVR. The fourth model included sex, age, and FEV1/FVC ratio. Linear trends across ordered DLCO categories were tested. To explore the shape of the association between DLCO and mortality, restricted cubic splines with knots at the 5th, 50th, and 95th percentiles were incorporated into Cox models.

Subgroup analyses compared DLCO < 60% predicted with DLCO ≥ 60% predicted across prespecified strata defined by sex, age (< 60 vs. ≥ 60 years), 6MWD (< 320 vs. ≥ 320 m), NT‐proBNP level above or below the median, and WHO functional class (I/II vs. III/IV). Effect modification was assessed by adding multiplicative interaction terms to the Cox models. Statistical analyses were conducted with R version 4.2.0, and two‐sided p values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Baseline Clinical Characteristics

Table 1 summarizes the baseline clinical characteristics according to DLCO category. Compared with patients with DLCO ≥ 60% predicted, those with DLCO < 60% predicted were older (68.76 ± 6.71 vs. 64.20 ± 7.98 years, p = 0.002) and had a lower body mass index (21.09 ± 3.42 vs. 23.73 ± 3.44 kg/m2, p < 0.001). Sex, smoking status, WHO functional class, and 6MWD did not differ significantly between the two groups.

Dyspnea was more frequent among patients with DLCO < 60% predicted than among those with DLCO ≥ 60% predicted (100.0% vs. 86.67%, p = 0.010). NT‐proBNP levels were numerically higher in patients with DLCO < 60% predicted than in those with DLCO ≥ 60% predicted, but the between‐group difference was not statistically significant (345.05 vs. 274.70 pg/mL, p = 0.069). PaCO2 was higher in the DLCO < 60% predicted group (36.02 ± 9.57 vs. 30.06 ± 13.79 mmHg, p = 0.014).

Pulmonary function differed substantially by DLCO category. Patients with DLCO < 60% predicted showed lower FEV1% predicted, FVC% predicted, FEV1/FVC ratio, and TLC% predicted than those with DLCO ≥ 60% predicted (all p < 0.001). As expected, DLCO% predicted was also markedly lower in the DLCO < 60% predicted group.

Hemodynamic profiles were also less favorable in the lower DLCO group, with higher mPAP (44.57 ± 13.92 vs. 38.42 ± 11.64 mmHg, p = 0.015), lower SvO2 (60.43 ± 9.90% vs. 65.63 ± 6.53%, p = 0.002), lower CO (4.46 ± 1.18 vs. 5.01 ± 1.15 L/min, p = 0.018), and higher PVR (7.43 vs. 5.67 wood units, p = 0.002).

Survival

During a median follow‐up of 36 months (IQR, 14.0–50.8), 27 patients died, corresponding to an all‐cause mortality rate of 25.5%. Overall survival is illustrated in Figure 3. Kaplan‐Meier curves demonstrated a clear survival difference between the two DLCO groups, with poorer survival in patients with DLCO < 60% predicted compared with those with DLCO ≥ 60% predicted (log‐rank p < 0.001; Figure 4).

Association Between DLCO and All‐Cause Mortality

Percent‐predicted DLCO showed an inverse relationship with all‐cause mortality in Cox proportional hazards analyses (Table 2). In the unadjusted model, a 10‐percentage‐point higher DLCO was associated with lower mortality risk (HR, 0.70; 95% CI, 0.57–0.84). This relationship was largely unchanged after adjustment for clinical factors (HR, 0.68; 95% CI, 0.55–0.84), hemodynamic parameters (HR, 0.68; 95% CI, 0.53−0.86), and spirometric impairment (HR, 0.69; 95% CI, 0.55–0.85). When DLCO was assessed using the 60% predicted threshold, patients with DLCO < 60% predicted had a substantially higher risk of mortality than those with DLCO ≥ 60% predicted, with HRs ranging from 6.90 to 7.80 across models. Exploratory analyses using alternative DLCO thresholds and ROC analysis of continuous DLCO values further supported the use of the 60% predicted cutoff for mortality prediction (Supplementary Table 1; Supplementary Figures 1 and 2). Restricted cubic spline modeling demonstrated a graded inverse association between DLCO and mortality, with no evidence of significant nonlinearity (Figure 5).

Discussion

In this multicenter cohort, we examined whether DLCO carried prognostic information in patients with PH‐FM, a rare form of pulmonary hypertension for which established risk‐stratification approaches remain limited. Baseline percent‐predicted DLCO was independently associated with all‐cause mortality, both when analyzed as a continuous variable and when categorized using the 60% predicted threshold. After adjustment for clinical, hemodynamic, and spirometric variables, each 10‐percentage‐point increase in percent‐predicted DLCO was associated with a 30% reduction in mortality risk. Consistently, categorizing DLCO at the 60% predicted threshold identified a lower‐DLCO group with markedly worse survival. This inverse association was broadly consistent across prespecified subgroups defined by sex, age, 6MWD, NT‐proBNP level, and WHO functional class. These findings extend the prognostic relevance of DLCO to PH‐FM and suggest that this simple, noninvasive physiological measure may complement existing approaches to risk assessment in this rare and clinically challenging condition.

Evidence from diverse cardiopulmonary cohorts indicates that DLCO is more than a routine pulmonary function measure. Lower DLCO has been linked to higher mortality in population‐based studies and in patients with cardiovascular disease, including those without a documented respiratory diagnosis [7, 8, 9, 12]. In COPD, DLCO < 60% predicted has been shown to identify patients at increased risk of all‐cause and respiratory mortality, including those with mild airflow obstruction [9]. Subsequent studies further suggest that DLCO provides prognostic information beyond conventional spirometric indices and may improve risk classification when incorporated into multidimensional assessment tools. Similarly, in interstitial lung diseases, lower baseline DLCO has been consistently associated with higher risks of death [10, 19]. Together, these studies suggest that impaired gas transfer reflects clinically relevant pathophysiological information that is not fully captured by spirometry or imaging alone.

In PH, DLCO also carries prognostic information. Although DLCO is often preserved or only mildly reduced in idiopathic pulmonary arterial hypertension (IPAH), marked impairment identifies a subgroup of patients with less favorable outcomes [20]. Trip et al. reported that patients with IPAH and DLCO < 45% predicted had substantially lower 3‐year survival than those with preserved DLCO, despite comparable hemodynamic profiles [21]. This observation suggests that DLCO may capture disease features not fully reflected by resting pulmonary hemodynamics alone. The prognostic relevance of DLCO has also been incorporated into contemporary risk assessment, as DLCO ≤ 40% predicted is included in the REVEAL 2.0 risk score [22]. In connective tissue disease‐associated PAH, especially systemic sclerosis‐associated PAH, reduced DLCO may reflect pulmonary vasculopathy, capillary loss, or coexisting parenchymal involvement, and has been associated with both the presence and future development of PH [23]. More recently, analyses from large systemic sclerosis cohorts have suggested that DLCO may provide prognostic information alongside established risk stratification tools. However, DLCO thresholds established for risk assessment in PAH may not be directly applicable to PH‐FM because the mechanisms underlying diffusion impairment differ between PAH and PH‐FM. Reduced DLCO in PH‐FM may arise from heterogeneous mechanisms, including pulmonary arterial and/or venous obstruction, regional perfusion abnormalities, and secondary parenchymal involvement. In the present study, exploratory analyses comparing alternative DLCO thresholds supported the potential clinical utility of the 60% predicted cutoff for risk stratification in PH‐FM.

Similar prognostic associations have been observed in other forms of PH. In chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension (CTEPH), reduced DLCO per unit of alveolar volume has been associated with poorer survival among medically treated patients, possibly reflecting peripheral microvascular disease in addition to proximal thromboembolic obstruction [24]. In PH due to heart failure, markedly reduced DLCO has been linked to higher mortality despite comparable baseline hemodynamic profiles, suggesting that impaired gas transfer may identify a clinically vulnerable subgroup [12]. Similar observations have been reported in mixed‐etiology PH cohorts, in which DLCO remained associated with survival after adjustment for concomitant lung disease [25]. Together, these findings support the concept that DLCO provides noninvasive information on pulmonary microvascular integrity and gas‐exchange impairment across PH phenotypes [26].

The mechanisms underlying the prognostic value of DLCO in PH are likely multifactorial. Beyond disease‐related mechanisms, DLCO may also be influenced by physiological factors, including hemoglobin concentration and altitude‐related physiological adaptation, which should be considered when interpreting DLCO values in clinical practice. DLCO is determined by both membrane diffusion and pulmonary capillary blood volume, and therefore reflects the structural and functional integrity of the alveolar‐capillary interface [27, 28]. In precapillary PH, reduced DLCO may result from pulmonary vascular remodeling, capillary rarefaction, impaired pulmonary capillary recruitment, or alveolar‐capillary membrane dysfunction, with or without coexisting parenchymal lung disease [29]. In the present study, DLCO was measured using hemoglobin‐corrected single‐breath techniques, which reduced potential variability related to hemoglobin concentration. Mohammad et al. reported that both percent‐predicted DLCO and hemoglobin‐corrected DLCO were associated with survival in PAH, and that this association persisted after adjustment for hemoglobin concentration [30]. These findings suggest that reduced DLCO in PAH is not simply explained by anemia, but may reflect underlying pulmonary vascular and gas‐exchange abnormalities. Consistent performance of DLCO across different PH phenotypes supports its role as an integrative physiological marker that captures disease severity beyond resting invasive hemodynamic measurements. In PH‐FM, this concept may be particularly relevant because extrinsic vascular compression can reduce effective pulmonary capillary blood volume and produce heterogeneous perfusion, both of which may contribute to impaired gas transfer.

Our study extends the prognostic relevance of DLCO to PH‐FM, a rare and anatomically distinct form of Group 5 PH. In PH‐FM, vascular obstruction is mainly caused by extrinsic compression from mediastinal fibrosis, rather than by primary remodeling of small pulmonary arteries. Despite this distinct mechanism, our findings suggest that DLCO remains closely related to disease severity and survival. Reduced DLCO in PH‐FM may reflect several overlapping processes, including decreased effective pulmonary capillary blood volume due to pulmonary arterial and/or venous compression, secondary microvascular dysfunction or capillary bed loss distal to major vascular obstruction, parenchymal abnormalities or atelectasis related to airway compression or lymphatic obstruction, and ventilation‐perfusion mismatch caused by heterogeneous pulmonary perfusion. The association between DLCO and mortality persisted after adjustment for mPAP, PVR, and spirometric measures, suggesting that DLCO captures aspects of gas‐exchange impairment and pulmonary vascular reserve that are not fully reflected by conventional hemodynamic or spirometric indices. This supports the concept that DLCO provides prognostic information complementary to standard clinical, hemodynamic, and pulmonary function assessments in PH‐FM [25].

This study benefited from a multicenter cohort drawn from 14 hospitals across 10 Chinese provinces, with PH‐FM confirmed using clinical, hemodynamic, and imaging data. Several limitations should nevertheless be noted. First, the retrospective design may have introduced selection bias. Second, DLCO was assessed only at baseline; therefore, we could not evaluate longitudinal changes in DLCO or their association with clinical outcomes. Serial DLCO measurements may provide additional prognostic information and should be explored in future studies. Third, unmeasured confounding may still have influenced the results, despite adjustment for predefined clinical, hemodynamic, and spirometric variables. Fourth, although the cohort was relatively large for PH‐FM, the number of deaths was modest, limiting the precision of subgroup analyses and the extent of covariate adjustment in multivariable models. Fifth, DLCO impairment may reflect different underlying mechanisms depending on the predominant pattern of vascular involvement or parenchymal abnormalities. Further studies are needed to clarify the prognostic implications of these different disease patterns. Sixth, retrospective assessment of specific causes of death was challenging because some patients died outside tertiary medical centers and complete terminal clinical information was not always available. Therefore, reliable classification of disease‐related versus non‐disease‐related mortality was not feasible in this retrospective cohort. Finally, all patients were recruited from China, where Mycobacterium tuberculosis is more commonly implicated in FM, whereas Histoplasma capsulatum is the dominant infectious association in many Western populations. Differences in demographic characteristics and geographic distribution of PH‐FM across regions may also influence the generalizability of our findings. Although the mechanisms leading to PH‐FM may be broadly similar, external validation in other ethnic and etiologic populations is needed.

In conclusion, in this multicenter cohort of patients with PH‐FM, lower baseline percent‐predicted DLCO was independently associated with a higher risk of all‐cause mortality. These findings suggest that DLCO, a readily available and noninvasive physiological measure, may serve as a useful adjunct for risk stratification in this rare disease.
