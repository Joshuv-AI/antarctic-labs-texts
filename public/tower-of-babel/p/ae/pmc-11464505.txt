A shift in substrate requirement might cause speciation of the lichenized fungi, Varicellaria hemisphaerica and V. lactea (Pertusariales, Ascomycota)
Authors: Magdalena Kosecka, Magdalena Oset
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Proper species recognition is required to correctly estimate species preferences and their vulnerability or for eco-evolutionary inference. Varicellaria hemisphaerica and Varicellaria lactea are almost completely morphologically homogeneous species with unclear identification features. To evaluate the importance of morphological, chemical, and ecological characteristics used in recognition of these species, we tested 670 specimens, of which 42 were analyzed phylogenetically using nucITS rDNA, SSU rDNA, and LSU rDNA markers. This integrated taxonomical approach showed that V. hemisphaerica is distinct from V. lactea, and that substrate requirements, together with phylogenetic differences and the size of soredia, differentiate these species. The chemical composition of secondary lichen metabolites in both analyzed species showed similar variation and, therefore, this feature is not diagnostic in species recognition, although suggested by previous studies. The potential speciation of the two species seems to be caused by the shift in the substrate requirements.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-74937-w.

Introduction

Nowadays, research on species diversity, especially the boundaries between them, integrates the traditional approach with genetic and ecological data and uses computational models and empirical species determination1–4. This integrated taxonomical approach is beneficial when traditional taxonomy is insufficient for exceptional lichenological cases like, for example, the genus Cora Fr.5, the family Teloschistaceae6,7, or the genus Peltigera Willd8. Unfortunately, morphological characters commonly used to designate species often appear to be insufficient in the context of molecular separation of taxa, e.g., in the Pectenia complex (Pannariaceae), where the production of asexual propagules and sexual structures does not characterize monophyletic groups9. However, the Pectenia complex includes two morphologically well-characterized main lineages. One of them is composed of two sub-lineages that are a case of cryptic speciation explained by a biogeographic pattern and potential geographical barrier that caused the speciation of two species: P. plumbea (Lightf.) P.M. Jørg., L. Lindblom, Wedin & S. Ekman, and P. atlantica (Degel.) P.M. Jørg., L. Lindblom, Wedin & S. Ekman, that are otherwise morphologically identical9.

On the other hand, the morphological features may be so complex that they are overlooked, like in the genus Xanthoparmelia (Vain.) Hale (Parmeliaceae), which is known as the ‘ultra-diverse’ group and consists of approximately 820 species10. In a comprehensive phylogenetic study, including diverse, geographically broad sampling, several morphologically and chemically various taxa were nested within this genus, even though they were previously assigned to very different genera, like Karoowia Hale, Omphalodiella Henssen, and Placoparmelia Henssen, which suggests low morphological diagnostic variation within the genus10. Without a complex approach, the revision would be impossible.

A more complicated case would be the absence of any features useful as diagnostics. This kind of situation occurred, for example, in the assessment of boundaries between species in the Parmotrema reticulatum complex (Parmeliaceae)11 and the Thamnolia vermicularis complex (Icmadophilaceae)12. The above examples emphasize that species recognition requires immense work to reach the proper explanation, often involving a complete revision of the genus10,13. At the same time, the proposed solution may only prove to be temporary.

Particularly valuable are studies allowing the identification of mechanisms of divergence of lichenized fungi populations14. They mainly concern diversification events caused by prolonged climate change15–17. Much simpler to capture and helpful in species delimitation is the observation of the speciation of direct lichen traits, for example, substrate preferences in the genus Blastenia A. Massal. (Teloschistaceae) with 6 infrageneric groups, always epiphytic species in the Hungarica and Herbidella groups, or the epilithic Festivella group13. However, Blastenia festivella (Nyl.) Vondrák (of the Festivella group) was also exceptionally epiphytic13. Another example applies to the genus Micarea Fr., in which the shift in substrate requirement from bark to wood leads to a different reproduction mode and becomes a speciation factor18.

Another aspect that may be crucial in species recognition is identifying the photosynthetic partner (photobiont) type. The existence of the photosymbiodems, the cyanobacterial and green algal morphs of the same lichenized fungi, is easily recognizable due to differences in the color and/or morphology of the thallus caused by the photobiont type19. The change in the type of photobiont, Trebouxiophyceae, Trentepohliaceae, or cyanobacterial, is mostly attributed to adaptation to changes in the lichen habitat20. Furthermore, some lichen genera can adopt different algal and cyanobacterial partners as photobionts, even simultaneously21–24. However, the importance of this feature to species recognition may be crucial, like in the case of Lecanographa amylacea (Ehrh. ex Pers.) Egea & Torrente, which can form morphologically distinct sexual and asexual thalli with Trentepohlia Mart. and Trebouxia Puymaly photobionts, respectively25.

The genus Varicellaria Nyl. (Varicellariaceae, Pertusariales, Ascomycota) currently comprises 12 species26–29. This genus is characterized by disciform apothecia (but they are not known in every species), non-amyloid hymenial gel, strongly amyloid asci, 1–2-spored asci, and 1- or 2-celled ascospores with more or less thick, single layer walls. Chemically, lecanoric acid is the major metabolite29,30. Varicellaria was described by Nylander31 for one species, V. microsticta Nyl., which was considered synonymous with the Pertusaria rhodocarpa Körb. by Fries26, who made the new combination Varicellaria rhodocarpa (Körb.) Th. Fr. The number of lichen species within the genus has increased as a result of molecular studies of the Pertusariales conducted by Schmitt and Lumbsch30. However, some of them have a very restricted distribution. For example, V. philippina (Vain.) I. Schmitt & Lumbsch, is only known from the Philippines32 and Papua New Guinea33, and V. culbersonii (Vězda) Schmitt & Lumbsch was reported from high altitudes in Central America27,30. Only V. hemisphaerica (Flörke) Schmitt & Lumbsch and V. lactea (Flörke) Schmitt & Lumbsch have wider geographical distributions and have been reported mostly from Europe, but also Asia and North America [e.g., 27,28,34–36. These two species are both sorediate and identical in morphology, and produce lecanoric acid. According to the determination key to the species of Varicellaria presented by Schmitt et al.27, they are possible to separate by the presence of variolaric acid in V. lactea, which is absent in V. hemisphaerica. Hanko37 and Chambers et al.34 presented a similar point of view. However, some other authors consider V. hemisphaerica to contain both lecanoric and sometimes variolaric acids, with the difference being in the type of substrate preferred by these species (V. hemisphaerica being corticolous and V. lactea being saxicolous)38. These discrepancies may suggest that either one species with variable chemistry and habitat preferences is involved or that the secondary chemistry or the substrate preference plays a crucial role in the separation of species. These incompatibilities led us to proceed with more extensive research on these two lichen species.

We present a comprehensive study of the morphology, chemistry, phylogeny, photobiont identity, and ecology of Varicellaria hemisphaerica and V. lactea. The main goals of the study were to discover whether (1) both taxa represent a single species or (2) the type of substrate, secondary chemistry or the size of soredia allows the delimitation of these two taxa.

Results

Morphology and chemistry

In this study, 670 specimens of Varicellaria were studied, of which 427 were corticolous and 243 were saxicolous (Table S1). Both groups were very similar in morphology. The corticolous specimens were characterized by a pale bluish-grey tinged thallus (often becoming pink in the herbarium), which was smooth and thin or cracked and thick, with convex soralia, 40–100 μm diam, paler or concolorous with the thallus (also often pinkish as the thallus in the herbarium). Saxicolous specimens were characterized by whitish grey, often rimose thallus, and slightly convex, discrete pure white soralia, 100–200 μm diam. None of the specimens had apothecia. In the case of the chemistry in all specimens, lecanoric acid was detected, while the presence of variolaric acid was noted in only 20 corticolous and 203 saxicolous specimens (Table S1).

Phylogenetic analyses

We successfully generated 42 nucITS rDNA, 15 mtSSU rDNA, 25 nucLSU rDNA fungal sequences, and 13 nucITS rDNA algal sequences (Table S2). The presented Bayesian phylogenetic tree attributed studied specimens of Varicellaria to two well-supported clades (PP = 1, ML = 100, Fig. 1). The first clade (PP = 0.96, ML = 93) clusters sequences obtained from specimens originating from the bark of trees, with soredia of 40–100 μm diam; while the second clade (PP < 0.75, ML = 93) consists of exclusively saxicolous specimens with soredia of 110–180 μm diam (Fig. 1; larger soredia were also found but in specimens not included in the phylogeny). Two chemotypes were identified in each clade: (1) with only lecanoric acid and (2) with lecanoric and variolaric acids. Furthermore, the specimens with the second variant are scattered across the tree and do not form a separate phylogenetic lineage. The ABGD analyses based on the nucITS rDNA dataset, as well as haplotype TCS network analysis based on the nucITS rDNA and nucLSU rDNA dataset (Figure S1 and S2), supported the separation of the sequences set into separate OTUs representing different species, the corticolous material referring to Varicellaria hemisphaerica and the saxicolous representing V. lactea. The remaining molecular marker (mtSSU rDNA) appeared to be insufficient for the species separation (data not shown).

In the case of photobionts, based on the presented Bayesian phylogenetic tree (Figure S3), twelve Varicellaria hemisphaerica specimens and one V. lactea were associated with the Trebouxia sp. OTU A03.

Statistical analyses

Welch’s two-sample t-test revealed that the chemotypes and size of soredia are statistically important variables to differentiate Varicellaria hemisphaerica and V. lactea (Table S3). Similar results we obtained in dbRDA analysis (Table S4). Furthermore, the values of fourteen bioclimatic variables of localities in which V. hemisphaerica and V. lactea occurred, were statistically important (Welch’s two-sample t-test; Table S3). In a comparison of the two chemotypes of V. hemisphaerica (chemotype I with lecanoric acid vs. chemotype II with lecanoric and variolaric acid), eight bioclimatic variables were statistically different. While, the values of bioclimatic variables of two chemotypes of V. lactea showed no difference. The same result was observed when comparing the chemotype II (with lecanoric and variolaric acid) of V. hemisphaerica and V. lactea. The importance of the type of substrate was not tested due to the representation of only two features - corticolous for V. hemisphaerica and saxicolous for V. lactea.

The variation partitioning analysis allowed us to estimate the impact of the above variables on the genetic diversity of the studied species. The substrate type explained the highest proportion of the variability (0.18), the chemotype the second highest (0.13), and the size of soredia the third highest (0.1; Fig. 2). However, the climate explained nothing by itself; it showed high cross-correlation with the above variables.

Discussion

So far, chemistry has most often been regarded as a main diagnostic feature that distinguishes Varicellaria hemisphaerica and V. lactea27,29,30,34,37,39–42. The thallus of the type specimen of V. hemisphaerica (isotype BM–4111!) was reported to contain lecanoric acid only, and the thallus of V. lactea (neotype UPS–201911!) both lecanoric and variolaric acids. Our study confirmed that the variolaric acid previously linked with V. lactea cannot be treated as diagnostic because this secondary metabolite was also identified in several specimens of V. hemisphaerica, and its presence is scattered across the phylogenetic tree in both species. The presence of variolaric acid in the thalli of Varicellaria hemisphaerica and V. lactea suggests that this particular secondary metabolite may be related to adaptation to the habitats in which both species may occur. This is confirmed statistically by detecting significant differences in bioclimatic variables of specimens with and without variolaric acid.

It was previously shown that the different chemical composition of the lichen thalli may be associated with differences in habitat conditions, for example, the Ramalina farinacea complex, in which a rare chemotype containing cyclographin was identified on the island of Sicily, Italy43. However, according to the authors, it is possible that this chemotype has been previously overlooked due to the use of less sensitive survey techniques. Interestingly, the Sicilian chemotype was morphologically different, exhibiting a long-stranded growth compared to the more tufted thallus typical of the common protocetraric acid chemotype. However, different chemotypes within Varicellaria hemisphaerica or V. lactea showed no variation in thallus morphology. The presence of variolaric acid in the examined Varicellaria specimens may be related to protection from sunlight. In the genus Ochrolechia A. Massal. within the parella group, variolaric acid was observed to be produced in the outer parts of the thallus cortex44–46. On the basis of the results obtained in the mentioned study, it could be concluded that variolaric acid affords specific photoprotection of photobiont cells from excessive sun radiation. Varicellaria hemisphaerica is a species that usually grows in shaded forests, and perhaps that is why it does not produce variolaric acid so often. On the other hand, V. lactea mostly grows in brighter conditions. Further studies require a more sophisticated estimation to establish the relation between light conditions and the presence of variolaric acid. Beyond that, the precise substrate type should be considered, as it may be correlated with the composition of thallus secondary metabolites47.

It was reported that soredia of V. hemisphaerica range between 40 and 100 μm in diam., while V. lactea (50–) are 100–160 (–220) µm in diam. and sometimes aggregate into consoredia34,48. According to our study of 184 specimens, the diameter of the soredia ranged between 40 and 100 μm in V. hemisphaerica and between 110 and 180 μm in V. lactea, so the observed difference in the size of soredia may be an important feature to distinguish these two species. The discrepancy of the diameter measurements in the literature, in comparison to our study, comes from the consideration of corticolous specimens with smaller soredia (50 μm), with lecanoric and variolaric acid as Varicellaria lactea. This is likely fallacious, due to the fact that V. hemisphaerica, as shown in our study, grows on bark and may also have variolaric acid.

Furthermore, the size of soredia in studied specimens is quite variable. However, the appearance of soredia in many species may depend on the age of the thallus and the rate of its development49,50, but also on environmental factors. In the case of Chrysothrix candelaris (L.) J.R. Laundon (Laundon 1981), the soredium-like granules were reported as large, 75–200 μm in diam. in comparison with Ch. xanthina (Vain.) Kalb51, but other authors reported them to be much smaller, 50–75 μm in diam52. In the revision of specimens from Poland, it was suggested that the size discrepancies may be caused by environmental conditions53. In that example, the size of soredia was not utilized as a main morphological feature in the species delimitation. In most cases, the size of the soredia is not the only diagnostic feature, for example, Candelariella rubrisoli D. Liu & J.-S. Hur has larger soredia in comparison to C. xanthostigmoides (Müll. Arg.) R. W. Rogers, but also irregular subsquamulose thallus, while C. makarevichiae S. Y. Kondr., L. Lőkös & J.-S. Hur has smaller soredia (20–30 μm in diam), but also a subsquamulose thallus composed of discrete, scattered areoles/squamules, not forming the continuous leprose crust49,50. In this study, we considered this feature as diagnostic, but with caution in parallel to the substrate requirement and the phylogeny.

Varicellaria lactea rarely forms apothecia48, but all studied specimens by us were sterile. This feature can potentially be used as the second morphological diagnostic feature in the V. hemisphaerica vs. V. lactea species delimitation48, however, due to the very occasional presence of this structure, it has very little value.

While Varicellaria lactea was always been noted as a saxicolous species48,54,55, V. hemisphaerica was mostly noted as corticolous54,55, but sometimes as ‘almost always on bark’48. According to our studies, which considered extensive sample size, the above statement may be misleading. The previous delimitations were probably based on the presence of only lecanoric acid in specimens from rock surfaces, which was linked to the direct delimitation of V. hemisphaerica. Our study indicates that such specimens have been erroneously labeled and should be correctly delimited as V. lactea.

From all the above features, the one that facilitates delimitation of the studied species is substrate preference – the bark of trees in the case of V. hemisphaerica and rocks in the case of V. lactea. We suggest that the transition of substrate might have caused the speciation into two species. Both species were almost the same in terms of morphology. The studied specimens showed only a difference in the size of soredia, which may potentially be associated with the habitat change. The phylogenetic signals strongly suggest they represent two different taxa. Furthermore, this change did not involve a switch in the photobiont type, as both Varicellaria species were associated with Trebouxia sp. OTU A03 (however, we were able to recover only one photobiont sequence from V. lactea). The changes of the Trebouxia photobionts, are common even within the species, especially between specimens of the same species, but from different substrata56,57. It is a mechanism that allows new niches to be adopted and inhabited. This phenomenon has been shown to occur throughout lichen evolution58–62. Therefore, our results suggest that a new niche to which a given species has moved, either V. hemisphaerica or V. lactea, was (1) rich in preferable photobionts and (2) might have similar environmental conditions.

Classical taxonomy methods assumed that taxa should diverge phenotypically. Phenotypic and chemical divergence, no less, may have lagged behind phylogenetic divergence, which may have led to speciation. This could be due, for example, to the fact that the taxa may have diverged recently, which has not yet become apparent in phenotypic divergence, or the changes in phenotype have been inhibited by the action of some factors63. According to the literature64–68), for some groups of lichens, the lack of distinct phenotypic characters and chemical variation with confirmed phylogenetic variation leads to the description of cryptic species. According to Vondrák et al.69, species that cannot be classified based on morphological and chemical variation but differ in distribution and ecological requirements should be defined as semi-cryptic63,68,70–72. The occurrence of semi-cryptic species in lichens is a fairly common phenomenon. It has so far been confirmed, for example, within the genus Lepraria Ach., in which Lepraria cryptovouauxii Kukwa, Flakus & Guzow-Krzemińska was described as a semi-cryptic species similar to L. vouauxii (Hue) R.C. Harris68. Both taxa are practically indistinguishable morphologically and chemically, but differ in their range of occurrence (L. cryptovouauxii occurs in South America and L. vouauxii in the Northern Hemisphere), and show significant differences in phylogeny68. At first, we considered both studied species of Varicellaria to be semi-cryptic, as the strictest feature was the substrate type. However, the studies of the soredia revealed that its size is an important but possible environment-dependent diagnostic feature73.

In summary, Varicellaria hemisphaerica and V. lactea are an example of a species pair, in which substrate requirements may have caused speciation and the substrate can serve as the feature that distinguishes two species in the simplest way. The size of the soredia, and the phylogenetic signal are parallel features that require more thorough consideration and resources. Furthermore, the variability of secondary metabolites may respond to particular environmental conditions, but a more detailed chemistry study using HPLC is required for further conclusions.

Materials and methods

Taxon sampling

Specimens from the following herbaria were used B, H, HBG, KRAM, KRAP, OLS, POZ, SLTC, TRN, UGDA, UPS, WA, WRLS (Table S1). Due to limited access to fresh materials, only 42 specimens were selected for molecular study.

Morphology and chemistry

All specimens’ morphological and anatomical characteristics were studied using a stereo microscope (Nikon SMZ-800) and compound microscope (Zeiss Axioskope 2). All specimens were examined to characterize thallus shape and color, and the presence or absence of reproductive structures. Soredia were measured using a light microscope equipped with a micrometer graduated eyepiece. Soredia were measured for specimens that were loaned for study, but were not measured when the study was carried out in situ in herbaria where there was no access to a stereoscopic microscope equipped with a micrometer scale eyepiece. A spot test was performed on the thallus, medulla, and soredia, and the color reaction was observed under a compound microscope. Thin-layer chromatography (TLC; solvents A and C) was performed to detect the secondary metabolites74.

Molecular methods

DNA from 42 specimens was extracted directly from pieces of thalli by a modified CTAB method75 or using the Plant and Fungi DNA Purification Kit (EURx, Poland). Three molecular markers were selected for lichenized fungi identification. The nucITS rDNA was amplified using: ITS1F76, ITS477; mtSSU rDNA by: mrSSU1, mrSSU3R78; and nucLSU rDNA by: LR379 and LROR80. In the case of photobiont identification, the nucITS rDNA was amplified using nr-SSU-1780-5’62 or a-nu-ssu-1752-581 and ITS477. Amplification was performed in a total volume of 25 µl containing 9.5 µl of water, 12 µl of Start-Warm HS-PCR Mix Polymerase (A&A Biotechnology, Poland), 1 µl of each primer (0.5 µM) and 1.5 µl of DNA template. Amplifications were performed using the following programs: for fungal nucITS rDNA: initial denaturation at 94 °C for 3 min, followed by 35 cycles of 94 °C for 30 s, 54 °C for 30 s, 72 °C for 1 min, and final elongation at 72 °C for 10 min; for algal nucITS rDNA the primer annealing temperature was set to 50 °C; for mt SSU rDNA marker: initial denaturation at 94 °C for 3 min, followed by 35 cycles of 94 °C for 40 s, 54 °C for 45 s, 72 °C for 1 min 10 s, and final elongation at 72 °C for 10 min; for nucLSU rRNA initial denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 35 cycles of 95 °C for 1 min, 54 °C for 1 min 30 s, 72 °C for 1 min, and final elongation at 72 °C for 7 min. The PCR products were purified using a DNA purification kit Clean Up (A&A Biotechnology, Poland). Each PCR product was sequenced in Macrogen (Amsterdam, Netherlands) using the PCR primers.

Phylogenetic analyses

First, sequence alignments were performed separately for each molecular marker, using multiple alignment using fast Fourier transform (MAFFT)82, with quality estimation using Guidance2 server83–85. The Guidance confidence scores were calculated, and columns with a score < 0.93 were removed from the alignments. In the case of fungi, we conducted the phylogenetic analyses on concatenated sequences of three selected markers, nucITS rDNA, mtSSU rDNA, and nucLSU rDNA, using Varicellaria velata (Turner) I. Schmitt & Lumbsch as an outgroup. For algae, we used nucITS rDNA and rbcL gene reference of Trebouxia A to delimit properly photobiont OTU associated with Varicellaria, following Muggia et al.86.

The best-fit evolutionary model was selected for the concatenated dataset based on the Akaike information criterion implemented in MrModeltest 2.087. The GTR + I + G model was selected to infer the phylogenetic relationship of fungi and algae. We applied Bayesian inference in MrBayes v.3.2.288 on the CIPRES Science Gateway89. Two parallel MCMC runs were performed, using four independent heated chains and five million generations, sampling every 1,000th tree. The initial 1,250 trees of each run (25%) were discarded as burn-in, and posterior probabilities were estimated by constructing a majority-rule consensus tree of all sampled post-burn trees. Next, the maximum likelihood (ML) analyses were performed on the IQ-Tree web server90, using ultrafast bootstrap (UFBoot91) with 1,000 bootstrap replications. Phylogenetic trees were visualized using FigTree v. 1.4.292.

Species delimitation

To delimit Varicellaria hemisphaerica and V. lactea, the Automatic Barcode Gap Discovery method (ABGD) was used, following Leavitt et al.93. The analyses were performed separately for the nucITS rDNA using the webserver (https://bioinfo.mnhn.fr/abi/public/abgd/abgdweb.html). The Jukes and Cantor (JC69) model was applied to calculate the genetic distance. Pmin = 0.001, Pmax = 0.01, step = 10, Nb bins = 20 and X (relative gap width) = 0.5 were used. Furthermore, the TCS network analysis94, performed in PopArt95, based on the nucITS rDNA and nucLSU rDNA dataset was performed.

Statistical analyses

To test the variables that potentially differentiate Varicellaria hemisphaerica and V. lactea, we performed Welch’s two-sample t-test, using function t.test in R. We first contrasted the chemotypes of both species, then the size of soredia. Next, we contrasted the climatic variables in which the studied species occurred, to determine whether the presence of variolaric acid is associated with particular climatic conditions; (a) Varicellaria hemisphaerica vs.. V. lactea, (b) V. hemisphaerica chemotype I vs. V. hemisphaerica chemotype II, (c) V. lactea chemotype I vs. V. lactea chemotype II, (d) V. hemisphaerica chemotype II vs. V. lactea chemotype II. Climatic data were obtained from the Global Climate Data-WorldClim Version 296 at a resolution of 2.5 arc minutes. To perform the bioclimatic analyses, we reduced the repeating records in the dataset, using 358 Varicellaria specimens (Table S4). All analyses were performed in R v 3.6.082, using RStudio v.1.2.133597.

We performed variation partitioning in redundancy analysis [following 98 to reveal the correlation between the substrate type, chemotypes, size of soredia, and climatic conditions with the phylogenetic variability of obtained Varicellaria nuITS sequences. We ran several variation partitioning analyses using different descriptive variables. Principal coordinates of neighbor matrices vectors (PCNM) representing the geographical distances at various spatial scales99 were obtained from the transformation of geographical distance values (latitude and longitude). Distance-based redundancy analysis (dbRDA)100 was used to select statistically significant predictors to explain variation for each of the data sets used in the variation partitioning analysis (Table S5).

We performed the PCA analyses of 19 Bioclim variables to detect and visualize the differential ordination of samples in the hyperspace. We performed this analysis jointly for all data and separately for each Varicellaria species. We grouped the results depending on (1) Varicellaria species assignment and (2) chemotypes.
