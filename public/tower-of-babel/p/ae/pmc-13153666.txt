Impact of Low Vision on Wound Healing in Patients With Chronic Limb-Threatening Ischemia
Authors: Eiji Karashima, Keiichiro Kishikawa, Shioto Yasuda, Takeo Kaneko
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLow vision is often observed in patients with chronic limb-threatening ischemia (CLTI) and tissue loss. However, the impact of low vision on wound healing in these patients has not been previously described. We aimed to investigate the relationship between low vision and the wound healing rate in CLTI patients with tissue loss.Methods and ResultsA total of 74 CLTI patients with de novo tissue loss who underwent endovascular therapy between January 2017 and December 2022 was enrolled in this study. The patients were divided into 2 groups based on the National Eye Institute’s criteria for low vision: a low vision group with 24 patients, and a normal vision group with 50 patients. The primary endpoint was the 2-year wound healing rate. The rates of diabetes and renal disease requiring hemodialysis were significantly higher in the low vision group. The 2-year wound healing rate was significantly lower in the low vision group than in the normal vision group (33.3% vs 76.0%; P<0.001). In multivariate analysis, low vision showed an independent association with wound healing outcomes.ConclusionsThe wound healing rate was lower in CLTI patients with low vision than in those without low vision. Low vision should be considered a factor associated with wound healing outcomes in CLTI patients.

Central Figure

Recently, the number of patients diagnosed with lower-extremity artery disease (LEAD) has been increasing.1 Chronic limb-threatening ischemia (CLTI), an advanced form of LEAD, is characterized by chronic lower-limb hypoperfusion that causes ischemic rest pain or tissue loss, including non-healing ulceration and gangrene.1,2 The prognosis for patients with CLTI is poor, with a high risk of lower-extremity amputation.1–4

The primary goal of treating CLTI with tissue loss is to achieve wound healing. To accomplish this, revascularization including endovascular treatment (EVT) is considered first-line therapy to restore blood flow to the affected lower extremities.1,2,5,6 However, successful revascularization alone is not sufficient to improve clinical outcomes in CLTI patients with tissue loss. Early diagnosis and a multidisciplinary approach are crucial for the effective management of tissue loss and for achieving better outcomes.

Various factors have been reported to influence wound healing in CLTI patients with tissue loss.1–8 Wounds, ischemia, and foot infections (WIfI) are the 3 primary factors affecting wound healing in CLTI.3 The Global Vascular Guidelines suggested an additional 3 factors, including patient risk, lower limb severity, and anatomical complexity of vascular lesions.2 Patient risk factors include advanced age, chronic kidney disease, coronary artery disease, congestive heart failure, diabetes, smoking, cerebrovascular disease, tissue loss, body mass index, dementia, frailty, and functional status.2

Low vision is a visual impairment that makes it difficult to perform daily activities.9–11 Daily observation and wound care in the lower extremities tend to be neglected, particularly in CLTI patients with low vision. However, to the best of our knowledge, the effect of low vision in CLTI patients has not been previously discussed.

This study aimed to investigate the impact of low vision on wound healing in CLTI patients with tissue loss who underwent successful EVT.

Methods

Study Design

This was a single-center, retrospective, observational study. First, patients with acute limb ischemia, anastomotic stenosis after bypass surgery, or a history of major amputation were excluded from the study. After that, we identified 106 adult CLTI patients (age ≥20 years) with de novo tissue loss, including ischemic non-healing ulcers and gangrene of the lower extremities (Rutherford category 5–6), who underwent EVT between January 2017 and December 2022 at Shimonoseki City Hospital. Eighteen patients who survived but underwent less than 6 months of follow up were excluded. At the time of EVT, 12 patients had a history of ophthalmic consultation, of whom 9 had low vision and 3 had normal vision. Among the remaining patients, medical records at the time of EVT clearly indicated that 12 had low vision and 22 had normal vision. In addition, physicians asked patients who visited between January 2023 and December 2024 whether they could clearly see the shape of their toenails. Based on the answer to this question, the visual status at the time of EVT could be additionally determined for 28 patients, of which 3 had low vision and 25 had normal vision. Fourteen cases with no history of ophthalmology visits, unclear vision status based on medical records, and no visits between January 2023 and December 2024 were excluded as unknown vision status. Ultimately, 74 patients were analyzed retrospectively. The patients were divided into 2 groups: a low vision group with 24 patients, and a normal vision group with 50 patients (Figure 1). Detailed information and baseline clinical data were collected at the initial visit for tissue loss. Wound severity was assessed using both the Rutherford classification and the WIfI classification.2,3 In addition, all vascular lesions were categorized according to the Global Limb Anatomic Staging System (GLASS) classification.2 The WIfI and GLASS stages were determined by at least 2 vascular specialists. The study protocol was developed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Shimonoseki City Hospital. All patients provided written informed consent for EVT. Due to the retrospective, observational nature of the study, the requirement for informed consent was waived. However, patients were given the opportunity to opt out of the study. Relevant information about the study has been made publicly available in accordance with the Ethical Guidelines for Medical and Health Research Involving Human Subjects.

Study Endpoints

The primary endpoint of the study was wound healing. Secondary endpoints included overall survival, amputation-free survival, freedom from major adverse limb events (MALE), limb salvage (freedom from major amputation), and the incidence of new-onset tissue loss at 2 years. Additionally, 2-year outcomes for minor amputation, major amputation, and new-onset tissue loss were evaluated separately for the leg with the worse wound and the contralateral leg. Factors associated with wound healing were also analyzed by comparing the low vision and normal vision groups. We selected the 2-year wound healing rate as the primary endpoint because wound healing after EVT in CLTI frequently requires prolonged follow up, and shorter windows (e.g., 12 months) may underestimate delayed but clinically meaningful healing.

Definitions

Low vision was defined as a visual impairment that makes it difficult to perform daily activities. Patients whose visual impairment could be corrected with glasses, contact lenses, or standard medical or surgical treatment were excluded from the low vision group.9 We used the National Eye Institute’s criteria for low vision, as these are based on clinical judgment, can be applied more easily in clinical practice, and do not require an ophthalmologic examination.9 In this study, blind patients were included in the low vision group. Additionally, low vision was defined as the inability to clearly see the shape of one’s own toenails. This visual status meets the National Eye Institute’s criteria for low vision. Moreover, we have obtained approval for this visual status as the definition of low vision by the Japanese Society for Low-vision Research and Rehabilitation.

Family or caregiver support was defined as the presence of a cohabiting family member or a caregiver who was involved in daily care, as documented in the medical records at the time of EVT. Procedural success of EVT was defined as residual stenosis of <50% in the aortoiliac and femoropopliteal segments and achievement of at least 1 straight-line flow to the foot in the below-the-knee segment. The follow-up period was defined as 2 years from the date of the first visit with tissue loss. Tissue loss was diagnosed by a vascular specialist when a patient had a non-healing lower-limb wound lasting longer than 2 weeks or gangrene, along with an ankle-brachial index of <0.9 and/or skin perfusion pressure of <40 mmHg. If the wound was only present on 1 leg, then that leg was designated as the worse wound leg. In cases with bilateral wounds on admission, the worse wound leg was defined according to the WIfI stage and wound size. Major amputation was defined as surgical excision of the limb above the ankle. Minor amputation was defined as amputation below the ankle of the index limb. Wound healing was defined as complete epithelialization of the reference tissue loss without death or major amputation. New-onset tissue loss was defined as the development of tissue loss in legs previously without tissue loss, or the recurrence of tissue loss after complete healing in legs with prior tissue loss. MALE was defined as a composite of major amputation or any reintervention during the study period.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous data are presented as mean±standard deviation, and categorical data are presented as number (percentage). Continuous variables were compared between the groups using an unpaired t-test or the Mann-Whitney U test, as appropriate. Categorical variables were compared using the Fisher’s exact test. Time-to-event outcomes (including wound healing and new-onset tissue loss) were analyzed using the Kaplan-Meier method and compared using the log-rank test. Associations between baseline characteristics and wound healing were evaluated using univariate and multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression models, and hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported. A 2-tailed P value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 27 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Adjuvant Therapies

During the study, Rheocarna, a novel apheresis device for no-option CLTI or poor-response CLTI after revascularization,12 was used in 3 patients in the low vision group, but in none of the patients in the normal vision group. Other adjuvant therapies, including hyperbaric oxygen therapy, spinal cord stimulation, and lumbar sympathetic ganglion block, were not performed in any patients.

Clinical Outcomes at 2 Years

There was no significant difference in the follow-up period between the low and normal vision groups (617±168 vs. 605±202 days; P=0.801). Among the patients included in this study, none underwent bypass graft surgery or hybrid revascularization during the follow-up period. Clinical outcomes, including wound healing, overall survival, amputation-free survival, freedom from MALE, limb salvage, and new-onset tissue loss, are summarized in Figure 2. At 2 years, Kaplan-Meier analysis showed a significant difference in the wound healing rate between the low and normal vision groups (33.3% [95% CI 23.7–42.9%] vs. 76.0% [95% CI 70.0–82.0%]; P<0.001; Figure 2A). No significant difference was observed in overall survival (77.2% [95% CI 68.2–86.2%] vs. 79.2% [95% CI 73.3–85.1%]; P=0.972; Figure 2B). However, significant differences were noted in amputation-free survival (45.8% [95% CI 35.6–56.0%] vs. 69.2% [95% CI 62.6–75.8%]; P=0.048), freedom from MALE (18.8% [95% CI 10.2–27.4%] vs. 43.4% [95% CI 35.9–50.9%]; P=0.049), limb salvage (52.9% [95% CI 42.5–63.3%] vs. 89.4% [95% CI 84.9–93.9%]; P<0.001), and new-onset tissue loss (58.3% [95% CI 48.2–68.4%] vs. 10.0% [95% CI 5.8–14.2%]; P<0.001; Figure 2C–F) at 2 years. Discussion

Given the retrospective and observational design, these findings should be interpreted as associative rather than causal. This study clearly demonstrated that the wound healing rate in CLTI patients with tissue loss was significantly lower in the low vision group than in the normal vision group at 2 years. In addition, low vision was identified as a factor associated with wound healing outcomes in the multivariate analysis. Because this was a retrospective observational study, these findings should be interpreted as an association and not as evidence of causality. The higher incidence of major amputation in low vision patients may have contributed to be the significant differences in amputation-free survival and incidence of MALE. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first report to investigate the relationship between low vision and wound healing in CLTI patients.

Low vision is a visual impairment that makes it difficult to perform daily activities.9–11 Patients with low vision have greater difficulty than others in observing and managing wounds on their legs. However, the concept of low vision is not yet widely recognized among physicians who perform EVT. One reason is that the most widely known diagnostic criteria for low vision, established by the World Health Organization, require an ophthalmologic examination.10,11 Although there were differences in definition between the World Health Organization and the National Eye Institute, the concept of low vision was almost the same. These reports stated that low vision has been linked to a decline in quality of life, increases the experience of social vulnerability, and worsening of social determinants of health.9–11

Wound healing is one of the primary goals of treatment for patients with tissue loss. Various factors have been reported to be associated with wound healing in these patients.6–8,12–15 EVT success has been reported to be associated with wound healing; however, in the present study, although not statistically significant, the low vision group tended to show lower EVT success rates. Despite the recognized importance of successful below-the-knee EVT for wound healing, no significant differences were observed in EVT success rates or post-procedural run-off vessel counts. The tendency to exacerbate lower-extremity wounds, including recurrence and the development of tissue loss, has also been considered a factor affecting wound healing.7,16 Based on the results regarding new-onset tissue loss in our study, as shown in Figure 2F and Table 3, patients with low vision may be more likely to develop wounds on their legs. Patients with low vision are more prone to bumping their toes or injuring their skin while cutting their toenails. In addition, reduced adherence to wound care and delayed recognition of infection may contribute to tissue loss or its recurrence. However, the exact mechanism underlying the occurrence of tissue loss in patients with low vision remains unclear.

WIfI stage 4 also remained as a significant predictor of wound healing. This result is acceptable because the WIfI classification was developed based on global results as an appropriate classification system to risk stratify patients with CLTI and support clinical decision-making.3 In this study, no significant difference was observed in the baseline WIfI stages between the low vision and normal vision group. Therefore, the relationship between the vision status and the WIfI stages could not be examined.

Recently, the relationship between social isolation and wound healing in CLTI patients has been reported.17 In addition, low vision is one of the causes of social isolation.9,10 It is well known that achieving complete wound healing often takes a very long time. Indeed, it was reported that only 50% of patients achieved complete wound healing 3 months after EVT.13 Therefore, family support plays an important role in wound healing.16,17 In addition to self-care limitations, inadequate wound care support may mediate the association between low vision and delayed wound healing. For example, patients with low vision may require family members or caregivers to assist with daily wound inspection, dressing changes, offloading, and timely recognition of infection. In fact, support from family members or caregivers was significantly less in the low vision group, and the length of hospitalization tended to be longer in this group, although the difference did not reach statistical significance. However, data on home-visit care or wound care services provided in the home setting were not available in the present cohort. Future prospective studies incorporating such social and care-related variables are warranted.

In the present study, the frequency of diabetes and hemodialysis was significantly higher in the low vision group than in the normal vision group. These baseline differences may have affected the results of our study. Indeed, various factors have been reported as risks for mortality and amputation-free survival in CLTI patients.4,5,18,19 However, the number of cases was too small to perform propensity matching. Although multivariable analysis was performed, residual confounding cannot be fully excluded. In addition, the multivariable analysis showed that diabetes and hemodialysis were not factors associated with wound healing outcomes in this cohort. In this study, end-stage renal disease was caused by diabetes in all hemodialysis patients with low vision. Furthermore, most patients in the low vision group (23 of 24; 95.8%) had diabetes. Therefore, diabetes may have contributed to the development of low vision in this study.

The risk of developing microvascular complications of diabetes, including diabetic nephropathy, neuropathy, and retinopathy, depends on both the duration of diabetes and the level of glycemic control.20 In this study, the low vision group had markedly higher prevalence of diabetes (95.8% vs. 58.0%) and hemodialysis (62.5% vs. 22.0%). While there was no difference in baseline blood sugar levels or HbA1c in this study, patients with low vision may have had poorer glycemic control and a longer duration of diabetes. In addition, patients with low vision may have had more diabetic neuropathy. However, this study did not collect data on the duration of diabetes, the level of glycemic control, or diabetic neuropathy, and these remain topics for future investigation.

According to a report by the World Health Organization, there are 285 million people with low vision worldwide.10 Similar to the population of patients with LEAD, the prevalence of low vision is expected to increase in an aging society.1,11 In this study, approximately one-third of CLTI patients had low vision. Although the prevalence of low vision among CLTI patients may be high, we could not find any reports describing the low vision population within this group. We believe that the adverse impact of low vision should be better known to physicians performing EVT, and that more detailed and larger studies investigating the relationship between CLTI and low vision should be conducted in the future.

Study Limitations

This study has certain limitations. It was a non-randomized, retrospective, single-center study with a small number of patients, and selection bias could not be excluded. Moreover, the low vision group had markedly higher prevalence of diabetes (95.8% vs. 58.0%) and hemodialysis (62.5% vs. 22.0%), both of which are known to affect wound healing. While multivariable analysis was performed, confounding cannot be fully eliminated, and the limited sample size restricts statistical power and precluded advanced adjustment methods such as propensity score matching or inverse probability weighting. Due to the small sample size, propensity matching could not be performed, no significant difference was observed in minor amputation, and there was insufficient power to detect the effects of various factors on wound healing. This study excluded patients who underwent bypass surgery or conservative treatment, so evaluations in such patients could not be performed. Factors thought to influence lower limb prognosis, such as duration of diabetes, degree of glycemic control, diabetic neuropathy, quality of life, social isolation, social vulnerability, and social determinants of health, could not be analyzed in this study because data on these variables were not collected. Indications for revascularization, revascularization methods, procedural endpoints, wound management, and the use of Rheocarna depended on the physician’s discretion. Therefore, the results may have been influenced by differences in the timing of repeat revascularization and decisions regarding wound care. The number of patients who underwent Rheocarna in this study was too small to discuss its effect on wound healing. Last, this study lacked an independent core angiography laboratory. Therefore, the generalizability of our findings to other populations and treatment settings should be interpreted with caution.

Conclusions

Our study demonstrated that the wound healing rate of CLTI patients with low vision was significantly lower than that of patients without low vision. In addition, low vision was independently associated with wound healing in CLTI patients with tissue loss.
