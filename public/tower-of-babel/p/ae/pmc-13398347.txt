Machine learning‐based prediction model for adverse pregnancy outcomes in women with gestational diabetes mellitus
Authors: Jooyeop Lee, Seoung‐Ho Choi, Hyeon Ji Kim, Jee Yoon Park, Tae Jung Oh, Sung Hee Choi, Hak Chul Jang, Joon Ho Moon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAims/IntroductionGestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) is one of the most frequent pregnancy complications. Investigating clinical risk factors for adverse pregnancy outcomes in women with GDM would help predict and prevent neonatal complications. We developed a machine learning model to discover risk factors for adverse pregnancy outcomes.Materials and MethodsWomen with GDM from tertiary hospitals in Korea were included (n = 305, discovery cohort; n = 911, validation cohort). Supervised machine learning classification models, including ExtraTree, RandomForest, GradientBoosting, AdaBoost, Bagging, XGBoost, and Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LGBM), were developed to predict adverse pregnancy outcomes. Outcomes included large for gestational age (LGA), small for gestational age (SGA), low Apgar score, and preterm delivery. The top‐ranked risk factors identified through feature importance were further validated using binary logistic regression analysis.ResultsIn predicting LGA, the RandomForest model achieved the highest AUROC of 0.726 on the validation cohort. For SGA, the RandomForest model achieved the highest AUROC of 0.628. For low Apgar score and preterm delivery, the ExtraTree model showed the best performance with AUROCs of 0.689 and 0.616, respectively.ConclusionsThis study presents machine learning models as a foundational tool for identifying factors associated with adverse pregnancy outcomes in women with GDM. These models may serve as a foundation for future development of clinical decision support tools.

INTRODUCTION

During pregnancy, women experience significant metabolic alterations, particularly an increase in insulin resistance, which is associated with the effects of placental hormones and the physiological changes related to weight gain. Gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) is one of the most frequent complications, increasing alongside the worldwide surge in obesity and its associated metabolic disorders 1 , 2 , 3 . GDM is associated with adverse pregnancy outcomes, including preterm delivery, primary cesarean section delivery, and preeclampsia, as well as maternal and fetal complications 4 , 5 , 6 . When a fetus is exposed to maternal hyperglycemia, the risk of large for gestational age (LGA) is increased due to fetal hyperinsulinemia, and the risks of childhood obesity and diabetes mellitus are also elevated 7 , 8 . Maternal insulin resistance and placental dysfunction are among the key contributors to the development of GDM‐associated complications 9 , 10 , 11 . Clinically, it is important to identify risk factors that increase the risk of adverse pregnancy outcomes in order to predict and prevent complications, but risk factors or predicting factors of adverse pregnancy outcomes remain elusive.

Machine learning (ML) models based on clinical parameters are a cost‐effective approach for risk stratification of disease development or complication 12 . In this study, we developed and validated ML models to identify clinical risk factors for adverse pregnancy outcomes from two independent GDM cohorts from South Korea. We validated the discovered clinical risk factors using the conventional logistic regression models.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Datasets

The data used in this cross‐sectional study were obtained from two independent longitudinal cohorts previously enrolled in prospective studies. Seoul National University Bundang Hospital‐based data were collected between 2020 to 2023. Eligible participants were selected from among women with GDM excluding those with missing HbA1c or fasting glucose and pre‐existing diabetes. Finally, 305 women were included for the discovery cohort. Data for external validation were obtained from a dataset from Ajou University Hospital (validation cohort), and 911 eligible women were selected. Eligible participants were identified based on inclusion and exclusion criteria similar to those applied in the discovery cohort, with the exception that women with gestational impaired glucose tolerance (GIGT) were also included. Women with preexisting diabetes or missing HbA1c or fasting glucose values were excluded. All eligible participants during the study period were included, minimizing the likelihood of selection bias. All procedures followed were in accordance with the ethical standards of the responsible committee on human experimentation (institutional and national) and with the Helsinki Declaration of 1964, as revised in 2013, and were approved by the Institutional Review Board of Seoul National University Bundang Hospital, Approval No. B‐2305‐826‐102. This is a retrospective study: informed consent was waived.

Maternal baseline characteristics, including demographic, anthropometric, and laboratory data, obstetric history, including pre‐pregnancy body mass index (BMI), GWG, and other pregnancy‐related variables, were obtained at enrollment after the diagnosis of GDM by OGTT during pregnancy. Adverse pregnancy outcomes, as well as neonatal anthropometrics and laboratory data, were collected at the time of delivery. All maternal and neonatal data were obtained from electronic medical records. The discovery cohort was selected for model development based on data completeness and consistency, as the external validation cohort exhibited substantially higher proportions of missing values in several key predictors, particularly GWG, insulin, and C‐peptide.

Datasets pre‐processing

The number of training dataset is 210, and the number of internal validation dataset is 95. For external validation, we utilized data from Ajou University Hospital (n = 911). Data preprocessing was performed for each training data, internal validation dataset, and external validation dataset using the StandardScaler function provided by sklearn (Figure 1). Missing values were handled by applying the InteractiveImputer method, which iteratively estimates missing values based on observed relationships among all other variables. Five imputed datasets were generated for each missing value, and model predictions from these datasets were pooled to obtain the final estimates. Random undersampling technique was applied to balance the class distribution by reducing the majority class samples. The trained model was subsequently applied to the external validation dataset.

Experimental method

Clinical factors included in the ML model are as follows: maternal age, pregestational BMI, GWG (calculated as the difference between prepregnancy weight and the last measured weight before delivery), insulin treatment during pregnancy, glucose concentrations at 0, 1, 2, and 3 h after OGTT, menstrual irregularity (an interval between menstrual cycles of less than 21 days or more than 35 days), history of preterm delivery, parity, sex of newborn, HbA1c, fasting serum insulin, and fasting C‐peptide at the diagnosis of GDM. Gestational age at delivery was included in the sensitivity analyses but excluded from the main analyses. Adverse pregnancy outcomes, which served as the primary outcomes of the study, were defined based on standard clinical criteria. LGA was defined as a birthweight above the 90th percentile for gestational age, whereas small for gestational age (SGA) was defined as a birthweight below the 10th percentile, according to the Fenton growth chart 13 . Low Apgar score was defined as a score of less than 7 at either 1 or 5 min after birth 14 , and preterm delivery was defined as birth occurring before 37 completed weeks of gestation 15 . The abbreviation index for the clinical factors is provided in Table S1.

For the ML model development, the classification model was implemented using seven machine learning algorithms: ExtraTree, RandomForest, GradientBoosting, AdaBoost, Bagging, XGBoost, and Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LGBM), using the scikit‐learn Python library (version 1.0.2). Model performance was assessed by AUROC Score, accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, balanced accuracy, and F1 score. To evaluate the feature importance, we used SHAP (Shapley Additive explain) values which were obtained from the best‐performing ML model (shap Python library, version 0.40.0). To validate the proper performance of the ML model, we included or excluded the factor “gestational age at delivery,” in predicting the outcome “preterm delivery,” defined solely by a gestational age at delivery. Binary logistic regression analysis was additionally performed to validate the association between the top‐ranked variables identified through machine learning feature importance and each outcome. This unadjusted analysis was intended to support the interpretation of the machine learning results rather than to establish independent causal risk factors.

RESULTS

Characteristics of study participants

A total of 210 women were included in the discovery cohort, among whom 13 (6.2%) had LGA, 18 (8.6%) had SGA, 19 (9.0%) had a low Apgar score, and 56 (26.7%) experienced preterm delivery. Among the participants, the mean age at birth was 35.6 ± 3.7 years. For external validation, 911 women were included, among whom 198 (21.7%) had LGA, 23 (2.5%) had SGA, 33 (3.6%) had a low Apgar score, and 54 (5.9%) experienced preterm delivery (Table 1). Overall, the proportion of missing data was generally low in the discovery cohort, whereas several variables in the external validation cohort showed relatively higher missing rates, likely reflecting differences in clinical data collection across institutions (Table S2).

Performance of clinical prediction models

In the discovery cohort, the mean gestational age at delivery was 35.0 ± 2.2 weeks for women who had preterm delivery, and 38.4 ± 0.9 weeks for those who had full‐term delivery. In the validation cohort, the mean gestational age at delivery was 34.9 ± 1.9 weeks for women with preterm delivery, and 39.0 ± 0.9 weeks for those without. The mean gestational age at delivery for other outcomes is detailed in Table S3.

When evaluating the predictive power of the preterm delivery outcome excluding the factor “gestational age at delivery,” the seven ML models demonstrated overall poor performance on the validation cohort, with AUROC values ranging from 0.528 to 0.616. Accuracy ranged from 0.277 to 0.735, and sensitivity ranged from 0.419 to 0.839. The F1 scores of the seven models ranged from 0.125 to 0.177 (Table S4). In contrast, when including the factor “gestational age at delivery,” the seven ML models demonstrated consistently superior predictability for preterm delivery (AUROC: 0.996–0.999; accuracy: 0.068–0.998; sensitivity: 0.968–1.000; specificity: 0.968–1.000; balanced accuracy: 0.500–0.999; and F1 score: 0.127–0.984) (Table S5). This finding supports the validity of the ML models. However, for further analysis, we examined all outcomes excluding the variable “gestational age at delivery,” given that gestational age at delivery cannot be a variable for perinatal adverse outcomes, which are determined at the time of birth.

Upon evaluation of LGA status, the RandomForest model exhibited the best performance in the validation cohort among the seven different models (AUROC: 0.726; accuracy: 0.694; sensitivity: 0.658; specificity: 0.658; balanced accuracy: 0.681; and F1 score: 0.484). Upon evaluating the SGA status among women in the validation cohort, the RandomForest model achieved the highest AUROC of 0.628. Additionally, other parameters demonstrated robust performance (accuracy: 0.842; sensitivity: 0.348; specificity: 0.348; balanced accuracy: 0.601; and F1 score: 0.100). Upon examination of the Apgar score status, the ExtraTree model demonstrated an AUROC of 0.689 with moderate performance across other parameters (accuracy: 0.738, sensitivity: 0.488, specificity: 0.488, balanced accuracy: 0.619, and F1 score: 0.143). Furthermore, in the evaluation of preterm delivery status among women in the validation cohort, the ExtraTree model exhibited superior performance, with an AUROC of 0.616, and demonstrated performance across other parameters (accuracy: 0.551, sensitivity: 0.629, specificity: 0.629, balanced accuracy: 0.587, and F1 score: 0.160) (Figure 2, Table S4).

In conjunction with predicting the likelihood whether GDM women would develop adverse pregnancy outcomes, one of our aims was to identify the clinical risk factors and level of association with each outcome (Figure 3). We performed an additional binary logistic regression analysis to evaluate whether the top‐ranked variables identified through the feature importance method demonstrate statistically significant associations with the outcome.

Among the 16 variables analyzed, pregestational BMI, fasting glucose concentration, GWG, glucose concentration measured 1 h after an OGTT, and HbA1c were factors associated with LGA in the ML model (Figure 3a). For the SGA, fasting glucose concentration, glucose concentration at 1 and 2 h after the OGTT, parity, and HbA1c were factors shown to be associated (Figure 3b). Among the top five ranked variables, none demonstrated statistical significance (Table 2).

The top five features influencing the prediction of the low Apgar score were parity, fasting glucose concentration, glucose concentration at 3 h after OGTT, HbA1c, and insulin treatment (Figure 3c). Fasting glucose concentration was found to have a statistically significant association with low Apgar scores (Table 2).

The top five factors contributing to the predictive performance of preterm delivery were sex of fetus, insulin treatment, parity, glucose concentrations measured at 1 h after the OGTT, and menstrual irregularity (Figure 3d). Among these variables, parity demonstrated significant associations with preterm delivery (Table 2).

DISCUSSION

In this study, we utilized ML models to predict adverse pregnancy outcomes and to identify their clinical risk factors in women with GDM. The RandomForest model demonstrated the highest performance in predicting LGA and SGA, whereas the ExtraTree model exhibited the highest performance in predicting low Apgar score and preterm delivery. Although some studies have focused on developing ML models to predict LGA outcomes 16 , 17 , we extend beyond LGA to include outcomes such as SGA, low Apgar scores, and preterm delivery. Additionally, we identified the clinical risk factors and their impact on adverse pregnancy outcomes, which were further validated through binary regression models.

The significantly increased risk of LGA with higher levels of clinical factors (odds ratio >1) is consistent with previous findings 4 , 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 . It is established that exposure to maternal hyperglycemia increases the risk of LGA 4 , 7 . Previous studies have identified pregestational BMI, GWG, and diabetes as independent risk factors for LGA 4 , 18 , 21 . In meta‐analyses, elevated maternal HbA1c levels have been significantly associated with an increased risk of LGA infants among women with GDM 20 . Given that these factors had significant association with LGA in the logistic model, we presented the utility of ML models in identifying the risk factors for adverse pregnancy outcomes.

We observed differences in the incidence of LGA, SGA, low Apgar scores, and preterm delivery between the discovery and external validation cohorts. Notably, the discovery cohort exhibited higher rates of SGA, low Apgar scores, and preterm delivery, whereas the validation cohort showed a higher incidence of LGA. One potential explanation is differences in maternal age distribution between cohorts. In our cohorts, maternal age was higher in the discovery cohort compared to the validation cohort (mean 35.6 vs 31.2 years). Advanced maternal age has been associated with increased risk of preterm birth and low birth weight, potentially related to age‐associated placental dysfunction and other obstetric complications 22 . Previous studies have also suggested that reduced placental efficiency in older women may contribute to fetal growth restriction and stillbirth 23 . Additionally, the interaction between maternal age and GDM may alter the risk profile for perinatal outcomes, with younger women with GDM showing stronger associations with LGA and macrosomia than older women 24 . Collectively, the differences in adverse outcome rates between the two cohorts may, at least in part, reflect differences in maternal age distribution, consistent with previously reported associations between maternal age and pregnancy outcomes.

Notably, although RandomForest demonstrated the highest AUROC for LGA and SGA, predictive performance for some outcomes and certain performance metrics, such as F1 score, remained modest. This may be attributable to the relatively small sample size, class imbalance observed in several outcomes, and the use of undersampling during model training, which may have limited the models' ability to capture complex clinical patterns. This limitation was particularly evident for preterm delivery in the external validation cohort, where ExtraTree achieved an AUROC of 0.616 despite showing the best performance among the evaluated models. This may partly reflect the relatively small number of preterm delivery events (n = 54) in the validation dataset, which may reduce the ability of machine learning models to identify stable predictive patterns. In addition, the clinical variables available in our dataset may not fully capture important determinants of preterm delivery, such as hypertensive or placental complications during pregnancy. We identified several risk factors for individual adverse pregnancy outcomes which were further validated in the logistic regression analysis.

This study has several additional limitations. First, we assessed the association but were unable to establish a causal relationship between the predictors incorporated into the model and the occurrence of adverse pregnancy outcomes due to the nature of cohort study. Further experimental studies are required to elucidate the biological pathways and mechanisms underlying the interactions between maternal factors and their impact on the development of adverse pregnancy outcomes. Second, potential selection bias may exist because the study population was derived from tertiary hospital‐based cohorts, which may not fully represent the broader population of women with GDM. Third, although missing values were handled through multiple imputation, which preserves the relationships among variables and reduces potential bias compared with simpler imputation methods during the preprocessing stage, the presence of missing data may still have influenced the model performance and statistical analyses. Fourth, the number of participants as well as the number of input variables was relatively small, which may have limited the stability of the model and the reliability of its performance estimates. Incorporating a broader range of variables in future research may yield more comprehensive understandings into adverse pregnancy outcomes. Fifth, discovery and validation cohorts were not entirely homogeneous. Whereas the discovery cohort consisted exclusively of women with GDM, the validation cohort additionally included women with gestational impaired glucose tolerance. This difference in cohort composition may have contributed to discrepancies in model performance and should be considered when interpreting the external validation results and the generalizability of the models.

From a clinical perspective, ML‐based prediction models may provide useful insights into identifying risk factors associated with adverse pregnancy outcomes in women with GDM. By integrating routinely collected clinical variables, such models may serve as a foundation for future development of clinical decision support tools. For example, patients identified as having a higher predicted risk of LGA or preterm delivery could benefit from more intensive glucose monitoring, nutritional counseling, or closer fetal surveillance. Therefore, ML‐based approaches may complement traditional risk assessment methods and contribute to more individualized prenatal care. Our model may serve as a foundational tool for providing insights and guiding future ML research on adverse pregnancy outcomes in mothers with GDM. We also observed that ML prediction models could offer significant contributions by validating existing knowledge and assisting in the prioritization of interventions for women, based on the importance of various variables. Interventions informed by ML evidence have the potential to cost‐effectively reduce adverse pregnancy outcomes and facilitate the efficient allocation of healthcare resources at a national level. Additionally, by identifying novel factors, these models can aid clinical physicians in understanding underlying mechanisms and contribute to the development of innovative treatment strategies.
