The roles of body image, drive for muscularity, and spiritual well-being in the relationship between disordered eating and exercise addiction among sports science students: a moderated model
Authors: Muhammed Şahin, Nisa Nur Ayhanci, Metin Erdem, Hıdır Apak, Zeynep Parlak Özer, Mehmet Ilkim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study aims to evaluate the roles of drive for muscularity, body image perception, and spiritual well-being in the relationship between exercise addiction and disordered eating.MethodsThe research was designed as a cross-sectional study and included 408 students enrolled in the Faculty of Sport Sciences at various public universities in Türkiye. Data were collected using a socio-demographic information form, the Eating Behavior Disorder Scale, the Drive for Muscularity Scale, the Exercise Addiction Scale, the Spiritual Well-Being Scale, and the Body Image Scale.ResultsThe findings revealed that exercise addiction is linked to disordered eating through the mediating role of the drive for muscularity. This indirect relationship changes depending on levels of body image and spiritual well-being. Specifically, the effect of the drive for muscularity on disordered eating was strongest among individuals with low body image and low spiritual well-being. In contrast, when body image was at low or moderate levels and spiritual well-being was high, this effect was no longer significant.ConclusionsThe findings indicate that negative body image and spiritual well-being are key correlates of disordered eating and exercise habits. In addition, these findings emphasize the importance of maintaining psychological and physical balance, particularly in sports science students who are under high pressure in terms of athletic performance.

Introduction

Athletes are required to maintain specific dietary patterns and intensive training programs in order to maximize athletic performance or to attain a sport-specific physique (e.g., a certain body weight, lean mass, muscle volume, or body shape). Both internal and external sport-related pressures to enhance athletic performance and maintain a particular physique, such as those from teammates, coaches, and sport systems, place athletes at high risk for disordered exercise behaviors (e.g., excessively long, frequent, or intense training), disordered eating (e.g., dietary restriction, self-induced vomiting), drive for muscularity, and body image concerns [1–5].

Regular and consistent physical activity has been shown to produce various positive health outcomes, including improvements in mental health [6], cardiovascular conditions [7] and overall well-being [8]. However, studies have also demonstrated that disordered exercise behavior can have negative physical, psychological, and social consequences [9]. Although a wide range of terms has been used to define this condition, there is general agreement on the use of the term exercise addiction [10]. Exercise addiction is defined as a maladaptive behavioral pattern in which individuals who engage in regular exercise experience difficulty controlling their exercise habits [11] Exercise addiction has been reported in 3% to 42% of athletes or individuals who exercise regularly. This prevalence rate varies depending on the population studied and the method used to assess exercise addiction [12–14].

As previously noted, another risk for athletes is disordered eating [15, 16]. Disordered eating refers to a range of maladaptive eating-related behaviors and cognitions, such as restrictive dieting, binge eating, or purging, that may not meet the clinical criteria for eating disorders but still pose health and psychological risks [17]. Disordered eating in athletes may progress to clinically diagnosed eating disorders, leading to significant health consequences [18]. (Thereafter, we used the term ‘disordered eating’ to refer to maladaptive eating behaviors assessed through validated tools, whereas the term ‘eating disorders’ was reserved for clinically diagnosed conditions.)

Such disorders are often associated with body image–related drives such as thinness or muscularity. While the drive for muscularity refers to attitudes and behaviors aimed at increasing muscle mass and volume [2], the drive for thinness is associated with a desire for lower body fat and a slimmer physique [19].

In a study examining the relationship between exercise addiction and the presence or absence of eating disorders, it was found that individuals with eating disorders were 3.5 times more likely to develop exercise addiction compared to those without such disorders [20]. However, not all individuals with eating disorders develop exercise addiction [21], which may be attributed to factors such as body image perception and other mediating variables. In line with this, one study demonstrated a correlation between body dissatisfaction and risky behaviors associated with both exercise addiction and eating disorders. The study also showed that individuals dissatisfied with their bodies are at higher risk of developing both conditions [22].

Body image can be defined as an individual’s perception of the size, structure, shape, and contour of their body, as well as the feelings associated with these features and their components [23, 24]. Disturbed body image can be classified as a variable risk factor that precedes eating disorders in the general population [25]. Moreover, a relationship has also been demonstrated between exercise addiction and body image disturbance. According to theoretical models of exercise addiction, exercise may develop into an addictive behavior through positive or negative reinforcement processes related to body image [26–30]. In Western societies, a thin and fit body image is idealized for women, whereas for men, a low-fat and more muscular body image is considered ideal [31]. The internalization of this idealized body image may lead individuals to develop eating behavior disorders and/or exercise addiction. However, the relationship between eating behavior disorders and body image appears to be more complex among athletes compared to the general population. A recent systematic review examining the relationship between athletes’ body image and eating disorders found that disturbed body image is not always a criterion for eating behavior disorders, and that such disorders can also develop in athletes who report being satisfied with their bodies [32]. It has also been suggested that the factors contributing to disordered eating and eating disorderss in athletes may differ from those in non-athletes. Several studies have shown that the necessity of acquiring sport-specific fundamental motor skills (such as agility, flexibility, and balance) and achieving certain physical characteristics (e.g., low body weight and body fat percentage, high muscle mass), as well as the athletic demands of specific sport positions, may lead to body image disturbances (involving a drive for thinness or muscularity) and/or or disordered eating or eating disorders in athletes [33–35]. Mancine et al. [18], demonstrated that the risk of developing attitudes and behaviors associated with the drive for thinness varies depending on the demands of different sports, and is higher in aesthetic sports. In contrast, in weight-class sports (e.g., boxing), athletes tend to strive for muscle gain while maintaining a low body weight [36]. Compte et al. [35] also showed that the playing position in sports (e.g., offensive vs. defensive roles in team sports) can influence thinness-related behaviors and attitudes due to differing athletic demands. Therefore, depending on the demands of the sport or the athlete’s position, athletes may develop behaviors and attitudes oriented not only toward thinness, but also toward muscularity.

One of the factors that influence and shape attitudes and behaviours related to the body is religiosity and spirituality [37]. There is no clear consensus on the definition of religiosity and spirituality, and most studies tend to treat these two concepts as a single construct. Akrawi et al. [38] define religiosity as “a system of organized beliefs, practices, rituals, and symbols designed to facilitate closeness to the transcendent,” while spirituality is defined as “a personal quest to understand answers to ultimate questions about life.” Religion is organized around a set of beliefs and practices, whereas spirituality, though often related to religion, is a connection that links something within oneself to something beyond oneself [39]. Religious views related to the body may lead individuals to perceive their bodies as sacred [40] or, conversely, evoke feelings of shame [41]. Focusing on divine struggles, including negative thoughts or feelings toward God [42], may involve emotions such as anger, frustration, and guilt related to overeating [43], but can also include religious beliefs around fasting and purging [44]. A systematic review investigating the relationship between religiosity/spirituality and eating behavior disorders and body image concerns concluded that strong and internalized religious beliefs, combined with a secure and fulfilling relationship with the Creator, were associated with lower levels of disordered eating, psychopathology, and body image concerns [38].

Spiritual well-being, which is closely related to religion and spirituality, is a multidimensional construct that encompasses both existential and religious domains, and is generally associated with a person’s sense of purpose and their connection with themselves, others, and a higher power [45]. Research has shown that enhancing spiritual well-being can promote resilience, reduce anxiety, and alleviate depressive symptoms [46]. For many individuals—particularly in the context of rehabilitation or recovery—a strong sense of spirituality can serve as an important source of motivation, providing meaning and purpose that go beyond mere physical existence [47].

Individuals with high levels of spiritual well-being may adopt healthier coping strategies, which can minimize the negative effects of eating disorders, exercise addiction, negative body image, and the drive for muscularity. Spiritual well-being can function as a self-regulatory mechanism that helps individuals cope with external pressures [48]. Conversely, low levels of spiritual well-being may be associated with higher tendencies toward exercise addiction as a coping mechanism, and may co-occur with disordered eating disorders [26, 49]. Individuals who cultivate a healthy spiritual life may find a sense of balance in their personal journey, which in turn can enhance their drive for strength and health, accompanied by deeper existential satisfaction [50].

In the Turkish higher education context, admission to sports science faculties—including programs such as sports management, coaching, and physical education—requires not only academic achievement but also success in a national aptitude examination [51]. This examination typically involves physical performance tests and an evaluation of candidates’ prior sport participation. Indeed, evidence shows that a large proportion of sports science students maintain an active involvement in sport; for example, 70.9% have been reported to hold a sports license [52]. Addition to this it was reported that, The Exercise Addiction Inventory scores of sport science students are significantly higher compared to exercisers from the general population [53]. Furthermore, throughout their studies, students are required to participate in practical courses across different sport branches, which helps them develop not only theoretical knowledge but also practical athletic skills [54]. Taken together, these structures indicate that sports science students generally adopt a lifestyle closely linked to sport.

In the literature, variables such as disordered eating or eating disorders, exercise addiction, body image, drive for muscularity, and spiritual well-being have been examined either individually or in the context of pairwise relationships; however, their comprehensive interaction, particularly with the drive for muscularity, has not been thoroughly addressed. Particularly among groups such as sports science students, where body composition, physical competence and performance are prioritized, has not been thoroughly examined in terms of how it interacts with psychosocial constructs and potential mediating effects.

This study aims to evaluate the roles of drive for muscularity, body image perception, and spiritual well-being in the relationship between exercise addiction and disordered eating employing a moderated mediation model, which allows for testing both direct and indirect relationships simultaneously. In this regard, the study aims to contribute to a more holistic understanding of psychosocial correlates among to athletes. By identifying and considering the role of mediating factors at the stage of disordered eating, preventive strategies can be implemented, thereby reducing the risk of progression to eating disorders and associated health consequences. The conceptual model of the study is presented in Fig. 1, which also illustrates the direct and indirect hypotheses formulated based on the literature reviewed above.

H1

The indirect effect of exercise addiction on disordered eating occurs through the drive for muscularity (mediation effect is present).

H2

Body image significantly moderates the effect of exercise addiction on the drive for muscularity.

H3

Spiritual well-being significantly moderates the effect of the drive for muscularity on disordered eating.

H4

The indirect effect of exercise addiction on disordered eating is jointly moderated by body image and spiritual well-being (moderated moderation effect).

Methods

Procedure and participants

The study was designed as a cross-sectional descriptive research. Ethical approval was obtained from the Non-Interventional Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Mardin Artuklu University to conduct the study. The research was carried out in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and students were included based on voluntary participation. A total of 408 adult students (> 18 years old) enrolled in the Faculty of Sport Sciences participated in the study. The data were collected using a questionnaire prepared by the researcher. The questionnaire was created in a web-based online format, and the form link was sent to the students via e-mail. The online questionnaire link was sent via e-mail to approximately 800 students enrolled in the faculty. Of these, 408 students agreed to participate by selecting the “I agree” checkbox, and completed the questionnaire in full, yielding a response rate of 51%. The data collection process was carried out between January and March 2025.

The questionnaire used in the study consisted of the following components: a socio-demographic information form including questions about participants’ gender, age, department, year of study, body weight, and height (self-reported), and five standardized scales: the Eating Behavior Disorder Scale, the Drive for Muscularity Scale, the Exercise Addiction Scale, the Spiritual Well-Being Scale, and the Body Image Scale. Permissions for the use of the relevant scales were obtained via e-mail correspondence with the original authors or copyright holders where necessary. Body mass index (BMI) was obtained by dividing body weight in kilograms by the square of height in metres [55].

Measures

Exercise addiction scale

The scale was developed by Tekkurşun Demir et al. [56] to assess the level of exercise dependence. It consists of 17 items and three sub-dimensions: excessive focus and mood change, postponement of individual-social needs and conflict, and development of tolerance and passion. It is a five-point Likert-type scale ranging from 1 (Strongly Disagree) to 5 (Strongly Agree). Total scale scores were categorized into five levels: normal [1–17], low risk [18–34], at risk [35–51], dependent [52–69], and high dependence [70–83]. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of the scale was reported as 0.88. In the present study, the internal consistency coefficient was calculated as 0.92. The evaluation was made based on the total score of the scale.

Eating behavior disorder scale

In our study, disordered eating was assessed through a validated screening tool administered to sports science students. This approach was chosen because of its applicability and feasibility in non-clinical educational settings, where the use of full diagnostic procedures would not be practical.

The scale was developed by Şahin [57]. It consists of 25 items in a five-point Likert format and includes three subdimensions: cognitive restrictive eating, hedonic eating, and emotional eating. The scale does not contain any reverse-coded items or cut-off values. The total score ranges from 25 to 125, with higher scores on the scale and its subdimensions indicating more disordered eating. The internal consistency coefficient of the scale was originally reported as 0.944. In the present study, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was calculated as 0.92. The evaluation was based on the total score of the scale.

Drive for muscularity scale

The scale was originally developed by McCreary and Sasse [58], and its Turkish adaptation was conducted by Selvi and Bozo [59]. The Drive for Muscularity Scale (DMS) is designed to assess individuals’ attitudes and behaviors related to the desire to be muscular. It consists of 15 items and three subdimensions: training behaviors for muscularity, attitudes toward muscularity, and dietary and supplement-related behaviors for muscularity. The scale uses a six-point Likert format (1 = Never, 6 = Always) and does not include any reverse-coded items. Higher scores on the scale and its subdimensions indicate a stronger drive for muscularity. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for the Turkish version was reported as 0.89. In this study, the internal consistency coefficient was calculated as 0.91. Evaluation was based on the total scale score.

Spiritual well-being scale

The scale was developed by Ekşi and Kardaş [60] and consists of 29 items with five-point Likert-type response options. It can be evaluated through three dimensions (transcendence, harmony with nature, and anomie) as well as via the total score. Higher scores on the scale indicate higher levels of spiritual well-being. The Cronbach’s alpha internal consistency coefficient was reported as 0.886 in the original development study. In the current study, the internal consistency coefficient was calculated as 0.93. The assessment was based on the total scale score.

Body image scale

The Body Image Scale was developed by Saylan and Soyyiğit [61] to assess body image among individuals in adolescence and young adulthood. It is a five-point Likert-type scale consisting of 21 items and four subdimensions: negative body perception, sensitivity to evaluation, positive body perception, and body modification. In the reliability analyses, Cronbach’s alpha internal consistency coefficients were calculated as 0.92 for the EFA sample and 0.88 for the CFA sample. The total body image score is obtained by summing the scores of all items. The minimum and maximum scores that can be obtained from the scale are 21 and 105, respectively. Items 4, 7, 15, 16, and 17 are reverse-coded. Higher scores on the scale indicate a more negative body image. In the current study, the internal consistency coefficient was calculated as 0.90. The evaluation was based on the total scale score.

Analysis

The data were analyzed using IBM SPSS version 27 (The Statistical Package for Social Sciences). A moderated mediation model was tested using PROCESS MACRO (Model 21), developed by Hayes [62]. To confirm the significance of indirect effects, a bootstrapping method with 5,000 random resamples was employed.

Before conducting the analysis, multicollinearity diagnostics were performed. Tolerance values (< 0.10), variance inflation factor (VIF > 10), and condition index (CI < 30) were examined (Şata, 2020). The multicollinearity results indicated that all values were within acceptable ranges.

As presented in Table 4, exercise addiction has a positive and significant effect on the drive for muscularity (β = 0.37, p < 0,01). Likewise, body image also has a significant positive effect on the drive for muscularity (β = 0.35, p < 0,01). The moderation effect of body image on the relationship between exercise addiction and drive for muscularity is positive and significant (β = 0.13, p = .01). The effect of exercise addiction on drive for muscularity significantly varies depending on the level of body image. This indicates that body image has a moderating effect. The effect of exercise addiction on disordered eating is positive and significant (β = 0.14, p < 0,01). The drive for muscularity also has a positive and significant effect on disordered eating (β = 0.12, p < 0,01). The effect of spiritual well-being on disordered eating is not significant (β = 0.05, p = .25).

However, the moderation effect of spiritual well-being on the relationship between drive for muscularity and disordered eating is negative and significant (β = − 0.07 p = .03). The effect of the drive for muscularity on disordered eating significantly varies depending on the level of spiritual well-being. The drive for muscularity was found to mediate the relationship between exercise addiction and disordered eating in a positive and significant manner (β = 0.05, p = .02). This mediation is moderated by both body image and spiritual well-being. In other words, the effect of exercise addiction on the drive for muscularity varies according to body image, while the effect of the drive for muscularity on disordered eating varies according to spiritual well-being. Consequently, the indirect effect changes under the moderation of body image and spiritual well-being.

Discussion

The main findings of the study revealed significant positive relationships between exercise addiction and body image, drive for muscularity, and disordered eating. In addition, significant positive correlations were found between body image and drive for muscularity, as well as between body image and disordered eating. A significant positive relationship was also identified between drive for muscularity and disordered eating, and between spiritual well-being and disordered eating.

The study examined the moderating effect of body image in the relationship between exercise addiction and drive for muscularity, and the moderating effect of spiritual well-being in the relationship between drive for muscularity and disordered eating. The findings indicated that as negative body image increases, the effect of exercise addiction on drive for muscularity also increases. Furthermore, it was found that when levels of spiritual well-being were low or moderate, the effect of drive for muscularity on disordered eating decreased, whereas at high levels of spiritual well-being, this effect became non-significant. Drive for muscularity was found to significantly and positively mediate the relationship between exercise addiction and disordered eating. This mediation was moderated by both body image and spiritual well-being. In other words, the perceived body image alters the effect of exercise addiction on drive for muscularity, and similarly, spiritual well-being alters the effect of drive for muscularity on disordered eating. As a result, the indirect effect varies depending on the moderating roles of body image and spiritual well-being.

One of the main hypotheses of this study was that psychosocial variables such as exercise addiction, drive for muscularity, and body image influence disordered eating, but that these effects may weaken in individuals with high levels of spiritual well-being. The hypothesis stating that the indirect effect of exercise addiction on disordered eating occurs through drive for muscularity and that there is a mediation effect (H1) was confirmed. The findings revealed that exercise addiction was associated with disordered eating both directly and indirectly through the drive for muscularity. These associations suggest that individuals with higher levels of exercise addiction also tend to report stronger drive for muscularity, which is linked to disordered eating tendencies. In this regard, the drive for muscularity may operate as a potential mechanism through which exercise addiction and disordered eating are interconnected.

Researchers have hypothesized that increasing levels of drive for muscularity are associated with appearance-altering behaviors such as increased exercise participation, disordered eating, and the use of muscle-enhancing supplements [63–65]. Previous studies have similarly shown that individuals with tendencies toward exercise addiction develop higher anxiety and a greater need for control over their physical appearance, which may lead to pathological consequences in disordered eating or eating disorders [66–68]. Other studies have demonstrated that efforts to attain an idealized muscular body image, such as excessive exercising, can lead to disordered eating [69–71]. In this context, the identified mediation effect suggests that the drive for muscularity may serve as an explanatory factor in understanding the associations observed between exercise addiction, drive for muscularity, and disordered eating.

In the study, the hypothesis H2, which stated that body image significantly moderates the effect of exercise addiction on the drive for muscularity, was confirmed. In the literature, various studies have examined the impact of body image perception on individuals’ exercise behaviors and drive for muscularity. For instance, Liu et al. [72], in a study conducted on male weight trainers, found significant relationships between body dissatisfaction, drive for muscularity, and exercise addiction. This study also indicated that the drive for muscularity partially mediated the relationship between body dissatisfaction and exercise addiction. Lavender et al. [73] reported that men exhibited behavioral changes such as lifting heavier weights, training more frequently, and consuming more energy to enhance their body satisfaction related to the drive for muscularity. Similarly, Hale et al. [74], in a study involving bodybuilders, powerlifters, and fitness athletes, identified a positive relationship between exercise addiction and the drive for muscularity. These findings indicate that individuals’ desire for a more muscular body is associated with higher engagement in exercise behaviors, which in some cases corresponds with greater risk of exercise addiction.

Tod and Edwards [75] found that investment in appearance, body image disturbance, and drive for muscularity strengthened the relationship with behaviors aimed at enhancing physical appearance. These findings support the notion that individuals’ perceptions of their body image influence their drive for muscularity and exercise behaviors. Furthermore, in their study on male strength and power athletes, Şentürk and Göbel [76] reported that as muscle dysmorphia scores increased, so did scores for exercise addiction and body image coping strategies. This finding strengthens the impact of body image perception on exercise addiction and drive for muscularity. Additionally, Hamurcu [77] observed that as levels of body dissatisfaction increased, individuals’ drive for muscularity and exercise addiction actually decreased. This finding appears to contradict our results and the commonly reported positive relationship in the literature between body dissatisfaction and such behaviors. One possible explanation for this inconsistency is that, although individuals may be dissatisfied with their bodies, if they have a low sense of self-efficacy regarding their ability to change their bodies, this dissatisfaction may render them behaviorally passive rather than motivated.

In the study, the hypothesis (H3) suggesting that spiritual well-being significantly moderates the relationship between the drive for muscularity and disordered eating was confirmed. Our findings showed that when spiritual well-being was low, the impact of the drive for muscularity on disordered eating became stronger, whereas this association weakened or disappeared when spiritual well-being was high. These results directly support the view that spiritual well-being can function as a protective factor, mitigating disordered eating even among individuals with high levels of muscularity drive. Several studies have shown that a high drive for muscularity is associated with various disordered eating such as restrictive eating, excessive exercise, and the use of supplements or performance-enhancing substances [63–65]. Individuals with a high level of spiritual well-being may adopt healthier coping strategies; thus, the likelihood of disordered eating may be reduced [48]. Richards et al. [78] emphasize that increased spirituality may positively influence well-being and reduce symptoms associated with eating disorders, such as emotional eating. Kristeller and Jordan [48] also discuss the self-regulatory benefits of spiritual well-being, highlighting that spirituality may promote wisdom and a sense of self-acceptance that counters the negative self-perception often linked to disordered eating. Similarly, Cottingham et al. [79] found that a secure attachment to God was associated with greater body satisfaction, suggesting that spiritual beliefs may serve as a buffer against the adverse effects of body image concerns.

Spiritual well-being is assumed to have a self-regulatory effect on eating behavior and may potentially provide individuals with a framework to cope with pressures associated with muscularity [48]. Individuals with a strong sense of spiritual well-being may be less sensitive to the negative effects of muscularity drive, as they can derive self-worth and identity from sources beyond physical appearance [48]. Conversely, a lack of spiritual fulfillment may lead individuals to seek validation through physical means, which can intensify the muscularity drive and associated disordered eating [80]. Together with our results, this evidence indicates that spiritual well-being not only regulates eating behavior but also provides individuals with alternative sources of meaning and self-worth, thereby reducing their reliance on physical appearance as a primary measure of value.

In our study, the hypothesis (H4) stating that the indirect effect of exercise dependence on disordered eating is conditionally moderated by both body image and spiritual well-being was supported. The findings indicate that the indirect effect of exercise dependence on disordered eating can be significantly moderated by body image and spiritual well-being, revealing complex interactions that require deeper understanding.

Research indicates that individuals with exercise dependence often exhibit higher levels of body dissatisfaction, which may in turn exacerbate unhealthy eating behaviors [22, 30]. Similarly, Shriver et al. [81] found that negative body image was associated with increased emotional eating, particularly among overweight and obese adolescents; this suggests that individuals with low body image satisfaction may resort to maladaptive eating behaviors as a coping mechanism. Studies have also shown that individuals with higher levels of body dissatisfaction are more likely to develop exercise dependence, which is linked to disordered eating [22, 26].

Spiritual well-being generally contributes to individuals’ self-esteem and body image, which are key factors in how they perceive and relate to their bodies [82]. For example, individuals with high levels of spiritual well-being may be more likely to find healthier outlets for their concerns and, as a result, may be less likely to develop maladaptive behaviors such as excessive exercise or restrictive eating. In contrast, those with lower levels of spiritual well-being may be more inclined to turn to exercise addiction as a coping mechanism for emotional struggles, and this may exacerbate disordered eating [49, 83].

These findings demonstrate that spiritual well-being serves as an important moderator on the path to maladaptive behaviours stemming from the drive to have a muscular body, contributing to theoretical models of exercise addiction and disordered eating. Traditional frameworks often emphasise sociocultural and psychological risk factors such as perfectionism, the internalisation of appearance ideals, or social comparison. This study expands these frameworks towards a more holistic biopsychosocial-psychological perspective by showing the buffering effect of spiritual well-being. This may be particularly significant for populations engaged in sports sciences.

From a clinical and preventive perspective, the results indicate that interventions targeting exercise addiction and disordered eating issues should not only focus on correcting maladaptive beliefs related to body image and muscularity but also enhance spiritual well-being. For example, psycho-educational programmes for sports science students could include counselling approaches that foster meaning-making, purpose, and mental resilience, as well as modules on body image awareness and media literacy.

The current study had both strenght and limitations. First, it is one of the few studies to simultaneously examine the roles of exercise addiction, body image, drive for muscularity, and spiritual well-being in relation to disordered eating among sports sciences students, thereby offering a more comprehensive understanding of these interconnected factors. Second, the relatively large sample size increases the reliability of the findings and allows for robust statistical analyses, including mediation and moderation models. However, the study also has some limitations. Its cross-sectional design does not allow for causal inferences, and longitudinal or experimental studies are needed to clarify temporal relationships between these variables. The sample was limited to students from sports sciences faculties in Türkiye, which may restrict the generalizability of the findings to other populations or cultural contexts. Future research should employ diverse samples, multi-method approaches, and longitudinal designs to build on the findings of this study.

Conclusion

This study revealed that the drive for muscularity plays a significant mediating role in the relationship between exercise addiction and disordered eating among students of sports sciences, and that this relationship varies meaningfully depending on levels of body image and spiritual well-being. This finding supports the view that body image perception is related to both exercise addiction and the drive for muscularity.

It has been observed that individuals with low levels of body image tend to develop a stronger drive for muscularity in their efforts to attain an idealized muscular appearance, which in turn fosters disordered eating. In contrast, individuals with higher levels of spiritual well-being appear less vulnerable to muscularity-related pressures. Spiritual well-being may function as a protective factor by helping individuals define their self-worth beyond physical appearance, thereby potentially reducing such pathological tendencies.

In conclusion, this study demonstrated that the drive for muscularity is not merely an individual inclination but a complex and multidimensional process closely associated with body image and spiritual well-being. In this context, it is recommended that psychosocial support be strengthened among students of sports sciences, body image awareness be enhanced, and programs that promote spiritual well-being be developed. Relevant institutions should implement preventive mental health services and awareness-based educational initiatives by taking these risk factors into account. Clinicians and mental health professionals working in university or sports settings can provide more comprehensive preventive mental health services by incorporating spiritual well-being into holistic treatment models.

Limitations

Although the response rate of 51% is fairly typical for studies of this type, the findings may not be generalisable to sports science students who did not participate.
