Intelligent vs. Cultural: Research on the path to improve the resilience of urban and rural community governance based on the AHP-FCE model
Authors: Hongxun Xiang, Yang Yang, Xingyu Qi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractCommunity resilience is a core component of grassroots governance modernization, and community resilience shows different characteristics depending on the type of community. Existing research on community resilience mainly uses direct questionnaire data, relatively ignoring the differences in weights of various dimensions. Different categories of community resilience components play different roles in community resilience. Based on this, the study cited the logic of the AHP-FCE model and added objective data based on empowering resilience, thus making up for the model's shortcomings while giving full play to the model's advantages. The quantitative analysis of 353 questionnaires from five urban districts and five rural communities in the sample city was based on the community resilience evaluation model. In the current process of community resilience construction, there are typical problems of a lack of cultural resilience in urban community resilience construction and a need for more intelligent resilience in rural community resilience construction. The key to future community resilience construction is reconciling the relationship between cultural and intellectual resilience to solve the problem of targeting deviation in urban communities and rural community resilience construction. It is necessary to adhere to the spirit of “long-term construction” to build the cultural resilience of urban communities, implement the path of “digital empowerment” to make the intelligent resilience of rural communities, and adhere to the standard of “complete redundancy” to form an urban and rural community resilience governance mechanism gradually, and then Move towards more resilient urban and rural community governance and help realize the modernization of grassroots governance.Keywords: Community resilience, Type of community, Cultural resilience, Intelligent resilience

Introduction

Background

“Rainbows and wind and rain coexist, opportunities and challenges coexist. This is an eternal dialectical law.” [1] Potential risks have emerged with the rapid advancement of modernization while enriching people's material and spiritual lives. Especially since the COVID-19 epidemic, the impact of public emergencies has been directly apparent, causing a direct and continuous effect on residents' lives [2]. As the nerve endings that perceive risks, urban and rural communities are often both the “front line” where risks breed and the “front lines” where risks are resolved [3]. The “double frontline” status highlights the importance of urban and rural communities in risk prevention. Improving community systems and mechanisms, strengthening community response capabilities, and building more resilient communities are essential guarantees for facing modernization risks and maintaining a happy and beautiful life for residents.

Community resilience is affected by many factors and results from the intersection of institutional, spatial, cultural, structural, process, and other factors [4]. Although the components of community resilience are diverse, the differences formed by the combination of factors are closely related to the type of community [5]. Different communities significantly differ in population type, economic level, culture, etc. These differences directly affect the community's needs, resources, and strategies in responding to public events, resulting in typical differences in community resilience [6].

Studying the resilience characteristics of different types of communities and promoting the continuous updating and improvement of theories and methods of community resilience building through comparison, refinement, and summary can help the government formulate more targeted policies and measures and promote communities to adapt to economic development continuously—and environmental changes to achieve more adaptable and sustainable development of the community. In short, exploring the resilience components of different types of communities can guide different kinds of communities, help various types of communities complement each other's strengths and learn from each other's weaknesses, and jointly help build a grassroots risk prevention and resolution mechanism system to meet the needs of Residents' yearning for a happy life can further help achieve the goal of modernizing grassroots governance.

Literature review

The word “resilience” originated from the Latin word “resilio”, which initially meant returning to the original state [7]. It was later gradually applied to physics [8], ecology [9], and sociology [10]. The evolution of the concept of resilience is mainly the proposal of “engineering resilience” and the leap from “engineering resilience” to “ecological resilience.” The former emphasizes resilience as “a response by which a system can maintain its initial stable state when subjected to disturbances and external pressures.” [11] In contrast, the latter emphasizes that “the system pays more attention to its repair capabilities than just maintaining its original stable state.” [12] Combining “engineering resilience” and “ecological resilience,” community resilience can be defined as the community's disruption after natural disasters [13], accidents [14], social security incidents [15], and other emergencies. Without assistance from external forces, it can maintain the regular operation of community residents' lives through self-organization, self-adjustment, and self-repair [16] while maintaining long-term and effective adaptation to future risks [17].

China started to focus on resilience relatively late but has also achieved many results. Regarding the types involved, Chinese scholars' attention to resilience is mainly concentrated in urban areas. Some scholars have discussed the factors affecting the resilience of Chinese cities through the 4R (i.e., robustness, rapidity, redundancy, and resourcefulness) analysis framework. They also used the DEMATEL-ISM method to analyze the interaction mechanism of the influencing factors. They proposed three implications: improving the policy system, strengthening urban resilience assessment, and integrating the concept of resilience into the urban system [18]. With the popularization of digital technology, the risk impact brought by the digital age has become the focus of urban resilience research. Some scholars have used a model that combines analytical network processes with fuzzy comprehensive evaluation to evaluate smart city CPS, exploring whether smart city construction can enhance citizens' sense of security [19]. Some scholars have also analyzed potential risks from the perspective of supply and demand matching based on citizens' attitudes. They have confirmed the heterogeneity of supply and demand matching caused by citizens' age and frequency of use, which brings potential information risks [20]. Similarly, some scholars have identified the factors that affect citizens’ sense of benefit from smart city services based on the citizen perspective, indicating the impact of expectation-perceived performance on CSGSCS (citizens' sense of gain of smart city services) [21]. To prevent potential risks.

Regarding community resilience, the academic community has achieved many significant results. Some scholars believe that community resilience is a process [22], others believe that community resilience is the exploration of community resources [23], and others believe that community resilience is a composite concept of economy, society, environment, resources, etc. [24], and is a comprehensive result [25]. Based on the conceptual definition, scholars have also constructed various community resilience models, such as DROP [26], CCRAM [27], CART [28], etc. Some scholars have integrated existing models and classified the models [29]. In recent years, Chinese scholars have also gradually paid attention to community resilience. Analyze the risks faced by the community from the dimensions of community public health risk sources [30], community health care risk sources [31], community disaster risk sources [32], agricultural relocation or agricultural reform community risk sources [33], etc. Community resilience is analyzed from the complexity perspective [34], public governance perspective [35], emerging risk perspective [36], and community resilience theory [37]. In the context of big data, some scholars are also concerned about issues such as “information islands” [38] and “efficiency paradox.” [39].

In summarizing academic research results, it is easy to see that scholarly research on community resilience focuses on conceptual definition, component elements, intuitive evaluation, etc., and no scholars have conducted differentiated studies on different types of communities. However, while there are commonalities between communities, there are also noticeable differences. Especially in China's urban-rural duality, there are typical differences in the resilience components of urban and rural communities. However, there is an inherent connection between the two. For example, urban communities gradually emphasize the creation of community culture, while rural communities gradually establish intelligent systems, which also shows that the two are attracted to each other. Furthermore, existing studies on community resilience either conduct qualitative discussions or use direct questionnaire data to assess resilience. Although the qualitative debate on community resilience has clarified the relevant concepts, it remains more theoretical, and it isn't easy to know the specific community resilience practices. However, some scholars have used quantitative data to assess community resilience and proposed practical suggestions. However, the existing evaluation methods have inherent flaws, such as overreliance on mathematical logic and relative neglect of the natural world outside of mathematics. Community resilience has different characteristics and needs at various historical stages. In other words, community resilience is situationally applicable, and assessing it requires determining its constituent elements rather than generalizing. Therefore, before evaluating community resilience specifically, it is essential to make a weighted judgment on the components of community resilience to assess community resilience accurately. This is precisely the shortcoming of existing research. Based on this, what are the main elements of urban community resilience? What are the main features of rural community resilience? How is the weighting distributed between urban or rural components? What are their similarities and differences, and what reference value do they have? These are issues that urgently need to be explored when constructing resilient communities in a binary context.

Methods

Sample

The research sample location was selected as Chengdu, mainly based on the following considerations. First, Chengdu is the central city in western China and the core of the “Twin Cities Economic Circle.” It was the first to establish urban and rural community development governance committees, leading in community governance. Secondly, as a “new first-tier” city, the contradiction between urban and rural areas is prominent in rapid urbanization. It is not only a representative of the dual differentiation of urban and rural areas but also a representative of the dual integration of urban and rural areas, which is typical. Third, Chengdu divides urban and rural communities into urban and rural park communities, which coincides with the research hypothesis and is feasible. In 2022, Chengdu will launch the “Top 100 Demonstration Communities” selection activity to evaluate community construction based on established judgment criteria. It should be noted that this evaluation system is very systematic, including multiple dimensions such as service scenarios, cultural scenarios, ecological scenarios, spatial scenarios, industrial scenarios, and co-governance scenarios, as well as nearly a hundred specific indicators. The evaluation results will serve as the basis for judging the effectiveness of community governance. The top 100 communities will become a template for community governance in Chengdu, and subsequent communities will be built based on this standard. Therefore, the study's choice of “Top 100 Demonstration Communities” as the sample range is scientific, representative, and forward-looking and can more fully answer the research hypothesis. The study is based on the classification standards of urban and rural park communities in Chengdu and the “Top 100 Demonstration Communities” selection results in 2023 (consistent samples). Simple random sampling is used to select communities for evaluation. The study selected the Sihe Community, Zhonghai Community, Xingfu Community, Shuangnan Community, and Yixin Community as urban community samples and Sanyu Village, Liangshui Village, Liyi Village, Tiangong Village, and Xiangyang Village as rural community samples.

Indicator

The selection of indicators mainly follows the principles of scientificity and feasibility. By combining existing community resilience research literature and community field interviews, a total of 35 community resilience assessment indicators were extracted. Considering the feasibility of the evaluation indicators, the study selected five experts from the urban and rural community governance expert database “Xinmin Think Tank” in the sample area to screen the evaluation indicators through the Delphi method. Based on the expert screening results, the study divided the indicators into six categories based on differences in the focus of the indicators: institutional resilience, cultural resilience, structural resilience, spatial resilience, process resilience, and intelligent resilience. Finally, a hierarchical index system for community resilience assessment was formed (Table 1).

Institutional resilience refers to the ability of institutional settings to maintain their core functions, restore their operational capabilities, and adapt to new situations in the face of various pressures, shocks, or unstable factors [40]. Cultural resilience refers to the ability of a cultural group to maintain, recover, or adapt to its core cultural values, traditions, and beliefs in the face of external shocks, pressures, or ongoing change [41]. It consists of community atmosphere creation and community commitment, both of which are perception indicators. Structural resilience can be understood as the ability of a system, organization, or social structure to maintain, recover, or adapt its underlying structure in the face of external pressures, shocks, or continuous changes, thereby ensuring the system's long-term stability [42]. Spatial resilience focuses on the ability of a space or geographical area to maintain, recover, or adapt its structure, function, and organization in the face of external pressures and impacts, including spatial layout and spatial environment [43]. Process resilience refers to the adaptability and stability of relevant systems, procedures, or policy-making processes in the face of instability, conflict, or external pressure [44]. It includes two parts: response process and variable space. Finally, intelligent resilience is a product of the development of the times. Intelligent resilience refers to the resilience of the use of science and technology in the context of big data and information age, including three indicators: data security, information barriers, and refined applications, all measured using a five-level scale [45].

Model

To assess community resilience, academic circles mainly use structural equation modeling [46], principal component analysis [47], coupled coordination model [48], analytic hierarchy process [49], etc. After considering the suitability of various methods, the AHP-FCE assessment model was comprehensively selected to assess community resilience. It is worth mentioning that the study considered the problem of the evaluation results of the AHP-FCE evaluation model being too interfered with by human factors and targeted optimization of the model. It mainly corrects the indicator layer indicators and introduces more deterministic objective judgment indicators using FCE fuzzy quantitative evaluation. Based on maintaining the operational logic of the FCE fuzzy model, the evaluation model can reduce the influence of human subjective factors as much as possible and make the results more objective. The AHP calculation logic can refer to the research results of Professor Satty TL [50], and the FCE can refer to the research results of Professor Zadeh LA [51].

In short, the logic of using the AHP-FCE evaluation model is to use AHP combined with the professional knowledge of think tank experts to determine the weight of the evaluation model and use FCE to quantify resident perception indicators unsuitable for quantification. While considering the professional expertise of think tank experts, it also incorporates residents' subjective data to reflect residents’ demands. In addition, the study added more deterministic objective judgment indicators to ensure the results' objectivity further and reduce the impact of model subjectivity. It should be noted that since the objective judgment index is assigned a value of “1″ or “0″, it does not meet the characteristics of the five-level scale. Considering that “1″ represents complete compliance and “0″ means total non-compliance, the value assigned to “1″ is 5, and “0″ is given the value 1.

Community resident questionnaire data is divided into objective and subjective evaluation data. Objective evaluation data is based on judgment standards and directly judged by evaluators. Subjective data were assessed using a five-point Likert scale. The researchers went to different sample areas and conducted random interviews with residents using convenience sampling. A total of 357 assessment questionnaires were collected in the study, including 353 valid questionnaires, 184 valid questionnaires for urban communities (accounting for 52.12 %), and 169 valid questionnaires for rural communities (accounting for 47.88 %). The study conducted a reliability and validity analysis on the returned questionnaire. The test results showed that the α coefficient was 0.692 (>0.6), and the KMO value after excluding the constant items was 0.892 (>0.8), which can be used for further analysis.

Results

Analysis of urban community resilience results

The overall resilience score rate of urban communities is 87.66 %, which falls within the range of 85–95. The community resilience is relatively high. Among them, institutional, structural, and spatial resilience are all higher than 85, and the current resilience level needs to be continued. The score rates of process resilience and intelligent resilience are 79.57 % and 81.43 %, respectively, in the range of 75–85. The resilience level is high but needs further improvement. The cultural resilience score is 72.80 %, lower than 75 and in the range of 65–75. This shows that cultural resilience is relatively weak in urban communities and is a prominent shortcoming in improving overall resilience. From urban communities' overall resilience assessment results, institutional resilience has the highest score, followed by structural, spatial, intelligent, process, and cultural resilience. This is consistent with the actual situation of community development and governance in Chengdu. Chengdu was the first to set up urban and rural community development and governance committees, and its system is highly complete. Due to their resource advantages, urban communities are in a leading position in terms of spatial layout, spatial environment, social organization development, and intelligent infrastructure construction. In contrast, the cultural sector could be weaker due to community members' heterogeneity and mixed nature (Fig. 1).

Resiliency assessment results of urban communities.

Take the Zhonghai Community, for example. Zhonghai Community was established in 2015 in Hezuo Street, High-tech Zone. It covers an area of 8.25 square kilometers and has a permanent population of more than 42,000. It is a typical urban community. The Zhonghai Community System has formulated relevant rules and regulations for party building to lead the community, formulated a community rule of law publicity manual based on current legal standards, established a community autonomy charter based on legal provisions, and regularly carried out moral model selection activities, so it scored the highest in the institutional resilience dimension. Secondly, Zhonghai Community currently has more than five community organizations, including the elderly team, volunteer service team, and child care team. It has built a digital governance mini-program in which the masses can participate widely, so its structural and intelligent resilience is also high. In addition, Zhonghai Community has set up a wide range of emergency sites, public passages, and safety exits to ensure residents' safety. It has also created a unique emergency response plan to guide incidents. However, it should be pointed out that the Zhonghai community is a typical pluralistic community. The community members are relatively complex, including residents, residents who bought houses after demolition, and migrant workers who rent houses. The complexity of community members has led to relatively low trust, sense of belonging, recognition, and satisfaction among community residents. In particular, the difficulty in forming a common community cultural identity has become the biggest obstacle to the resilient development of the Zhonghai community. This problem also exists in the Sihe, Xingfu, Shuangnan, and Yixin Communities and has become a common shortcoming of urban communities.

Analysis of rural community resilience results

The overall score of rural community resilience assessment results is 74.82 %, which falls within the range of 65–75. Community resilience is weaker than that of urban communities, which is consistent with the current situation in China. Compared with urban communities, rural communities develop later and are limited by their conditions, making their development far lower than urban communities. These differences include abundant resources, information flow, emergency preparedness, and response speed. These factors together result in the resilience of rural communities being much lower than that of urban communities.

Judging from the specific indicators of resilience assessment, institutional resilience and cultural resilience have the highest score rates, which are 91.42 % and 81.72 %, respectively, higher than 74.82 % (overall assessment level). They are the driving factors for the current resilience of rural communities. Institutional construction started early in rural areas. As early as 1998, the Ninth National People's Congress passed the Organic Law of Villagers' Committees, and it was revised at the 11th National People's Congress in 2008 and the 13th National People's Congress in 2018. The system Construction has always been at the forefront. Rural society has always been a society of acquaintances. Compared with urban communities, the people in rural communities are more stable and have a stronger sense of locality. Their recognition, trust, and understanding of belonging to the collective are more robust than in urban communities.

Different from institutional resilience and cultural resilience, structural resilience, process resilience, spatial resilience, and intelligent resilience are fragile, with score rates of 65.11 %, 64.76 %, 65.80 %, and 61.52 %, respectively, which are far lower than urban communities and overall assessment level. However, in recent years, with the implementation of rural revitalization and the implementation of measures such as “integration of three governance,” “integration of five governance,” and “multiple combined forces,” structural resilience, process resilience, and spatial resilience are gradually being improved. However, it is challenging to implement intelligent resilience, which is closely related to infrastructure, professional managers, and the level of cadres of the two committees. The above three are often complex for rural communities, so the intelligent resilience assessment results are the weakest (Fig. 2).

Resiliency assessment results of rural communities.

Contrary to traditional perceptions, rural communities have performed well in institutional resilience. Whether it is Sanyu Village, Liangshui Village, Liyi Village, Tiangong Village, or Xiangyang Village, their party-building leading mechanism, legal guarantee mechanism, self-governance strengthening mechanism, moral governance education mechanism, intelligent governance support mechanism, and soft governance service mechanism are all relatively complete, which has become a typical advantage and a driving factor for the resilience construction of rural communities. However, compared with the benefits of institutional resilience, rural communities need to be more robust in structural, spatial, and process resilience. However, with the advancement of the rural revitalization strategy, these problems have been alleviated to a certain extent. The most severe problem is intelligent resilience. For example, Xiangyang Village has improved its basic infrastructure to a certain extent with the advancement of the macro strategy. So far, Xiangyang Village has achieved full network coverage and introduced a digital governance platform. However, in contrast, the villagers have low digital literacy, and the cadres need more relevant thinking and still rely on traditional methods to manage the villagers. Therefore, although the coverage of digital infrastructure has improved, the data utilization rate could be higher. According to the survey results, Xiangyang Village's digital technology only stays at the data statistics level and does not involve refined use. However, the opposite of intelligent resilience is cultural resilience. Villagers in rural communities have a high degree of identification with the village and show high trust, belonging recognition, and satisfaction with the village committee, which needs to be improved in urban communities.

Comparative analysis of urban and rural communities

Comparing urban and rural communities, it is easy to find that the overall score rate of urban communities is 85–95, while the score rate of rural communities is 65–75. The resilience of urban communities is much higher than that of rural communities. However, comparing the indicators at the quasi-measurement level, it is easy to see that although the overall resilience of urban communities is higher than that of rural communities, the scoring rate of urban communities in the cultural resilience section is 72.80 %, which is lower than the scoring rate of rural communities of 81.72 %. The score rates of urban communities in the structural resilience, spatial resilience, and intelligent resilience sectors are most different from those of rural communities (Fig. 3). However, structural and spatial resilience has significantly been improved due to the implementation of a series of policies. For example, the growth rate of social organizations and mass self-organizations in rural communities is much higher than in urban communities. From this, urban communities are much higher than rural communities in the intelligent resilience construction sector. Rural communities are more elevated than urban communities in the cultural resilience sector.

Comparison of score rates between urban communities and rural communities.

The typical differences between rural and urban communities are consistent with the industrial development logic of urban communities and the rural development logic of rural communities [52]. With the development of science and technology and the improvement of infrastructure, urban communities have achieved remarkable results in promoting the construction of intelligent communities through big data and informatization. Especially after the COVID-19 epidemic, the popularity of intelligent and refined management has skyrocketed, promoting the continuous improvement of urban communities' competence levels. Rural communities under the dual structure are more traditional than urban development. Due to the outflow of young and middle-aged people, there are more left-behind older adults or children in the village, which is a significant obstacle to implementing intelligent community construction. However, contrary to the resistance to implementing intelligent community construction, the rural community's local characteristics are preserved due to the large number of older adults in the village. What maintains the stability of rural communities is the informal institutional arrangements centered on blood ties and clans, that is, rural culture, which runs counter to urban communities. In other words, due to the inherent differences between urban industrial logic and rural cultural logic, urban communities have significant advantages in intelligence. In contrast, rural communities have more tremendous cultural advantages. In urban-rural integration, intelligence and culture are apparent driving factors for resilient communities. Both of them make it possible to move towards more resilient community governance. Intelligence and culture are essential for more resilient urban and rural community governance. Therefore, rural communities need to learn about the intelligent experience of urban communities, and metropolitan communities need to learn about the cultural experience of rural communities.

Discussion

Through comparative analysis of community resilience assessment results, it can be concluded that urban communities have cultural deficiency problems in the construction process of intelligent communities. In contrast, rural communities need more intelligence to develop cultural communities. From the community resilience framework perspective, the current prominent shortcoming in the resilience construction of urban communities lies in cultural resilience. In contrast, the restrictive factor in the resilience construction of rural communities is intelligent resilience. The construction of community resilience is indispensable. Improving the resilience level of urban and rural communities requires both intelligent driving force and cultural traction. Therefore, integrating culture into the construction of intelligent communities and intelligence into the development of cultural communities are vital issues that need to be urgently solved in the current resilience construction of urban and rural communities. In addition, after solving the outstanding shortcomings, the process resilience and structural resilience issues in urban and rural communities also need to be resolved urgently.

Adhere to the spirit of “long-term construction” and build the cultural resilience of urban communities

Community resilience construction is a long-term and systematic project. In the face of the outstanding shortcomings of the current urban community resilience construction, it is imperative to enhance community cultural resilience [53]. To improve cultural resilience, we must be aware of first correctly guiding the value judgment on the urgency and necessity of building resilient governance and consolidating the cultural foundation of resilient governance. As the glue of community resilience, cultural resilience can effectively promote public participation awareness and capacity improvement and build a community governance community. The construction of community cultural resilience is not limited to various external cultural activities but also includes community values, behavior norms, community cultural symbols, community resident relations, community trust, community recognition, and other dimensions [54]. Specifically, the construction of community cultural resilience includes two specific contents: community commitment mechanism and community building mechanism [55].

The community building mechanism is the key to cultivating community cultural resilience, and community building emphasizes that under the guidance of unique values, community residents are guided to explore community culture, establish characteristic community cultural symbols, shape unique community spirit, and create a community governance community under the guidance of outstanding values. Through participation in various activities, community residents enhance their recognition of community culture and the recognition and participation spirit of the community. First, we should put ideas, strengthen the party's leading role in cultural construction, and make community culture guided by socialist core values. Second, we should pay full attention to systematic planning from top to bottom, understand the importance and significance of community cultural construction, enhance the strategic position of cultural resilience construction, and conceptually promote the upgrading of communities from traditional disaster reduction communities to resilient communities; Third, we should actively cultivate community culture, explore community cultural characteristics, shape community spirit, and enhance public participation awareness.

The community commitment mechanism can effectively promote the construction of community cultural resilience; community commitment comes from the theory of organizational commitment, which initially refers to the identification and emotional belonging of members within the organization to the values of the organization, etc., and community commitment emphasizes the identification of residents within the community to the community, including community trust, community recognition, community belonging, etc [56]. Creating soft environments such as community publicity, community interaction, and cultural activities enhances residents' faith and credit of the community. Creating institutional links such as information disclosure, community participation, agenda setting, expanding community participation channels, cultivating residents' participation spirit, and stimulating subjects' enthusiasm to participate enhance residents' sense of belonging to the community [57].

Implement the path of “digital empowerment” and build the intelligent resilience of rural communities

Intelligent resilience is a new requirement to adapt to the construction of community resilience in the era of big data, which not only helps to release the efficiency of rural community governance system but also provides strong technical support for resilient communities to cope with abnormal situations [58]. Intelligent resilience is to give full play to the maximum utility of big data and informatization, integrate various essential data resources, and make them better contribute to improving community governance and service levels [59]. Combined with the experience of intelligent community construction in urban communities, rural communities need to make efforts from multiple aspects.

First, it is necessary to formulate rules and regulations related to intelligent governance, ensure data application specifications and security, formulate relevant specifications, classify and store data based on integration, and ensure the efficient use of data [60]. Second, it is necessary to break the “information island” and “data barrier,” promote the interaction of data between and within different departments, play the role of data integration, and form a three-dimensional data network [61]. Third, integrate extensive data analysis, detection, and application into community refined governance, use digital technology to empower community governance, form an intensive and unified information platform, promote real-time sharing of community data resources and dynamic assessment of community risks, provide data support for community refined governance, and enhance community wisdom and resilience [62].

Specifically, the first is to establish a community risk intelligent identification and early warning system through the Internet of Things and other advanced computer technologies to intelligently identify, dynamically detect, and real-time early warning potential risk sources in the community. The second is to establish an information platform for data exchange, inventory information, and data resources within and outside the organization and realize multi-level and multi-dimensional real-time sharing of resources to reduce the waste of public resources. Finally, it is necessary to establish a community staff team with extensive data processing capabilities, pay attention to the role of professional and technical training, form a regular learning mechanism, and strengthen the thinking and ability of staff in digital governance and intelligent governance.

Adhering to the standard of “complete redundancy,” build a resilient governance mechanism for urban and rural communities

The formation of urban and rural community resilience governance mechanisms requires not only mutual learning between intelligent resilience and cultural resilience but also efforts from multiple dimensions such as institutional resilience, structural resilience, spatial resilience, and process resilience. Based on the advantages of the resilience of the existing system, give play to the leading role of politics and unite the joint force of community emergency governance; Strengthen the guarantee role of the rule of law and improve the community's emergency response management capacity; Give play to the vital foundation role of autonomy and stimulate the vitality of community governance; Give play to the indoctrination role of rule by virtue and pragmatic community emergency management; Give play to the supporting role of intelligent governance and empower community emergency management mechanisms; Give space to the service role of soft governance and enhance the resilience of community emergency response [63].

Based on the existing structural resilience, and with the institutional construction of participation in governance and services as the guarantee, optimize the institutional environment for the construction of a pluralistic community; Expand the scope of community governance subjects through channels to guide the growth of community organizations; Take the construction of a platform for participation in governance as an opportunity to promote the construction of community governance subjects; Use project-based operation as a means to stimulate the vitality of multiple issues to participate in community governance [64]. Drawing on the experience of urban community spatial resilience construction, optimize the layout of public space structure in rural communities and enhance spatial redundancy; Improve the planning of public space routes in rural communities and enhance spatial interoperability; Functional zoning of public space in complex rural communities to enhance spatial diversity; Improve the environmental quality of public spaces in rural communities and enhance the quality of play [65].

In terms of process resilience, urban and rural communities must make efforts. Regarding the response process, “full-cycle management” should be introduced. Full-cycle thinking should be implemented in the whole process of community governance, such as early warning, triggering, action, recovery, and iteration, and incorporated into the community governance organism, sort out the relationship between nodes, clarify the responsibilities of each subject of the community, and ensure that different stages continue to play a guiding role. That is, to promote the sinking of resources, decentralization, and enhance the autonomy of community governance. Setting up relevant early warnings, implementing plans, and carrying out daily drills around high-incidence public events are necessary. It is required to develop community education and teach residents how to prevent emergencies scientifically [66]. Regarding a flexible governance system, we should pay attention to process redundancy and play a role in the positive factors of redundancy. Such redundant elements refer to the hidden techniques, structures, mechanisms, procedures, etc., existing in the governance system. Such redundancy factors reduce shocks caused by emergencies and enable rapid decision-making responses. Such redundant elements take a long time to form but have long-term stability. In the construction of resilient communities, a flexible response mechanism in emergencies is created through the construction of learning communities, community culture construction, and community identity maintenance.

Conclusions

Community resilience building has become a hot topic in community development and urban planning in recent years, and the concept emphasizes the ability of communities to effectively adapt, recover, and thrive in the face of various disasters, crises, or unstable situations. Resilience is not just about returning to the way it was but about becoming more robust and resilient after meeting challenges. Improving community resilience is critical to socio-economic and environmental stability, improving residents' quality of life, and maintaining public safety. In a hyper-connected world, residents are affected by natural disasters, public events, pandemics, etc.; communities that link “inside and outside” are on the front lines of various crises. A resilient community can recover more quickly from the effects of events, ensure that basic needs are met, and bring activities back to normal as soon as possible.

In China's field, the construction of community resilience needs to face the urban-rural dual structure. There are indeed typical differences between urban and rural communities. In recent years, urban districts have gradually implemented the construction of community culture. In contrast, rural communities have gradually popularized digital and information infrastructure, a typical signal of binary integration. Therefore, based on the urban-rural dual community category and the existing research results, the research designs six types of community resilience assessment models, including institutional resilience, cultural resilience, structural resilience, process resilience, spatial resilience, and intelligent resilience, taking Chengdu as an example, and selects representative urban and rural communities for community resilience assessment.

The assessment results found that the community resilience of urban communities is much higher than that of rural communities. Still, the evaluation score in the cultural resilience section is lower than that of similar rural communities. From the perspective of disparity, although the rest of the indicators of urban communities are ahead, the most significant difference is intelligent resilience. This shows that cultural resilience is its weak link in urban communities, while intellectual resilience is the most prominent shortcoming in rural communities. Therefore, the study concludes that there are problems in the current process of urban and rural community resilience construction, and there are problems of a lack of cultural resilience in the process of urban community construction and a lack of intelligent resilience in the process of rural community construction. Since both are outstanding shortcomings in the resilience construction of urban and rural communities, promoting the integration of intelligence and culture is the general trend. In constructing resilience in urban and rural communities, it is necessary to base not only on the cultural resilience of local complexes but also on the intelligent resilience of big data and informatization.

This paper also has many things that could be improved. First, in selecting evaluation models, the research is mainly based on the existing research results for model construction and index selection. Still, the natural environment is constantly changing, and practice-based experience condensation should be included. Secondly, due to the size of the research team, it is impossible to evaluate the research subjects as a whole and the random sampling method is chosen, which will cause the problem of whether the sampling representation is sufficient. Finally, with the combination of qualitative and quantitative indicators, the research adopts the method of hierarchical empowerment by default, which makes the horizontal comparison of indicators only stay at the criterion layer but cannot enter the indicator layer. Although the specific indicators are analyzed based on the AHP-FCE evaluation model, further improvement is needed in constructing mathematical logic.
