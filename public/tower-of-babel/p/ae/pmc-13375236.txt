Adherence to the WHO Eight‐Contact Antenatal Care Model and Associated Factors Among Postpartum Mothers in Sululta District, Ethiopia: A Cross‐Sectional Study
Authors: Ebisa Zerihun, Anane Mulatu, Roza A/Michael, Abdi Deksisa, Shilema Urge, Bereket Bedore
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsAdequate antenatal care (ANC) contacts are essential for reducing complications during pregnancy and childbirth. Recognizing that inadequate ANC utilization is associated with high maternal mortality, the World Health Organization (WHO) revised its ANC guidelines in 2016, recommending at least eight ANC contacts for all pregnant women. Despite this recommendation, ANC coverage remains suboptimal globally, particularly in East Africa. In Ethiopia, especially in the Sululta district, evidence regarding adherence to the 8+ contact ANC model is limited. Therefore, this study aimed to assess adherence to the WHO‐recommended eight‐contact ANC model and identify its associated factors among postpartum mothers attending public health facilities in the Sululta district, Ethiopia, in 2024.MethodsAn institution‐based cross‐sectional study was conducted in public health facilities of the Sululta district from October 10 to November 10, 2024. A total of 410 postpartum mothers were selected using systematic random sampling and all participants (100% response rate) provided complete data. Data were analyzed using logistic regression analysis. Variables with p value <0.05% and 95% confidence intervals (CI) were considered statistically significant.ResultsOverall adherence to the WHO‐recommended eight‐contact ANC model was 25.4%(104/410; 95% CI: 21%–30%). Factors significantly associated with adherence included urban residence (AOR: 3.06; 95% CI: 1.17–8.03), previous cesarean delivery (AOR: 3.33; 95% CI: 1.39–7.97), history of obstetric complications (AOR: 6.16; 95% CI: 2.69–14.10), maternal satisfied with ANC services (AOR: 6.78; 95% CI: 2.46–18.66), and receiving ANC at a health center rather than a hospital (AOR: 2.95; 95% CI: 1.24–6.93).ConclusionAdherence to the WHO‐recommended eight‐contact ANC model was low among postpartum women in the Sululta district. Interventions led by the regional and district health offices, health facility managers, healthcare providers and community health extension workers should focus on improving maternal satisfaction with ANC service, reducing waiting times and increasing access for rural populations through targeted counseling and community awareness initiatives to enhance adherence.Keywords: adherence, antenatal care, eight‐contact ANC model, Ethiopia, maternal health, postpartum mothers, Sululta district

Introduction

Antenatal care (ANC) is a key maternal health intervention provided by skilled health professionals to reduce pregnancy‐related complications and improve maternal and neonatal outcomes [1]. It enables early detection and management of obstetric risks, provides preventives interventions, and offers health education that promotes healthy pregnancy and childbirth [2]. Evidence shows that timely initiation and adequate utilization of ANC services significantly reduce maternal and neonatal morbidity and improve overall pregnancy outcomes [3].

In 2016, the World Health Organization (WHO) updated its ANC guideline, increasing the recommended minimum from four focused visits to at least eight contacts [4]. This shift emphasizes not only the frequency but also the quality and continuity of care, promoting meaningful interaction between pregnant women and healthcare providers [5]. The eight‐contact ANC model is designed to reduce perinatal mortality and contribute to global targets of reducing maternal mortality to below 70 per 100,000 live births and neonatal mortality to 12 per 1000 live births by 2030 [6]. The recommended schedule includes the first ANC contact before 12 weeks of gestation, followed by two contacts in the second trimester and five in the third trimester [7], thereby strengthening continuity of care and promoting a more person‐centered approach [8, 9].

Despite its proven benefits, adherence to the recommended ANC schedule remains low, particularly in low‐ and middle‐income countries [10]. In sub‐saharan Africa, only a small proportion of women (6.8%) complete the recommended eight contacts [11], while coverage in Ethiopia is estimated to be as low as 3% [12]. Although more than 90% of pregnant women attend at least one ANC visit, this does not necessarily translate into completion of the recommended continuum of care, reflecting persistent gaps in service quality, accessibility, and continuity [12, 13, 14]. Globally, ANC coverage remains suboptimal, with approximately 64% of women completing the recommended contacts [15], while regional estimates show coverage of 53.4% in Eastern Africa and 58.5% in sub‐Saharan Africa [16]. Evidence also indicates that ANC utilization can reduce maternal mortality by about 20%, highlighting its critical role in improving maternal health outcomes [8, 15, 17].

Ethiopia continues to face a high maternal mortality burden, with an estimated 19,000 maternal deaths annually [7] and a maternal mortality ratio of 412 per 100,000 live births [15]. Evidence suggests that late initiation of ANC and inadequate numbers of contacts are associated poorer maternal and neonatal outcomes [8, 18]. However, evidence on adherence to the WHO eight‐contact ANC model remains limited, particularly in peri‐urban settings where population mobility, mixed health service access, and socio‐demographic diversity may influence service utilization differently.

Sululta district, located near Addis Ababa, represents a rapidly urbanizing peri‐urban area where maternal health service utilization patterns remain insufficiently explored. Understanding adherence to the WHO‐recommended eight‐contact ANC model and its associated factors in such contexts is essential for informing targeted interventions and improving implementation of maternal health policies. Therefore, this study aimed to assess adherence to the eight‐contact ANC model and its associated factors among postpartum women in the Sululta district, North Shewa, Ethiopia.

Methods and Materials

Study Area and Period

This study was conducted in the Sululta district, within the district of Oromia, Northwestern Addis Ababa, Ethiopia, from October 10 to November 10, 2024. In this district, there are six health centers and one primary hospital. Of these, two health centers are not fully serving because of security concerns. In 2024, Sululta was reported to have a total population of approximately 199,000. This included 32,899 women of childbearing age (15‐49 years). The average annual number of births in this district is 6644, with 7104 first postnatal visits.

Study Design

An institutional‐based cross‐sectional study design was employed.

Study Populations

The study population consisted of postpartum mothers within 42 days after delivery who had attended at least one ANC contacts during their most recent pregnancy and were admitted to the postpartum units of selected public health facilities in the Sululta district during the study period.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

All mothers admitted to the postpartum care unit during the study period with documented ANC follow‑up records and who provided informed consent were included. Mothers who were referred from facilities outside the district, lacked ANC follow‐up records, or were severely ill and unable to provide consent were excluded to ensure data completeness and consistency.

Sample Size Determination

Sample size was determined using single population proportion formula by taking into account 95% of CI, margin of error (d) (0.050), and proportion (P) of adherence (41%) taken from the most recent facility‐based study done in southern Ethiopia among pregnant women of Arbaminch town [19].

Sampling Procedure

Participants were recruited from postpartum wards of selected public health facilities in the Sululta district. These wards were considered appropriate entry points to reach postpartum women during the immediate postpartum period following delivery. A systematic random sampling technique was employed to select study participants from four health centers and one primary hospital. The total sample size was proportionally allocated to each facility based on the number of eligible postpartum women recorded during the preceding 3‐month period (June to August 2024), which represented the most recent complete and stable reporting period prior to data collection. The sampling frame consisted of 802 eligible postpartum women identified from delivery and postnatal care registration records across the selected health facilities during the study period. This number represented the total source population available for sampling before selection.

Proportional allocation was applied to ensure representativeness of participants across facilities according to their relative client load (see Figure 2). Systematic sampling was then conducted by calculating the sampling interval (K) as K = 802/410 ≈ 2. The first participant in each facility was selected using a simple random lottery method from the first two eligible women and thereafter every second eligible woman was included until the required sample size was achieved.

Variable of the Study

The dependent variable was adherence to the ANC 8+ model. Independent variables included socio‐demographic factors (age, sex, marital status, educational status, occupation); obstetric factors (parity, history of abortion, wanted pregnancy, previous mode of delivery, history of obstetric complications); healthcare‐related factors (waiting time, distance to facility, and cleanliness of facility); knowledge‐related factors (awareness of danger signs and ANC timing); partner support; maternal decision‐making ability; and maternal service satisfaction.

Operational Definition

Adherence to ANC: Women were considered adherent if they initiated their first ANC visit during the first trimester, attended at least eight ANC contacts at the recommended intervals and completed the schedule before delivery [15]. Additionally, women who delivered at or after 38 weeks of gestation after regularly attending ANC contacts on schedule but before reaching eight contacts where classified as adhered to the 8 + ANC. This approach prevented misclassification of women who fully complied with the ANC schedule but delivered early due to biological or obstetric factors beyond their control. No participants met this condition in the present study.

Non‐adherence to ANC: Those women who start ANC after 12 weeks of gestation and fail to attend ANC contact at the recommended schedule were considered as non‐adhere [15].

Timing of eighth ANC contact was defined as the gestational age (in weeks) at which women attended their eighth ANC visit, regardless of whether they completed all recommended ANC contacts. This variable was used solely to describe the distribution of ANC timing and was not used as a criterion for classification of adherence status.

Maternal knowledge about ANC was assessed using 10 structured questions adapted from previously validated ANC knowledge instruments [20]. Each correct response was scored one point, yielding a total score range of 0–10. Based on the total score, mothers were categorized as having poor knowledge [1, 2, 3, 4], fair knowledge [5, 6, 7], and good knowledge [8, 9, 10], consistent with classification used in similar studies [20].

Distance to the facility was defined based on mothers self‐reported travel time rather than physical distance in kilometers. Mothers were asked how long it typically took them to reach the health facility using their usual mode of transport (walking, public transport, or private vehicle). Travel time was categorized as short (< 30 min), moderate (30–60 min), and long > 60 min) [15].

Waiting time referred to the duration mothers waited to receive services at the health facility. It was categorized as short if the waiting time was ≤ 30 min and long if it was > 30 min [19].

Mothers' decision making: Three options were given regarding the decision‐making process: decide by themselves, decide with their partner, and decide only by their partners. From these options mothers those participate during decision‐making process, that means those responded “decide with them self and decide with their partner” were considered to be autonomous and those responded “only by their partner” were considered as not autonomous.

Maternal satisfaction was measured using an 8‐item scale adapted from previous studies conducted among women in Ethiopia. Each items was rated from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree), yielding total scores ranging from 8 to 40 (Cronbach's α = 0.835). Participants scoring ≥ 30 (≥ 75%) were categorized as satisfied, while those scoring < 30 points (< 75%) were categorized as not satisfied. This 75% cutoff was consistent with prior Ethiopia studies [21]. Sensitivity analyses using alternative cutoffs (70%, 80%) and continuous scores produced consistent results, confirming the robustness of the classification.

Partner support was assessed using an 8‐item spousal support scale with responses ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 6 (strongly agree), yielding total scores from 8 to 48. In this study, the mean partner‐support score was 40 (±6), and women scoring above the mean were categorized as having higher support, while those scoring at or below the mean were categorized as having lower support. Although mean cutoffs can be sensitive to outliers, this approach aligns with prior literature [22, 23].

Family size refers to the total number of people living in the household, including the mother, her children, partner, and any other dependents residing in the same home.

Data Collection Tools and Procedures

Data were collected using a structured, interviewer‐administered questionnaire. The questionnaire was adopted from previously published studies conducted in Ethiopia and similar low‑resource settings and modified to fit the objectives and context of the current study after reviewing relevant literature and guidelines [1, 19, 22]. The tool included sections on socio‐demographic characteristics, obstetric factors, healthcare‐related factors, ANC‐related knowledge, partner support, maternal decision‐making power, and maternal satisfaction. In addition to the interview data, relevant clinical and service‐related information was supplemented through a review of patient folders.

The questionnaire was initially prepared in English, translated into the local language (Afaan Oromo) and then back‐translated into English to ensure consistency. Data were collected by trained data collectors under the supervision of field supervisors. Face‐to‐face interviews were conducted in the postnatal care unit after mothers had rested for at least 8 h postpartum and continued until discharge.

Data Quality Control

To ensure data quality, the questionnaire was initially prepared in English, translated into Afaan Oromo, and back‐translated into English by language experts with input from health professionals to maintain consistency and clarity. Data were collected by five trained BSc midwives under the supervision of one senior BSc midwife, all of whom had prior experience in data collection. Before data collection, data collectors received 2 days of training on participant approach, interview techniques, and proper handling of information.

A pretest was conducted 1 week prior to data collection on 5% of the sample (21 mothers) at Muka Turi Hospital to assess clarity, language, and appropriateness, after which necessary adjustments were made. To ensure consistency in data extraction from patient charts, inter‐rater reliability was assessed by double‐entering a random 10% sample of records, yielding a kappa coefficient of 0.86, which indicates strong agreement. Data quality was further monitored through regular supervision of data collectors, daily consistency checks, and verification of extracted information against source documents. During the actual data collection, supervisors checked questionnaires daily for completeness and accuracy.

Data Processing and Analysis

The data were collected using Kobo Toolbox and exported to SPSS Version 25 software for analysis. Descriptive statistics including frequencies and percentages were used to summarize the data. A binary logistic regression model was fitted to identify factors associated with adherence to the WHO recommendation of ANC 8+ contacts. To select potential explanatory variables for the multivariable analysis, bivariable logistic regression was first performed. Variables with a p value ≤ 0.25 in the bivariable analysis were considered for inclusion in the multivariable model. This more liberal threshold was chosen to avoid prematurely excluding potentially important confounders at the bivariate stage, thereby increasing likelihood of identifying true associations after adjusting for other covariates. All candidate variables included in the multivariable model are presented in the results tables, regardless of statistical significance to ensure transparency.

Model fitness was assessed using the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness‐of‐fit test, which evaluates how well predicted probabilities align with observed data. The test result (p = 0.157) was not significant, indicating adequate model fit. To complement this, additional fit statistics were reported: the final model had an Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) = 842.6, Cox & Snell R 2 = 0.21, and Nagelkerke R 2 = 0.28, all supporting acceptable explanatory power and overall fit. Multicollinearity among independent variables was evaluated using variance inflation factors (VIF). The calculated VIF values for all candidate predictors ranged from 1.23 to 3.87, which are well below the commonly used thresholds, indicating no evidence of multicollinearity. Statistical significance was declared at a p‐value ≤ 0.05 with 95% confidence intervals.

Ethical Consideration

Ethical clearance was obtained from the Institutional Ethical Review Board of Arsi University College of health science (Ref. No: A/U/H/S/C/120/4228/17). Permission to conduct the study was obtained from the Sululta district Health Bureau and respective health facilities. Written informed consent was obtained from all study participants prior data collection. Participants were informed about the purpose of the study and confidentiality of their personal information was maintained throughout the study.

Results

Socio‐Demographic Characteristics of Mothers

A total of 410 mothers participated in the study, yielding a 100% response rate. The mean age was 26.7 years (SD ± 5.68), with 133/410 (32.4%) aged 25–29 years. Most participants were urban residents (263/410, 64.1%) and married (394/410, 96.1%). Regarding education, 160/410 (39.0%) had completed at least primary school, and 58.5% were housewives. The mean monthly family income was 7890 ETB (≈51 USD), with 141/410 (34.4%) of mothers reporting an income between 1500 and 5000 ETB (≈10–32 USD) (Table 1).

Obstetric Characteristics of Mothers

Among the 410 postpartum women, 293 (71.2%) were multiparous, and 349 (85.1%) had planned pregnancies. Of the multigravida women, 91/293 (31.1%) reported a history of spontaneous abortion (miscarriage), and 28/293 (6.6%) experienced neonatal or child loss. Regarding the previous delivery prior to the most recent one, 202 women (75.7%) gave birth via spontaneous vaginal delivery. In addition, 108 women (40.4%) reported experiencing at least one obstetric complication during their prior pregnancies, with 38 (35.2%) specifically leakage of fluid per vagina before the onset of labor, consistent with preterm rupture of membranes (PROM). Additionally, 200 (62.7%) of mothers had discontinued ANC schedules for various reasons, and only 151 (36.8%) initiated their first ANC contact at or before 12 weeks of gestation (Table 2).

Personal and Health Facility Related Characteristics

The participants' knowledge scores ranged from 2 to 10, with a mean of 6 (±2); 316/410 (77.1%) women demonstrated good knowledge regarding ANC. Maternal satisfaction scores ranged from 8 to 40, with a mean of 31 (±5); using the predefined cutoff of ≥ 30 points (≥ 75%), 288/410 (70.2%) women were categorized as satisfied. Partner support scores ranged from 12 to 48, with a mean of 40 (± 6); 361/410 (88.0%) women had higher support. Overall, 380/410 (92.7%) women reported autonomous decision‐making. Regarding access to health facilities, 118/410 (28.8%) reported no nearby facility, whereas 305/410 (74.4%) reached a facility within 30 min. Nearly half of the participants (201/410, 49.0%) walked to the health facility for ANC, and the majority (272/410, 66.3%) received services at health centers. Overall, 69.1% obtained ANC within 30 min of travel (Table 3).

Level of Adherence to New 8 Antenatal Care Model

Overall, adherence to the WHO‐recommended eight‐contact ANC model defined as early initiation of ANC and completion of at least eight contacts at the recommended intervals was observed among 104 of the 410 mothers (25.4%, 95% CI: 21%–30%), as shown in Figure 3. The majority of mothers, 306 (74.6%), did not meet this full definition of adherence during their most recent pregnancy.

Factors Associated With Adherence to New 8 Antenatal Care Model

Bivariable logistic regression identified age, residence, maternal and husband's education, husband's occupation, pregnancy planning, previous mode of delivery, history of obstetric complications, type of health facility, maternal knowledge, and satisfaction as candidates for multivariable analysis. Multivariable logistic regression revealed that previous mode of delivery, history of obstetric complications, type of health facility, maternal satisfaction, and residence were significantly associated with adherence.

Mothers with a history of cesarean delivery were 3.3 times more likely to adhere to the eight‐contact ANC model compared to those with prior spontaneous vaginal delivery (AOR: 3.33; 95% CI: 1.39–7.97). Those who had previously experienced at least one obstetric complication were six times more likely to adhere (AOR: 6.16; 95% CI: 2.69–14.10). Mothers satisfied with ANC services had 6.8 times higher odds of adherence than unsatisfied mothers (AOR: 6.78; 95% CI: 2.46–18.66). Receiving ANC at a health center increased adherence likelihood by 2.9 times compared to hospital attendees (AOR: 2.95; 95% CI: 1.24–6.93), and urban residents were three times more likely to adhere than rural residents (AOR: 3.06; 95% CI: 1.17–8.03) (Table 4).

Discussion

This study aimed to determine the level of adherence to the WHO‐recommended eight‐contact ANC model and enabling factors among postpartum women in the Sululta district. We found that only one‐quarter of mothers achieved full adherence, with previous mode of delivery, obstetric complications, type of health facility, maternal satisfaction, and residence significantly associated with adherence. These findings highlight persistent gaps in ANC utilization and underscore the need for targeted interventions to improve maternal health outcomes.

The overall adherence to the WHO‐recommended eight‐contact ANC model in the Sululta district was 25.4%. This finding is comparable to the study conducted in Uganda (23.4%), suggesting similar challenges in implementing the updated ANC model across East Africa settings [24]. However, the adherence level observed in the present study was higher than reports from Bangladesh (6%) [23], Cameroon (9%) [25], Nigeria (17.4%) [20], and another Ethiopian study (3%) [12], but lower than findings from Ghana (41.9%) [26] and Arbaminch town, southern Ethiopia (41%) [19]. These differences may be related to variations in health system readiness, maternal awareness of the updated ANC recommendations, accessibility of maternal health services, and sociocultural contexts across study settings. Overall, the findings indicate that adherence to the WHO‐recommended eight‐contact ANC model remains suboptimal in many low‐resource settings.

Women with a prior cesarean section were more likely to adhere to the 8+ ANC model, consistent with evidence from Ethiopia and other settings [12]. This may reflect heightened risk perception, increased counseling during discharge, and concerns about scar‐related complications, all of which encourage closer follow‐up. Similarly, mothers with a history of obstetric complications demonstrated higher adherence, likely due to the risk of recurrence and the need for closer monitoring. These findings emphasize the importance of tailoring ANC counseling and follow‐up to women with prior complications or surgical deliveries [19].

Residence also played a critical role: urban mothers were significantly more likely to adhere compared to rural mothers. This is supported by the study conducted in Gondar on women intention to use to 8+ ANC [15] and the study which was done in Nigeria [27]. This disparity reflects barriers such as distance to facilities, limited financial resources, and lower awareness of ANC benefits in rural areas. Addressing these inequities requires strengthening rural health infrastructure, improving transport access, and expanding community‐based education.

This study found that maternal satisfaction was significantly associated with adherence to the WHO‐recommended eight‐contact ANC model. Women who reported respectful care, shorter waiting times, and better perceived service quality were more likely to complete all recommended ANC contacts. This finding is consistent with previous studies conducted in Ethiopia [21], which reported that respectful maternal care reduced waiting time, effective provider communication, and better quality of ANC services were associated with improved maternal satisfaction and continued utilization of maternal health services. These findings highlight the potential importance of patient‐centered care in promoting sustained ANC utilization

Interestingly, mothers attending health centers were more likely to adhere the WHO‐recommended ANC contacts compared to those attending hospitals, a finding that contrasts with results reported from Arbaminch town [19]. This observed association should be interpreted cautiously, as differences in referral patterns, patient case mix, and facility functions may partially explain the finding. Hospitals often serve as referral centers for high‐risk pregnancies and complicated obstetric conditions, which may result in higher patient volumes, longer waiting times, and disruptions in continuity of routine ANC follow‐up. In contrast, health centers are more accessible and community‐based, potentially facilitating continuity of care for uncomplicated pregnancies.

To explore whether geographic accessibility could explain this finding, we further examined the relationship between facility type and distance‐related variables in our dataset and found no meaningful correlation. Therefore, the higher adherence observed among women attending health centers is unlikely to be explained solely by proximity to health facilities. This suggests that facility‐level characteristics, such as service organization, patient flow, continuity of care, or provider–client interactions, may contribute to the observed differences in adherence. Nevertheless, given the cross‐sectional nature of the study, causal inferences cannot be established. Further comparative and qualitative studies are needed to better understand the influence of facility type on adherence to the WHO‐recommended ANC model.

Several variables that were significant in bivariate analysis, such as maternal knowledge of ANC benefits, higher education, and husband's occupation, lost statistical significance in the multivariable model after adjustment. This attenuation likely reflects confounding or mediation effects. For instance, maternal knowledge may influence ANC adherence indirectly through mediating pathways such as maternal satisfaction with care and facility preference, rather than exerting a direct effect alone. Similarly, maternal education and occupation may operate through enhanced health literacy, improved health‐seeking behavior, and greater engagement with health services. These findings underscore that ANC utilization is shaped by interrelated social, behavioral, and health system factors rather than single independent predictors. Future studies employing prospective cohort designs are recommended to better capture the timing and frequency of ANC contacts and to address limitations related to recall bias and temporality.

Although maternal autonomy was high in this study (92.7%), it was not significantly associated with adherence to the WHO‐recommended eight‐contact ANC model in the final model. This may suggest that individual‐level decision‐making power alone may be insufficient to ensure full ANC utilization in the presence of structural barriers such as distance to health facilities, accessibility challenges, and health system constraints, which may exert a stronger influence on service uptake.

Overall, the findings have important practical and policy implications. The low adherence to the WHO‐recommended eight‐contact ANC model underscores the need for targeted interventions to improve maternal engagement with ANC services. Potential strategies include reducing waiting times, enhancing maternal satisfaction, and increasing community awareness. The results also emphasize the importance of social and behavioral considerations in maternal health programming. Taken together, these findings provide actionable insights for policymakers and stakeholders to strengthen continuity of care, promote institutional delivery, and improve maternal and neonatal health outcomes at the population level.

Strengths and Limitations

A key strength of this study was the use of maternal medical records alongside self‐reported data, which helped reduce recall bias. Nevertheless, several limitations should be considered. Social desirability bias may have influenced responses, as interviews were conducted within health facilities. The cross‐sectional design also limits causal inference. Excluding severely ill mothers may have underestimated the relationship between poor ANC adherence and adverse outcomes. Additionally, restricting the study population to mothers admitted to postpartum units and excluding those without ANC follow‐up records may have introduced selection bias, systematically omitting women with poor adherence or those who delivered at home. This could bias the sample toward higher ANC adherence and institutional delivery, limiting generalizability of our findings.

In addition, the modest sample size was not powered for subgroup analyses, which may limit the precision of effect estimates; consequently, the wide confidence intervals observed should be acknowledged and the findings interpreted with appropriate caution. Finally, study measurement tools were adapted from prior Ethiopian and similar low‐resource studies and pre‐tested. Internal consistency was acceptable and a sensitivity analysis was confirmed robustness, ensuring contextual validity while highlighting the need for future standardized validation.

Conclusion

The adherence to the WHO‐recommended eight‐contact ANC model among postpartum women in the Sululta district was low, with only one‐quarter of mothers completing all recommended contacts. Factors such as previous mode of delivery, obstetric complications, type of health facility, maternal satisfaction, and residence were significantly associated with adherence. To address these gaps, the Ministry of Health, regional and district health offices, health facility managers, healthcare providers, and community health extension workers should strengthen patient‐centered ANC services, improve service accessibility for rural women, reduce waiting times, and enhance community‐based health literacy to promote completion of recommended ANC contacts. Strengthening implementation of the WHO‐recommended ANC model may improve maternal and neonatal health outcomes.

Given the significant association between maternal satisfaction and adherence, improving respectful care, effective communication, and responsiveness to women's needs may further enhance adherence through strengthened patient‐centered ANC services. Future studies should use longitudinal designs to better understand causes of low adherence and guide sustainable interventions.
