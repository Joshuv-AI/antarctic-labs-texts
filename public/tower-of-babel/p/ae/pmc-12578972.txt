Effect of varying nano-boron nitride content on foamed concrete containing titanium dioxide nanoparticles
Authors: Md Azree Othuman Mydin, Nadhim Hamah Sor, Ziad N. Taqieddin, Haytham F. Isleem, Mohd Mustafa Al Bakri Abdullah, Paul O. Awoyera, Olaolu George Fadugba, Taher A. Tawfik
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study focused on investigating mixing boron nitride (BN) and titanium dioxide (TiO₂) nanoparticles in enhancing the foamed concrete (FC) characteristics. Despite the presence of independent studies on each material addition, there is little study on their combined effects, especially with enhancements in durability, mechanical properties, and thermal performance of FC for sustainable building usage. The current research aims to examine the effects of varying BN doses (0.025–0.1% by weight of cement) with the use of constant 1% TiO₂ on fresh characteristics, microstructure, pore structure, mechanical performance, shrinkage, and thermal behavior. The results showed a slight reduction in slump and setting time, accompanied by a rise in dry density. At 0.075% BN, the 28-day compressive, flexural, and splitting tensile strengths increased by 44.27%, 52.1%, and 57.14%, respectively. Thermal conductivity increased by 7.2%, whereas shrinkage decreased by 52.30% at 28 days. Mercury intrusion porosimetry (MIP) proved the enhanced pore architecture characterized by a reduced average pore diameter and an increased volume proportion of fine capillary pores. The findings demonstrate the capability of the BN–TiO₂ hybrid system to greatly improve the durability and thermal performance of FC, causing an increase in service life and a decrease in environmental impact.

Introduction

The rapid growth of concrete buildings within the construction industry is a developing phenomenon in contrast to the advancements in wood construction and masonry1–3. However, the numerous advantages of concrete materials have led to their global acceptance and widespread use in infrastructure and building construction projects. These advantages include high impact resistance, superior thermal and insulation characteristics, excellent fire resistance, adequate flowability, and exceptional strength4–8. Previously, the authors utilized many waste materials as cement replacements to enhance their mechanical and durability characteristics, such as fly ash9,10, slag11,12, cement kiln dust13, and metakaolin14,15. Moreover, many studies were performed on the utilization of ultra-fine materials as cement replacements, such as nano clay16, eggshell powder17, and sunflower ash18,19. Negm et al.20 studied the use of grounded vitrified clay pipe (GVCP) waste as a supplementary cementitious material (SCM) in producing green concrete. The optimum cement substitution levels were 10% for normal-strength concrete and 20% for high-strength concrete in improving mechanical characteristics and durability, including resistance to chloride penetration. Negm et al.20 demonstrated that GVCP is a feasible, environmentally friendly alternative for minimizing the carbon footprint of concrete while enhancing its performance. Shawki et al.21 investigated the use of marble powder (MP) as a partial replacement for cement in self-compacting concrete (SCC), revealing that a mixture containing 10% MP and 33% fly ash achieved favorable outcomes. Shawki et al.21 showed that mixes containing MP need more water to achieve similar workability. Nevertheless, they maintained enhanced rheological properties and reduced cement use by up to 11%, leading to economic and environmental benefits. The results indicated that mixtures, including MP, necessitated increased water for comparable workability. However, they preserved superior rheological characteristics and decreased cement use by as much as 11%, providing economic and environmental advantages21. The use of concrete has been extensive owing to its many advantages, leading to a substantial increase in the prevalence of concrete structures compared to other forms of construction22–26. Furthermore, ongoing enhancements are being carried out27,28. The utilization of foamed concrete (FC) is well-known as a substantial material within the worldwide building sector29–32. Compared to conventional building materials, FC can improve performance and cost-effectiveness across several applications33.

Foamed concrete (FC) can be described as a lightweight material exhibiting a porous or cellular composition, which allows it to flow freely. It is adaptable and can be efficiently employed in various contexts34. FC exhibits a variety of densities, commonly ranging from 450 to 1950 kg/m3. The material’s compressive strength varies between 1 MPa and 20 MPa35–37. Nevertheless, despite increasing recognition within the construction sector, FC has significant limitations. The characteristics mentioned earlier encompass its inherent brittleness, heightened shrinkage, notable permeability, and elevated susceptibility to cracking because of its porous composition and voids. The constraints impose restrictions on using it in load-bearing applications within the field of building construction38–40. Several research studies have indicated that FC may demonstrate volatility, especially when there is a decline in density. Nevertheless, even minor fluctuations in FC density can substantially affect FC durability. The decrease in FC density led to a notable rise in voids with larger dimensions41. Additionally, it has been shown that when the density of FC is low, it displays a significant vulnerability to fracture42. The application of nanoparticles is a well-established notion in natural phenomena and scientific investigation43.

Nanotechnology is a rapidly expanding area that attracts scientists from various industries, including the construction sector, who actively seek to make progress in the area. Concrete is the most extensively utilized construction material globally due to its cost-effectiveness and structural robustness. In 2019, cement production reached around 4.2 billion tons, driven by a consistent annual increase in demand44. Researchers in the field of science and engineering persist in grappling with the challenge of augmenting the stability of structures while concurrently striving to improve their durability, cost-effectiveness, and safety. In addition, it should be noted that concrete has superior stiffness, durability, and compressive strength characteristics; nonetheless, it is essential to acknowledge its inherent brittleness and limited tensile strength45. The latest developments in nanotechnology indicate that hybrid nanocomposites can achieve greater mechanical characteristics through improving interfacial bonding and dispersion of nanoparticles. Çelebi, Çelebi et al.46 found that extending milling duration significantly enhanced the tensile strength and hardness of B₄C–GNP-reinforced AA2024 composites because of microstructural refinement. In similar research, Çelebi et al.47 determined that GNPs demonstrated excellent reinforcing performance compared to other carbon-based additives, whereas Çelebi et al.48 showed that extended milling enhanced the wear resistance of ZA40–B₄C–graphene nanocomposites by improving particle dispersion and decreasing porosity.

It is essential to acknowledge that materials at the nanoscale show distinct characteristics compared to their counterparts at the microscale. Various concrete categories are formulated and tailored to specific requirements by altering mixture design, building procedures, and application contexts. To mitigate certain significant drawbacks of concrete, such as its high porosity and poor hydration, researchers have explored the incorporation of nanoparticles to modify their characteristics49,50. Concrete experiences a decline in strength and durability due to the presence of pores, which comprise around 2–6% of the overall concrete volume51. Consequently, by filling these detrimental voids with nanoparticles, the deterioration in the durability and strength of concrete can be counterbalanced. Recent studies focus on studying the important role of nanomaterials in improving thermal, mechanical, and durability characteristics in many engineering applications. Theja et al.52 revealed that nano-TiO₂ enhanced the strength and durability of GGBS-based geopolymer concrete during ambient curing. Heris et al.53 and Mohammadfam & Heris54 showed that graphene-COOH/water and ternary magnetic nanofluids greatly improved heat transfer and thermal conductivity in plate heat exchangers and circular tubes, respectively. Heris et al.55 illustrated that the modification of MWCNT/water nanofluids showed long-term dispersion stability while improving exergy efficiency. Zeinali Heris et al.56 studied tribological nanolubricants, which showed huge enhancements in heat conductivity and friction reduction by using TiO₂–MnO₂–GO hybrids. In the energy industry, Pourpasha et al.57 found that ZnFe₂O₄ nanoparticles improved heat transmission and dielectric strength in insulating oils. Kargaran et al.58 illustrated the promise of oscillating heat pipes using nanofluids for cooling photovoltaic thermal systems. However, Soodmand et al.59 studied the modeling of phase change materials (PCMs), including nanofillers, that highlighted difficulties in three-dimensional geometries and stability. These results together verify the multifunctionality of nanomaterials such as TiO₂, graphene derivatives, and BN in improving concrete and fluid systems for thermal, structural, and energy applications.

In research by Mohseni et al.60, an investigation was carried out to examine the combined impact of nano-titanium dioxide, nano-alumina, and nano-silica on self-compacting mortar’s mechanical, rheological, and durability characteristics. The study also incorporated the use of fly ash as an additive. The findings suggest that the compressive strength of nanoparticles, namely nano-alumina, nano-silica, and nano-titanium dioxide, was optimized at binder concentrations of 1%, 3%, and 5%, respectively, when each nanoparticle was utilized independently. In the present combination, there was a notable rise in strength, culminating on the 90th day. Joshaghani61 conducted a study in which a mixture of nanoparticles, namely nano-titanium dioxide and carbon nanofibers (CNF), was utilized to substitute cement partially. Nanoparticles have the potential to enhance the pore structure of concrete and thus enhance its durability and strength when employed as a partial substitute for cement. Gencel et al.62 studied the impact of incorporating basalt fibers and silica fume on foam concrete’s mechanical properties and durability. The test findings indicated substantial enhancements, with a compressive strength of 46 MPa, as silica fume improved the pore structure and fiber-matrix bonding. This study illustrated the feasibility of producing exceptionally durable and insulating foam concrete suitable for structural applications. Nevertheless, Gencel et al.63 investigated the use of rice husk ash (RHA) as a cement substitute and waste marble powder (WMP) as a sand substitute in FC exposed to high temperatures and freeze-thaw cycles. Incorporating 10% RHA and 50% WMP improved mechanical properties and reduced drying shrinkage, particularly after 90 days. However, mixtures with 10% RHA but no WMP showed a strength reduction of up to 59.8% during freeze-thaw conditions. On the other hand, Özkan et al.64 found that the use of sawdust (SD) as a substitute for silica sand (SS) in foam concrete reduces environmental impact and investigates industrial byproducts. A 25% SD1 (0–1 mm) blend with 500 kg/m³ cement achieved the highest compressive strength (3.56 MPa) after 28 days, while 100% SD4 (0–4 mm) improved thermal insulation by 17%. Another study investigated the impact of the water/binder (w/b) ratio and pigment concentration on the compressive and flexural strengths of colored concrete. Experiments with different water-to-binder ratios and pigment concentrations showed that both variables substantially affect mechanical characteristics65. Bayraktar et al.66 revealed that the blend with 10% ceramic powder and 0.6% waste polyester exhibited the greatest compressive strength (8.78 MPa) and commendable resistance to dry shrinkage. Still, the formulation with 0% ceramic powder and 0.2% waste polyester demonstrated superior thermal insulation. Gencel et al.57 noted that the use of bottom ash and recycled fine aggregates resulted in reduced thermal conductivity of foam concrete (FC); however, mixtures containing limestone sand demonstrated superior physico-mechanical properties. Gencel et al.67 also demonstrated that discarded waste glass sand enhanced the physico-mechanical and durability properties, while expanded perlite improved thermal insulation due to its high porosity. Benli68 investigated waste glass powder and sand as partial replacements for granulated blast-furnace slag and river sand. The alkali-activated foam concrete with 50% glass sand showed a 71.4% increase in compressive strength (3.48 MPa) compared to the reference mix, along with improved sorptivity and thermal conductivity. Bayraktar et al.69 demonstrated that basalt fiber (BF) content had a variable effect on compressive strength depending on the foaming agent concentration. The best thermal insulation (0.645 W/mK) was achieved with 1% BF and 50 kg/m³ foam, while the combination of 1% BF and 30 kg/m³ foam showed the highest resistance to magnesium sulfate. Additionally, 2% BF with 30 kg/m³ foam provided optimal resistance to hydrochloric acid.

In contrast, boron nitride (BN) is an isoelectronic counterpart to graphene, exhibiting comparable characteristics such as elevated heat conductivity, substantial hardness, and notable mechanical strength70,71. Nevertheless, nano-BN exhibits distinct features that diverge significantly from graphene, mostly attributed to the disparity between B-N and C = C bonds. Nano-BN has superior thermochemical stability compared to graphene and holds promise as a novel high-performance inorganic material for mitigating short circuits in lithium batteries72. Furthermore, BN has significant oxidation resistance, rendering it very resistant to corrosion caused by sulfuric acid73,74. Additionally, the barrier characteristics of BN and its exceptional attraction for molecular hydrogen (H2) make it a promising candidate for applications in pollutant purification, adsorption, and gas storage75–77. Latest studies have shown that combining hexagonal boron nitride (h-BN) with durable ceramic nanoparticles such as B₄C or SiC in metal matrix nanocomposites can have positive effects on both materials. Çelebi et al.78 showed that adding 2 wt% h-BN to Al2024/B₄C composites eliminated wear loss by up to 75% because it improved the lubrication and the particles spread out more evenly. Çanakçı et al.79 further stated that Al-based hybrid composites with more B₄C were harder, stronger in tensile strength, and better at resisting corrosion. Karabacak et al.80 carried out further studies on AlSi10Mg–h-BN nanocomposites made using a laser-based powder bed process. They found that adding h-BN enhanced their resistance to wear and decreased the friction coefficient in particular when using 3 wt% h-BN. These results indicate that BN can be used in numerous applications in hybrid systems in particular within improving durability, tribological behavior, and structural integrity by acting as a lubricant and barrier.

Researchers have investigated individual usage of nanoparticles such as TiO₂, SiO₂, and nano-silica in cementitious composites to enhance concrete characteristics. However, the use of boron nitride (BN), especially in combination with TiO₂, is still not widely recognized, especially in foamed concrete (FC) systems. FC is characterized by its lightweight and good insulation properties, but it often fails to provide enough mechanical strength or durability. To solve this issue, the current study investigates the hybrid addition of a constant 1% TiO₂ with different percentages of BN (0%, 0.025%, 0.05%, 0.075%, and 0.1% by weight of cement) for the first time in order to evaluate the mixture’s impact on the fresh properties, mechanical performance, microstructure, shrinkage behavior, thermal performance, and early-age durability of FC. The novelty of this study is that it examines the new nano-hybrid system (BN–TiO₂) in FC and explains its mechanism of operation to improve both mechanical and durability properties. This assists in producing lightweight concretes that are high-performance, long-lasting, and in line with modern construction and environmental goals.

Materials

Cement

The FC preparation involves using Ordinary Portland Cement (OPC) grade 52.5 MPa, obtained from CIMA, Malaysia, according to BS-EN197-181. Table 1 illustrates the OPC chemical composition.

The fine aggregate utilized in this experimental program consisted of fine river sand. The parameters of aggregates, as outlined in ASTMC33-0372, include a specific gravity of 3.15 and a maximum particle size of 4.75 mm. Figure 1 displays the grading curve for the utilized fine river sand.

Potable water

The mixing process involved using potable water and adhering to the guidelines outlined in BS-EN 314883.

The protein-based foaming agent, Noraite PA-1, provided by DRN Technologies Sdn Bhd in Malaysia, conforms to the specifications outlined in ASTM C796/C796M-1984. Synthesis of hybrid TiO2 nanoparticles and BN

Figure 4 illustrates synthesizing a hybrid TiO2 and BN material.

Step 1

A specific quantity of BN is introduced into a nitric acid solution. The resulting mixture is then subjected to continuous stirring for 6 h.

Step 2

A predetermined quantity of TiO2 nanoparticles was subjected to acoustic energy to stimulate particle movement inside a sample immersed in deionised water for 45 min.

Step 3

A solution with an optimal concentration of 65% nitric acid was gradually introduced into the solution of TiO2 nanoparticles while maintaining a constant stirring motion for 5 h.

Step 4

The aqueous solutions underwent a series of four washes utilising deionised water to eliminate any remaining acid residues.

Step 5

During the blending process, a predefined quantity of BN was introduced into the solution containing TiO2 nanoparticles. After undergoing a continuous stirring operation for 5 h, a stable solution was successfully produced. The composite material utilised in the solution consisted of a hybrid composition comprising BN and TiO2 nanoparticles.

Fig. 4Synthesis of hybrids composed of TiO2 nanoparticles and BN.

Synthesis of hybrids composed of TiO2 nanoparticles and BN.

The BN exhibited a negative charge, while the surface charge of the TiO2 nanoparticles was sensitive to the solution’s acidity level. In a nitric acid medium, the surface of TiO2 nanoparticles exhibits a positive charge; consequently, this positive surface charge facilitates the constant creation of a BN-TiO2 nanocomposite form with the negatively charged BN monolayer via electrostatic attraction upon incorporation into cement-based materials such as FC. The energy required for the adsorption of TiO2 nanoparticles onto BN in the BN-TiO2 composite was negative, indicating that the two-dimensional heterogeneous structures achieved lower energy and a more rigid arrangement, which led to a decrease in entropy when incorporated into FC.

To accomplish the goals of this research, five different mixes of FC were formulated, each including varying proportions of BN ranging from 0 to 0.10%. Additionally, all combinations maintained a consistent weight% of 1% TiO2 nanoparticles by weight of the cement content. The density of FC and the cement-to-sand and water-to-cement ratios for all mixtures were consistently set at 1050 kg/m³, 1:1.5, and 0.47, respectively. Experimental works

The primary objective of this study was to investigate the effect of TiO2 and BN nanoparticles on different FC properties, such as their thermal characteristics, pore structure properties, mechanical characteristics, transport characteristics, and fresh properties. Figure 5 presents a detailed summary of the experimental plan.

Fresh state properties

Flow table test

The workability of using different proportions of BN in combination with a consistent proportion of TiO2 nanoparticles in FC was evaluated by implementing a flow table test, often referred to as a slump test, following the guidelines outlined in ASTM C230-9785. Figure 6-a depicts the experimental setup for conducting the flow table test. The correct positioning of the cone-shaped mold necessitates its placement at the exact center of the flat circular shield. Following this, the freshly made concrete was meticulously poured into the mold in a cone shape. This procedure eliminated surplus concrete beyond the mold’s maximum capacity to achieve a flat and uniform surface. Subsequently, the cone-shaped mold was removed. The recently produced FC was allowed to distribute uniformly throughout the spherical shield. The measurement was performed at the moment when the spreading of the substance ceased, enabling the estimation of the slump flow.

Setting time

The FC specimens were produced and evaluated using the protocols specified in BS EN 196–386. The specimens utilized in this study were prepared by varying the amounts of BN while keeping the proportion of TiO2 nanoparticles constant. These samples were then evaluated for their setting time and soundness. A single specimen was utilized to conduct the setting time test for every batch of the mixture. The amount of water required to achieve a predetermined consistency level was adjusted during the mixing procedure. To ensure uniformity, it is crucial to maintain a specific range of 6 ± 2 mm between the plunger and the base plate while releasing the plunger. The mixture was then meticulously placed into a Vicat mold and completely immersed in a water container in a temperature-controlled chamber set at 20 ± 1 °C. It was kept in this environment until the first and final phases were achieved. Figure 6-b depicts the experimental setup for conducting the flow table test.

Fig. 6Fresh concrete tests of FC: (a) slump test and (b) consistency test.

Fresh concrete tests of FC: (a) slump test and (b) consistency test.

Fresh density

The density test for fresh concrete followed the guidelines specified in the BS En 12350–687. The mass of a container with a known volume, denoted as Vc, was measured and recorded as mce. Subsequently, the container was filled with newly mixed concrete in two layers, with each layer occupying around 50% of the container’s height. Subsequently, upon depositing each layer into the container, it was compacted using a vibrating table for the shortest duration required to completely compact the concrete without introducing excessive laitance or segregation. Once the uppermost layer had been compressed, the surface was carefully smoothed using a trowel, and the exterior of the container was thoroughly cleaned to provide a clean appearance. The mass of the container and its contents was determined by re-weighing, and the resulting value was recorded as mcf.. The formula (mcf. - mce)/vc was utilized to compute the fresh density.

Transport properties

Sorptivity test

To evaluate moisture ingress, the sorptivity was measured, which is the porous material’s ability to absorb and transfer water through capillary action. Cubic samples of FC with dimensions of 50 × 50 × 50 mm in size were used for the rate of water absorption test, which was conducted on the 7th and 28th days. After the curing process for 7 and 28 days, the specimens were dried in an oven for about 24 h in preparation for the test. The mortar cubes heated in an oven were subsequently placed in a container on a flat surface. They were partially submerged in 3.5 mm of water, with their top faces in contact with the sample supports, as seen in Fig. 7-a. To mitigate evaporation, the container was well covered. The water absorption rates for each combination were measured according to the guidelines specified in ASTM C1403-1588. The measurements were taken at 10, 20, 30, 40, and 50 h.

Intrinsic air permeability

To assess pore connectivity as well as potential durability, the intrinsic air permeability was determined, which is measuring the easiness of air that can flow through a porous material under pressure. The intrinsic air permeability results for hardened mortar mixes were assessed by employing an air permeability cell, following the methodology suggested by Cabrera and Lynsdale89. Cylindrical test samples, measuring 50 mm in diameter and 45 mm in height, were subjected to a drying process in an oven for 72 h to eliminate moisture content. The oven-dried FC cylinders were subsequently placed into an air permeability cell, as seen in Fig. 7-b. After the samples were correctly installed within the cell, a gas pressure of 2 bar was introduced to the upper end of the sample. The pressure applied to the other side of the sample was constantly kept at an ambient pressure of 1 bar. A time period of 15 min was designated to establish a stable condition in the flow of nitrogen gas. The determination of gas flow rate through the samples was achieved by monitoring the duration of gas flow across a constant distance within a flow meter with a diameter of 3 mm, which was linked to the permeability cell. Mechanical properties

Compressive strength

The compressive strength of FC was assessed by altering the BN ratios while maintaining a constant proportion of TiO2 nanoparticles. This evaluation was conducted using a compression-testing machine (Fig. 8-a) at ages 7, 28, and 56 days. The loading rate utilized for the experiment was set at 0.1 (kN/s) according to the standards outlined in BS En 12390–390. Three specimens, each with cubic dimensions of 100 mm, were utilized for testing purposes for each combination.

Flexural strength

Three prisms with 100 × 100 × 500 mm dimensions were utilized at 7, 28, and 56 days to evaluate the four-point flexural strength of different concrete mixes, as seen in Fig. 8-b. This assessment was conducted following the parameters specified in BS En 12390–591.

Splitting tensile strength

The concrete splitting tensile strength was evaluated using the approach depicted in Fig. 8-c, per the recommendations stated in BS En 12390–692. For this assessment, curing times of 7, 28, and 56 days were observed. Cylindrical 100 × 200 mm specimens were constructed for each distinct concrete mix.

Elastic modulus

The modulus of elasticity was determined in accordance with the guidelines outlined in ASTM C46993. Cylinder samples with dimensions of Ø100 × 200 were subjected to a curing process in water and subsequently evaluated using various mixes at 28 days.

Drying shrinkage

The experiment’s objective was to examine and quantify the drying shrinkage of different concrete mixtures. The rate of free drying shrinkage for different combinations was assessed using prismatic specimens of 75 × 75 × 285 mm, per the ASTMC157 standard94. The measurements were taken at intervals, namely at 0, 10, 20, 30, 40, and 50 days, as seen in Fig. 8-d. At each age, the drying shrinkage of the specimen was assessed, with consistent utilization of the orientation and the same side throughout all measurements. As mentioned earlier, the methodology was implemented to reduce the potential inaccuracies resulting from warping.

Thermal properties

The experiment on thermal conductivity was conducted with FC samples of 28 × 28 × 12 mm, following the guidelines specified in the ASTMC17795 standard. The study aimed to quantify the FC specimens’ thermal conductivity, thermal diffusivity, and specific heat capacity. Figure 9 provides a complete illustration of the experimental setup. The concrete panels underwent an initial polishing process on both surfaces to achieve the most effective contact between the specimens and the hot or cold plates. The specimens were later exposed to oven drying to reduce the potential influence of moisture on the thermal conductivity of the FC samples. The panel specimen was put into a container and subsequently positioned onto a hot plate, with a cold plate on top to provide coverage. A heat source was applied to provide a continuous temperature of 40 ◦C on the hot plate, while a chiller system was utilised to maintain a constant temperature of 25 ◦C on the cold plate. The control unit regulated the temperature difference between the cold and hot plates. A Campbell Scientific CR3000 data recorder was installed in the sealed container to enable temperature monitoring. The temperatures were recorded at one-minute intervals, and the experiment was conducted for 20 h for each specimen. The thermal conductivity of the FC samples was assessed using the transmission of heat, temperature gradient, and analysis of specimen form96. Calculations were then performed to determine the thermal diffusivity values and specific heat capacity.

Pore structure characteristics

The investigation of pore structure involved assessing water absorption on concrete specimens and using mercury intrusion porosimetry (MIP) on paste specimens.

The water-permeable porosity of FC was assessed per the guidelines stated in ASTM C642. The mass of a concrete mass, denoted as mA, was determined during 24 h of drying at a temperature of 105°C, followed by a subsequent cooling process within a desiccator. The specimens were thereafter entirely submerged in water for 48 h at a temperature of 21 °C. They were then subjected to boiling in tap water for 5 h, followed by a cooling period of 24 h until reaching a temperature of 21 °C. The specimens underwent surface drying and subsequent weighing to determine their surface dry mass, denoted as mB. Results and discussion

Fresh properties

Setting time

The findings depicted in Fig. 10 demonstrate a reduction in setting time as the concentration of BN increased. As the concentration of BN rose from 0 to 0.1%, there was a drop in both the initial and final setting times. The initial setting time fell from 85 min to 69 min, while the final setting time was reduced from 204 min to 184 min. The acceleration ratios observed during the initial setting time were 8.23%, 12.9%, 15.3%, and 18.82% for mixtures containing 0.025%, 0.050%, 0.075%, and 0.10% BN, respectively, in comparison to the control mixture. Nevertheless, the observed decrease in the final setting was 4.41%, 6.37%, 7.35%, and 9.8% for BN concentrations of 0.025, 0.050, 0.075, and 0.10%, respectively. Previous studies have demonstrated that an increase in the dose of BN leads to a decrease in both the initial and final setting time of the blended binder. Several factors exist that contribute to the decline. One primary factor is that incorporating BN with TiO2 nanoparticles at the nanoscale level can serve as crystal nuclei, accelerating cement hydration. Consequently, this reduces the hydration dormant period and substantially decreases the initial setting time97. In addition, it has been observed that the interaction between nanoparticles and Ca(OH)2 might facilitate the pozzolanic reaction, enhancing the hydration procedure and decreasing the final setting time98. The present findings align with those of Majeed et al.99, who noted that raising the concentration of silicon dioxide nanoparticles to 2.5% led to a reduction in the initial and final setting times of FC by 27.34% and 14.74%, respectively. Moreover, Zhang et al.100 observed a comparable impact of nanoparticles on both the initial and final setting times of concrete, acting as an accelerator. On the contrary, She et al.101 stated that introducing mixtures with a short setting time can stabilize air bubbles and reduce the range of pore sizes, leading to enhanced mechanical strength and durability.

Slump flow

The drop of concrete is commonly utilized as an indicator of its workability. Figure 11 displays the outcomes of the slump flow test for including FC with different proportions of BN. Subsequently, measurements of the slump were recorded, yielding values of 24.5 cm for (M0) and 24.2 cm for (M4). The findings indicate that incorporating FC with different proportions of BN while maintaining a constant ratio of TiO2 nanoparticles resulted in a little reduction in the concrete workability. When comparing the slump flow of FC without the addition of BN, it was observed that the inclusion of BN at concentrations of 0.025, 0.05, 0.075, and 0.1% resulted in a decrease in slump flow by 0.41%, 0.81%, 0.81%, and 1.22%, respectively. The large surface area of the nanoparticles and the unique physical characteristics of BN are responsible for the observed decrease in slump flow with increased BN concentration. Compared to spherical nanoparticles, its hydrophobic, platelet-like properties cause a greater reduction in fluidity by increasing friction and interfering with the water layer between particles. Moreover, BN nanosheets have an ability to agglomerate, hence restricting dispersion and decreasing the availability of free water for lubrication. Kong et al.102 verified considerable slump reductions in cementitious systems with BN nanosheets, but Ranjkesh Roudi et al.103 showed decreased workability in BN-modified composites in comparison with controls.

The findings of this result were largely consistent with the concrete workability test results, as reported by Khalaf et al.104. Khalaf et al.104 observed that including nanoparticles reduced the concrete slump. As the quantity of nanomaterials grows, there is a corresponding gradual reduction in slump. One possible explanation is that nanoparticles have a specific surface area, whereas higher packing efficiency and surface interactions caused by BN’s structure may slightly raise the need for efficient dispersion in water without changing the mix’s overall water content. The present findings align with previous research105,106, where Mydin et al.106 discovered that increasing the percentage of nanosilica [from 0 to 5%] in FC reduced its workability. However, Mydin et al.107 noted that increasing the weight fractions of ferrous–ferric oxide nanoparticles [from 0.1 to 0.35%] improved the workability of FC.

A notable reduction in air permeability was detected in all mortar mixes, which included different proportions of BN compared to the control FC without any BN component. The observed decline in the intrinsic air permeability value with increasing levels of cement substitution by BN up to 0.075% may likely be attributed to the predominant influence of the micro-filler properties of BN particles. This finding improved pore structures in BN FCs for 28 days109,110. Nevertheless, a further augmentation in BN content (0.1%) continues to contribute to the decline in the intrinsic air permeability of the mixture during this specific age. Because of a weight fraction of BN over 0.75%, the inherent air permeability of FC rose. This can be attributed to the accumulation of BN, which reduces its ability to fill the voids. At the age of 28 days, the addition of BN at levels of 0.025%, 0.05%, 0.075%, and 0.1% in the FC mixtures led to a decrease in the intrinsic air permeability of the mixture by 8.62%, 14.69%, 23.57%, and 18.78%, respectively, compared to the FC specimen without any additives. Compared to the control FC mixture, all FC mixtures containing silicon dioxide nanoparticles demonstrated decreased intrinsic air permeability values. Notably, adding silicon dioxide nanoparticles at concentrations between 0.5% and 2.5% led to a reduction in inherent air permeability of approximately 14.29% to 35.09%, respectively.

Mechanical properties

Compressive strength

Figure 17 displays the outcomes of FC mixtures, namely the compressive strength from day 7 to day 56. The experiments involved the concentration of BN varying between 0.025% and 0.1%. The compressive strength of FC mixes with BN was higher than that of the reference mixture throughout all test days. In the early stages of hydration, namely within the first 56 days, it was noted that introducing cement replacement materials at varying amounts, ranging from 0% to 0.1% in increments of 0.075%, led to a progressive decline in the FC compressive strength. Beyond 0.075% of nano-BN led to some reduction in strength because the cementing system seems to be saturated, and poor dispersion of the nanoparticles generated by their high surface area may create weak zones in the system. This finding is in agreement with previous work113,114. Including BN at concentrations of 0.025%, 0.05%, 0.075%, and 0.1% relative to the cement content leads to enhancements in the compressive strength of the concrete mixture after 7 days. These improvements are measured at 34.75%, 41.96%, 54.09%, and 20.32%, respectively, compared to the control mixture M0, which exhibits a compressive strength of 3.77 MPa. The observed gains in compressive strength for the combinations over the M0 mix after 28 days (24.93%, 31.03%, 44.27%, and 15.64%) align with findings reported in the previous studies115–117. Similarly, at 56 days, the same mixes demonstrate improvements of around 25%, 30.89%, 46.46%, and 18.16%. Due to their extensive surface area, nanoparticles can serve as nucleation sites during cement hydration. This indicates they offer supplementary surfaces for forming hydration products, such as calcium silicate hydrates (C-S-H). This improved nucleation expedites the initial hydration process, resulting in a more rapid and denser formation of C-S-H, the primary factor in the strength and durability of concrete. TiO₂ and BN may enhance the mechanical characteristics of foam concrete by facilitating nucleation, leading to a denser and more refined microstructure. TiO₂ nanoparticles may engage with hydration products, especially calcium silicate hydrate, and integrate into the microstructure. TiO₂ can affect the distribution of hydration products, potentially improving foam concrete’s compressive strength and durability. BN nanoparticles are recognized for their superior thermal conductivity and chemical stability. The interaction of BN with hydration products may be less pronounced than that of TiO₂. Yet, its incorporation into the matrix can improve thermal performance and high-temperature resistance while enhancing overall mechanical stability. Using BN may enhance foam concrete’s durability in harsh locations by mitigating deterioration caused by environmental conditions and a more compact and sophisticated microstructure. One possible explanation for this phenomenon is BN’s efficient filling of pores with similar dimensions. Furthermore, incorporating BN with the nucleation effect results in the interweaving of calcium-silicate-hydrate (C-S-H) gels produced on its surface, which substantially enhances the structural integrity of cementitious composites115. These findings align with the results reported by the authors in a separate study on concrete filled with nanoparticles82.

On the other hand, BN nanoparticles have significant reactivity and a pronounced propensity to aggregate into clusters when present in excess. When the concentration of BN surpasses the ideal 0.075%, these nanoparticles may aggregate rather than disperse evenly inside the matrix. The agglomeration creates weak zones in the concrete, where the clusters of BN function as flaws, diminishing the overall integrity and compressive strength of the material. Fundamentally, instead of reinforcing the concrete, the agglomerated particles provide locations of vulnerability. In addition, excess BN nanoparticles may compromise the pore architecture of the foam concrete. Higher concentrations of BN can increase porosity rather than fill gaps or strengthen the matrix, resulting in diminished compressive strength. An increased number of pores and microcracks diminishes the material’s load-bearing capacity, rendering the concrete more vulnerable to failure under compression. Furthermore, excess BN nanoparticles may impede the development of hydration products, such as calcium silicate hydrate (C-S-H), which is essential for the compressive strength of concrete. The excessive presence of BN may disrupt the cement hydration process, diminishing the production of C-S-H, which is crucial for robust bonding and a thick microstructure.

Drying shrinkage

Drying shrinkage is a critical factor that contributes to cracking in foamed concrete (FC), ultimately affecting its long-term durability45. The reason for this shrinkage is often that FC has a lower volume of solid content than conventional concrete, which makes it more prone to contraction during drying115,116. Figure 22 presents the drying shrinkage results over a 56-day curing period. A consistent increase in shrinkage was observed across all mixtures over time. Among them, Mix M3 (containing 0.075% BN) exhibited the lowest shrinkage of 0.33 mm at 56 days, whereas the control mix (M0) reached a maximum of 0.75 mm. This indicates a 56% reduction in drying shrinkage with the optimal BN content. Although the shrinkage for Mix M4 (0.1% BN) slightly increased compared to M3, it still remained significantly lower than that of M0. A number of linked reasons are responsible for the observed reduction in drying shrinkage. First, the addition of BN improves matrix densification by decreasing capillary pore volume, which causes a decrease in capillary tension that is considered a key factor contributing to shrinking. This is supported by SEM results, which show that BN-modified mixtures have a denser microstructure with finer, more uniformly distributed pores. Second, despite BN being characterized by its hydrophobicity, moisture can physically adsorb onto its surface due to its large surface area and nano-layered structure. These nanoparticles may act as short-term moisture buffers during pre-soaking that leads to releasing water gradually as the material cures. This process maintains hydration and promotes the continuous development of calcium silicate hydrate (C-S-H) gel by facilitating internal curing especially at older ages. Improved C-S-H formation aids in shrinkage reduction by enhancing the dimensional stability of the hardened matrix.-layered structure. The samples with different ratios of BN demonstrated an increase in thermal conductivity, which can be attributed to the refinement of pore structures. This phenomenon may also be ascribed to the reduced pore size observed in the control FC sample, which exhibited enhanced thermal insulation properties. According to the results, the thermal conductivity rose as the BN content increased. This can be attributed to the rise in solid content besides the BN material binding the FC cementitious matrix and reducing the voids within the FC. The FC thermal diffusivity values with weight percentages of 0.025, 0.05, and 0.075 wt% showed increases of 7.40%, 10.53%, and 13.78% compared to the control sample. The thermal diffusivity of mixture M4 exhibited a decrease relative to that of mix M3; however, it remained more significant than the thermal diffusivity of the control mixture (M0). The findings indicated that including BN resulted in a modest enhancement in heat conduction in the pastes. One possible explanation for the observed increase in thermal diffusivity might be due to the elevated thermal conductivity of BN, which is comparable to that of graphene due to its isoelectronic nature73,115. Additionally, this effect may also be caused by the uniform dispersion of BN in the cement matrix125–127. Another factor is the increased formation of hydration products, especially calcium silicate hydrate (C-S-H) that leads to a denser microstructure in the FC. Beyond thermal conductivity, the improvement in diffusivity is also strongly linked to microstructural compaction and moisture content reduction. As illustrated in SIM images (Fig. 30), M2 and M3 mixes show more refined and compacted internal structure as well as fewer and smaller pores when compared to the control mix (M0). This reduction in porosity enables more effective heat transfer by limiting insulating air pockets and forming a continuous heat flow path within the matrix. Moreover, moisture content plays a key role. The water absorption results (Fig. 13) show a noticeable reduction in absorbed moisture for M1–M3. Lower water content means less latent heat absorption and fewer phase change effects that causes more stable and higher thermal diffusivity. These findings are further supported by the increase in dry density (Fig. 11) and reduction in average pore diameter (Fig. 28), which confirm improved matrix densification and compaction. Despite the higher BN content, M4’s slight decrease in thermal diffusivity is probably caused by nanoparticle agglomeration, which reintroduces voids and microcracks as observed in SEM images. These disrupt the uniform heat transfer pathways and counteract the thermal benefits of BN at higher dosages. Therefore, the enhancement in thermal diffusivity results from a combined effect of increased thermal conductivity, improved microstructural compaction, and reduced moisture content, all of which facilitate more efficient thermal energy transfer in the foamed concrete matrix.

Specific heat capacity

The specific heat is a physical parameter that quantifies the ability of concrete to undergo thermal fluctuations. Figure 25 depicts the variance in heat capacity (Cp) of concretes, revealing that the heat capacity of all concretes, regardless of the different ratios of BN, is lower than that of the reference FC (M0). The data also demonstrates a negative correlation between the BN content and the heat capacity, indicating a drop in heat capacity with increasing BN level. The combination containing 0.075% BN exhibits a reduced heat capacity compared to the other mixes. Compared to the reference mixture (M0), the specific heat of M1, M2, M3, and M4 declined by 1.24%, 2.21%, 4.13%, and 3.07%, respectively. The observed reduction can be attributed to the enhanced network structure of the calcium-silicate-hydrate (C-S-H) gel, leading to significant alterations in the overall characteristics of the cementitious composites. Furthermore, the observed increase in the Ca/Si ratio in nano-BN-filled cementitious composites can likely be attributed to replacing Si-O-Si bonds within the silicate tetrahedron of C-S-H with O-Ca-O bonds. This substitution leads to a higher concentration of calcium ions being incorporated into the C-S-H structure, reducing the number of interfaces and resulting in a lower specific heat112. Additionally, the high thermal conductivity of BN serves as another contributing factor. Majeed et al.99 reported similar results regarding the effect of nanoparticles on the specific heat capacity of foam concrete (FC). They found that as the proportion of silicon dioxide nanoparticles increased, the specific heat capacity of the FC mixtures decreased, which is consistent with the current findings.

Pore structure

Apparent porosity

Figure 26 illustrates the outcomes of the permeable porosity of FC-BN with various concentrations. An observed correlation was found between the weight% of BN in the FC mixtures and a reduction in permeable porosity. Adding BN at concentrations of 0.025%, 0.05%, and 0.075% to the control FC sample leads to a decrease in permeability porosity by 2.18%, 3.17%, and 5.1%, respectively, compared to the control FC sample. Incorporating BN inside the interfacial region between the binder paste and the filler reduced the reactivity of capillary pores. It is essential to acknowledge that the pores were effectively filled with BN. The observed correlation between permeability and porosity values can be ascribed to modifying the microstructure in the BN presence. The nucleation impact in these BN materials leads to interwoven C-S-H gels forming on their surfaces, contributing to the observed trend in permeability porosity values117. Using the BN mitigates the continuous and discrete gaps present in the samples, decreasing capillary action and promoting water absorption in the FC samples. The detected phenomenon entails a reduction in the dimensions of the largest pores on the external surface due to the formation of novel hydrated compounds, which may be attributed to the BN-augmented concentration. As stated earlier, prior studies have demonstrated that BN contributes to the enhancement of the structural integrity of the silicate tetrahedron in C-S-H. Hence, the function of nano-BN extends beyond serving as just a filler inside the matrix since it actively alters the molecular structure of hydration products. The presence of nano-BN has a noticeable impact on the polymerization state of the silicate tetrahedron [SiO₄]⁴⁻ inside the C-S-H gel. The enhancement of the network structure of the C-S-H gel leads to a decrease in pore volume71.

Pores distribution

The FC pore structure is critical in determining its mechanical characteristics, thermal insulation capabilities, and hygroscopic behavior. Figure 28 illustrates the obvious impact of incorporating 1% TiO2 nanoparticles on the pore size distribution of BN. The study’s findings indicated that incorporating BN led to a more homogeneous distribution of pore sizes. The observed increase was evident in the diameters of 100 μm, 200 μm, 300 μm, and 400 μm. The findings of this study also revealed a decrease in the size of pores more significant than 800 μm. When a concentration of 0.075% BN was present, the observed peak value reached 18%. A 20% increase was observed in the experimental group, in contrast to a 15% increase in the control group. As the concentration of BN rose, there was a corresponding rise in the number of pores falling within the size range of 100–400 μm. On the contrary, there was a decrease in the size of pores bigger than 900 μm, leading to an improvement in strength and enhanced resistance to water absorption. Hence, it was demonstrated that incorporating BN facilitated pore size dispersion in the FC. The findings aligned with the previously conducted research117. This phenomenon can be attributed to the presence of uniformly distributed pores, which facilitate the development of a highly efficient structure that effectively resists water absorption. The average pore size values are depicted in Fig. 29. The study revealed that the mean size of the pore of M0 was measured to be 550 μm, whereas M1, M2, M3, and M4 exhibited average pore sizes of 510 μm, 450 μm, 410 μm, and 490 μm, respectively. The study’s findings revealed that a slight decrease in the average pore size was observed when the concentration of BN increased. The inclusion of varying amounts (0.025, 0.05, 0.075, and 0.1%) of BN resulted in a marginal reduction in the observed porosity of FC, with decreases of 7.27%, 18.18%, 25.45%, and 10.90%, respectively. According to the analysis, incorporating BN has the potential to enhance the pore structure of FC containing 1% TiO2 nanoparticles, leading to a more uniform distribution. Consequently, this improvement is expected to increase the compressive strength of the FC.

Morphology assessment

The scanning electron microscope (SEM) was utilized to study the micro-morphology of air pore structures in FC in its as-prepared state. Figure 31(a)–(e) illustrates the impact of varying BN concentrations (0, 0.025%, 0.05%, 0.075%, and 0.1%) on the pore distribution in FC. The BN-modified samples (particularly M3) showed a more refined pore structure with smaller, more uniformly distributed micropores and noticeably thicker pore walls. On the other hand, the control sample (M0) had large, irregular, and interconnected pores with thin pore walls.

The densest microstructure was specifically shown by M3 (0.075% BN) that had a more continuous cementitious phase, a smaller number of large voids, and little pore coalescence. This improvement can be attributed to BN’s dual role: acting as a micro-filler due to its nanoscale platelet structure and providing nucleation sites that stimulate additional C-S-H gel formation. In contrast with the looser and sparser C-S-H structures observed in the control (Fig. 31a), Fig. 30(d) displays enhanced C-S-H network intergrowth around BN-TiO₂ particles.

These structural changes are expected to significantly enhance mechanical strength and durability by reducing permeability and shrinkage potential. Therefore, using the appropriate BN content (0.075%) leads to increased density of the hydrated cement matrix and inner pore surfaces, as shown in Fig. 31(d). Furthermore, SEM comparison shows that the presence of BN bridges microcracks and restricts pore coalescence, further improving the integrity of the material. This can be attributed to the filling of micropores with BN, which exhibits a nucleation impact and promotes the interweaving of C-S-H gels deposited on its surface. Consequently, the structural integrity of cementitious composites is greatly enhanced. Mydin et al.115 explored how nanoparticles influence the microstructure of FC and found that introducing calcium carbonate nanoparticles led to a notable reduction in void size within the mixture. These results are in agreement with the outcomes of the present study.

Although the addition of BN up to 0.075% improved mechanical properties, decreased porosity, and densified the matrix, a further increase to 0.1% (M4) led to a slight decrease in performance. As previously reported in the literature and confirmed by the microstructural results of this investigation, this decrease can be ascribed to the agglomeration of BN nanoparticles at higher concentrations. The M4 sample had a less uniform pore distribution and void clusters, as seen in Fig. 31(e), which suggests that BN agglomerates were acting as defects rather than reinforcing agents. The homogeneity of the cement matrix is disrupted by these agglomerates that may introduce micro-defects, which reduce strength and eliminate the advantages of pore refinement. Moreover, increasing the amount of BN may cause a localization in water demand and interfere with efficient hydration that would further lower the matrix’s bond quality. Thus, the findings show that 0.075% BN is the best threshold where the negative effects of particle agglomeration start to cancel out the improvements in mechanical performance and porosity reduction when using more than this level.

Long-term durability expectations for BN-based composites

Although early-age performance of the foamed concrete (7–56 days) was mainly studied in this research, the chemical and structural properties of boron nitride can be used to determine the long-term durability ability of BN-modified mixtures. The excellent oxidation resistance, thermal stability, and chemical inertness of BN, especially in its hexagonal form (h-BN), are well-known even under the most extreme environment conditions127–129. These characteristics show that BN is unlikely to break down or leak out over time that leads to maintaining its reinforcing role inside the cementitious matrix. As shown in earlier research, BN shows long-term structural stability in alkaline and acidic media that are common in cement-based systems130,131. Also, by decreasing the pathways for moisture and aggressive ion ingress, the decreased porosity and improved microstructural densification observed in this study especially for the 0.075% BN mix, should improve long-term durability. Future research should confirm these predictions through studying BN-modified FC under conditions of prolonged aging, freeze-thaw, sulfate attack, and carbonation.

Statistical analysis

This study conducted a two-way ANOVA to determine statistically significant process parameters. An analysis of variance was employed to ascertain the effect of the independent variable on the dependent variable. The analysis results are presented in Tables 6, 7 and 8, and 9 under various conditions. The BN nanoparticle content and curing period have been selected as independent components. At the same time, properties such as compressive strength, flexural strength, fracturing tensile strength, and drying shrinkage have been designated as dependent factors. The experimental parameters that were statistically significant for the properties were identified by analyzing the test findings from this study using Minitab software at a significance threshold of 0.05. The P-values in all of the columns serve as evidence of the test parameters’ relevance to the attributes. A parameter is considered a significant factor in the test result if the P-value is less than 0.05. In an additional effort to ascertain how much each independent variable affected the dependent variable, the contribution percentage was computed. A higher contribution percentage indicates a greater effectiveness of a parameter in influencing the investigated attribute. Similarly, a low percent contribution indicates that the factors’ impact on the specific response was negligible. The analysis results indicate that the dependent variations were substantially influenced by the BN content and curing period. Concerning the P-values obtained from the two-way ANOVA, the statistical analysis results showed that the BN content and curing period impacted all properties.

Conclusions

The primary aim of this research is to study the effect of using BN as a partial substitute for OPC in producing normal foamed concrete (FC) containing 1% TiO₂ nanoparticles. Thus, pore structure, shrinkage, mechanical strength, density, setting time, slump, and durability were examined. The findings lead to the following conclusions:
