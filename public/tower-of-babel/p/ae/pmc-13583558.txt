Leadless Pacemaker–Induced Metallic Artifact Causing Reduced Septal Uptake on Single Photon Emission Computed Tomography
Authors: Shusuke Arigami, Toru Awaya, Masako Asami, Taeko Kunimasa, Hidehiko Hara
Published: 2026

Right ventricular (RV) pacing by transvenous pacemakers has been associated with perfusion defects involving the septal, inferior, and apical walls on single photon emission computed tomography (SPECT) imaging. Leadless pacemakers (LPMs) have recently emerged as an important therapeutic option for bradyarrhythmias, offering advantages such as avoidance of both lead-related complications and infection risk. As the number of these implants is expected to increase worldwide, recognition of their imaging characteristics is becoming increasingly important. Although artifacts caused by LPMs have been reported in computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), no such reports exist in nuclear medicine, including SPECT.

Case Presentation

An 82-year-old man was admitted to our hospital for pneumonia complicated by worsening heart failure. He had a history of permanent atrial fibrillation with complete atrioventricular block and had undergone leadless pacemaker (LPM) implantation 2 years earlier (Fig. 1A). Electrocardiography at admission showed a paced rhythm with a 60–beat per minute heart rate. Pacemaker interrogation revealed that 78% of all heartbeats were ventricular paced rhythm.

Chest CT revealed pulmonary congestion, right-sided pleural effusion, and pneumonia in the right lower lobe. The LPM location was confirmed to be in the RV mid to apical septum (Fig. 2). Intravenous diuretics and broad-spectrum antibiotics were initiated, resulting in rapid improvement of the pneumonia and heart failure symptoms. Myocardial ischemia was assessed after clinical stabilization. Given the renal dysfunction (creatinine, 262 μmol/L), further contrast-enhanced coronary evaluation was avoided, and myocardial perfusion SPECT imaging was performed.

Pharmacologic stress was induced using adenosine (PDR Pharma, Tokyo, Japan), and technetium-99m tetrofosmin (Nihon Medi-Physics, Tokyo, Japan) was used as the radiotracer. Pharmacologic stress SPECT (GCA9300R, Canon Medical Systems, Otawara, Japan) showed reduced tracer uptake extending from the mid-septum to the inferior apical wall in both the stress and rest phases (Fig. 1B). These findings did not correspond to coronary artery territories, and owing to the absence of significant stenosis on coronary angiography 2 years earlier, a perfusion defect due to coronary artery disease was considered unlikely. A phase analysis, performed using Quantitative Gated SPECT software (Cedars-Sinai Medical Centre, Los Angeles, CA), demonstrated a bandwidth of 30°, phase standard deviation of 7.1°, and entropy of 37.9%, suggesting no evidence of marked dyssynchrony (Fig. 1C). CT images were acquired (Aquilion ONE, Canon Medical Systems, Otawara, Japan) and then fused with SPECT data (Fig. 2). The images revealed an area of attenuation consistent with the LPM location, which correlated with the area of decreased tracer uptake on SPECT. Again, this finding did not match any coronary territory, further supporting the suspicion of an LPM-related attenuation artifact.

Discussion

RV pacing by transvenous pacemakers has been associated with perfusion defects involving the septal, inferior, and apical walls on SPECT imaging.1 In addition, artifacts related to LPMs have been reported previously on CT and cardiac MRI.2,3 However, reports describing potential LPM-related attenuation artifacts on myocardial perfusion SPECT remain limited.

LPMs and transvenous pacemakers have notable differences. Using the Medtronic Micra and Medtronic transvenous lead as examples, the LPM has a diameter of 6.7 mm (20 Fr),3 whereas standard transvenous leads typically measure approximately 2.0 mm (6 Fr)—a more than 3-fold difference. Although the 2 devices are composed of similar metallic materials, the substantially larger size of the LPM may increase the likelihood for artifacts. A cardiac MRI study has reported artifacts from LPMs in the interventricular septum to inferior apical regions, attributed to metallic artifacts from the device.2 In the present case, the reduced tracer uptake observed on SPECT corresponded to the same distribution previously described in an MRI study of LPM-related artifacts (Fig. 1B).2

Abnormal electrical activation induced by RV apical pacing can cause wall-motion abnormalities, resulting in regions with abnormal perfusion compared with normal electrical activation on SPECT.4 In addition, RV apical pacing-induced ventricular dyssynchrony causes myocardial remodelling and microvascular dysfunction, leading to reduced coronary flow reserve and perfusion defects on SPECT.

The phase analysis showed that the bandwidth, phase standard deviation, and entropy remained within conventionally reported normal ranges (Fig. 1C). However, gated SPECT-derived phase analysis may have limited accuracy in ventricular paced rhythms, particularly when older-generation systems and lower framing rates were used. Therefore, although pacing-related perfusion abnormalities cannot be completely excluded, the close spatial concordance between the LPM location and reduced tracer uptake may suggest a contribution from metallic attenuation artifact. Cardiac sarcoidosis was considered as a differential diagnosis; however, the absence of extracardiac findings and lack of progression during follow-up made this diagnosis clinically unlikely.5

One important limitation of this case was that the Canon 3-detector SPECT system lacked an integrated CT-based attenuation correction.6 Although fusion with noncontrast CT enabled anatomic LPM localization, the absence of correction may have exaggerated the apparent perfusion defect from the septum to the inferior and apical walls. The substantial metallic components of the LPM, combined with the lack of attenuation correction, may have produced non-uniform radial attenuation effects. These effects could have created the appearance of distinct perfusion defects involving both the mid-septum and the apical inferior wall. However, ischemia involving the proximal-to-mid left anterior descending, left circumflex, and right coronary arteries, excluding the septal branches, can still be evaluated adequately with an LPM because the metallic attenuation effect is localized in the septal-to-apical region (Fig. 1B). Thus, SPECT remains clinically valuable for assessing perfusion in other coronary territories despite potential artifacts from LPM placement. In this case, although pacing-related perfusion abnormalities cannot be completely excluded, the decreased tracer uptake observed on SPECT may have been influenced by metallic attenuation artifacts from the LPM. As LPM use is increasing, awareness of potential artifacts is essential to avoid overestimating perfusion defects and prevent unnecessary additional testing or interventions.
