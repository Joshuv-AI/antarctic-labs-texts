The mitochondrial genome of Lavandula angustifolia Mill. (Lamiaceae) sheds light on its genome structure and gene transfer between organelles
Authors: Jun Wang, Xiaoyan Liu, Mengting Zhang, Renbin Liu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLavandula angustifolia holds importance as an aromatic plant with extensive applications spanning the fragrance, perfume, cosmetics, aromatherapy, and spa sectors. Beyond its aesthetic and sensory applications, this plant offers medicinal benefits as a natural herbal remedy and finds use in household cleaning products. While extensive genomic data, inclusive of plastid and nuclear genomes, are available for this species, researchers have yet to characterize its mitochondrial genome. This gap in knowledge hampers deeper understanding of the genome organization and its evolutionary significance.ResultsThrough the course of this study, we successfully assembled and annotated the mitochondrial genome of L. angustifolia, marking a first in this domain. This assembled genome encompasses 61 genes, which comprise 34 protein-coding genes, 24 transfer RNA genes, and three ribosomal RNA genes. We identified a chloroplast sequence insertion into the mitogenome, which spans a length of 10,645 bp, accounting for 2.94% of the mitogenome size. Within these inserted sequences, there are seven intact tRNA genes (trnH-GUG, trnW-CCA, trnD-GUC, trnS-GGA, trnN-GUU, trnT-GGU, trnP-UGG) and four complete protein-coding genes (psbA, rps15, petL, petG) of chloroplast derivation. Additional discoveries include 88 microsatellites, 15 tandem repeats, 74 palindromic repeats, and 87 forward long repeats. An RNA editing analysis highlighted an elevated count of editing sites in the cytochrome c oxidase genes, notably ccmB with 34 editing sites, ccmFN with 32, and ccmC with 29. All protein-coding genes showed evidence of cytidine-to-uracil conversion. A phylogenetic analysis, utilizing common protein-coding genes from 23 Lamiales species, yielded a tree with consistent topology, supported by high confidence values.ConclusionsAnalysis of the current mitogenome resource revealed its typical circular genome structure. Notably, sequences originally from the chloroplast genome were found within the mitogenome, pointing to the occurrence of horizontal gene transfer between organelles. This assembled mitogenome stands as a valuable resource for subsequent studies on mitogenome structures, their evolution, and molecular biology.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-024-10841-x.Keywords: Lavandula, Gene transfer, Mitochondrial genome, Phylogenetic analysis

Background

Lavandula angustifolia, a native Mediterranean flowering plant, belongs to the mint family, Lamiaceae. The Lavandula genus comprises approximately 41 species [1] and is cultivated globally for both ornamental and medicinal purposes [2]. L. angustifolia has garnered significant commercial attention primarily for its aromatic essential oil properties [3]. This oil finds extensive applications in industries including cosmetics, household cleaning, and perfumery [4, 5]. Furthermore, L. angustifolia is a staple in aromatherapy, with its extracts infused into candles, massage oils, and diffusers frequently found in spas and wellness centers [6]. Lavender extracts have demonstrated numerous health benefits, likely attributed to their rich phytochemical composition, including terpenoids and flavonoids [7]. Historically, lavender oil has been linked with anti-depressive, sedative, anti-microbial, and anti-inflammatory properties [8]. Moreover, preclinical studies have indicated promising anti-diabetic, anti-cancer, and anti-mutagenic effects of these phytochemical extracts from lavender [8–11]. With the emergence of long-read sequencing and chromosome conformation capture technologies, L. angustifolia has embarked on the genomic era [12, 13]. This advancement led to a high-quality genome assembly, facilitating evolutionary research within Lamiaceae and the identification of genes integral to the terpenoid biosynthesis pathway [12]. While the chloroplast genome of L. angustifolia has been previously characterized [14], its mitochondrial genome remains to be elucidated and awaits assembly. Significant research has been undertaken to elucidate the phylogenetic placement of lavender within the Lamiaceae family. Phylogenetic analyses using chloroplast sequences have consistently positioned L. angustifolia in the subfamily Nepetoideae, more precisely within the tribe Ocimeae [14–16].

Mitochondria, often described as powerhouses of the cell, have a central role in cellular energy production, primarily producing adenosine triphosphate (ATP), the main energy currency for many cellular processes [17]. The assembly of plant genomes presents a formidable challenge due to the complex nature of mitogenomes. These can assume a variety of conformations, including sigma-like, fragmented, branched, circular, linear, or multi-chromosomal structures [18, 19]. Numerous extensive repeats can serve as potential locations for inter- or intra-molecular recombination [20, 21], resulting in the creation of various alternative configurations. While the structural conformation of the ornamental orchid Paphiopedilum micranthum [22] consists of multiple rings, the mitogenomes of some plant species, such as Lactuca sativa [23] and Nymphaea colorata [24], exhibited linear and circular shapes, respectively.

The size of plant mitogenomes varies considerably, ranging from as small as 66 kb in Viscum scurruloideum to as large as 11.3 Mb in Silene conica [25], with a characteristic abundance of repetitive sequences, spanning both short and long repeats [26]. Various forms of recombination, encompassing homologous intragenomic recombination [27], homologous recombination [27], inter- or intramolecular recombination [23], as well as repeat-mediated recombination [24], have been documented as mechanisms capable of altering the architecture of plant mitochondrial genomes. The frequency of recombination events that modify the structure of plant mitochondrial genomes varies depending on the plant species and the type of recombination involved. For instance, in the mitochondrial genome of the Norway spruce, it was observed that the frequency of recombination events occurring at inverted repeats ≥ 50 base pairs in length and sharing ≥ 80% pairwise identity is notably high [24]. Furthermore, mechanisms such as horizontal gene transfer and intracellular gene transfer, occurring between organelles or with nuclear genomes, have been extensively documented in plant organelles [28–30]. In a comprehensive study conducted by Zhao et al. [31], involving a diverse set of 24 plant species, encompassing both eudicots and monocots, their findings have provided compelling evidence that points towards the omnipresence of both mitochondrial-plastid gene transfer and intergenomic gene transfer across all the taxa under investigation. Subsequently, the key findings highlighted the ubiquitous nature of mitochondrial gene transfer in all 24 plants, its occurrence as a single event between common ancestors during evolutionary divergence, the varying sequence characteristics of homologies in donor and recipient genomes, and the critical role in small repeats mediating gene transfer by recombination within the recipient genome [31].

With the emergence of long-read sequencing technologies, assembling plant mitochondrial genomes has become considerably more attainable. As a result, long-reads, either on their own or in conjunction with short reads, have facilitated the assembly of mitochondrial genomes in several species. This includes Vitex trifoli (NCBI Reference Sequence: NC_065806.1), Phaseolus vulgaris [32], Ipomea batatas [19], and Salvia miltiorrhiza [33]. Given the intricate nature of the mitogenome, it is imperative to investigate gene transfer events between organelles to fully understand the evolutionary dynamics of plant mitochondrial genomes.

In summary, this study aims to build a more comprehensive picture of the genomic and evolutionary landscape of L. angustifolia by assembling its mitochondrial genome, exploring intra-organelle gene transfer mechanisms, and situating it within the broader phylogenetic context of the Lamiaceae family. The findings are expected to have far-reaching implications, ranging from basic science to applied research in ecology, medicine, and agriculture.

Results

Genomic features and DNA migration from plastid to mitogenome of L. Angustifolia

The assembled L. angustifolia mitogenome measures 355,345 bp, characterized by a nucleotide composition of 27.55% adenine (A), 27.31% thymine (T), 22.45% cytosine (C), and 22.69% guanine (G). The annotation of the mitochondrial genome (Fig. 1) identifies a total of 61 genes: 34 protein-coding, 24 tRNA, and 3 rRNA genes. Notably, the typical mitochondrial rRNA genes rrn5, rrn18, and rrn26 are consistent with those commonly observed in plant mitogenomes [34, 35]. Among the protein coding genes, rps10 and nad4 were duplicated (Table 1). Interestingly, NADH dehydrogenase genes, namely nad1, nad2 and nad5, exhibited the highest number (n = 5) of exon. By aligning the chloroplast sequences with the assembled mitogenome, we identified 24 fragments originating from plastidial sequences within the L. angustifolia mitogenome (Fig. 2, Table S1). These inserted plastid fragments totaled 10,645 bp, constituting 2.94% of the entire mitogenome. At the gene level, we observed a complete sequence for seven tRNAs (trnH-GUG, trnW-CCA, trnD-GUC, trnS-GGA, trnN-GUU, trnT-GGU, trnP-UGG) and four protein-coding genes (psbA, rps15, petL, petG) that are native to the chloroplast (Fig. 2, Table S1). This finding indicates horizontal gene transfer between chloroplast and mitochondrial genomes as previously observed between organelles and/or cellular compartments [36, 37].

Repeat sequences analysis

Within the L. angustifolia mitogenome, we identified a total of 88 SSRs (Fig. 3, Table S2). The tetrameric type was the most abundant (n = 34) followed by dimeric (n = 20), trimeric (n = 14) and monomeric (n = 14) types. Meanwhile, hexameric SSRs were absent the studied mitochondrial genome. In the L. angustifolia mitogenome, 15 tandem repeats were observed (Fig. 3, Table S3). Regarding dispersed repeats, we identified 14 palindromic and 87 forward repeats (Fig. 3, Table S4). The sizes of the palindromic repeats varied from 30 to 3,675 bp, while the forward repeats ranged between 30 and 202 bp (Table S4). The transposon search revealed the presence of various transposable elements, including DNA transposons (157 fragments) and retrotransposons (227 fragments of LTR and 97 fragments of non-LTR (Table S5). LTR was the prominent transposon type accounted for 4.60% of the mitogenome size followed by DNA transposon (3.14%), and non-LTR types (1.69%) (Table S5).

Overview of the repeats content in L. angustifolia mitochondrial genome. (a) Motif length distribution of simple sequence repeats (SSRs). (b) Number of tandem and dispersed repeats identified in the studied mitogenome

RNA editing sites

Our analysis predicted 364 RNA editing sites distributed across 34 protein-coding genes (Fig. 4). Notably, the ribosomal protein S3 (rps3) gene displayed no editing sites, whereas the cytochrome c biogenesis B (ccmB) gene exhibited the highest number of such sites. Of the identified editing sites, 35.44% (129) were located at the first position of the codons, and a predominant 64.56% (235) corresponded to the codons’ second base (Table S6). Remarkably, every editing site exhibited a cytidine to uridine (C to U) conversion, which represents the predominant type of conversion observed in plant organelles [38]. The most frequent amino acid transition was from serine to leucine, accounting for 21.43% (78 sites). This was followed by proline to leucine at 17.03% (62 sites) and serine to phenylalanine at 14.83% (54 sites) (Table S6).

Codon usage

Detailed codon usage for individual amino acids is cataloged in Table S7. Overall, codon usage bias is evident for most amino acid codons, with notable exceptions being the start codon AUG (RSCU = 1) and tryptophan (UUG) (RSCU = 1), as illustrated in Fig. 5. Interestingly, the stop codon UAA exhibited the highest RSCU value at 1.73. For example, the GCU codon, with an RSCU value of 1.61, is predominantly selected for encoding alanine.

Conserved block analysis

The detection of synteny blocks was performed with a set of eight species including L. angustifolia, Scutellaria barbata, Pogostemon heyneanus, Ajuga reptans, Rotheca serrata, Vitex trifolia, Plastoma chinense, and Salvia miltiorrhiza. From the multi-synteny plot (Fig. 6), several homologous collinear blocks between L. angustifolia and its related species became evident. For instance, the top two longest matches were noted for Lamiaceae members Vitex trifolia (10,098 bp), and Platostoma chinense (6,079 bp) (Table S8). Notably, these blocks are of relatively short length, suggesting a lack of conservation in the mitogenome structure among these species. Furthermore, distinct gaps observed within these sequences underscore their uniqueness to the L. angustifolia mitogenome, suggesting a lack of homology with the other species examined. Collectively, our findings suggest pronounced genomic rearrangements in the mitochondrial genome of L. angustifolia relative to its closely related counterparts.

Sequence collinearity between L. angustifolia and relative species in Lamiaceae. The red curved areas indicate inverted sequences regions, and the gray areas indicate homologous sequences regions

Phylogenetic analysis

To deduce the phylogenetic positioning of L. angustifolia within the Lamiales species based on its mitogenome, we incorporated 22 other species including Scutellaria tsinyunensis (MW553042.1), Scutellaria franchetiana (NC_065026.1), Scutellaria barbata (NC_065025.1), Pogostemon heyneanus (MK728874.1) Ajuga reptans (NC_023103.1), Ajuga ciliata (MT075725_6.1), Rotheca serrata (NC_049064.1), Vitex trifolia (NC_065806.1), Plastoma chinense (OP537517.1), Salvia miltiorrhiza (NC_023209.1), Aeginetia indica (MW851294.1), Christisonia kwangtungensis (OM219025_7.1), Castilleja paramensis (NC_031806.1), Rehmannia glutinosa (OM397952.1), Genlisea tuberosa (OK274069.1), Utricularia reniformis (NC_034982.1), Aragoa abietina (OK514181.1), Aragoa cleefii (OK514182.1), Boea hygrometrica (NC_016741.1), Haberlea rhodopensis (MH757117.1), Ligustrum quihoui (MN723864.1), and Osmanthus fragans (NC_060346.1) into our analysis. The maximum likelihood tree was subsequently constructed (Fig. 7). The topology of the tree aligned seamlessly with the recent classification presented by the Angiosperm Phylogeny Group, emphasizing the reliability of tree inference based on conserved genes. In line with expectations, L. angustifolia grouped with Lamiaceae species, forming a subclade that encompassed Platostoma chinensis and Salvia miltiorrhiza.

Unrooted maximum likelihood phylogenetic tree showing the placement of L. angustifolia within Lamiales species. The bootstrap values, generated through 1000 iterations, are indicated above the tree branches

Discussion

The swift evolutionary dynamics of plant mitochondrial genomes have resulted in pronounced genomic reorganization, leading to heterogeneity and diverse gene arrangements across different species [25, 39]. As a result, comparative analyses of plant mitochondrial genomes are challenging due to these evolutionary intricacies [40]. Deciphering mitochondrial genomes has shed light on their structural organization, genetic diversity, and evolutionary dynamics in both animals [41] and plants [42, 43]. Additionally, investigations into plant mitochondrial genomes offer insights into their roles in energy production, respiration, and overall plant development [44, 45].

In this study, we report, for the first time, the mitochondrial genome of L. angustifolia, an aromatic plant widely utilized in the cosmetics industry [46]. The mitochondrial genome of L. angustifolia appears to be organized in a circular configuration, consistent with other Lamiaceae species such as Vitex trifolia (NCBI Reference Sequence: NC_065806.1), Rotheca serrata (NCBI Reference Sequence: NC_049064.1), and Platostoma chinensis [47]. Mitochondrial genomes can exhibit diverse structural arrangements, including linear, multi-chromosomal, loop-like, and fragmented configurations [18]. For Salvia miltiorrhiza, a member of the same Lamiaceae family as lavender, two independent studies have described differing mitogenome structures [33, 48]. The initial study, utilizing short-read data, identified a linear mitogenome structure [48]. In contrast, the subsequent study, employing both short and long-read data, reported a multi-chromosomal arrangement [33].

In our study, a comparative analysis of mitogenome sizes highlighted notable variations within Lamiaceae mitogenomes. The mitochondrial genome of L. angustifolia spans 355,345 bp, making it smaller than that of its close relatives P. chinense (394,599 bp) and S. miltiorrhiza (499,236 bp), yet larger by 81 Kbp compared to V. trifolia (274,779 bp). Such variations in mitogenome size are believed to be influenced by factors including the abundance of intergenic regions [25, 49], intracellular gene transfer (IGT) [30], horizontal gene transfer (HGT) [29], and recombination of repeats [26]. In the past two decades, mounting evidence has highlighted the frequent transfer of genetic material between organelles and, in some instances, the nuclear genome [28, 50, 51]. By comparing the plastome and mitochondrial genomes of L. angustifolia, we identified fragments of the plastid genome embedded within the mitogenome, indicating potential horizontal gene transfer between these organelles.

At the present, there are still many unanswered questions regarding the mechanisms underlying the transfer of genetic sequences between the organelle genomes in L. angustifolia, and their implications at functional level. It may facilitate metabolic integration by coordinating pathways spanning these organelles, such as photosynthesis or energy metabolism [52]. The integration of chloroplast genes into the mitochondrial genome can profoundly remodel its structure. Repetitive sequences proliferation, and novel regulatory elements or intronic regions might be introduced [53]. Such integrations could contribute to mitogenome expansion, architectural restructuring, and structural diversification among lineages [25]. While not conclusively demonstrated in this study, it’s worth noting that intracellular gene transfer might mediate the exchange of genetic material between L. angustifolia organelles.

Alignment of the L. angustifolia mitogenome with those of related species revealed that L. angustifolia harbors short homologous sequences shared with other species within the Lamiales order. Nonetheless, it also contains distinct non-conserved sequences, underscoring its unique genetic signature within the species. Ample evidence has highlighted the pivotal role of RNA editing, particularly its impact on mitochondrial gene expression and function in higher plants [38, 54]. This study notably underscores the prevalence of C to U conversion, a phenomenon consistently observed across various plant mitogenomes [55].

Plant mitochondrial genomes frequently harbor both short and long repeats. Simple sequence repeats (SSRs), often characterized by short repetitive nucleotide units typically ranging from 1 to 6 base pairs, are a distinct class of DNA sequences [56]. Their importance is underscored by their polymorphism, relative abundance, codominant inheritance, and extensive genome coverage [57]. Long repeats including tandem, palindromic, forward, reverse, and complementary repeats are mostly present in wide range of organisms [58]. These repeats play pivotal roles in various biological processes, such as gene regulation, genome stability, and evolution, and serve as invaluable genetic markers in disciplines like forensics and population genetics [59–61]. Recombination events centered on repetitive sequences can influence genome structure, culminating in genetic rearrangements [18, 44]. Our analyses revealed a pronounced abundance of tetrameric SSRs in L. angustifolia, distinct from its congener P. chinense [47], where mononucleotide SSRs predominated. Given their repetitive nature and ubiquity across genomes, SSRs serve as valuable tools in population genetics and polymorphism evaluation [62]. Regarding long repeats in the L. angustifolia mitogenome, palindromic repeats emerged as the most predominant. Such repeats are known mutation hotspots and contribute significantly to organelle size expansion [39, 63, 64]. The characterized repeat sequences may prove invaluable for crafting markers to probe genetic diversity both between and within species. In our analysis, a mitogenome-derived phylogenetic tree positioned L. angustifolia within the Lamiaceae family, showing a close affinity to P. chinense and S. miltiorrhiza. This result concurs with the Lamiaceae plastid-based phylogenies previously presented by Ma [14] and Zao [15]. Notably, several Lavandula species, including L. minutolii, L. dentata, L. abrotanoides, L. bipinnata, and L. atlantica, remain unstudied at the genomic level. Enhanced sampling of these taxa and the addition of genomic data could enrich our understanding of the genus.

Conclusions

In the present study, we sequenced, assembled and annotated the mitochondrial genome of L. angustofolia, paving the way for comparative mitogenome analysis in the Lamiaceae family. Furthermore, we identified sequence fragments that appear to have transferred from the chloroplast genome to the mitochondrial genome, indicative of a potential horizontal gene transfer event. Our collinearity assessment reaffirmed the distinctiveness of the assembled genome. Additionally, the phylogenetic tree topology aligned with the previously established placement of L. angustifolia within the Lamiaceae. Overall, this research offers invaluable genetic resources for future comparative mitogenomic structural analyses and phylogenetic explorations.

Methods

Sequencing data retrieving

Long-read PacBio sequencing data were retrieved from the National Center for Biotechnology and Information (NCBI). The data were publicly available under the NCBI project number PRJNA762277 (SRR12615113, SRR12615133, SRR12615114, SRR12615115). The PacBio reads were error-corrected with Canu v2.2-0 [65] while the short-reads were processed for adapter-removal and quality trimming with fastp v.0.23.4-3 [66].

Assembly and annotation

We employed a baiting and iterative mapping strategy for mitochondrial assembly using GetOrganelle v1.7 [67], with parameters set as follows: -F embplant_mt, -k 57,77,97,117,127. To ensure assembly accuracy, we visualized contig configurations interactively using Bandage v0.8.1 [68] (Fig. S1). The quality of the assembly was then manually verified and refined by connecting the contigs, resulting in a fully circularized mitogenome. To address complexities in regions with repetitive sequences, we employed PacBio long-read data, mapping mitochondrial long-reads back onto the assembled genome. Furthermore, to validate the integrity of the assembled mitochondrial genome, the long-read data was leveraged to extract mitogenome-specific reads. These extracted reads were then assembled independently using the Canu assembler v2.2-0 (Table S10). Subsequently, the resulting assembly was aligned back to the initial mitogenome assembly obtained earlier (Fig. S2, File S1), thereby ensuring the reliability and accuracy of the assembled mitochondrial genome. Following this, annotation of the final assembly was executed using the GeSeq online tool [69] (https://chlorobox.mpimp-golm.mpg.de/). Annotation was performed through homology-based methods, drawing from well-annotated mitochondrial genomes of species such as Nicotiana tabacum (NC_006581), Arabidopsis thaliana (NC_037304), Brassica napus (NC_008285), Glycine max (NC_020455), and Salvia miltiorrhiza (NC_023209.1).

Within the GeSeq toolbox, annotation of rRNA, tRNA, and DNA was achieved through BLAST search, employing threshold identities of 85% for nucleotide sequences and 50% for protein sequences. To ensure the accuracy of the tRNA annotation, tRNAscan-SE v2.0.7 [70] was utilized as a secondary verification method. Subsequent to this, the annotated genome assembly was manually inspected and refined. Visualization of the genome map was accomplished using the Organellar Genome DRAW (OGDRAW) tool [71].

Integration of chloroplast-derived DNA into the mitochondrial genome analysis

Given the known transfer of genetic material between cellular compartments or organelles [36, 37], we explored the potential migration of DNA from chloroplast to mitochondrion. To achieve this, a BLAST search was executed using the reference chloroplast genome NC_029370.1, adhering to the criteria: E-value ≤ 1e− 10, match rate ≥ 70%, and alignment length ≥ 40.

Analysis of repeats elements

We identified simple sequence repeats (SSRs) using the MISA-web program (https://webblast.ipk-gatersleben.de/misa/) [72], employing the default settings. For the detection of palindromes, complements, forward, and reverse sequences, we used the REPuter web server (https://bibiserv.cebitec.uni-bielefeld.de/reputer) [73]. The specified parameters were a hamming distance of 3 and a minimal repeat size of 30 bp. Transposable elements were also investigated with CENSOR (https://www.girinst.org/censor/index.php) [74].

RNA editing and codon usage bias analyses

RNA editing is a widespread process observed in eukaryotes, including plants. This post-transcriptional modification involves changes like the addition, deletion, or conversion of bases within the coding region of the transcript [75]. Consequently, RNA editing is instrumental in species evolvability and is a determinant of adaptive evolution [76]. We utilized the PREP-mt package from the PREP suite (Mower, 2009) to predict RNA editing sites for each species, applying a threshold value of 0.8.

The Relative Synonymous Codon Usage (RSCU) is a metric employed to quantify the bias in synonymous codon utilization within genes [77]. This measure sheds light on the preferential selection of specific codons for the same amino acid. RSCU juxtaposes the observed frequency of a given codon against its expected frequency under the assumption of uniform usage among all synonymous codons. This metric has gained traction in molecular biology and genomics, serving as an essential tool for exploring gene evolution, expression, and optimization across diverse organisms [78, 79]. An RSCU value approximating 1 signifies a neutral bias. For the analysis of relative synonymous codon usage bias, we employed MEGA v7 [80].

Sequence collinearity analysis

To explore conserved mitochondrial regions among closely related species, including Scutellaria barbata, Pogostemon heyneanus, Ajuga reptans, Rotheca serrata, Vitex trifolia, Plastoma chinense, and Salvia miltiorrhiza, we performed pairwise alignments using BLASTN v2.10.1+. Only matches exceeding 500 bp were retained to evaluate synteny patterns across the mitochondrial genomes under investigation. Subsequent visualization of the multiple synteny plots was accomplished using TBTools v1.098746 [81].

Phylogenetic analysis

Phylogenetic analysis was conducted using a set of 16 conserved genes (atp1, atp4, ccmB, ccmC, ccmFC, ccmFN, cob, cox2, cox3, matR, nad1, nad2, nad3, nad5, nad6, rps13) derived from the complete mitochondrial genomes of 23 species, as listed in Table S9. This dataset included 11 species from Lamiaceae, four from Orobanchaceae, two each from Lentibulariaceae, Plantaginaceae, and Gesneriaceae. Two species from the Oleaceae family, Ligustrum quihoui and Osmanthus fragrans, were designated as outgroups. The conserved genes underwent multiple sequence alignment (MSA) using MAFFT v7.505 [82]. Subsequently, poorly aligned regions in the MSA files of the conserved genes were refined using trimAl [83], and the files were concatenated with PhyloSuite [84]. The final concatenated data matrix was employed to construct a maximum likelihood phylogenetic tree using IQ-TREE v2.0.3 [85], applying 1,000 bootstrap replications.
