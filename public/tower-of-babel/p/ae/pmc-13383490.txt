Knockout in zebrafish suggests a role of BDNF in behavioural and cognitive plasticity
Authors: Eleonora Rovegno, Elia Gatto, Elena Frigato, Cristiano Bertolucci, Tyrone Lucon-Xiccato
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
An adaptive mechanism observed in virtually all living animals is the phenotypic plasticity, a phenomenon by which different phenotypes can develop from the same genotype depending on the environment experienced. Phenotypic plasticity can involve a range of life-history, physiology, morphology, and also behavioural and cognitive traits. For example, individuals exposed to complex and enriched environments generally show greater learning abilities compared to individuals raised in simpler or barren environments. Several studies suggest that brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) may be involved in this plasticity, as it is often differentially expressed in individuals exposed to different environments. We investigated this possibility in fish, taking advantage of a knockout zebrafish line lacking the gene coding for BDNF (bdnf). Zebrafish from both the knockout (bdnf -/-) and control (bdnf +/+) lines were raised in either barren or enriched environments, and their behavioural and cognitive phenotypes were analysed. bdnf -/- zebrafish exhibited higher behavioural and cognitive plasticity compared to bdnf +/+ controls, as evidenced by a pronounced increase in activity and learning scores following exposure to environmental enrichment. These results suggest that BDNF may play a modulatory role in phenotypic plasticity, although broader organisational alterations resulting from the absence of BDNF during development in knockout zebrafish cannot be ruled out. An additional exploratory analysis of the expression of other neuroplasticity-related genes identified potential pathways that may be involved in this effect.

Introduction

Phenotypic plasticity, i.e., the potential of an individual to express different phenotypes in response to environmental conditions, is a widely recognized mechanism for local adaptation [1, 2]. It enables individuals to develop traits suited to the environmental conditions they experience during their lifetime. For example, individual prey exposed to predators develop morphological changes that reduce predation risk [3, 4]. A growing number of studies suggest that behavioural and cognitive traits are also plastic. Tadpoles raised in the presence of predators develop better predator recognition abilities [5], and mice raised in more complex environments are more explorative [6–9] and show improved learning and memory [10, 11]. This plasticity appears to be particularly pronounced in fish, where it can be triggered by a number of factors including habitat complexity [12–19], resource predictability [20–22], social environment [23–27], temperature [28–30], and light conditions [31, 32].

While the mechanisms underlying behavioural and cognitive plasticity are still under investigation, an important role has been suggested for a protein belonging to the neurotrophin family, the brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF; [33]). For example, levels of BDNF vary significantly among individuals exposed to different environments [34–36]. Rodents exposed to complex environments that affect learning abilities also show a simultaneous increase in BDNF expression [6, 33, 35–42]. Moreover, a few experimental studies based on experimental manipulations such as gene knockout and receptor inhibition have demonstrated that BDNF is functionally required for plasticity in these mammalian models [36, 43–45].

Similar evidence has been reported in studies conducted in fish [46–48]. Indeed, the structure and function of BDNF are highly conserved across vertebrates [49, 50]. Although teleost fish underwent an additional whole-genome duplication [51], the zebrafish, our study species, possesses only a single bdnf ortholog. The zebrafish bdnf genomic locus is more compact than its mammalian counterpart, but the complexity of its promoter structure and differential expression patterns closely mirror those observed in murine models [52]. Remarkably, the primary amino acid sequence of zebrafish BDNF shares 91% identity with the human sequence [53, 54], supporting the use of zebrafish as a highly relevant model for studying BDNF-mediated plasticity.

Exploiting a CRISPR/Cas9-generated zebrafish mutant line lacking the gene coding for BDNF (bdnf), we sought to experimentally test the effect of this molecule on plasticity. In line with findings in mammals, the zebrafish mutant lacking bdnf, despite being reproductively and morphologically normal [55], exhibits maladaptive behaviours [22] and learning impairments [25] when compared to their wild-type counterparts. We exposed 4 days post-fertilization (dpf) larvae of the knockout (bdnf -/-) line and wild-type controls (bdnf +/+) to either a complex, enriched environment or a simple, barren environment for 10 days [15, 16, 56]. We then assessed activity and habituation learning at 14 dpf, as these traits are known to be plastic [16, 57]. We hypothesized that bdnf is involved in phenotypic plasticity and therefore that bdnf -/- and bdnf +/+ larvae would differ in their responses to environmental enrichment. We aimed to identify such differences by examining the genotype × treatment interactions for the behavioural and cognitive variables.

Immediately after the behavioural and cognitive tests, the larvae were euthanised at 14 dpf, to conduct preliminary gene expression analysis via qPCR, focusing on genes involved in neural plasticity. This analysis aimed to explore potential pathways involved in the effect of BDNF on plasticity in response to environmental conditions.

Methods

Experimental subjects

The experiments were conducted using zebrafish larvae, obtained from adult breeders of the knockout (bdnf -/-) line and the wild-type line (bdnf +/+) as control. The bdnf -/- and bdnf +/+ zebrafish lines used were originally obtained from heterozygous crosses (bdnf +/- ⋅ bdnf +/-). The heterozygous founders of the lines were generated via CRISPR/Cas9 mutagenesis in the AB strain through a 40 bp deletion in the coding sequence that impacts all bdnf gene isoforms [55]. For the experiments, larvae were obtained from homozygous crosses of bdnf -/- and of bdnf +/+ (bdnf -/- × bdnf -/- and bdnf +/+ × bdnf +/+ crosses, respectively) maintained under identical laboratory conditions. Therefore, the bdnf +/+ zebrafish were sibling-derived controls of the bdnf -/- zebrafish. We used this breeding approach because it allowed us to obtain several clutches simultaneously from multiple breeders housed in different maintenance tanks. This was helpful to reach the relatively large sample size required for our behavioural tests and to obtain cohorts of subjects born and tested on the same days, thereby limiting well-known environmental confounds in behavioural experiments. Moreover, alternative approaches, such as directly comparing siblings derived from heterozygous crosses, require genotyping the subjects before testing, which, based on standard methods, may affect their behaviour. In addition, these crosses also produce heterozygous individuals that must be genotyped, resulting in additional use of experimental animals. In contrast, with our approach we were able to genotype only the adult breeders instead.

The two lines were kept separately in 50 L glass tanks (60 × 40 × 40 cm, 6 maintenance tanks per line), with around 20 individuals of mixed-sex (50:50 male: female ratio) in each tank, at the University of Ferrara facility. The housing conditions of the adults were as follows: photoperiod 14 h: 10 h light: dark; water temperature 27 ± 1 °C; conductivity 606.7 ± 60.18 µS/cm; nitrite below 0.1 mg/L; nitrate below 50 mg/L). The adult zebrafish were fed two to three times per day with a combination of live brine shrimp nauplii (Artemia salina) and commercial dry food (Staple food Vipan, Sera, Heinsberg, Germany).

For this study, we used a total of 320 zebrafish larvae (160 bdnf -/-, 160 bdnf +/+). To avoid procedures that could then affect the behavioural assessment in the larvae, their genotype was confirmed indirectly by performing a PCR analysis on the parents. Moreover, given the large number of larval subjects necessary for the behavioural assessment, this approach significantly reduced the number of genotyping procedures required. Fertilized eggs were collected and incubated in Petri dishes (10 cm Ø, h:1.5 cm) containing a solution of E3 1x (deionized H2O; 5 mM NaCl, 0.17 mM KCl, 0.33 mM CaCl2, 0.33 mM MgSO4) and Methylene blue (0.0016 g/L). The larvae were transferred to fish water (0.5 mM NaH2PO4, 0.5 mM Na2HPO4, 0.15 g Instant Ocean, 1 L deionized H2O) after hatching and maintained under a 12:12 light: dark cycle at 28 °C. Starting from 5 dpf, larvae were fed twice a day with powdered food containing zooplankton and phytoplankton (Sera Micron Nature). As the zebrafish were in the larval stage at the time of testing, we were unaware of their sex. Based on previous observations in the facility, we estimate a sex ratio of approximately 50:50 male: female in the subjects.

Environmental manipulation

To induce plasticity, we manipulated the larval maintenance environment, following an approach used in previous studies [15, 16] (Fig. 1). This protocol aimed to expose half of the larvae to a complex environment enriched with stimuli, and the remaining half to a simple, barren environment. The treatment started at 4 dpf, when the larvae from the two lines were divided into two environmental treatment groups (enriched or barren), yielding four experimental conditions: enriched bdnf -/-, barren bdnf -/-, enriched bdnf +/+ and barren bdnf +/+. Eighty larvae were assigned to each condition, maintained separately in four glass tanks per condition (19 × 13 × 10 cm; 20 larvae per tank) filled with 250 ml fish water (1.5 cm water column). The health condition of the larvae was checked daily during feeding. Thirty minutes after the feeding, the tanks were cleaned by removing faecal materials and uneaten food. The tanks were then refilled with new fish water to ensure stable conditions as much as possible. All treatment aquaria were externally covered with white panels and kept in an isolated experimental room to reduce disturbance from external factors. Each treatment tank was illuminated with LED strips (natural daylight, 6000 K; ELCART, Italy).

In the enriched conditions, each tank contained 9 Lego® bricks differing in colour and shape, while in the barren condition, the tanks were empty. The use of these bricks as enrichment is common in experimental studies [15, 58, 59] and provides several advantages: the procedure can be easily replicated and compared across laboratories; the bricks are made of non-toxic material; and the bricks are easy to clean. In line with a widely adopted enrichment protocol [60], we changed the bricks’ positions twice, at 8 dpf and 13 dpf. We also introduced novel stimuli by randomly substituting two of the bricks with others of the same colour, but a different shape. Upon position changes and substitutions of the bricks in the enriched condition, we performed a pseudo-manipulation on the larvae in the barren condition using a Pasteur pipette. At 14 dpf, we randomly selected 208 larvae (N = 52 per condition) from the pool that survived at the end of the treatment. These larvae were moved to individual wells in 24-well plates for behavioural and cognitive assessments. A total of nine plates were tested: 8 plates contained 6 larvae per condition, while a 9th plate containing 4 larvae per condition was included to ensure the minimum sample size of 8 larvae per sample for the gene expression analysis. Larvae were randomly sampled from the four tanks within the same treatment group and transferred to plates for behavioural and cognitive assessment. The surviving larvae that were not used for these experiments were transferred to a separate system to be maintained as future breeding stock.

Behavioural assessment: locomotor activity

To perform the behavioural test, the larvae were collected from the treatment tanks and individually moved in separate wells (Ø = 19 mm) of 24 well-plates filled with 2.5 ml of fish water (water column: 1 cm). The position of the larvae from each condition in the array of wells was pseudorandom and counterbalanced across trials. The plates were inserted into the DanioVision system (Noldus, Wageningen, the Netherlands), which automatically recorded the locomotor activity of each larva as distance moved (mm). We set the same tracking settings for all plates. The locomotor activity was analysed for a 60-minute period divided in 1-minute time bins to examine temporal changes in behaviour [61].

Cognitive assessment: habituation learning

After scoring the activity, we performed the cognitive assessment by administering 30 vibrational pulses to the larvae using a solenoid (Tapping Device DVOC 004x/T, DanioVision, Noldus, Wageningen, the Netherlands) controlled by EthovisionXT software. Each stimulation was set at intensity level of 8 (scale 8 on 8, the most intense in the system). The stimuli, separated by a 1-second interval, were designed to induce a startle response, resulting in a temporary increase in activity [62, 63]. The larvae were tracked based on the same settings calibrated in the initial behavioural assessment, but the data were clustered into 1-second time bins. We used the distance moved in the second following the stimulation as a measure for individuals' startle response. Due to the habituation learning, the intensity of the startle response was expected to progressively decrease across the stimulations [62]. As in Lucon-Xiccato and colleagues [25] and Gatto and colleagues [16, 29], an index for the reduction in activity for each (i-th) stimulation was calculated using the following formula:

Using this index formula, we obtained 30 repeated measures datapoints per larvae, where the first datapoint was set to zero. A greater decrease in the startle response resulted in lower index values, indicating greater habituation learning. Because individual differences in the baseline startle response might affect the index, we also analysed the distance covered in response to the first stimulation.

RNA extraction and gene expression analysis

After the cognitive test, 206 larvae were euthanized in MS222 (200 mg/L; Sigma-Aldrich, Milano, Italy), as two out of the 208 tested larvae (both enriched bdnf +/+) died during testing. The sacrificed larvae were dissected under stereomicroscope, to obtain the heads for RNA extraction [64]. Heads of larvae from the same conditions were pooled together (8/9 heads per sample, 6 samples per condition) for RNA extraction. RNA was extracted using Trizol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) following manufacturer’s instructions. RNA concentration and quality were measured with BioSpec-nano (Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan). One microgram of DNaseI-treated RNA was used to perform cDNA synthesis using iScript cDNA Synthesis Kit (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA). After retrotranscription, we amplified via PCR the first-strand cDNA with a CFX Connect Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA) using SsoAdvanced ™ Universal SYBR ® Green Supermix (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA).

Gene-specific primers (Table 1) were used to quantify the expression of 7 plasticity-related genes: neto1L [65, 66], shisa7b [67], neuroD1 [18, 68], neuroD2 [69, 70], notch1a [71], notch3 [72], and ntrk2b [73, 74]. Moreover, we quantified the expression of a neurogenesis-related gene that was downregulated in the mutant zebrafish, neuroD4 [55, 75], and the housekeeping gene ef1α [76]. We verified the efficiency of the primers by constructing standard curves for all genes investigated and we then performed melting curve analysis to confirm amplicon specificity. We performed assays in technical triplicate and we used the 2–ΔΔCT method (where CT is the number of cycles at which the signal reaches the threshold of detection) for relative expression quantification [77], using the mean CT value of the control samples (barren bdnf +/+) of each gene as reference sample for the ΔΔCT calculation of the respective gene.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed in RStudio version 4.2.2 (https://www.R-project.org/). The significance threshold for the statistical tests was set at P < 0.05. Eighteen larvae out of 206 were removed from the dataset before the statistical analysis. Two of these larvae were removed due to logistic issues during testing; sixteen larvae were removed due to automatic tracking errors causing extremely high activity scores (more than 60 mm per second, corresponding to more than one well-perimeter per second). This resulted in the following final sample size: N total = 188; N enriched bdnf -/- = 48; N barren bdnf -/- = 43; N enriched bdnf +/+ = 48; N barren bdnf +/+ = 49).

The activity (distance moved) was analysed minute by minute using with a linear mixed-effect model (LMM, ‘lmer’ function) fitted with line (bdnf -/- vs. bdnf +/+) and treatment (enriched vs. barren) as fixed factors. A significant interaction between these two factors would indicate plasticity differences due to the lack of bdnf as hypothesised. We also fitted minute (1–60) as covariate to assess temporal variation within the test due to behavioural modulations such as acclimatization to the testing area and exploration of the new environment. We included subject’s ID, tank, and experimental plate as random effect. Significance of the models’ parameters was then evaluated via the Satterthwaite’s degrees of freedom method using the ‘anova’ function from the ‘car’ R package. To investigate the effect of treatment within lines and study significant interactions involving this main effect, we ran a similar model for the two lines separately, including treatment as fixed factor, minute as covariate, and subject ID, tank and plate as random factor. This planned comparison was necessary to study whether the two lines responded differently to the treatment and thus showed different plasticity as for our hypothesis.

For the cognitive test, we first analysed the distance moved after the first stimulation using a LMM fitted with treatment and line as fixed factors, and tank and plate as random factor. Due to the right-skewed distribution of residuals, we increase model fitting by log-transforming the response variable distance moved.

The habituation learning index was a repeated measures variable ranged from 0 (low performance) to -1 (high performance). We analysed it with a LMM fitted with line and treatment as fixed factors, aiming to study their interaction to detect a role of BDNF in plasticity. Moreover, we fitted the stimulation (1–30) as covariate because in this test learning is identified as a change of response over time. Last, we fitted subject’s ID, tank and plate as random effects. The predictor ‘stimulation’ was log-transformed to better model the decrement of habituation learning index across stimulations [16, 18, 78]. To deal with a right skewed distribution and improve model fitting, we log-transformed the response variable habituation learning index. Because log-transformation values of 0 and negative number are undefined, we add a constant (1.0001) to all index values to make them all positive before the log-transformation. When finding a significant effect including treatment × genotype, we performed the planned comparison as described for the analysis of the activity response.

To study the effect of the environmental enrichment on the gene expression profile of the two lines, we performed an exploratory analysis on the effect of treatment and line on the expression of each gene with a two-way ANOVA. The significance of parameters was assessed via the Satterthwaite’s degrees of freedom method. As each linear model evaluated separately the hypothesis whether the treatment affected gene’s expression, this resulted in independent testing [79, 80]. When finding a significance interaction between fixed factors (line × treatment), we investigated the effect of treatment within each line by performing post-hoc tests using ‘emmeans’ function from the “emmeans” library. We reported mean ± standard deviation of relative expression level of genes differing between treatments. For the within-lines comparison, treatment effects were calculated as the difference between groups [barren – enriched]; accordingly, positive values indicate higher expression in the barren group, while negative values indicate higher expression in the enriched group.

Results

Behavioural assessment: locomotor activity

The distance moved by the larvae varied significantly between the lines (LMM: F1,13 = 23.116, P < 0.001, partial η2 = 0.65) and the treatments (F1,13 = 7.561, P = 0.016, partial η2 = 0.37): bdnf +/+ were overall more active than bdnf -/-, and larvae raised in enriched environment were more active than those raised in barren environment. The LMM also revealed a significant change in the distance moved over time (F1,11088 = 4954.291; P < 0.001, partial η2 = 0.31) and a significant line × time interaction (F1,11088 = 36.678, P < 0.001, partial η2 = 0.00). In addition, the LMM revealed a significant triple interaction (line × treatment × time: F1,11088 = 7.346, P = 0.007, partial η2 = 0.00). The analysis of the treatment effect within each line found a significant effect of treatment (F1,86 = 8.315, P = 0.005, partial η2 = 0.09) and a significant treatment × time interaction (F1,5367 = 5.083, P = 0.024, partial η2 = 0.00) in the bdnf -/- line (Fig. 2b), while both effects were absent in the bdnf +/+ line (F1,7 = 1.496, P = 0.259, partial η2 = 0.17; F1,5721 = 2.503, P = 0.114, partial η2 = 0.00; respectively; Fig. 2a). No other significant interactions were found in the initial model (line × treatment: F1,13 = 0.759, P = 0.399, partial η2 = 0.05; treatment × time: F1,11088 = 0.150, P = 0.699, partial η2 = 0.00).

Cognitive assessment: habituation learning

The analysis of the first startle response did not reveal significant effects of treatment (F1,13 = 3.046, P = 0.105), line (F1,12 = 1.598, P = 0.230), or interaction line × treatment (F1,13 = 0.388, P = 0.544).

The analysis of the habituation learning index revealed a significant effect of stimulation (F1,5448 = 634.104, P < 0.001, partial η2 = 0.10), as the startle response decreased progressively, and a significant effect of the line (F1,28 = 6.277, P = 0.018, partial η2 = 0.19), with bdnf -/- showing lower index values compared to the control group. While the overall effect of treatment was not significant (F1,30 = 1.000, P = 0.325, partial η2 = 0.03), we found a significant line × stimulation interaction (F1,5448 = 9.936, P = 0.002, partial η2 = 0.00). Additionally, the analysis revealed a significant line × treatment interaction (F1,30= 5.725, P = 0.023, partial η2 = 0.16) and, more interestingly, a significant triple interaction (line × treatment × stimulation: F1,5448 = 6.898, P = 0.009, partial η2 = 0.00). The analysis split by line revealed a marginally significant effect of the treatment in the bdnf -/- zebrafish (F1,12 = 4.696, P = 0.051, partial η2 = 0.28; Fig. 3b), but not in the bdnf +/+ zebrafish (F1,22 = 1.551, P = 0.226, partial η2 = 0.07; Fig. 3a). The interaction treatment × stimulation was significant in the bdnf +/+ line (F1,2811 = 4.206, P = 0.040, partial η2 = 0.00) and showed a trend towards significance in the bdnf -/- zebrafish (F1,2637 = 2.809, P = 0.094, partial η2 = 0.00), suggesting subtle differences in the response to repeated stimulations in the two lines. Lastly, the treatment × stimulation interaction in the initial model was not significant (F1,5448 = 0.025, P = 0.875, partial η2 = 0.00).

Gene expression

The analysis of gene expression levels revealed no significant main effect of the line (LM: all F1,19 < 3.654, all P > 0.071). There was a significant main effect of treatment for shisa7b (relative expression level: enriched larvae 0.51 ± 0.34 (SD), barren larvae 1.26 ± 0.81 (SD); F1,19 = 8.218, P = 0.010, partial η2 = 0.30) and notch3 (relative expression level: enriched larvae 0.50 ± 0.16 (SD), barren larvae 0.96 ± 0.45 (SD); F1,19 = 10.427, P = 0.004, partial η2 = 0.35), but not for the other genes (all F1,19 < 3.511, all P > 0.076). The within-lines analysis revealed that the effect of treatment on the expression of shisa7b was significant in the bdnf -/- (P = 0.016, Cohen’s d and 95% CI = 1.53 [0.33;2.74]; Fig. 4), but not in their wild-type counterparts (P = 0.186, Cohen’s d and 95% CI = 0.83 [-0.44;2.10]). The treatment significantly affected the expression of notch3 in the bdnf +/+ line (P = 0.015, Cohen’s d and 95% CI = 1.61 [0.35;2.88]; Fig. 4), but not in the knockout one (P = 0.072, Cohen’s d and 95% CI = 1.10 [-0.11;2.31]). Additionally, the comparisons suggested that for neuroD1 there was a significantly effect of treatment only in the bdnf +/+ line (bdnf +/+: P = 0.047, Cohen’s d and 95% CI = -1.29 [-2.55;-0.02; bdnf -/-: P = 0.722, Cohen’s d and 95% CI = 0.21 [-1.00;1.42]). No other effects of treatment within line were detected (for all genes P > 0.107) (Fig. 4).

Discussion

In the present study, we experimentally tested whether the neurotrophin BDNF is involved in the plasticity of behaviour and cognition. To this goal, we exploited a knockout zebrafish line lacking bdnf. Our results indicated marked differences in behaviour and cognition between bdnf -/- zebrafish exposed to enriched and barren environment, suggesting stronger plasticity in response to environmental enrichment in zebrafish lacking bdnf.

BDNF levels are typically reported to increase under environmental enrichment conditions [6, 35, 37], and studies in mammals directly suggest that this molecule may enhance plasticity [36, 42]. Indeed, BDNF is known to promote neuronal growth and differentiation [81, 82] as well as synaptic remodelling [83, 84]. However, our results instead highlight a potentially different role for BDNF. The absence of BDNF did not prevent plasticity, suggesting that this protein is not necessary to initiate plastic responses in zebrafish. Moreover, the absence of BDNF resulted in unexpectedly large plasticity. Understanding the exact mechanism underlying these effects is challenging, given that BDNF is involved in a wide range of neurophysiological processes [85–87], including both organisational and activational effects [88–90]. A first explanation, although not directly demonstrated by our data, would be that BDNF may act during plasticity by modulating its magnitude, in a pattern consistent with a negative feedback mechanism: once plasticity is initiated, BDNF may act to regulate its extent, which could explain why zebrafish lacking BDNF exhibited an exaggerated plastic response. A second possible explanation involves broader organisational changes resulting from the absence of BDNF during development in bdnf -/- zebrafish. In this case, it is worth considering the possibility that the observed effect may not reflect a physiological role of BDNF in plasticity, but rather represent an artefact of the knockout-induced absence of BDNF.

To gain preliminary insights into the underlying mechanism, we performed qPCR analyses on a set of genes involved in neural plasticity to identify distinct response patterns to enrichment between the two zebrafish lines. Among the eight genes analysed, two (shisa7b and notch3) showed average differential expression between treatments, with both being downregulated in enriched zebrafish from both lines, although the magnitude of this effect may have varied between lines. The most notable difference was observed for neuroD1, which was upregulated in wild-type zebrafish exposed to enrichment but remained unchanged in knockout zebrafish under the same conditions. Interestingly, increased neuroD1 expression has also been reported in salmon raised in enriched environments [18]. These findings suggest that, if BDNF modulates plasticity, it may do so by directly or indirectly upregulating neuroD1. We acknowledge that this gene expression analysis was exploratory and carried a risk of false positives, and importantly, that its outcome depended on the initial gene panel used. Nevertheless, it suggests that future studies investigating the role of bdnf in plasticity may benefit from including neuroD1 among the genes analysed.

It is worth noting that part of our results on the control zebrafish line (bdnf +/+) were difficult to interpret. First, bdnf +/+ zebrafish were more active than bdnf -/- zebrafish, while in another study in adults we found the opposite pattern [91]. It is not clear whether this contrast is due to the known behavioural differences between larvae and adults or to methodological factors. For instance, larvae are known to behave differently from adults in certain behavioural tests: they tend to approach bright environments, whereas adults avoid them [92]. These differences may influence how individuals in the two studies perceived and responded to the novel environment. In addition, the two studies relied on different experimental apparatuses. The arena used in the adult experiment was proportionally much larger, and arena size is known to be an important determinant of behavioural responses in this type of test [93, 94]. Moreover, it should be noted that our analyses did not find significant evidence of plasticity in bdnf +/+ zebrafish, while several earlier studies have reported that environmental enrichment induces plasticity in wild-type zebrafish [95, 96], including in early developmental stages [15, 16, 56]. In the case of activity, we believe that this was just a matter of statistical power or a ceiling effect, as Fig. 2a clearly showed plasticity in bdnf +/+ zebrafish. For the habituation learning it is not clear whether the control larvae actually lacked plasticity or they showed it with a reduced extent that could not be detected by our experiment. Nevertheless, these considerations suggest that further research on the bdnf knockout is needed, also using different approaches to clarify these issues. Among others, we used sibling-derived lines because this allowed us to obtain the number of larvae necessary to perform all tests simultaneously and from multiple clutches, thereby reducing environmental confounds. However, to better characterise potential genetic background effects, it may be useful in future studies to directly compare full siblings derived from bdnf +/- crosses.

Another important consideration for future studies, and a potential limitation of the present study, concerns the method used to induce plasticity. We focused on manipulating environmental enrichment because it is the most reliable experimental paradigm for studying behavioural and cognitive plasticity in the literature [13, 15, 16, 18, 58, 97]. Similarly, the choice of bricks as enrichment stimuli was based on previous studies [15, 58, 59]. However, the role of BDNF may be more prominent in responses to other enrichment methods, as well as to other environmental factors. Altered BDNF expression in the brain has indeed been reported in response to social factors and stressors [43, 98, 99]. Similarly, we investigated early-life plasticity, as this is likely most influential in shaping an individual’s development, but plasticity also occurs in adulthood. This may broaden the conditions in which our findings apply, particularly for fish, which exhibit extended brain plasticity compared to other vertebrates at both morphological and cellular levels (e.g. [100, 101]), Although experimentally demanding, extending research to adult plasticity would allow exploration of a wider array of behaviours and cognitive abilities, given that adult fish display a more complex behavioural repertoire than larvae, often comparable to that of mammals and birds [102], and may shed light on the organizational or activational nature of the effect reported in our study.

Conclusions

The current study utilized bdnf-knockout zebrafish to experimentally test the role of BDNF in mediating behavioural and cognitive plasticity in response to environmental enrichment. Our results demonstrated a stronger plastic response in the knockout line compared to wild-type controls under enriched conditions. This finding can be interpreted as indicating that BDNF exerts an effect on plasticity in zebrafish that differs from what was previously hypothesised.
