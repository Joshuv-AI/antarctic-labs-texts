Diffuse glioma with FGFR3‐TACC3 fusion in adults is not a homogenous clinicopathological and molecular entity
Authors: Xiaoli Su, Jiawen Wu, Pingling Wang, Liwen Hu, Yupeng Chen, Caihong Ren, Fangling Song, Hangrui Lin, Chenlin Ji, Yucheng xie, Xingfu Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAdult diffuse gliomas with FGFR3‐TACC3 (F3T3) fusion are rare and highly heterogeneous central nervous system (CNS) tumors. Current research on the biological behavior of these tumors is limited, especially regarding whether histopathologically low‐grade tumors are indolent or represent early‐stage high‐grade tumors, thereby posing challenges for grading. In this single‐center study of 17 patients with adult F3T3 fusion diffuse gliomas (F3T3 gliomas), both low‐ and high‐grade F3T3 gliomas presented distinctive recurrent histopathological features, such as oligodendrocyte‐like cells, branched vessels and frequent calcifications. Molecularly, TERT promoter mutations and 7+/10− chromosomal alterations were common; one patient with recurrent glioma with histopathological features of polymorphous low‐grade neuroepithelial tumor of the young (PLNTY) had additional CDK4 and MDM2 amplifications. Methylation profiling of 3 samples revealed varied results. A patient whose tumor had histopathological features consistent with PLNTY and a methylation subtype classified as the mesenchymal subtype of glioblastoma (GBM) experienced tumor recurrence 8 months after surgery. After 5–62 months of follow‐up, seven patients relapsed, and six died; the primary tumors of the three patients with recurrence presented histopathological characteristics of low‐grade glioma (LGG). GBM patients had worse overall survival than LGG patients (p = 0.035) but similar progression‐free survival (p = 0.47), indicating that LGG patients may experience recurrence. For adults with tumors with histopathological features of PLNTY, further molecular and methylation analyses are needed for grading. If TERT promoter mutations are present, even with a PLNTY methylation profile, these tumors can still exhibit the biological behavior of high‐grade gliomas.Keywords: FGFR3–TACC3 fusion, adult diffuse glioma, heterogeneity, classification and grading, molecular characteristics, methylation profiling

Introduction

With the integration of next‐generation sequencing (NGS) into the diagnostic process, novel molecular characteristics of diffuse gliomas, including FGFR3‐TACC3 (F3T3) fusion, have been identified [1]. F3T3 fusion is found in approximately 4% of IDH wild‐type (IDHwt) glioblastomas (GBMs) [2, 3], as well as in rare cases of low‐grade histology [2, 4]. The F3T3 gene fusion has been recognized as an oncogenic driver in some gliomas [5, 6, 7].

F3T3 gliomas in adults encompass a heterogeneous group of CNS tumors [8]. Systematic methylation analyses confirmed that F3T3 gliomas lack a unified ‘F3T3‐specific methylation cluster’ and instead cluster within IDHwt GBM's molecular classification [2, 3] – findings that inform their epigenetic origin. However, these studies have limitations for adults: Métais et al's 21‐case cohort included only five adults (unbalanced for adult methylation characterization), while Wu et al focused solely on GBMs (no low‐grade F3T3 gliomas). Neither explored methylation‐subtype versus clinicopathological feature (e.g., age, grade, recurrence) associations in adult F3T3 gliomas [2, 3]. Unanswered questions – unique adult methylation‐subtype distribution? prognostic differences by subtype? – are critical for adult epigenetic stratification and treatment, but targeted data are scarce.

Another unresolved issue concerns the biological behavior and grading criteria of low‐grade F3T3 gliomas. The 2021 WHO Classification specifies that IDHwt diffuse gliomas carrying TERT promoter mutations or +7/−10 chromosomal alterations should be classified as GBM, even in the absence of high‐grade histological features [9]. However, a multicenter study by Ramos‐Fresnedo et al demonstrated that low‐grade histological tumors with these molecular features had significantly longer overall survival (OS) than classic GBMs [10], suggesting that the grading of F3T3 gliomas requires a comprehensive assessment of both histological and molecular characteristics. Regrettably, existing studies did not distinguish between adult and pediatric low‐grade cases, failing to clarify the prognostic weight of molecular features such as TERT mutations and CDK4/MDM2 amplification in adult low‐grade F3T3 gliomas [2]. This leaves clinicians without dedicated evidence to evaluate the malignant potential of adult low‐grade F3T3 gliomas, resulting in a ‘one‐size‐fits‐all’ limitation in current grading standards. In terms of differential diagnosis, F3T3 gliomas share significant diagnostic overlap with polymorphous low‐grade neuroepithelial tumor of the young (PLNTY): both may harbor the F3T3 fusion and exhibit overlapping histopathological features, including oligodendrocyte‐like cells and multinodular calcification [11, 12]. PLNTY is classically characterized by ‘age at onset <40 years’ and ‘long‐term epilepsy (duration >1 year)’ [13], but the distribution of these features in adult F3T3 gliomas remains unclear. Additionally, the value of molecular markers such as TERT promoter mutations for distinguishing between the two entities has not been validated in adult single‐center cohorts. Existing differential diagnostic studies included only three adult cases [12]; the limited sample size prevents confirmation of whether a combination of age, epilepsy history, and molecular markers can serve as a precise discriminative system for adult F3T3 gliomas and PLNTY. This ambiguity often leads to misdiagnosis of adult PLNTY‐like F3T3 gliomas, highlighting the urgent need for adult‐specific diagnostic criteria.

While previous studies have established associations between TERT mutations, +7/−10 chromosomal alterations, and the prognosis of F3T3 gliomas [2, 14], exploration of other potential prognostic molecules remains insufficient. NF1, a tumor suppressor gene encoding neurofibromin, regulates tumor progression by inhibiting the RAS‐MAPK pathway [15], yet its mutation frequency and prognostic significance in adult F3T3 gliomas have not been reported. The clinical value of this molecular marker requires further investigation.

To address these gaps, we enrolled 17 adult F3T3 glioma patients and systematically analyzed integrated data (clinical, imaging, histopathological, molecular genetic, epigenetic). Our objectives were to: (1) explore adult F3T3 glioma methylation subtype distribution and establish a ‘methylation subtype‐clinicopathological feature‐prognosis’ model to supplement adult epigenetic stratification; (2) analyze key molecular features–prognosis associations in adult low‐grade cases and propose a targeted grading scheme; (3) validate age, clinical manifestations, and TERT promoter mutations for distinguishing adult F3T3 gliomas from PLNTY and build an adult‐specific system; and (4) investigate NF1 prognostic significance to refine adult prognostic assessment.

Compared with previous multicenter studies involving mixed populations [1, 2], the core innovation of this study lies in its focus on a single adult cohort to address ‘population‐specific’ questions in adult F3T3 gliomas. By integrating multi‐dimensional data, this study provides clinically targeted evidence for the precise diagnosis, grading, differential diagnosis, and prognostic stratification of adult F3T3 gliomas, filling the gap in adult cohort research and advancing personalized diagnosis and treatment for this patient population.

This study was reviewed and approved by the First Affiliated Hospital of Fujian Medical University ethics committee (MRCTA, ECFAH of FMU [2021]686). Clinical characteristics of F3T3 gliomas

The histological classification is determined according to the WHO 2021 Classification of Central Nervous System Tumors: LGG refers to Grade 2 glioma, and HGG refers to Grades 3 and 4 gliomas. Morphological subtypes are classified based on H&E staining characteristics, where the ‘PLNTY‐like subtype’ requires simultaneous fulfillment of the following criteria: multifocal calcification, oligodendrocyte‐like morphology, absence of necrosis or microangioplasia, and positive immunohistochemical CD34 tumor cell staining.

Long‐term epilepsy: defined as epileptic seizures lasting >1 year (consistent with typical PLNTY clinical features), annotated as ‘Long‐term epilepsy (duration: X years)’.

Acute epilepsy: defined as epileptic seizures lasting <3 months (more common in high‐grade lesions), annotated as ‘Acute epilepsy (duration: X weeks/months)’.

No epilepsy history: clearly annotated as ‘No history of epileptic seizures’ to avoid ambiguity.

Among the 17 patients in this study, only one case (Case 11, GBM subtype) had acute epileptic seizures (lasting 2 weeks), and no patient presented with long‐term epilepsy (>1 year) – a typical feature of PLNTY. Statistical analysis showed that the ‘no epilepsy history/acute epilepsy’ characteristic of F3T3 gliomas (especially the PLNTY‐like LGG subtype) was significantly different from the ‘high incidence of long‐term epilepsy (incidence >80%)’ of PLNTY reported in the literature (p < 0.001). This further supports that ‘duration of epilepsy history’ can serve as one of the key discriminative indicators between adult F3T3 gliomas and PLNTY.

HGG, high‐grade glioma; LGG, low‐grade glioma.

Pathological analysis and immunohistochemistry

The samples were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) according to standard protocols. The assessment was carried out by two neuropathologists according to the fifth edition of the WHO classification of CNS tumors: (1) glioblastoma (GBM, Grade 4) was defined as meeting ‘both brisk mitotic activity (≥5 mitoses per 10 high‐power fields, HPF) + either microvascular proliferation (MVP) or tumor necrosis’; specifically, cases with brisk mitotic activity alone (without MVP or necrosis) were not classified as GBM, but further categorized as Grade 3 gliomas based on the Ki‐67 index. (2) Low‐grade gliomas (LGG, Grade 2) were defined as having ‘no brisk mitotic activity (<5 mitoses per 10 HPF), no MVP, and no tumor necrosis’; specifically, cases with the typical features of PLNTY (multifocal calcification, oligodendrocyte‐like cells, no necrosis/MVP, and positive CD34 immunohistochemical staining in tumor cells) classified as PLNTY‐like subtype; cases with the features of diffuse astrocytoma, without PLNTY features classified as non‐PLNTY‐like diffuse astrocytoma subtype. Immunohistochemical staining was performed on a Roche fully automated immunohistochemistry (IHC) instrument (Roche XT). Primary antibodies against the following antigens were procured from the specified manufacturers: GFAP, NeuN, Olig2, CD34, IDH1 R132H, EMA, H3K27M, ATRX, H3K27me3, and Ki‐67. All the antibodies were purchased from Beijing Zhongshan Jinqiao Biotechnology Co., Ltd. The experimental procedures were executed in accordance with the manufacturers' instructions.

NGS analysis

For all patients with a preliminary diagnosis of ‘suspected diffuse glioma’ admitted to our institution between January 2019 and March 2024, routine NGS testing (covering fusion targets including FGFR2 and FGFR3) was conducted. The ‘Human 106 Gene Mutation Detection Kit’ (Nanjing Geneseeq Technology, Inc., Nanjing, PR China) used in this study includes genes associated with glioma diagnosis and prognosis. The complete list of genetic and chromosomal alterations included in this panel – covering key markers such as 1p/19q codeletion, IDH1/2, TERT, and FGFR3 – has been provided as supplementary material, Table S1, with detailed annotations for chromosomal abnormalities and clinically relevant molecular markers in the table legend. Genomic DNA was extracted from tumor‐enriched areas of patients' FFPE samples using the QIAamp DNA FFPE Tissue Kit (56404) (First Affiliated Hospital of Fujian Medical University). For library construction: DNA was sonicated (Covaris M220) to fragment, end‐repaired, ligated with adapters (known sequences) and indexed to generate a prelibrary; hybridization capture was then performed using probes from the Human 106 Gene Mutation Detection Kit (Nanjing Geneseeq Technology, Inc.) to build the final library. Quality control included: Nanodrop Lite (sample/library purity), Qubit 3.0 with dsDNA HS Assay Kit (concentration), and Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (prelibrary/final library length distribution). Paired‐end sequencing was conducted on an Illumina NextSeq 500Dx, and gene mutation data were obtained via bioinformatic analysis.

GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis

This study's pathway enrichment analysis focused on the ‘F3T3 glioma‐specific key molecular subtypes’ without external comparisons with LGG or GBM data from the TCGA database. The specific analytical steps were as follows: conducted pathway enrichment analysis using KEGG and GO databases for de‐duplicated mutated genes; converted to Entrez IDs via the R language's clusterProfiler package (bitr function, dependent org.Hs.eg.db annotation package), using the human genome‐wide corresponding database‐annotated gene set as background; performed analyses separately using enrichGO and enrichKEGG functions (parameters: species ‘hsa’, hypergeometric test, p < 0.05, BH‐corrected q < 0.2, pathway gene count 10–500); defined significantly enriched pathways with q ≤ 0.05; visualized results through Cytoscape by constructing ‘pathway‐gene’ network diagrams.

Fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH)

FGFR3 FISH testing conducted at our institution is classified as confirmatory testing. For cases where NGS testing indicated FGFR3 rearrangement (including FGFR3::TACC3 fusion), FISH was conducted on 4‐μm sections of formalin‐fixed, paraffin‐embedded tissue blocks via the FGFR3 gene break probe. The FGFR3 break probe was provided by Guangzhou Anbiping Medical Technology Co., Ltd. The FISH signals were analyzed in at least 50 nonoverlapping intact cell nuclei. If more than 20% of the cell nuclei exhibited a signal pattern consistent with gene rearrangement (separation of red and green signals or a single green signal), the sample was considered positive.

DNA methylation profiling

DNA‐based methylation profiling was performed for three patients (patient 8, patient 12, and patient 13) with available FFPE unstained sections. All procedures were performed with a commercially available Illumina Infinium EPIC_v2.0 Beadchip array according to the Infinium HD Methylation Assay Protocol as previously described [16]. To determine the genomic DNA methylation pattern subgroup affiliation of our samples, we used the reference DNA methylation data published by Capper et al [17] available from the Gene Expression Omnibus database (GSE73801). Our samples were combined with 2,801 reference CNS tumor samples and control brain tissue samples for unsupervised hierarchical clustering as previously described [17]. To ensure the reliability of results, two mainstream dimensionality reduction methods were used for unsupervised hierarchical clustering in this study: (1) UMAP (Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection) with parameter settings: init = ‘random’, min_dist = 0, spread = 3.0, and other parameters set to default; (2) tSNE (t‐distributed stochastic neighbor embedding) with parameter settings: theta = 0, max_iter = 3,000, and other parameters set to default. Ultimately, the tSNE clustering plot and the UMAP validation results were included in the supplementary material, Figure S1A, B.

DKFZ random forest classification was performed using the Epignostix platform (https://epignostix.dkfz.de/). Integration of Public F3T3 IDAT Data: IDAT files from 11 GBM‐F3T3‐O glioma cases obtained from the GSE200647 database (PMID: 35567606) were merged with three samples from this study for re‐clustering.

Statistical analysis

All the statistical tests were executed in R/4.4.2, including Fisher's exact test for categorical data, a two‐sample Mann–Whitney test for continuous data, a log‐rank test for Kaplan–Meier curves, and Cox proportional hazards regression for the calculation of hazard ratios (HRs) and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs). Survival analysis was performed via the R package survival. The statistical significance threshold was defined at p < 0.05.

Results

Clinical characteristics

A total of 17 patients with F3T3 fusions were included in this study to investigate the clinicopathological and genetic variations related to F3T3 fusions in the Chinese population (Table 1). The cohort consisted of 7 women and 10 men. The median age at the time of diagnosis was 53 years, ranging from 34 to 69 years. These patients primarily presented with headache and limb disorders, without a long‐standing epilepsy history. CT scans revealed that 9 patients had masses accompanied by calcification. MRI revealed large lesions in the cerebral hemispheres with ill‐defined margins. In 13 patients, the T1‐weighted enhanced scan revealed nonhomogeneous enhancement or ring‐like enhancement (Figure 1).

Typical MR imaging features of F3T3 gliomas. T1‐weighted enhanced scans revealed heterogeneous or ring‐like enhancement (6#, 11#–14#). No enhancement was observed on T1‐weighted enhanced scans (5#, 17#). Upper left corners: the numbers correspond to the patient IDs.

Histopathological features

Histopathologically, F3T3 gliomas presented recurrent morphological characteristics, such as calcification, an endocrinoid vascular pattern composed of thin and branched vessels, oligodendroglial‐like tumor cells with oval and uniform nuclei and a distinct perinuclear halo, and desmoplasia (Figure 2D–F). Among the 17 F3T3 glioma cases in this study, there were 6 cases (35.3%) of low‐grade gliomas (LGG, Grade 2) (Figure 2G–I), along with a low proliferation index (Ki67 <2%) (Figure 2K), including 5 PLNTY‐like subtypes and 1 non‐PLNTY‐like subtype; 11 cases (64.7%) were high‐grade gliomas (HGG), all classified as GBM (Figure 2A, B), and some also presented tumor giant cells (Figure 2C). Extravascular CD34 expression was detected in five patients (all LGG patients) (Figure 2J).

Molecular features

No IDH1/2 or H3 mutations were detected in any of the patients. All the patients demonstrated F3T3 fusion through FISH and NGS (Figures 2I and 3A). The NM transcript number and genomic breakpoint location of the FGFR3::TACC3 fusion have been supplemented in supplementary material, Table S2, ensuring the traceability of sequence information and providing a reference for subsequent validation experiments. The most prevalent genetic alteration identified in F3T3 gliomas was TERT promoter mutation (16 out of 17 patients; 94.12%), followed by 7+/10− chromosomal alterations (6 out of 17 patients; 35.29%). None of the patients demonstrated amplifications in EGFR. In addition, three recurrent copy number variation (CNV) alterations were found: Homozygous deletion of CDKN2A/B in five cases (29.4%); CDK4 amplification in three cases (17.6%) and MDM2 amplification in two cases (11.8%). No novel CNV patterns specific to F3T3 gliomas were identified. It should be noted that, since whole exome sequencing (WES) – which provides high‐coverage data necessary for reliable detection of arm‐level copy number variations (CNVs) – was not conducted for consistency verification in this study, chromosome 19 gain and chromosome 20 gain were not included in the current study's analysis to ensure result reliability. This exclusion is not permanent: we intend to validate and analyze these arm‐level CNVs in a future follow‐up study with a larger cohort, where WES will be performed to confirm their presence and potential clinical significance (Figure 3A).

(B) Association between genetic alterations and histopathological subtypes. PTEN mutations are significantly enriched in F3T3 gliomas with glioblastoma (GBM, WHO Grade IV) features (Fisher's exact test, p < 0.05). (C) GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of key mutated/altered genes. The analysis revealed that CDKN2A/B (homozygous deletion), PTEN (mutation), and NF1 (mutation) – genes enriched in GBM subtypes – are significantly concentrated in signaling pathways such as the cell cycle regulatory pathway (q ≤ 0.05, BH‐corrected). Of note, no mutations were detected in IDH1 (R132 site), IDH2 (R172 site), or H3F3A (K27M, G34R/V sites) among the 17 F3T3 fusion‐positive glioma cases in this study. This finding suggests no significant co‐occurrence between the aforementioned gene mutations and the F3T3 fusion event, and this result has been incorporated into the statistical analysis.

Additionally, correlation analysis between gene mutations and histopathological features of the 17 samples in this study showed that PTEN mutations, CDKN2A/2B homozygous deletions, and NF1 mutations were significantly enriched in the GBM subtype, while SMARCB1 mutations were significantly enriched in the low‐grade glioma subtype (Figure 3B). GO and KEGG enrichment analyses revealed that these genes were significantly enriched in cell cycle regulation pathways (all meeting q value ≤0.05), suggesting these genes may be potential molecular mechanisms driving progression and poor prognosis of F3T3 fusion‐related GBM (Figure 3C).

To explore imaging‐molecular phenotype relationships, we analyzed calcification enrichment with key co‐mutations (SMARCB1, PTEN). The SMARCB1 mutation rate was higher in patients with calcification (4/9, 44.4%) than in those without (1/8, 12.5%; Fisher's exact test, p = 0.029); the PTEN mutation rate was higher in patients without calcification (6/8, 75.0%) than in those with calcification (1/9, 11.1%; Fisher's exact test, p < 0.001). This indicates potential associations between calcification and SMARCB1/PTEN mutations, offering a reference for predicting molecular phenotypes via imaging. Detailed visualization of the mutation rate differences between calcification groups is provided in supplementary material, Figure S2.

DNA methylation profiling data

DNA methylation profiling was carried out on three samples. A combined strategy of ‘dimensionality reduction analysis plus DKFZ random forest classification’ was adopted for methylation classification in this study (DKFZ classification was performed via the Epignostix website, version 12.8). This showed that case 1 (histologically consistent with PLNTY features) was classified as ‘GBM RTK2 subtype’ with a confidence score of 0.67, and case 2 (recurrent case) was classified as ‘GBM mesenchymal subtype’ with a confidence score of 0.62. Both met the reliability criterion of confidence score >0.6. Detailed classification results are shown in Figure 4.

Clinical outcomes

Follow‐up data were obtained for all patients, with a median follow‐up duration of 16.8 months (ranging from 5 to 62 months). Seven patients experienced recurrence or progression (comprising three LGG patients and four GBM patients). Six GBM patients died from the disease (including one patient who relapsed) (see Table 1).

Notably, for one patient with histological characteristics of GBM, molecular profiling revealed only a TERT promoter mutation; no other high‐risk molecular features were identified. The OS of this patient reached 52 months, which was markedly superior to that of other GBM patients. Additionally, the prognostic analysis demonstrated that the OS of patients with tumors exhibiting histological features of GBM (WHO Grade 4) was significantly inferior to that of patients with histologically classified LGG (WHO Grade 2) (p = 0.035). However, there was no significant difference in progression‐free survival (PFS) between the two groups (p = 0.47) (Figure 5A, B). This finding implies that patients with histologically defined LGG might still be at risk of recurrence. The results of univariate prognostic analysis indicated that NF1 mutation was a high‐risk factor for recurrence or death in patients with F3T3 gliomas (Figure 5C, D). However, PTEN mutation was not associated with clinical prognosis.

Brief presentation of three cases of F3T3 LGG in which the patients had unusual clinical outcomes

We encountered three cases of F3T3 LGG in which the patients had unusual clinical outcomes (patient 4, patient 13, and patient 15). MRI revealed a significantly and heterogeneously enhanced mass in the hemisphere (Figure 6A, D). Histologically, the primary tumor exhibited the histological features of LGG (Figure 6B, C). Extravascular CD34 expression was noted (Figure 6H). The recurrent tumor had the histological features of GBM (Figure 6E), along with a significantly increased proliferation index (Figure 6F). Moreover, immunostaining revealed that the tumor cells were negative for CD34 (Figure 6I). NGS analysis revealed the presence of a TERT promoter mutation. In addition, patient 13 had additional CDK4 and MDM2 amplifications. Moreover, all these alterations were also present at the initial diagnosis (Figure 6G, J).

Discussion

Methylation subtypes and co‐occurring mutations in prognostic stratification of adult F3T3 gliomas

F3T3 diffuse gliomas with LGG features show marked genetic and epigenetic heterogeneity, complicating accurate classification and grading [18]. As reported by Marastoni et al [18], histopathologically low‐grade adult F3T3 gliomas with GBM‐associated molecular features may act as precursor lesions for HGG. In this study, methylation profiling reclassified two LGG with PLNTY‐like histology as GBMs, indicating a biological continuum between low‐ and high‐grade F3T3 gliomas. One such case – with high‐grade genetic alterations (MDM2/CDK4 amplification) – recurred 8 months post‐surgery. This suggests low‐grade histological F3T3 gliomas harboring high‐grade genetic/epigenetic features (e.g., GBM methylation subtype) may already have high‐grade epigenetic hallmarks, validating the ‘molecular‐first’ approach in the 2021 WHO CNS Tumor Classification [9] and informing optimized F3T3 glioma grading.

Reassessing grading and classification of adult low‐grade F3T3 gliomas: toward a more clinically relevant framework

The 2021 WHO Classification specifies adult IDH‐wildtype diffuse gliomas with TERT promoter mutations as GBM [9]. However, our data – consistent with Ramos‐Fresnedo et al [10] – challenge this for adult low‐grade F3T3 gliomas: most (83.3%) of our low‐grade F3T3 cases carried TERT mutations, yet their median OS (45.6 months) was significantly longer than classical IDHwt GBMs (14.6 months). Further, cIMPACT‐NOW update 11 prohibits standalone TERT mutations for upgrading LGG [19], so diagnosing adult low‐grade F3T3 gliomas with isolated TERT mutations as GBM requires caution.

Notably, three TERT‐mutant low‐grade F3T3 cases recurred (one also had CDK4/MDM2 amplification), and histopathologically low‐grade versus GBM cases showed no significant PFS difference – indicating high recurrence risk in TERT‐mutant low‐grade tumors. Conversely, one histopathologically confirmed GBM with only TERT mutation had a 52‐month OS, outperforming other GBMs. These findings confirm that grading adult low‐grade F3T3 gliomas demands comprehensive assessment of high‐risk molecular factors (TERT promoter mutation, MDM2 amplification, and CDK4 amplification) rather than reliance on single molecular markers or histological features alone.

Thus, to address the ‘one‐size‐fits‐all’ limitation of current criteria [2] and provide a practical framework for clinical treatment decisions, we propose a stratified grading scheme for adult low‐grade F3T3 gliomas: (1) low‐grade F3T3 gliomas with high‐risk factors (e.g., MDM2/CDK4 amplification) = ‘Grade 3’ (aggressive intervention); (2) isolated TERT mutations = ‘Grade 2 (high‐risk)’ (close follow‐up); (3) no high‐risk alterations = standard ‘Grade 2’ (routine management).

Developing a multidimensional diagnostic system for distinguishing adult F3T3 gliomas from PLNTY

F3T3 gliomas and PLNTY share F3T3 fusion and histopathological features (oligodendrocyte‐like cells, multifocal calcification, CD34 immunoreactivity) [11, 12], complicating diagnosis. Notably, in adult patients, the predictive value of PLNTY's classic features (age <40 years, long‐standing epilepsy [13]) is low, further complicating diagnosis. While Bale et al [12] previously conducted relevant research, their study included only three adult cases; the limited sample size compromised the reliability and generalizability of their conclusions. Addressing this gap, this study validated a three‐dimensional diagnostic system for adults: (1) age: adult F3T3 gliomas had a median onset age of 53 years, with few (23.5%) <40 years – higher than PLNTY's median age <40 years [12]; (2) epilepsy: no adult F3T3 cases had PLNTY's typical long‐standing epilepsy (>1 year), versus >80% in PLNTY [13] (p < 0.001); (3) molecular markers: among the 17 adult F3T3 gliomas, 16 (94.1%) harbored TERT promoter mutations, whereas the mutation rate of TERT in PLNTY is <10% and most mutations are non‐pathogenic variants [12].

Three TERT‐mutant adult low‐grade F3T3 gliomas recurred – contrary to PLNTY's indolent behavior. Combined with their histopathological similarities, this finding suggests that adult F3T3 fusion‐positive GBMs may originate from low‐grade precursors. This supports classifying F3T3 fusion‐positive gliomas as an independent entity or ‘GBMs with low‐grade features’.

This diagnostic system avoids over‐reliance on overlapping features such as the F3T3 fusion and provides a clear workflow: screen adult PLNTY‐like tumors via age/epilepsy history, confirm with TERT promoter mutation detection.

Exploring the prognostic impact of NF1 mutations in adult F3T3 gliomas

This is the first study to investigate NF1 mutations in adult F3T3 gliomas: 45.5% of adult F3T3‐type GBMs had NF1 mutations. Ballester et al reported NF1 mutations associated with low‐grade F3T3 progression to HGG [20], and our univariate analysis identified NF1 mutations as a high‐risk factor for early recurrence/death in F3T3 fusion‐positive gliomas. GO/KEGG enrichment showed NF1 mutations – enriched in GBM – cluster in cell cycle pathways, suggesting they drive F3T3 GBM progression and poor prognosis.

Previous studies confirm NF1 (a negative RAS‐MAPK regulator) drives mesenchymal subtype transformation [21] – a function consistent with its role in other aggressive gliomas. Accordingly, NF1 mutations may serve as a ‘high‐risk’ marker for mesenchymal‐subtype adult F3T3 gliomas, aiding prognostic assessment and therapeutic target selection.

Study limitations and future directions

This study has four limitations: (1) single‐center retrospective design and small sample size may introduce selection bias; conclusions require validation in large multi‐center adult cohorts to confirm methylation‐subtype/prognostic‐marker associations and imaging calcification–molecular mutation correlations. (2) Median follow‐up (24.5 months) is insufficient to assess late‐stage progression; extended follow‐up is needed to verify methylation subtype/prognostic marker stability. (3) Lack of functional experiments (e.g., NF1 knockout) prevents confirming NF1 mutation‐tumor aggressiveness causality, requiring further mechanistic studies. (4) Sample constraints limited DNA methylation analysis in some cases, reducing completeness.

Future research should focus on: (1) validating the grading protocol and identification system in large multi‐center adult cohorts, and clarifying imaging calcification–mutation correlations; (2) exploring subtype‐specific targeted therapies (e.g., RAS‐MAPK inhibitors for NF1‐mutant mesenchymal GBMs); (3) integrating methylomics/transcriptomics to identify epigenetic regulators for novel targets; and (4) complementing DNA methylation analysis to fully characterize F3T3 gliomas.

Conclusions

This study addresses key gaps in adult F3T3 glioma research: it analyzes methylation subtypes and their prognostic value, proposes a stratified grading scheme, establishes a three‐dimensional (age–epilepsy–TERT) system to distinguish F3T3 gliomas from PLNTY, and confirms NF1 mutations as a high‐risk prognostic marker, laying a foundation for prognostic stratification and targeted therapy. These findings refine classification, diagnosis, and prognostic assessment for adult F3T3 gliomas, supporting personalized clinical management.
