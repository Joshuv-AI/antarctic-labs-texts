Temporal trends, geographic patterns and predictors of chronic health conditions in Australian children: a mixed multilevel analysis
Authors: Ayele Mamo Abebe, Zahirul Hoque, Rasheda Khanam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study examined temporal trends, spatial distribution and multilevel predictors of chronic conditions using nationally representative data from the Longitudinal Study of Australian Children.Study designLongitudinal population-based cohort study using multilevel and spatial analyses.MethodsMultilevel mixed-effects logistic regression models were used to identify child-level, parent-level and context-level predictors across waves 2–8. Model fit was assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient and Akaike Information Criterion. Spatial mapping was performed using Statistical Area Level 2. Significance was set at p < 0.05.ResultsThe prevalence of chronic conditions rose from 32.1% to 58.7%. Significant child-level predictors included severe sleep problems (adjusted odd ratio (AOR) = 2.98), sedentary behaviour (AOR = 1.27), poor diet (low fruit/vegetable intake: AOR = 1.14; high-fat foods: AOR = 1.88), sugary drink intake (AOR = 1.23) and obesity (AOR = 1.43). Parental and contextual predictors included Indigenous status (AOR = 1.68), financial hardship (AOR = 1.16), maternal medical conditions (AOR = 1.48), paternal depression (AOR = 1.61), low household income ($A <500: AOR = 1.26), paternal smoking (AOR = 1.41), heavy alcohol consumption by mothers (AOR = 1.77) and fathers (AOR = 1.72), residence in non-metropolitan areas (AOR = 1.53) and living in moderately to very remote areas (AOR = 2.01).ConclusionChronic conditions among Australian children are increasing, driven by interconnecting child, family and geographic risk factors. Multilevel interventions are needed to address these rising inequalities.

Introduction

A chronic health condition refers to a long-term health problem lasting 6 months or more, requiring continuous care or lifestyle adjustments.1 These include asthma, diabetes, allergic diseases, ear and eye problems, respiratory conditions and heart diseases. Chronic conditions require regular management to prevent exacerbation and ensure optimal health outcomes.1

According to the WHO, childhood chronic conditions are increasing globally and have become a major public health concern.2 In Australia, approximately 40% of children aged 0–14 years are affected by one or more chronic conditions, posing a significant challenge to public health systems.3

Understanding childhood chronic conditions requires a framework that captures how individual, family and environmental factors interact over time. Bronfenbrenner’s Bioecological Model provides such a structure, conceptualising child development within nested systems—ranging from individual biological characteristics (eg, weight, sleep, activity) to family environments (eg, parental health, mental health and socioeconomic resources) and broader contextual systems (eg, community disadvantage, remoteness and geographic access). Within this model, chronic conditions arise from the dynamic interplay between proximal processes (daily interactions and caregiving environments), individual characteristics and broader social and structural conditions.4

Consistent with this framework, previous studies have identified risk and protective factors across multiple ecological levels. Children’s sleep patterns,57 free time activity,8 9 dietary intake,10 11 family-level determinants such as financial hardship,12 13 household income,1 14 15 ethnicity,16 parental illness,17 18 mental health,19 20 substance or alcohol use21 22 and contextual factors such as region of residence,23 24 and remoteness25 reflect broader structural inequities in healthcare access and community opportunity. Chronic conditions can impair growth, delay puberty and hinder organ system development. They may also compromise endurance, immune function and homeostasis.26 Psychosocial impacts include limitations in physical activity, disrupted peer relationships, school absenteeism, poor academic performance and reduced quality of life.27 28

Despite increasing awareness, limited research in Australia has explored how chronic conditions evolve over time and vary geographically.28 Existing work has described prevalence patterns by socioeconomic status and geography,29 yet little is known about temporal changes, geographical differences and how multilevel predictors interact to shape health trajectories over the course of childhood and adolescence.30 Most prior studies focused on static prevalence, overlooking temporal trends and spatial variation.2830

Guided by the Bioecological Model, this study addresses these gaps by examining temporal trends, identifying child, parent and contextual predictors and mapping geographic patterns of chronic conditions using nationally representative longitudinal data. This ecological approach offers a more comprehensive understanding of how biological, behavioural, familial and structural factors jointly influence chronic disease burdens in Australian children, providing evidence to inform equitable and targeted public health strategies. The findings aimed to inform policy and support targeted public health interventions to reduce inequities in managing and preventing chronic diseases among Australian children.

Methods

This study used data from the Longitudinal Study of Australian Children (LSAC), a nationally representative cohort following children from birth through adolescence since 2004. The study comprises two cohorts: B cohort (5107 infants aged 0–1) and K cohort (4983 children aged 4–5), selected via stratified, clustered sampling from the Medicare enrolment database. LSAC collects extensive data on child development, family dynamics and social environments. Population weights were applied to adjust for the complex survey design.30 We used B cohort data from waves 2 (2004) to 8 (2018), excluding wave 1 (no chronic condition data) and wave 9 (potential COVID-19 disruptions). The final sample included 3127 children who participated in all seven waves. Data were primarily reported by parent 1 (typically the mother). Only variables with complete data across waves were included.

Guided by Bronfenbrenner’s Bioecological Model, the study conceptualised chronic conditions as the product of dynamic interactions across multiple environmental systems.4 In this model: child-level (microsystem), parental and family characteristics (microsystem/mesosystem) and contextual socioeconomic and geographic environments (exosystemic/macrosystem) interact over time to influence health trajectories. By using established developmental research, we hypothesised that chronic condition trajectories would be influenced by early-life socioeconomic, parental, environmental and behavioural exposures.31 32

The outcome was the presence of any chronic health condition, based on parental responses to the question: “Does the child have any chronic conditions?” (Yes/No). These included asthma, allergies, eczema, diabetes, chronic fatigue, abdominal pain, constipation, ear infections, vision problems, joint or bone issues and other long-term illnesses.

Consistent with Bronfenbrenner’s Ecological Model, explanatory variables were organised into three domains. Child-level factors (microsystem) included body mass index (BMI), dietary behaviours (fruit and vegetable intake, consumption of high-fat foods and sugary drink intake), sleep patterns and free-time physical activity. Family-level factors (microsystem/mesosystem) encompassed parental physical and mental health, parental smoking and alcohol use, household income and indicators of financial hardship. Contextual-level factors (exosystemic/macrosystem) captured broader environmental influences, including region of residence and area-level remoteness. Detailed operational definitions and coding procedures for all variables are presented in online supplemental table 1.

Data management and analysis

Data were cleaned, recorded and checked prior to analysis. Missing data were addressed using multiple imputations, with complete case analysis for sensitivity. Imputed results were more robust. Population weights corrected for survey design, and descriptive statistics summarised sample characteristics and trends in chronic conditions trends.

Analytical choices were guided by Bronfenbrenner’s Bioecological Model, which conceptualises child health as shaped by interacting influences at multiple ecological levels—child (microsystem), family (microsystem/mesosystem) and broader contextual environments (exosystemic/macrosystem).4 Reflecting this hierarchical structure, multilevel logistic mixed-effects models were employed to estimate the odds of chronic condition occurrence over time while simultaneously accounting for variation across children, families and geographic contexts. Patient and public involvement

This study involved secondary analysis of data from the Longitudinal Study of Australian Children (LSAC). Patients and the public were not directly involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this research.

Temporal trends of chronic conditions

Child-related factors

Chronic condition prevalence rose from 32.1% in wave 2 to 58.7% in wave 8 (figure 1a), likely due to increased diagnoses in adolescence. Obese children consistently had the highest rates, followed by overweight and underweight peers (figure 1b). Children with severe sleep problems had the highest burden, with gaps widening over time (figure 1c). Children who did sedentary activities had higher rates than those with balanced activities (figure 1d).

Trends of chronic conditions by child-related factors. BMI, body mass index.

Indigenous children consistently had higher rates than non-Indigenous peers, with widening disparities after wave 5 (figure 1e). Children in moderately or very remote areas had the highest rates—above 0.65 for males and 0.7 for females by wave 8. Children in accessible and highly accessible areas had lower but rising rates (figure 1f).

Parent-related factors

Children of mothers with chronic illness consistently had higher chronic condition rates, with disparities growing over time (figure 2a). Children whose fathers had a medical condition consistently experienced a higher mean proportion of chronic conditions across all waves (figure 2b). Maternal smoking was linked to higher child chronic illness, especially by wave 8 (figure 2c). Fathers’ smoking showed a similar trend after wave 5 (figure 2d). Children in non-metropolitan areas experienced faster increases in chronic illness than metropolitan peers, especially between waves 5 and 6 (figure 2e). Remoteness remained a strong predictor, with the highest rates in moderate or very remote areas, though all areas showed rising trends (figure 2f).

Multilevel analysis of the predictors of chronic conditions

Children with mild sleep problems had 1.35 times higher odds (AOR=1.35; 95% C 1.23 to 1.49), moderate sleep issues 2.07 times (AOR=2.07; 95% CI 1.76 to 2.43) and large problems 2.98 times (AOR=2.98; 95% CI 2.27 to 3.92) than those without sleep problems. Sedentary activity was also significant (AOR=1.27; 95% CI 1.13 to 1.41). Low fruit and vegetable intake (AOR=1.14; 95% CI 1.02 to 1.28), high-fat food intake (AOR=1.22; 95% CI 1.08 to 1.39), moderate sugary drink intake (AOR=1.19; 95% CI 1.08 to 1.31) and high sugary drink intake (AOR=1.23; 95% CI 1.12 to 1.36) were significantly associated with the occurrence chronic conditions.

Children who were underweight (AOR=1.24; 95% CI 1.04 to 1.50), overweight (AOR=1.14; 95% CI 1.03 to 1.26), or obese (AOR=1.43; 95% CI 1.16 to 1.76) had increased odds compared with those with normal BMI. Children whose families had financial hardship had higher odds (AOR=1.16; 95% CI 1.01 to 1.32).

Parental health significantly influences children’s health outcomes. Maternal illness was associated with 1.48 times higher odds (AOR=1.48; 95% CI 1.28 to 1.71), fathers’ illness with 1.33 times (AOR=1.33; 95% CI 1.14 to 1.56). Maternal borderline depression increased child risk (AOR=1.28; 95% CI 1.10 to 1.49), as did fathers’ depression (AOR=1.61; 95% CI 1.23 to 2.12).

Lower income was also a risk factor. Children from households earning $A <500/week (AOR=1.26; 95% CI 1.06 to 1.50) and $A 500–999/week (AOR=1.17; 95% CI 1.01 to 1.35) had higher odds than those from higher-income households. Children of smoking fathers had 1.42 times higher odds (AOR=1.41; 95% CI 1.21 to 1.64). Maternal moderate alcohol use (AOR=1.60; 95% CI 1.28 to 2.00), fathers’ moderate alcohol use (AOR=1.20; 95% CI 1.01 to 1.43), and heavy fathers’ drinking (AOR=1.77; 95% CI 1.39 to 2.26) were all significantly associated to develop chronic conditions.

Children in non-metropolitan regions had higher odds of chronic conditions than those in metropolitan areas (AOR=1.53; 95% CI 1.33 to 1.77). Those in moderately or very remote areas had twice the odds compared with highly accessible regions (AOR=2.01; 95% CI 1.71 to 2.37) (table 4).

Fitness of multilevel logistic models

Median OR (MOR) dropped from 4.25 to 3.03, showing reduced between-cluster variation. Model fit improved consistently. Deviance declined from 26 139.69 (model 0) to 24 301.42 (model 3), and AIC from 26 143.69 to 24 389.42, confirming model 3’s best fit (table 5).

Spatial distribution of chronic conditions

The reason for conducting spatial mapping was the observed increase in the prevalence of chronic conditions from Waves 5 to 8. In wave 5, 42% of children had chronic conditions, concentrated in urban and coastal areas of Queensland, Victoria and Western Australia. In wave 6, 54% of children had chronic conditions and mainly distributed in New South Wales, Victoria and Queensland whereas central and northern regions showed fewer cases distributions. In wave 7, similarly, 54% of children had chronic conditions and mostly distributed in eastern and southeastern states, expanding into Tasmania and South Australia. Central and northern regions remained less affected (figure 3).

Spatial distribution of childhood chronic conditions by using waves 5, 6, 7 and 8.

By wave 8, prevalence increased to 58%, with broader geographic spread in Victoria, New South Wales and Queensland. Southwestern Western Australia showed increased clustering, while central/northern areas, particularly the Northern Territory, continued to report fewer cases.

Generally, higher proportions of children with chronic conditions were concentrated in major metropolitan and coastal regions, particularly along the eastern seaboard. These included Brisbane, the Gold Coast, Sunshine Coast, Sydney, the Central Coast, Newcastle–Maitland, Wollongong, Melbourne, Geelong and the Gippsland coastline. Additional elevated areas were observed around Adelaide, Perth and the surrounding southwest region of Western Australia, the Darwin region, Far North Queensland and parts of Tasmania. In contrast, lower-prevalence areas were predominantly located in inland New South Wales and Queensland, and across remote interior regions of South Australia and Western Australia (figure 3).

Discussion

This study demonstrated a substantial increase in the prevalence of chronic health conditions across childhood, consistent with Australian evidence documenting rising rates of mental, respiratory and developmental conditions.3 26 Prevalence estimates were higher than those reported in comparable US studies,5 likely reflecting differences in study design, population characteristics and broader contextual factors. Overall, the findings indicate that childhood chronic conditions are shaped by interacting child, family and contextual influences that accumulate over time rather than by isolated risk factors.

Interpretation within the Bronfenbrenner bioecological framework

Interpreted through Bronfenbrenner’s bioecological PPCT framework, the findings highlight how chronic health conditions emerge through dynamic interactions across multiple ecological levels. At the person and proximal process levels, child behaviours such as sleep, physical activity, diet and weight status were consistently associated with chronic conditions, reinforcing the importance of early-life routines and health-related behaviours.4611 28 34 These proximal processes operate within the microsystem, where family socioeconomic circumstances and parental health shape children’s exposure to both risks and protective factors.

At the mesosystem and exosystem levels, parental physical and mental health, substance use and financial hardship further influenced children’s health trajectories, consistent with evidence on intergenerational transmission of health risk.122233 37 At the macrosystem level, geographic and structural disadvantage contributed to persistent inequalities, particularly for children living in regional and remote areas.232529 Together, these findings underscore the interconnected nature of ecological systems and the cumulative processes through which chronic conditions develop across childhood.

Comparison with existing literature and contribution of this study

The findings align with a growing body of Australian and international literature demonstrating socioeconomic and geographic disparities in childhood chronic health conditions.121523 Previous studies have largely relied on cross-sectional designs or focused on individual risk factors such as obesity, parental smoking or socioeconomic disadvantage.6921 22

This study extends existing evidence in several important ways. First, by examining temporal trends across childhood, it shows that disparities persist and intensify over time rather than diminishing as children age. Second, the use of longitudinal multilevel modelling combined with spatial mapping provides stronger evidence for contextual influences beyond individual characteristics.2830 Third, situating these findings within a bioecological framework offers a theory-driven interpretation that connects behavioural, familial and structural determinants of child health—an approach that remains underutilised in the current literature.

Implications for children, families, communities and health services

For children and families, the findings highlight the cumulative burden of modifiable behavioural risks alongside socioeconomic and psychosocial adversity. Families experiencing disadvantage may face constraints that limit opportunities for healthy sleep, nutrition and physical activity, while simultaneously managing parental physical or mental health challenges.1720

At the community level, the observed geographic clustering of chronic conditions suggests the need for place-based approaches that address local service availability, infrastructure and environmental exposures.232529 For health services, these findings emphasise the importance of equitable access to preventive and chronic care services, particularly in socioeconomically disadvantaged, regional and remote areas.

Future research should examine mechanistic pathways linking parental mental health, family processes and child health; assess how intersecting disadvantages shape trajectories; and evaluate the effectiveness of targeted interventions to reduce long-term chronic disease risk.

Policy implications

From a policy perspective, the results support integrated, equity-oriented child health strategies that address upstream social and structural determinants rather than focusing solely on individual behaviours. Policies should prioritise investment in disadvantaged and rural communities through strengthened primary healthcare, improved access to preventive services and coordinated support for families experiencing financial and psychosocial adversity.121523 Cross-sectoral policies linking health, education, housing and social services are likely to be essential in reducing persistent inequalities in childhood chronic conditions.

Health service response

Health systems should strengthen early identification and long-term management of chronic conditions through accessible, family-centred primary care models. Integrating mental health support, screening for social risk factors and care coordination into routine paediatric services may help mitigate the compounded effects of socioeconomic and geographic disadvantage.192237 Community-engaged and culturally responsive service delivery models remain particularly important for children living in regional, remote, and underserved communities.

Strengths and limitations

Key strengths of this study include its longitudinal design, nationally representative sample, use of survey weights and integration of multilevel and spatial analyses. Limitations include reliance on reported health conditions, potential misclassification and attrition over time. In addition, the restricted LSAC dataset limited detailed subgroup analyses for some populations, including Indigenous children. Despite these limitations, the study provides robust evidence of persistent and widening inequalities in childhood chronic health conditions in Australia.

Conclusion

Using a bioecological lens, this study demonstrates that childhood chronic health conditions arise from the interaction of individual behaviours, family circumstances and broader socioeconomic and geographic contexts that accumulate across development. Addressing the growing burden of chronic conditions among Australian children will require coordinated, multilevel responses spanning family support, community resources, health services and policy action, with particular attention to children experiencing socioeconomic disadvantage or living in regional and remote areas.
