Ultrasound-guided suction using negative pressure, curettage, and percutaneous drainage to treat rectus sheath hematoma
Authors: Libing Huang, Heng Yi, Weizong Liu, Zhengkun Xiao, Yongheng Yang, Qian Jiang, Zhengyi Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study aimed to introduce and evaluate a new treatment method for rectus sheath hematoma: ultrasound-guided suction using negative pressure, curettage, and percutaneous drainage.MethodsThis retrospective study involved five patients with rectus sheath hematoma rectus sheath hematoma who had cesarean sections. For all patients, ultrasound revealed that the maximum diameter of their hematoma exceeded 70 mm. We summarized and analyzed the therapeutic effects of five patients and introduced the procedure, technical points, precautions, advantages, and indications of the procedure.ResultsIn all five patients, hematoma area was significantly reduced as confirmed by ultrasound at follow-up, and all patients’ abdominal pain were significantly relieved. No complications, including bleeding or infection, were detected at both the 2-h postoperative and 3-day follow-up assessments. The hematoma volume reduction rates were 90 %, 67 %, 81 %, 68 %, and 70 % at three days re-examinations, and 99 %, 96 %, 98 %, 98 %, and 90 % at the 30-day follow-up.ConclusionThe removal of rectus sheath hematoma by suction using negative pressure can prevent patients from undergoing abdominal wall exploration, offering a less invasive alternative while potentially achieving comparable therapeutic effect to surgical hematoma removal in certain cases. This treatment method shows potential for clinical application, particularly in relatively young and healthy postpartum women, and is expected to be popularized and applied in clinical practice.Keywords: Curettage, Negative pressure, Percutaneous drainage, Rectus sheath hematoma, Suction

Introduction

Rectus sheath hematoma (RSH) is an uncommon clinical condition characterized by bleeding into the rectus sheath, typically caused by injury to the epigastric arteries or direct muscular tear [1]. The etiology of RSH can be classified into two categories: traumatic hemorrhage, which includes causes like direct abdominal trauma, iatrogenic injury, and severe acute cough leading to intense contraction of the rectus muscle; and spontaneous hemorrhage, associated with factors like anticoagulation therapy, pregnancy, and diseases that affect blood vessels or coagulation [1]. The overall mortality rate for RSH is approximately 4 %, but it can increase to 25 % in anticoagulated patients. Mortality rates is also higher in pregnant patients, sometimes reaching two to three times of the general population [2].

Current treatment methods for RSH include conservative management, digital subtraction angiography with arterial embolization, vessel ligation, hematoma evacuation, ultrasound-guided (USG) percutaneous drainage, and percutaneous catheter thrombolysis [1,3,4]. The choice of treatment is primarily determined by the patient's condition, particularly their hemodynamic stability and whether active bleeding is present [1]. Even patients with stable vital signs may experience active bleeding, which must be carefully evaluated. (1) For patients with hemodynamic stability, no active bleeding, and a hematoma less than 50 mm in diameter, conservative treatment may be considered. This includes bed rest, pain management, and ice packs for symptomatic relief. However, there is a risk of infection later on due to slow and incomplete hematoma absorption [5]. (2) Digital subtraction angiography with arterial embolization is a safe and effective option for patients with large hematomas and hemodynamic instability or active bleeding [6]. However, it exposes both patients and doctors to radiation and is primarily effective for achieving hemostasis, with limited impact on an existing hematoma. In cases of large hematomas, surgery is often necessary. (3) Surgical intervention, including vessel ligation and hematoma evacuation, is recommended for patients with unclear diagnoses, restricted mobility or bleeding, and hemodynamic instability that does not respond to fluid resuscitation [6]. (4) For stable patients without active bleeding, if a large hematoma is unsuitable for conservative treatment, USG percutaneous drainage or percutaneous drainage thrombolysis is commonly employed to manage RSH [3]. Both methods have the advantages of minimal trauma and relative safety. However, USG percutaneous drainage often yields unsatisfactory outcomes [7], leading to prolonged hospitalization and recovery, and increasing infection risk [8]. If a hematoma fails to resolve or becomes infected, surgical incision and drainage may be necessary. Previous studies have shown that USG percutaneous drainage thrombolysis is effective for hematomas larger than 50 mm, but its efficacy significantly diminishes if the hematoma has been present for over two weeks [3].

To improve the treatment of large, long-standing hematomas, clinicians have adopted the use of USG suction and curettage, combined with negative pressure suction and intraoperative urokinase injection. This study retrospectively summarizes and analyzes the therapeutic effects of five patients treated with USG suction using negative pressure, curettage, and percutaneous drainage. By detailing its indications, procedure, technical aspects, precautions, and benefits, we aim to present an effective new method for managing RSH.

Methods

Subjects

This study retrospectively summarized and analyzed five patients treated with USG suction using negative pressure, curettage, and percutaneous drainage in Shenzhen Second People's Hospital between April 2020 and June 2023. The ethics committee of Shenzhen Second People's Hospital waived the informed consent due to the retrospective observational nature of the study (Approval number 20220425005).

Preoperative Preparation

Routine blood tests were performed on all of the patients. Information such as bleeding time, prothrombin time, and liver/kidney function assessment. Written informed consent was obtained from each patient before treatment, ensuring full awareness of the procedure's potential benefits and risks.

Equipment, surgical team, and procedure

Equipment

Ultrasound examinations were performed using MylabTwice machines (Esaote, Genoa, Italy) equipped with a convex array probe (CA541) operating at a frequency range of 1.0–8.0 MHz for conventional ultrasound, contrast-enhanced ultrasound (CEUS), and USG.

Surgical team

The surgical team consisted of experienced ultrasound physicians, each holding advanced degrees in Medical Imaging and Nuclear Medicine. All surgeons received specialized training in ultrasound-guided procedures, accumulating over 200 h of hands-on experience, as well as completing advanced courses in ultrasound diagnostics and interventions. These qualifications ensure the high technical proficiency required for this procedure.

Procedure

Preoperative ultrasound evaluation

As shown in Fig. 1A and B, two-dimensional ultrasound was used preoperatively to document the hematoma's size and internal echo structure. In addition, color Doppler was employed to perform a preliminary evaluation of the blood supply surrounding the hematoma.

(A) and (B) Preoperative ultrasound images of patient 3, showing the hematoma size: 136 × 120 × 69 mm. (C) and (D) Preoperative contrast-enhanced ultrasound images of patient 3, highlighting significant enhancement around the hematoma.

CEUS for vascularization assessment

CEUS was used to further clarify vascularization before the procedure. As shown in Fig. 1C and D, 2.4 ml of sulfur hexafluoride contrast agent was injected intravenously preoperatively, and the contrast agent's distribution within the lesion was observed to mark small vessels to avoid.

Routine disinfection

Standard disinfection was applied to the surgical area to maintain sterile conditions.

Local anesthesia

Lidocaine was administered locally around the planned puncture site for anesthesia, ensuring the patient's comfort during the procedure.

Establishing the Entry path

Using an intrauterine probe (Shanghai Jinzong, model F10205, length 28 cm), the subcutaneous tissue was gently expanded to create a path for the metal suction tube to reach the hematoma.

Hematoma Aspiration with negative pressure suction

As is shown in Fig. 2A and B, a metal suction tube (Shanghai Jinzong, model F0Q010, size No. 5) was connected to the negative pressure suction device. As shown in Fig. 3A and B, under USG, the negative pressure device was activated, the maximum pressure value was set to 0.05 MPa, and the metal suction tube was used to suction the hematoma at multiple angles in the center of the hematoma.

(A) The negative pressure suction machine used during the procedure. (B) The No.5 metal suction tube connected to the negative pressure machine, inserted into the hematoma for scraping and suction.

(A) The procedure for scraping and suctioning the hematoma in patient 3, using a metal suction device to avoid areas with high enhancement identified on preoperative CEUS. (B) The placement of the postoperative drainage catheter within the hematoma, positioned both upward and downward, with the tail end partially uncurled.

Curettage of residual hematoma

Once the central hematoma was aspirated, any residual hematoma adjacent to blood vessels was excised using a curette.

Placement of drainage tubes

Following curettage, two pigtail drainage tubes (10Fr, 12Fr; model NNU12LPT; Bard Access Systems, Inc.) were positioned on either side of the hematoma, with partially uncurled ends to facilitate irrigation and drainage. A syringe was then used to aspirate fluid. Once no further fluid was aspirated, the tube was opened for 10 min to check for fresh blood.

Urokinase administration

Following observation, urokinase (Livzon Group, 100,000 U dissolved in 50 ml of normal saline) was injected through the drainage tube, which was then clamped for 2–3 h.

Postoperative treatment

Postoperative blood tests were performed 2 h post-procedure to monitor for active bleeding, with routine blood tests and infection markers monitored regularly throughout the postoperative period. Hematoma was irrigated twice daily through the drainage tube. The 10Fr tube served as the inlet pipe for irrigation, while the 12F tube acted as the outlet for drainage. Flushing solutions, including saline or metronidazole, were administered based on bacterial culture results.

Results

Between May 2020 and May 2023, five female patients underwent USG suction using negative pressure, curettage, and percutaneous drainage following cesarean sections. The characteristics of the patients, operative details, and postoperative courses are summarized in Table 1. The ages of the five patients ranged from 29 to 42 years (mean ± SD: 37 ± 5 years), with four being classified as senior mothers (>35 years). Except for patient 3, all the other patients were primiparous. All five patients successfully delivered their babies via lower-segment cesarean section. Varying degrees of anemia were observed, with patient 3 diagnosed with idiopathic thrombocytopenic purpura and patient 5 presenting with thrombocytopenia. Consequently, these two patients underwent preoperative and intraoperative CEUS to further observe the blood supply around the hematoma to ensure the location of possible bleeding. The volume of intraoperative and postoperative blood loss (within 24 h) is detailed in Table 1. Four patients, except for patient 5, received postoperative blood transfusions. Postoperative symptoms included abdominal pain and fever, patients 1 and 4 having previously experienced unsuccessful ultrasound-guided catheter drainage before receiving the current treatment. No complications occurred during or after surgery for any of the patients, and the mean catheterization duration was 7.8 days.

Follow-up ultrasounds conducted three days post-treatment showed a hematoma volume reduction of more than 60 % in all patients. One month later, the hematoma volume had decreased by more than 90 %. Discussion

This study aimed to evaluate a new method for hematoma removal using ultrasound-guided suction with negative pressure, curettage, and percutaneous drainage in treating RSH. RSH, while rare, can pose significant clinical challenges, especially in patients with complicating factors such as anticoagulation or postpartum status. Currently methods, such as continuous negative pressure drainage, are often ineffective for large or long-standing hematomas due to insufficient suction [9]. In the case of patient 1 in this study, the size of the hematoma showed no significant reduction after eleven days of continuous negative pressure drainage. Hematomas change over time, becoming organized and harder, making them difficult to discharge or absorb, a process visible on CT imaging [10]. Traditional treatment methods often result in prolonged recovery and higher infection risks [8], making it essential to explore more effective techniques. This study analyzed five patients, demonstrating promising outcomes for a minimally invasive approach aimed at reducing hematoma volume and minimizing complications.

This technique is indicated for patients who are hemodynamically stable, with no active bleeding, and whose hematomas exceed 70 mm in diameter and are difficult to dissolve. These patients are not suitable for conservative treatment, or previous interventions have failed. This treatment method shows potential for clinical application, particularly in relatively young and healthy postpartum women.

The key technical points of USG suction curettage and percutaneous drainage in RSH are as follows: (1) Intraoperative CEUS allows clinicians to precisely avoid areas with rich blood supply, while also determining the optimal extent of suction and curettage. The CEUS image can be read repeatedly to minimize bleeding risks, and if necessary, it can be used postoperatively to confirm whether significant bleeding has occurred. (2) The negative pressure machine can be adjusted to provide varying levels of suction and curettage depending on the degree of hematoma organization. It is crucial to start with a lower pressure (0.05 MPa) to avoid unnecessary tissue damage, gradually increasing the suction strength as the procedure progresses. (3) After performing suction and curettage, two drainage tubes are placed on either side of the hematoma, and the tubes’ ends are partially uncurled to facilitate effective irrigation and drainage. Aseptic techniques must be strictly followed during irrigation to reduce the risk of infection. (4) After catheterization, urokinase is injected through the drainage tube into the hematoma, maintaining a high concentration of the drug in direct contact with the blood clot. Relevant studies have shown that urokinase acts quickly and is particularly effective for dissolving newly formed thrombus and clots [3].

The innovative aspect of this technique lies in the combination of ultrasound guidance with negative pressure suction and curettage, followed by the use of urokinase for thrombolysis. This multi-step approach allowed for more complete evacuation of the hematoma, while maintaining a minimally invasive profile. The preoperative use of CEUS to assess vascularization was another key aspect, ensuring precise targeting and minimizing the risk of vascular injury. The careful placement of dual drainage tubes allows for continuous irrigation and drainage, with the option to adjust the irrigation solution based on bacterial culture results. This approach not only helps to successfully reduce the hematoma size but also prevents infection. Overall, both treatment and recovery times are effectively shortened, reducing the risk of infection and the need for re-incision. Another key benefit is that the procedure is performed under local anesthesia, sparing patients the trauma and discomfort associated with abdominal wall exploration or general anesthesia. This is particularly advantageous for postpartum patients or those with RSH complications after surgery, as it allows for faster postoperative recovery with minimal impact on diet and mobility. Patient acceptability is notably high due to the reduced discomfort and faster rehabilitation. Additionally, this technique has demonstrated remarkable efficiency, with a significant reduction in hematoma size within three days and an overall 90 % reduction after one month. Overall, this technique enhances patient safety and comfort while ensuring effective and minimally invasive management of RSH.

In addition, we acknowledge several important limitations in this study: (1) This retrospective study was conducted at a single institution, may introduce recall and selection bias. (2) The study has a small sample size and more patients need to be collected in the future to demonstrate the effectiveness of this method further. (3) The lack of a standardized pain scale to assess symptom improvement. Future prospective studies should incorporate validated pain scales to provide a more objective and quantifiable evaluation of symptom relief. Despite these limitations, the five clinical patients presented here may provide a new therapeutic pathway for clinicians in the treatment of RSH. This method may also be applied to the treatment of soft tissue hematomas of the body and limbs, and as such has broad clinical application prospects.

Conclusion

The removal of RSH by suction curettage using negative pressure can save patients from undergoing abdominal wall exploration offering a less invasive alternative while potentially achieving comparable therapeutic effect to surgical hematoma removal in certain cases. This treatment method shows potential for clinical application, particularly in relatively young and healthy postpartum women, and is expected to be popularized and applied in clinical practice.
