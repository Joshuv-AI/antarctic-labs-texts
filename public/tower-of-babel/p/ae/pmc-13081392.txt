Calibrating the RT-cGAS-STING axis to drive cold-to-hot tumor transformation: mechanistic foundations and translational strategies for combination with immunotherapy
Authors: Yi-Fan Kang, Jin-Ming Chen, Bai-Cheng Xu, Jun-Nv Xu, Qi Qin, Hai-Lan Wu, Ju Wang, Rui-Kang Wang, You-Rong Wu, Wen-Jun Tang, Yue-Can Zeng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRadiotherapy (RT) is a cornerstone of cancer therapy that exerts cytotoxic effects while also modulating anti-tumor immunity. RT-induced DNA damage can, under specific biological conditions, activate the cytosolic DNA sensor cyclic GMP-AMP synthase (cGAS) and its downstream stimulator of interferon genes (STING) pathway, eliciting type I interferon (IFN-I) responses and dendritic cell-mediated T-cell priming. This signaling is tightly regulated by TREX1-mediated DNA degradation, apoptotic caspases, and chromatin-bound nuclear DNA, resulting in heterogeneous immune outcomes. Notably, tumors with mismatch repair deficiency, such as MLH1 loss, exhibit pre-existing cGAS-STING activity, in which RT primarily amplifies innate sensing. However, even in this context, excessive or sustained STING activation may paradoxically induce immune tolerance, and the clinical application of STING agonists remains constrained by dose-limiting toxicities and delivery challenges. Here, we review the context-dependent interplay between RT and the cGAS-STING axis, highlighting mechanistic regulation, tumor genetics, and the tumor microenvironment. We propose strategies for precision calibration of RT and STING agonist delivery to enhance anti-tumor immunity, enable cold-to-hot tumor transformation, and guide rational combination with immunotherapy.Keywords: Radiotherapy, cGAS-STING pathway, Immune checkpoint inhibitors, Cold-to-hot tumor transformation, Precision calibration, Immunotherapy resistance

Introduction

Immunotherapy, exemplified by immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), has achieved remarkable success across various malignancies. However, durable clinical benefits are largely limited to a subset of “immunologically hot” tumors, whereas many “cold” tumors remain resistant to treatment. Solid tumors such as hepatocellular carcinoma, pancreatic cancer, and specific subtypes of non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) are characterized by a profoundly immunosuppressive tumor immune microenvironment (TIME). This environment presents a dual hurdle: the paucity of infiltrating effector immune cells and the enrichment of immunosuppressive populations, compounded by physical barriers formed by aberrant vasculature. Together, these factors arrest the cancer-immunity cycle at both the initiation and effector phases [1, 2]. Consequently, overcoming these physical and metabolic barriers to effectively ignite specific immune responses against cold tumors represents a critical bottleneck in current immuno-oncology. Concurrently, the “abscopal effect” induced by RT suggests that local irradiation can release tumor antigens and danger signals sufficient to prime a systemic anti-tumor immune response [3, 4]. However, clinical data indicates that spontaneous abscopal effects are rare, implying that RT-induced immune signals are often insufficient in intensity or are dampened by complex negative regulatory networks, thereby failing to translate into observable systemic clinical benefits [3, 4]. Therefore, unlocking the full potential of RT-mediated immune remodeling to enable the transformation of cold tumors into hot tumors has become a pivotal imperative.

The cyclic GMP-AMP synthase-stimulator of interferon genes (cGAS-STING) pathway, the central axis of cytosolic DNA sensing, is recognized as the molecular hub connecting RT-induced DNA damage to systemic immune memory [5, 6]. Recent studies elucidate that RT drives cGAS-STING pathway activation by modulating the generation and clearance of cytosolic double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) [5–7]. Upon activation, STING upregulates type I interferons (IFN-I) and chemokines (e.g., CXCL9/CXCL10), promoting dendritic cell maturation and antigen cross-presentation [8]. This cascade enhances the infiltration of CD8⁺ cytotoxic T cells and natural killer (NK) cells while partially mitigating the suppressive functions of Tregs [9] and MDSCs [10]. Ultimately, this remodels the immunosuppressive TIME into an activated state, improving local control by RT and providing a responsive immune foundation for therapies such as ICIs [7–10].

Crucially, the interaction between RT and the cGAS-STING axis represents a double-edged regulatory system rather than a unidirectional immune amplifier. Insufficient activation fails to sustain durable anti-tumor immunity, whereas excessive activation or spatiotemporal dysregulated activation may paradoxically induce PD-L1 upregulation, myeloid-mediated immunosuppression, radioresistance, and normal tissue toxicity, including inflammation and fibrosis [11, 12]. The expanding understanding of STING checkpoints and downstream negative feedback loops indicates that clinical outcomes depend on the precise calibration of pathway magnitude and timing. Translationally, novel modalities such as STING agonists (e.g., the small-molecule dimer diABZI) and advanced delivery systems (e.g., manganese-based strategies, alginate-manganese hydrogels, and diverse nanocarriers) provide new opportunities for precise pathway modulation. Despite their preclinical promise, clinical application faces severe challenges: dose-limiting toxicities associated with systemic administration; insufficient intratumoral targeting efficiency and stability; and the absence of standardized protocols for sequencing RT and drug administration, resulting in heterogeneous and inconsistent synergistic outcomes [5–7, 13, 14]. Overcoming these pharmacological and biological barriers to achieve precise in situ activation remains a central challenge in the field.

This review systematically examines the synergistic anti-tumor immune effects of RT and the cGAS-STING axis. We delineate the molecular mechanisms by which RT-induced DNA damage activates innate immune sensing and reshapes the TIME. We further critically evaluate the development and bottlenecks of STING agonists and targeted delivery strategies. Finally, we integrate current evidence to propose rational frameworks for optimizing radio-immunotherapy. We argue that precise spatiotemporal synchronization is essential to circumvent deleterious feedback, achieved by aligning TREX1-evading fractionation regimens with targeted agonists and sequential immune checkpoint blockade [15, 16]. Such calibrated approach aims to convert the pathway’s inherent duality into a predictable and directional driver of cold-to-hot tumor transformation [13].

Microenvironmental barriers to RT-induced anti-tumor immunity and the cGAS-STING pathway as a mechanistic bridge

Immunomodulatory effects of radiotherapy and the clinical rarity of the abscopal effect

With advances in tumor immunology, RT is now recognized as a potent modulator of the tumor immune phenotype. First, RT expands the antigenic peptide repertoire and upregulates MHC-I expression, thereby enhancing the tumor cells recognized by effector T cells [17]. Second, RT induces the release of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) during immunogenic cell death (e.g., HMGB1 and ATP), as well as nucleic acid signals, thereby promoting dendritic cell (DC) maturation and cross-presentation [18, 19]. Finally, RT bridges innate and adaptive immune responses via the type I interferon axis [20, 21], potentially converting local irradiation into systemic immune activation-a concept referred to as “in situ vaccination” under specific conditions [22].

One of the most compelling immunological phenomena associated with RT is the abscopal effect, defined as the regression of tumor lesions outside the irradiated field. However, clinical practice and systematic reviews indicate that its incidence is extremely low, making it difficult to establish the abscopal effect as a stable and reproducible therapeutic strategy [3]. Recent studies indicate that although case reports and small-sample studies support the existence of the abscopal effect and its association it remains rare and highly heterogeneous across the broader patient population [3, 23].

The scarcity of the abscopal effect is multifactorial. First, the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) is often characterized by significant immunosuppression, specifically manifested by the enrichment of Tregs and MDSCs, as well as the activation of various immune checkpoints and exclusion pathways, which hinder effector T cell infiltration or limit their function within the tumor. Consequently, although RT releases antigens and danger signals, these signals may be counteracted by these complex inhibitory networks [24, 25]. Furthermore, dose and fractionation regimens are critical determinants of immune outcomes. High-dose single-fraction irradiation can induce the expression of the DNA exonuclease TREX1 in tumor cells, which degrades cytosolic DNA and inhibits cGAS-STING activation [15]. In contrast, moderate fractionation (e.g., 8 Gy × 3) has demonstrated superior immune-sensitizing potential in multiple preclinical studies, suggesting that RT parameter settings are key variables determining immunological responses [15, 26].

Regarding combination therapy, the integration of RT with immune checkpoint inhibitors has been shown to enhance systemic immune responses or objective response rates (ORR) in some studies [27, 28], suggesting it aids in amplifying the clinical visibility of the abscopal effect. However, overall efficacy remains constrained by multiple factors, including patient stratification, RT protocols, tumor type, and immune-related toxicities, and has not yet reached a stable and universally applicable therapeutic standard [23, 27, 28].

Characteristics of immunologically cold tumors and their resistance to immunotherapy

Immunologically cold tumors typically exhibit spatial immune patterns characterized by a scarcity or exclusion of T cells and are commonly categorized into “immune-desert” and “immune-excluded” phenotypes. A shared hallmark of these phenotypes is the absence of effective in situ interactions between effector T cells and dendritic cells, resulting in impaired immune priming and effector response [29, 30].

Mechanistically, the immunologically cold phenotype is driven by overlapping inhibitory axes. First, tumor cells block in situ immune priming via intrinsic signaling pathways; for instance, the activation of WNT/β-catenin signaling inhibits DC recruitment, thereby restricting CD8⁺ T cell infiltration into the tumor parenchyma [31]. Second, the stromal-fibroblast-TGF-β axis creates a physical and signaling barrier that sequesters peripheral T cells at the tumor margin-a classic resistance mechanism to anti-PD-1/PD-L1 therapy [32]. Furthermore, VEGF-mediated vascular abnormalities not only impair tumor perfusion and permeation but also directly inhibit the activity of DCs and effector T cells, further reinforcing the immunosuppressive environment [33]. These pathways act synergistically to establish an immune-excluded or immune-desert microenvironment, where effective immune synapses and the subsequent amplification of immune responses are difficult to establish even in the presence of tumor antigens and damage signals.

Clinically, cold tumors lacking pre-existing T cells and IFN-γ signaling demonstrate low overall response rates to ICIs. While predictive transcriptional signatures (e.g., IFN-γ-related gene sets) and tumor mutation burden (TMB) correlate positively with response, they are not sufficient conditions for efficacy [34, 35]. Moreover, defects in antigen presentation (e.g., MHC-I loss via B2M mutations, HLA loss of heterozygosity) and JAK1/2 functional deficiencies (rendering cells insensitive to IFN-γ) can lead to primary or secondary resistance [36, 37]. Concurrently, the enrichment of Tregs and MDSCs exacerbates immunosuppression by secreting inhibitory factors like IL-10 and TGF-β, depleting metabolic substrates, and suppressing DC cross-presentation and cytotoxic T lymphocyte (CTL) cytotoxicity [9, 38].

Therefore, for cold tumors, merely releasing immunosuppression with ICIs is insufficient to restart the immune response, as the microenvironment lacks the substrate of activatable effector cells [39, 40]. To breach this immune silence, therapeutic paradigms should pivot from passive checkpoint disinhibition to the active induction of innate immunity. Consequently, coupling radiotherapy-induced immunogenic cell death (ICD) with the activation of the cGAS-STING cytosolic sensing axis emerges as a critical mechanistic imperative for driving “cold-to-hot” tumor transformation.

The pivotal role of the cGAS-STING pathway as a bridge between DNA damage and systemic immunity

Core mechanisms

The cGAS-STING pathway serves as a critical conduit for cytosolic DNA sensing and signal transduction [41–44]. cGAS functions as a sensor for cytosolic double-stranded DNA (dsDNA), catalyzing the synthesis of the second messenger 2′3′-cGAMP. This messenger binds to and activates STING, which is anchored in the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) membrane, subsequently inducing the expression of type I interferons (IFN-I) and various inflammatory cytokines. Upon activation, STING translocates from the ER to the Golgi apparatus and post-Golgi compartments, where it undergoes palmitoylation at critical sites to assemble a signaling complex. This complex recruits and activates TBK1, phosphorylates IRF3, and simultaneously initiates downstream pathways such as NF-κB and autophagy, thereby promoting the transcription of IFN-I and chemokine genes [45–47]. Structurally, cGAS interacts with dsDNA through a positively charged DNA-binding surface (including zinc-finger domains), triggering a conformational rearrangement that facilitates the efficient catalytic synthesis of 2′3′-cGAMP and subsequent STING activation [48].

DNA damage and activation in the context of radiotherapy

Genomic instability in tumor cells and DNA damage induced by radiotherapy lead to the formation of micronuclei during late mitosis. Following the rupture of the micronuclear envelope, exposed chromatin becomes accessible to the cytosolic sensor cGAS. However, subsequent immune activation is context-dependent. Evidence suggests that nucleosomal binding can actively sequester cGAS in an inactive conformation, functioning as a negative regulator; thus, the rupture of micronuclei does not inevitably precipitate functional pathway activation [49–51]. Under RT conditions, the accumulation of cytosolic dsDNA is further subject to threshold regulation by the exonuclease TREX1: high-dose single-fraction irradiation can significantly upregulate TREX1 expression, accelerating cytosolic DNA degradation and attenuating cGAS-STING pathway activation and IFN-I signaling [15].

In various tumor models, the RT-induced IFN-I-Dendritic Cell (DC)-CD8⁺ T cell axis plays a crucial role in anti-tumor immunity. The deletion of host STING or IRF3 significantly impairs RT-induced CD8⁺ T cell activation and effector function, highlighting the core role of the cGAS-STING pathway in bridging innate and adaptive immunity [21]. Strategically, transforming cold tumors to hot requires simultaneously enhancing antigenicity and improving infiltration. For instance, optimizing RT fractionation to achieve superior in situ vaccination effects [22], combined with the TIME remodeling-such as TGF-β blockade to dismantle stromal barriers [32], anti-VEGF therapy to normalize vasculature, and PD-L1 inhibition-has demonstrated enhanced immune sensitization in preclinical and clinical studies across multiple cancer types [52].

Bridging local damage to systemic immunity

cGAMP not only mediates intracellular signaling but also functions as an intracellular immunotransmitter. Under conditions of RT or other stress-induced damage, 2′3′-cGAMP released by tumor cells can enter neighboring cells (e.g., DCs and T cells) via SLC19A1 (folate transporter) or LRRC8/VRAC channels. This intercellular transfer amplifies STING-IFN-I signaling in a paracrine manner, thereby enhancing antigen cross-presentation and promoting T cell activation [53–55]. Downstream, this pathway induces not only IFN-β but also the expression of chemokines such as CXCL9 and CXCL10, driving the recruitment of Th1 cells and cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs), and promoting DC maturation and CD8⁺ T cell infiltration [56]. While this signaling mechanistically underpins the RT-induced abscopal effect, its clinical realization is frequently stymied by host heterogeneity and compensatory immunosuppressive networks.

Therapeutic implications and the double-edged sword effect

Based on these mechanisms, STING agonists have garnered widespread attention as immune sensitizers for RT and immunotherapy. Non-nucleotide small-molecule agonists (e.g., the amidobenzimidazole derivative diABZI [57] and the orally available MSA-2 [58]) have effectively induced IFN-I signaling in various tumor models, demonstrating significant synergistic effects when combined with PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors or RT [13, 58]. Clinically, while first-generation cyclic dinucleotide agonists (e.g., MIW815/ADU-S100) are capable of activating immune responses, their single-agent anti-tumor efficacy has been limited, suggesting a need to optimize pharmacokinetics, targeted delivery strategies, and combination regimens [59]. Furthermore, the failure of the early agonist DMXAA in clinical trials-despite promising results in mouse models-due to its lack of activity against human STING, provides critical lessons regarding cross-species pharmacological differences [60].

Crucially, the cGAS-STING pathway exhibits a pronounced double-edged immunologic effect. Excessive activation can lead to an overzealous IFN response and autoinflammatory diseases, such as STING-associated vasculopathy with onset in infancy (SAVI) caused by TMEM173 gain-of-function mutations, and pathological IFN production mediated by cGAS in TREX1-deficient models [61, 62]. Recent structural evidence reveals that in normal tissues, cGAS is primarily localized to the nucleus, where its enzymatic activity is suppressed by high-affinity binding to chromatin and nucleosomes to prevent the misrecognition of self-DNA, a mechanism essential for maintaining immune homeostasis [51, 63].

Consequently, therapeutic paradigms need to pivot from indiscriminate stimulation to transient, rhythmic activation within a strictly defined spatiotemporal window [64]. By synchronizing this pulsatile signaling with immune checkpoint blockade (PD-1/PD-L1) or inhibitors of negative regulators (e.g., TREX1/ENPP1), clinical strategies can expand the effective immune window to intercept adaptive resistance while minimizing systemic toxicity [16, 65]. Ultimately, the radiotherapy regimen (dose, fractionation, and timing) and STING axis modulation (agonist class, delivery, and sequencing) constitute the two calibration scales for realizing “cold-to-hot”tumor transformation, necessitating rigorous alignment with tumor-specific biomarkers (Fig. 1) [15, 64, 66].

Molecular mechanisms underlying RT-induced cGAS-STING activation and its double-edged immunological effects

Molecular mechanisms: from RT-induced DNA damage to systemic immunity

While early models posited that micronuclei formed from nuclear DNA (nDNA) breaks were the primary triggers [49, 50], the precise DNA source responsible for functional cGAS activation remains a subject of ongoing debate. The hierarchy of DNA sources regulating functional cGAS-STING activation has proven to be far more complex than a simple dose-response relationship.

Micronuclei rupture indeed exposes genomic material to the cytosol; however, this exposure does not inevitably lead to pathway activation. Structural analyses reveal that cGAS binds to nucleosomes with even higher affinity than to naked DNA, locking cGAS in an inactive conformation that inhibits oligomerization [51]. This mechanism explains the recently observed discordance where cGAS localization to ruptured micronuclei fails to trigger downstream signaling [67]. Furthermore, under RT conditions, the accumulation of cytosolic nDNA is further restricted by the exonuclease TREX1: high-dose radiation can significantly upregulate TREX1 expression, accelerating cytosolic DNA degradation [15].

Beyond nDNA, radiation-induced leakage of mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) constitutes another potential trigger for cGAS activation. Under radiation stress, oligomerization of voltage-dependent anion channels (VDAC) compromises mitochondrial membrane integrity, facilitating mtDNA efflux into the cytosol [68, 69]. Unlike nDNA, mtDNA lacks inhibitory histone packaging and is rich in unmethylated CpG motifs, features that theoretically facilitate cGAS recognition [70, 71].

Crucially, however, the immunogenic potential of mtDNA is governed by tight dichotomy. While mitochondrial permeabilization facilitates mtDNA release, it frequently coincides with cytochrome c efflux, triggering the apoptotic caspase cascade. As established by the seminal works of Rongvaux et al. and White et al., activated apoptotic caspases, particularly Caspase-3 and Caspase-7, proteolytically cleave cGAS, STING, and IRF3, thereby actively suppressing IFN-I production to promote immunologically “silent” cell death [72–74]. Furthermore, Han et al. demonstrated that tumor cells can hijack Caspase-9 signaling post-irradiation to uncouple DNA damage from immune activation [75].

Consequently, a scientific controversy remains regarding the dominant DNA source. Rather than a single dominant trigger, pathway activation is governed by a dynamic, spatiotemporal competition: the accessibility of nDNA and mtDNA versus the counter-regulatory forces of nucleosomal tethering, TREX1 degradation, and caspase-mediated silencing.

When this kinetic balance favors immune activation, cGAS catalyzes the synthesis of the second messenger 2′3′-cGAMP. This molecule functions not merely as an intracellular transducer but as a soluble “immunotransmitter”. It is transferred to neighboring non-irradiated cells via gap junctions [76] or membrane transporters such as SLC19A1 and LRRC8/VRAC [53–55]. This paracrine mechanism, termed the “bystander effect” functions as the immune alert throughout the tumor microenvironment. In recipient cells, cGAMP binding induces STING conformational changes and its translocation from the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) to the Golgi apparatus. This trafficking event is a prerequisite for STING palmitoylation, the recruitment of TBK1/IRF3, and the subsequent transcriptional induction of type I interferons (IFN-I) and chemokines (e.g., CXCL9/10) [45, 77, 78]. At the effector phase, sustained or excessive STING signaling may instead promote adaptive immune resistance, characterized by IFN–JAK–STAT–mediated PD-L1 upregulation [16] and T-cell metabolic exhaustion [79].

Synergistic logic: RT-triggered cGAS-STING activation feeds back to the TIME to drive cold-to-hot transformation

The synergy between radiotherapy and innate sensing unfolds not as an isolated event but as a continuous cascade of “innate priming -adaptive expansion - relief of immunosuppression -amelioration of physical/metabolic barriers”. Rather than merely inducing cytotoxicity, the activated cGAS-STING axis remodels the tumor immune microenvironment by initiating a positive feedback loop. First, RT-induced IFN-I and various chemokines promote dendritic cell (DC) maturation and antigen cross-presentation, completing the local in situ immune priming [8, 21]. Second, STING activation further upregulates key chemokines such as CXCL9 and CXCL10, which synergize with RT-induced tumor antigen release and presentation to drive the recruitment and effector expansion of CD8⁺ T cells and natural killer (NK) cells, significantly increasing effector cell density and expanding their cytotoxic radius [21, 80]. However, the subsequent deep infiltration and actual anti-tumor efficacy of these recruited T cells are governed by highly complex spatial and cell-specific regulations. At the cellular level, the immunological consequences of cGAS-STING activation are profoundly cell-type specific, necessitating a compartmentalized perspective of the TIME. While STING activation in dendritic cells (DCs) is indispensable for robust IFN-I production and efficient cross-priming, the direct or sustained activation of STING signaling within T cells themselves paradoxically induces endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress and apoptosis, ultimately precipitating T cell exhaustion and dysfunction [79, 81]. At the spatial and microenvironmental level, immune infiltration following RT is further constrained by the neutrophil compartment, which can act as a physical and functional barrier to effective T-cell penetration. Recent immunological evidence reveals that ionizing radiation triggers neutrophils to undergo NETosis, extruding neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) that construct a physical barrier impeding the deep intratumoral infiltration of newly recruited circulating CD8 + T cells [82]. This barrier effect mechanistically elucidates a critical clinical observation: pharmacological blockade of new T-cell infiltration (e.g., using the S1P receptor modulator FTY-720) frequently does not abrogate the therapeutic efficacy of local RT [83]. This underscores that the primary drivers of RT-induced tumor clearance are often not the newly circulating T cells trapped in the periphery, but rather the pre-existing, tissue-resident CD8 + T cells, which exhibit remarkable intrinsic radioresistance and are reinvigorated by DC-derived in situ signals [83]. This compartmentalized reality dictates that blindly escalating systemic STING activation may cause collateral T-cell exhaustion without overcoming the local NETosis barrier.

Concurrently, cGAS-STING activation actively dismantles the immunosuppressive networks orchestrated by Tregs and MDSCs through intricate phenotypic and metabolic reprogramming. Mechanistically, STING-driven type I IFN signaling profoundly destabilizes the regulatory phenotype of Tregs by downregulating Foxp3 expression and inducing “Treg fragility” thereby abrogating their suppressive capacity. For MDSCs, moderate STING activation not only curtails their pathological recruitment but also drives their terminal differentiation into mature, non-suppressive myeloid cells. This transition concomitantly represses their expression of key metabolic suppressors, such as arginase-1 (ARG1) and inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS), directly neutralizing their metabolic suppression of effector T cells. Furthermore, this pathway interferes with metabolic inhibitory pathways such as IDO, thereby alleviating immune exclusion and desertification and rendering the TIME more permeable to effector immune cell infiltration and remodeling [9, 38, 84].

At the vascular microenvironmental level, the cGAS-STING axis acts as a pivotal regulator of endothelial reprogramming. Beyond merely ameliorating hypoxia, STING activation within tumor endothelial cells directly induces the production of IFN-β, which subsequently upregulates critical endothelial adhesion molecules, including vascular cell adhesion molecule 1 (VCAM-1) and intercellular adhesion molecule 1 (ICAM-1). This phenotypic shift transforms the endothelium from a physical barrier into an active “conduit” that selectively facilitates the tethering, rolling, and diapedesis of CD8 + T cells [85]. Additionally, STING signaling promotes pericyte recruitment and the structural maturation of capillaries while counteracting pathologic pro-angiogenic signaling, effectively sealing leaky vessels and lowering interstitial fluid pressure [86]. This process aligns closely with the “vascular normalization window” theory originally proposed for anti-VEGF therapy, further highlighting the mechanistic complementarity between RT and immune microenvironment remodeling [33, 87].

Overall, RT-induced DNA damage, cGAS-STING pathway activation, and the TIME remodeling have the potential to constitute a self-reinforcing positive feedback loop of “RT induction -STING amplification -TIME reprogramming”. This closed loop is proposed as a key biological basis for achieving clinically reproducible immune sensitization effects.

Immunological effects of the cGAS-STING pathway: favorable versus unfavorable outcomes

Moderate and transient activation typically generates favorable anti-tumor immunity. In this optimal window, the STING-TBK1-IRF3 axis robustly upregulates IFN-I and CXCL9/10, promoting DC maturation and the cross-priming of CD8 + T cells to elevate response rates to immune checkpoint inhibitors [8, 21].

However, when the cGAS-STING pathway is subjected to sustained or excessive activation, the immune effect may shift from sensitization to suppression, manifesting a series of deleterious consequences including adaptive immunosuppression, immune escape, T cell dysfunction, and tissue injury. Continuous IFN-JAK/STAT-IRF1 signaling can induce tumor cells to upregulate PD-L1 expression, forming a negative feedback loop of immunosuppression that is closely linked to ICI resistance [16]. Beyond adaptive PD-L1 upregulation, the temporal dynamics of STING signaling dictate T-cell fate. While transient activation licenses innate immunity, chronic STING engagement directly within T cells precipitates profound ER stress and terminal metabolic exhaustion [79, 84]. Concurrently, this sustained inflammatory pressure actively skews the myeloid compartment, driving CCL2-dependent MDSC recruitment and reinforcing Treg-mediated metabolic suppression (e.g., via the IDO pathway) [9, 84, 88]. Ultimately, these overlapping negative feedback loops subvert the initial immune sensitization, driving the tumor into a state of adaptive immune escape.

These changes collectively drive the tumor toward an adaptive immune escape state. Therefore, the intensity and duration of STING activation should be carefully calibrated to balance immune activation and immune tolerance, preventing the transition of T cells from an effector state to an exhausted state. Systemically and at the tissue level, excessive STING activation may also trigger systemic inflammation and normal tissue damage, associated with adverse events such as radiation pneumonitis, fibrosis [88]. These phenomena suggest that chronic activation of the cGAS-STING pathway not only weakens anti-tumor immunity but may also exacerbate RT-related toxicity, compressing the acceptable therapeutic window.

Notably, some tumors acquire immune evasion advantages by upregulating the exonuclease TREX1 to degrade DNA, or by hijacking apoptotic pathways (e.g., Caspase-9) to proteolytically cleave cGAS/STING, thereby aborting the signal at its inception [15, 75, 80].

Consequently, pharmacological interventions targeting these negative regulatory nodes (e.g., ENPP1 inhibitors or TREX1 blockers) represent a pivotal research direction for restoring the immunotransmitter function of cGAMP and enhancing RT–immune synergy (Fig. 2).

Strategic modulation: maximizing synergy while mitigating adverse effects

Given the established functional duality of the cGAS-STING pathway capable of driving both immune sensitization and paradoxical immunosuppression-the central translational challenge lies in the precise calibration of physical and pharmacological parameters to maintain pathway activation within a favorable therapeutic window.

In the context of radiotherapy, the dose and fractionation regimen function as the primary determinants of activation intensity. As elucidated by Vanpouille-Box et al. RT-induced immunogenicity is tightly gated by a dose-dependent threshold of the DNA exonuclease TREX1 [15]. Consequently, clinical protocols should avoid single ultra-high ablative doses (e.g., > 12–18 Gy in murine models), as these induce TREX1 upregulation and rapid cytosolic DNA degradation, thereby abrogating the IFN-I signaling cascade [15]. In contrast, moderate hypofractionated regimens (e.g., 6–8 Gy × 3) are preferred to bypass this threshold and sustain cytosolic DNA accumulation, effectively inducing IFN-I signaling while preserving the immunogenic integrity of the tumor microenvironment [15, 64, 65].

Equally critical is the temporal optimization of combination therapies, which should follow a logic of “RT induction-STING amplification-ICI consolidation”. Specifically, STING agonists should be administered within the optimal priming window post-RT to amplify innate signals, followed sequentially by ICIs to consolidate the systemic adaptive response [13]. This staged sequencing not only reinforces “in situ vaccination” but also mitigates the functional T-cell impairment and memory loss associated with the excessive, concurrent activation of intrinsic STING signaling [79, 84].

Finally, spatial precision is paramount to resolving the toxicity bottleneck. Agonist delivery should be confined to the tumor locality using microenvironment-responsive vehicles (e.g., pH-sensitive or ligand-targeted nanocarriers) to maximize intratumoral exposure while minimizing systemic inflammation [89]. Furthermore, emerging strategies targeting intracellular trafficking nodes-specifically ACBD3-mediated Golgi recruitment and COPII-dependent ER export-offer a mechanistic dimension to finely tune signal duration, aiming to maximize RT synergy while preventing exhaustion-associated pathway overactivation [77, 78].

Current status and challenge in radiotherapy and the cGAS-STING pathway

Research progress and core challenges

The translational gap: synergy and barriers

Despite extensive preclinical validation demonstrating that radiotherapy (RT) synergizes with STING activation to enhance type I interferon (IFN-I) responses and effector T cell infiltration, recapitulating these effects in the broader patient population remains a formidable challenge [3, 23, 66]. While murine models often exhibit abscopal effects and prolonged survival, clinical reproducibility is currently constrained by a complex interplay of immune toxicity and resistance. The therapeutic application of this axis is constrained by a narrow homeostatic window: potent or sustained activation can engage negative feedback mechanisms-most notably PD-L1 upregulation through the IFN-JAK/STAT signaling cascade-and precipitate systemic toxicities, including radiation pneumonitis and fibrosis [16, 64, 88]. Consequently, achieving a therapeutic window that balances anti-tumor immunity with tissue homeostasis represents the core obstacle in current trial designs.

Pharmacological and biological limitations

Further complicating translation are the limitations of current delivery systems and intrinsic resistance mechanisms. Systemic administration of first-generation cyclic dinucleotides (CDNs) is frequently dose-limited by cytokine release syndrome (CRS), while their intratumoral application often suffers from rapid clearance [59, 90]. Biologically, tumors evade surveillance through multiple layers, ranging from the rapid apoptotic clearance of immune signals via caspases to the epigenetic silencing of cGAS/STING and the upregulation of DNA repair machinery. Although DNA methyltransferase (DNMT) inhibitors show promise in restoring pathway competency, single biomarkers like TMB or IFN signatures remain insufficient for patient selection [91, 92]. Future precision medicine efforts should therefore integrate multi-dimensional profiles-encompassing pathway expression, the TIME composition, and vascular status-to stratify patients for personalized regimens [34, 35].

Clinical signals across tumor types

Clinical exploration has now expanded across melanoma, breast cancer, and head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC). While STING agonist monotherapy has shown limited efficacy, combination strategies are yielding encouraging signals.

Notably, preliminary data from the dose-escalation phase of a Phase I study in HNSCC reported a partial response in 4 of 8 evaluable patients treated with the intratumoral agonist MK-1454 combined with pembrolizumab [93]. However, these efficacy signals await validation in larger randomized cohorts. Similarly, early data from the exoSTING program (exosome-delivered CDN) suggest localized immune activation [64]. However, high-level evidence from large-scale randomized trials remains absent, and the optimal integration of RT parameters with these agents in refractory settings like pancreatic cancer awaits clarification.

Clinical translation: optimizing combination strategies

Synergy with immune checkpoint inhibitors

The integration of RT with ICIs is fundamentally based on radiation’s ability to convert immunologically “cold” tumors into inflamed, antigen-presenting microenvironments. Preclinical studies have indicated that moderate hypofractionated radiotherapy-rather than single ablative doses-sustains cGAS-STING-dependent type I interferon signaling, which is essential for systemic immune priming and abscopal sensitization [65]. This biological rationale has been partially supported by clinical evidence from trials such as PEMBRO-RT, where sequential radiotherapy combined with pembrolizumab significantly improved ORR in advanced NSCLC [27]. To mitigate adaptive resistance (e.g., IFN-induced PD-L1 upregulation), clinical protocols should prioritize a sequential strategy in which moderate hypofractionated RT precedes ICIs [16, 34, 94].

Synergy with STING agonists: from systemic limitations to precision delivery

To circumvent the TIME barriers-most notably TREX1-mediated DNA degradation and ENPP1-mediated cGAMP hydrolysis-the pharmacological landscape has evolved from unstable intratumoral cyclic dinucleotides (CDNs) to second-generation systemic small molecules (e.g., diABZI, MSA-2) and precision delivery systems (Table 1).

Pharmacological characteristics, clinical status, and rationale for combination strategies of representative STING agonists

The key translational bottleneck lies in the intrinsic interdependence of the cancer-immunity cycle: radiotherapy typically acts as the essential primer for antigen exposure(or an amplifier in genetically primed dMMR contexts); STING agonists serve as the signal boosters; and Immune Checkpoint Inhibitors (ICIs) function as the consolidator to intercept adaptive tolerance. Consequently, maintaining the integrity of this therapeutic cycle requires the organic, spatiotemporally coordinated integration of these three components into a “tripartite” paradigm, rather than the isolated optimization of single agents [13, 29].

Nanocarriers/exosomes and composite delivery

Through tumor-targeted chemical/biological modifications and responsiveness to the tumor microenvironment (pH/ROS/enzymes), these systems achieve enrichment and controlled release. Compared with agonists, animal models have demonstrated higher intratumoral cGAS-STING activation and reduced systemic inflammatory responses with these approaches [64, 89].

Hydrogels and metal-coordinated systems

Injectable hydrogels facilitate the sustained local release of STING agonists, while Manganese (Mn²⁺) cofactors can amplify DNA sensing. Advancing this concept, recent innovations have introduced X-ray-triggered bimetallic nanoassemblies. These smart systems not only function as radiosensitizers to enhance physical DNA damage but also unleash STING agonists specifically upon irradiation, achieving a precise “on-demand” CDT/radio-immunotherapy strategy that minimizes off-target toxicity [97].

Antibody-Drug Conjugates (ADCs/ISACs)

These involve conjugating non-nucleotide STING agonists to tumor antigen antibodies (e.g., the HER2-directed XMT-2056). In murine models, they enable efficient intratumoral release following systemic administration and exhibit enhanced anti-tumor efficacy compared with unconjugated agonists, while reducing systemic inflammation when combined with HER2 drugs or ADCs (Antibody-Drug Conjugates), currently undergoing Phase I clinical exploration [95].

Radiotherapy-activatable prodrugs

Beyond physical delivery vehicles, a paradigm shift towards “chemical precision” has emerged through the development of radiotherapy-activatable prodrugs. These chemically “caged” molecules remain pharmacologically inert systemically but are structurally cleaved into active agonists specifically by RT-induced reactive oxygen species within the irradiated tumor field. Highlighting this technological leap, a recent breakthrough by Ding et al. developed single-atom engineered caged immune agonists that achieve highly spatiotemporally restricted activation [96]. Crucially, this platform demonstrates how chemical engineering can seamlessly synergize with the RT-cGAS-STING axis: while RT-induced cytosolic DNA intrinsically primes endogenous STING signaling, the concurrent, on-demand local release of uncaged agonists can engage complementary innate networks, including the MyD88 pathway, thereby orchestrating robust dendritic cell maturation without dose-limiting systemic toxicity. This highly integrated approach represents a compelling solution to the longstanding conflict between delivery efficiency and systemic safety.

Therefore, for the combined application of RT + STING agonists, priority should be given to spatiotemporally precise strategies, ranging from intratumoral delivery to radiotherapy-activatable prodrugs, paired with moderate hypofractionated RT. This approach aims to maintain cytosolic dsDNA signals, enhance in situ immune activation, and minimize systemic toxicity. Concurrently, dynamic monitoring of therapeutic response and toxicity risks via pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic markers (e.g., IFN-related transcripts, peripheral cytokines) is recommended.

The triple combination paradigm

In the triple-combination strategy, RT initiates in situ immune priming by inducing tumor antigen release and danger-associated signals; STING agonists further amplify type I interferon (IFN-I) signals and enhance antigen cross-presentation by dendritic cells (DCs); and ICIs restore and maintain effector T cell function by relieving PD-1/PD-L1-mediated immunosuppression. These three modalities are mechanistically complementary and non-redundant and have been shown to synergistically promote immune killing and memory formation in multiple animal models, with efficacy significantly superior to any dual combination [13, 89].

While preclinical evidence for dual combinations is robust, evidence for triple regimens in human populations remains largely limited to early-phase exploratory studies, with substantial heterogeneity and a lack of rigorously designed randomized controlled trials to confirm the true magnitude of benefit and reproducibility. Compounding this challenge is the difficulty in quantifying optimal dosing and sequencing. Meanwhile, variations in target area, irradiated volume, and technical delivery approaches alter the strength of in situ vaccination, making it difficult to establish a standardized and generalizable model for the optimal “RT→STING→ICIs” therapeutic window [15].

Delivery and on-target instability also constitute major bottlenecks. Intratumoral administration is limited by lesion accessibility, while systemic administration carries risks of systemic inflammation and uncertainty regarding “cell-type specific internalization” (e.g., myeloid vs. lymphoid uptake). Furthermore, the ENPP1-cGAMP export axis may partially neutralize STING signals, further amplifying inter-individual variability [80]. Given these uncertainties, patient stratification is currently unsystematic: single indicators are often insufficient to capture the full biological continuum of the “cold-to-hot” immune transformation. Although composite indices combining histology, transcriptomics, circulating fluid biopsy, and radiomics hold methodological promise, their thresholds and workflows await standardization [34, 94]. Crucially, the management of the safety window is paramount: sustained or excessive activation of the STING-IFN-I axis can not only induce PD-L1 upregulation and suppressive myeloid recruitment, but also exacerbate RT-associated inflammation and fibrosis. The lack of prospective, programmed early warning and intervention pathways makes this risk difficult to manage adequately [16, 64, 88].

To navigate these complexities, controllability should be embedded into the protocol design through rigorous stratification and dynamic monitoring. Enrollment should prioritize patients with intact IFN-γ signatures and signaling competency, alongside comprehensive molecular assessment (e.g., cGAS/STING/TREX1 status). Furthermore, if baseline TREX1 levels are high, a moderate fractionation strategy should be pre-specified over ablative dosing to maximally preserve the dsDNA-IFN-I axis [15, 34].

At the dosimetric level, ultra-high single fractions should be avoided whenever feasible in favor of moderate fractionation. Crucially, where anatomically feasible, the radiation field design should adopt a “lymph node-sparing” strategy. Excessive irradiation of draining lymph nodes (DLNs) should be minimized to preserve the integrity of antigen-presenting cells and lymphocyte trafficking, which are indispensable for initiating systemic immunity [15, 98].

To facilitate on-target activation, it is recommended to use short-term cytokine kinetics (e.g., the “rise-peak-fall” curve of serum CXCL10/IFN-γ/IL-6) as a proximal pharmacodynamic readout. The absence of a peak suggests insufficient delivery or dosage, warranting adjustment of the administration site, vector, or dose; conversely, a sustained high peak indicates excessive stimulation, warranting dose reduction or extended intervals to avoid amplifying systemic inflammation (Fig. 3) [16, 64, 88].

The entry window for ICIs can tentatively reference an empirical range of 3–7 days post-RT, combining IRF1-mediated PD-L1 upregulation and the CXCL10 peak as biological triggers for intensifying PD-1/PD-L1 blockade. If persistent systemic inflammatory signals are present, combination therapy should be deferred until inflammatory markers subside to balance efficacy and toxicity [15, 64].

Programmed review and parameter adjustment should be implemented during the course: mid-term efficacy evaluation targets should include a decline or clearance of ctDNA within 2–8 weeks [99], early expansion of peripheral Ki-67⁺PD-1⁺CD8⁺ T cells (indicating T-cell reinvigoration) [100], and improvement in CD8-related radiomics scores [34, 94].

In summary, while the biological rationale for the triple strategy is clear, it has not yet met the requirements for widespread adoption in terms of clinical evidence level, reproducibility, and quantitative safety window management. Rather than indiscriminately escalating treatment intensity, a more viable strategy is to implement a chain-link protocol: “Baseline entry→Moderate RT priming→STING on-target closed-loop→Biologically triggered ICI entry→Programmed review and adjustment”. This approach transforms combination therapy from an empirical intervention into a quantifiable, monitorable, and reproducible clinical workflow [15, 64, 88, 94, 101].

Translational landscape and emerging preclinical evidence

The efficacy of radiotherapy-induced immune activation is strongly influenced by physical parameters. As outlined in Table 2, preclinical evidence indicates that moderate hypofractionated regimens are preferable to single ablative doses for avoiding TREX1-mediated DNA clearance [15]. Furthermore, high-linear energy transfer particle therapy, such as carbon ions, shows potential in maximizing complex DNA damage [102]. However, translating these mechanistic insights into clinical success requires overcoming significant delivery barriers. Early-phase clinical trials evaluating first-generation intratumoral STING agonists combined with immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) demonstrated acceptable safety but restricted systemic efficacy (Table 2) [59]. This localized effect underscores the physicochemical limitations of early delivery methods in breaching systemic immune tolerance.

Challenges and future perspectives

While radio-immunotherapy offers the potential for in situ vaccination, its clinical translation remains constrained by a narrow therapeutic index. Amplifying the IFN-I and chemokine network via triple combinations (RT, STING agonists, and ICIs) also increases the risk of cytokine release syndrome (CRS) and immune-related adverse events (irAEs) [59, 88]. Consequently, future protocols should prioritize strategies for toxicity management, such as IL-6 blockade, alongside interventions to counteract tumor-intrinsic resistance mechanisms. For instance, DNMT inhibitors have demonstrated preclinical promise in reversing epigenetic silencing of the antigen-processing machinery [92]. Moreover, overcoming intrinsic “apoptotic brakes” presents a critical therapeutic opportunity. Integrating pan-caspase inhibitors (e.g., Emricasan) represents a rational strategy to prevent the proteolytic degradation of cGAS and STING, thereby preserving and extending the immunogenic signaling initiated by radiation-induced DNA leakage [73, 75].

However, the limitations of current evidence should be explicitly recognized. A substantial translational gap persists, as most synergistic data derive from homogeneous murine models that inadequately recapitulate the complex spatial heterogeneity and highly immunosuppressive stroma of human solid tumors. This discrepancy likely contributes to the limited efficacy observed in early clinical trials of first-generation agonists. Furthermore, tumor heterogeneity implies that the cGAS-STING axis is not universally functional, with subsets of patients exhibiting high baseline TREX1 expression or inherent signaling defects potentially remaining refractory regardless of dose optimization.

To decouple local potency from systemic toxicity, the field is moving towards next-generation delivery platforms-including non-nucleotide agonists, tumor-specific ADCs, and responsive hydrogels-that maximize intratumoral exposure while limiting systemic leakage [95, 97]. These precision delivery systems serve as effective amplifiers for spatially fractionated radiation therapy (SFRT), balancing immunogenic priming with functional organ preservation [106]. Finally, reliance on single biomarkers is often insufficient. Therefore, precision medicine requires the validation of artificial intelligence(AI)- and multi-omics-driven algorithms that integrate radiomics, histology, and transcriptomics to optimize patient stratification and treatment decision-making.Standardizing these workflows is a critical step to shift the field from empirical experimentation toward a reproducible, data-driven precision paradigm [34, 94].

Conclusion

Radiotherapy (RT) can engage the cGAS-STING pathway to link localized DNA damage with anti-tumor immune activation, but this effect is neither uniform nor inherently beneficial. Current evidence indicates that RT-induced STING signaling is a conditional process, determined by DNA availability, TREX1-mediated degradation, apoptotic caspase activity, tumor genetic background, and immune cell context. In practice, productive interferon signaling and T-cell priming are more consistently observed when STING is activated in antigen-presenting cells, whereas tumor-intrinsic activation is often restrained by immune-suppressive checkpoints. In selected settings, such as mismatch repair-deficient tumors, RT appears to amplify pre-existing innate immune signaling rather than initiate it de novo. These observations support a shift from indiscriminate immune stimulation toward precision calibration of the RT-cGAS-STING axis. Importantly, cold-to-hot tumor transformation should be viewed not as a guaranteed outcome of radiotherapy, but as a context-dependent consequence that arises only when radiation parameters, immune contexture, and regulatory constraints are properly aligned, providing a rational basis for combination with immunotherapy.
