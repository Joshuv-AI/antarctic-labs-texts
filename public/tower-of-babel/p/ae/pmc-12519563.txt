Spatiotemporal profiling reveals the impact of caloric restriction in the aging mammalian brain
Authors: Zehao Zhang, Alexander Epstein, Chloe Schaefer, Abdulraouf Abdulraouf, Weirong Jiang, Wei Zhou, Junyue Cao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SUMMARYCaloric restriction (CR) is a well-studied intervention that extends lifespan and slows cognitive decline across species, yet the specific cell populations and molecular pathways involved remain elusive. In this study, we profiled >500,000 cells from 36 control and CR mouse brains across three age groups with EasySci single-nucleus transcriptomics and performed imaging-free IRISeq spatial transcriptomics on twelve brain sections from CR and control aged mice. We thereby explored the impact of CR in >300 cellular states and 11 brain regions. CR delayed expansion of inflammatory cell populations, preserved neural precursor cells, and broadly reduced the expression of aging-associated genes involved in cellular stress, senescence, inflammation, and DNA damage. CR restored the expression of region-specific genes linked to cognitive function, myelin maintenance, and circadian rhythm. In summary, we provide a high-resolution spatiotemporal map of the aging mouse brain’s response to CR, detailing precise cellular and molecular mechanisms behind its neuroprotective effects.

INTRODUCTION

Aging induces functional decline in the mammalian brain, increasing its vulnerability to cognitive impairments and neurodegenerative disorders.1 Among the various interventions to slow aging and delay age-related diseases, caloric restriction (CR) is particularly notable for its consistency in extending lifespan across species, including worms, flies, rats, and mice.2–5 Importantly, CR has demonstrated beneficial effects on brain function, enhancing learning and memory and increasing resilience against neurodegenerative diseases.6 However, established methods such as bulk transcriptomics yield little insight into how CR acts on highly heterogeneous brain cell populations and regions to mitigate the molecular and cellular changes of aging.

Recent advances in single-cell and spatial transcriptomics have enabled the precise measurement of gene expression changes across distinct cell populations and brain regions.7,8 However, the low throughput of standard approaches remains a challenge,6,9 impeding the study of how hundreds of different brain cell states respond to anti-aging treatments, particularly for rare yet critical aging-associated cell populations (e.g., neurogenic cells and activated microglia). To improve throughput, we recently developed two scalable approaches, EasySci10 and IRISeq,11 which enable comprehensive single-cell and spatial transcriptomic analysis of the mammalian brain across ages and conditions.

In this study, we first applied EasySci10 to profile transcriptomes from >500,000 nuclei derived from 36 individual mouse brains, spanning different ages and fed ad libitum or CR diets. We thereby identified specific genes and pathways influenced by CR in various cell populations and demonstrated that CR can rescue aging-associated changes in cell population proportions and dynamics. Next, we utilized IRISeq, an optics-free spatial transcriptomics platform,11 to investigate the molecular changes in different brain regions upon CR in aged mice, revealing both region-specific and widespread effects of CR on gene expression. We thereby identified specific molecular and cellular pathways through which CR benefits the aging brain, suggesting potential therapeutic targets for aging and neurodegenerative disease.

RESULTS

Single-nucleus transcriptomic profiling of brain cell dynamics in response to CR

We performed EasySci single-nucleus transcriptomic analysis on 36 mouse brain samples from a diverse cohort spanning three age groups: 12, 18, and 24 months (Figure 1A, top). Each age group includes 12 individual brains, equally divided between those undergoing 60% CR initiated at 4 months of age and ad libitum-fed controls (Table S1). As expected, CR significantly reduced the body weight of animals in all age groups, while its impact on brain weight was relatively minor (Figures 1B and S1A). We also applied IRISeq spatial transcriptomics to assess region-specific gene expression dynamics in twelve brain sections from six aged (24-month-old) mice in CR or control conditions, yielding a total of 65,128 spatially barcoded transcriptome profiles (Figures 1A, bottom, and 1D).

Following sequencing of the EasySci library and filtering out low-quality cells and doublets, we obtained 514,920 high-quality single-nucleus gene expression profiles across various conditions. The number of nuclei profiled per age and diet condition ranged from 57,917 to 106,493, with an average of 14,303 nuclei recovered per individual (Figures 1C and S1B). Each single-nucleus profile contained a mean of 5,369 unique transcripts (unique molecular identifiers [UMIs]) and 1,504 genes (and a median of 1,994 UMIs and 974 genes) (Figure S1D). Notably, all samples were processed and sequenced concurrently in the same experiment, resulting in minimal experimental batch effects (Figures S1E and S1F).

Next, we automated annotation of the main cell types recovered in the current single-nucleus study (referred to as the “CR atlas”) using a support-vector-machine-based classifier to transfer labels from a previously published single-cell mouse brain aging atlas10 (referred to as the “reference atlas”) (Figure 1E). When training on the reference atlas, we performed 5-fold cross-validation to validate classifier accuracy, obtaining substantially higher F1-scores on true labels than on permuted labels (Figures S2A–S2D). In the CR atlas, we identified all 31 main cell types defined in the reference brain atlas10 (Figures 1F and 1G), including abundant cell types, such as cerebellum granule neurons (44.58%) and oligodendrocytes (13.89%), and rare cell types, like Purkinje neurons (0.10%) and pituitary cells (0.02%). Performing unsupervised dimensionality reduction on the CR atlas yielded highly distinct cellular clusters that closely matched the annotated cell types (Figure S1C), validating our annotation method. Integrating the CR atlas with the reference atlas showed strong correspondence of cell types (Figure S1G). We observed significant correlations in main cell proportions between both the CR and reference atlases (Spearman’s rho = 0.85, p = 5.0e–07) (Figure S1H), even though the algorithm did not have access to cell-type proportion information. Distinct molecular states of transferred annotations were further confirmed by cell-type-specific gene signatures (Figure S2E), such as choroid plexus epithelial cells marked by Ttr,12 vascular leptomeningeal cells marked by Slc47a1,13 and microglia marked by Csf1r.13 In addition to our primary annotation, we also provide cell-type labels derived from the Allen Brain Initiative Cell Census Network (BICCN) reference, which show strong alignment with our dataset (Figures S1I–S1K).

CR rescues age-related gene expression changes

To assess the impact of aging and CR on brain gene expression, we first aggregated single-cell transcriptomes from individual brains and performed principal-component analysis (PCA) (Figures S3A and S3B). The ad libitum samples exhibited a smooth trajectory from 12 to 18 to 24 months, reflecting a consistent age-related progression (Figure S3A). Although CR did not markedly alter the pseudobulk brain transcriptomes at 12 and 18 months, at 24 months, the CR-treated brains shifted significantly toward a younger transcriptional state (p = 0.014), albeit with higher variability among individual samples compared with the ad libitum controls (Figures S3A and S3B).

To address the variation in CR samples at 18 and 24 months and enhance statistical power, we combined 18- and 24-month-old mice into one “aged” group and compared them to the 12-month “adult” group for differential expression gene analysis. Using stringent criteria (false discovery rate [FDR] ≤ 1e-5 and fold change ≥ 2 between adult and aged), we identified 456 cell-type-specific differentially expressed genes (DEGs) (Table S2), which showed strong concordance with DEGs identified in the direct 12- vs. 24-month comparison but also yielded more robust results (Figure S3C). Notably, certain cell types—vascular leptomeningeal cells, olfactory bulb (OB) neurons, and inferior olivary nucleus neurons—exhibited a greater number of DEGs, suggesting significant molecular alterations during aging (Figure 2A).

Most DEGs were specific to individual cell types (Figure S3D). For example, aged microglia showed increased expression of genes associated with cellular proliferation (e.g., Ptchd114), motility and phagocytic activity (e.g., Myo1e15), lipid and amyloid-beta metabolism (e.g., Apoe16), and immune response (e.g., Axl17). These aging-associated changes were also present in our previous EasySci brain atlas,10 suggesting dysregulated microglial activation in the aged brain (Figure 2B). Meanwhile, we identified 49 genes that are significantly altered across multiple cell types with a highly consistent trend (Figure S3D). These genes were involved in pathways such as intercellular communications (e.g., Rab3a and Scg2), genomic instability (e.g., Tspy14 and Oip5os1), and loss of proteostasis (e.g., Hsp90aa1 and Hsp90ab1) (Figures S3D and S3E).

We next examined whether CR rescued age-related changes in gene expression. A substantial fraction (324 out of 456) of cell-type-specific age-associated gene signatures (AAGs) showed significant changes between CR and ad libitum conditions at 24 months (FDR < 0.05) (Figures 2C and S3G). Remarkably, nearly all of the cell-type-specific genes that were significantly changed in both aging and CR (323 out of 324) were changed in opposite directions in aging and CR (Figures 2C and 2D), suggesting that CR mitigates age-related changes in gene expression. Some genes (e.g., Ptchd1 in microglia) were rescued by CR at 12 or 18 months; others, like Apoe and Axl in microglia, only showed significant differences between CR and control conditions at 24 months (Figure 2B), highlighting the need to capture multiple time points to evaluate anti-aging interventions.

Interestingly, CR showed patterns in which AAGs it rescued. While 81.1% of genes upregulated in aging were rescued by CR, only 47.5% of downregulated genes were rescued (Figure 2E). Genes that increase with aging but were mitigated by CR were enriched in oxidative phosphorylation, immune signaling, antigen processing, and neurodegeneration processes (Figures 2G and S3F). Genes common across multiple cell types were more likely to be rescued by CR: 86.9% of shared aging-associated genes were significantly rescued by CR, compared to only 54.6% of cell-type-specific genes (Figure 2F). This is not only because shared genes were more likely to be upregulated: even among upregulated genes, shared AAGs are more frequently rescued by CR (90%) than those unique to specific cell types (67%). This suggests that CR preferentially mitigates broad changes in gene expression, such as those related to cellular stress responses.

CR rescues age-related cell population changes

CR could rescue AAGs either by altering expression uniformly in all cells of a given type or by modulating the abundance of specific subpopulations that express AAGs. To distinguish between these two mechanisms, we investigated how CR impacts the abundance of aging-associated cell subpopulations. As with main cell types, we trained a support-vector-machine-based classifier on labels from the reference brain atlas10 to annotate subpopulations (Figures 3A and S2D). We identified over 300 subpopulations with distinct molecular profiles, many of which represent either transitional states within a broader cell type (e.g., disease-associated microglia [DAMs]) or rare cell types that form discrete clusters upon subclustering (e.g., T cells and B cells). There was a significant correlation in the abundance of subpopulations between the reference and CR atlases (Spearman rho = 0.76, p < 2.2e—16) (Figure 3B), demonstrating that our annotation algorithm preserved cellular population proportions.

Using these annotations, we performed differential abundance (DA) analysis to pinpoint cell subpopulations significantly altered with aging in both the CR and reference brain atlases. Despite the different time frames of our CR study (12 vs. 24 months) and the reference brain atlas10 (6 vs. 21 months), 21 out of 25 aging-associated subpopulations identified in both datasets (FDR < 0.05, ≥1.5-fold expression change) exhibited consistent aging-associated dynamics (Figure 3C). Subpopulations expanded in both atlases included DAMs19 (microglia-9, marked by Csf1 and Apoe), disease-associated oligodendrocytes (DAOs; oligodendrocyte-7, marked by C4b and Serpina3n), and several OB neuron subpopulations. The aging-associated expansion of these glial cells aligns with prior single-nucleus and spatial genomic studies,10,11 suggesting increased inflammatory stress in the aged brain. Conversely, we observed depletion in several endothelial cell subpopulations (Figure 3C), indicating damage to the neurovascular system and compromised blood-brain barrier function in the aged brain. Additionally, we detected a reduction in myelin-forming oligodendrocytes (oligodendrocyte-6, marked by Tcf7l1 and Slc9a3r2), consistent with the diminished remyelination capacity observed in the aged brain.20

We found that CR significantly reversed changes in abundance in 12 out of 21 aging-associated cell subpopulations (Figure 3D). For example, several endothelial subpopulations, which typically deplete at 24 months, were restored following CR (Figure 3D). This observation aligns with previous studies suggesting that CR helps maintain cerebral vascular homeostasis.6 In addition, CR mitigated the aging-associated expansion of several reactive glial cell populations, including DAMs (microglia-9) and DAOs (oligodendrocyte-7) (Figures 3D, S4A, and S4E), suggesting a reduction in brain inflammatory stress upon CR.

To delve deeper into the cell state transitions underpinning the effect of CR, we focused on several key rescued glia subpopulations. We first integrated single-nucleus transcriptomes of microglia from our dataset with microglia from the reference brain aging atlas10 and a mouse progenitor cell atlas18 (Figure 3E). The integrated dataset contained twelve distinct cellular states that were conserved across all datasets and validated with cluster-specific gene signatures; they included non-microglial immune cell populations (e.g., T cells, B cells, and dendritic cells) and several microglial subpopulations (e.g., homeostatic microglia, proliferating microglia, and Cxcl10+ interferon-responsive microglia) (Figures 3E, 3F, and S4B–S4D). Consistent with our cell population analysis, we observed an age-dependent transition from a homeostatic microglia state to DAM state (Figure 3G). We show that CR obstructs this transition by blocking the formation of a less-characterized intermediate microglia state (Jarid2+, Lars2+, Il31ra+) in the aging process (Figures 3H and 3I).

Similarly, we delved into oligodendrocyte dynamics by integrating single-nucleus transcriptomes of oligodendrocytes with two external datasets,10,18 identifying detailed oligodendrocyte subpopulations (e.g., DAOs and mature oligodendrocyte [MOL] subpopulations) and associated molecular signatures (Figures 3J, 3K, and S4E–S4H). Notably, we observed aging-associated transitions to two C4b+ DAO populations (C4b+ Piezo2high DAOs and C4b+ Piezo2low DAOs), which were both obstructed by CR (Figure 3L).

Aging typically triggers rapid depletion of neurogenic cells at an early stage,21 which is observable in our reference EasySci brain atlas (Figure 3N). To examine the effects of CR on adult neurogenesis, we integrated neurogenic cells (neural stem and progenitor cells and neuroblasts) from our study with neurogenic cells from the progenitor cell atlas.18 The integrated dataset revealed a continuous cellular differentiation trajectory from progenitor cells to OB neuroblasts (Figures 3M, S5A, and S5B), aligning with findings from our previous studies.18 In ad libitum mice from our CR atlas, we observed very few neurogenic cells at any age (Figures 3N and 3O), suggesting that neurogenic cells may already be depleted at 12 months. In contrast, CR mice exhibited a 1.8-fold average increase in neural stem and progenitor cells and a 5-fold increase in neuroblasts at 12 months relative to ad libitum mice (Figures 3N and 3O). To identify molecular mechanisms, we performed differential expression analysis on OB neurogenic cells from 12-month-old mice (Figures S5C and S5D), revealing 57 upregulated genes and 34 downregulated genes in CR. Upregulated genes (e.g., Top2a and Egfr) were associated with enhanced proliferation, while downregulated genes were linked to neuronal differentiation processes (e.g., cilium assembly) and negative regulation of canonical Wnt signaling (Figures S5E and S5F), suggesting that CR expands the OB progenitor pool by promoting proliferation and suppressing neuronal differentiation. However, while CR delayed the depletion of neurogenic cells beyond the 12-month time point, it did not completely prevent it: neurogenic cells were depleted at 18 and 24 months with CR (Figures 3N and 3O). This underscores the importance of including multiple time points to capture the age-dependent effects of CR on cell population dynamics.

Spatial transcriptomic profiling reveals region-specific responses to CR

To explore region-specific molecular responses to CR, we utilized a scalable imaging-free spatial transcriptomics platform, IRISeq,11 to profile twelve brain sections from six 24-month-old mice under CR and ad libitum conditions (Figure 1A, bottom). IRISeq employs 50-μm barcoded gel beads with bead-bead interaction oligos to reconstruct the spatial distribution of gene expression within a tissue.11 We sectioned the brain to include regions such as the cortex, hippocampus, midbrain, hypothalamus, and white matter, all of which exhibit significant structural and functional changes during aging.22 We recovered 65,128 transcriptionally barcoded spatial spots, detecting a median of 2,700 unique transcripts per spot (Figure 4A).

Unsupervised clustering of the gene expression data revealed eleven transcriptionally distinct clusters of beads corresponding to specific brain regions (Figures 4B and 4C), which were annotated based on region-specific gene markers (Figures S6A and S6B; Table S3). Using bead connection data, we reconstructed the spatial position of each bead within its brain section; the result corresponded closely to our gene expression-based annotations (Figures 4D and S6C). The top region-specific gene features identified were highly consistent with previous studies, including markers such as Plp1 for white matter, Pmch for the hypothalamus, and Camk2n1 for the cortex11 (Figure S6C). These results underscore the capacity of IRISeq to yield high-quality spatial transcriptomes without pre-indexed oligo arrays or optical imaging.

We next performed differential expression analysis to study how CR affects region-specific gene expression. To prevent small numbers of beads containing cells from multiple regions from yielding false positives, we filtered out genes expressed in <10% of cells within a given region. We identified 663 DEGs in CR across various regions. Notably, the ventricle region displayed the highest number of DEGs (Figure 4E), aligning with the strong rescue effect of CR on cell populations in this area. Most CR-associated DEGs (549 out of 663) exhibited high region specificity (Figures 4F and 4G). In the hypothalamus, the top three genes upregulated by CR, all of which were consistent across replicates (Figures S6D–S6F), are markers of hypothalamic neuronal populations involved in metabolic control. These are Hcrt (orexin/hypocretin), involved in sleep behavior regulation and neuronal activation by dietary amino acids;23,24 Pomc, associated with promoting satiety and reducing appetite25; and Npy, known for its crucial role in energy homeostasis and being significantly increased in the hypothalamus under CR.26 Additionally, CR elevated the expression of oligodendrocyte-associated genes, such as Ugt8, Sox2ot, Aspa, and Dip2a (Figure 4F), especially in the ventricular region, supporting a beneficial role of CR in oligodendrocyte maturation and maintenance in the aging brain.

Meanwhile, 58 genes were upregulated in CR across more than one brain region, with most exhibiting highly consistent changes across these areas (Figure 4G). These included circadian rhythm genes (e.g., Dbp27,28) (Figures 4G and 4H), suggesting that CR may restore dysregulated circadian rhythms observed in the aged brain.29 CR also enhanced the expression of genes associated with myelination (e.g., Pllp30 and Opalin31) across the ventricles, white matter, and hippocampus; myelination was the top Gene Ontology term in response to CR (Figures 4F, 4H, and 4I). This is consistent with previous findings that CR ameliorates myelin degeneration in neurodegenerative disorders.32

We also identified 66 genes that are significantly depleted across more than one brain region in response to CR. Notably, several were key players in senescence pathways, including Cdkn1a, Cdkn1c, and Bcl2l1 (Figures 4F, 4G, 4J, and S6G–S6I). Reduced expression of these genes in ventricles and white matter is correlated with decreased cellular stress in these regions, evidenced by the suppression of genes involved in DNA damage (e.g., Ddit4), inflammation (e.g., Nfkbia and Tsc22d3), and oxidative stress (e.g., Sgk1) (Figures 4F, 4G, and 4J). To validate that CR reduces senescence, we employed the SenMayo gene set33—a curated panel of 125 validated genes associated with cellular senescence—to assess major cell types in our single-cell dataset. For most cell types, SenMayo scores increased with age as expected (Figure S7A). Quantifying the effect of CR across all ages using a generalized linear model revealed a partial but significant rescue of senescence increases in microglia and oligodendrocytes (Figure S7B). In oligodendrocytes, senescence scores increased across several subclusters with increasing age (Figure S7C); the phenomenon was not driven by a single subcluster, and the aging-associated oligodendrocytes-7 were no more senescent than other subclusters. By contrast, microglia-9 (DAMs), which accumulate in aging and are less abundant in CR, seemed to drive the aging-associated increase in microglial senescence: they exhibited the highest SenMayo score of any subcluster in 24-month-old ad libitum mice (Figure S7D). These results are consistent with previous studies that identified senescent microglia as either a subset of DAM34 or as a separate signature expressing similar markers such as Trem2 and Apoe.35 We suspect that the limited number of microglia in our study prevented clear discrimination between senescent microglia and DAMs. Together, the single-cell and spatial analyses confirm that CR significantly modulates cellular senescence across brain cell types.

Additionally, CR significantly alters the region-specific expression of lesser-characterized non-coding RNAs, notably increasing Neat1 and depleting Gm42644 (Figures 4I and 4J). This change, like most others observed in the spatial atlas, predominantly occurs in the ventricles and white matter of the aged brain (Figures 4I and 4J). Several aging-associated cell population changes rescued by CR also occur in these regions, including depletion of neurogenic cells and expansion of DAMs and DAOs. They may therefore represent attractive areas of focus for therapeutics targeting aging and neurodegenerative diseases.

DISCUSSION

Using the highly scalable EasySci and IRISeq methods, we created a comprehensive single-cell and spatial atlas to explore the effect of CR on mouse brain aging. The single-cell dataset contains >500,000 nuclei from 36 control and CR mice brains at three different ages (12, 18, and 24 months) and pinpoints >300 distinct cell states; the spatial dataset contains >65,000 spatial spots from 6 control and CR aged mouse brains (2 sections per brain) assigned to 11 distinct brain regions. Substantial aging-related cell population changes were observed, including the expansion of inflammatory cell states such as DAMs and DAOs. We also noted an age-associated depletion of cell populations vital to neurovascular integrity (e.g., endothelial cells) and the brain myelination pathway (e.g., myelin-forming oligodendrocytes), consistent with previous single-cell studies.10

CR rescued proportion changes in several key aging-associated cell populations. Consistent with previous studies,36 we observed a depletion of neurogenesis-related cells (i.e., neuronal stem and progenitor cells and OB neuroblasts) before 12 months. Under CR, this decline was delayed to 18 months. The expansion of inflammatory cells, typically observed after 18 months, was markedly inhibited by CR during these later stages. Additionally, we noted a CR-mediated rescue effect on endothelial cell depletion at 24 months, although this was not evident at earlier stages.

CR exhibited both general and population/region-specific effects on gene expression. Genes rescued broadly across cell populations and brain regions were predominantly involved in brain-wide processes such as genomic instability, loss of proteostasis, cellular stress, inflammation, and senescence. The consistent suppression of stress responses and senescence, observed in both single-nucleus RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) and spatial transcriptomics analyses, suggests that CR effectively mitigates aging-associated cellular damage. Our findings are also consistent with the anti-senescence effect of CR on other mouse tissues, such as the intestine and liver.37 The reduction in cellular senescence may facilitate the rejuvenation of cell populations in these regions, such as microglia and neurogenic cells.

Genes rescued in specific brain regions included several genes associated with cognitive functions and metabolic activities, such as Hcrt and Npy in the hypothalamus. In the ventricles, CR significantly upregulated genes involved in neurogenesis and myelin maintenance. These changes make sense given the observed enhancement of neurogenesis following CR. We were also able to detect some less well-known changes upon CR, such as the depletion of several non-coding RNAs in the ventricles and white matter. Future studies will be needed to determine their exact role in aging and CR.

Our analysis highlights that cell population proportion changes could serve as an early indicator of mammalian aging. They are directly linked to functional changes in mammalian organs and are robust against experimental batch effects, as cellular states are defined by combinations of molecular markers. Using cell populations, we observed that the effect of CR is apparent even at 12 months or earlier, delaying depletion of neurogenic cells. Our study also revealed the timeline of cell population proportion changes in aging: for example, depletion of neuronal stem and progenitor cells precedes expansion of inflammatory cells. This observation aligns with our previous studies that cataloged distinct temporal dynamics of cell population changes across different age windows.38 Such findings underscore the importance of selecting appropriate time windows for evaluating anti-aging interventions targeted at specific cell populations.

In summary, we systematically explored the impact of CR on global cell population dynamics and the associated cell-type-specific and region-specific molecular alterations by highly scalable single-nucleus genomics and spatial genomics. In the future, our affordable and high-throughput strategy could be applied to identify key cellular and molecular mechanisms shared between multiple longevity interventions,39 suggesting valuable therapeutic targets for aging and aging-related neurodegenerative disorders.

Limitations of the study

The present study is limited by the characteristics of the data acquired. Our single-nucleus atlas begins at 12 months of age, which may preclude the detection of aging-associated changes that occur earlier in adulthood unless (as in the case of neural stem and progenitor cells) these changes are delayed by CR. Similarly, our spatial atlas was limited to 24-month-old mice, preventing the analysis of early, region-specific transcriptional changes. On a technical level, as each IRISeq bead captures transcripts and spatial barcodes from multiple cells via diffusion, ambiguity can arise in the annotation of cells at regional borders. We imposed a conservative statistical threshold to mitigate this, which may have inadvertently excluded some true positive differentially expressed genes. Finally, this study was conducted exclusively in male mice, and CR has been reported to be sexually dimorphic.40 Future studies incorporating both sexes, a wider range of time points, and leveraging advances in high-resolution spatial technologies are therefore essential to build upon this work.

EXPERIMENTAL MODEL AND STUDY PARTICIPANT DETAILS

Animals

C57BL/6JN wild-type male mouse brain samples were obtained from the National Institute of Aging colony at Charles River. Caloricrestricted (CR) animals and ad libitum (AL) animals were housed separately and individually to monitor diet intake. Mice were weaned at 3–4 weeks of age and then caged in lots of four mice until 14 weeks of age. Caloric restriction begins at 14 weeks of age when animals are fed 90% of the predetermined amount of food consumed by AL age-matched controls. At 15 weeks of age, animals are fed 75% of the predetermined diet, and at 16 weeks are fed 60% of the predetermined diet. Metadata for each animal, including mouse individual ID, gender, age, accession dates, and body and brain weights, can be found in Table S1.

METHOD DETAILS

Whole brain nuclei extraction for EasySci

The samples were processed by EasySci10 with minor modifications. In brief, frozen brain tissues were added to a 6 cm cell culture dish containing 3mL of lysis buffer solution (EZ Lysis Buffer containing 1% diethyl pyrocarbonate). Each sample was homogenized with a razor blade until the solution could be aspirated with 1000μL tips to pass through a 40μM filter above a 50mL tube that contained 6 mL of lysis buffer solution. We then used the plunger from a 10mL syringe to further homogenize the tissue on the filter until the solution passed through the filter completely. The filters were washed with 1 mL of additional lysis solution. The nuclei were concentrated by centrifugation at 500g for 5 min at 4°C. The nuclei were washed 3 times in nuclei wash buffer (NWB), containing nuclei buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 10 mM NaCl, 3 mM MgCl2 in molecular grade water) supplemented by 1% Tween 20, 1% recombinant albumin, and 0.1% SUPERase·In RNase Inhibitor. After the washes, nuclei were aliquoted into 2 cryovials containing 500μL of nuclei resuspended in NWB containing 10% dimethyl sulfoxide and stored in slow freezers, cooling at 1°C per minute, at −80°C overnight.

The following day, one aliquot of the nuclei from each sample was rapidly thawed in a 37°C water bath and centrifuged at 500g for 5 min at 4°C. The supernatant was removed, and the nuclei were resuspended in freshly prepared NWB containing 0.005 mg/mL DAPI for fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS). The nuclei were stained for 10 min before performing FACS on an SH800 Cell Sorter with a 100 μm sorting chip (Sony, #LE-C3210), to capture all DAPI-positive singlet nuclei and to exclude cellular debris and doublet cell populations. Nuclei were collected into a 1.5 mL DNA Lo-Bind tube containing 100 μL of NWB, vortexed to coat the tubes for optimal nuclei capture, and subsequently centrifuged at 500g for 5 min at 4°C before proceeding directly to library construction.

EasySci library construction and sequencing

The subsequent steps for the generation of sequencing libraries followed the published EasySci RNA protocol.10 Initially, the sorted nuclei were distributed across 96-well plates (Geneseesci, #24–302) for reverse transcription (RT). Prior to RT, indexed oligo-dT and indexed random hexamer primers were utilized to introduce the first index. The nuclei were pooled, washed, and redistributed into new 96-well plates for the addition of the second index through ligation. After subsequent pooling and washing steps, the nuclei were diluted and distributed into new plates for second-strand synthesis and AMPure XP beads purification. After elution of the cDNA, tagmentation with Tn5 transposase was performed, followed by a 16-cycle PCR reaction with indexed P7 primers. The resulting PCR products were then pooled and purified twice using 0.8X volume of AMPure XP. The library concentration and fragment size were measured using an Agilent TapeStation, and sequencing was carried out on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 System with an S4 Flow Cell.

Sequencing data preprocessing

BCL files were demultiplexed using the barcode information from the last round of PCR indexing utilizing Illumina’s bclfastq2 program (version 2.20.0.422) to convert the file format to FASTQ. For single-nucleus RNA-seq, read alignment and gene/exon count matrix generation were conducted using our EasySci pipeline.10 Cells from the gene count matrix were filtered according to the following parameters: unmatched rate less than 0.4, combined oligo-dT and random hexamer UMI (Unique Molecular Identifier) count less than 200, and gene counts less than 100. Scrublet (version 0.2.3) was utilized for the doublet removal set with the following parameters min_count = 3, min_cells = 3, vscore_percentile = 85, n_pc = 30, expected_doublet_rate = 0.08, sim_doublet_ratio = 2, n_neighbors = 30. Cells with a doublet score of more than 0.2 were discarded, which corresponded to 10% of the processed and filtered dataset.

Single-cell sequencing datasets integration and label transfer

To increase the resolution of main cell population clustering, we integrated this dataset with the published EasySci brain atlas.10 The EasySci brain atlas containing ~1.5 million cells was first subsampled to 126,285 cells by subsetting 5,000 cells from each main cell population (for cell populations that have <5,000 cells, we subset all cells). The subsampled EasySci brain atlas was then integrated with the full brain CR dataset using Seurat55 integration functions. Briefly, each dataset was normalized and the top 5,000 highly variable features are selected using the “vst” method. Integration features were selected by SelectIntegxrationFeatures() and anchors were determined using FindIntegrationAnchors(). The two datasets were then integrated with the IntegrateData() function. To visualize all the cells together, we co-embedded all the cells in the same low-dimensional space.

To transfer labels from the EasySci dataset to the brain CR dataset, we implemented a two-step label transfer process targeting both the main cell population and subcluster levels. First, we identified the common genes shared by both datasets and subsetted the datasets based on this shared gene set. The annotated EasySci dataset was then randomly subsampled to 1,000 cells per main cell population, retaining all cells if a cell population contained <1,000 cells. We employed a support vector machine (SVM) classifier, specifically the LinearSVC function from scikit-learn,48 to train the model using the log-transformed expression values of the subsampled cells, with the corresponding main cell populations serving as the target variable. The trained SVM model was evaluated by comparing it with a model trained on permuted data and was then applied to the brain CR dataset to predict the main cell population labels. For subcluster prediction, a similar approach was employed: for each EasySci main cell population, a maximum of 1,000 cells per subcluster was subsampled, and an SVM model was trained for each main cell population. Each classifier was then applied to the brain CR data, subsetted by the predicted main cell population labels, to predict the subcluster identities.

Differential gene analysis for single-nucleus data and spatial genomics

To identify differentially expressed genes for each main cell population between adult and aged groups and for each brain region between caloric restriction and ad libitum groups, we employed the likelihood ratio test to identify genes significantly associated with specific cell populations/subpopulations, using the differentialGeneTest() function in Monocle2 (version 2.28.0). To ensure both statistical significance and biological relevance, we applied stringent selection criteria. For the single-cell dataset, we only examined main cell types with >1,000 cells in our ad libitum dataset. Within each cell type, we required FDR <1e-5, >2-fold enrichment and >50 transcripts per million in the higher expression condition. Within each brain region of our spatial dataset, we required FDR <0.05 and imposed a threshold that DE genes must be expressed in >10% of cells, to avoid false positives from mixed or mislabeled beads. We then required >1.5-fold enrichment and >50 transcripts per million in the higher expression condition.

Cell state transition analysis by integrating datasets across studies

To identify detailed subpopulations of microglia, oligodendrocytes, and those involved in neurogenesis, we integrated corresponding main cell populations from this dataset, EasySci,10 and TrackerSci18 (for identifying the potential progenitor states). Specific main cell populations were subset from each dataset and integrated using Seurat55 integration functions. Similar to the abovementioned full dataset integration, each dataset is normalized and the top 5,000 highly variable features are selected using the “vst” method. Integration features are selected by SelectIntegrationFeatures() and anchors are determined using FindIntegrationAnchors(). The datasets were then integrated with the IntegrateData() function and co-embedded in the 2D space.

Differential abundance analysis for single-nucleus dataset

To assess cell population dynamics across different age groups and diet conditions, we conducted the differential abundance test as demonstrated by Sziraki et al..10 Briefly, cell count matrices across replicates (cell population × replicates) were constructed by counting cell numbers of each cell population and then normalizing against the total cell number recovered from each specific replicate. We then employed the likelihood-ratio test for identifying differentially abundant cell populations using the differentialGeneTest() function of Monocle2 (version 2.28.0). For fold change calculations, we first normalized the number of cells in each cell population relative to the total cell count in the respective condition. We then compared these normalized values between the case and control conditions, adding a small pseudocount (1e-6) to reduce the noise from very small clusters. To assess the significance of cell population dynamics of specific cell populations, we use an FDR threshold of 0.05 and a fold change >1.5 between conditions.

Senescence score analysis

Gene expression counts in cells from each individual mouse and main cell type were pseudobulked by sum using the decoupler-py package. This data was normalized to 10,000 total counts and logarithmized with a pseudocount of 1; the Scanpy score_genes function was then used to calculate SenMayo score using the SenMayo mouse gene list downloaded from Saul et al.33 Before plotting, pseudobulk groupings with fewer than 20 cells or 10000 counts were removed. Main cell types with fewer than 30 remaining mice were filtered out. For subcluster-specific score calculation, the same method was employed except that cells were pseudobulked by each individual mouse and predicted subcluster; pseudobulk groupings with fewer than 10 cells or 5000 counts were removed. Significance of age-associated changes between individual timepoints was calculated using a two-sided Welch’s t test.

To calculate SenMayo rescue significance, we used the edgeR package to fit pseudobulked count data (not normalized) to a generalized linear model, with age in months as a covariate and diet as a binary factor. For each main cell type, genes with fewer than 100 counts were removed; main cell types represented in fewer than 25 mice were also removed. The ROAST rotation gene set test56 was then performed using the SenMayo gene set for each cell type, with 1000 rotations. One-sided p-values were calculated for age and diet and multiple hypothesis correction was performed using the Benjamini-Hochberg method.57

Gene-level analysis for neurogenic cells

Cell types involved in neurogenesis (OB neurons 1_11 and OB neurons 1_17) were aggregated together and pseudobulked by sum for each individual 12-month-old mouse using the decoupler package. Mice with fewer than 10 cells or fewer than 100 expression counts were removed. Total counts per mouse were normalized and genes with fewer than 40 counts per million were removed. The differentialGeneTest() function from Monocle2 was used to calculate the significance of differential expression between 12-month-old AL mice and 12-month-old CR mice for each gene. Upregulated genes in CR were defined as having a q-value <0.05 and a log-fold-change ≥1; downregulated genes in CR were defined as having a q-value <0.05 and a log-fold-change ≤ −1. Overrepresentation analysis was performed using Enrichr51,52 to identify biological processes from the GO Biological Process 2023 dataset that were significantly enriched in upregulated or downregulated genes.

IRISeq library construction and sequencing

The samples were processed using the IRISeq protocol.11 To capture RNA from tissues, a beads array is generated by mixing 5 μL of receiver beads and sender beads in a 6X SSC buffer in a 1:3 ratio, respectively. Then gel beads are monolayered into a square shape utilizing a gasket barrier of the desired shape and size, and semi-dried for a few minutes until their stabilization on the glass is observed, and no movement is observed. Then, 10 μm thick tissue sections are placed on the beads array, and RNA hybridization is performed for 13 min without additional buffer addition to prevent array disturbance. Next, photocleavage of sender beads is performed for 2 min in a UV device (alpha thera AT8001). Library preparation follows Slide-seqV2 protocol58 with slight modifications. Following second strand synthesis, cDNA was released from receiver beads utilizing 0.1 N NaOH and quenched with 1 M Tris-HCl pH 7.0, followed by 1X AMPure XP beads purification. cDNA amplification followed protocols58 utilizing SMRT primer and Truseq_Read1. Amplified cDNA is then tagmentated. For receiver beads-sender beads DNA interactions library preparation, after collecting supernatant for cDNA library preparation, beads were washed with TE-TW buffer (1X TE buffer with 0.01% Tween 20), and then resuspended in 25 μL of H2O. Then, DNA connections between sender and receiver beads were amplified by performing on-bead PCR and utilizing the NEBnext master mix. 0.25 μM of P5_Truseq_Read1 primers and indexed Read2 sequencing primers were utilized for PCR. After PCR, perform gel extraction of the connections band which should be approximately 250 bp in size.

Spatial data processing and spatial map reconstruction

The established IRISeq pipeline was used to process the data.11 To reconstruct the spatial location of the bead array, interaction counts between sender and receiver beads were filtered to remove low-quality interactions, applying a cutoff of 10 counts as the minimum threshold for considering a connection as real. From the filtered data, a matrix of sender-by-receiver bead counts was generated, then scaled and log1p transformed. This matrix was then subjected to PCA transformation, followed by UMAP analysis on a GPU using cuML, with parameters slightly adjusted from the IRISeq data to recreate the square-shaped array.
