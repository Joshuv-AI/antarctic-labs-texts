Performance of high-resolution PET detectors consisting of long GAGG slabs with different reflectors
Authors: Zhonghua Kuang, Samuel Mungai Kinyanjui, Ling Zhang, Ning Ren, Zheng Liu, Qiaolai Tan, Jianquan Huang, Zhanli Hu, Yongfeng Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Background

Positron emission tomography (PET) is a molecular imaging technique that is widely used in the early diagnosis of many major diseases and in biomedical research [1–3]. A high spatial resolution is required for PET scanners to detect the small lesions with high quantitative accuracy by reducing the partial volume effect [4, 5]. Depth of interaction (DOI) encoding PET detectors are essential for achieving uniformly high spatial resolution in PET scanners by reducing parallax errors, especially for small-animal and organ-specific PET scanners using detectors with a high aspect ratio of crystal length to width [6–13].

The main DOI encoding techniques in PET detectors include: (1) single-ended readout of pixelated scintillator crystal arrays using multiple layers of crystals with different decay times [14, 15] or inter-crystal reflector arrangements [16, 17]; (2) single-ended readout of pixelated scintillator crystal arrays with depth-dependent scintillation photon modulation [18–21]; (3) dual-ended readout of pixelated scintillator crystal arrays [22–24]; and (4) single-ended readout of monolithic crystals [25–29]. Detectors using single-ended readout of pixelated scintillator crystal arrays achieve DOI encoding capability at the cost of increased detector complexity and, to some extent, sacrificing other detector properties, such as energy and timing resolutions. Dual-ended readout detectors are capable of achieving high DOI resolution; however, their main disadvantage is the high detector cost, since two photodetectors are required for each detector module and the readout electronics are doubled. Monolithic scintillator detectors offer high sensitivity, low manufacturing cost, and inherent DOI encoding capability; however, the spatial resolution in the monolithic direction at the detector’s edge is severely degraded due to the reflection and absorption of scintillation photons at the edges. Additionally, spatial resolutions in both the monolithic and DOI directions degrade as the crystal thickness increases.

Semi-monolithic detectors, composed of scintillator slabs, can not only maintain the inherent DOI encoding capability of monolithic scintillator detectors but also significantly reduce edge effects by using long rectangular crystals. The position resolution in the segmented direction can be improved by employing thinner scintillator slabs. Due to these advantages, the development of PET detectors using semi-monolithic crystals has become a hot research area in recent years. Research has been conducted on optimizing slab thickness, surface treatment, inter-slab reflectors, and positioning methods [30–39]. These studies have primarily focused on lutetium yttrium oxyorthosilicate (LYSO) scintillator crystals, which are currently widely used in PET detectors. Table 1 shows the properties of the newly developed gadolinium aluminum gallium garnet (GAGG) scintillator and LYSO scintillator that are from the website of Epic Crystal Co. (Shanghai, China) [40]. Compared to LYSO scintillator, the main advantage of GAGG scintillator is the higher light output, enabling better energy resolution and a potentially higher three-dimensional positioning accuracy in semi-monolithic PET detectors. The disadvantages of GAGG scintillator are the lower detection efficiency, slower decay time and higher cost [41]. Many PET detectors based on GAGG scintillator have been investigated [42–45]. In this work, the performance of high-resolution PET detectors using long semi-monolithic GAGG scintillator slabs with different inter-slab reflectors is investigated for the first time.

Methods

Detector design

A photograph of the two semi-monolithic scintillator arrays with different inter-slab reflectors, along with a schematic of the semi-monolithic scintillator detector, is shown in Fig. 1. The parameters of the two detectors are described in detail in Table 2. The scintillator slabs of the two detectors were fabricated from GAGG scintillator with a light output of ~ 54,000 photons/MeV (GAGG-HL, Epic Crystal Co., Shanghai, China). Each semi-monolithic scintillator array consisted of 12 GAGG slabs measuring 0.96 ⋅ 56 ⋅ 10 mm3. Either an enhanced specular reflector (ESR) or a barium sulphate (BaSO4) reflector was inserted between the slabs and placed on the two outermost large faces of the slabs. The total thickness of each reflector layer, including the optical glue, was 0.08 mm for both detectors. The external dimensions of both scintillator arrays were 12.4 ⋅ 56 ⋅ 10 mm3. For both detectors, the scintillator slabs were polished on the two large surfaces and on the bottom surface that interfaces with the silicon photomultiplier (SiPM). Based on our previous work [30, 31, 33], the front and two end surfaces of the scintillator slabs of both detectors were unpolished and painted with black ink.

Each semi-monolithic detector was read out by a 4 ⋅ 16 SiPM array composed of Hamamatsu S14160-3050HS SiPMs. Each SiPM has a dimension of 3.4 ⋅ 3.4 mm2 and an active area of 3 ⋅ 3 mm2. The pitch size of the SiPM array is 3.65 mm. A row and column summing readout circuit was designed to compress the 64 SiPM outputs into 4 row and 16 column signals. A 14.5 ⋅ 58.4 ⋅ 1.5 mm3 glass light guide with two 0.2 mm wide and 1 mm deep cuts located 2.05 mm from the two long edges was inserted between the GAGG slabs and the SiPM array to improve edge slab identification. Optical grease was applied between the scintillator slabs and the light guide, as well as between the light guide and the SiPM array. The SiPM bias voltage was set to 44 V. The 20 signals were routed to a preamplifier board for initial amplification. A timing signal was subsequently generated by summing the four pre-amplified row signals. This timing signal together with a 50-mV threshold were fed into three high-speed comparators to produce three differential timing signal pairs. The three differential timing pairs and the 20 row and column energy signals of the test detector, together with one differential timing pair and one energy signal of the reference detector were processed by a 64-channel singles processing unit (SPU) that had been developed previously for the SIAT bPET scanner [7, 46, 47]. In the SPU, the energy signals first underwent shaping and amplification and the resulting waveforms were subsequently digitized by analog-to-digital converters (ADCs) at a sampling rate of 62.5 MHz. The digital signals were processed within a field programmable gate array (FPGA) to obtain the energy of the signal by summing 18 samples of the waveform and subtracting 4 samples of the baseline. Each differential timing pair was also digitized by a time-to-digital converter inside the FPGA to obtain a timestamp. The 20 energies and 3 timestamps of the semi-monolithic detector, along with the energy and timestamp of the reference detector, were saved as list-mode data. More details of the electronics can be found in our previous publications [33, 38].

Experimental setup

The experimental setup is similar to that used in our previous studies [33, 38] for semi-monolithic scintillator detectors using LYSO and will be briefly described here. For the flood histogram measurements, an 898 kBq 22Na point source (0.3 mm diameter) was placed 24 mm away from the semi-monolithic detector and data were acquired for 600 s in singles mode by uniformly irradiating the y-z plane of the semi-monolithic detector. For the y-position (monolithic direction), DOI, and energy resolution measurements, the same source was positioned in between the semi-monolithic detector and a coincidence reference detector used for electronic collimation. A 5 mm-thick tungsten collimator with a 1 mm-diameter hole was also inserted between the source and the semi-monolithic detector for mechanical collimation and to reduce the singles event rate of the semi-monolithic detector. The reference detector consisted of a single 1⋅1⋅20 mm3 LYSO crystal wrapped in Teflon tape and read out via an individual Hamamatsu S14160-3050HS SiPM pixel with an active area of 3.0 × 3.0 mm². Different (y, z) positions of the semi-monolithic detector were selectively irradiated by moving the tungsten collimator, point source, and the reference detector relative to the semi-monolithic detector using both electronic and mechanical collimation. Regarding both detectors, measurements were carried out at 27 × 5 positions. These positions starting from the (y, z) coordinate of (2 mm, 1 mm), with a step size of 2 mm in both the y and z directions, as depicted in Fig. 1b. For every position, the acquisition time was set to 600 s. All measurements were carried out inside a light-tight box, with the temperature around the detector maintained at approximately 14 °C.

Y-position resolution

Figure 6 shows the y-profiles of slab 6 at a depth of 5 mm for the two detectors, acquired using both the COG method and the squared COG method. Figure 7 shows the FWHM y-position resolutions obtained with both methods and averaged over all slabs across all measured (y, z) positions. The y-position resolution degrades toward both ends and depths farther away from the SiPM array. Detector 2 (ESR reflector) retains slightly better y-position resolution at both ends than detector 1 (BaSO4 reflector). The squared COG method significantly improves the y-position resolution, with more pronounced improvements at the detector ends. Averaged over all irradiation positions and slabs, the COG method provides y-position resolutions of 2.21 ± 0.77 mm for detector 1 and 2.16 ± 0.55 mm for detector 2, whereas the squared COG method provides y-position resolutions of 1.60 ± 0.32 mm for detector 1 and 1.59 ± 0.26 mm for detector 2.

Discussion and conclusion

Based on our previous experience in developing semi-monolithic scintillator PET detectors using LYSO scintillators [31, 38], the front surface and the two end surfaces of the scintillator slabs were left unpolished and painted black to improve both y-position resolution and DOI resolution. The detector using the ESR reflector exhibited a 30% lower but more uniform photopeak amplitude, 6% better energy resolution, and a 13% lower PVR in the flood histograms, while achieving similar y-position and DOI resolutions compared to the detector using the BaSO₄ reflector. The pronounced depth-dependent photopeak position variation observed in the detector with the BaSO₄ reflector is attributed to the diffuse nature of the BaSO₄ reflector, which poses greater challenges for energy calibration in a PET scanner. Thus, semi-monolithic scintillator detectors using the ESR reflector may be preferred for use in PET scanners.

The SiPM array employed in this study had large gaps between SiPM pixels (3.65 mm pitch and 3⋅3 mm2 active area), which results in degradation of the performance of the detector, as well as variation of the detector performance such as the y resolution obtained with the squared COG method as the DOI is close to the SiPM array. Future improvements in slab identification, as well as energy, y-position, and DOI resolutions, can be achieved by using a SiPM array with smaller inter-pixel gaps.

Figure 4 shows that the photopeak positions are not symmetrical for the right and left sides of the detector. This non-symmetry of the photopeak position may come from the difference in the transmission lengths of the column signals from the readout board to the pre-amplifier board, which results in a difference in the start timing of the waveform sampling. Fortunately, this small non-symmetry of the photopeak position does not cause any non-symmetry of the energy, y and DOI resolutions. The signal readout circuit of the 4 × 16 SiPM array needs to be optimized in the future to address this issue.

The GAGG scintillator utilized in this work [40] has a light output of ~ 54,000 photons/MeV, which is ~ 80% higher than that of the LYSO scintillator (~ 30,000 photons/MeV). However, due to the mismatch between the photon detection efficiency (PDE) of the Hamamatsu S14160-3050HS SiPMs employed in this work and the emission spectrum of the GAGG scintillator (as shown in Fig. 14), the higher light output of the GAGG scintillator did not translate to better detector performance compared to the detector using the LYSO scintillator [33]. The 3D positioning accuracy and energy resolution of the GAGG-based semi-monolithic scintillator PET detector can be further improved if SiPMs with higher photon detection efficiency at longer wavelengths (~ 520 nm) are developed in the future. In this work, only simple analytical positioning algorithms were used to compare the performance of simi-monolithic scintillator detectors using different inter-slab reflectors and scintillator materials. Both the y and DOI resolutions of the detectors could be further improved by applying more sophisticated positioning algorithms such as the least-square minimization, the maximum likelihood and machine learning [30, 32, 38].

In this work, poor CTRs of ~ 4.5 ns and ~ 3.3 ns were achieved for the semi-monolithic scintillator detectors using the GAGG and LYSO crystals, respectively. The poor CTR results resulted from the unoptimized timing pick-off circuit and the row/column summing readout method used in this work, rather than the semi-monolithic scintillator detector. Timing resolution of <300 ps has been obtained for semi-monolithic LYSO detectors from a previous work [50]. However, the GAGG scintillator is not suitable for TOF PET detectors due to its long decay time (~ 150 ns for GAGG and ~ 40 ns for LYSO). The GAGG scintillator has no self-radiation, which makes it suitable not only for PET detectors, but also for SPECT detectors.

In summary, two long semi-monolithic PET detectors, composed of GAGG scintillator slabs with dimensions of 0.96 ⋅ 56 ⋅ 10 mm3 and two different reflectors (ESR and BaSO4) were evaluated in this work. The two detectors clearly resolve 0.96 mm-thick scintillator slabs, achieve y-position resolutions of ~ 1.6 mm, DOI resolutions of ~ 2.3 mm, and energy resolutions of ~ 15%. These detectors are suitable for constructing small-animal and high-resolution organ-specific PET scanners.
