Background

Throughout its historical development, labour has been influenced by intensive medical care and developing technologies. Advances in obstetric procedures have made it possible to deliver the foetus abdominally when vaginal delivery is not possible. In addition, attempts have been made to control labour pain with anaesthetics, intervene in labour with assistive equipment or support contractions with synthetic oxytocin, allowing smooth vaginal delivery. However, these interventions have led to a shift away from the concept of natural birth, resulting in increased caesarean section and intervention birth rates. Pregnancy and labour are normal and physiological events that do not require any medical intervention under normal conditions [1–3].

The long-standing fascination with childbirth, which began at the dawn of humanity, is hidden in the primitive brain of women. That natural flow that has existed for millions of years is preserved, and it is released and guides the woman and her baby only when the time and opportunity are given [1, 4]. However, sharing of negative experiences during childbirth and showing painful birth images in the media can lead to a negative perception of birth in women. Thus, women characterise vaginal birth process as a painful, bloody, dangerous, tiring, and frightening event [5]. These perceptions and unrealistic expectations of women create fear and negatively affect their attitudes towards birth, which is essentially a physiological act [6]. In the case of fear in childbirth, the sympathetic system is activated, stress hormones are secreted, and the circulating blood goes to the organs involved in the body’s defence system. Because the uterus is not involved in the immune system, the arteries to the uterus constrict and less blood and oxygen are supplied to the uterus. However, blood and oxygen are vital for uterine muscles to function. With less blood and oxygen, the cervix tenses and hardens, causing prolonged labour. Low oxygen supply to the uterus leads to low oxygen supply to the baby and jeopardises labour. This cycle is called the fear–tension–pain cycle [7], which leads to the need for emergency caesarean section, prolonged labour, difficulty in pain management, increased intervention deliveries, increased preference for caesarean section, delays in postpartum mother–infant bonding, development of mother’s mental health issues, negative effects on breastfeeding, decreased birth satisfaction, and increased risk of postpartum haemorrhage, embolism, and infection [8–11]. The fear of childbirth can be prevented, and these negativities can be prevented by changing the perception of childbirth owing to the innate force that women have in this natural process. Positive childbirth perception could be disseminated through prenatal education services that nurses and/or midwives, as health professionals, can independently provide [12, 13]. However, each woman is unique, and everyone’s perception of childbirth is different. A standardised education cannot be expected to meet the needs of every woman at the same level. To provide an effective education service, determining the level of birth perception of each woman and providing a personalised education service specific to the individual according to her needs will lead to more beneficial results. Additionally, the literature showed no specific measurement tool that can measure the perception of childbirth. From this perspective, we will develop a scale to determine individuals’ perceptions towards childbirth more conveniently.

However, despite the growing recognition of the impact of childbirth perceptions on maternal outcomes, there is currently no specific psychometrically validated instrument designed to comprehensively assess women’s perceptions of childbirth. Existing studies have primarily focused on related constructs such as fear of childbirth, birth satisfaction, childbirth expectations, or attitudes toward mode of birth. Although these concepts are associated with childbirth perception, they do not directly evaluate how women perceive childbirth as a physiological, medical, emotional, and experiential process. As a result, healthcare professionals may have difficulty identifying individual perception patterns that influence women’s decisions, fears, expectations, and educational needs during pregnancy.

In clinical practice, prenatal education and counselling services are often delivered using standardized approaches. However, women differ considerably in their beliefs, expectations, and perceptions regarding childbirth. The absence of a dedicated assessment tool limits the ability of nurses, midwives, and other maternity care professionals to identify these differences and provide individualized support. A valid and reliable instrument that measures childbirth perception may facilitate early identification of women with negative perceptions, guide tailored antenatal interventions, and contribute to the promotion of positive birth experiences and informed decision-making. Therefore, the present study aimed to develop and psychometrically evaluate the Ahsun Birth Perception Scale (ABPS) for assessing women’s perceptions of childbirth.

Methods

Study design

This was a methodological, cross-sectional study.

Study sample

Data were collected between January and September 2023 in a university hospital in the western region of Turkey. In the literature, the recommended sample size for psychometric studies should be as follows: excellent, up to 1000; very good, up to 500; and good, 200–500 [14]. Because the number of items in the draft scale was 43, at least 430 pregnant women should be included the sample, and the study was completed with 635 pregnant women. The inclusion criteria were gestational age of > 14 weeks, not a high-risk pregnancy, no mental illness, and voluntarily agreeing to participate in the research. The exclusion criteria were not knowing Turkish and illiteracy.

Procedure

Development of candidate items

First, candidate items were generated based on an extensive review of the literature on childbirth perception, fear of childbirth, birth attitudes, birth experiences, medicalized birth, and natural birth philosophies [7, 8, 10, 12, 13]. Existing instruments related to childbirth beliefs, attitudes, fear, and perceptions, including the Wijma Delivery Expectancy/Experience Questionnaire (W-DEQ), Childbirth Attitudes Questionnaire (CAQ), Birth Attitudes Profile Scale (BAPS), Perception of Birth Scale (POBS), and Childbirth Perception Scale (CPS), were also reviewed during item development. Based on this review, an initial pool of 43 items was created to reflect two broad conceptual domains of childbirth perception: perceptions of childbirth as a physiological and natural process and perceptions of childbirth as a medicalized and intervention-oriented process. The candidate items were then preevaluated by an expert panel consisting of seven researchers.

The expert opinions were evaluated using the content validity index (CVI) based on the Polit and Beck technique. The content validity of each item was aimed to be at least 0.78, and items with low scores were revised in line with expert recommendations to improve clarity, wording, and comprehensibility [15, 16]. Subsequently, content validity was reassessed using feedback obtained from 10 experts.

Pilot test

After obtaining expert opinions, the revised draft scale was administered to 50 pregnant women as a pilot study to evaluate the comprehensibility, readability, and applicability of the items. Participants reported that the items were generally clear and easy to understand. Based on participant feedback, minor wording revisions were made to improve item clarity before the final administration of the scale. Subsequently, item–total correlations and Cronbach’s alpha coefficients were calculated. The scale was finalized by considering the pilot study findings, statistical analyses, and expert recommendations.

Data collection tools

Data collection was conducted face-to-face using the finalised Ahsun Birth Perception Scale (ABPS), Sociodemographic/Obstetrics Information Form, and Ministry of Health Risk Assessment Form.

ABPS

This scale consists of 43 candidate items created by the researchers as a result of literature review and expert panel consultations participated by seven experts. This scale was developed for this study.

The final version of the ABPS consists of 17 items and two subscales: Medical Birth Perception and Natural Birth Perception. All items are rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (“strongly disagree”) to 5 (“strongly agree”). Total scores range from 17 to 85, with higher scores indicating a more positive perception of childbirth.

Sociodemographic/obstetrics information form

This form contains 15 questions that determined the women’s sociodemographic/obstetrics characteristics, such as age, educational status, working status, number of births, and gestational age.

Ministry of Health Risk Assessment Form

This form, which is standardised by the Ministry of Health and used in all health institutions in our country, functions as a pregnancy risk assessment form consisting of three sections: obstetric history, current pregnancy, and medical history [17].

Data analysis

Data were analysed using the Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) 25 and AMOS version 21 statistical software packages. Data normality was determined using Shapiro–Wilk normality test. The coherence of expert opinions was analysed using CVI. The item–total score analysis and Pearson correlation coefficient were calculated to explain the relationship between the scores from the scale items and the total scale score. Factor analyses for the evaluation of construct validity were conducted using the Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin coefficient, Barlett sphericity test, exploratory factor analysis equivalent and variance explained, confirmatory factor analysis compatibility indices, and Hotelling T2 test for scale response bias. Significance was determined using p < 0.05.

Results

Sample characteristics

The mean age of the participants was 27.23 ± 4.53 years, and the mean gestational age was 30.65 ± 4.50 weeks. Additionally, all participants were married, 61.1% were high school graduates, 51.2% were unemployed, and 53.9% of the participants’ income was equal to their expenses. Moreover, 56% of the women were nulliparous, 58.7% had a caesarean delivery, 37.7% chose their own method of delivery, and 69.5% did not receive prenatal education (Table 1).

Validity

Content validity

To determine the content validity of the scale, 10 experts provided their opinions, and the CVI was calculated using the Polit and Beck technique. The item-based CVI ranged from 0.80 to 1.00, whereas the scale-based CVI was 0.95.

Construct validity

The construct validity of the scale was evaluated using exploratory and confirmatory factor analyses. For construct validity analyses, the sample was divided into two independent groups. Exploratory factor analysis was conducted using data from 435 pregnant women, whereas confirmatory factor analysis was performed using data from a separate sample of 200 pregnant women.

Exploratory factor analysis

In the exploratory factor analysis (EFA), the Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) coefficient was 0,827, and the Bartlett’s test result (χ2(406) = 14,309,743; p < 0,001) was significant.

After analysis of the results, the scale consisting of 43 items had a nine-factor structure in the first place, and we excluded items that showed overlapping (1, 2, 5, 15, 29, and 37), those that reduced the explained variance ratio (2, 3, 4, 31, and 35), and those not making sense in the theoretical dimensions (33, 41, and 38). Thus, the relevant measurement tool was a two-factor measurement tool consisting of 29 items. Based on the EFA results, the developed scale explains 62.534% of the total variability. The EFA also revealed that the scale’s factor load values ranged from 0.581 to 0.819.

Confirmatory factor analysis

In the confirmatory factor analysis (CFA), the chi-square degrees of freedom statistics (χ2/df) = 1,994, root mean square error approximation (RMSEA) = 0,075, comparative fit index (CFI) = 0,969, goodness fit index (GFI) = 0,870, adjusted goodness fit index (AGFI) = 0,829, non-normed fit index (NNFI) = 0,963, incremental fit index (IFI) = 0,969, and standardised root mean square residual (SRMR) = 0,029. Based on the CFA results, items with low factor loadings and low fit values were excluded. Based on the results of the structural equation modelling, a scale consisting of 17 items and two factors was obtained (p = 0,000). Correlations between subscales were 0.86, and p < 0.01, and the factor loadings of all items were between 0.676 and 0.937 (Fig. 1).

Reliability

Internal consistency analysis (Cronbach’s alpha, split-half, item–total correlation) and time invariance analysis (test–retest) were used to evaluate the reliability of the scale. Table 2 shows the item–total correlations calculated for the scale. The Cronbach’s alpha internal consistency reliability coefficient of the scale total was 0,976, and that of the subscales was 0.974and 0.944 for factors 1 and 2, respectively. Split-half reliability analysis was also used for internal consistency analysis. The single- and double-numbered items in the scale were divided into two halves, and the correlation coefficient between the two halves was 0.949 for the measurement tool. The Spearman Brown and Guttman split-half coefficients were 0.974 and 0.971, respectively. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of the first and second parts of the scale was 0.954 and 0.951, respectively. The test–retest technique was applied at 4-week intervals to evaluate time invariance. Based on the analysis results, a significant relationship and correlation (r = 0.970; p < 0.001) were found between the first and second measurements.

Hotelling’s T2 test

The response bias of the scale was analysed using the Hotelling T2 analysis method. The item averages were different, and no response bias was found (Hotelling T2 = 871.695; p = 0,000).

Scale features

The ABPS is a valid and reliable measurement tool consisting of two subscales and 17 items (Table 3). The first subdimension (medical birth perception) consists of items 2, 3, 5, 9, 11, 12, 13, 14, 16, and 17, and the second subdimension (natural birth perception) consists of items 1, 4, 6, 7, 8, 10, and 15. The scale is a five-point Likert-type scale, and the items forming the first subdimension should be coded in reverse. The positive birth perception of women increases as the score obtained from the scale increases. The minimum and maximum scores were 17 and 85, respectively.

Discussion

We developed ABPS in this study. The scale has 17 items and a two-factor structure.

Content and construct validity were used to analyse the scale’s validity. Both I-CVI and S-CVI values should be > 0,80 to verify whether there is agreement between experts’ opinions [15]. In our study, the I-CVI values of the items were between 0.80 and 1.00, and the S-CVI value of the scale was 0.95, which showed agreement among the experts and achievement of high degree of content validity.

The construct validity of the scale was analysed using EFA and CFA. To apply factor analysis in scale development studies, the KMO test is required to determine the adequacy of the sample, and Bartlett’s sphericity test is required to evaluate multivariate normal distribution of the data. In the literature, KMO value is recommended to be at least > 0.60 and Barlett’s sphericity test should be p < 0.05 level [18–20]. After study analysis, the KMO value was 0,827. Bartlett’s sphericity test results were determined as χ2(406) = 14309,743 at p < 0.001. Therefore, the data set was suitable for factor analysis; thus, the EFA was applied to the scale. In the determination of the factors of the items in the scale in EFA, factor loading value of each item should be > 0,30, and that the item with a high value under one factor should have a low factor loading in the other factor [21]. Factor loadings were defined as high if they are > 0,60, regardless of their sign, and moderate if they are between 0.30 and 0.59 [19, 22]. Based on the EFA results, the developed scale explained 71.560% of the total variability. When the factor loadings were analysed, the lowest and highest were 0.712 and 0.890 for factor 1, and 0.533 and 0.884 for factor 2, respectively. Accordingly, the contribution of the two identified factors to the total variance was sufficient, and the factors were compatible because the values confirmed the reference value ranges.

The CFA was used to analyse the two-factor model obtained in EFA. According to the literature, model fit indicators should have a χ2/df value of < 5, RMSEA of < 0.08, fit indexes (GFI, NNFI, and CFI) of > 0.90, and factor loads of all items of > 0.30. In addition, the CFA results were consistent with the criteria found in the literature. Because 17 items remained after the removal of the items from the structure obtained in EFA after CFA, EFA was conducted again to ensure and verify the subdimensions. Therefore, KMO:0,857 and Bartlett: χ2(406) = 7338.433; p < 0.01 were calculated, and its suitability for factor analysis was redetermined [18–20]. By applying principal component analysis and varimax rotation, revalidation was performed by ensuring that it was a two-factor and 17-item structure with an eigenvalue > 1.

The reliability of the scale was initially tested by calculating Cronbach’s α coefficient, a method that evaluates internal consistency. The closer the coefficient is to 1, the higher the internal consistency of the items in the scale [23]. In this study, Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of factor 1 and 2 subscales was 0.974 and 0.944, respectively. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of the whole scale was 0.976, which showed strong internal consistency. The split-half technique was also used to evaluate the internal consistency of the scale. The correlation between the two halves obtained randomly from the scale indicates whether the scale is a reliable measurement tool and is expected to be > 0.70 [24, 25]. In the study, the items were divided into two halves with odd and even ordinal numbers, and the correlation coefficient between the two halves was > 0,70 (r = 0.949) for the measurement tool, supporting high internal consistency. The method used to indicate the stability of a measurement tool is the test–retest. In the test–retest method, a period of at least two and at most six weeks between the two measurements and at least 30 individuals participating in the measurement are recommended. In the literature, a test–retest correlation coefficient of 0.80 is recommended [18, 26]. In this study, a significant relationship and correlation (r = 0.970; p < 0.001) was found between the test–retest measurements applied to 35 pregnant women four weeks apart. Hotelling’s T2 test was used to determine whether the questions were perceived with the same approach by women and whether the difficulty levels of the questions were equal [27, 28]. In the present study, Hotelling T2 test (Hotelling T2=871.695; p = 0.000) results showed that individuals answered the items according to their own opinions, and no response bias was found in the scales.

The ABPS may have important implications for clinical practice. By identifying women’s perceptions of childbirth during pregnancy, healthcare professionals can better recognize negative or maladaptive perceptions that may influence childbirth-related decisions, expectations, and experiences. The scale may support nurses, midwives, and other maternity care professionals in planning individualized antenatal education and counseling interventions tailored to women’s specific needs. Furthermore, the ABPS may serve as a useful tool for evaluating the effectiveness of interventions aimed at improving positive childbirth perceptions.

Future studies should examine the psychometric properties of the ABPS in different populations and cultural settings. Cross-cultural adaptation and validation studies would help determine the applicability of the scale beyond the Turkish context. In addition, longitudinal studies are needed to investigate how childbirth perceptions change throughout pregnancy and how these perceptions influence childbirth experiences and postpartum outcomes.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the data were collected from a single university hospital in the western region of Türkiye, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to different populations and settings. Second, all data were based on self-reports and may therefore be subject to response and social desirability biases. Third, although a large sample was used for psychometric analyses, the study included only pregnant women; therefore, further studies are needed to evaluate the scale in different populations and cultural contexts.

Conclusion

Because of the analysis, the ABPS was determined to be a valid and reliable measurement tool consisting of two subscales and 17 items. The ABPS can be used to determine women’s perceptions of the birth process in both pregnant women and women who plan to become pregnant. The ABPS is recommended to be used before prenatal education to provide individualised education specific to the needs of the person in prenatal education.
