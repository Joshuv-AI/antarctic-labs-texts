Privacy, Exploitation and Global Disease Surveillance: Can We Justly Prevent the Next Pandemic?
Authors: Anand Sergeant
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn light of the COVID-19 pandemic, global health organizations have called for the implementation of robust global disease surveillance systems to recognize and respond to emerging pathogens. These active surveillance technologies would have a significant global benefit by preventing the spread of current pandemics and informing future pandemic responses. In this paper, I examine the extent to which we can sacrifice individuals’ privacy through global disease surveillance in order to benefit current and future generations. First, I outline disease surveillance technologies and explain how disease surveillance would occur primarily in low-income, rural communities in the Global South. Next, I outline privacy-related harms that these individuals would experience as a result of disease surveillance. I argue that within our current distributional system for global health resources, pandemic surveillance would impose privacy-related burdens on marginalized communities, who would receive inadequate benefits from these programs. This is unfair because it exploits the worst off in order to benefit individuals in wealthy nations. I conclude that to justifiably implement global disease surveillance, we ought to adopt a ‘prioritarian’ approach to health distribution. To impose privacy-related burdens on the worst off, we must ensure that they benefit significantly.

Introduction

It is broadly agreed upon that global health systems were underprepared for the COVID-19 pandemic (Inzaule et al., 2021). Despite warning calls, health systems were underfunded and unable to respond as quickly as needed to the disease outbreak (World Health Organization, 2021). At the time of writing, the COVID-19 pandemic has killed over 7 million individuals, with 778 million confirmed cases—likely vast underestimates (WHO COVID-19 Dashboard, 2025). This is in addition to the detrimental impacts the pandemic has had on economies and individuals through disease control measures such as lockdowns (Micah et al., 2023).

The health, economic and social impacts of COVID-19 have prompted calls to implement more advanced global surveillance systems to monitor infectious diseases (Magouras et al., 2020; Hilderink and De Winter, 2021; Inzaule et al., 2021; World Health Organization, 2021; Worsley-Tonks et al., 2022). In theory, robust disease surveillance will help us better control outbreaks and learn about emerging pathogens in order to prevent and prepare for future pandemics. The World Health Organization’s (WHO) independent panel on pandemic prevention called for the ‘WHO to establish a new global system for surveillance (…) using state of the art digital tools to connect information centres around the world’ (WHO, 2021: 53). The panel contends that improved technological systems should be used to surveil public health threats, in order to prevent future pandemics.

The subject of this paper will be global disease surveillance systems, as referenced by the WHO panel. First, I describe the surveillance tools that have been proposed to better surveil zoonotic disease outbreaks and I explain how, in practice, this sort of surveillance would be conducted primarily within farming communities in low-income countries. Next, I discuss the value of privacy and outline privacy-related harms that these marginalized populations would risk experiencing through global disease surveillance programs. I argue that while imposing privacy-related harms on marginalized individuals to benefit global health interests would be unfair within our current global health distributional system, it may be justifiable within a distributional system grounded in prioritarianism, an approach to distributive justice that holds that it is more valuable to benefit individuals who are worse off. To place privacy-related burdens on already marginalized populations, we must ensure that these populations will significantly benefit from pandemic prevention systems.

Global Disease Surveillance

To identify emerging pathogens and control the spread of viruses, disease surveillance systems need to focus on high-risk areas of infectious disease outbreaks. It is well established that most emerging infectious diseases threatening humans, such as SARS-COV-2, are zoonotic in nature (Jones et al., 2008; Rosenthal et al., 2015; Hilderink and De Winter, 2021). As such, disease surveillance programs must target areas in which pathogens are likely to transfer from animals to humans (WHO, 2021; Worsley-Tonks et al., 2022).

Following the outbreak of COVID-19, one area of supposed pathogen transfer which received significant media attention was the ‘wet market’—a term used for fresh-food markets where animals are sold and slaughtered (Magouras et al., 2020; Lin et al., 2021; Naguib et al., 2021; Petrovan et al., 2021). Wet markets are possible spillover sites for the spread of zoonotic diseases due to the presence of live animals, unhygienic conditions and the close interaction between animals and humans (Lin et al., 2021). However, wet markets present merely the final node in a much broader network of zoonotic transmission within the global wildlife supply chain, and only a very small proportion of wet markets pose a significant risk to humans (Lin et al., 2021). In reality, the highest-risk areas for zoonotic spread, with well-documented and consistent outbreaks, occur in low and middle-income countries (LMICs) within rural areas with close contact between humans and wildlife (Inzaule et al., 2021; Worsley-Tonks et al., 2022). Jones et al.’s (2008) predictive model for infectious disease outbreaks determined that future infectious diseases were most likely to originate within wildlife populations, in lower-latitude regions such as tropical Africa, Asia and Latin America. This prediction has proved reliable with hundreds of disease outbreaks occurring in these settings in Africa every year, with remote rural areas in central Asia and South America also commonly experiencing emerging zoonoses (Inzaule et al., 2021; Worsley-Tonks et al., 2022).

Rural farming and hunting regions in LMICs are particularly high-risk for the spread of zoonotic pathogens for several reasons. First, these are regions that involve close contact between humans and wild animals due to geographical proximity and the high prevalence of wildlife hunting and farming (Magouras et al., 2020). Interactions between humans and wild animals can transmit infectious diseases, as seen with outbreaks of monkeypox and Ebola (Hilderwink and de Winter, 2021). These remote wildlife regions in LMICs are also at risk due to the impact of climate change and disruptions to biodiversity. Zoonotic transmission is amplified in regions that have experienced ecological disruption, such as deforestation and climate shifts, as species are forced into atypical interactions that promote pathogen transmission (Pongsiri et al., 2009). Communities living on the precipice of disrupted and changing environments are thus at particular risk, and many of these regions are situated within warm and humid climates, such as the rainforest, semiarid and grassland climates found in Africa, South America and central Asia. Finally, the risk of transmission in these regions is exacerbated by a lack of infrastructure to support public health measures and more stringent hygiene standards (Magouras et al., 2020). The confluence of poverty, ecological considerations and amplified human-wildlife interaction results in a high prevalence of emerging zoonoses, making these regions a prime target for global infectious disease surveillance.

Although these rural sites are at risk of infectious outbreaks, most of the surveillance in these areas is currently done through passive case detection, in which local physicians report cases to health officials. However, this system is inadequate as it overlooks large portions of the population, while also being slow and failing to provide real-time information about emerging diseases (Worsley-Tonks et al., 2022). Thus, to adequately recognize emerging pandemic threats, more active surveillance techniques are necessary.

Active disease surveillance technologies are growing quickly and may be able to provide timely, targeted surveillance to help public health agencies promptly recognize disease threats and control them before they become a global event. These technologies include the surveillance of mobile phones to track symptoms of humans and animals in remote areas; the use of electronic health records; the monitoring of individuals’ social media and internet searches to recognize outbreaks in real-time; the use of drones to map relationships between individuals and wildlife and active viral surveillance requiring specimen collection and analysis (Murray and Cohen, 2017; Poljak and Šterbenc, 2020; Inzaule et al., 2021; Worsley-Tonks et al., 2022; Pilipiec et al., 2023). Active disease surveillance also includes the rapidly developing field of genomic surveillance, wherein genetic sequencing tools can quickly analyse collected specimens to provide information on viruses’ characteristics and transmission patterns, which can be used to inform outbreak responses and vaccine development (Inzaule et al., 2021; Struelens et al. 2024).

Active disease surveillance mechanisms would collect data on individuals living in remote rural regions in LMICs to help global health partners better recognize and prevent infectious disease threats. This could have an enormous global benefit by mitigating the harms caused by infectious disease pandemics. If these technologies work well, we would have strong moral reasons in favour of implementing them.1 However, given how quickly these active viral surveillance mechanisms are being developed and implemented, it is urgently important that we analyse their moral justifiability. This is because, as I will discuss in the next section, imposing active surveillance on broad populations produces significant risk of infringing on individuals’ privacy and producing privacy-related harms.

The Value of Privacy and Privacy-related Harms

Despite considerable potential benefits, the implementation of surveillance technologies in LMICs would infringe on local individuals’ privacy. In this section, I discuss the value of privacy and outline some of the privacy-related harms that individuals being surveilled may face.

While it is debated whether privacy holds intrinsic value, it is widely agreed that much of the value that privacy contains is instrumental; privacy is valuable as a means to protect us against harm (Rachels, 1975; Moor, 1997). This might include protection against discriminatory or malicious use of personal data, the adverse consequences of private information becoming public or the embarrassment of publicly known information (Rachels, 1975). Privacy holds value by enabling individuals to avoid the negative consequences of personal information being publicly held.

Given that disease surveillance would collect and analyse personal data, individuals being surveilled using these technologies would be subject to various privacy risks. First, the personal information collected through disease surveillance could pose harms related to the misuse of individuals’ data. Pandemic surveillance systems would require cooperation between a central agency, such as the WHO, local and national governments and public health organizations, to implement and maintain surveillance systems (WHO Guidelines, 2017). While it is not clear who will have access to the data and where it will be stored, it is likely that governments will have significant involvement in the surveillance occurring on their own population. There are important concerns related to the storage of data in centralized state databases, because of the potential for data misuse from state authorities in controlling and discriminating against marginalized populations (Hendl and Roxanne, 2022; Schaefer and Ballantyne, 2022). If individuals in remote farming communities were determined to have increased risk of spreading zoonotic diseases, for instance, this information could be used to create legislation tailored to prevent specific farming practices that certain populations rely upon for economic stability. In fact, numerous calls for the banning of wild animal hunting have been made, despite the fact that these practices are extremely important to the livelihood of many local communities, both for subsistence and commercially (Hilderink and De Winter, 2021; Petrovan et al., 2021). Rather than investing in rural farming regions to promote healthier farming trades, governments may prefer to prevent outbreaks by restricting individuals’ freedom, outlawing subsistence practices and modifying important economic structures regarding people’s sale of, and interaction with, livestock. Governments may also misuse data to discriminate against marginalized populations in ways that are unrelated to the control of infectious diseases.

Not only is there a potential for data misuse, there is in fact a history of governments using the data of marginalized communities to further colonial goals of erasure and assimilation (Cormack and Kukutai, 2022; Hendl and Roxanne, 2022). Many marginalized communities, especially Indigenous populations, have had their data used by governments in an effort to exert colonial control (Cormack and Kukutai, 2022; Hendl and Roxanne, 2022). Data collected from surveilled Indigenous communities have been used to justify land dispossession, increased policing and harmful policy changes towards Indigenous peoples, often under the guise of increased safety and security (Cormack and Kukutai, 2022). It is very possible that marginalized communities being surveilled for infectious disease risk would be the target of such policies, as data portraying these communities as public health risks would provide a powerful political justification for governmental incursion into their lands and other forms of remedial discipline. Surveillance practices have also harmed many Indigenous groups’ ability to retain sacred community practices and traditional knowledge and has led to intergenerational distrust towards governments’ data practices (Hendl and Roxanne, 2022). It is thus unclear how willing marginalized rural communities would be to engage in these surveillance programs, and it is very possible that even if they do participate, there would be high levels of distrust and discomfort. Therefore, exacerbating historically grounded sentiments is another type of harm that could stem from privacy infringements.

One might argue that many of these privacy-related risks would not be big concerns if the proposed disease surveillance technologies were implemented and managed carefully—for instance, if affected communities and individuals were consulted regarding implementation, and the collected data were protected closely, anonymized and used in aggregate. However, individuals in the surveilled communities would still be subject to privacy-related harms; specifically, harms related to the responses to disease surveillance. Upon a detected outbreak, individuals in surveilled communities would be subjected to liberty-related burdens from imposed lockdowns and spread-reducing policies. These sorts of responses would also have economic impacts from the disruption of work and impose social and interpersonal harms, as seen during the COVID-19 pandemic. Economic harms would also include practices such as the culling of diseased animals without economic compensation to farmers, which currently occurs in some LMICs to control the spread of diseases (Goutard et al., 2015). In addition, disease outbreaks would require individual testing and specimen collection, which can be uncomfortable and time-consuming for individuals, and medical tests such as blood tests, which carry small risks of medical complications. It is of course possible that these burdens could be considered beneficial to surveilled communities in situations where the early detection and response to emerging pathogens prevent local morbidity and mortality. However, it is likely that many of these liberty-related harms would be placed on individuals unnecessarily, because technologies for disease detection can be over-predictive, which would result in a serious risk of false positives (Murray and Cohen, 2017). The difficulty in determining which potential pathogens and zoonotic interactions are actual threats to humans, and the likelihood of false positive testing, would subject individuals in surveilled communities to the harms of surveillance responses in many cases without benefit.

Importantly, privacy-related harms would be particularly problematic in the sorts of communities in which disease surveillance would likely occur. Individuals within rural communities of LMICs are already marginalized groups politically, which would decrease their ability to advocate for themselves and resist harmful changes recommended by global organizations and governments. Furthermore, enforcing responses such as quarantine—even when it is unclear whether they are necessary—would place additional economic burdens on these individuals, many of whom will already be burdened economically and for whom income loss would be particularly devastating. Finally, as much as pandemic prevention efforts may help communities to a degree by limiting the spread of viral zoonoses, it is likely that the people in remote regions in LMICs will not be a high priority in our current global health distributional system, as I will explain in the following section. Thus, despite taking on many of the burdens, these individuals will likely not receive many of the benefits of the surveillance programs they are subjected to.

Disease Surveillance and Justice

While pandemic surveillance systems will expose vulnerable communities to a host of privacy-related risks, these systems would have a massive global benefit through the prevention of widespread disease outbreaks. I now examine the extent to which the trade-off between these risks and benefits can be made.

It is clear that, in some cases, privacy trade-offs can be justifiable. Parker et al. (2020) argue that the scale of suffering prevented by technologies such as contact tracing provides a strong moral reason for privacy infringements. Should small infringements of privacy help save thousands of lives by tracking and preventing the spread of an infectious disease, the benefit of these gains likely outweighs the privacy-related harms. In some cases, privacy infringements can also enhance individuals’ autonomy by preventing liberty-reducing responses such as lockdowns (Parker et al., 2020). We might have a moral reason, then, to expose some individuals to privacy-related risks, as in the case of disease surveillance, in order to provide more widespread autonomy and health-related benefits by preventing pandemics.

In this paper, I assume that, at least in some cases, particularly when adequate protections are in place, privacy infringements for the prevention of pandemics can be morally justifiable for the reasons mentioned above. The focus of this paper, however, is on the distribution of the burdens and benefits of pandemic surveillance systems. Given that these systems will impose burdens on specific populations, I argue that in our current global health distributional system, pandemic disease surveillance would be unjust.

There are two important features to consider when it comes to our global health distribution system. First, wealthy nations spend substantially more on health-related goods. Broadly, global health spending comes from four sources: governments, out of pocket spending, prepaid private spending and development assistance for health (DAH) (Micah et al., 2023). DAH represents resources, often from government donations and charities, that are transferred to LMICs via organizations such as the WHO to improve health outcomes (Micah et al., 2023). However, DAH represents a tiny fraction of total health spending globally; in 2019, 43.1 billion of DAH was provided to LMICs, compared to the 9.2 trillion spent worldwide (Micah et al., 2023). LMICs rely substantially on development assistance, so health spending in LMICs is hundreds of times smaller than in high-income countries, who have significant national health budgets. During COVID-19, high-income countries spent 293 times more than low-income countries, and were much better equipped to manage the pandemic by dominating markets for vaccines and personal protective equipment (Tatar et al., 2021; Micah et al., 2023).

Second, our current model for global health spending is overwhelmingly utilitarian in nature (Sharp and Millum, 2018; Marseille and Kahn, 2019; Winters et al., 2020). In global health rationing, we rely on a maximizing ethos to dictate the allocation of resources (Winters et al., 2020). Economic efficiency in the form of cost-effectiveness analyses typically informs the distribution of global health resources from funds such as DAH, which means that resources are allocated to maximize benefit—often defined in terms of lives saved or Quality-Adjusted-Life-Years (Marseille and Kahn, 2019). The focus on efficiency results in the prioritization of diseases and contexts in which resources will be most effectively spent. Thus, dense urban settings in which resources can be distributed more effectively, and settings with robust health infrastructure who can implement health measures most effectively, are favoured. Remote, resource-poor settings are therefore a low-priority target when limited health resources are being distributed.

These two features of global health distribution—the enormous disparity between the rich and poor, and the utility-maximizing ethos of development assistance spending—have several implications for the use of global surveillance systems. Firstly, given the disparities in health resources between high and low-income nations, it is clear that high-income countries will be better equipped to spend on the health of their citizens during future pandemics. Thus, should a future pandemic occur in which countries can use the data obtained from surveillance systems to inform vaccine development and public health strategy, high-income countries are much more likely to benefit from this information. Secondly, given the utility-maximizing ethos of global health spending, the limited resources that are donated to low-income countries are likely to be allocated to larger urban centres with more distributional capacity—precisely the regions that individuals within remote rural communities will not be able to access. The combination of these factors means that the very individuals taking on surveillance-related burdens will not have access to many of the benefits of these programs during future pandemics.

This distribution is unfair. Under our current distributive system, marginalized individuals from remote LMIC communities would be asked to bear the majority of the privacy-related burdens of disease surveillance. However, these individuals would experience an extremely inequitable proportion of the benefits. This is exploitative: it amounts to taking unfair advantage of the vulnerability of the most politically and socially marginalized individuals in order to benefit the more privileged (Zwolinski et al., 2022). As Mayer (2007) explains, the fundamental wrong of exploitation is ‘a failure to benefit others as some norm of fairness requires’, which can consist of an absent, insufficient, or inauthentic benefit (142). In this case, wealthy nations would be exploiting surveilled communities because the amount that these communities would gain from surveillance programs is insufficient—there would exist ‘a disparity between price and value’ as thousands of low-income communities bear the burdens of surveillance year after year, without guarantee that they will benefit if a pandemic strikes (Mayer 2007: 144). These individuals would thus suffer a relative loss compared to what should be owed to them in a cooperative global system that is gaining at their expense. This is the epitome of an exploitative transaction (Mayer, 2007).2

If we, as a global community, are to commit to working together to prevent disease threats, we must ensure that we distribute the benefits and burdens of these systems equitably. Under our current system, global disease surveillance would entail placing further burdens on already burdened individuals in order to benefit well-off nations; it would exploit the worst off of society. This distribution of benefits and burdens is unfair towards communities participating in global surveillance programs.

A Prioritarian Solution

It has been acknowledged elsewhere that the implementation of global infectious disease surveillance in low-resource communities must be done equitably, given the burdens that this surveillance entails. In the 2017 WHO guidelines on ethical issues in public health surveillance, health surveillance is characterized as a moral obligation of countries, given its wide-ranging population health benefits. The guidelines, however, call for the ethical collection and dissemination of data. With respect to low-resource settings, this includes incorporating the concerns of local communities in the implementation of surveillance systems, avoiding the placement of unnecessary burdens on already vulnerable populations and ensuring adequate protection of collected data sets (WHO Guidelines, 2017).

While involving surveilled communities in decision-making and implementing protective measures is certainly important, the guidelines are less clear on how the benefits of global surveillance systems can be distributed equitably. The authors of the WHO report state that ‘equity sometimes requires that the most vulnerable people receive what may appear to be disproportionate resources: that is, the unfair distribution of risks requires additional resources to balance the scales’ (WHO Guidelines, 2017). However, the authors do not elaborate upon what exactly this entails, and the meaning of the disproportionate benefit is not reflected in the report’s 17 Guidelines. For instance, while Guideline 6 calls for the global community to support lower-income countries in implementing sustainable surveillance systems, it remains unclear how surveilled communities will be supported in receiving adequate benefit from such systems.

Over the remainder of the paper, I argue that the imposition of privacy-related harms could be justifiable in distributional systems in which the communities bearing privacy-related burdens would be adequately prioritized for the benefits produced by these systems. I contend that a ‘prioritarian’ approach to global health resource distribution would provide the most equitable solution to satisfy this end, and could justify the harms that global health surveillance would pose.

Prioritarianism is an approach to distributive justice that submits that it is more valuable to benefit individuals the worse off they are (Sharp and Millum, 2018; Nielsen, 2022). From a prioritarian perspective, benefiting individuals who are worst off holds intrinsic, equity-related value. This means that a just distribution would allocate resources in a manner which prioritizes those who are worst off, even if this requires sacrificing some of the aggregate well-being and even if it conflicts with efficiency considerations—which runs contrary to the utilitarian ethos of global health distribution. Similar to utilitarianism, prioritarian approaches maintain that inequality between individuals is not intrinsically wrong. However, unlike utilitarianism, prioritarianism holds that it is more valuable to increase the well-being of an individual who is worse off (Buchak, 2022). For instance, a prioritarian approach may deem it more valuable to provide moderate relief to an individual who is severely suffering than to more substantially benefit an individual who is already well-off (Otsuka, 2015). This is not because the individual who is worse off can be benefitted to a greater extent, or because it is easier to benefit them, but because benefitting an individual who is worse off is deemed more valuable under prioritarianism—so benefitting this individual should be weighed more heavily when determining how goods are distributed (Sharp and Millum, 2018). Thus, while prioritarianism may be considered a sub-form of utilitarianism, it involves added equity-related considerations by prioritizing the well-being of those who are suffering more significantly, which contrasts with our current global health distributional scheme. Various scholars have argued for prioritarian approaches to distributive justice within pandemic and digital health contexts (DeBruin et al., 2012; Winters et al., 2020; Nielsen, 2022). Since pandemics often severely affect the worst off, a prioritarian approach can prevent pre-existing inequities from being exacerbated.

Prioritarianism in Practice

Identifying the Worst Off

Implementing a prioritarian approach to distributive justice requires a definition of who constitutes the ‘worst off’. One of the most complete accounts of this question is provided by Sharp and Millum (2018). First, they propose, there is the question of time: should an individual’s current well-being matter when deciding who should be considered the ‘worst off’, or should individuals’ entire life be accounted for? Sharp and Millum (2018) contend that a justifiable prioritarian view should account for both lifetime disadvantage and current disadvantage. This is because when looking at an individual’s overall well-being, ‘a given amount of advantage or disadvantage matters equally regardless of when it occurs’, and disadvantages occurring at one time can be compensated by benefits at a different time in someone’s life (5). However, within acute contexts like an imminent pandemic, it is also important to consider who the worst-off populations are right now, as our responses to infectious disease threats have the potential to alleviate or exacerbate current suffering. This was seen during the COVID-19 pandemic, in which individuals with current disadvantages such as poverty, disability and comorbid health conditions were most severely impacted and required further protection from the disease, regardless of their lifetime levels of advantage. Ultimately, since both current and lifetime disadvantage matter, Sharp and Millum (2018) argue that the value of benefitting someone depends on both; thus, the lower a person’s lifetime advantage and the lower their advantage at a particular time, the worse off they are and the more it matters to benefit them.

Second, in identifying the worst off, we must decide on the currency that should be used to measure disadvantage. Within ‘health exceptionalism’, the only disadvantages that matter when determining the worst off are those relating to health (Sharp and Millum, 2018). This view is very common within global health distribution, and includes measures such as ‘Quality Adjusted Life Years (QALYs), in which the value of an intervention is solely based on the number of years of healthy life gained. However, while an individual’s health is clearly important to their level of well-being, there are many other ways a person, or group of people, can be disadvantaged, such as through poverty, lack of education or limited autonomy. These dimensions of advantage are unaccounted for within measures such as QALYs, yet they are crucial to consider within health policy to determine how a given health intervention impacts individuals’ ability to live better lives as a whole (Sharp and Millum, 2018). Ultimately, to provide a more complete account of disadvantage, Sharp and Millum (2018) argue that indices such as resources, opportunities, well-being and health tend to converge and can be captured by a multidimensional measure of poverty. One such measure is the Multidimensional Poverty Index (MPI), an index developed by the Oxford Poverty & Human Development Initiative and the UN, which includes factors such as individuals’ education levels, health and living standards within and across countries (Oxford Department of International Development, 2018; Sharp and Millum, 2018). This sort of indicator, if broadened and further adopted by local countries, could help provide a more holistic proxy for identifying which communities are the worst off, to help guide the distribution of global health resources.3

In the case of pandemic prevention, individuals in low-income, rural communities within LMICs would likely be among the worst off under this definition. Many of these individuals do not have quality access to healthcare, they live in poverty and they are disadvantaged by their remote geography. Examining the results of MPI 2023, the majority of the countries with the highest MPI index arise from Sub-Saharan Africa, South and East Asia and Latin America, which are high-risk regions of zoonotic spread requiring disease surveillance (Oxford Department of International Development, 2023). Moreover, it is clear from the report that rural regions within these countries experience significantly higher MPI indices, meaning much worse outcomes within health, poverty and education, compared to urban regions (Oxford Department of International Development, 2023). These individuals would be burdened even further through surveillance-related harms.4

A prioritarian approach to global health distribution would thus prioritize these individuals. While disease surveillance would still have to occur within these communities, given their exposure to zoonotic diseases, these communities would receive far more equitable access to the benefits from pandemic surveillance programs under a prioritarian distributional model. This would reduce their risk of being exploited by offering them a fairer benefit for the harm they incur.

Priority for What?

After identifying the worst off, we must determine which types of resources these communities should receive within the global health distributional system. As I’ve outlined, much of the global health resources spent in low-income countries arises from government donations and private charities in the form of DAH, which is then distributed by organizations such as the WHO to meet global health objectives. As a start, these funds should be included within the prioritarian distribution scheme. Prioritizing the most disadvantaged communities for funds aimed at health capacity building, preventative medicine and health infrastructure would help individuals in these communities receive higher-quality healthcare. Investment into the health of these communities would also have secondary benefits, such as economic improvements, by allowing individuals to work more consistently and more productively. This could help surveilled communities be better compensated for economic losses incurred by pandemic surveillance, even if this money could be more efficiently used elsewhere. Finally, given that many of these communities are also the highest risk for infectious disease spread, investing in these regions would contribute to what surveillance systems were designed for in the first place—to identify and control infectious disease threats as quickly as possible.

These principles extend to the prioritization of the worst-off communities in more acute situations, such as the distribution of health resources during an emerging pandemic. COVID-19 demonstrated that lower-resource countries were largely left to support themselves in the markets for vaccines, therapeutics and other public health goods such as personal protective equipment. When future pandemics arise, the worst-off communities should be better prioritized for these resources. Not only should this be the case for DAH funnelled through third-party organizations such as the WHO, but it will require more significant direct investments from well-off nations. Given that these nations will be sharing in the benefits of surveillance, they should provide support for those communities being asked to take on the burdens. Increased sharing and financial support for goods such as vaccines, therapeutics and medical equipment during infectious disease threats would reflect the fact that infectious diseases are not confined by borders and require global cooperation to manage.

A prioritarian distributional system would function most effectively in a collaborative global infectious disease system, in which countries work together to detect and respond to pandemics. At the moment, there is an inclination to spend the limited funds available for low-income countries as efficiently as possible, given the vast global demand for health assistance. It may be viewed as problematic to implement a prioritarian distributional scheme with funds that are already reserved for low-resource settings, because the increased resources given to the worst off would be taken away from other disadvantaged communities. Thus, ensuring that wealthier nations contribute more significantly to global health funds, particularly during situations of acuity, is crucial. This could be implemented by requiring all countries above a certain threshold of GDP who are benefitting from global disease surveillance to contribute a proportion of their public health budget towards a global pool, which could then be distributed to communities of priority. Alternatively, countries receiving data from the monitoring of disease threats in high-risk regions could be asked to enter an agreement in which they fund the dissemination of the corresponding vaccines and therapeutics to lower-resource settings prior to procuring these resources themselves.

Determining the Extent of Priority

Finally, the application of a prioritarian distribution system requires an answer to an even more difficult question—how much priority should the worst off receive in a global health distributional system? This determination requires making difficult trade-offs, for instance, between the extent that an intervention prioritizes the worst off and its effectiveness, as it is possible that prioritizing resource-poor, remote settings will divert resources away from denser regions with more robust health infrastructure, which could use them to help a greater number of individuals. This is especially a concern given the overall lack of resources available for low-income settings within global health. DAH provided by private charities and foreign governments accounts for much of the resources available for the distribution of health goods in LMICs, and it is limited. Even with increased investment from higher-income countries benefitting from the global surveillance system, the distribution of global health goods will not be comprehensive and choosing to benefit some populations will inherently detract from our ability to benefit others.

It is therefore important to develop a robust framework to help determine the extent of priority that the worst-off populations should receive. Here, I will outline one promising approach to the implementation of a prioritarian model for global health distribution, utilizing Multi-Criteria Decision Analysis (MCDA), as set out by Baltussen and Niessen (2006). While more work is needed on the question of extent, and it is out of the scope of this paper to provide a more complete framework, I argue that MCDA provides a realistic pathway towards an implementable and adaptable model for a prioritarian global health distributional system.

MCDA is an approach to decision-making that allows policy-makers to integrate numerous relevant decisional criteria (Baltussen and Niessen, 2006). Traditionally, priority-setting decisions within global health are guided by a single indicator, such as cost-effectiveness, burden of disease or equity value (Baltussen and Niessen, 2006). As I’ve described, the global health distributional system is often guided by utilitarian principles in the form of QALY analysis and cost-effectiveness to maximize health benefits in settings with limited resources. However, approaches like these are insufficient as they reduce complex decisions with numerous important considerations into a single isolated criterion. For instance, in the case of pandemic distribution, I have argued that equity considerations should be central to decisions regarding which interventions and populations to prioritize. Certain criteria like cost-effectiveness or burden of disease analysis, do not account for these concerns. Conversely, if an equity-related metric like poverty weres used alone to guide resource allocation, efficiency concerns would be neglected, which may be problematic during an acute threat such as a pandemic, which requires allocating limited resources effectively. Policy-makers may also want to concurrently prioritize certain age ranges, individuals with comorbid diseases, those sustaining surveillance burdens or those of lower socio-economic status. Doing so requires a more holistic decision-making system.

MCDA supports integrated priority-setting by incorporating decision-making criteria into a matrix to establish rank orders for a range of intervention options (Baltussen and Niessen, 2006). Once a set of decision-making criteria are determined, they are constructed into scales, with each criterion having weights assigned to it based on its relative importance. Each possible decision can thus be turned into a total score, helping determine which option is optimal based on the decided-upon weighting scheme. While traditionally, MCDA has been used to compare different types of interventions—such as different treatment options—to determine which ones should be funded, the principles of MCDA can also be applied to the comparison of different geographical regions (Thokala et al., 2016).

For example, suppose an intermediary for DAH funds such as the WHO is tasked with distributing global health funds for a primary care intervention involving the dissemination of insulin for diabetes management. To apply the MCDA model, they must determine what factors should matter when determining priority. Let us say that, in this case, policy-makers want to focus on individuals in the 30–50 age range, to prevent long-term complications of untreated diabetes. Furthermore, given the limited resources available, they would like to consider the cost-effectiveness of the intervention so the available resources can be distributed efficiently. Finally, it has been decided that the most disadvantaged communities should receive priority. Under MCDA, each of these criteria can be converted into a numerical score, with more preferred outcomes given higher values. For instance, if MPI is used to measure a community’s disadvantage, the MPI could be converted into a score between 1 and 100 based on how significantly the MPI of a given community differs from the mean. The cost-effectiveness score of the intervention could similarly be calculated for each of the possible geographic locations, and then converted into a value between 1 and 100 reflecting the cost effectiveness of a given location relative to the others. An equivalent score could be calculated for the preferred age range by measuring the relative proportion of individuals in this age range in each of the target communities.

Once scores are calculated for each of the three criteria, each criteria must be assigned a weight, depending on its importance to the priority-setting. Suppose that in this case, the cost-effectiveness and prioritarian considerations are deemed equally important, with the age targets being a lower priority for decision-makers. A weighting scale of 40-40-20 could be applied, with the cost-effectiveness score and disadvantage scores being multiplied by 0.4, and the age-fulfilment score being multiplied by 0.2. By adding up each of the three weighted values, each community could receive a total priority score, reflecting the extent to which they fulfil the three measures of a valuable intervention (Baltussen and Niessen, 2006). In this example, communities with both a high degree of disadvantage and with demographic characteristics most likely to benefit from the intervention would be highest on the rank-list of priority. This information can then be used by policy-makers to guide the proportion of funding supplied to these regions.

By altering the weighting of each of the criteria, different sorts of communities will be prioritized under the MCDA approach. For example, in the case of a more acute situation, in which limited vaccines for a newly arising epidemic must be distributed, it is possible that a priority-setting model should value greater efficiency and uptake given the imminent nature of the threat. Furthermore, it may be important to consider communities’ burden of disease to ensure that protective vaccines are provided to those in the greatest current need. The MCDA model may account for these considerations by increasing the weight that is placed on cost-efficiency or burden of disease in the model, sacrificing some extent of prioritarianism. This may be appropriate in acute settings due to the importance of rolling out the program quickly and efficiently in the face of an imminent health threat. Denser urban populations in large centres may be more cost-effective in this example as they would be easier to reach and less costly when considering the transportation costs and storage challenges with vaccinations in remote regions. Nonetheless, even by increasing the weight of utility characteristics, the incorporation of a measure such as MPI in the model would ensure that worse-off populations nonetheless remain prioritized. Should two geographical regions be similarly cost-effective, for instance, the worse-off region would be prioritized given the weight assigned to disadvantage within the MCDA model.

The MCDA approach, utilizing an indicator of disadvantage such as the MPI as one of the primary weighted criteria, represents a realistic pathway forwards for the implementation of a prioritarian distributional scheme. By incorporating a measure of disadvantage into the priority-setting matrix, the MCDA can ensure that a community’s level of disadvantage directly translates into the level of priority that they receive when making allocation decisions. This satisfies the core principle of prioritarianism by weighing benefits to the worst off more heavily. Furthermore, the MCDA model allows trade-offs against other indicators, such as cost-effectiveness or burden of disease, to be determined transparently, creating flexibility for the model depending on the type of resource and the level of acuity.

Determining the relative weighting of the criteria used in an MCDA model will require consultation with relevant stakeholders, such as community members, government officials, healthcare providers and policy-makers.5 The WHO guidelines on ethical issues in surveillance recommends incorporating the values of local communities when planning and implementing disease surveillance; this should also apply to the determination of fair priority-setting. Discrete choice experiments, in which respondents make choices between hypothetical alternatives, have been used previously to determine the relative importance of decision-making criteria in MCDA priority-setting models (Baltussen et al., 2006; Youngkong et al., 2010). For instance, Youngkong et al. (2010) asked three groups of stakeholders—policy-makers, people living with HIV/AIDS and village health volunteers—to complete questionnaires ranking priority-setting criteria for HIV/AIDS initiatives in Thailand, to determine how each criteria should be weighed. Scenarios asked participants to compare choices with varying demographic characteristics, intervention types (prevention vs treatment), effectiveness and quality of evidence, to understand what different stakeholders value, and in turn integrate these values into a unifying framework (Youngkong et al., 2010). Similar experiments more specific to prioritarianism have found that individuals are inclined to make prioritarian trade-offs rather than choosing standard health maximization choices (Ottersen et al., 2014). Using empirical research like this can help us understand the extent to which relevant stakeholders feel comfortable making trade-offs between priority and efficiency and may provide one pathway towards determining the weight that a community’s MPI should hold within MCDA. While the relevance of public opinion on normative theory is debatable, this sort of approach can at least provide a level of procedural justice by involving relevant stakeholders and providing transparency within the decision-making process (Baltussen and Niessen, 2006; Youngkong et al., 2010; Thokala et al., 2016). Prioritarian models like Cookson et al.'s (2022) , which are more mathematical in nature, may be seen as more objective applications of prioritarian theory, but applying these complex models may limit the ability for the public to understand the process behind important priority-setting decisions.

Objections

Several objections might be made against the proposal to prioritize the worst-off communities within global disease surveillance systems. One might argue that since the communities being surveilled face the greatest risk from zoonotic diseases, these communities will inherently benefit the most from disease surveillance, so surveilling them would not be exploitative. This argument is incorrect for three reasons. First, given that many emerging pathogens will be unfamiliar, it is not clear that once they are recognized, community members will be immediately protected. While temporary lockdowns may help to slow the spread, it would likely take time before a novel pathogen is understood well enough to be targeted. Many of the benefits of disease surveillance, such as vaccine production, would occur in the future, once a virus has been thoroughly studied. Moreover, given that thousands of communities would need to be surveilled year-upon-year for the emergence of potentially worrisome pathogens, it is unlikely that surveillance responses would provide proportional benefits to their burdens. The number of false-positive tests and responses to potential threats would likely outweigh the number of times an actual threat is curbed by such protocols, because while benign infectious diseases are common, the number of truly harmful zoonotic threats are relatively rare. Finally, the mere fact that an individual benefits from a transaction does not prevent the relationship from being exploitative (Mayer, 2007; Zwolinski et al., 2022). What makes a relationship exploitative is whether an individual is ‘worse off than she should have been, had she been treated fairly’ (Zwolinski et al., 2022). In the global distribution of burdens and benefits, the limited benefits surveilled communities would enjoy are unfair and insufficient considering the privacy-related burdens they would have to endure over time. This makes the relationship exploitative, regardless of the potential absolute benefit.

Secondly, it could be argued that the potential benefits from preventing a global pandemic are so great that small injustices in the distribution of benefits and burdens are justifiable, even if this requires the exploitation of marginalized communities. However, if one is solely concerned with the benefits that can be produced by disease surveillance, there remain consequentialist reasons for adopting a prioritarian distribution system. Individuals who are the worst off can often benefit the most compared to other individuals, so prioritarian distributions can overlap with utility-related concerns (Nielsen, 2022). Furthermore, surveillance systems would function much more effectively under an equitable global health system, because individuals in surveilled communities would be more willing to cooperate. Disease surveillance systems would require significant cooperation from participating communities through periodic disease testing, the implementation of surveillance technology and compliance with control measures. This sort of cooperation would require individuals to trust those implementing the programs, which is far more likely if individuals in surveilled communities believe that they are being treated fairly. Thus, an equitable process for global health distribution is necessary to ensure the utility of disease surveillance programs.

Conclusion

Infectious disease surveillance has the potential to substantially reduce pandemic-related suffering for current and future generations. However, within our current global health distributional system it is very likely that the implementation of disease surveillance would be unjust; it would require already burdened populations to take on further burdens for the benefit of more privileged nations. We ought to pay closer attention to the way in which global health resources are distributed in order to justify imposing burdens on marginalized populations in the name of public health. A prioritarian approach to global health distribution provides a promising path forward for implementing globally beneficial systems in a way that is not exploitative towards those who already disproportionately bear the world’s economic, health and environmental problems.
