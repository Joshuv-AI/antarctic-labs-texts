Immunologic desensitization to nilotinib in a patient with chronic myeloid leukemia with hypersensitivity and complex comorbidities
Authors: Triaji Suryo Condro Widagdo, Deasy Fetarayani, Wita Kartika Nurani, Ari Baskoro, Gatot Soegiarto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDesensitization to nilotinib offers a viable solution for patients with chronic myeloid leukemia who are experiencing hypersensitivity, thus enabling continuation of targeted therapy. This approach supports molecular remission even in the presence of complex metabolic and neurologic comorbidities.Key words: Chronic myeloid leukemia, nilotinib, drug hypersensitivity, desensitization

Nilotinib is a second-generation tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI) that is widely used as a second-line therapy in patients with chronic-phase chronic myeloid leukemia (CML), particularly when the response to imatinib is suboptimal or intolerance occurs.1 Previous studies have reported that approximately 71% of patients with CML who were receiving nilotinib achieved a major molecular response (MMR) within 12 months, compared with only 41% of patients in the group receiving imatinib.2 However, nilotinib’s effectiveness may be hindered by potentially serious hypersensitivity reactions, including rash, pruritus, and eosinophilia, which have been reported in approximately 15% to 20% of adult patients with CML.3 This challenge becomes more complex in patients with metabolic comorbidities such as type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), as nilotinib has also been associated with an increased risk of hyperglycemia and insulin resistance.4 In this context, drug desensitization emerges as a highly relevant yet rarely reported strategy, particularly in Southeast Asia.5 This case report presents evidence of successful nilotinib desensitization in a patient with CML with multiple comorbidities, including T2DM, Parkinson disease, and nephrolithiasis, demonstrating that with a stepwise protocol and close clinical monitoring, targeted therapy can be safely and effectively continued. These findings also highlight the potential for adapting international protocols to more contextually relevant regional practice.

Case presentation

A 49-year-old man presented with a diagnosis of chronic-phase CML. His medical history was notable for multiple comorbidities, including T2DM, Parkinson disease, chronic gout, and nephrolithiasis with a history of 3 sessions of extracorporeal shock wave lithotripsy. Initial CML therapy with hydroxyurea and imatinib failed to elicit an adequate response, prompting a switch to second-line treatment with nilotinib.

On the third day of nilotinib administration, the patient developed cutaneous rash, nausea, vomiting, and right testicular pain that were suspected to be drug-related adverse effects. Physical examination revealed a positive Phren sign and cremasteric reflex without scrotal edema or erythema. Laboratory findings at that time showed a leukocyte count of 9.85/mm3, platelet count of 578,000/mm3, hemoglobin level of 12.8 g/dL, serum creatinine level of 1.4 mg/dL, and random blood glucose levels ranging from 144 to 198 mg/dL. Quantitative BCR-ABL examination demonstrated a markedly elevated level of 44.26%, indicating significant disease activity.

Given the hypersensitivity reaction to nilotinib, a stepwise desensitization protocol was initiated, starting at 30 mg and escalating to 45 mg and 60 mg over the first 3 days. To minimize the risk of recurrence, premedication with an antihistamine (cetirizine) and a corticosteroid (methylprednisolone) was administered. Once clinically stable, the patient was discharged and continued treatment with an outpatient dose escalation regimen as follows: 150 mg once daily in the first month, 150 mg twice daily in the second month, and 300 mg daily in the third month. Throughout this process, no recurrent allergic reactions were observed, and the patient’s gastrointestinal symptoms gradually improved.

Molecular evaluation at 3 months demonstrated a reduction in BCR-ABL levels to 8%, indicating an early positive therapeutic response. Glycemic control was maintained through insulin aspart and degludec, along with dietary adjustments as recommended by endocrinology. Other comorbidities, including Parkinson disease and gout, remained stable under ongoing pharmacologic management, without exacerbations during therapy. At the 6-month follow-up examination after desensitization, the patient’s BCR-ABL levels had declined significantly to 0.03%, consistent with an MMR. These results demonstrate the medium-term success of targeted therapy and support the continued use of nilotinib as a safe and effective treatment option, even in patients with multiple comorbidities and a history of hypersensitivity.

Discussion

CML is a myeloproliferative neoplasm characterized by the t(9;22)(q34;q11) translocation, resulting in the Philadelphia chromosome and the oncogenic BCR-ABL1 fusion gene. Constitutive activation of the BCR-ABL1 tyrosine kinase drives uncontrolled proliferation of myeloid cells, making it a principal target for molecular therapy.1,6 Nilotinib, a second-generation TKI, exhibits higher affinity for BCR-ABL than imatinib does and has been shown to induce a more rapid and deeper MMR in patients with chronic-phase CML.7

However, the effectiveness of nilotinib may be limited by hypersensitivity reactions, which are reported in 10% to 15% of patients, with manifestations including rash, pruritus, and gastrointestinal symptoms.3 In the present case, the patient developed cutaneous reactions and gastrointestinal intolerance within 3 days of initiating nilotinib treatment, necessitating temporary discontinuation and implementation of a desensitization protocol. Although not yet established as standard practice, this approach has been reported to improve tolerance to TKIs in patients with a history of nonanaphylactic drug allergy.8

Stepwise desensitization in this patient achieved clinical success, as evidenced by a reduction in BCR-ABL levels from 44.26% to 0.03% over 6 months, meeting the criteria for MMR. This outcome supports previous findings indicating that TKI desensitization can be performed safely with premedication and gradual dose escalation, particularly in patients without equivalent therapeutic alternatives.8

Metabolic comorbidities such as T2DM add further complexity to treatment, as nilotinib is known to impair glucose homeostasis through inhibition of the phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K)/protein kinase B (AKT) pathway, which plays a key role in insulin sensitivity.4 In this patient, glycemic control was maintained with basal-bolus insulin therapy and daily blood glucose monitoring. Several studies have shown that patients with T2DM who are receiving nilotinib have a higher risk of hyperglycemia and require active adjustment of antidiabetic regimens.4,9

Overall, this case report underscores the fact that even in the presence of multiple comorbidities, nilotinib desensitization can be an effective and safe strategy in patients with CML with early intolerance. This approach enables continuation of targeted therapy and achievement of an optimal molecular response while contributing to the development of regionally relevant adaptive protocols, particularly in Southeast Asia.
