Storying syndemics: understanding migrant women’s experiences of diabetes and distress through narratives of self-care
Authors: Sumaira Khan, Catherine Trundle, Tarryn Phillips, Natalie Araújo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMigrant women face disproportionate rates of co-occurring type 2 diabetes and distress globally. Biomedicine’s individualized care model for people with diabetes conceptualizes distress as an added comorbidity to be managed. However, this negates the ways in which diabetes and distress may be interwoven with complex forms of social suffering at the confluence of gender, socio-economic status, racialized identity, chronic illness, and migration, and experienced as a co-occurrence of multiple illnesses. Anthropologists have proposed the syndemic framework as an alternative to “comorbidity” in order to capture the synergistic interactions between diseases that are shaped by social inequalities. This study extends the syndemic framework through centering the life narratives of migrant women at the nexus of diabetes and distress.MethodsThis study draws from a year of ethnographic fieldwork conducted in 2024. We utilized an ethnographic approach to conduct in-depth, participant-led interviews with 20 South Asian migrant women living with diabetes in Melbourne, Australia.ResultsParticipants followed the prescribed diabetes self-care advice given to them through their interactions with the healthcare system in Australia. Yet in order to navigate distress, they moved beyond biomedical knowledge and interactions with the healthcare system. We identified four key ways in which participants located and articulated the relationship between diabetes and distress, including: 1) The process of becoming a migrant woman and making sense of emotions, 2) Losing relational ties as a result of distance, death, and familial discord, 3) The stressful and often gendered juggle between domestic chores and professional work, and 4) Navigating food within fractured socialities and identities.ConclusionWe argue that a narrative understanding of how diabetes and distress intersect reveals the complex ways in which relational processes shape disease co-occurrence in contexts of migration and social inequality. Ethnographic attention to life narratives offers new insights into the nuances of syndemic interactions between diabetes and distress, which are especially relevant for policymakers and clinicians working with migrant individuals.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-26956-7.Keywords: Migrant mental health, Storytelling, Diabetes, Illness narratives, Self-care, Social syndemics

Introduction

Background

Migrants experience an elevated risk of co-occurring diabetes and mental health distress compared to non-migrants in their adopted countries [1, 2]. Along with biological mechanisms that interlink diabetes and depression [3, 4], this co-occurrence can partly be explained by migrants facing structural, sociocultural, and psychological stressors that intersect and interact with diabetes over time. These stressors may include factors like the loss of social status and social support upon migration, cultural alienation, language barriers, facing discrimination, unemployment and difficult work conditions, as well as limited prospects for upward social mobility [5, 6].

In this context, a particularly vulnerable migrant community is South Asian migrants, for whom diabetes is a major public health concern. Global estimates suggest that diabetes has the fastest growing prevalence among South Asians, with an estimated increase by over 151 per cent between the years 2000 and 2030 [7]. Further, among South Asians, the epidemic is estimated to affect over 134 million Indians by the year 2045 [8].

Moreover, a significant proportion of South Asians who move as migrants to high-income western countries remain at an elevated risk of developing type-2 diabetes mellitus (henceforth, diabetes) compared to the general population [9]. Post-migration, the South Asian diasporic population is noted to encounter a challenging sociocultural, emotional and nutritional landscape in navigating diabetes self-care, which unsurprisingly also impacts their mental wellbeing [10–13].

For South Asian migrant women, these challenges are multifold as they live with the double burden of being at high risk for both diabetes and gestational diabetes during pregnancy [14]. Further, women also experience the gendered dimensions of life stressors that accompany migration. These may include social and relational problems like losing family support, the unequal burdens of care-work and transformed gendered expectations in settlement communities, stress and depression stemming from divorce, separation or bereavement, and facing interpersonal abuse at home [15].

South Asian migrant women with diabetes in Australia

Qualitative public health literature in Australia posits that there is a lack of culturally appropriate care and dietary advice for South Asian migrant women with diabetes in the Australian healthcare system [13, 14, 16–18]. This can be particularly evident during certain life stages. For example, there are sociocultural differences between ideas of rest and physical exercise during and after pregnancy for these women, and they are also vulnerable to post-partum depression [14, 16, 17, 19]. Medical practitioners have noted that South Asian migrant women’s experiences, such as lacking family support after giving birth as well as feelings of isolation and loneliness, impact their attendance for Glucose Tolerance Test (GTT) postpartum [17]. Further, women also report being confused and dissatisfied by lifestyle modification and nutrition advice given to them by Australian health practitioners [14]. Psychological distress around nutrition, emotional responses to diagnosis, and lack of emotional support from family members for this group has also been captured in other high-income western contexts [12, 20].

Gap in public health literature

In line with such research, biomedicine and public health have sought to develop integrated psychosocial care models for co-occurring diabetes and distress [21–23] which are important for migrant communities and within them, vulnerable sub-sections like migrant women. However, a majority of biomedical and public health studies continue to focus on quantitative models that examine risk factors and the genetic and biological pathways that link depression and diabetes [24–26]. The way that distress (as a wider category that includes depression) and diabetes are intertwined and embedded within the sociocultural and emotional context of people’s lives remains overlooked. This is largely because biomedicine’s individualized diabetes care model still considers the symptoms of distress as an added comorbidity to be managed, where the context of diabetic individuals’ lives remains invisible [27].

Moreover, there is increasing recognition of the need to respond to the linguistic, social, and cultural dimensions of healthcare and wellbeing, and to offer nuanced approaches to understand the multiple and intersecting barriers. This is especially the case for migrant populations, for whom resources tend to focus on resource availability, health literacy, and linguistic translation and interpretation [28–30]. This has the effect of flattening the migrant experience and ignoring the “the multiplicity and fluidity of migrant identities” and how they interact with the lived experience of diabetes ([31], p.5). As a result of this, the ways in which diabetes and distress may be interwoven with complex forms of social suffering, such as at the confluence of gender, chronic illness, and migration in the sociocultural and emotional context of individuals’ lives, has not been extensively studied.

Building on a syndemic approach: diabetes, distress, and social suffering in anthropology

Speaking to this gap, research in medical anthropology has long considered the impact of social suffering in highlighting the links between diabetes and distress [32]. Researchers have examined lay perspectives on the interconnections between stress and diabetes. In particular, there is a body of literature linking this connection to the stressful life experiences of socially disadvantaged groups, such as migrant women, Indigenous communities and lower-caste groups [33–36].

More recently, anthropologists have proposed the syndemic framework as an alternative to “comorbidity” in order to capture the synergistic interactions between diseases that are shaped by inequalities [37–39]. Cautioning against the use of syndemics as “a gloss for comorbidity or multi-morbidity in biomedicine and public health” ([37], p.2), Mendenhall developed the concept of syndemic suffering. Building on syndemic theory that has extended work on the social context of disease clustering and interaction [39], Mendenhall captured the lived experience of syndemic suffering in her ethnographic work with Mexican migrant women to “reveal how social and cultural factors shape individual-level experiences of co-occurring diseases” ([37], p.2).

Through ethnographic research using the syndemic framework among first and second generation, low-income Mexican migrant women in urban United States, Mendenhall coined the VIDDA syndemic [37, 40]. In her framework, VIDDA refers to the complex interaction of Violence, Immigration, Depression, Diabetes, and Abuse that animates the lives of socio-economically disadvantaged Mexican migrant women and works to shape and worsen the loop of diabetes and depression as well as the social suffering in these women’s lives. Building on this research, more recently, through ethnographic work with Indian women who live with diabetes in New Delhi, India, Weaver [41] has elucidated how the ‘one-disease-plus-one-disease’ biomedical framework for comorbid diseases betrays the complex suffering that accompanies living with an overlap of multiple chronic illnesses. As Weaver [41] elaborates, comorbid suffering has complex interactions with multiple areas of life including gendered family dynamics and the everyday social suffering that emerges from encountering structural barriers like access to preventative care and medications.

As Mendenhall reflects on dissolving the boundaries between diabetes and trauma: “Without recognizing the intimate, personal scars from social trauma and violence — which are often never cared for, despite its crippling impact on people’s lives — the physical scars cannot recover” ([42], P.657). Thus, building on the framework of syndemic suffering, through extensive ethnographic fieldwork in other contexts of vulnerability, Mendenhall and Weaver have challenged the idea of diabetes being an endpoint diagnosis, or that the connection between diabetes with depression is merely a comorbidity. Instead, they have illustrated that diabetes and depression are part of a syndemic, biosocial feedback loop, or causes and effects of social trauma that results in a stressful life [37, 38].

While these are important contributions, as has been recognized elsewhere, there is also a need to expand the framing of syndemic theory to include social phenomena that perpetuate social stress as themselves constituting an epidemic that may be interacting with diseases in meaningful ways [43, 44]. For example, social phenomena such as gendered experiences, racialized identity and migration status can themselves have a socially syndemic relationship with diseases, instead of only being understood as risk factors and social drivers that co-constitute and worsen the interactions of biological diseases. Building on this literature, this paper examines how South Asian migrant women living with diabetes in Australia themselves understand these relationships.

Methods

Theoretical framework: the experiential dimensions of syndemics and ethnographic storytelling

In focusing on the experiential dimensions of our participants’ lives, we analytically attended to the moments where diabetes and distress interacted in their narrative accounts. Our use of syndemic theory was inspired by how Weaver and Mendenhall [38] approached the analysis of co-mingled narratives of depression and diabetes in their case-studies in which VIDDA syndemic analysis was used as a framework “for conceptualizing the broad social forces shaping the distribution of each disease, through which individualized narratives of social, psychological, and physical distress can be understood” [38], p.96).

Since syndemic interactions can drastically vary between local contexts [45, 46], qualitative social sciences studies “are uniquely suited to exploring how those who are affected by the syndemics experience and make sense of syndemic-forming health and social conditions” ([46], p.415). In this vein, we are not focused upon illuminating the biological mechanisms through which illnesses cluster per se. Rather, we focus on how participants, when given the opportunity to lead the direction of research interviews, independently identify the clustering of illnesses and how they make sense of it on their own linguistic, experiential, and conceptual terms and respond to it in their everyday lives. This includes, especially, how people themselves understand the interwoven nature of diabetes and distress and how they interact with structural factors like migration and marginalization through gendered, racialized, ethnic, and linguistic lenses. Life narratives are crucial to this exercise: as stated by Paul Farmer, “the experience of suffering, it is often noted, is not effectively conveyed by statistics or graphs. The “texture” of dire affliction is perhaps best felt in the gritty details of biography” ([47], p.262–263).

Attention to life narratives is not only important for sense-making but also has practical and policy implications for caregiving and receiving [48]. Especially in a neoliberal framework, emotional and relational self-care is often relegated to a space outside of the biomedical model and is not considered central to the ways in which diseases are understood [49]. As the results below indicate, how women conceptualize and practice self-care in the context of migration and diabetes diagnosis tells us a lot about how they understand and experience the syndemic relationship between diabetes and distress.

Methodology and study design

This study is part of Author 1’s ongoing ethnographic doctoral research project aimed at understanding diabetes self-care among South Asian migrant women living in Melbourne, Australia. We have employed a social constructionist perspective in understanding the participants’ narratives. Narratives are meaning-making tools that participants and researchers co-construct and use to understand, reframe, and recalibrate their illness experiences, their social and emotional worlds, and their identities as migrants. The project employs an ethnographic approach to health research in which fieldwork, unstructured participant-led interviewing, writing, thinking, and communicating are conceived as a collaborative, co-learning experience in which the human relationship between researcher and participant is centered [50–51]. The study design is rooted in an exploration of the sociocultural and emotional context of the participants’ lives. Their stories of migration, illness, and self-care are understood “on their own terms and in their own words” ([51], p.2). Hence, the study follows an interpretative methodological paradigm in which conversations and data generated are co-created, subjective, and embedded in the context of participants’ lives. Continuous reflexive journaling allowed Author 1 to reflect upon and explore how her complex insider status (Hindi and Urdu speaking South Asian migrant woman) and outsider status (younger, unmarried and non-diabetic researcher) shaped her positionality and her research encounters. Notably, her shared positionalities opened space for vulnerability, intimacy and trust in the sharing of emotionally resonant experiences [52].

Selection criteria, sampling, and recruitment

Our selection criteria included first, second or third generation South Asian migrant women living with diabetes in Melbourne, Australia. While South Asia constitutes one of the most linguistically diverse regions globally, the participants in this study were restricted to migrant women who spoke in Hindi, Urdu and English as Author 1 is well versed in these three languages. Four women also spoke different South Indian languages. Conversing in a mix of Hindi, Urdu and English let participants be understood in their own voice and helped to maintain linguistic consistency for the purpose of analysis. Further, participants were women who had been diagnosed with diabetes at least within the last 6 months before the date of the interview. The sample was purposive and iteratively snowballed. We aimed to ensure diversity of age and education. Project flyers were distributed across diabetes clinics in the suburbs of Melbourne with a high number of South Asian migrants. Flyers were also sent through e-mail to key informants such as South Asian dieticians and researchers in Melbourne working with migrant communities. Further, a study description with an invitation to participate in the form of text messages was circulated in some WhatsApp support groups for South Asian migrant women living with diabetes in Melbourne. Author 1 conducted all the interviews and sought both written and verbal consent to conduct, record and publish the findings of the study, in line with her human research ethics approval.

Data collection

Interviews utilized open-ended prompts on the following themes: a) Initial life experiences with migration and settling in, b) Daily life experiences with diabetes well-being, c) Experiences with family and social support, d) Experiences with the healthcare system, and e) Reflections on living as South Asian migrant women with diabetes in Australia. The interview guide was developed by Author 1 with supervisory guidance from Authors 2, 3, and 4 (Supplementary Appendix). On average, the interviews lasted between 45 min and 90 min. Interviews were conducted in participants’ homes, allowing participants comfort and control. Following an inductive approach, participants were encouraged and empowered to steer the conversations as they wished and Author 1 adapted the prompts to follow their stories. The words ‘syndemic’, ‘cluster’ or ‘interaction’ were not used by the interviewer, and neither was the relationship between diabetes and distress directly probed or even anticipated. Rather, participants raised these themselves with surprising salience using their own linguistic expressions. This kind of openness created a space for the participants to craft and express their self-narratives in their own ways. Moreover, this was especially beneficial in generating narratives in which participants moved beyond western biomedical categories of health and illness.

Data analysis

As Hammersely [53] notes, while ethnography as a methodological approach does not have clear definitions, it comprises the ethnographer’s open-ended, explorative orientation to deeply immerse themselves in the context of people’s lives. Since an ethnographic approach entails the facilitation of in-depth investigation [50], ethnographers engage with analyzing a few cases on a small-scale, but which may include a range of sources to draw from [53]. These sources may include unstructured or lightly structured informal conversations, participant observation, and fieldnotes that not only describe the details of the ethnographic interview but the ethnographers’ self-observations, personal reactions to their participants’ stories, and how their thinking with and about their project evolves and changes shape over time. Many ethnographers call this continual engagement with the field site and data as a kind of “recursive dwelling” in which ethnographers keep going back to the data, their fieldnotes, and their memories of the field site to deepen and nuance their understanding, analysis and the writing up of their participants’ lives [54].

The data sources included 1) the interview transcripts which were generated from audio-recorded interviews transcribed verbatim in Hindi or Urdu and then translated in English, 2) Author 1’s descriptive hand-written field notes which included observations of non-verbal data in the field and Author 1’s personal intellectual and emotional observations and reactions to certain moments in the interviews and during fieldwork, 3) Author 1’s voice memos and reflective journal entries about her relationship with the research, her participants, and experiencing her migrant identity, cultural alienation and integration, and evolving ideas about self-care, and 4) iteratively generated insights from collaborative thinking with the data during in-person white-board sessions along with Author 2 and Author 3, and 5) and a focus on non-verbal data in understanding the impact of certain moments in the participants’ lives such as their tone, gestures, sighs, as well as pauses and silences as different topics were covered in the interview.

The findings were collated thematically as well as in the form of individual case studies. In this paper, we have presented the findings thematically. The generation of themes was undertaken manually. Author 1 read and re-read interview transcripts. The emergence of broad themes in the data came through iteratively as Author 1 coded and re-coded themes and sub-themes. Subsequent collaborative thinking with Authors 2 and 3 further made clearer and succinct the emerging themes, which were then organized and re-organized to generate findings.

Demographic profile

Of the 20 women who were interviewed, the mean age of participants was 48.7 years with the youngest being 31 years old and the oldest being 77 years old. Thirteen women (65 per cent) were highly educated (Master’s degree qualification or above) while three women (15 per cent) held a Bachelor’s degree and one held a Diploma after completing their school education. Seventeen were married and participants had an average of two children at the time of the interview. Two women were single and one woman was widowed. During the year of fieldwork (2024), the mean number of years since migration for the participants was 16 years. This is notable as there remains a tendency within the literature to focus on the experiences of particular categories of migrants such as precarious or undocumented migrants, recently arrived migrants, or those with low literacy levels in the language of the receiving community [55–57]. Moreover, of the 20 women interviewed, 15 women (75 per cent) were engaged in professional work. However, only six of these 15 women (40 per cent) were engaged in full-time work while nine (60 per cent) were engaged in part-time work. The presence of many highly educated women in the sample is indicative of two things. Firstly, like many western countries, Australia tends to attract and retain highly educated South Asians, with South Asian women being similarly qualified as compared to South Asian men [58, 59]. Secondly, since Author 1 is an academic researcher and is familiar with highly educated social circles, her project flyers and study descriptions reached more women from such circles, which means the results of this study are more relevant to understanding the experiences of migrant women with a higher level of education.

Results

We have generated four key themes that spotlight how diabetes and distress are intertwined in the participants’ life narratives and situated in the changing sociocultural, emotional, and nutritional landscape of their lives post-migration.

The process of becoming a migrant woman and making sense of emotions

Despite having been resident in Australia for an average of 16 years, in their narratives, participants expressed a still palpable range of distressing emotions related to the process of migration and clearly understood and experienced these as having a deep impact on their health. They framed their emotional challenges in the early years of migration as directly causative of diabetes. Women used words such as “anxiety”, “loneliness” and “fear” to describe their emotions during the process of immigration and (re-)imagining their life as a migrant in Australia. Some participants even said that they had a “premonition” that they were going to be diagnosed with diabetes someday, since the initial years of living as a migrant in Australia “caused a lot of ongoing stress”, and their parents “anyway lived a life of stress and had diabetes”.

One participant, Tara, a migrant of Pakistani origin in her early 40s who moved to Melbourne with her family nearly 10 years ago, was pregnant at the time of migration and was diagnosed with gestational diabetes during her immigration medical check-up. Describing this diagnosis as a “shock”, Tara quickly changed the narrative from focusing on her distress to focusing on coping. She said that she experienced only “a little anxiety and stress” because she is “strong” and “accepts things very quickly”. Notably, however, later in the interview, Tara became more reflective about her life and contradicted her earlier expressions of quick acceptance of her emotions during the time of her diagnosis and migration. She patched together different aspects of her life since migration, expressing her distress more deeply, and said that the stress and loneliness of migration, the lack of domestic helpers in Australia, as well as the onset of her fourth pregnancy post-migration “gave ignition” to her diabetes diagnosis.

In this way, Tara’s fluctuating recollections of diagnosis were filtered through gendered expectation of stoicism and strength and through shifting socio-economic expectations, such as the availability of domestic help. She said that she “feared several things at that time (of migration)”. This included her gestational diabetes diagnosis, the thought of moving to another country, and the loneliness of navigating her pregnancy without the support of her close relatives. Moreover, Tara said that the distress following the death of her parents weighed on her emotionally after being diagnosed with gestational diabetes along with the “overwhelming feelings” of migrating to Australia. Tara also said that her diagnosis was “not a surprise” as she had a “very strong history of diabetes” in her family. Moreover, Tara said that diabetes self-care for her was a matter of “checks and balances” and “on and off” phases in her life while the stress of living with diabetes and her daily life responsibilities remained constant.

Another participant, Kiran, who was also diagnosed with diabetes during her immigration medical check-up, recounted her experiences with diabetes-related stigma and body shaming from within the family after her diagnosis. Kiran is a migrant of Indian origin in her late 50s who moved to Melbourne in the mid 90s when she was in her 20s. With an increasingly agitated pitch in her voice, Kiran exclaimed that her “stress” came from being perceived as an “ugly duckling” right before being married and migrating to Australia. Kiran then recounted in extensive detail how she went through an emotionally challenging process of “reclaiming her identity” as a migrant woman who was “dark-skinned”, a “dutiful wife” and a “woman with diabetes”. In filtering her reflections on diagnosis and distress through a broader discussion of identity, Kiran’s narrative highlights the ways in which emotive responses to a multiplicity of perceived sources of stigma or shame, on the one hand, and self-transformation on the other, can be intractably interwoven with seemingly disparate experiences of illness and distress.

For these two women, reflective accounts of being diagnosed with diabetes when they were migrating contained references to difficult emotions triggered by events concurrent to migration, such as bereavement, the loss of relational support, body shaming and diabetes stigma. These women understood and articulated their diabetes as originating in complex socioemotional histories of migration, gendered expectation, and socio-economic transformation. Through their narratives they highlighted the enduring distress that these engendered.

Losing relational ties through distance, death and familial discord

Almost all the participants described experiencing prolonged emotional distress, either from the fear of losing loved ones who lived in their home countries or from the grief of having already lost them to old age or illness. Women also experienced the loss of interdependence within their in-laws’ homes, as well as losing “blood relationships” to family feuds, meaning their relationships with their siblings or paternal and maternal uncles and aunts. They described how the prolonged distress tied to losing such relational ties impacted the emergence and management of diabetes.

Women used biomedical and non-biomedical idioms such as “trauma”, “depression” and “thinking too much” to describe the distress of unintended or undesired relational shifts. One participant, Shaheen, expressed how she thinks that the hardship of losing relational ties is the main cause of why South Asians “catch diabetes” when they live as migrants in western countries. Shaheen is a migrant of Pakistani origin in her late 30s who moved to Melbourne nearly 10 years ago. She exclaimed that diabetes does not “happen just suddenly” or because of “eating too much”. Rather, she articulated that it happens when one is consumed by the “deafening silence” of living in a “beautiful home” in Australia, but one that is devoid of the “noisy environment” and “bickering” of living in South Asia. As she reflected on South Asians “getting trapped” in “life-long illnesses”, Shaheen sighed, looked out of the window of the restaurant she was sitting in with Author 1 for the interview, and said:

You come here (to Australia) and suddenly you’re lost in a jungle…there is nobody except trees…except greenery… so…that life that we live (in South Asia) and then this life (in Australia)…this is why we get sick early here…we get serious illnesses…the people who go on a journey…they become victims of serious illnesses…their lives are halved.

To positively tether her emotions in the present and not let her thoughts “eat away” at her and “turn into entire nights of thinking and not sleeping”, Shaheen says that she keeps herself busy with her favorite hobby: knitting clothes. She says that being “consumed with knitting” on a regular basis “controls her sugar” and gives her “feelings of happiness”. Shaheen’s narrative highlights the ways in which the ruptures of relational ties not only translate to long-term grief and distress, but also shape the illness experiences of migrants, and are interwoven with how diabetes is experienced in everyday life.

Kiran, on the other hand, has experienced deep relational loss and grief from having lost her close family members to prolonged illnesses post-migration, as well as one of her pets to death. These experiences made her “loose grip” over diabetes care for the long stretches of time in which she was experiencing “grief” and “depression”. Using the lens of her professional therapeutic expertise, Kiran described “trauma” as a kind of “long-term grief” that she has not been given “emotional closure” from. However, she thinks that once closure is achieved, the “everyday life symptoms” of “living with the grief of loss” becomes one of the major factors that continues to impact upon diabetes self-care.

These narratives reveal how the relational ruptures of migration are embodied and experienced as long-term distress among women, which can manifest as the onset or progression of diabetes if left unaddressed. Moreover, they identify a positive relationship between personal strategies for alleviating distress and improvements in diabetes. These personal strategies included: ways of focusing on the present and being present, engaging in calming activities that bring joy and happiness, and finding meaning in their everyday lives. They also described how expanding their social network and experiencing social connection and support improved their diabetes. These personal strategies for alleviating distress are reflective of how cultivating self-care while experiencing distress entangled with diabetes in their everyday lives.

The stressful juggle between domestic chores and professional work

Many participants expressed the everyday stressors of having to juggle between domestic chores and professional work. Often, women said that they were acutely aware of the phases in their lives when they “lost grip” over diabetes self-care due to experiencing ongoing stress, but they just kept waiting for a time when they could get “some time off from work” and begin thinking about themselves. One participant, Riya, a migrant of Indian origin and a full-time working professional in her mid 50s, said in a dejected and angry voice that even if one has a family, the isolation of struggling with professional work the entire day and then “having nobody to talk to at night since everyone in the family has their own schedules” is what “impacts the sugar levels of South Asian migrants”.

Participants described how diabetes diagnosis itself added a second source of stress to their busy lives. One participant in particular, Saakshi, described in evocative detail how the unrelenting stress from her professional work entangled with her pre-diabetes and following diabetes diagnosis. Saakshi is a migrant of Indian origin in her mid 60s who moved to Melbourne nearly 20 years ago. Saakshi recalled a conversation that she had with her general practitioner upon diagnosis. The general practitioner told Saakshi to “not blame herself” for the diagnosis because once an individual “gets pre-diabetes”, a diabetes diagnosis can be “triggered by stress”. Saakshi said that she was stressed after being diagnosed with pre-diabetes and that the stress “only increased after that”. Being diagnosed with diabetes while working in a “high pressure job” was a kind of “wake up call” for Saakshi, and she began “subtly noticing and stopping” the things in her daily life and her diet which were “not right”. In a reflective tone, Saakshi recounted:

I realized that I was using cigarettes, like if my day was a sentence, I was using cigarettes as, you know, punctuation marks. Like if my day was a paragraph then it was, you know, the different things you did after your food or with your coffee, with your tea, you smoke a cigarette. So, I began to notice that, oh, you know, I was smoking, I was having tea with plenty of honey in it. So, all of these things, you know, working together, and then suddenly you stop the sugar, the honey in your tea, no cigarette, just like noticing lots of things, right? But it doesn’t mean that, you know, that puts stress on me, additional stress on me. It just made me aware that, oh, that’s what I was doing with myself. But the stress was the stress. It was work-related. The inability, and the intensity of it, the situation of it all….

These narratives expressed a keen awareness of how modern work pressures interacted with the gendered division of labour in South Asian homes. Participants sometimes felt overwhelmed by the responsibility to ensure care for other family members with busy schedules when they themselves were time-poor and did not have others offering them care and support [60]. They described gendered expectation to care for the family before caring for the self, the pressures of domestic and professional work and the stress to financially support families and be economically upwardly mobile. All these conditions, they felt, worked against healthy habits and self-care and exacerbated both their distress and their diabetes.

Navigating food within fractured socialities and identities

In their narratives, South Asian migrant women frequently described being deeply disappointed with how the Australian dietary advice was at odds with traditional South Asian diet. While some were able to acclimatize to, as they called it, “the Aussie way of eating”, many expressed how “being a foodie” was part of the South Asian way of living and considered the healthy Australian dietary advice as “too bland” for their taste. Most women were knowledgeable about the kinds of South Asian foods they needed to eat to maintain their diet because they had observed their diabetic parents, siblings or other close family members follow such diets.

Women were also aware of the latest dietary recommendations, and some women had read widely on topics such as healthy eating and diet maintenance for the South Asian community. However, many of them lacked family support in being able to prepare diabetes-friendly home-cooked meals for themselves. While women who lived alone or did not have children were better able to focus on their diet, women who had children expressed that they did not have enough time to prepare exclusive diabetes friendly meals for themselves, and all their time was consumed with doing domestic chores. Moreover, their experiences of trying to eat “the right kinds of foods” were narratively entangled with distressing emotions of lost social ties and marginalized identities.

These marginalized identities were multilayered. Maya was a migrant of Indian origin in her early 30s who was neurodivergent. She recounted how when she started “reading a lot of diabetes literature” after her diagnosis, she realized how her various identities such as having been perceived as “a fat kid” in her childhood and a “big foodie” in her adulthood shaped her relationship to food. Maya said that even though she was seeing a psychotherapist for other life problems, understanding herself better in therapy led her to “change her relationship with food”.

Shaheen, on the other hand, exclaimed how for her, not being able to “taste” healthy food was related to the loss of her parents and her sibling and the distress this caused. As Shaheen sadly recounted:

it is because of this loneliness…this stress…it is because my father passed away…my sister passed away… so just thinking about all of this….all of these thoughts ruin a person… you lose sleep at night…you are not sleeping right… you are not eating right… when you are stressed you find whatever rice is there…you eat whatever junk food you can find…vegetables and all that?… I say that a person can eat vegetables and lentils happily when that person themselves is ‘fresh’…that is when you can really taste food…you won’t be able to taste that food in stress.

These narratives highlight the ways in which diabetes was understood beyond nutritional self-care and instead, reflected participants’ emotional and social relationships to food. For these participants, food evoked emotions, places, and identities that powerfully shaped the experience of diabetes onset, progression, and self-care in the context of migration.

Discussion

This study adds critical ethnographic nuance to a large body of qualitative health literature that has explored patient perspectives on themes such as diabetes distress [61, 62], self-care agency [63–65], coping and resilience [66–69], as well as motivation for lifestyle change [70]. In particular, we advance previous anthropological work conducted at the intersection of distress, diabetes and gender in different contexts of marginalization [71–75]. These studies center the social worlds in which the relationship between diabetes and distress has emerged. Our study builds on and extends this approach by focusing on narratives of self-care as a mechanism for understanding how participants’ make sense of the entangled nature of diabetes and distress in their own linguistic, experiential, and conceptual terms. Through these articulations, we gain insight into how participants use diverse medical and non-medical idioms of distress to craft their life narratives, cultivate resilience in their lives, and practice diabetes self-care.

The presence of persistent social and relational stressors that trigger distress among South Asian migrant women in varying high-income contexts has been widely documented. These may include: discrimination [76], diminished social support and alienation [76–78], declining social mobility and economic instabilities [79, 80], marital abuse and interpersonal family problems [77, 81], intergenerational conflicts [78], and changes in climate and food patterns [79, 82]. For the migrant women involved in this study, ‘distress’ evoked the pernicious presence of social and relational stressors in their lives, which were intellectually and emotionally woven into their sense-making of diabetes onset, progression, and self-care during the process of migration.

Further, as seen in our study and well-noted elsewhere, South Asian women may be inclined to internalize the “feelings of depression in terms of coping, endurance and being strong” ([83], p.447) and use somatic idioms of distress to express mental and emotional distress [84–86]. Women locate distress in the materiality of the body and as “constituting an illness state” ([83], p.444). This is highlighted in our study as women related migration stress, the prolonged grief of losing loved ones to death and discord, and the alienation of living as migrants to developing diabetes. These findings accord with Mendenhall and colleagues’ findings in their work with Mexican immigrant women living with diabetes in the United States that ‘idioms of distress’ are used “both as a product of social suffering and an expression of such suffering” ([72], p.222). In our study, this was highlighted in how participants directly related the challenging emotions accompanying migration as being directly causative of diabetes and conceptualised their diabetes diagnosis as having created the conditions for prolonged stress in their lives. These stressors in turn interacted with their social worlds and elicited social responses that impacted their self-perception.

Moreover, this paper reinforces the ways in which “social lives become embodied in [women’s] physical and mental health” ([87], p.127). In the contexts of their countries of origin, South Asian women may find satisfaction with maintaining key gender roles that are socially rewarded and facilitate their relational social status and social integration [87]. However, as noted in our study, the same behaviours may lead to feelings of alienation and disconnection when women migrate to high-income western countries. Alongside severed family ties and social connections, these women often experience the compounded burdens of engaging in professional work along with performing domestic duties and engaging in diabetes self-care. The competing priorities and tensions that they experience in diabetes self-care demonstrate that ‘distress’ is contextually located and is socially, emotionally, and relationally interwoven with experiences of living with diabetes. This aligns with work conducted in other contexts of marginalization [88], whereupon patients’ explanations of diabetes causation and control is not simply biomedical but rather a process of self-making situated in their social and emotional worlds [88].

Through close attention to self-care and sense-making experiences, our study contributes to an understanding of the multiple, layered interactions between diabetes, distress and migration. As Tronto notes, self-care practices are generally perceived to operate outside of institutionalised contexts and are often perceived as dimensions of personal or private lives [49]. However, the entanglement of illness and social process - understood here through attention to experiential reflections of migrant women living with diabetes - cannot be dismissed as a private matter.

As such, our study has important implications for policymakers and clinicians working with South Asian migrant women living with diabetes in Australia, as well as other migrant women and underserved communities in Australia and elsewhere. Firstly, for policymakers, multicultural diabetes support resources should include materials that clearly highlight the recognition of and provide resources for the feelings of alienation, loneliness, and disconnection that may accompany and impact diabetes diagnosis and self-care post-migration and settlement. Secondly, for clinicians, training must emphasise that the absence of any obvious signs of distress should not be taken at face value. Clinicians should be provided support to facilitate safe, contextually informed, and relationally based communication. For example, diabetes care consultations should involve asking migrant women questions about any experiences that may be impacting their diabetes care and then discussing strategies to alleviate such distress. Moreover, culture should be considered as a dynamic phenomenon and not a static concept; one that continuously shapes experiences of health, illness, and recovery in order to reduce static representations of certain cultural groups in a given context. Taking this approach opens deeper and more responsive opportunities to examine the interactions between individual wellbeing, social environments, and health systems.

Strengths and limitations

Using an ethnographic approach and syndemic thinking, this study provides valuable insight into the daily life experiences and narrative constructions of diabetes and distress in the narratives of South Asian migrant women living with diabetes in Australia. Ethnographic methods used in this study are not predicated on sample size and did not aim to produce a representative sample. Instead, the methods emphasise depth of data collection and the variation and instability of migrant women’s perceptions of self-care [89]. This study focuses on migrant women of South Asian origin, particularly from India and Pakistan, with a skew towards an understudied population – highly educated, long-term migrants. While this is a strength of this study, it does mean that we were not able to capture a broader diversity of voices of women who have other experiences of South Asian migration.

Conclusion

This study builds on and extends the syndemic framework by centering the experiential dimensions of syndemics through focusing on the narratives of migrant women at the nexus of diabetes and distress. Through focusing on life narratives and people’s own sense-making process, we offer new insights into the multi-layered experiences of syndemic disease interactions. In this study, the interwoven narratives of diabetes and distress for South Asian migrant women were situated within the fractured realities of a changing sociocultural, emotional, gendered, racialized, and nutritional post-migration landscape. Self-care for women was shaped and constrained by these realities and the relationalities of their pre-migration socioemotional histories and their post-migration realities. Given limited evidence in this area of research in public health, we contend that an ethnographic focus on life narratives in syndemic analysis is a crucial tool when working with diverse individuals and communities, such as underserved migrants, and other racially, linguistically, sexually and gender-diverse marginalized groups in Australia and elsewhere. While it may be more acutely evident for individuals from disadvantaged and underrepresented groups living in high-income countries, their experiences are a reminder that diabetes and distress are likely to be deeply contextually embedded and entangled in ways that are ‘untranslatable’ within western biomedical categories. Centering life narratives foregrounds relationally, socially and culturally embedded meanings, bringing attention to the inevitable complexities of how distress, diabetes and self-care are entangled in lived experience.
