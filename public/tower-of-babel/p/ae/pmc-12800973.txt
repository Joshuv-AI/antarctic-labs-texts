Tips for Successful Double-Bundle Posterior Cruciate Ligament Reconstruction
Authors: Alexander P Decilveo, Neil Patel, Nils Calderón Tejerina, Joseph N Liu, Bruce A Levy, George F Rick Hatch
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPosterior cruciate ligament (PCL) injuries are a relatively common knee injury that can occur in isolation but are more commonly associated with multiligamentous knee injuries. PCL reconstruction is a technically challenging and complex orthopaedic procedure. Improper reconstruction techniques may result in significant complications, including residual instability, graft failure, and damage to adjacent neurovascular structures. Several strategies can be implemented to obtain improved outcomes while mitigating complications, including techniques for optimizing arthroscopic visualization, graft preparation/tensioning, tunnel positioning, and postoperative rehabilitation. This Technical Note outlines our tips for successful double-bundle PCL reconstruction.

Operative management of posterior cruciate ligament (PCL) injuries is technically challenging, given the unique anatomy of the PCL and the location of its femoral and tibial insertions. No consensus exists on the optimal technique to perform PCL reconstruction (PCLR). Traditionally, PCLR has utilized a single-bundle technique, but recent studies have shown that double-bundle reconstruction is biomechanically superior at restoring posterior knee stability.1,2 Graft selection, preparation, and tensioning also play a critical role in restoring knee stability and patient outcomes.3 Appropriate implementation of postoperative rehabilitation protocols and PCL unloader brace wear are critical factors related to safe return to play.

Surgical Technique

Patient Positioning and Examination Under Anesthesia

The patient is placed supine on the operative table (Video 1). A high thigh tourniquet, lateral hip post at the level of the tourniquet, a foot bump, and lateral wedge allow for resting knee flexion of 90°. This ensures the leg does not have to be manipulated during PCL tibial tunnel drilling. A full-size fluoroscopic unit should be available and come in from the ipsilateral side. If performing a posterolateral corner reconstruction with a PCLR, the fluoroscopy unit should enter from the contralateral side. On preoperative imaging, note the position of the popliteal artery, as 2% to 3% of patients have an aberrant anterior tibial artery that lies anterior to the popliteus muscle (Fig 1).4 Exam under anesthesia with a posterior drawer test under fluoroscopic guidance should be performed. Bedside arterial Doppler assessment confirms adequate flow of the dorsalis pedis and posterior tibial artery.

Graft Preparation

The grafts are prepared with dual suspensory fixation, which allows for sequential tensioning of the grafts in both knee flexion and extension (Fig 2). The size recommendations are 9 to 10 mm × 70 to 90 mm (diameter × length) for the anterolateral bundle (ALB) graft and 6 to 7 mm × 70 to 90 mm for the posteromedial bundle (PMB) graft. For option 1, the ALB femoral limb can be prepared with a TightRope ABS (attachable button system) with InternalBrace (IB) (Arthrex. Naples, FL). The femoral limb for the PMB can be prepared with a TightRope ABS with or without an IB. A separate medial incision will be required over the femoral tunnels to allow for later ABS button placement. For option 2, the femoral limb of the ALB can be prepared with a TightRope RT (Arthrex) implant with an IB and the PMB with a TightRope RT with or without an IB. Option 2 does not require a separate medial incision on the femur as the button can be flipped at the medial femoral cortex. The tibial limb of the ALB can be prepared with a TightRope ABS and the PMB prepared with a FiberTag TightRope (Arthrex) implant. Each TightRope from the tibial limbs of the ALB and PMB, as well as the InternalBrace, is later tensioned over a large 14-mm or 20-mm ABS button (Arthrex).

Graft preparation. Dual suspensory fixation allows for sequential tensioning of both bundles of the posterior cruciate ligament. The anterolateral bundle graft is prepared 9.5 × 75 mm with dual suspensory fixation (thick red arrow tibial side, thin red arrow femoral side). The posteromedial bundle graft is folded over and prepared to 6.5 × 75 mm with dual suspensory fixation (thick green arrow, tibial side; thin green arrow, femoral side).

Accessing the Posterior Knee Arthroscopically

With the knee in 90° of flexion, use an arthroscopic 90° burner and shaver to remove the footprint of the native PCL. Carefully resect the PMB of the PCL until the posterior horn of the medial meniscus can be visualized. Take care not to injure the root of the posterior horn of the medial meniscus, which is immediately adjacent to the PCL tibial insertion. Leave some tissue remaining to help identify the femoral origin of the PCL for femoral tunnel drilling (Fig 3). Using a modified Gilquist maneuver, place your arthroscope in the posterior knee from the anterolateral portal. At this time, switching to a 70° scope if available may aid with visualization. Alternatively, if all PCL work will be done through anterior portals and without a 70° scope, we recommend making a high and long anteromedial portal (AMP).

After debriding the posterior cruciate ligament femoral footprint (red arrow), the posterior knee (yellow arrow) is accessed using a modified Gilquist maneuver.

Establishing the Posteromedial Working Portal

With the eyes of the arthroscope facing medially, an 18-gauge spinal needle is inserted just proximal to the posteromedial capsular fold, aiming toward the PCL remnant (Fig 4). The spinal needle should be placed posterior to the posterior femoral cortex and 3 to 4 cm proximal to the posterior border of the medial femoral condyle. Using the light from the arthroscope against the skin can aid in spinal needle placement. Proper portal placement ensures the posteromedial portal (PMP) is not too close to the femoral condyle, which would inhibit instrument freedom of movement. If the PMP is placed too low, it can be difficult to reach the distal end of the PCL tibial facet. While maintaining the spinal needle in the knee, use a No. 11 blade to make a skin incision over the spinal needle, followed by insertion of a switching stick. Using metal dilators, the skin and capsular tissue are sequentially dilated until a threaded cannula can be inserted.

The landmarks for creation of the posteromedial portal are posterior to the posterior femoral cortex and 3 to 4 cm proximal to the posterior border of the medial femoral condyle. (B) Left knee, 70° arthroscope in the anteromedial portal in the posterior knee, anterior to the left, posterior to the right. With the eyes of the arthroscope facing medially, a spinal needle, followed by a switching stick and threaded cannula, is used to establish a posteromedial working portal. The spinal needle/switching stick should aim toward the posterior cruciate ligament tibial footprint and be made proximal to the posteromedial capsular fold to allow for proper instrumentation.

Clearing Out the PCL Recess and Placement of the PCL Guide

With the arthroscope in the anterolateral portal (ALP), use a 90° electrocautery through the PMP to clear a soft tissue plane between the posterior midline septum and the posterior horn of the medial meniscus. Use caution to not resect or violate the shiny white fibers of the posterior horn of the medial meniscus. Resect all PCL fibers on the tibial footprint until the champagne drop-off posteriorly. Visualize the popliteus muscle and the mammillary bodies, which lie on either side of the tibial PCL (Fig 5). The medial and lateral mamillary bodies are sometimes referred to as the medial groove and lateral cartilage point. Once you delineate the tibial footprint, switch the arthroscope into the PMP to prepare for tibial tunnel drilling. Set the side-specific tibial PCL guide to 60°. The PCL guide is inserted through the AMP. Hook the base of the PCL facet to capture the entire tibial footprint and then drop your hand. If the PCL tibial footprint is very wide, aim the drill guide slightly lateral to approximate the anatomic origin of the ALB.

Drilling the Tibial Tunnel

Fluoroscopy confirms the sagittal position of the guide on the PCL facet, while arthroscopy confirms the coronal position. Placement of the fluoroscopic monitor adjacent to the ipsilateral shoulder of the patient allows the surgeon to view both the fluoroscopic and arthroscopic views simultaneously (Fig 6). Drill a 2.4-mm guide pin under fluoroscopic guidance with the 70° scope in the PMP, followed by a 3.5-mm FlipCutter (Arthrex). After visualizing the FlipCutter in the posterior knee, detach the tibial guide from the bullet and use the guide itself to retract the posterior capsule to protect the neurovascular structures. Depending on graft preparation, the FlipCutter is set to an appropriate width and drilled a minimum of 35 mm in length. If concerned about graft tunnel mismatch, drill a longer or even full tibial tunnel. Pass a suture passage stick retrograde through the tibial tunnel. Retrieve the sutures through the AMP. Shuttle 3 passing sutures of different colors antegrade through the tibial tunnel for later passage of the ALB, IB, and PMB. Ensure the looped ends of the passing sutures exit the AMP.

Drilling the Femoral Tunnels

Switch to a 30° arthroscope and view through the ALP. Drill the ALB first, as this is the larger and more important limb for posterior knee stability. We recommend 2 options for femoral drilling. Option 1 is outside-in with the femoral anterior cruciate ligament guide set to 55°. The guide is placed through the AMP at the anatomic origin of the ALB, abutting the cartilage margin (Fig 7). With an orientation 20° anterior to the transepicondylar axis in the axial plane of the distal femur, a 3.5-mm FlipCutter is drilled 10 to 20 mm in length by an appropriate diameter. Following the ALB femoral tunnel, drill a 10- to 20-mm tunnel for the PMB at the PMB femoral origin with the guide set to 55° and an orientation 10° anterior to the transepicondylar axis in the axial plane of the distal femur. For the PMB femoral tunnel, ensure the tunnel is posterior to the previously drilled ALB tunnel and spaced at least 5 mm apart to avoid tunnel convergence.

Graft Passage

A 12-mm PassPort cannula (Arthrex) can be placed in the AMP to avoid suture bridges. All graft passage is performed under direct arthroscopic visualization and through the AMP. The PassPort cannula may need to be removed if graft passage is too tight through the cannula. Suture management using different-colored passing sutures or clamping your passing sutures with different clamps limits errors with graft passage. The order of graft passage into tunnels can be performed per surgeon preference. Our preference is that each graft is brought into the tibial tunnel first, followed by the respective femoral tunnels. The PMB graft is passed first, as this will allow the graft to seat in the anatomic medial aspect of the tibial tunnel (Fig 8). The graft is then bottomed out (i.e., 10-20 mm) in the femoral tunnel. The ALB graft and IB are passed second and bottomed out. If TightRope RT implants are prepared on the femoral side of the graft, the femoral buttons can be flipped under arthroscopic visualization while viewing from the ALP. Button flipping can be confirmed with fluoroscopy. As backup fixation, if the medial femoral cortex is perforated during drilling, a medial incision can be made over the tunnel, and a large concave cortical button can be placed deep to the TightRope RT button prior to tensioning. If the femoral end of each graft was prepared with a TightRope ABS, cortical buttons can be placed after suture passage. A medial incision should be made over the femoral tunnels in order to place a cortical button on the medial femoral cortex.

Graft Fixation and Tensioning

The arthroscope is removed. The knee is cycled to remove creep. On the tibial side, a single slotted cortical button is used for fixation for the ALB graft TightRope, PMB graft TightRope, and IB from the ALB. Tension the ALB in 85° to 90° of knee flexion with an anterior drawer and the PMB in 10° of knee flexion. Observe tibial reduction in relationship to the femur under fluoroscopic guidance prior to final tensioning (Fig 9). A SwiveLock (Arthrex) anchor can be used as backup fixation in the tibia for the IB. A posterior drawer test is performed to assess construct fixation. The arthroscope is reintroduced into the knee joint to confirm final graft positioning. Final range of motion is evaluated prior to final tensioning and closure. The tourniquet is deflated, and arterial Doppler studies are performed bedside in a sterile fashion to rule out arterial injury.

Postoperative Rehabilitation

The operative extremity is placed in a hinged knee brace locked in extension. The patient is nonweightbearing for 2 weeks postoperatively and transitioned to toe-touch weightbearing for 4 additional weeks thereafter. Physical therapy begins on postoperative day 2, with a goal range of motion of 90° of knee flexion by 1 week postoperatively. Emphasis is placed on early quadriceps muscle activation and prone passive knee range of motion from 0° to 90°. At 2 weeks, if swelling permits, the patient can be transitioned to a custom dynamic PCL unloader brace to be worn always, except for bathing, for a total of 9 months postoperatively (Rebound brace; Ossur). If a PCL unloader brace is not readily available, patient education on limiting active hamstring firing is imperative. We restrict active hamstring firing for a minimum of 4 months postoperatively. Brace discontinuation may be required if range of motion is limited to prevent arthrofibrosis. A running program may be initiated at 6 months postoperatively if adequate quadriceps and hamstring strength are achieved.

Discussion

Nonoperative management for most low-grade isolated PCL injuries has shown patient satisfaction and high rates of return to play.5, 6, 7 Agolley et al.6 showed return-to-sport rates of over 90% with the utilization of a dynamic PCL unloader brace and PCL-specific rehab protocol. However, in patients who have unsuccessful conservative management or have multiligamentous knee injuries, PCL repair or reconstruction remains the mainstay treatment option. The unique anatomy of the PCL has led to the development of various techniques for PCLR, including single-bundle versus double-bundle, tibial inlay versus transtibial approaches, and variations in graft preparation.

The tibial attachment sites for the anterolateral and posteromedial bundles of the PCL place the posterior meniscal roots and popliteal vessels in danger during exposure and drilling.8 Our Technical Note recommends the utilization of a modified Gilquist maneuver as well as a 70° scope if available to safely gain access to the posterior knee. Additionally, we recommend obtaining preoperative magnetic resonance imaging to assess for the presence of an aberrant anterior tibial artery lying anterior to the popliteus muscle, which may place the patient at a greater risk of arterial injury during drilling.4 Utilization of the posteromedial portal has been described to improve tibial footprint identification to allow for accurate tibial tunnel placement and avoid injury to neurovascular structures.9,10 Additionally, visualization of the popliteus muscle and the mammillary bodies on either side of the PCL remnant may further ensure proper positioning of the PCL guide and tibial tunnel. Prior to tibial drilling, we recommend the utilization of both fluoroscopy and arthroscopy to confirm sagittal and coronal positions, respectively. The tibial guide can be detached from the bullet and used to retract the posterior capsule to protect the neurovascular structures as additional security.

The inlay technique has been developed, given the theorized risk of mechanical wear and graft failure from the traditional transtibial approach due to the “killer turn.”11 However, the existing literature has shown no significant differences in clinical outcomes, residual laxity, or rates of graft failure.12, 13, 14, 15, 16 Given these findings, our technique utilizes outside-in tibial drilling while viewing from the PMP to improve visualization of the tibial attachment site of the PCL to minimize the effects of the “killer turn.” This allows for the anatomic placement of the tibial drill pin under direct visualization and antegrade passage while inlaying the graft to minimize the mechanical stress experienced by the graft.10

Our technique involves a double-bundle graft augmented with an IB to optimize graft strength and mitigate graft-related complications. Double-bundle PCLR has gained popularity as studies have shown less residual laxity, translation, and improved functional outcome scores in comparison to single-bundle PCLR.1,2,17 Dasari et al.17 conducted a meta-analysis that found double-bundle PCLR led to superior biomechanical and clinical outcomes. Several studies have validated the use of the IB or suture tape during PCLR to reduce translation and maximize the strength of the construct.18, 19, 20, 21, 22 Root et al.22 performed a systematic review, which found that PCLR with suture tape augmentation provided superior biomechanics and stability with equal or improved patient-reported outcomes. Additionally, drilling the femoral tunnels with an outside-in technique lowers mean and peak contact pressures at the femoral aperture (“critical corner”) compared to an inside-out technique.23 The combination of these techniques aims to maximize the biomechanical strength of the grafts while minimizing factors that may contribute to increased risk of failure.

Implementation of a postoperative rehabilitation protocol and PCL unloader brace wear are critical components to optimize return to play and functional outcomes. We recommend restriction of full weightbearing for the first 6 weeks postoperatively and emphasize early quadriceps muscle activation while restricting hamstring firing for a minimum of 4 months postoperatively to protect the graft. A dynamic PCL unloader brace applies a force that varies in relation to the degree of knee flexion, replicating the kinematic forces experienced by the native PCL.24 This enables early range of motion while keeping the tibia reduced. Although our Technical Note provides important tips for improving the surgical technique related to PCL reconstruction, it is important to consider the limitations and disadvantages associated with our recommendations (Table 2). Several factors, including a full-size fluoroscopic unit and a 70° scope, may not always be readily available or technically challenging to use. Additionally, the use of dual suspensory fixation and a PCL unloader brace may lead to higher implant and patient costs, respectively. The utilization of a posteromedial portal to improve visualization does increase the risk of neurovascular injury during portal creation as well as operative time. An outside-in technique can be technically challenging, requires surgical assistance, and does not avoid the effects of the “killer turn,” which may contribute to graft degeneration and tunnel enlargement. Lastly, the use of a double-bundle technique requires 2 femoral tunnels, which increases the risk of tunnel convergence and medial femoral cortical wall blowout.
