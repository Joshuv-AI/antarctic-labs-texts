Cross-RNA transferable sequence representation learning for lncRNA m6A site detection via novel deep domain separation networks
Authors: Zhixia Teng, Zhenjiang Li, Di Liu, Chunyu Wang, Guohua Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractN6-methyladenosine (m6A) is a key epitranscriptomic marker enriched in long noncoding RNAs (lncRNAs) that is closely involved in complex disease mechanisms. Although accurate detection of m6A sites in lncRNAs is essential for understanding disease mechanisms, the development of effective computational predictors remains challenging due to the limited number of annotated sites. Moreover, most existing predictors are specifically designed for messenger RNAs (mRNAs) based on abundant mRNA-specific knowledge, yet they exhibit limited generalizability to lncRNAs. Given the similarities between mRNAs and lncRNAs, a transferable framework capable of leveraging their shared features is critical for advancing m6A site prediction in lncRNAs. To address this challenge, we propose DSNm6A, a deep learning framework that learns cross-RNA transferable sequence representations for effective lncRNA m6A site detection. To comprehensively capture patterns and signals of m6A sites, lncRNA and mRNA sequences are first encoded from complementary multiple facets, including One-Hot encoding, nucleotide physicochemical properties and cumulative frequency, and position-specific propensity. Based on these sequence encodings, a domain separation network integrating CNN, Bi-LSTM, and BERT modules is then employed to explicitly disentangle domain-invariant features shared between mRNAs and lncRNAs from their domain-specific counterparts. The shared features are finally fed into a fully connected layer for accurate lncRNA m6A sites prediction. Cross-validation and independent test results demonstrate that DSNm6A consistently outperforms existing methods across nearly all performance metrics, attributed to its superior capacity to learn transferable m6A-related features across RNA types. In addition, DSNm6A exhibits strong robustness and generalization across species.Keywords: lncRNA methylation, m6A site prediction, transfer learning, representation learning, domain separation network

Introduction

N6-methyladenosine (m6A) is a prevalent methylation modification in long noncoding RNAs (lncRNAs) that plays a crucial role in regulating gene expression and protein synthesis. Recent studies have shown that lncRNA m6A is closely related to many complex diseases, such as thyroid tumors [1], prostate cancer [2], and acute myeloid leukemia [3]. Moreover, previous studies have demonstrated that lncRNA m6A can precisely regulate RNA stability, processing, and interactions with proteins, and even single m6A sites such as single site (A783) on HOTAIR can significantly influence cancer cell proliferation, invasion, and drug response [4, 5]. However, our limited understanding of m6A site occurrence in lncRNAs hampers insights into the molecular mechanisms of disease. Given that wet-lab experiments are labor-intensive and time-consuming, developing efficient computational models to identify m6A sites in lncRNAs is essential for advancing our understanding of their roles in human complex diseases.

In recent years, several computational models have been developed to identify m6A sites in RNAs, which can be broadly categorized into machine learning (ML)-based and deep learning (DL)-based methods. ML-based approaches typically extract sequence and genomic features, manually construct feature representations, and then train a classifier to detect potential RNA m6A sites. For example, M6AMRFS [6] employed the Extreme Gradient Boosting classifier to detect m6A sites of RNAs in Saccharomyces cerevisiae using dinucleotide binary encoding and local position-specific dinucleotide frequency of their sequences. iRNA-m6A [7] represents RNAs using One-Hot encoding, nucleotide physicochemical properties, and cumulative nucleotide frequency. It then applies the minimum redundancy maximum relevance strategy to refine these features and trains a support vector machine classifier to identify m6A sites of RNAs in Homo sapien, Mus musculus, and Rattus norvegicus. However, these methods typically rely on linear combinations or shallow learning of handcrafted RNA sequence features, which limits their ability to capture the underlying complex patterns of m6A sites. As a result, the generalization capability and predictive performance of ML-based models are often compromised.

To overcome this limitation, DL techniques have been employed to predict m6A sites in RNA due to their strong ability to learn deep features and capture complex patterns. For example, im6A-TS-CNN [8] employed a convolutional neural network (CNN) to identify m6A sites in RNA based on One-Hot encoding of their sequences. EDLm6APred [9] first encoded RNA sequences using One-Hot, Word2vec, and RNA2vec, and then exploited a Bi-LSTM network to analyze these encodings to detect m6A sites in RNAs. Although DL models for m6A site prediction have been developed for messenger RNAs (mRNAs). LncRNAs also play unique and critical roles, as m6A–lncRNA interplays have been shown to regulate cancer drug resistance [5], and even a single m6A site on the lncRNA HOTAIR can drive breast cancer progression [4]. However, only a limited number of validated lncRNA m6A sites are currently available, which makes it difficult to train effective DL models and often leads to overfitting and poor generalization.

So we propose DSNm6A, a novel DL framework that extracts transferable sequence features between mRNAs and lncRNAs to enable accurate m6A site prediction in lncRNAs. This design is motivated by two fundamental observations: first, the notable sequence and structural similarities between mRNAs and lncRNAs make it feasible to transfer m6A-related features between them; second, the abundance of annotated m6A sites in mRNAs provides a rich resource for effectively training DL models. Building upon these insights, DSNm6A employs a multifaceted encoding scheme and a deep domain separation network (DSN) architecture to effectively capture and transfer sequence representations associated with m6A sites.

Specifically, the multifaceted encoding scheme employs One-Hot encoding, nucleotide physicochemical properties and cumulative frequency, and position-specific propensity (PSP) to comprehensively characterize the patterns and signals of m6A sites in RNA sequences. The DSN architecture consists of three hybrid feature encoders, which integrate CNN, BiLSTM, and BERT modules to disentangle domain-specific and domain-invariant features. It includes two private feature encoders for the source domain (mRNA) and the target domain (lncRNA), respectively, as well as a shared feature encoder for learning transferable representations. Within each encoder, CNNs capture local sequence motifs and physicochemical properties, BiLSTMs model contextual dependencies within sequences, and BERT encodes global semantic information from the multifaceted RNA sequence encodings. Through hierarchical integration of CNN, Bi-LSTM, and BERT, DSNm6A accurately learns informative and distinctive representations of m6A-related sequence and contextual features in RNA sequences. Finally, DSNm6A adopts a joint training strategy combined with an MLP classifier to guide the shared encoder in learning transferable representations while suppressing leakage of private features into the shared space. Overall, the model first learns robust shared representations using abundant annotated mRNA data, and then transfers this knowledge to lncRNAs for accurate m6A site prediction. Extensive experiments demonstrate that DSNm6A consistently outperforms existing methods across nearly all performance evaluation metrics under various settings. Moreover, DSNm6A exhibits strong robustness and generalizability on imbalanced datasets and cross-species prediction tasks. To summarize, the main contributions of DSNm6A are as follows.

Materials and methods

Multifaceted encoding scheme of RNA sequences

To comprehensively extract multilevel features relevant to m6A sites in RNA sequences, we employed three complementary encoding strategies including One-Hot encoding, Nucleotide Physicochemical Property and Frequency encoding (NPPF), and Position-Specific Propensity (PSP) encoding. Specifically, One-Hot encoding captured the primary sequence composition, NPPF integrated nucleotide physicochemical properties, and global distribution characteristics of nucleotides, while PSP characterized the position-specific local contextual preferences of m6A sites. These encoding methods provided complementary information from structural, biophysical, and positional perspectives, enabling a more comprehensive representation of m6A-related sequence patterns and contextual signals. The following subsections detail the implementation of each encoding strategy.

Experimental results

Data collection and implementation details

To estimate the performance of DSNm6A, RNA sequences including m6A sites for lncRNAs and mRNAs in Homo sapien, Mus musculus, Rattus norvegicus, Danio rerio, and Arabidopsis thaliana were collected from the latest release of m6A-Atlas V2.0 [21] and RMBase V3.0 [22] . Each sequence contained 101 nucleotides, with an adenine (A) positioned at the center and 50 nucleotides on each side. Then, CD-HIT [23] was utilized to measure sequence similarities, and sequences that sharing over 80% similarity with others were removed following previous studies [24–27]. It could minimize redundancy while still retaining sufficient sequence diversity. Subsequently, these remaining sequences were organized into different positive sample datasets based on a 1:5 ratio of lncRNAs to mRNAs. Meanwhile, the negative samples were generated using the same strategy in the previous literature [28–30].

Specifically, at the upstream and downstream of the positive sample sites, sequence fragments with A length of 101nt centered on adenine (A) and matching the RRACH motif were intercepted. Then, CD-HIT was used to eliminate sequence fragments greater than the preset similarity threshold and those overlapping with the positive sample sites. The remaining set of sequence fragments was taken as the negative sample set. To evaluate the generalization ability and robustness of DSNm6A, the Homo sapien sample set was randomly split into parts DS_H and DS_HI at a ratio of 8:2. Furthermore, three unbalanced datasets: DS_H2, DS_H5, and DS_H10 were created by randomly selecting samples from DS_H with positive-negative sample ratios of 1:2, 1:5, and 1:10, respectively. The datasets of Mus Musculus, Rattus Norvegicus, Danio Rerio, and Arabidopsis Thaliana were marked as DS_M, DS_R, DS_D, and DS_A, respectively. Details of these datasets, including the numbers of positive and negative samples for both lncRNAs and mRNAs, are summarized in Table 1, where , , , and indicate the numbers of positive and negative samples in lncRNAs and mRNAs, respectively..

As the sequence length increases, the performance of DSNm6A initially improves significantly, indicating that shorter sequences may lack sufficient contextual information for accurate prediction. Cross-species lncRNA m6A site prediction

In this study, we applied DSNm6A to datasets from five species including DS_H, DS_M, DS_R, DS_D, and DS_A, to evaluate its cross-species generalization ability. Specifically, DSNm6A was trained on lncRNA and mRNA data from one species at a time and tested independently on the lncRNAs from the other species. This design ensures that the model leverages shared transcriptomic features within a species (e.g. nucleotide composition biases, posttranscriptional modification context, and sequencing-related biases), while avoiding inter-species distribution differences that could lead it to capture species-specific rather than molecular-category-specific patterns. Heatmaps illustrating the predictive performance in terms of F1-score, AUC, and AUPR across species are shown in Fig. 4.

Heatmap of DSNm6A performance across species. This heatmap visualizes a matrix of F1, AUC, and AUPR scores obtained from cross-species training and evaluation. Each cell indicates the performance of DSNm6A when trained on the species in the corresponding row and tested on the species in the corresponding column.

These heatmaps reveal a prominent diagonal reddish trend, indicating that models achieve the best performance when evaluated on the same species used for training. In contrast, off-diagonal regions, especially those at the periphery, display a bluish hue, suggesting reduced accuracy in cross-species predictions. These observations indicate that cross-species prediction performs well among closely related species such as Homo sapien, Mus musculus, and Rattus norvegicus, whereas performance declines for more distantly related species like Danio rerio and Arabidopsis thaliana. Interestingly, relatively high performance is observed when DSNm6A is trained on the Rattus norvegicus dataset and tested on the Danio rerio dataset. This may be attributed to the limited amount of lncRNA m6A data available for both species, leading to less stable and potentially less generalizable feature representations.

To investigate the performance differences of DSNm6A across species, we employed Nucleotide Acid Components (NAC) to quantify the frequency distribution of nucleotides within sequences and examined species-specific sequence variations by comparing NAC profiles between positive and negative samples. As shown in Fig. 5, NAC histograms across the five species consistently indicate that adenine (A) is the most abundant nucleotide in both positive and negative samples.

NAC analysis of candidate m6A sites across the five species.

Further analysis revealed a positive correlation between the magnitude of NAC differences between positive and negative samples and the predictive performance of DSNm6A across species. Specifically, a larger NAC difference enabled DSNm6A to better distinguish between positive and negative samples, resulting in superior predictive accuracy. In contrast, DSNm6A exhibited relatively poor performance in Arabidopsis thaliana since its nucleotide frequency distributions are highly similar across the four bases A, G, C, and U.

From a cross-species prediction perspective, Homo sapien, Mus musculus, and Rattus norvegicus showed relatively minor NAC differences, corresponding to better sequence representation transferability among these species. In contrast, Danio rerio and Arabidopsis thaliana displayed greater NAC divergence, which coincided with a marked decline in cross-species prediction accuracy. These findings demonstrates the importance of sequence composition similarity in enhancing generalizability of DSNm6A across species and provide valuable insights for developing transferable predictive frameworks.

Effectiveness of the transferable m6A representations

To assess the effectiveness of the transferable m6A representations learned by DSNm6A, we visualized the distribution of positive and negative samples from multiple species in both the initial input space and the latent feature space. As shown in Fig. 6, the feature embeddings of positive (red) and negative (blue) samples were projected into a 2D space using t-SNE [34]. In the initial space (left), samples from different classes and species are largely intermixed, suggesting that the raw input features lack discriminative. In contrast, after representation learning by DSNm6A (right), positive and negative samples are more clearly separated and tend to cluster into distinct regions regardless of species origin. This clear separation indicates that DSNm6A successfully extracts domain-invariant and discriminative features, enabling effective generalization across species boundaries.

Interpretation of DSNm6A

To investigate the underlying rationale for the effective transferability of knowledge from mRNA to lncRNA in predicting m6A sites, we performed an in-depth analysis of sequence-level similarities between mRNAs and lncRNAs using the Two Sample Logo tool (http://www.twosamplelogo.org/) [35]. The positional nucleotide enrichment patterns for all five species are presented in Fig. 7.

Comparative analysis of m6A site motifs in mRNAs and lncRNAs in all five species.

In Arabidopsis thaliana, lncRNA m6A sites show a distinct nucleotide preference, with enriched U at positions –4, –3, 1, 4, and 9, and enriched G at positions –2, –1, and 2. mRNA m6A sites in this species display increased A at positions –3, –2, –1, 2, and 9, and C at position 1. These trends are again consistent with the sequence preferences seen in mRNAs.

For Danio rerio, m6A sites tend to have a higher frequency of A, while non-m6A sites contain more U. Compared with mRNAs, lncRNA m6A sites exhibit less C, which may be attributed to the limited availability of lncRNA data in this species, leading to less pronounced sequence patterns and, consequently, partial differences in similarity between lncRNA and mRNA.

For Homo sapien, lncRNA m6A sites exhibit enrichment of cytosine (C) and uracil (U) at positions –2 and –1, along with increased guanine (G) downstream of the modification site. In contrast, mRNA m6A sites show enrichment of adenine (A) and G at positions –2 and –1, and elevated C at positions 1, 2, and 3. These nucleotide distributions closely resemble those observed in mRNAs. Similar patterns are also observed in Mus musculus and Rattus norvegicus.

For Mus musculus, lncRNA m6A sites exhibit significant enrichment of C and U at positions −2 and −1, along with a clear increase in G content downstream. Non-m6A sites show enrichment of A at positions −3, −2, and −1, and more C at positions 2 and 3. Additionally, lncRNA and mRNA display similar sequence distribution patterns.

In Rattus norvegicus, m6A sites in lncRNAs show notable enrichment of C and U at positions −2 and −1, with a tendency for increased G at position 3. Non-m6A sites tend to accumulate more C in the downstream region (e.g. position 2 and beyond). Although the number of rat lncRNA m6A sites is relatively small, making the sequence feature analysis less statistically significant, the distribution patterns between lncRNA and mRNA still show certain similarities.

Overall, the similarity in nucleotide composition between m6A sites across lncRNAs and mRNAs supports the feasibility of learning transferable sequence representations with DSNm6A. Furthermore, the high degree of motif similarity among Homo sapiens, Mus musculus, and Rattus norvegicus aligns with their superior cross-species prediction performance, reinforcing the generalizability of DSNm6A across closely related species. These findings offer a mechanistic explanation for the effectiveness of both cross-RNA and cross-species transferability in m6A site prediction by DSNm6A.

Biological validation

To verify whether DSNm6A truly captures biologically meaningful features, we performed an analysis on the prediction results. Specifically, we conducted a significance analysis on subsequences of different lengths to identify which subsequences DSNm6A predicts as m6A. First, we selected high-confidence samples (prediction probability >.9) from DS_H, and identified subsequences that occurred significantly more frequently than others ( <.05). We then compared these results with the subsequence frequencies of all positive samples from DS_H. The approach involved identifying the subsequence with the highest frequency within the same length group, then excluding it from the analysis and repeating the process with the remaining subsequences until no subsequence showed significantly higher frequency than the others. Through this stepwise filtering process, we were able to identify subsequences with important biological significance in the prediction results and further validate the ability of DSNm6A to learn biological features.

The specific results are shown in Fig. 8. The Pearson correlation coefficient between the frequencies of high-confidence samples and positive samples is 0.9195, indicating that DSNm6A has learned the data distribution of true methylation sites. Furthermore, we conducted additional analysis on the subsequences predicted by DSNm6A and found that some of them align with findings from existing literature. For example, GAC and ACC were found in 90% of m6A sites [36]. And TGACA aligns with the distribution trend of DRACH motifs in the SARS-CoV-2 genome. According to previous studies, the abundance of DRACH motifs in SARS-CoV-2 shows a nonrandom distribution, and TGACA is one of the top 15 most common DRACH motifs, appearing 5.9 occurrences/genome [37]. This finding suggests that TGACA may play an important role in the m6A modification and immune evasion of SARS-CoV-2. Additionally, the m6A modification sites on LINC00667 is TGACC, which is also significantly identified by our model [38]. It shows that our analysis demonstrates that the model effectively captures biologically meaningful features and identifies key sequence patterns associated with known m6A modifications.

Conclusion

In this paper, we have developed DSNm6A, a novel DL framework specifically developed for predicting m6A sites in lncRNAs. By leveraging a transfer learning strategy from the mRNA domain to the lncRNA domain, DSNm6A effectively utilizes prior knowledge to address the data scarcity and complexity inherent in lncRNA m6A site prediction. DSNm6A integrates complementary multifaceted encoding schemes, incorporating One-Hot, NPPF, and PSP with a hybrid deep architecture comprising CNN, Bi-LSTM, and BERT. The encoding schemes provide diverse and informative RNA sequence features, while the multimodule architecture enables DSNm6A to extract local motifs, model global dependencies, and capture deep contextual semantics associated with m6A sites. Through this joint design, DSNm6A effectively learns robust and transferable representations of m6A sites across mRNAs and lncRNAs.

Extensive experiments, including five-fold cross-validation and independent testing, demonstrate that DSNm6A achieves superior performance compared with existing methods, with improvements in prediction accuracy, robustness, and generalization. Furthermore, cross-species evaluations on Mus musculus, Rattus norvegicus, Danio rerio, and Arabidopsis thaliana confirm the generalizability of DSNm6A across diverse biological domains. Overall, our study provides a practical and effective solution for cross-RNA m6A site prediction and offers valuable insights into the development of transferable models for epi transcriptomic analysis.

In future work, we aim to enhance m6A site prediction through two strategies. First, we will incorporate sequence motifs, domains, and RNA secondary or tertiary structure information to provide a more comprehensive representation of m6A site features. Second, we will develop refined encoding schemes for nucleotide functional groups and chemical bonds, capturing nucleotide chemical and structural properties from multiple dimensions to more effectively extract key features associated with m6A sites.
