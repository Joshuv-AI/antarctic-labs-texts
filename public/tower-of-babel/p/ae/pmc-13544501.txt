Correlation of Local Nasal Biomarkers and Olfactory Dysfunction in Chronic Rhinosinusitis: A Systematic Review and Meta‐Analysis
Authors: David Kayekjian, Neeti Gandra, Shaun A Nguyen, Shaina W Twardus, Rodney J Schlosser
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundTo systematically evaluate the local biomarkers correlated with olfactory dysfunction (OD) in chronic rhinosinusitis (CRS).MethodsA systematic review was conducted in CINAHL, Cochrane Library, PubMed, and Scopus from inception to July 2025. Studies on CRS patients who underwent olfactory testing and measurement of biomarkers within olfactory mucus were included. Collected variables included CRS phenotype, olfactory test, mucus protein levels, quantification method, and reported correlation coefficients. Meta‐analysis of outcome measures in 351 participants included continuous measures (mean), proportions (%), and correlations (r) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs).ResultsSeven prospective studies met criteria, with 4 studies comprising 201 patients with CRS with nasal polyps (CRSwNP) and 150 with CRS without nasal polyps (CRSsNP) for meta‐analysis. The CRSwNP and CRSsNP cohorts were 53.08% and 58.09% male, respectively, with a mean age of 49.09 (1.36) and 46.84 (0.4), respectively. In participants with CRSwNP, IL‐5, IL‐6, IL‐10, IL‐13, IgE, CCL2, and CCL3 were inversely correlated with olfactory function scores (r = −0.223 to −0.333; all p ≤ 0.016). CXCL5 and VEGF‐A were positively correlated in CRSwNP (r = 0.240–0.247, both p < 0.01). Other Type 2 cytokines, including IL‐4, IL‐23, and IL‐33, did not show any significant correlations. In CRSsNP, IL‐8 was negatively correlated (r = −0.182, p = 0.028) and CXCL5 was positively correlated with olfactory function scores (r = 0.274, p = 0.01).ConclusionOD in CRSwNP is moderately or mildly associated with specific Type 2 cytokines and other biomarkers. OD in CRSsNP is weakly correlated with IL8 and CXCL5, but not Type 2 cytokines. Further prospective studies are needed to elucidate causal relationships and prognostic utility between local biomarkers and OD.Keywords: biomarkers, chronic rhinosinusitis, mucus, nasal polyps, olfactory dysfunction

Introduction

Olfactory dysfunction is a highly prevalent and clinically significant feature of chronic rhinosinusitis (CRS), affecting approximately 72.5% of patients [1]. Olfactory dysfunction represents an important clinical consideration due to its substantial impact on quality of life, including effects on safety, nutrition, and mental health. The association between olfactory dysfunction and CRS is thought to be multifactorial, involving both conductive mechanisms, such as obstruction from nasal polyps and mucosal edema, and sensorineural injury resulting from damage to the olfactory neuroepithelium. Despite this, relatively few studies have examined the role of specific inflammatory endotypes or the relationship between local nasal cytokine profiles and olfactory dysfunction [2].

CRS is a heterogenous inflammatory condition characterized by infiltration of multiple immune cell populations, including T helper cells, cytotoxic T cells, natural killer cells, B cells, eosinophils, neutrophils, mast cells, basophils, innate lymphoid cells, macrophages, and dendritic cells [3]. This complex immune response results in the release of various cytokines, including interleukins (IL)‐4, IL‐5, IL‐13, IL‐17A, and interferon‐gamma (IFN‐γ) [3]. CRS can be broadly categorized into inflammatory endotypes: Type 1 (viral/intracellular; IFN‐γ–mediated), Type 2 (eosinophilic; IL‐4, IL‐5, IL‐13–mediated), and Type 3 (bacterial/fungal; Th17/neutrophil‐driven) [3].

CRS with nasal polyps (CRSwNP) is predominantly associated with Type 2 inflammation, characterized by elevated levels of IL‐4, IL‐5, IL‐13, and immunoglobulin E (IgE), and is often linked to tissue remodeling marked by edema and polyp formation [4]. In contrast, CRS without nasal polyps (CRSsNP) is more commonly associated with Type 1 inflammation, with key mediators including IFN‐γ, transforming growth factor‐beta (TGF‐β), and elevated levels of IL‐6 and IL‐8; this phenotype is typically associated with fibrosis and remodeling driven by TGF‐β [4, 5, 6].

Prior evidence suggests a mechanistic link between inflammation and olfactory loss that extends beyond simple mechanical obstruction. Chronic inflammation may directly damage the olfactory epithelium, resulting in sensorineural dysfunction. Proposed mechanisms include cytokine‐mediated injury to olfactory neurons, disruption of olfactory signal transduction pathways, and chronic epithelial remodeling [3]. In particular, Type 2 cytokines such as IL‐4 and IL‐13 promote the recruitment and activation of eosinophils and mast cells, leading to tissue edema, increased IgE production, and structural remodeling that may impair olfactory function [7].

However, prior studies examining the relationship between inflammatory biomarkers and olfactory outcomes have demonstrated mixed results, often limited by small sample sizes and lack of stratification by CRS phenotype [2, 8, 9]. There remains a lack of consistent data correlating local nasal biomarkers with objective measures of olfactory function, highlighting the need for a better understanding of how specific inflammatory profiles contribute to olfactory dysfunction and may serve as potential therapeutic targets. The objective of this study was to evaluate the relationship between local nasal inflammatory biomarkers and olfactory dysfunction in patients with CRS.

Methods

Data Collection and Selection

This study was conducted and reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [10]. The objective of this study was to systematically evaluate local biomarkers associated with olfactory dysfunction in CRSwNP and CRSsNP. A comprehensive literature search was performed in PubMed (National Library of Medicine/National Institutes of Health), Scopus (Elsevier), CINAHL Complete (EBSCOhost), and the Cochrane Library (Wiley) from database inception to July 2025 by authors D.K. and S.W.T. Search terms included keywords related to CRS, cytokines, and other biomarkers (e.g., chemokines, ILs), and olfaction. The search strategy was adapted to meet the requirements of each database, and the full search strategy is provided in the Supporting Information.

References identified from the four databases were uploaded into Covidence (Veritas Health Innovation Ltd., Melbourne, Australia) for title and abstract screening, followed by full‐text review. Screening was performed independently by authors D.K. and S.W.T., with disagreements resolved through discussion or resolution by a third author (S.A.N.) when needed.

Selection Criteria

Studies were included if they were published in English, involved participants aged ≥ 18 years, and evaluated a CRSwNP and/or CRSsNP population with reported, extractable data on local mucus or nasal secretion biomarkers and their respective correlational coefficients with olfactory dysfunction scores from the Sniffin’ Sticks Threshold, Discrimination, and Identification (TDI) scores, Smell Identification Test (SIT) scores, or the Sino‐Nasal Outcome Test (SNOT‐22) question 21 on the sense of smell. Studies involving only CRS participants without stratification by phenotype, non‐CRS etiologies of olfactory dysfunction, or focusing on tissue or systemic biomarkers were excluded. Eligible study designs included observational studies (cohort, case‐control, and cross‐sectional), while case reports and case series were excluded.

Data Extraction

Data were independently extracted by authors D.K. and S.W.T., with disagreements resolved by a third author (S.A.N.) when necessary. Extracted variables included study characteristics (e.g., title, year of publication, and authors), patient demographics, and outcome data, including correlation coefficients.

Quality Assessment

The level of evidence for each article was assessed using the Oxford Centre for Evidence‐Based‐Medicine (OCEBM). Risk of bias was assessed using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Critical Appraisal Checklists. Each study was evaluated using the appropriate checklist for case‐control (8 items) or cross‐sectional studies (10 items). Each item was scored as “1” for yes and “0” for no, unclear, or not applicable. A total score was calculated as the sum of all items. Studies were included in the analysis if more than 70% of checklist items were rated as “yes.” Risk of bias assessments were conducted by one author (N.G.) and independently verified by a second author (D.K.).

Statistical Analysis

Meta‐analyses of baseline characteristics were performed using mean differences (MDs) for continuous variables and odds ratios (ORs) for categorical variables in Cochrane Review Manager (RevMan), version 5.4 (The Cochrane Collaboration, United Kingdom, 2020). Effect estimates were reported with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) and weighted according to study sample size. A meta‐analysis of correlation coefficients was conducted to evaluate the relationship between specific biomarkers and olfactory outcomes. Pooled Spearman correlation coefficients with 95% CIs were calculated using MedCalc version 20.011 (MedCalc Software Ltd.). The following thresholds were used for subjective assessment of correlation values (r): 0–0.3, small (mild); 0.3–0.5, medium (moderate); ≥0.5, large (strong) [11]. Spearman correlation coefficients from studies using either the composite TDI score or the SIT were used, as both the TDI and SIT are validated psychophysical tests of olfactory function with established strong convergent validity. Though the tests are distinct psychophysical tests, the SIT has shown good correlation with the identification subcomponent of the TDI as well as composite TDI scores (r > 0.80 for both) [12, 13]. To assess the robustness of pooled estimates and address combining of psychophysical tests, a leave‐one‐out sensitivity analysis for biomarkers reported from at least three studies was performed. Specifically, the study utilizing the SIT was excluded while retaining the other studies utilizing the composite TDI score, in order to ensure a minimum of two studies were used for meta‐correlational analysis.

Heterogeneity was assessed using the χ 2 test and quantified with the I 2 statistic. A random‐effects model was used in the presence of significant heterogeneity (p < 0.05), whereas a fixed‐effects model was applied when heterogeneity was not significant (p ≥ 0.05) [14, 15]. Publication bias was assessed through visual inspection of funnel plots and quantitatively using Egger's regression test to evaluate funnel plot asymmetry [16, 17]. A p‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all analyses.

Results

Study Characteristics

After exclusion of duplicates, 6554 titles and abstracts were screened, of which 251 full‐text articles underwent eligibility review. A total of 7 studies with 539 participants were included in the systematic review, with 4 studies comprising 351 participants eligible for meta‐analysis due to overlapping local biomarkers, olfactory dysfunction testing, and correlation coefficients (Figure 1) [8, 18, 19, 20, 21, 22, 23]. Studies were published between 2016 and 2022 and conducted in the United States (4), China (1), Russia (1), and Sweden (1). One study used a case‐control design, and six were cross‐sectional, with Oxford Levels of Evidence of 2c for 6 studies and 3 for 1 study (Table 1). Most studies used the composite TDI score to assess olfactory function (six datasets), while one study used the SIT. Cytometric bead array was the most common method for quantifying mucus biomarkers, particularly in olfactory cleft mucus; other studies assayed anterior nares or nasal cavity secretions using ELISA or enzyme immunoassay (Table 1).

Out of the 351 participants who were included in the meta‐analysis from 4 studies, 201 were diagnosed with CRSwNP and 150 with CRSsNP. CRSwNP and CRSsNP cohorts were similar with respect to sex distribution, smoking status, alcohol use, and mean SNOT‐22 scores. CRSwNP patients were, however, slightly older than CRSsNP patients (mean 49.09 ± 1.36 vs. 46.84 ± 0.40 years; MD: 1.18, 95% CI: 0.09–2.28, p = 0.03) (Table 2). Comorbid Type 2‐associated conditions were more prevalent in CRSwNP. Allergic rhinitis, asthma, and aspirin‐exacerbated respiratory disease/aspirin intolerance were significantly more frequent among CRSwNP participants, as was a history of prior endoscopic sinus surgery (all p < 0.05; Table 2). CRSwNP participants also demonstrated more extensive radiographic disease burden, with higher Lund–Mackay scores (MD: 6.82, 95% CI: 5.75–7.89, p < 0.00001). Olfactory performance was significantly worse in CRSwNP across all psychophysical domains. CRSwNP patients had lower composite TDI scores than CRSsNP patients (MD: −7.68, 95% CI: −11.52 to −3.84, p = 0.0001), as well as lower TDI subscores (all p < 0.00001; Table 2).

Quality Assessment

All seven studies met the prespecified quality threshold on JBI checklists, with more than 70% of items rated “yes,” indicating generally high methodological quality (Table S1). The main sources of potential bias were limited identification of confounders and a lack of explicit strategies to address them. Funnel plot inspection and Egger's test (2.22, 95% CI: −9.55 to 14.10, p = 0.6473) suggested minimal publication bias, with little funnel asymmetry (Figure S1).

The four studies included in the meta‐analysis used similar eligibility criteria, typically excluding patients with ESS within at least 6 months, systemic autoimmune or inflammatory/immune‐mediated disease, non‐CRS causes of olfactory dysfunction, or recent systemic immunosuppressive therapy within 4 weeks. All four quantified olfactory cleft mucus biomarkers using cytometric bead array and used Spearman correlation coefficients to relate biomarker levels to olfactory outcomes; olfactory dysfunction was assessed with the TDI in three studies and the SIT in one.

CRSwNP

In CRSwNP, several olfactory cleft mucus biomarkers demonstrated statistically significant correlations with olfactory dysfunction, although most correlations were small in magnitude (Table 3). Pooled analyses showed inverse correlations between TDI (or SIT) scores and the Type 2 cytokines IL‐5 (r = −0.289, 95% CI: −0.413 to −0.155, p = 0.001) and IL‐13 (r = −0.281, 95% CI: −0.405 to −0.146, p = 0.001), as well as non‐Type 2 cytokines IL‐6 (r = −0.280, 95% CI: −0.405 to −0.145, p = 0.001) and IL‐10 (r = −0.243, 95% CI: −0.371 to −0.105, p = 0.001) (Table 3). Total IgE (r = −0.223, 95% CI: −0.388 to −0.043, p = 0.016) and chemokines CCL2 (r = −0.333, 95% CI: −0.483 to −0.165, p = 0.001) and CCL3 (r = −0.250, 95% CI: −0.410 to −0.075, p = 0.005) were also inversely associated with olfactory function scores, with CCL2 showing the strongest pooled correlation in the medium or moderate range (Table 3).

In contrast, VEGF‐A (r = 0.240, 95% CI: 0.064–0.401, p = 0.008) and CXCL5 (r = 0.247, 95% CI: 0.0716–0.407, p = 0.006) were positively correlated with olfactory function scores in CRSwNP (Table 3). Other Type 2‐associated biomarkers, including IL‐4, IL‐23, and IL‐33, did not show significant pooled correlations with olfactory function scores. Likewise, IL‐2, IL‐8, IL‐9, IL‐12, IL‐17A, IL‐17F, TNF‐α, and several chemokines and growth factors were not significantly associated with TDI or SIT scores in pooled analyses (Table S2).

Single‐study, non‐pooled analyses identified additional candidate biomarkers associated with olfactory function scores in CRSwNP (Table 4). In one study, neuron‐specific enolase levels in anterior nares mucus and LTE4 in nasal cavity secretions were each inversely correlated with TDI scores (Table 4). In another, model‐based estimates suggested that higher olfactory cleft IL‐6, IL‐7, and VEGF‐A levels were associated with worse composite TDI performance. When TDI components were examined separately, VEGF‐A remained significantly associated with threshold and discrimination, whereas IL‐6 was associated with identification, and IL‐7 with all three subdomains (Table 4).

CRSsNP

In CRSsNP, the pooled biomarker profile differed from that observed in CRSwNP (Table 3). No Type 2 cytokines demonstrated significant correlations with olfactory test scores in meta‐analysis (Table 3). Instead, IL‐8 (r = −0.182, 95% CI: −0.335 to −0.020, p = 0.028) showed a weak inverse association with olfactory function scores, while CXCL5 (r = 0.274, 95% CI: 0.023–0.527, p = 0.01) was positively correlated with olfactory function scores (Table 3).

Single‐study, non‐pooled analyses identified additional candidate biomarkers in CRSsNP (Table 4). Neuron‐specific enolase levels in anterior nares mucus demonstrated a negative correlation with TDI scores in one study, similar to findings in CRSwNP (Table 4). Overall, the lack of significant Type 2 cytokine correlations and the prominence of neutrophil‐associated chemokines such as IL‐8 and CXCL5 support a distinct inflammatory profile underlying olfactory dysfunction in CRSsNP (Tables 3 and 4).

Sensitivity Analysis

Leave‐one‐out sensitivity analyses were performed for all biomarkers with at least three contributing studies in each phenotype (Table S3). When the SIT‐based study was excluded and only TDI‐based datasets were retained, pooled correlation coefficients changed by less than 0.05 in absolute value for all biomarkers, and all previously significant associations remained statistically significant in both CRSwNP and CRSsNP cohorts (all p‐values remained < 0.05). These findings indicate that the direction and magnitude of associations between local mucus biomarkers and olfactory function scores were robust to the choice of olfactory test and to the influence of any single study.

Discussion

This systematic review and meta‐analysis shows that olfactory function scores in CRSwNP is mildly to moderately inversely correlated with some but not all Type 2 inflammatory biomarkers. Lower olfactory function scores reflect worse olfactory function; thus, inverse (negative) correlations indicate that higher local biomarker concentrations correspond to more severe smell loss and olfactory dysfunction. Olfactory dysfunction in CRSsNP demonstrated an alternative local biomarker profile with mild correlations with IL‐8 and CXCL‐5, while having no significant correlations with Type 2 inflammatory biomarkers. With current evidence in the literature remaining limited to individual studies, these pooled results further substantiate the evidence of local inflammatory biomarkers correlating with olfactory dysfunction in CRS [8, 18, 20, 21, 22]. Clinical practice and research have been shifting from systemic and tissue‐based biomarkers to local mucus biomarkers [8, 20, 24, 25]. Thus, the findings of the present study provide an essential reference point for biomarkers that can be obtained in a non‐invasive manner over repeated longitudinal assessments, can reflect anatomic specific insight from a local inflammatory microenvironment, and can be generalized to a broader, medically managed CRS population that may never undergo surgery [2, 20, 26, 27].

Of note, IL‐5 and IL‐13 were mildly, yet significantly inversely correlated with olfactory function scores in CRSwNP, with persistence of significance after leave‐one‐out analysis. This corresponds with Soler et al.’s endotype cluster analysis showing that the two clusters associated with the greatest olfactory dysfunction were dominated by IL‐5, IL‐13, as well as IgE, which was also significantly inversely correlated with olfactory function scores in the present study, albeit in only two pooled studies [2]. Apart from the four meta‐analyzed studies in the present study, our previous work has demonstrated significant inverse correlations between IL‐5 and TDI scores and Zhang et al. confirmed that IL‐5 was a strong predictor of poor olfactory outcomes after endoscopic sinus surgery in patients with nasal polyposis [20, 28]. However, previous work, contrary to the meta‐analysis in the present study, found no correlation between IL‐13 and objective olfactory scores [20]. This inconsistency and overall mild correlations are evidence against a simple causal relationship between local biomarkers and olfactory dysfunction and demonstrate that local biomarkers can only in part explain olfactory dysfunction. In search of their role in smell loss, multi‐step pathway models have been described showing the potential downstream effects of IL‐5 such as eosinophil recruitment and activation leading to cytotoxic granule protein release, olfactory sensory neuron apoptosis and disruption of basal cell neurogenesis, and neuroepithelial erosion, and of IL‐13 such as epithelial barrier dysfunction, mucus hyperproduction, and tissue remodeling leading to mechanical obstruction of the olfactory cleft and altered olfactory mucus composition [7, 28, 29, 30, 31]. This biological plausibility is further suggested with randomized controlled trials and meta‐analyses showing modest to robustly improved olfactory function in participants on T2 pathway blocking biologics such as dupilumab, mepolizumab, omalizumab, and benralizumab [32, 33, 34, 35, 36, 37]. However, IL‐4 was not significantly correlated to olfactory function in this meta‐analysis, raising the question as to whether dupilumab's effects are mediated more so through the blockage of IL‐13. Furthermore, the causality of local biomarkers and olfactory dysfunction remains unproven due to potential confounding biologic effects of mechanical obstruction reduction and mouse models demonstrating mature sensory neurons remaining unaffected in the presence of elevated IL‐4, IL‐5, and IL‐13 in the olfactory epithelium [32, 33, 38]. Thus, rather than directly causing olfactory dysfunction, local biomarkers may reflect downstream neuroepithelial inflammation and tissue injury, serving as surrogate markers of the underlying inflammatory processes rather than independent mechanistic drivers of olfactory dysfunction.

The two other main biomarkers inversely, yet mildly correlated to olfactory function scores in CRSwNP were not Type 2 inflammatory markers, including IL‐6 and IL‐10, which have many speculatively proposed mechanisms linking them to chronic inflammation and olfactory stem cell failure [24, 39, 40, 41]. However, again, a causal role cannot be defined due to IL‐6's relation to olfactory dysfunction seen in multiple diseases other than CRS, and mechanistic evidence for IL‐6 and IL‐10 coming from animal models and non‐olfactory neural systems [41, 42]. The only biomarkers with positive correlations with olfactory function scores, indicating high biomarker concentrations were associated with better olfactory function, were VEGF‐A and CXCL‐5, though only seen with two pooled studies. Schlosser et al. posited that the VEGF‐A's neutrophic and neovascularization effects may improve olfactory neuron function, though this was purely speculative [20]. CXLC‐5, positively correlated with olfactory function scores in both CRSwNP and CRSsNP, has also displayed potential angiogenic properties and wound healing implications in other contexts, such as diabetic foot ulcers, but remains unexplored and unsubstantiated in CRS [43].

All four meta‐analyzed studies individually found certain but few biomarkers correlated with olfactory dysfunction in CRSsNP but when pooled together, the majority of local biomarkers demonstrated non‐significant correlations, except for the mild correlation coefficients of IL‐8 and CXCL5, indicating a potentially different mechanism of olfactory dysfunction [22, 44]. IL‐8 is an ELR+ CXC chemokine that plays a part in the activation of neutrophil pathways, coinciding with CRSsNP being shown to be characterized by neutrophil‐dominant inflammation; however, in an Asian population [45, 46]. CXCL5 is also an ELR + CXC chemokine but possesses anti‐inflammatory properties, as previously stated, potentially explaining its positive correlation compared to IL‐8's negative correlation as a purely pro‐inflammatory biomarker [43]. However, these two chemokines have not been extensively examined in the context of objective olfactory scores, warranting further investigation.

Though studies aforementioned have attempted discovering the mechanistic role of local biomarkers in smell loss, the findings for CRSwNP and CRSsNP in this present study must be interpreted cautiously. First, while statistically significant, most significant correlation coefficients were weak to moderate in magnitude, suggesting that local inflammatory profiles may only account for a modest proportion of olfactory dysfunction in CRS. Second, olfactory dysfunction in CRS is multifactorial in nature, involving conductive obstruction, olfactory epithelial injury, and central olfactory processing changes, none of which can be explained by a single‐timepoint mucus cytokine measurement. Also, correlation does not establish causation, and it remains unclear whether biomarker levels drive neuronal dysfunction, reflect secondary epithelial damage, or simultaneously occur with other unmeasured pathologic processes. Thus, the present data must be interpreted as associational rather than mechanistically explanatory, and it cannot imply that local biomarker levels are sufficient to produce olfactory loss.

Furthermore, the results of this meta‐analysis and the difficulty of finding a pattern of olfactory‐related cytokines across the literature in CRSsNP suggest that this population is composed of heterogenous endotypes and requires more granular classifications [20]. Even in the CRSwNP, cytokine correlations of olfactory loss are heterogenous across and within phenotypes. The traditional CRSwNP/CRSsNP dichotomous classification treats olfactory dysfunction as a symptom that is more severe in the CRSwNP phenotype. However, current evidence demonstrates that phenotype alone is insufficient and that olfactory dysfunction tracks better with endotype. Several studies across continents have demonstrated this, such as from Europe, New Zealand, China, and the United States, suggesting up to 6–10 endotypes, each with various inflammatory profiles, clinical behavior, and heterogenous olfactory dysfunction [47, 48, 49, 50, 51]. In a real‐world clinical setting, this becomes critical in providing patient‐centered precision medicine through endotype‐guided biologic selection. Though olfactory therapies target Type 2 inflammation, the non‐Type 2 correlations in our study and evidence for non‐Type 2 endotypes warrant future endotype‐ and biomarker‐specific drug development beyond T2 signatures. Also, future randomized controlled trials should assess whether the most robustly correlated local biomarkers with olfactory dysfunction in this study and in the growing body of evidence could predict treatment‐specific outcomes with biologics or anti‐inflammatory medications, to guide therapy selection before initiating expensive, long‐term treatment. Finally, future studies should include mechanistic designs to elucidate causality of local biomarkers in CRS associated olfactory dysfunction.

Limitations

The present study has important limitations to consider. First, participants from North America were the predominant population included in the meta‐analysis, preventing generalizability of findings due to the heterogenous inflammatory make up of patients across regions [2]. Second, meta‐analysis of studies with cross‐sectional design cannot be used to infer causality of the analyzed cytokines, nor can it be used to assess any temporal dynamics due to single time‐point measurements. Third, due to sparseness of extractable data on healthy controls, there was a lack of comparisons with healthy controls in the pooled analysis. This prevents addressing the question if these local biomarkers associated with olfactory dysfunction are specific to CRS. Fourth, this systematic review includes various mucus sampling sites which can introduce meaningful variability in biomarker interpretation. Olfactory cleft sampling seems to provide the most anatomically relevant data due to it being the most direct assessment of the microenvironment surrounding olfactory neurons, and thus to minimize sampling site confounders, the meta‐correlations in this present study were limited to studies sampling olfactory cleft [20]. However, middle meatus concentrations have now been well correlated with both olfactory cleft and ethmoid tissue levels, with nasal lavage and anterior nares sampling possessing the least validation [8, 52]. Nonetheless, the optimal sampling site remains an unresolved challenge and the standardization of collection methods is still needed [27]. Fifth, the meta‐analysis of correlation coefficients, though it mainly included studies using TDI composite scores, did include one study using the SIT. The TDI and SIT are distinct psychophysical tests of olfactory function. However, studies have documented a good correlation between tests, and this limitation was addressed with the leave‐one‐out sensitivity analysis. Finally, in order to reduce heterogeneity of sampling techniques, statistical measures, local environments, biomarker analysis, and olfactory dysfunction outcomes, the meta‐analysis was limited to four studies, with leave‐one‐out analysis including three studies at a time. Thus, power was limited, particularly with the smaller CRSsNP cohort. Therefore, the results should be interpreted with caution.

Conclusion

The results of this systematic review and meta‐analysis demonstrates that olfactory dysfunction in CRSwNP is mildly, yet significantly correlated with specific but not all Type 2 biomarkers such as IL‐5 and IL‐13, as well as with non‐Type 2 biomarkers such as IL‐6 and IL‐10, with persistence of significance after leave‐one‐out analysis. In contrast, olfactory dysfunction in CRSsNP is mildly correlated with IL‐8 and CXCL5, but not with Type 2 cytokines. These findings provide a reference point for non‐invasive, longitudinal biomarker assessment and suggest that future randomized controlled trials should investigate whether baseline local inflammatory profiles can predict therapeutic outcomes, and whether these biomarkers carry a causal relationship with olfactory dysfunction.
