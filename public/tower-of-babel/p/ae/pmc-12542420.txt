Maternity care professionals’ views on good counselling for prenatal anomaly screening: a qualitative inquiry
Authors: Joyce M Kors, Rashmi A Kusurkar, Lidewij Henneman, Corine J M Verhoeven, Petra C A M Bakker, Janneke T Gitsels-van der Wal, Evi Oosterhoorn, Sifra de Groot-van der Linden, Saskia M Peerdeman, Linda Martin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMaternity care professionals find the art of counselling for prenatal screening challenging. An approach that could help professionals support patients' self-regulated behavior, enabling them to make their own choices, is autonomy-supportive counselling, which is based on the self-determination theory. This study aimed to gain insight into professionals' views on good counselling. Understanding their perspectives can shed light on the underlying mechanisms that drive professionals to focus on providing information and options rather than on decision-making support. These insights, in turn, can be instrumental in tailoring education.MethodsThis qualitative survey study was conducted in the Netherlands among Dutch maternity care professionals involved in counselling for prenatal screening. Thematic analysis was used to analyze the answers to the open-ended question: In your opinion, what constitutes ‘good’ counselling for prenatal anomaly screening? Next, the research team discussed the identified themes and related these themes to the concept of autonomy-supportive counselling. Lastly, the researchers conducted a quantitative analysis of the qualitative data to draw attention to specific patterns. ResultsOf 2813 invited registered counsellors, 1426 were included in the study. The themes regarding the context of good counselling were time, counsellors’ competence, and ethical sensitivity. Exploring the parents' values was found to be an overarching theme in the interaction between patients and professionals during the process of good counselling. Almost all participants mentioned providing information and facilitating free choice as essential themes in the interaction before and during decision-making. Other identified themes were exploring the impact of the test result on the parents and guiding. Regarding the outcome, the most frequently mentioned theme was parents making their own choices, other themes were making the right choice and no assurance of getting a healthy baby. ConclusionsMost participants consider that in good counselling, important themes are providing information as a mechanism, and parents making their own free choices as an outcome. The results and discourse reflect a more non-directive attitude before and during the decision-making process. Since a non-directive attitude can complicate parents’ more autonomous decision-making, we suggest paying attention to more directive guidance by providing structure to facilitate parents’ more autonomous decision-making.Keywords: Autonomy-supportive counselling, Prenatal screening, Maternity care

Background

Prenatal screening programs generally comprise first-trimester screening for aneuploidies and first and second-trimester screening for structural fetal anomalies [1, 2]. In the past few years, the scope and timing of prenatal screening have been broadened through improved techniques and broader availability of the tests [2–4]. Since the introduction of non-invasive prenatal testing (NIPT) in 2011, its use has increased to 11 million tests yearly in more than 60 countries [5]. This trend means a rise in the number of people who need pre-test counselling, which enables parents to make a well-informed decision about whether to pursue testing. The counselling should be personalised and should provide sufficient information to promote autonomous decision-making [6]. However, counselling is an art that includes much more than providing information; counsellors help counselees to choose between various options considering their experiences, values, and norms [7]. This art is challenging for the professionals involved in counselling for prenatal screening [7]. Prenatal screening is offered internationally in diverse healthcare systems. Many systems have opted for a program in which initial counselling is provided by midwives, genetic counsellors, or specialised nurses, usually with specific training and certification. Obstetricians and family doctors may also be involved [8].

The basic principles of contemporary counselling for prenatal screening are based on 'person-centerd counselling', an approach that was developed by the psychologist Carl Rogers. He stated that people can make choices appropriate to their lives at a given moment in time [9]. In other words, parents can make their own choices on whether to carry out prenatal screening. During the decision-making process, the professional helps by adopting a non-directive attitude; the client is not directed towards a particular choice but encouraged to come up with their own ideas, leading to an autonomous, informed choice [10, 11]. Hence, counsellors ideally have a non-directive attitude towards the content of the decision but provide directive guidance during the decision-making process. Directive guidance offers parents structure to make autonomous choices, for example, by making a decision balance [10], whereas autonomy without structure can become laissez-faire [12].

Kater-Kuipers (2020) [13] described three possible phases of prenatal counselling. The first phase involves exploring the parents’ personal values and attitudes towards prenatal screening. This phase promotes awareness of the freedom of choice; the parent may accept or reject the information about prenatal screening and the screening offer itself. The second phase involves providing parents with written and oral information about prenatal screening. In addition to medical-technical information, professionals must also support parents’ decision-making process. The final phase, post-test counselling, occurs after the screening and concerns follow-up and support if parents receive an abnormal test result [12]. In the context of pre-test counselling, Kater-Kuipers (2020) identified four elements for good prenatal screening: exploring parents' values and attitudes, awareness of freedom of choice, providing information, and decision-making support [13].

Given maternity care professionals’ strong views about non-directiveness in prenatal counselling to support parents’ autonomy in choosing or refusing prenatal screening, professionals search for ways to help the parents in their decision-making process [14]. Despite the relatively favorable counselling context, in which counsellors must participate in continuing professional education, which has resulted in positive effects, there is room for improvement [15]. Professionals tend to focus on providing information during decision-making instead of offering decision-making support [15, 16].

Autonomy-supportive counselling based on the Self-Determination Theory (SDT) could help professionals support patients' decision-making process [17]. SDT, a macro-theory of human motivation, postulates that the quality of motivation is arranged along a continuum, with autonomous motivation at one end and controlled motivation at the other. Autonomous motivation occurs when a person engages in specific behavior out of interest or because they find it essential, without perceptions of internal or external pressure. Controlled motivation, on the other hand, occurs when a person shows specific behavior for external reasons, such as receiving praise or avoiding feelings of guilt or shame or out of expectation of rewards [18] (Fig. 1). More autonomous forms of motivation stimulate more self-regulated behavior, which helps patients to make their own choices regarding their health [17, 19].

Self-determination theory continuum based on Deci and Ryan (1985)

Healthcare professionals can facilitate patients’ autonomous self-regulated behavior by fulfilling the three basic psychological needs: autonomy (feeling of ownership, endorsement, and choice), competence (feeling of capability and growth) and relatedness (feeling of belonging and connection) [18]. Professionals can meet patients’ needs using a need-supportive interaction style, contributing to an autonomy-supportive healthcare climate [17, 19]. In the present study, we looked at counselling for prenatal screening through the lens of autonomy-supportive counselling.

This qualitative study aimed to gain insight into maternity care professionals' views on ‘good’ counselling. Knowledge of these views might provide more insight into the mechanisms that explain professionals’ focus on providing information and offering screening options rather than decision-making support. Furthermore, these findings might help tailor education towards a more helpful approach in training professionals to provide decision-making support, given the expanded scope and the increasing complexity of prenatal screening.

Methods

Study design

This qualitative study of maternity care professionals' views on ‘good’ counselling for prenatal screening was analysed from an inductive data-driven approach, from a more constructivist paradigm [20]. This approach enabled us to inquire about the views on counselling for prenatal screening in maternity care. The survey was part of the TRIDENT-2 study, which evaluated the introduction of NIPT (see publication for more details about the study design and data collection [21]). This study made use of data collected during the pre-implementation survey. This survey included questions regarding maternity care professionals' knowledge of prenatal screening, background characteristics of the participating maternity care professionals and the open-ended question: ‘In your opinion, what constitutes ‘good’ counselling for prenatal anomaly screening?’ [21].

Study context

This study was conducted in the Netherlands, where counselling for prenatal screening tests is mainly done by midwives, sonographers, and obstetricians with a contract with one of the seven Regional Centers for Prenatal Screening. Only maternity care professionals with a contract are allowed to counsel for prenatal screening, they need their registration to order the test and send an invoice [22]. Approximately 85% of patients are counselled by a primary care midwife or a sonographer [23]. To ensure the quality of the counselling, the Regional Centers have formulated specific requirements, such as participating in training, gaining medical knowledge, and improving the counsellor’s counselling skills [24]. The centers also prescribe that counselling should take at least 35 minutes [22]. In the Netherlands, there is a strong awareness of the social and ethical ramifications of counselling for prenatal screening [14, 25–27].

Ethical approval

The study protocol was approved by the Medical Ethical Committee of the VU University Medical Center, Amsterdam, the Netherlands (no. 2017.165). Online informed consent was collected from all participating maternity care professionals.

Participants and procedure

All 2813 maternity care professionals registered as counsellors were invited to participate in the TRIDENT-2 survey study by email in February 2017 before NIPT was implemented in April 2017 [21]. This email contained a link to the online questionnaire. To ensure the quality of our study, three additional inclusion criteria were formulated: a) written informed consent must be obtained, b) the question: ‘In your opinion, what constitutes ‘good’ counselling for prenatal anomaly screening?’ must be answered, and c) the professional must be actively involved in counselling for prenatal screening, while having a contract with a Regional Center for Prenatal Screening.

Data analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to analyze the background characteristics of participating professionals. For the available variables, the study sample was compared with the national population of professionals involved in counselling for prenatal screening.

Thematic analysis was used to analyze the answers to the open-ended question: ‘In your opinion, what constitutes ‘good’ counselling for prenatal anomaly screening? [20, 28]. First, three researchers (EO, SdG and JK) independently performed initial open coding of the written answers using the MAXQDA software program. The unit of analysis was defined as an individual word or phrase found within a single text response. Complex answers were discussed within the research team until consensus was reached about what code should be used. Secondly, the first author grouped the resulting codes into a cohesive cluster and identified themes from the data. Themes were identified at a more semantic or explicit level [20]. Thirdly, the research team discussed the identified themes and related the themes to the concept of autonomy-supportive counselling. For that purpose, they used the figure proposed by Kors et al. (2020) [29] based on their realist review on autonomy-supportive consultation. For the present study, they explicitly used the healthcare professionals' perspective. (Fig. 2). The quotes presented in the present paper were translated into English with the help of a native English speaker with Dutch fluency. To check the translations, these fragments were translated back by a native Dutch speaker with English fluency.

Concept of autonomy-supportive counselling based on Kors et al. (2020)

Lastly, the researchers (EO, SdG, JK) conducted a quantitative analysis of the qualitative data following the method used by Kusurkar et.al (2022) [30]. The researchers established the number of comments for each theme. This quantification is meant to draw attention to specific patterns in the data, not to suggest generalizability of the sample. Quantification of qualitative data can be justified if all participants have had the opportunity to answer the same question and if the sample size is large enough, at least 50 [31]. The results are reported according to the recommendations for reporting the results of the quantification of qualitative data [31].

Reflexivity

In qualitative research, the involvement of a multidisciplinary research team can improve the quality of the analysis by incorporating diverse viewpoints [32]. For that reason, our research team consisted of ten persons with different backgrounds. All members found it essential that professionals offer decision-making support so parents can make autonomous choices regarding participation in prenatal screening. The differences in profession, work experience, life experience, age, philosophy of life, and cultural background made them view the importance of the different aspects of counseling for prenatal screening differently. For instance, if they had a background in an individualistic or collectivistic culture or if they had a physiological or more medical view on pregnancy. In the discussions during all phases of the study, all voices were given equal importance [33].

Results

Of the 2813 maternity care professionals registered as counsellors for prenatal anomaly screening who received that online survey, 1426 professionals met our inclusion criteria and were included, representing 50.7% of all registered Dutch prenatal screening counsellors (1426/2813) (Fig. 3).

Context of prenatal counselling

The themes mentioned under this heading are not part of the mechanisms governing the interaction between counsellor and counselee, but they could affect the counselling (Fig. 4).

Maternity care participants' views on good counselling

Time

Time occurred as a contextual theme for good counselling (71 individual words or phrases are related to this theme). Participants mentioned time to inform people and to deliberate on the possibilities and impossibilities of screening (Quote 1, Table 2) and time for the parents to think about their options and to arrive at a well-considered choice (Quote 2). Some participants mentioned that it is hard to allocate sufficient time for the encounter in which the counselling takes place. They also reported finding it hard to schedule the counselling early enough in the pregnancy so that there is enough time for the parents to consider and to make their decisions. Finally, it was noted that professionals find it hard to make the prescribed time for counselling, as they think the specified time is not always necessary (Quote 3).

Counsellors’ competence

Another contextual theme is the counsellors' competence (80); participants found it essential that counsellors have up-to-date knowledge to inform parents (Quote 4) and good communication skills, such as listening and asking open questions.

Ethical sensitivity

The participants who answered the question more broadly wrote more about the ethical aspects of prenatal screening, regarding the counselling of individual parents and the maternity care system (14) (Quote 5). Their answers sometimes seem to reflect their difficulties with counselling for prenatal screening (Quote 6). They seem to draw attention to the ethical dilemmas that prenatal screening may prompt.

Mechanisms of prenatal counselling

We looked for themes regarding the mechanisms, in this case, the interactions between professionals and parents during prenatal screening counselling, which were mentioned as core elements of good counselling. Among the mechanisms, themes were found to be related to the different phases of the decision-making process. The overarching themes are placed at the top; the themes that relate to the time before the decision-making are placed in the left column, those that are relevant during the decision-making are placed in the central column, and those relating to the time after the decision-making are placed in the right column (Fig. 4).

Exploring the values of the parents

Participants who described their views on good counselling more broadly mentioned stimulating people to explore their values as an element of prenatal counselling (237 individual words or phrases). They reported doing this by encouraging people to explore their considerations for participating in prenatal screening (Quote 7) or their views on disabled children (Quote 8).

Providing information

Almost all participants mentioned ‘providing information’ as a core element of good counselling (1858). They emphasized that the information needs to be objective and correct and to include the pros and cons of prenatal screening tests (Quote 9). Furthermore, the participants mentioned the need to tailor the information to the individual couple’s health literacy, previously acquired knowledge, and personal values (Quote 10). Also, the participants mentioned the need for information materials that can be used before, during, and after the counselling (Quote 11). Especially participants who explained more about the content of the information pointed out that information needs to be nuanced with explicit attention to the limitations of the tests (Quote 12). These participants also explicitly mentioned the right ‘not to know’, the right to reject information about prenatal screening (Quote 13). These were the same participants who also noted the general ethical aspects of prenatal screening.

Facilitating free choice

Another core element of good counselling, which was noted by participants, is ensuring that parents make a free choice (889). The participants underlined the importance of this element by naming it more than once in different formulations (Quote 14). Participants were not explicit in how they facilitated free choice; they mainly used terms such as being non-directive, offering room to the parents or having no opinion on accepting or declining screening (Quote 15 & 16). This theme focuses on facilitating free choice, i.e., on the interactions (mechanism) participants use to encourage parents to make a free choice. The underlying fragments were more related to the process of making a free choice than to the outcome.

Guiding

Participants named guiding (480) as another core element of good counselling. They described their guidance using the word guiding or concrete interactions, such as asking questions, summarizing and listening (Quotes 17 & 18).

Exploring the impact of the test results on the parents

A few participants mentioned the importance of discussing the possible implications of the test result as an element of good counselling (57). They pointed out that before participating, parents need to think about the consequences and the decisions they must make if the test indicates an abnormality (Quotes 19 & 20).

Outcome of good counselling

The themes mentioned under this heading represent the core elements of the outcome of good counselling (Fig. 4).

Parents making their own choice

Many participants emphasized that the outcome of good counselling must be that parents feel free to make their own well-considered, informed choices (424 individual words or phrases) (Quote 21). They emphasized their answers using capital letters or exclamation marks (Quote 22 & 23). Although this theme is related to the theme Facilitating free choice, the fragments underlying this theme are more related to the outcome of good counselling than to the process of decision-making support.

Right choice

The answers showed that the participants feel responsible for ensuring that parents can make the right choice. According to the participants, a choice is ‘right’ when parents decide together and are unlikely to regret their decision in the future (14) (Quotes 24, 25 & 26).

No assurance of getting a healthy baby

The participants who answered the question more broadly pointed out that parents need to understand that a good test result does not necessarily mean a healthy child and thus does not provide complete assurance (28) (Quote 27).

Results quantitative analysis

Based on our quantitative analysis, the most mentioned themes for the context of counselling for prenatal screening are time and counsellors’ competence. The most mentioned themes regarding the mechanisms are providing information and facilitating free choice. The most mentioned theme regarding the outcome of good prenatal counselling is parents making their own choices. This analysis shows that non-directiveness is a pattern in the core elements that participants described.

Discussion

This study aimed to gain insight into maternity care professionals' views on good counselling for prenatal screening and to better understand the mechanisms that explain professionals' focus on providing information and on offering screening options rather than on decision-making support.

Important themes in participants' views on good counselling are providing information, facilitating free choice, and parents making their own choices. The non-directive character of counselling for prenatal screening is dominant in the discourse of participants on this topic. The focus on a non-directive attitude could possibly partly explain why professionals tend to focus on providing information. With some caution, given the limitations of our data, professionals offer decision-making support by facilitating free choice and guiding. They seem to guide parents mainly by helping them to explore their thoughts. Although data was collected using a questionnaire, reflecting on the topic of counselling for prenatal screening gave rise to emotional responses. Participants used capital letters and exclamation marks to emphasize their answers.

Within the context of counselling for prenatal screening, time is an often-mentioned theme, specifically allocating enough time for counselling and for decision-making after counselling. Prenatal screening in the Netherlands is surrounded by awareness of the social and ethical ramifications of counselling [14, 25–27]. From this perspective, it is explicable that ethical sensitivity is a recurring theme, especially considering that research shows that some parents perceive social pressure to decide in a certain way [34]. Some participants seem to feel responsible for guarding social and ethical consciousness. These participants indicated that they act according to their own values, incorporating the ethical principle of ‘do no harm’ in their counselling for prenatal screening by, for example, explicitly reminding parents that they have the right ‘not to know’ [3]. These participants seem to interact more with parents during decision-making than the other participants.

When looking at themes within mechanisms through the lens of autonomy-supportive counselling, parents’ basic psychological needs are not fully met within the themes that were mentioned as core elements of good counselling (Fig. 4). To meet the parents’ need for relatedness, parents need to be known and understood by the professionals [17, 19, 29]. The interactions within the theme exploring the patient’s values could facilitate parents to feel known and understood by the professional [35]. Parents' need for competence is met within the theme providing information. Participants focus on providing information and hardly named discussing the information as a core element of counselling. This finding is in line with the outcomes of other studies [16]. To fully meet the parents' need for competence, parents should be enabled to understand the information and to apply it to their personal situation [34]. To be effective, parents must be well-informed and able to actively discuss information about prenatal screening with professionals. To do so, professionals can use interactions such as asking the parents to repeat or summarize the information provided to them [36]. Parents' need for autonomy is met within the themes facilitating free choice, guiding and exploring the impact of the test results on the parents. Participants focus on facilitating free choice to fulfil parents’ need for autonomy. However, parents also need directive guidance from professionals offering structure to facilitate their decision-making process and to help them make an autonomous choice [37]. Parents can perceive autonomy without structure as ‘let alone’ policy [12]. Phrases related to exploring parents' knowledge or values were found within the theme guiding. Only a few phrases referred to providing structure to help parents in making an autonomous choice. The elements within the theme exploring the impact of the test results on the parents could facilitate parents making an autonomous choice. The themes regarding the outcomes of counselling for prenatal screening showed that participants felt responsible for creating a climate in which parents can make their own choices.

The results and discourse show participants' non-directive attitudes towards the decision-making process and outcome. Although professionals need to be non-directive regarding the outcome of the decision in order to facilitate autonomous choice, they do need to give directive guidance on the decision-making process by providing structure [37, 38]. Our findings regarding the participants’ non-directive attitude is in line with the findings of earlier research [16]. Martin et al. (2015) [39] showed that less than 25% of the midwives regard actively exploring parents’ values as a part of counselling for prenatal screening. The inadvertent assumption that a non-directive attitude regarding the outcome automatically implies a non-directive attitude towards the decision-making process could possibly partly explain why professionals tend to focus on providing information and hardly provide directive guidance by structuring the decision-making process for parents. This problem might possibly be overcome in continuous professional training by paying explicit attention to the balance between non-directive information and facilitating free choice on the one hand, and, on the other hand, directive decision-making support by providing structure.

Strengths and limitations

This study's strengths include a large research team with expertise in a broad range of disciplines and the use of a theoretical framework. The framework facilitates viewing the upcoming themes from the perspective of supporting parents’ autonomous decision-making. A limitation of this study is that the qualitative data were collected by means of an open-ended survey question instead of in-depth interviews or focus groups. Although this approach enabled us to include a large group of professionals involved in counselling for prenatal screening, it limited the depth of the answers. The fact that most professionals gave concise answers to the open-ended question complicated further interpretation, which limited the possibility of answering the second part of the research question.

Research using semi-structured interviews or focus groups is needed to provide more insight into professionals' interactions with parents to facilitate free choice and to offer guidance. Also, the professionals’ focus on a non-directive approach needs further exploration.

We could include 1426 (50.7%) of the 2813 invited counsellors, which may limit the generalizability of our findings. However, we assume that our results are largely representative of registered Dutch counsellors because the study sample was comparable with the registered Dutch counsellors for the available variables: age, profession, and work experience. Also, our response rate was within the average response of 45.8% plus or minus 25.0% for online surveys among healthcare professionals, as found by Meyer et al. (2022) [40] in their systematic review.

Another limitation is that this study examined the core elements of prenatal counselling reported by professionals. Healthcare professionals tend to overestimate the extent to which they provide decision-making support [41]. This tendency could mean that in actual practice, the core elements mentioned in this study are a less explicit part of counselling for prenatal screening. Finally, it could be that the counsellors who participated in the survey were more interested in good counselling for prenatal screening than the other counsellors.

Conclusions

Participants consider providing information to be a very important mechanism, and parents making their own free choices is a very important outcome of good counselling for prenatal screening. The results and discourse reflect a more non-directive attitude before and during the decision-making process. Since a non-directive attitude can complicate parents' more autonomous decision-making, we recommend explicitly paying attention to more directive guidance by providing structure to the decision-making process to facilitate parents' autonomous decision-making. Literature shows that healthcare professionals other than maternity care professionals also tend to focus on providing information to enable decision-making [42]. Based on the results of this study, it is worthwhile to explore professionals' attitudes regarding non-directive or directive guidance in other contexts to facilitate the decision-making process.
