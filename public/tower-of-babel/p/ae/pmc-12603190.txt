Functional improvements correlate with enhanced quality of life following mesenchymal stromal cell therapy in chronic spinal cord injury
Authors: Hanan Jafar, Fatima Jamali, Dana Al-Hattab, Abdullah Ammar, Afnan Al-hayali, Halla Sroji, Abdulrahman Al Shudifat, Nael El Adwan, Abdalla Awidi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMesenchymal stromal cell (MSC) therapy is an emerging promising treatment for spinal cord injury (SCI) management. However, and even though sensory and motor evaluations of this treatment are being reported, there is a deficiency in reporting standardized evaluation of the Quality of Life (QoL) measures, especially as it relates to bowel/bladder control in patients. Subtle enhancements in these functions, even though undetectable by routine examination and clinical assessments, may have a significant impact on patients’ perceived well-being. In this study, we specifically examine how MSC therapy affected self-reported QoL of measures of SCI patients. A prospective analysis was conducted on 20 chronic SCI patients receiving MSC therapy [bone marrow (BM) or Wharton’s jelly (WJ) derived]. Bowel function was assessed using the Neurogenic Bowel Dysfunction Score (NBDS) and Wexner Score, while bladder function and QoL were evaluated via the modified King’s Health instrument and WHOQOL-BREF. Follow-up extended for one-year post-treatment. Both BM- and WJ-MSC groups showed progressive improvement in bowel/bladder function, with significant declines in NBDS (P < 0.05) and Wexner scores (P < 0.001). QoL domains (physical, psychological, social, environmental) improved significantly (P < 0.05), with environmental QoL scoring highest in BM-MSC recipients. Bladder control improved in sensory perception but not continence, concurring with prior findings. Our results show that MSC therapy enhances bowel/bladder function and QoL in chronic SCI patients, though individualized assessment tools are essential to capture subtle improvements. Introduction

Chronic spinal cord injury (SCI) is a debilitating condition that leads to significant disabilities, including motor impairment, sensory alterations, and autonomic dysfunction affecting gastrointestinal and urinary function1. Chronic Spinal cord injuries (SCIs) are classified as traumatic or non-traumatic, with road traffic accidents (RTAs) being the leading cause, followed by falls and gunshot wounds. The level and severity of injury vary depending on etiology2. Traumatic SCIs affect the cervical (50%), thoracic (35%), or lumbar (11%) spine 3. The annual incidence of traumatic SCI is approximately 22.55 per million in developing countries and 18 per million in Jordan4,5.

The consequences of SCI extend beyond direct spinal cord damage, encompassing social and psychological factors that impact mood and coping mechanisms6. These factors are physical, mental, and financial impacts necessitate multidimensional assessment7 encompassed within the broader concept of Quality of Life (QoL), which the World Health Organization (WHO) defines as "individuals’ perceptions of their positions in life in the context of the culture and value systems in which they live and with their goals, expectations, standards, and concerns”8. Many individuals with SCI report reduced QoL, which correlates with the severity and level of injury9. Additionally, dysfunctions related to urination and bowel movements are prevalent and pose significant challenges to recovery, contributing to morbidity10. QoL, in this context, reflects individuals’ self-perceived impact of excretory dysfunction, such as neurogenic bowel and bladder, on their physical, mental, emotional, and social well-being11.

Advanced therapeutics are a new class of treatments that include both cell and gene therapies. Collectively, they are being investigated as viable options for diseases with no satisfactory outcomes12. Cell therapy, primarily mesenchymal stromal cell (MSC) therapy, has been extensively studied both in vitro and in vivo, and shown to be safe and efficacious in multiple diseases13–15. MSCs are particularly attractive due to their availability from different tissue sources, ease of isolation, capacity for multi-fold expansion, and low immunogenicity, making them excellent candidates for allogeneic, off-the-shelf therapies16,17.

Currently, no treatment has been shown to reverse the neurological outcomes, especially the motor ones in SCI. Despite limited treatments, improving QoL remains a priority, yet few studies have addressed it. This study aims to evaluate the QoL in individuals with CSCI before and after treatment with allogeneic Umbilical Cord mesenchymal stromal cells (UCMSC), specifically focusing on the impact on bladder and bowel dysfunction.

Materials, methods and patients

Study design and participants

This previously published prospective study18 included 20 patients with chronic traumatic spinal cord injury (≥ 1 year; mean duration: 22.65 months) treated at the Cell Therapy Center in Amman, Jordan. Inclusion and exclusion criteria can be found in the Supplementary Material (Table S1). Patients received mesenchymal stromal cells derived from either autologous bone marrow (BMMSCs, n = 11) or allogeneic Wharton’s jelly (UCMSCs, n = 9). Informed consent was obtained in accordance with the Helsinki declaration. Approval was obtained from CTC IRB and the trial registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT04288934; Registration date: 28/02/2020). The current study utilized a verbal questionnaire to assess patients’ quality of life (QoL) using the WHOQOL-BRE19, the modified King’s Health Questionnaire20, Wexner Score21, and Neurogenic Bowel Dysfunction Score (NBDS)22. Assessments were conducted at baseline, 6 months, and 12 months following mesenchymal stromal cell (MSC) intervention.

Quality of life WHOQOL-BREF

The WHOQOL-BREF is a well-validated and widely used instrument for assessing quality of life (QoL) in individuals with spinal cord injury19. The questionnaire consists of 26 items: the first two evaluate overall QoL and general health, while the remaining 24 are divided into four domains—physical health (7 items), psychological well-being (6 items), social relationships (3 items), and environmental factors (8 items). Each item is rated on a 5-point Likert scale. Domain scores were calculated according to the official guidelines and transformed to a 0–100 scale, with higher scores indicating better QoL.

Urination-related dimensions of QoL

The modified King’s Health Questionnaire (KHQ) was used to assess patients’ quality of life (QoL) with a particular focus on urinary symptoms20. The KHQ comprises eight domains: the first two evaluate general health status and overall impact of urinary symptoms, while the remaining six assess role limitations, physical/social limitations, personal relationships, emotional status, sleep/energy disturbances, and symptom severity. Each domain contains specific questions about the daily life impacts of bladder dysfunction, scored on a 4-point Likert scale (1 = 'not at all’ to 4 = ‘a lot’), except for personal relationships, which includes an additional ‘not applicable’ (0) option. While the KHQ also evaluates specific urinary symptoms (frequency, nocturia, urgency, and incontinence), these components were excluded from our analysis as they are irrelevant to the current study.

Wexner score for obstructed defecation syndrome (ODS)

The Wexner Score (also known as the Cleveland Clinic Incontinence Score) is a validated tool for assessing fecal incontinence severity, with scores ranging from 0 (complete continence) to 20 (total incontinence)21. This instrument evaluates three key components: incontinence of solid stool, liquid stool, and flatus. Additionally, it measures the condition’s impact on quality of life, including the need for protective pads and lifestyle modifications. The Wexner Score is particularly valuable for assessing the societal consequences of fecal incontinence in spinal cord injury (SCI) patients.

Neurogenic bowel dysfunction score (NBDS)

The Neurogenic Bowel Dysfunction Score (NBDS) was employed to assess both overall quality of life and the specific impact of neurogenic bowel disorder22. This validated instrument evaluates 10 clinical parameters, including: defecation frequency, duration of bowel movements, defecation-related discomfort, use of constipation medications (tablets/drops), and perianal skin complications. Total scores range from 0 to 47, with 0–6 indicating very minor dysfunction and scores ≥ 14 reflecting severe bowel dysfunction.

Statistical analysis

Data were organized in Microsoft Excel and analyzed using two-tailed paired t-tests. Descriptive statistics (means ± SDs) were calculated for continuous variables, including quality of life measures and changes in defecation/urination patterns at baseline, 6 months, and 1-year post-treatment. Categorical variables were summarized as counts and percentages. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05, with P < 0.001 denoting highly significant differences. These analyses evaluated longitudinal changes in outcomes across the three time points.

Patients who received bone marrow-derived mesenchymal stromal cell (BM-MSC) therapy, showed a significant improvement (P < 0.05) over the follow-up period for all measured domains—physical, psychological, social, and environmental. The mean scores for each domain increased progressively, peaking at one-year post-treatment (Table 2). Similarly, patients who received Wharton’s jelly-derived MSCs (WJ-MSCs) also demonstrated significant improvements (P < 0.05) across all domains, with gradually increasing mean values throughout follow-up (Table 2).

Assessment of bowel function outcome

To assess bowel dysfunction severity and fecal incontinence over time, we utilized the Neurogenic Bowel Dysfunction Score (NBDS) and Wexner Score, where higher scores indicate more severe symptoms (Table 3). Both scoring systems demonstrated significant improvements during follow-up as follows; NBDS showed a statistically significant reduction (P < 0.05) up to one-year post-treatment. A gradual decline in mean scores was observed for both measures, reflecting progressive symptomatic improvement in bowel function (Fig. 1A,B).

Discussion

Quality of life (QoL) is a critical measure of psychosocial adaptation in chronic illnesses and disabilities23. Spinal cord injury (SCI), with its multifaceted long-term consequences, profoundly impacts patients’ lives. While our prior study assessed motor/sensory impairments using ASIA scores post-treatment18, QoL evaluation—particularly general health and excretory dysfunction—remains challenging due to its individualized nature. Standardized tools like WHOQOL-BREF24 help objectively quantify these impacts.

Our results demonstrate gradual improvements in bowel/bladder function over one year, alongside significant QoL enhancement (WHOQOL-BREF). More specifically, patients reported experiencing better energy level, quality of sleep and ability to perform their daily living activities. Most also reported being more satisfied in their personal relationships, and one of the patients got married during the study. These findings concur with studies from India25 and Australia26, despite the existence of variability—e.g., environmental QoL scored highest in our cohort, contrasting with Tanzanian27 and Canadian28 reports emphasizing physical health impacts. Such disparities likely reflect cultural and methodological differences.

To better understand this, one must look to the differences between Arab communities and Western ones. Social support networks, beliefs, and family unity are highly valued in Arabic communities. Even when there are functional or physical constraints, these factors frequently lead to an enhanced perception of emotional and environmental well-being. Extended family networks, for example, can offer patients emotional support and continuous caregiving, improving their perceived quality of life in areas like accessibility, safety, and security in their homes, so they will have strong social bond and spiritual contentment and community belonging.

On the other hand, the cultural orientation is typically more individualistic in many western cultures, emphasizing physical functionality, independence, and autonomy. Functional independency and occupational performance measurements may therefore be given priority in QoL instruments in Western studies. Regardless of the social or emotional support offered, a patient’s mobility constraints may result in a worse environmental quality of life score in certain populations. As such, environmental factors significantly influence patient outcomes and improvements. For instance, even when a patient shows limited progress in other domains, optimal environmental conditions can still lead to observable enhancements in their overall well-being. This phenomenon has been evidenced in our patient cohort.

Neurogenic bladder significantly affects QoL29. While our modified King’s Health instrument noted progressive bladder improvement post-MSC therapy, urinary incontinence persisted despite sensory gains18. Patients consistently reported “feeling” better related to urine continence, they gave this as unsolicited feedback when asked to freely express “How they felt differently with regard to urine continence”. The question was asked in a non-leading manner, as part of each follow-up session initial physical exam. Functional scores (e.g., ASIA30) often fail to capture these subjective yet critical improvements. Prior studies corroborate bladder-related QoL declines31–34, underscoring the need for patient-centered metrics.

Bowel dysfunction, affecting ~ 80% of SCI patients36, severely compromises QoL35,37,38. Baseline fecal incontinence (Wexner score: 1639) improved progressively, mirroring literature linking symptom severity to QoL40,41. Patients reported better control over bowel continence, with fewer episodes of fecal leakage and improved ability to delay defecation. Further, they reported significant gains in bowel management, including decreased incidence of constipation and fecal incontinence, as well as a decreased need for interventions like laxatives or enemas. In addition to less time spent on each defecation with no uneasiness and sweating during defecation.

This was a prospective clinical trial with no placebo control. In the context of chronic spinal cord injury (SCI)—where patients typically face limited therapeutic options—the use of sham procedures, particularly those mimicking invasive intrathecal delivery, raises significant ethical concerns regarding both risk–benefit balance and the potential for patient deception. However, the stability of their condition allowed them to serve as their own controls. Future studies may consider delayed-treatment or other ethically acceptable designs to minimize bias.

Vaquera et al.42 showed that cytokines released by transplanted cells can improve bowel and bladder functions in chronic complete paraplegia. They suggested that these cytokines may activate preserved but non-functional neural circuits, rather than facilitate nerve pathway regeneration. In a further study43, the same group, and others44, suggested that autologous MSCs may act through the release of many neurotropic factors, albeit the exact mechanism of action remains unknown.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that MSC therapy significantly improves bowel/bladder function and quality of life in chronic SCI patients over one year, though variability exists across QoL domains. Standardized assessments and patient-centered metrics, such as conducting semi-structured interviews to explore more about patient experiences, Functional Independence Measure (FIM) to assess the functional status of the patients, and the multi-dimensional assessment tool SF-36 Health Survey, remain essential to evaluate long-term therapeutic outcomes.
