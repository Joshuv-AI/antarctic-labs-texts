Quality of life among patients with epilepsy in the western region of KSA: A cross-sectional study
Authors: Bayan M Almarwani, Manar J Alahmadi, Shouq T Alharbi, Meaad A Alrehaili, Maydaa M Dabi, Rim K Benthami, Samaher N Alsaeedi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAlthough epilepsy can be managed, it markedly affects individuals’ quality of life (QOL), through factors such as seizure severity and social stigma. This study examined QOL in patients with epilepsy living in the western region of KSA.MethodsA cross-sectional design considering clinical and sociodemographic factors was used to assess QOL in patients with epilepsy. Purposive and snowball sampling were used to recruit participants from the western region of KSA. Data were collected via a structured questionnaire administered electronically and in person at King Fahad Hospital in Almadinah Almunawwarah. The questionnaire included Quality of Life in Epilepsy-31 (QOLIE-31), which assesses seven QOL domains, scored from 0 to 100, with higher scores reflecting better QOL.ResultsAmong the respondents, 57.1 % were women, 60.1 % were single, 56.2 % had a bachelor's degree, and 57.7 % were unemployed. The mean QOLIE total score for the participants was 56.56 ± 16.03, and the energy/fatigue domain had the lowest score. Factors such as the presence of chronic disease (p < 0.01) and frequent seizures (p < 0.05) were associated with poorer QOL in patients with epilepsy. Furthermore, emotional well-being, energy, and fatigue were ameliorated with age (p < 0.01). The findings indicated high cognitive functioning in married people with epilepsy (p < 0.05), high QOL in participants who had earned a high school degree (p < 0.05), high social functioning in men (p < 0.05), and high emotional well-being in patients with an annual income of 10,000–15,000 SAR (p < 0.01).ConclusionChronic diseases and seizure duration, type, and frequency were found to affect the individual dimensions of QOL and overall QOL among patients with epilepsy. The findings highlight the importance of tailored interventions, such as educational campaigns and self-management empowerment, to address QOL differences associated with patient- and epilepsy-related factors.Keywords: Epilepsy, Quality of life, KSA, Western region

Introduction

Epilepsy is a common neurological disorder1 that has been diagnosed in more than 50 million people worldwide.2 In KSA, the reported prevalence of epilepsy among males and females is 3.99 and 3.94 per 1000 people, respectively.3 A recent systematic review and meta-analysis has indicated that 6.4 % of people in KSA believe that epilepsy is a contagious disease, whereas 38.6 % consider it a psychological disorder.4 The prevalence and public awareness of epilepsy, as well as attitudes toward people with epilepsy, markedly influence quality of life (QOL) among individuals with epilepsy.4

Epilepsy is a chronic brain condition characterized by a lasting tendency toward epileptic seizures, as well as various neurobiological, cognitive, psychological, and social effects.5 According to the International League Against Epilepsy (ILAE), epileptic seizure is defined as transient occurrence of signs and/or symptoms resulting from abnormal and excessive synchronous neuronal activity in the brain.5 The disease has major social implications, because of its high prevalence and the stigmatization of affected individuals.6 Epilepsy is characterized by 15 types of seizures and 30 types of syndromes, and is often accompanied by depression, substantial comorbidity, anxiety, and elevated mortality.7

Epilepsy is potentially treatable, and remission is possible in many individuals.8 Epileptic seizures can be treated with cannabidiol,9 acupuncture, cauterization,10 deep brain stimulation, responsive neurostimulation, and vagus nerve stimulation.11 An estimated 60 %–70 % of patients with epilepsy can achieve seizure control with adequate and appropriate treatment with antiseizure medications (ASMs).12, 13, 14 These treatments can improve patients’ medical and social outcomes, enhance their functional capacity,8 and positively affect their QOL.

The World Health Organization defines QOL as “an individual's perception of their position in life, in the context of the culture and value systems in which they live, and in relation to their goals, expectations, standards and concerns.”15 Many factors influence QOL in people with epilepsy, including uncontrolled seizures, poor medication adherence, use of illicit drugs, anxiety, and depression.16 Several tools can measure QOL in people with epilepsy, including health-related QOL,17 the Quality of Life in Epilepsy-10 inventory, the Quality of Life in Epilepsy-31 inventory (QOLIE-31), and the Quality of Life in Epilepsy-89 inventory.18 This study used the QOLIE-31 questionnaire, which provides a composite score from 0 to 100 that indicates the overall well-being of a person with epilepsy, including effects on cognition and social functioning.19

According to a systematic review and meta-analysis of public awareness and attitudes toward epilepsy in KSA, most prior studies were conducted in the Riyadh and Makkah regions (central and western KSA, respectively), whereas only several were conducted in regions such as Qassim (central), Aseer and Al-Baha (southwestern), Tabuk (northwestern), and Al-Madinah (western).4 People with epilepsy in various regions of KSA face several language-related, economic, and technological barriers to timely diagnosis and treatment.20 Furthermore, given the prevalence of epilepsy in the Saudi population, and its various adverse social, physical, and psychological effects, the effects of epilepsy on an individual's QOL can be substantial. Therefore, this study was aimed at investigating the influence of epilepsy on QOL among individuals in the western region of KSA and examining the correlation between QOL and sociodemographic factors. An additional aim was to identify the pertinent clinical factors affecting QOL among adults with epilepsy in KSA.

Materials and Methods

Study design and setting

This study used a cross-sectional research design to compare QOL among individuals with epilepsy, on the basis of both epilepsy-related characteristics and sociodemographic factors. A self-administered, online questionnaire created in Google Forms was disseminated to the target population through social media platforms, such as WhatsApp and X (formerly Twitter), as well as in the neurology clinic of King Fahad General Hospital in Almadinah Almunawwarah, KSA. The study was conducted from March 2023 to December 2023.

Study population and sampling

Both purposive and snowball sampling were used to select the target population. Through purposive sampling, we selected participants according to the following inclusion criteria: residence in the western region of KSA, ability to complete the questionnaire, and age of 18 years or older. The study excluded patients who had been diagnosed with progressive central nervous system diseases, acute or severe chronic central nervous system conditions, disturbances in consciousness, or language impairments, as well as patients with a history of psychiatric illness or a history of drug use that might influence QOL. Snowball sampling was also used to reach participants from various regions through social networks and multiple referral chains, to optimize the diversity of the sample.

Bias

The questionnaire was carefully designed to include specific, structured questions regarding the participants’ medical history and seizure characteristics, to minimize recall bias. In addition, the study used a validated Arabic version of the QOLIE-31 questionnaire to ensure that the participants could respond in a culturally relevant, easily understandable manner, thereby mitigating any potential confusion in their answers.

To address response bias, we used an anonymous questionnaire to promote honest reporting. Anonymity provided a safeguard against participants giving socially desirable answers, particularly regarding sensitive topics, such as seizure frequency and QOL. Finally, we aimed to standardize the assessment of QOL by using validated tools, such as the QOLIE-31 questionnaire, to ensure consistent and reliable data collection from all participants.

Sample size estimation

G∗Power was used to estimate the sample size.21 The parameters used for estimation were as follows: effect size (f2) = .45, alpha = .05, power = .80, and allocation ratio N2/N1 = 1. This method suggested that a total sample size of 158 participants should be enrolled. Our study included 163 participants.

Research instruments

The study used a structured, validated, self-administered questionnaire, available both electronically via Google Forms and in paper format, to gather information from participants. The questionnaire was divided into four main sections. The first section pertained to sociodemographic characteristics, such as sex, age, marital status, place of residence, educational level, financial status, and employment status. The second section focused on the clinical characteristics of seizures, including age of onset, duration of epilepsy, and number of ASMs used.22,23 The third section focused on the respondents' medical histories of chronic diseases (e.g., diabetes, hypertension, or asthma) and the types of seizures experienced. The fourth section consisted of the QOLIE-31 questionnaire. To align with participants’ everyday language, seizure types were reported with the terms “full” and “small,” which reflect self-reported experiences rather than ILAE clinical classifications.

The research instrument was a validated Arabic version of the QOLIE-31.19 The QOLIE-31 was developed by RAND Health Care in 1993 and translated into Arabic by Maryam Alami Merrouni.24,25 The QOLIE-31 questionnaire was used to assess QOL in patients with epilepsy across seven subscales: seizure worry (five items measuring anxiety regarding seizures), emotional well-being (five items assessing mood and psychological state), energy/fatigue (four items reflecting vitality), cognitive functioning (six items evaluating memory and concentration), medication effects (three items assessing treatment adverse effects), social functioning (five items evaluating social role engagement), and overall QOL and health (two items). Most items were scored on 5- or 6-point Likert scales, then linearly transformed to a 0–100 scale with the QOLIE-31 version 1.0 scoring manual, with higher scores indicating greater QOL. All scoring and conversion were performed by the authors in accordance with the manual.26

Data management and analysis plan

The data collected from the questionnaire were analyzed in Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) version 27 software. Descriptive statistics were calculated to delineate the sociodemographic characteristics of the respondents. Independent-sample t-tests and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) were used to compare QOLIE-31 scores across clinical and sociodemographic variables. T-tests were used for variables with two categories (e.g., sex), whereas ANOVA was used for variables with three or more categories, such as seizure frequency, education level, and employment status. Pearson's correlation analysis was used to assess the relationship between seizure duration and QOLIE-31 scores, because both variables were continuous. Multivariate analysis of variance (MANOVA) was used to examine the combined effects of multiple demographic variables, including sex, age, marital status, education level, financial status, and employment status, on QOLIE-31 scores.

Ethical considerations

Before data collection, ethical approval was obtained from the Research Ethical Committee of Taibah University in Almadinah Almunawwarah, KSA, under study ID STU-22-015 (reference number: IORG0008716-IRB00010413). Consent was obtained from the participants after they were informed of the study's purpose and the confidentiality of the shared information. The participants were also informed that their participation was voluntary and that they had the option to withdraw from the study at any time. All research procedures were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki regarding ethical principles for medical research involving human participants.27

Results

Descriptive statistics

Frequencies and percentages were calculated to determine the distribution of respondents across sociodemographic characteristics (Table 1). A total of 57.1 % of respondents were women, whereas 42.9 % were men. Five respondents were excluded from the final data because they were younger than 18 years. The final sample consisted of 163 participants, 67.5 % of whom were 18–34 years of age. Most respondents were not married (60.1 %), 56.2 %) and most were unemployed (57.7 %). Nearly half the respondents resided in Almadinah Almunawwarah (45.4 %), whereas 15.3 % lived in Makkah, and 15.3 % lived in Jeddah. A smaller proportion of respondents resided in Riyadh (6.7 %), which is outside the western region of KSA. In addition, most respondents reported an annual household income of 15,000–20,000 SAR (45.4 %). Finally, most respondents participated in the study via the online questionnaire (80.4 %), whereas the remainder, all of whom were from Almadinah Almunawwarah, filled out a paper questionnaire at the hospital (19.6 %) (see Table 2).

Reliability test

Cronbach's alpha was calculated to evaluate the reliability of the subscales and the overall scale of the QOLIE-31. Two items from the “energy/fatigue” subscale were eliminated because of low correlation scores of .172 and .075 with other items in the same subscale. Cronbach's alpha values for the subscales, which measured seizure worry, overall QOL, emotional well-being, energy/fatigue, cognitive function, medication effects, and social function, were .760, .611, .665, .683, .815, .650, and .688, respectively. These scores indicated acceptable reliability. Cronbach's alpha for the overall QOLIE-31 scale was .902, indicating excellent reliability.

The mean QOLIE total score for the participants was 56.56 (±16.03). The seizure worry domain had a score of 49.98 (±24.56), and the overall QOL domain had a score of 75.23 (17.36). The emotional well-being domain had a score of 56.66 (±18.19), whereas the energy/fatigue domain had a score of 43.74 (±23.68). The cognitive functioning domain had a score of 54.61 (±22.16), the medication effects domain had a score of 56.01 (±24.72), and the social functioning domain had a score of 59.02 (±22.33). Therefore, the energy/fatigue domain had the lowest score.

Comparison of QOL in epilepsy according to epilepsy characteristics

Epilepsy was assessed according to five distinct characteristics: (1) presence of chronic disease, (2) epilepsy duration, (3) seizure type, (4) seizure frequency, and (5) seizure duration. The first four variables were measured on an ordinal or nominal scale, whereas the fifth characteristic was measured on a continuous scale. For the nominal variable, regardless of whether chronic disease was present, independent samples were used for comparison, because two categories (“no” or “yes”) were analyzed.

For the ordinal variables, i.e., epilepsy duration, seizure type, and seizure number, one-way ANOVA was used for comparison, because more than two categories were analyzed. For the continuous variables, i.e., seizure duration, Pearson's correlation was calculated to determine the association between seizure duration and QOLIE. The assumptions of the respective tests were thoroughly investigated, and the test results are presented in Table 3, Table 4.

The presence of chronic disease was associated with diminished overall QOL (t = 2.507, p < 0.05), impaired cognitive function (t = 2.085, p < 0.05), diminished social function (t = 2.449, p < 0.05), and, as a whole, diminished QOLIE (t = 2.598, p < 0.01) and elevated disability from epilepsy (Table 3). Only energy/fatigue significantly varied across seizure durations (F-statistic (F) (3, 162) = 2.672, p < 0.05). Tukey's post hoc tests demonstrated that having a seizure for 3–5 min was associated with less energy and greater fatigue ((mean (M) = 39.3, standard deviation (SD) = 24.34)) than having a seizure of unknown duration (M = 62.2, SD = 21.67); consequently greater disability from epilepsy was observed for the individuals experiencing a seizure for 3–5 min (Table 3).

We observed significant differences in seizure worry (F (2, 162) = 3.401, p < 0.05) and overall QOL (F (2, 162) = 3.865, p < 0.05) according to seizure type. Tukey's post hoc analysis revealed that individuals experiencing a full seizure reported less seizure worry (M = 44.0, SD = 24.56) than those experiencing small seizures while awake (M = 54.8, SD = 23.37). In addition, post hoc analysis revealed that those with full seizures had lower overall QOL (M = 71.9, SD = 20.17) than those experiencing small seizures while awake (M = 79.0, SD = 13.94; Table 4).

We observed significant differences in seizure worry (F (4, 162) = 7.720, p < 0.001) and total QOLIE (F (4, 162) = 2.402, p < 0.05) according to seizure frequency. Post hoc analysis with Tukey's test indicated that individuals experiencing one or more seizures per month reported lower seizure worry (M = 38.3, SD = 22.04) than those who had not experienced a seizure for more than 1 year (M = 61.3, SD = 22.86) or had one seizure every 6 months (M = 40.5, SD = 22.86). Furthermore, participants experiencing one seizure per year reported greater seizure worry (M = 58.4, SD = 20.71) than participants with one or more seizures per month (M = 38.3, SD = 22.04) or with one seizure every 6 months (M = 40.5, SD = 22.86). Individuals who had not experienced a seizure for more than 1 year reported greater QOLIE (M = 60.7, SD = 15.27) than participants who had one or more seizures per month (M = 49.4, SD = 17.89).

We observed no significant association of seizure duration with seizure worry (r = −.112, p = 0.155), overall QOL (r = −.046, p = 0.559), emotional well-being (r = −.100, p = 0.206), energy/fatigue (r = −.033, p = 0.678), cognitive function (r = −.048, p = 0.546), medication effects (r = −.015, p = 0.844), social function (r = −.099, p = 0.210), and overall QOLIE (r = −.104, p = 0.187).

Discussion

This study evaluated QOL among Saudi patients with epilepsy and explored influencing factors, including sociodemographic characteristics (such as age, sex, education level, employment, and financial status), clinical variables (such as seizure frequency, duration, and type), and comorbid conditions. We focused on five crucial factors: chronic disease status, epilepsy duration, seizure type, seizure frequency, and seizure duration. The majority of the sample comprised participants 18–34 years of age (67.5 %), participants who were single (60.1 %), participants with bachelor's degrees (56.2 %), and participants who were unemployed (57.7 %). A substantial portion consisted of participants who resided in Almadinah Almunawwarah (45.4 %) and participants who had a household income of 15,000–20,000 SAR (45.4 %).

The mean QOLIE total score was 56.56 ± 16.03 across all assessed domains. We observed variable scores across domanis of seizure worry (49.98 ± 24.56), overall QOL (75.23 ± 17.36), emotional well-being (56.66 ± 18.19), energy/fatigue (43.74 ± 23.68), cognitive functioning (54.61 ± 22.16), medication effects (56.01 ± 24.72), and social functioning (59.02 ± 22.33). The energy/fatigue domain had the lowest score, thereby reflecting considerable fatigue and diminished vitality among participants.

The average QOLIE total score (56.56 ± 16.03) was consistent with previous findings, such as the scores of 64.23 ± 17.8, 58.48 ± 21.22, and 62.5 ± 14.5 reported in studies conducted in the Qassim region of KSA, Ethiopia, and Uganda, respectively.16,28,29 Moreover, our results were similar to the worldwide average score of 59.8 ± 8.0, and within the range from 57.7 ± 14.1 in Russia to 68.2 ± 23.6 in Canada.30,31 The comparative average QOLIE total scores highlighted the global burden of diminished QOL in people with epilepsy.

Interestingly, our findings aligned with those of a study conducted in the Qassim region of KSA.28 In both studies, the highest score was observed for the social function domain. However, the lowest score was for energy/fatigue in our study but for seizure worry in the Qassim study. This difference suggests that seizure type might substantially influence the QOL of individuals with epilepsy.

In the current study, seizure frequency was significantly associated with both seizure worry and overall QOLIE. Studies conducted in Nigeria, Europe, and Rome have also indicated an association between a higher number of seizures and poorer QOL.32, 33, 34 Despite substantial differences in socioeconomic status across those countries, including variations in healthcare systems, cultural attitudes, and available epilepsy care resources, the results were consistent with those found in KSA in our study.

Furthermore, our findings highlighted major differences in seizure worry and overall QOL by seizure type: participants who experienced full seizures showed lower levels of seizure worry and lower overall QOL than participants experiencing small seizures while awake. This finding is similar to those from an investigation conducted in the Qassim region of KSA28 but contradicts findings from a study conducted in Sweden, which indicated significantly lower QOL in individuals with focal epilepsy rather than generalized seizures.35

Our study findings further suggested that the presence of chronic disease correlated with diminished overall QOL as well as compromised cognitive and social functioning. This finding is consistent with the similar conclusion drawn from prior research in the European population.32 Our findings highlight the importance of addressing comorbidities in epilepsy management to enhance overall patient well-being.

Moreover, our results suggested that individuals experiencing seizures lasting 3–5 min reported greater fatigue than those with seizures of unspecified duration. This finding was consistent with those from a previous systematic review.36 Therefore, seizure duration might influence daily functioning, particularly energy levels. Our findings further emphasize the importance of personalized management approaches to address specific seizure characteristics and their effects on QOL.

We observed that the emotional well-being, energy, and fatigue of people with epilepsy improves with age. This finding was consistent with those from Tombini et al.,33 who highlighted a positive association between emotional well-being and age, and Honari et al.,37 who reported elevated energy and fatigue among Iranian people with epilepsy, over the age of 35. Furthermore, we observed that the cognitive functioning of married people with epilepsy was higher than that of single people with epilepsy. This finding was consistent with observations by Xu et al.38 indicating higher cognitive functioning among married people than single people with epilepsy.

We observed higher QOL in people with epilepsy who had pursued higher education, in agreement with findings from Saadi et al.39 Furthermore, participants with annual household incomes of 10,000–15,000 SAR exhibited the highest emotional well-being scores, compared with other income groups. Finally, male participants with epilepsy showed greater social functioning than female participants, in agreement with findings from Lui et al.,40 in which QOL social functioning was higher for men than women.

Our findings have several clinical implications. Healthcare providers could address potential sex-associated disparities by implementing various strategies.41 These strategies should include sex-sensitive care provision, tailored support groups, mental health screening and interventions, education campaigns, self-management empowerment, and collaboration with community organizations and policymakers. Therefore, our findings underscore the importance of a comprehensive approach to epilepsy care that considers not only medical management but also psychosocial and demographic factors, to improve patient outcomes and enhance overall QOL.

In addition, we observed that individuals with epilepsy who also had chronic disease experienced lower overall QOL, greater cognitive function impairment, and lower social functioning than participants without chronic diseases. Clinicians should prioritize comprehensive care for patients with epilepsy with comorbid chronic conditions, to address these additional challenges and improve overall well-being. Similarly, our study highlighted that seizure duration was associated with lower energy levels and fatigue: individuals who experienced seizures lasting 3–5 min reported elevated fatigue. Therefore, healthcare providers should consider seizure duration when assessing and managing patients with epilepsy, because prolonged seizures might contribute to increased fatigue and subsequent disability.

Variations in seizure types and frequency were significantly associated with seizure worry and overall QOL. Having full seizures was associated with less seizure worry but lower overall QOL than observed in participants who experienced small seizures while awake. Moreover, individuals experiencing one or more seizures per month reported less seizure worry but lower overall QOL than participants with no seizures for more than 1 year. Notably, people with epilepsy with more frequent seizures might have less seizure worry, because they might use psychological mechanisms such as adaptation, desensitization, or normalization to manage seizures and the resultant seizure worry.42,43 Hence, clinicians should tailor treatment plans to address the specific challenges associated with various seizure types and frequencies, to optimize patients’ QOL.

Strengths, limitations, and recommendations for future research

This study used a comprehensive approach to data collection, including detailed sociodemographic information, clinical seizure features, medical history of chronic diseases, and QOL assessment with the validated QOLIE-31 questionnaire. This method enabled a thorough understanding of the clinical profile of the study population, including factors such as age of onset, epilepsy duration, ASMs use, and comorbidities. Moreover, the use of social media for questionnaire dissemination facilitated broad participant recruitment, thereby enhancing sample diversity.

However, the use of snowball sampling through social media might have introduced selection bias by favoring the inclusion of younger, more digitally connected individuals. Although residents of all cities were invited to complete the online questionnaire, the study population did not equally represent each city; consequently, geographic overrepresentation, sampling bias, and even unclear eligibility enforcement might have been present. Such sampling bias would have limited our findings’ ability to reflect the entire population and consequently their generalizability. Future research could replicate the study design in the western region to confirm the findings herein. More studies should be conducted in the Middle East and other regions of KSA to confirm the validity of the findings.

Interestingly, having more frequent seizures was associated with lower seizure worry and lower QOL. Additional studies should be conducted to explore the reasons underlying this contradictory finding and to potentially determine ways to improve QOL through various dimensions.

In addition, this study used a cross-sectional design that might have introduced temporal ambiguity, that is, inability to determine the direction of the relationship between variables or establish causality. Additionally, seizure types in this study were reported by the participants in general, nonclinical terms, such as “full seizure” and “small seizure while awake.” Detailed semiological features and supporting clinical data, such as electroencephalography or magnetic resonance imaging, were not collected. Therefore, classification according to the ILAE framework was not applied, to avoid potential misclassification.

Other notable limitations of this study include the lack of measurement of the duration of ictal impairment of consciousness, the frequency of falls during seizures, and medication adherence, all of which might have contributed to unmeasured confounding. Future studies with a follow-up design would be valuable to address the abovementioned limitations and to explore the associations between the determinants of QOL and knowledge of, and attitudes toward, epilepsy care.

Conclusion

This study identified key factors influencing QOL among patients with epilepsy. The presence of other chronic diseases was associated with diminished overall well-being and cognitive function, whereas seizure duration and type were associated with seizure worry and overall QOL. Greater seizure frequency was associated with less seizure worry but potentially poorer overall QOL. We observed high cognitive functioning in married participants with epilepsy, high QOL in participants who had earned a high school degree, high social functioning in male participants, and high emotional well-being in participants with epilepsy with an annual household income of 10,000–15,000 SAR. These findings highlight the need for tailored interventions to address the diverse challenges faced by patients with epilepsy.
