From Minimally Invasive Endodontics to Functionally Intelligent Endodontics
Authors: Mohammed Turky, Paul MH Dummer
Published: 2026

Minimally invasive endodontics (MIE) aims to preserve tooth structure to enhance long-term retention.1 Several conservative approaches—such as vital pulp therapy, selective caries removal, and regenerative endodontic procedures—demonstrate predictable outcomes while maintaining structural integrity.2, 3, 4 These advances reflect an important shift toward tissue preservation in contemporary endodontics.

However, structural preservation alone may no longer be an adequate guiding principle. In an era increasingly shaped by digital technologies, artificial intelligence (AI), and systems-based clinical reasoning, endodontic decision-making should extend beyond the question of how little dentine can be removed.

We propose a broader framework: functionally intelligent endodontics. Rather than focusing solely on minimal intervention, this approach asks: What level of intervention best balances biological control, biomechanical resilience, and patient-specific risk? Preservation remains important, but it becomes one variable within a more comprehensive decision model.

Teeth function within complex biological and mechanical environments. Inadequate microbial control—particularly in biofilm-driven apical periodontitis—remains a primary cause of post-treatment disease.5 Conversely, excessive tissue removal may compromise structural integrity and reduce fracture resistance.6 Therefore, both overly conservative and overly aggressive strategies may be suboptimal.

Functionally intelligent endodontics integrates three domains:

1. Biological intelligence that requires effective biofilm disruption using mechanical, chemical, and hydrodynamic strategies tailored to root canal complexity.7

2. Biomechanical intelligence that recognises that preserved dentine is beneficial only if the remaining tooth can withstand functional loads over time.6 Access design and restorative planning must therefore consider stress distribution and fatigue behaviour.

3. Contextual intelligence that acknowledges patient variability—systemic health, compliance, occlusal risk, and access to care. A technically conservative approach may not always represent the most appropriate long-term strategy.

Importantly, this framework does not reject MIE; it builds upon it. Structural preservation remains essential but is evaluated alongside microbial control, mechanical durability, and patient-centred risk.

Emerging technologies may accelerate this shift. Digital twins, three-dimensional imaging, computational modelling, and AI-driven risk assessment increasingly allow simulation of treatment outcomes before irreversible intervention.8, 9, 10, 11, 12 Such tools support personalised, predictive decision-making rather than reliance on historically uniform protocols.

Adopting a functionally intelligent philosophy has implications for education, guidelines, and research. Training may prioritise integrated biological-biomechanical reasoning; clinical frameworks may incorporate structured risk stratification; and outcome reporting may focus more on long-term function and patient-centred measures. Optimising durability may also enhance sustainability by reducing retreatment rates and cumulative healthcare costs.

Minimally invasive dentistry has emphasised doing less harm. The next evolution may be ensuring that every intervention—whether minimal or extensive—is strategically calibrated to optimise biological success, mechanical performance, and patient welfare. It may be timely to expand our philosophy from minimal intervention to intelligent intervention.
