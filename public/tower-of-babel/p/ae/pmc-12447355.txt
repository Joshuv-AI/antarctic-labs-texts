Assessment of Knowledge, Attitudes, and Practices Concerning Cervical Cancer Among Women of Early Reproductive Age in Bangladesh: A Cross‐Sectional Study
Authors: Abu Bakkar Siddique, Chand Sultana, Chandon Roy, Tafhimur Rahman, Mst. Jemi Hkatun, Maria Haider, Sraboni Akter, Chakema Carmack, Lakshmi Rani Kundu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsCervical cancer is a significant global health concern, especially in low‐ and middle‐income countries (LMICs) like Bangladesh. Despite its impact on the health of Bangladeshi women of reproductive age, research on their knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP) related to cervical cancer, particularly among women in early reproductive age, is notably lacking. This study aimed to address this gap by exploring the KAP related to cervical cancer among young women in Bangladesh.MethodsA cross‐sectional survey using convenience sampling was conducted in Dhaka, Bangladesh, in September and October 2023, with 627 female participants (mean age: 21.65 ± 1.49 years). A semi‐structured questionnaire was designed to include informed consent, socio‐demographic data, and an 11‐item section on cervical cancer KAP. Analysis utilized Stata (Version 15.0) and SPSS (Version 26.0).ResultsAverage scores KAP were 5.40 ± 3.59, 27.10 ± 3.00, and 20.80 ± 5.99, respectively. Higher scores were associated with various socio‐demographic factors, including age, employment status, presence of cooperative family members, social media usage duration, body mass index (BMI), and more. The study revealed associations between higher education, employment, younger age, lower BMI, increased social media use, and higher knowledge, attitudes, and practices regarding cervical cancer.ConclusionThe study underscores critical gaps in cervical cancer‐related knowledge, attitudes, and practices among young women in Bangladesh, influenced by factors such as education, employment, BMI, and social media use. These findings emphasize the urgent need for tailored, youth‐focused awareness programs and socio‐behavioral interventions to improve cervical cancer prevention and control efforts in similar LMICs settings.

Introduction

Cervical cancer (CC), a malignant neoplasm of the cervix uteri, is the fourth most common cancer among women globally, causing approximately 604,000 new cases and 342,000 deaths in 2020 [1]. In addition to its human toll, CC imposes significant economic burdens on individuals, families, and healthcare systems [2]. Expenses related to diagnosis, treatment, and palliative care can plunge the affected households into poverty, perpetuating financial hardship [3]. Furthermore, the wider economic repercussions encompass reduced workforce productivity due to disability and premature mortality, which can strain national economies [4]. High‐income countries have made notable progress in reducing the burden of cervical cancer; however, the situation in low‐ and middle‐income countries (LMICs) remains challenging [5].

Despite significant progress in high‐income countries through effective screening and vaccination programs, it remains a formidable public health challenge in LMICs, including Bangladesh [4, 6]. In Bangladesh, cervical cancer ranks as the second most prevalent cancer among women of reproductive age, accounting for approximately 12% of all female cancers and underscoring the urgency of addressing this public health issue [7, 8]. In 2018, there were an estimated 8068 new cases (10.6 per 100,000 women) and 5214 deaths (7.1 per 100,000 women) [9]. A study conducted in Bangladesh reported that women between the ages of 18 and 29 had approximately 3.3 times higher odds of developing cervical cancer compared to those aged 30 to 69. However, there exists a noticeable research gap pertaining to the KAP surrounding CC among women within this demographic [6].

CC ranks as a predominant contributor to cancer‐related illnesses and deaths among women of reproductive age in Bangladesh [7]. The epidemiological context of this disease in the country is marked by delayed diagnoses, constrained access to screening and treatment, and a pervasive lack of awareness concerning risk factors, symptoms, and preventive measures [7, 10]. Furthermore, a substantial proportion of women lack the requisite knowledge and engage inadequately in preventive practices against CC. These circumstances emphasize the critical need to investigate the KAP surrounding CC, with a view to comprehending and tackling the particular challenges encountered by this vulnerable demographic [4]. The demographic group of women in early reproductive age (defined here as women aged less than 25 years) is of particular interest in this context [11]. These women represent a crucial segment of the population, as their health behaviors and knowledge can significantly influence their future risk of developing CC [12].

However, there is a paucity of research dedicated to understanding the KAP of CC prevention and early detection among women in this age group [12]. Existing research on cervical cancer in Bangladesh primarily concentrates on older women or broader health concerns, creating a gap in our comprehension of the distinct requirements and obstacles encountered by women in the early reproductive age [7, 13]. This study aimed to fill this gap by delving into the intricacies of KAP regarding CC among young women in Bangladesh.

Given the preventable nature of CC through vaccination, early detection, and timely treatment, it is imperative to investigate the level of knowledge and awareness among this demographic group [14]. Furthermore, understanding their attitudes and practices related to CC can provide critical insights into the barriers that need to be overcome to reduce the burden of this disease. By identifying knowledge gaps and misconceptions regarding CC, this study can pave the way for educational campaigns and interventions aimed at improving women's health. Raising awareness about risk factors, knowledge, and preventive measures can empower women to take proactive steps to protect themselves.

Materials and Methods

Study Area

The study employed a cross‐sectional survey based on in‐person interviews. Data was collected from different areas of Dhaka district in Bangladesh, between September 1 to October 30, 2023.

We anticipated a prevalence estimate (p) of 50% in our study. A sample size of approximately 424 individuals was calculated, allowing for a 10% nonresponse rate [17, 18]. Nonetheless, we recruited 627 participants to enhance the study's robustness.

Study Design, Participants, and Procedure

The present study employed a cross‐sectional survey based on in‐person interviews using semi‐structured questionnaires, conducted from September 1 to October 30, 2023. Participants were recruited via non‐probability (convenience) sampling. Data were collected through door‐to‐door visits at the household level. In households with multiple eligible participants, one respondent was randomly selected to minimize intra‐household correlation and reduce clustering effects. Each interview lasted approximately 20–30 min. Initially, 690 individuals participated, with 627 responses were considered for final analysis after excluding incomplete responses. Missing data were deemed random and was addressed during the data entry phase. Data collection utilized paper‐based semi‐structured questionnaires in the participants’ native language, Bengali. Given the sensitivity of the topic CC, only female research assistants conducted data collection, ensuring strict confidentiality. The data collection was conducted by a team of 12 trained research assistants, all of whom were public health graduates. To ensure strict confidentiality and participant comfort, they received specialized training in professional data management, research ethics, standardized interviewing techniques, and confidentiality protocols. The entire training and data collection process was supervised by the principal investigator, a recognized expert in the field, to ensure adherence to the highest quality standards.

A pilot survey involving 30 individuals from the same target population was conducted to assess the questionnaire's acceptability and clarity. Following this survey, minor adjustments were made to the questionnaire based on feedback. These pilot responses were not considered into the final analysis. The initial page of the questionnaire featured an informed written consent statement outlining the study's objectives, procedures, and participants’ rights to opt out. Before commencing the survey, participants were required to provide informed written consent by affirming their willingness to participate voluntarily. Inclusion criteria comprised women of early reproductive age (below 25 years) [11], proficiency in Bengali language, willingness to participate, and residency in Bangladesh. Participants aged below 18 and equal or more than 25 years were excluded from the interview process.

Measures

Socio‐Demographic Measures

Socio‐demographic data was collected through inquiries regarding participants’ age, marital status (married/unmarried), education (below university/university level) [19], employment status (employed (any type of employment like job, business, freelancing, etc.)/unemployed), place of residence (rural/urban), monthly family income (less than 20000 Bangladeshi Taka [BDT]/20000 BDT to 30000 BDT/more than 30000 BDT) [17], genital problems [UTIs/vaginal bleeding/vaginal discharge/vaginal dryness, etc.] (yes/no), family history of CC (yes/no), cooperative family members (yes/no), family members with genital problems (yes/no), self‐perception about own physical health (good/bad), self‐perception about own mental health (good/bad), Body Mass Index [BMI] (underweight/normal/overweight [measurements of height and weight were obtained using scales, followed by the calculation of BMI], daily sleeping time (less than 7 h/7 to 9 h/more than 9 h) [18], smoking status (yes/no), alcohol intake (yes/no), social media usage in a day (less than 2 h/2 to 5 h/more than 5 h).

Knowledge, Attitudes, and Practices Measures

In this study, we utilized a set of 33 questions covering knowledge (11 items), attitudes (11 items), and prevention practices (11 items) related to CC. These enquiries were taken from well‐known, verified research [6, 20, 21, 22]. The skewness and kurtosis of all total scores were between ±2. This methodology was taken from previous validated KAP studies [17, 18, 23].

Participants were presented with a set of eleven questions, each had three options (yes, no, or don't know), aimed at assessing their knowledge of CC and its associated health impacts. (e.g., “Does the risk of cervical cancer increase with multiple partners?, Is cervical cancer a preventable disease?” (See details in Figure 1). The examination involved assigning a score of “1” to responses marked “yes,” and codes of “0” to responses of “no” or “don't know.” The cumulative score, ranging from 0 to 11, was determined by adding up individual item scores, with a higher score indicating increased knowledge [13]. Additionally, the study documented participants’ information sources on CC. The Cronbach's alpha coefficient for the knowledge items was computed as 0.86, indicating high internal consistency [24].

Knowledge regarding cervical cancer.

To assess attitudes of CC, a set of eleven questions was utilized, employing a three‐point Likert scale (e.g., 1 = disagree, 2 = neutral, 3 = agree). Instances of such inquiries encompass phrases such as: “I believe that cervical cancer is a serious health concern” and “I feel comfortable discussing cervical cancer and related topics with healthcare providers” (see details in Figure 2). The overall score was calculated by adding up the individual scores of each item, ranging from 11 to 33. A higher score suggests a more positive attitude [18, 23]. The Cronbach's Alpha for the attitude items was calculated as 0.82. Negative statements were coded in reverse (I am hesitant to get the HPV vaccine due to concerns about its side effects, etc.).

Attitudes regarding cervical cancer.

To assess the participants’ practices, eleven questions were posed, such as “How often do you perform self‐examinations to check for any abnormal changes in your reproductive health? How often do you engage in practices that can reduce the risk of cervical cancer (e.g., limiting sexual partners, not smoking, not taking alcohol, etc.)?” (see details in Figure 3). Participants responded using a three‐point scale, denoting “never,” “sometimes,” or “always.” These responses were then coded numerically: “never” as 1, “sometimes” as 2, and “always” as 3. Total scores, indicative of adherence levels, ranged from 11 to 33, with higher scores suggesting greater adherence to practices [18, 23]. The Cronbach's Alpha coefficient for the practice items was computed as 0.79.

Statistical Analysis

The data underwent analysis using various statistical software tools, including Microsoft Excel (2021), IBM SPSS Statistics version 26.0 (IBM Corp., NY, USA), and Stata version 15.0 (StataCorp LLC, TX, USA). Microsoft Excel was employed for data cleaning, coding, and sorting tasks. Afterwards, the prepared Excel file was imported into SPSS for generating descriptive statistics such as frequencies, percentages, means, and standard deviations. Subsequently, bivariate and multivariable linear regression analyses were carried out using STATA, incorporating the aggregated scores of knowledge, attitudes, and practices measures. A significance threshold of p < 0.05 was applied to all analyses [18]. A p‐value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all analyses, with regression tests performed as two‐tailed. All variables in the univariate regression analysis were subsequently included in the multivariable regression models. Before performing the multivariable linear regression, multicollinearity among the variables was evaluated. The analysis showed that all tolerance values were above 0.1 and the variance inflation factors (VIF) remained under 10, indicating an acceptable level of multicollinearity and low correlation among the independent variables [25].

Results

General Characteristics of the Participants

A total of 627 participants were included in the final analysis. Among them, the majority had below university‐level education (53.1%, n = 333/627), were unmarried (93.3%, n = 585/627), resided in rural areas (51.4%, n = 322/627), had a monthly family income exceeding 30,000 BDT (45.8%, n = 287/627), and were not employed (90%, n = 564/627). Furthermore, a significant portion of the participants reported having no genital problems (90.4%, n = 567/627), no family history of cervical cancer (94.1%, n = 590/627), cooperative family members (62.4%, n = 391/627), and no family history with genital problems (86.9%, n = 545/627). The majority of participants indicated that they did not smoke (96.2%, n = 603/627), refrained from alcohol consumption (95.9%, n = 601/627), and did not engage in daily physical exercise (57.3%, n = 359/627). Additionally, most of them perceived their physical health as good (84.2%, n = 528/627), and a majority reported good perceived mental health (77.8%, n = 488/627). Approximately 60.3% of participants reported sleeping an average of 7–9 h (n = 378/627), 52.6% fell into the category of 2–4 h of daily social media usage (n = 330/627), and 71.5% of women had a normal BMI (n = 448/627). The mean age of the participants was 21.65 ± 1.49 (Table 1).

Discussion

The present study assessed the KAP regarding CC among women of early reproductive age in Bangladesh. The study found that higher knowledge regarding CC was associated with social media use exceeding 4 h daily. Positive attitudes toward CC were linked to older age, unemployment, family history of CC, cooperative family members, poor self‐perceived physical health, normal sleeping patterns, and 2–4 h of social media use. Better practices were associated with education below university level, higher family income, cooperative family members, and regular physical exercise.

In this study, one positively predicting factor for higher knowledge scores was the use of social media for more than 4 h a day compared to those who used it less than 2 h. A similar result was found in a study of Ethiopian women [2]. This association could be attributed to the increasing presence of health‐related information on social media platforms [26]. Women who spent more time on social media may have come across more educational content about CC [27]. These findings suggest that digital platforms, especially social media, may serve as effective tools for disseminating CC‐related information, particularly among younger or more digitally engaged populations [27]. Surprisingly, living in urban areas was associated with lower knowledge scores compared to residing in rural areas. This contradicts with an American study which found that residents from urban areas poses higher knowledge [28]. This finding could be influenced by various factors, such as the accessibility and availability of healthcare facilities and information in particular rural areas [29]. Further research is needed to clarify this finding.

This study found that comparatively older participants had more correct attitudes toward CC. A study conducted among women in Nepal found older participants had higher level of positive attitudes regarding CC [30]. In contrast, a study conducted in southern Ethiopia reported a significant association between lower age and positive attitudes [22]. The positive attitudes among older individuals in our study may be explained by their greater life experience compared to younger, higher likelihood of having encountered health issues either personally or within their social circles, and increased engagement with healthcare services over time [30, 31, 32]. These findings highlight the importance of tailoring awareness and intervention strategies to different age groups, such as age‐specific health education campaigns [33]. Unemployed younger women exhibited more correct CC attitudes. This outcome could be explained by the additional free time that unemployed individuals often have, allowing them to engage in health‐related discussions and activities [34]. They may also have more opportunities to access and absorb health information [35]. Further research is needed to establish and clarify this association. Targeted awareness programs could leverage the availability and openness of unemployed younger women to further enhance CC–related attitudes [36].

Participants with a family history of CC were found to have more positive attitudes. A study in Ethiopia found a similar association between a family history of cervical cancer and related knowledge and a positive attitude [37]. This could be due to personal experiences within their families, making them more aware of the risks associated with CC. Such individuals may be more inclined to seek information and adopt preventive measures when prevention behaviors are normalized [4, 38]. Having close family who has gone through similar situations (e.g., CC treatment, CC screening) increases one's motivation and acts upon their subjective norms to carry out those same positive behaviors. Women with cooperative family members tended to have more correct attitudes. This positive association underscores the role of familial support and a conducive environment for discussing and addressing health issues. Supportive family members can facilitate open conversations about health, encouraging awareness and positive attitudes [39, 40]. These findings highlight the importance of leveraging family engagement and support in CC awareness programs [41]. Interventions that involve family members and encourage open dialog about health may be more effective in fostering positive attitudes and motivating preventive behaviors among women [42].

Participants with a negative self‐perception of their physical health exhibited more correct attitudes. This intriguing finding suggests that those who perceive themselves as less healthy may be more motivated to engage with health‐related topics [43]; keeping in mind the self‐reported nature of the assessment and that actual quality of health was not measured in the current study. It could be an indicator of heightened health consciousness and a greater willingness to learn and change behaviors [44]. Participants with normal sleeping patterns had more correct attitudes. A study of Botswana, South Africa found strong correlation between sleep and CC related knowledge [45]. Adequate sleep is known to have a positive impact on cognitive function and overall well‐being [46]. Those with normal sleep patterns may be better equipped to absorb and retain health information, leading to more positive attitudes [47]. These findings suggest that promoting healthy sleep habits could be a valuable component of interventions aimed at preventive behavior about CC, ultimately enhancing overall health [48].

Women who reported 2–4 h of social media usage per day had more correct attitudes in addition to higher average knowledge scores. This could be attributed to the potential exposure to health‐related information on social media platforms as well [49]. Social media can serve as a source of health education and awareness, influencing individuals’ attitudes toward health issues [2]. These findings suggest that social media platforms could be leveraged as effective tools for public health interventions, providing targeted and reliable information to promote positive health attitudes and improve knowledge among women [26].

Women with educational qualifications below the university level exhibited more positive attitudes and practices towards CC. In a recent systematic review, it was observed that students at the college or high school level exhibit improved rates of CC screening and vaccination [50]. This may be because women with lower education levels may be more exposed to health education and outreach programs, which are often designed to be accessible and understandable to a broader audience [51]. However, this finding contradicts much of the established literature and should therefore be interpreted with caution. Further research is needed to confirm this in the context of Bangladeshi women of early reproductive age.

Participants with a monthly family income exceeding 30000 BDT had more correct attitudes and practices regarding CC. An Indian study reported a similar outcome, indicating that individuals with higher economic status tend to have greater access to healthcare services related to CC [14]. This may be due to increased access to healthcare services and health‐related information for families with higher incomes [52]. These findings suggest that socioeconomic status plays a pivotal role in shaping attitudes and practices toward CC, highlighting the need for targeted interventions to improve awareness and access to healthcare services among lower‐income groups [33]. Having supportive and cooperative family members was associated with better CC practices. This further highlights the importance of familial support in promoting health‐seeking behavior and preventive practices [53].

Engaging in daily physical exercise was positively associated with better practices. Several prior studies involving CC patients have discovered that engaging in daily exercise can mitigate the severity of CC [54, 55]. These findings suggest that promoting regular physical activity could serve as an indirect yet effective strategy to enhance preventive practices against CC, highlighting the potential benefits of integrating lifestyle interventions into public health programs [54, 55]. Participants with a normal BMI were more likely to adopt positive practices. Several studies have revealed that having a higher BMI is associated with an increased risk of CC, while maintaining a normal BMI can lower the risk of developing CC [56, 57]. Maintaining a healthy body weight is often linked to overall health‐consciousness, which could extend to awareness and adherence to preventive health measures [58]. Thus, maintaining a healthy physique is not only important for reducing the physiological risk of CC but also encourages engagement in healthier lifestyle choices and preventive practices.

Strengths and Limitations of This Study

This study has several key strengths. First, it addresses a significant research gap by focusing specifically on women of early reproductive age in Bangladesh, a demographic often underrepresented in CC‐related KAP research. Additionally, the study incorporates relevant socio‐demographic variables enabling the identification of important factors of CC‐related KAP. However, the study has various limitations that deserve recognition. Firstly, the use of convenience sampling might introduce selection bias, potentially limiting the applicability of the findings to a wider population. Secondly, the collected data might suffer from recall bias, response bias, and social desirability bias, affecting response accuracy due to reliance on self‐reported measures. Additionally, the study's cross‐sectional design prevents the establishment of causal relationships and understanding changes over time. Furthermore, the lack of a comparison group and the limited generalizability to other regions or countries restrict the relevance of the findings. This study focused only on Dhaka. Future studies should extend the research to include other districts as well. These limitations should be considered when interpreting the results and drawing conclusions. Conducting a more comprehensive longitudinal or prospective study is needed to address these limitations.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study underscores the urgent need for targeted interventions to enhance knowledge and practices related to CC among women of early reproductive age in Bangladesh. While attitudes appear generally positive, knowledge gaps persist, with social media emerging as a potential avenue for education. Public health efforts may capitalize upon this by implementing CC awareness public health advertisements within popular social media platforms. Family support and maintaining a healthy lifestyle also play pivotal roles in CC knowledge and practices. Community awareness campaigns may be implemented within communities to highlight the importance of women's health and by using approaches that capitalize upon the social support aspects of women's health (e.g., awareness events whereby women are asked to bring a female friend or relative to participate alongside them). Overall, these findings emphasize the importance of comprehensive awareness campaigns and accessible healthcare services in both rural and urban areas to mitigate the burden of CC in this vulnerable demographic.
