Favorable midterm clinical results of medial unicompartmental knee arthroplasty guided by coronal limb alignment using an image-free navigation system
Authors: Kengo Shimozaki, Shingo Shimozaki, Eiji Shimozaki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroduction Medial unicompartmental knee arthroplasty results in good postoperative motion and high patient satisfaction. However, inaccurate bone resection during manual procedures can cause tibial fractures, implant subsidence, or lateral compartment osteoarthritis. To improve accuracy, we performed medial unicompartmental knee arthroplasty using an image-free navigation system, with a target postoperative alignment of 2°–5° varus. This study evaluated midterm clinical outcomes and safety.Materials and methodsThis single-center retrospective case series included 144 consecutive knees (25 in men and 119 in women; mean age, 72.2 years) that underwent medial unicompartmental knee arthroplasty for varus osteoarthritis between 2011 and 2021, with at least three years of follow-up (mean, 63.1 months). A fixed-bearing implant (Triathlon PKR; Stryker) was used in all cases. The navigation system guided tibial and femoral resections to achieve a hip–knee–ankle angle of 2°–5° varus, while minimizing tibial resection. Pre- and postoperative outcomes were compared for range of motion, Western Ontario and McMaster Universities Osteoarthritis Index (WOMAC), ability to sit in the seiza position, implant survival, and complications.ResultsMean knee extension improved from − 5.7° to − 0.6°, flexion from 141.6° to 145.9°, and the mean WOMAC score improved from 41.5 ± 9.9 to 4.9 ± 3.5. The number of patients able to sit in the seiza position increased significantly. Four knees underwent lateral unicompartmental knee arthroplasty for progression of lateral osteoarthritis, and one was converted to total knee arthroplasty for infection. No tibial fractures or implant subsidence occurred. The Kaplan–Meier implant survival rate, with failure defined as reoperation for any cause, was 96.5%.ConclusionsAlignment-guided medial unicompartmental knee arthroplasty targeting 2°–5° varus using image-free navigation achieved favorable midterm outcomes without major complications.Level of evidenceLevel IV.Keywords: Medial unicompartmental knee arthroplasty, Navigation, Coronal limb alignment, Midterm outcomes

Introduction

Unicompartmental knee arthroplasty (UKA) provides excellent postoperative outcomes, including range of motion (ROM) and high patient satisfaction, and is less invasive than total knee arthroplasty (TKA) [1–3]. However, because the operative field is narrow and reliable anatomical landmarks are limited, conventional manual instrumentation is susceptible to component malpositioning and coronal malalignment [4, 5], which can lead to complications such as tibial fracture, implant subsidence, and progression of lateral compartment osteoarthritis due to overcorrection [6–8]. Therefore, navigation-assisted UKA has been advocated to improve implant positioning accuracy and reduce alignment outliers [9–11].

Although minimizing tibial resection is considered important in medial UKA, many conventional descriptions rely primarily on distance-based references rather than coronal limb alignment [12, 13]. Without considering whole-limb alignment, tibial resection may result in excessive varus deformity or require additional bone resection, potentially increasing the risk of tibial fracture and implant subsidence [14, 15]. Therefore, the present study focuses on alignment-guided targeting rather than navigation technology itself. In our practice, an image-free navigation system is used intraoperatively to evaluate coronal whole-limb alignment as well as the femoral and tibial resection planes. Previous studies have suggested that slight varus alignment may be associated with better functional outcomes than neutral alignment after medial UKA [16, 17]. Our surgical concept is to (i) minimize tibial resection and seat the tibial component on the subchondral bone to reduce the risk of tibial fracture, and (ii) achieve a postoperative hip–knee–ankle (HKA) angle of 2°–5° varus to improve patient satisfaction while avoiding excessive varus deformity because of undercorrection and lateral compartment overload due to overcorrection [18]. Although alignment-guided surgery carries a risk of under- or overcorrection depending on the individual soft-tissue balance [19], we address these issues in our technique by adjusting the amount of distal femoral resection.

This study aimed to evaluate the midterm clinical outcomes of medial UKA performed with alignment-guided targets using image-free navigation and determine the safety and utility of this surgical technique. We hypothesized that this approach would achieve favorable clinical outcomes with low rates of implant-related complications and reoperation.

Materials and methods

This was a single-center retrospective study approved by the institutional ethics committee, and informed consent was obtained from all patients. Between 2011 and 2021, 238 consecutive knees that underwent medial UKA for varus osteoarthritis at a single institution were identified. Patients with < 3 years of follow-up were excluded because the aim of this study was to evaluate midterm outcomes. Of these, 144 knees with a postoperative follow-up of at least three years were included, whereas 94 knees with a follow-up of less than three years were excluded. There were 25 and 119 knees in men and women, respectively. The mean age at surgery was 72.2 years, and the mean follow-up period was 63.1 months (range, 36–122 months).

The inclusion criteria for medial UKA were as follows: (1) severe osteoarthritis or osteonecrosis with pain in daily activity confined to the medial compartment, (2) no significant cartilage degeneration of the patellofemoral and lateral compartments on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI, Outerbridge grade ≤ 1), (3) no ligament injury of the knee confirmed by MRI, (4) coronal limb alignment classified as Kennedy’s Zone 1 to C in HKA angle confirmed by non-weight-bearing whole lower limb radiography [20], (5) knee extension better than − 15°, (6) body mass index ≤ 35, (7) age ≥ 50 years, and (8) Tegner activity level ≤ 4. We retrospectively investigated the HKA angle using navigation and non-weight-bearing whole lower limb radiography, knee ROM, the number of patients able to sit in the traditional Japanese style (seiza), the Western Ontario and McMaster Universities Osteoarthritis Index (WOMAC), implant survival rate, and complications from the preoperative period to the final follow-up to evaluate the midterm clinical outcomes of our surgical technique. Knee ROM, the ability to sit in seiza, and WOMAC scores were assessed by physical therapists at routine clinical visits. Non–weight-bearing whole-leg radiographs were used because pain-related limitations in knee extension during weight-bearing acquisition may compromise accurate alignment assessment. Furthermore, because navigation measurements are performed under non–weight-bearing conditions, this approach was adopted to ensure consistency between radiographic and navigation-based evaluations.

Surgical technique

A total of 144 knees were operated on by two senior surgeons (120 knees by Surgeon A and 24 knees by Surgeon B). In all cases, the other surgeon served as the assistant, and the surgical technique was standardized. The Triathlon PKR (Stryker, USA), a fixed UKA system, was used in all cases. Additionally, an image-free navigation system (Stryker, USA) was used to achieve the target postoperative coronal limb alignment (HKA angle 2°–5° varus), consistent with our concept.

The surgical procedure was as follows: The alignment-guided surgical workflow is summarized in Fig. 1. The surgery was performed under general anesthesia with a tourniquet applied. Following skin incision, the knee was exposed via a mid-vastus approach with minimal soft-tissue release and osteophyte removal, limited to that required for the insertion of a retractor into the posteromedial tibia. Navigation pins were inserted into the femur and tibia, and preoperative coronal limb alignment was measured using the navigation system before proximal tibial resection (Fig. 2).

The coronal osteotomy angle of the tibia was adjusted according to each patient’s medial proximal tibial angle (MPTA) [21]. In cases with an MPTA > 5° and classified as Kennedy’s Zone 1-C, the tibial cut was set at 5° varus. However, because most cases with an MPTA > 5° were categorized as Kennedy’s Zone 0, TKA was performed in these patients. In the sagittal plane, the tibial slope was determined according to each patient’s native posterior tibial slope, while ensuring that the angle did not exceed 10° (Fig. 3). The amount of tibial resection was adjusted to preserve the subchondral bone at the medial edge; if no cartilage remained, the cut was set to approximately 2 mm at the medial edge. After the initial tibial cut, the coronal varus angle and posterior slope were measured (Fig. 4), and additional resection was performed as necessary to achieve the target al.ignment. We considered preserving the subchondral bone at the tibial implant site to prevent implant-related complications. Therefore, tibial resection was limited to the minimum that allowed the insertion of an 8-mm spacer combined with a 1.5-mm filler gauge at 90° flexion, at which point coronal limb alignment in extension was confirmed (Fig. 5).

Femoral resection was performed in a tibia-dependent manner. If the coronal alignment at this stage fell within the predefined target range, distal femoral resection equal to the implant thickness was performed. In cases of valgus alignment relative to the target, indicating overcorrection, an additional 1–3 mm of the distal femoral bone was resected to restore alignment. Conversely, if the varus exceeded the target alignment, the amount of distal femoral resection was reduced by 1–3 mm. Femoral resection was performed within 2° of varus or valgus and 2–5° of flexion (Fig. 6). After bony preparation, trial components were inserted, and appropriate coronal alignment was confirmed (Fig. 7), along with the ability to insert a 1.5-mm filler gauge at 90° of flexion. Peg holes were prepared for implant fixation, and the final components were implanted using bone cement.

The position of the femoral cutting guide was confirmed using the navigation system before femoral resection (a). The resection thickness of the distal femur was determined based on the coronal alignment of the entire limb, and the cutting plane was set at within 2° of varus/valgus and 2°–5° of flexion (b)

Postoperative coronal limb alignment in radiograph (a) and intraoperative coronal limb alignment using image-free navigation after implantation (b). Whole-leg coronal radiograph and navigation show 3° of varus

The drain was removed on the first postoperative day, and full weight-bearing ambulation and ROM training were initiated. The patients achieved a flexion ROM > 120° within a couple of weeks postoperatively and were subsequently discharged. Outpatient rehabilitation was continued twice weekly for an average of four months.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using JMP software, version 19.0.0 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Preoperative and final follow-up values were compared for ROM, the number of patients able to sit in the seiza position, and WOMAC scores. For continuous variables, comparisons between pre- and postoperative values were performed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Categorical variables were compared using McNemar’s test. The level of significance was set at α = 0.05. Implant survival was estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method, with failure defined as reoperation for any cause. Patients without reoperation were censored at the final follow-up. There were no missing data for the evaluated outcomes in the included knees. The required sample size was calculated using G*Power, which indicated that 106 cases would be necessary with an effect size of 0.25, a power of 0.95, and α = 0.05. The magnitude of change was described using mean differences with 95% confidence intervals to facilitate clinical interpretation.

Results

The pre- and postoperative results are presented in Table 2. Intraoperative navigation–based assessment of preoperative and postoperative lower-limb alignment closely matched the measurements obtained from non–weight-bearing whole-leg radiographs, supporting achievement of the postoperative target alignment and accurate surgical execution. The mean follow-up period was 63.1 ± 24.7 months (median, 56.0 months; range, 18–122 months). Of the 144 knees, 63 (43.8%) were followed for ≥ 5 years, and 36 (25.0%) were followed for ≥ 7 years. In all 144 knees, the knee extension range improved significantly from − 5.7° (range − 15° to 0°) preoperatively to − 0.6° (range − 10° to 0°) postoperatively, and the flexion range improved from 141.6° (range 100° to 150°) to 145.9° (range 120° to 150°) at final follow-up. The mean changes from preoperative to final follow-up were + 5.10° in knee extension (95% CI, 4.33–5.88) and + 4.37° in knee flexion (95% CI, 2.55–6.19). Regarding the number of knees able to achieve the seiza position, McNemar’s test was performed for paired binary data before and after surgery. Seven patients who were able to sit in the seiza position preoperatively became unable to do so postoperatively, whereas 25 patients who were unable to sit in this position became able to after the operation. The ability to sit in seiza significantly increased after surgery. The WOMAC score improved from 41.5 ± 9.9 preoperatively to 4.9 ± 3.5 postoperatively. The mean improvement in WOMAC score was − 36.6 points (95% CI, − 38.28 to − 34.92). Reoperations due to complications included lateral UKA in four knees for symptomatic progression of lateral compartment osteoarthritis with radiographic changes and conversion to TKA in one knee due to postoperative infection. Reoperations occurred at 18 months (two lateral UKAs), 24 months (one conversion to TKA for infection), 36 months (one lateral UKA), and 72 months (one lateral UKA) postoperatively. In contrast, no tibial fracture or implant subsidence was observed, and the Kaplan–Meier implant survival rate, with failure defined as reoperation for any cause, was 96.5% (Fig. 8).

Discussion

The most important finding of the present study was that medial UKA performed with image-free navigation using coronal whole-limb alignment as a reference demonstrated favorable midterm clinical outcomes. No reoperations were required because of tibial fracture due to implant malpositioning or implant failure. Although longer-term follow-up beyond 10 years is needed, this method can be considered safe and effective in the midterm. These findings should be interpreted as demonstrating the feasibility and safety of an alignment-guided strategy rather than superiority over other surgical techniques.

Several previous studies have reported favorable mid- to long-term survival rates for UKA, ranging from 86% to 95% at 10 years, with the most frequent causes of failure being loosening of the femoral component and progression of osteoarthritis in the lateral compartment [3, 22, 23]. In fixed-bearing UKA, the femoral components are prone to varus malposition [24], and greater deviations in implantation angles increase the risk of failure [25]. Navigation systems reduce outliers in implant positioning and achieve good clinical outcomes [26, 27]. In the present study, accurate component positioning achieved with navigation likely contributed to the favorable outcomes.

Furthermore, no early postoperative complications such as tibial fractures or implant subsidence were observed. This may be attributed to our surgical technique, in which tibial resection is minimized and the coronal tibial cut is performed with slight varus alignment according to the MPTA [22, 28], rather than perpendicular to the tibial axis. Minimizing bone resection not only allows implantation on strong subchondral bone but also enables the use of larger components supported by the anterior and medial cortical bone, thereby preventing anterior subsidence and tibial fractures [29, 30].

In contrast, minimizing tibial resection may shift coronal limb alignment from varus toward valgus. In UKA, valgus alignment in the coronal plane should be avoided because it increases the load on the lateral compartment, leading to poor clinical outcomes [31, 32]. Therefore, we intraoperatively confirmed coronal whole-limb alignment using an image-free navigation system, and in cases prone to valgus alignment relative to our target, we performed an additional distal femoral resection of 1–3 mm beyond the implant thickness to maintain a postoperative HKA angle of 2°–5° varus. In such cases, increasing the amount of femoral resection results in elevation of the postoperative joint line by approximately 1–3 mm compared with the native joint surface. Although joint line elevation may theoretically influence knee kinematics and soft-tissue tension, previous studies have reported that elevation of less than 4 mm does not adversely affect clinical outcomes [33]. Consistent with these reports, favorable clinical outcomes were achieved in our study.

Although only a few studies have investigated target coronal limb alignment in medial UKA, patients with a postoperative varus alignment of 2°–7° have been reported to demonstrate better functional scores than those with neutral alignment [16, 17]. However, excessive varus deformity may increase the risk of tibial fractures and reoperation, potentially decreasing patient satisfaction [30, 31]. Conversely, Freigang et al. [34] reported favorable short-term outcomes in a series of 31 knees by targeting neutral alignment (0°–2° varus) in medial UKA. However, postoperative valgus alignment was observed in some cases, which may predispose patients to degeneration of the lateral compartment. Even when using navigation or robotic assistance, final implant placement is performed manually; therefore, some degree of error is inevitable.

In recent years, robotic-assisted UKA has also been increasingly adopted and has been reported to improve implant positioning accuracy and reduce alignment outliers compared with conventional techniques [35]. However, direct comparisons between robotic-assisted UKA and alignment-guided navigation-assisted UKA remain limited, and further comparative studies are warranted to clarify the relative advantages of each approach. Although robotic-assisted UKA has demonstrated improved accuracy in component positioning, several studies have reported comparable short- to midterm clinical outcomes and implant survival between robotic-assisted and navigation-assisted or conventional UKA [4, 5, 32]. Therefore, improved technical accuracy does not necessarily translate into superior clinical outcomes. From this perspective, the present study does not aim to compete with robotic technology but rather proposes an alignment-guided concept that may be applied using various surgical platforms. Our findings suggest that careful intraoperative control of coronal whole-limb alignment, rather than the navigation or robotic system itself, may be a key factor contributing to favorable outcomes.

To minimize complications and avoid postoperative valgus or excessive varus alignment, we aimed for a target coronal alignment of 2°–5° varus in UKA. To our knowledge, no previous study has reported midterm outcomes of UKA performed with this intraoperative coronal alignment control. In the present study, this alignment-guided strategy was associated with favorable midterm results and a low rate of implant-related complications and reoperation.

The ability to sit in the seiza position significantly improved after surgery. Although seiza is a culturally specific outcome in Japan, it reflects deep-flexion function, which may also be relevant to activities requiring kneeling or floor-based movements in other populations. Such deep-flexion activities are clinically relevant not only in Asian populations but also in occupational or recreational tasks that require kneeling, squatting, or floor-level mobility.

This study has several limitations. First, this was a single-center, retrospective case series without a control group, and therefore causal conclusions regarding superiority over other techniques cannot be drawn. Second, patient satisfaction was not evaluated, and future studies should investigate whether satisfaction differs between patients within and outside the target postoperative alignment range. In particular, patient-reported quality-of-life measures may provide additional insight into whether alignment-guided strategies translate into meaningful patient-centered benefits beyond objective clinical scores. Third, only a limited number of the 238 knees could be followed up for more than three years, which may have introduced attrition bias and reduced the accuracy of complication assessment. However, the primary reason for exclusion was insufficient follow-up duration rather than postoperative complications or adverse events; therefore, the included cohort is unlikely to represent a selectively favorable population. Nevertheless, baseline characteristics of excluded knees were not consistently available for retrospective comparison, and the potential impact of attrition bias cannot be fully excluded, which remains a limitation of this study. Fourth, radiographic measurements were performed by a single observer, and intra- and interobserver reliability were not evaluated. Fifth, non–weight-bearing whole-leg radiographs may not fully reflect functional weight-bearing alignment. This methodological difference should be considered when comparing our alignment results with studies using weight-bearing radiographs, as alignment values obtained under non–weight-bearing conditions may not be directly interchangeable with functional alignment measurements. Sixth, navigation registration error or accuracy metrics were not routinely recorded in this study. However, previous studies have demonstrated that image-free navigation systems provide reliable coronal alignment measurements with acceptable accuracy, supporting the validity of navigation-guided alignment assessment in the present study [26, 27]. Finally, the present findings were obtained using a fixed-bearing UKA system and strict inclusion criteria, and therefore may not be generalizable to mobile-bearing implants or broader patient populations.

In conclusion, medial UKA performed with image-free navigation using intraoperative coronal whole-limb alignment to guide a target postoperative HKA angle of 2°–5° varus yielded favorable midterm outcomes with few major complications. Further comparative studies are needed to validate the proposed alignment target and clarify its applicability to different implant systems and patient populations.

Conclusion

Medial UKA performed with image-free navigation, aimed at a postoperative coronal whole-leg alignment of 2°–5° varus in HKA, yielded favorable midterm outcomes. This alignment-guided navigation strategy may represent a safe and feasible approach for medial UKA in carefully selected patients.
