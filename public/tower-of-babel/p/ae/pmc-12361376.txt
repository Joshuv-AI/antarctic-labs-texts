Lightweight multiscale information aggregation network for land cover land use semantic segmentation from remote sensing images
Authors: Yahia Said, Oumaima Saidani, Ali Delham Algarni, Mohammad H. Algarni, Ayman Flah
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Land Cover and Land Use (LCLU) segmentation plays a fundamental role in various remote sensing applications, including environmental monitoring, urban planning, and disaster management. Traditional models often face limitations in real-time processing and deployment on resource-constrained devices due to their high computational requirements. This paper presents a lightweight neural network designed to address these challenges by integrating dense dilated convolutions with pyramid depthwise convolutions for multiscale feature extraction. The proposed encoder-decoder architecture utilizes dense connections to aggregate spatial and contextual information across different resolutions, enhancing segmentation accuracy while minimizing computational overhead. The model’s performance was rigorously evaluated using the NITRDrone and UDD6 datasets, demonstrating a segmentation accuracy of 94.8%, with a significantly reduced parameter count compared to state-of-the-art methods. The compact design of the network facilitates its implementation on low-power devices, enabling real-time LCLU analysis across diverse environmental conditions. This work underscores the potential of lightweight neural networks to advance remote sensing image processing, offering scalable and efficient solutions for practical applications in geospatial analysis.

Introduction

Land Cover and Land Use (LCLU) classification represents a fundamental component of cartographic data essential for urban planning and environmental monitoring. Accurate and up-to-date LCLU maps are critical for the effective management of large-scale environmental and socio-economic events. These maps serve as a vital tool in environmental assessment, resource management, and policy-making processes. Land use pertains to the characterization of land based on its functional role in human activities—such as residential, agricultural, or industrial utilization—whereas land cover refers to the biophysical materials present on the Earth’s surface, including vegetation, water bodies, and bare soil. City life is what drives economic expansion. How city climates impact thermal characteristics, ecological services, and everyday life is the single most significant environmental aspect. The changing weather patterns, reduced rainfall, diminishing water supplies, intensified droughts, and floods in urban areas are further ways in which climate change diminishes environmental sustainability. Due to the loss of arable land, natural habitats, and water supplies, food insecurity is a major consequence of unanticipated changes in urban LULC.

Land use and land cover (LULC) classification plays a fundamental role in urban planning, environmental monitoring, resource management, and disaster response. With increasing pressures from deforestation, urban expansion, and climate change, accurate and timely LULC mapping has become essential for sustainable development and ecological preservation. These changes, which occur at large scales, can have immediate and long-term impacts on ecosystem processes and biodiversity. Consequently, there is a growing need for effective tools that can support environmental governance through reliable and detailed spatial information.

Remote sensing (RS) technologies, including both satellite and aerial imagery, offer powerful capabilities for capturing large-scale landscape dynamics. These data sources enable continuous monitoring of land surfaces and facilitate the detection of changes in land use and land cover patterns. However, unplanned urbanization and abrupt LULC changes pose several challenges to modern cities. These include urban sprawl, infrastructure strain, reduced green cover, water scarcity, and increased pollution levels1. Additionally, such developments compromise urban sustainability and heighten ecological vulnerability to both regional and global climate shifts2.

Automatic classification of RS data has emerged as a key technological solution to support environmental protection, land management, and precision agriculture3. Despite this progress, traditional classification techniques often struggle with the complexity and variability inherent in multi-resolution RS imagery. Ground objects may exhibit considerable diversity in shape, texture, and spectral characteristics depending on imaging conditions, making consistent classification difficult4.

Recent advances in deep learning (DL) have transformed the field of computer vision, enabling remarkable improvements in applications such as object detection5, indoor navigation6, pedestrian tracking7, road sign recognition8, and medical image analysis9. Inspired by these developments, DL-based techniques have been increasingly applied to RS data, offering enhanced capabilities for automatic LULC classification. The growing availability of multi-source and multi-temporal RS datasets, along with improvements in computational power and algorithmic sophistication, further strengthens the feasibility and importance of such approaches10.

In the context of precision agriculture and sustainable land management, accurate and reliable classification of cropland and land use patterns is vital for supporting data-driven decision-making at governmental and institutional levels11. Meanwhile, urbanization continues to accelerate due to population growth and economic development. While urbanization contributes positively to infrastructure, technology, and living standards, it also introduces challenges such as waste management, biodiversity loss, and environmental degradation12.

Given these challenges and opportunities, LULC classification has become a critical task across multiple domains. Deep learning-based methods have shown considerable promise in addressing classification complexity, achieving state-of-the-art results and improving the reliability of environmental and agricultural monitoring systems.

The primary goal of this study is to design a novel deep learning-based system for land use and land cover classification. We propose a lightweight multiscale information aggregation architecture that is computationally efficient and suitable for deployment on resource-constrained devices. The system aims to deliver high accuracy in LULC classification while maintaining scalability and adaptability. To validate the performance and generalizability of the proposed model, we conducted extensive experiments on two benchmark datasets: the Urban Drone Dataset (UDD6) and the NITRDrone Dataset (NITRDD).

The remainder of the paper is the following: Section “Related work” details the state-of-the-art works concerning land cover land use classification using deep learning techniques. In Section “Proposed approach”, the methodology that was suggested for developing a system to detect LCLU is described in depth. Section “Experiment and results” details all the experiments that were carried out in this study and includes the results. Section “Conclusion” provides the conclusion.

Related work

Land use and land cover data (LULC) are of great importance to applications utilizing earth observation. Effective classification tasks are necessary for urban planning, environmental management, climate change monitoring, and other socioeconomic concerns. Numerous investigations have been sparked by the recent difficulties brought on by rapidly changing land cover and high resolution remotely sensed data. A lot of work has been put into achieving accurate categorization using very high resolution and very fine spatial resolution.

Classifying land uses and land covers is essential for managing and comprehending Earth’s surface. It entails classifying and depicting various land cover types—forests, cities, bodies of water, farmland, and natural landscapes—and the land use activities associated with each. Manually interpreting satellite images is a common and sometimes subjective component of traditional LCLU categorization methodologies. However, a huge leap forward in automating and boosting accuracy has occurred with the introduction of deep learning algorithms. Feature learning is fundamental to ML, however, techniques based on deep learning have made it possible to skip the tedious human feature learning and get right to the meat of processing massive datasets. These deep-learning techniques were first suggested in the late 80 s13, but they were never widely used because of the lack of accessible training data and processing capacity.

In order to determine the perimeters of the buildings that have been segmented, Lee et al.14 presented a methodology that relies on boundary detection in aerial photographs. To extract roads from satellite-captured aerial photos, Zhang et al.15 presented an ensemble method based on convolutional neural networks (CNNs). The extraction of vegetation from images taken by unmanned aerial vehicles (UAV) has also been considered16.

A fully convolutional architecture is employed for several aerial picture segmentation tasks17. This architecture consists of two phases: one for collecting contextual information and another for expanding the feature matrix to match the original input image resolution. Also, to address the present segmentation problems in image segmentation tasks, the researchers18 came up with a plethora of designs. The complexity of UAV-based aerial pictures, caused by the high number of ground objects in each image, makes semantic segmentation a more daunting task.

To classify land uses and land covers from aerial photos, Zhang et al.19 suggested a combined deep learning approach. The suggested method set out to discover the ideal input scale. To suit the joint distribution that is implicit in the joint deep learning, a scale sequence joint deep learning was used. The suggested model combined a convolutional neural network with a multilayer perceptron. There were two locations in the south and north west of England where the suggested method was tested.

In order to classify LCLU from satellite photos, a deep learning-based approach was suggested in20. By combining a context module with a generative adversarial network (GAN)21, the suggested approach hopes to improve a CNN model’s performance. All three of these networks—the base, the base with GAN, and the base with context module—are combined to produce the final output. The fundamental concept was to construct an ensemble learning technique using the basic network and its improved variants. We trained and evaluated the suggested model using Landsat 5/7 data. Due to its computational complexity, the suggested solution necessitates resources with a high-performance computing capability.

Random forest algorithms22 were suggested for evaluation and comparison with recurrent neural networks23 and temporal convolutional neural networks24 by Pelletier et al.25. The GRU network’s exceptional performance in capturing long-distance connections and solving the vanishing gradient issue led to its adoption as a recurrent neural network. Three 64-neuron convolution layers made up the temporal CNN, with a further 256 neurons in a fully linked layer and finally a classification softmax layer. This work’s data came from Sentinel 2 photos that had a resolution of 250 m × 250 m and were of uniform vegetation and water regions. Comparing several methods revealed that the temporal CNN outperformed the GRU network, with the random forest producing the poorest results for LCLU categorization.

The authors of26 put forth a methodology for detecting changes in land use and land cover using a siamese global learning algorithm. Given the amount of parameter computations performed by the DL network, a large amount of labeled data is continuously required. There is a sample imbalance issue with semantic change detection due to DL methods’ patch-based learning architecture, which just considers the immediate area, while identifying changes. To tackle these issues, the authors suggested a novel method for detecting semantic changes in HSR remote sensing images—the Siamese global learning (Siam-GL) framework.

To eliminate spectral ambiguity among different forms of land cover, land use and land cover (LULC) mapping—a prominent use of remote sensing—requires extremely high spatial and spectral resolutions. Data such as aerial hyperspectral photographs are well-suited for such uses because of their ability to detect tiny objects in the field and their abundance of spectral bands. To obtain more dependable land use map mapping utilizing high-resolution hyperspectral image data and to increase the classification accuracy of land use/cover mapping applications, a novel framework is suggested in27.

Using high-resolution (0.8 m) remote sensing pictures, three deep convolutional neural networks were evaluated to create high-precision Fractional Vegetation Coverage (FVC) in drylands: Unet, Pspnet, and Deeplabv3+28,29. An example of a typical study case was the lower Tarim River Basin. The three models with the best accuracy rates were Unet (93.38%), Pspnet (88.14%), and Deeplabv3 + (88.91%). Compared to the FVC generated from the normalized difference vegetation index (NDVI) and land use/land cover data from ESRI and ESA, the Unet FVC map was determined to be more in agreement with the on-site field observations. We found that among the drivers impacting dryland FVC, groundwater depth is more significant than topographical and climatic variables. Groundwater depths greater than -3 m reduce the probability of high FVC development by 50%. In drylands where vegetation is very variable in space, this study introduces a new method for obtaining the FVC. This allowed for more accurate quantification of carbon storage, improved upon the present dataset’s inadequacy, and served as a helpful reference for monitoring plant change.

An approach was suggested30 to automatically recognize and separate tree tops in digital photographs taken by UAVs using Detectron2. The main objective of the work was to train the algorithm to recognize and segment tree crowns. Video captured by a drone above a tangerine orchard near Palermo, Sicily, Italy, used as the experimental site. The exact boundary of each tree was determined by the algorithm. The outcomes of tree boundary segmentation were compared with those of the recently suggested technique using a Support Vector Machine (SVM) supervised classifier, a very promising object segmentation approach. The results of the two approaches were compared using direct digitization, the most exact but laborious process. The identified area, false area rate, skipped area efficiency, and wasted area rate were estimated for both methods to determine their accuracy. Detectron2 had better results than the SVM model in two of the three metrics used to evaluate data segmentation efficiency. In comparison to the digitalization method, the Detectron2 algorithm achieved higher fidelity rates for both the commonly detected area rate (0.959%) and the skipped area rate (0.041%). The SVM yielded results of 0.097% and 0.902%, respectively. When compared to the SVM classification’s 0.035 percent accuracy rate, the Detectron2 algorithm’s 0.056 percent was much lower. Assessing the tree’s health was the last step after the Detectron2 algorithm produced a credible boundary estimate.

Research in31 builds a novel LU/LC classification model based on Fuzzy Swin Transformers based on Yelagiri Hills LISS-III satellite data. Fuzzy clustering was used to collect LU/LC features, which were then used to train the Swin Transformer model in the study. The transformer finds the LU/LC classification map using the training patches, which is both computationally hard and very accurate. We used cluster, stratified, simple random, and systematic sampling techniques to guarantee the accuracy of the acquired LU/LC classification map. The proposed Fuzzy Swin Transformer model obtains an average classification accuracy of 98.43% when using Simple Random Sampling, 97.45% when using Stratified Random Sampling, 97.36% when using Systematic Sampling, and 96.77% when using Cluster Sampling. Land resource planners may be able to use this study’s finding of a LU/LC transition to better save hill station forests by pinpointing the area most in need of conservation efforts.

He et al.32 present LECOS for semantic segmentation in LCLU, a technique for learnable correlation-based sub-pixel mapping. A parameter-free "self-attention in self-attention" operation was developed to adaptively learn the diverse global correlation patterns between pixels and subpixels. Then, teleconnections using the “mutual retrieval” mechanism of the self-attention operation was presented to enable more distant locations or objects to be mutually correlated. It then uses the learnt spatial correlations to reason about the prevalence of each class on a sub-pixel scale33.

To facilitate semantic segmentation of remote sensing images, a massive annotation dataset (Globe230k)34 was proposed. It was proposed with focus to three advantages. First, vast size: the 232,819 annotated images in the Globe230k dataset have dimensions of 512 × 512 and a spatial resolution of 1 m. These images are organized into 10 first-level categories. Second, the annotated images come from all over the world, covering an area of more than 60,000 km2, which shows a lot of variety and variability. Third, in addition to RGB bands, the Globe230k dataset incorporates useful features for Earth system research, such as NDVI, DEM, VV and VH bands for vertical–vertical and vertical-horizontal polarization, respectively, which can aid in multimodal data fusion studies. By putting various state-of-the-art semantic segmentation algorithms through their paces on the Globe230k dataset, we discovered that it can assess techniques in various key areas for land cover characterization, such as multiscale modeling, detail reconstruction, and generalizability. Now that the information is available to the public, it can serve as a standard against which to measure future advancements in semantic segmentation algorithms and global land cover mapping35.

Proposed approach

The proposed LCLU system brings uniformity, consistency, and interoperability to the classification of land cover and land use. Applications supported by it include land management, environmental monitoring, spatial planning, and decision assistance. By creating a unified framework, it improves data exchange, analysis, and integration, ultimately promoting sustainable land management and well-informed decision-making. A new efficient land cover land use segmentation system is proposed in this paper. To contribute to the proposed work, a new neural network architecture is proposed. Various techniques have been applied to this architecture to contribute for better performances. In the following, we will detail the proposed architecture.

The proposed neural network architecture proposed in this paper for land cover land use segmentation purposes presents encoder-decoder architecture. This type of structure provides various benefits as:Contextual information capture: Both local and global contextual data can be captured using the encoder-decoder architecture. The network’s encoder section effectively extracts high-level characteristics and global context by gradually reducing the spatial dimensions of the input while increasing the number of channels. In order to enable exact localization and segmentation, the decoder part reconstructs the spatial dimensions while maintaining the learned context.Multiscale feature fusion: To handle objects of various sizes and capture fine-grained details, semantic segmentation frequently necessitates the incorporation of information at numerous scales. Through skip connections, the encoder-decoder architecture enables multi-scale feature fusion. The encoder and decoder’s respective layers are connected by skip connections, enabling the model to take advantage of both low-level and high-level features.Parameter efficiency and computation speed: In comparison to fully convolutional networks (FCNs) without skip connections, encoder-decoder topologies are more computationally and parameter-efficient.Generalizability and Adaptability: The architecture of the encoder and decoder is adjustable to various input sizes and resolutions. Regardless of the input’s size or aspect ratio, the network’s encoder portion learns solid and universal properties from it. Then, using the decoder component, segmentation maps with the same resolution as the input can be produced.Integration of contextual and local information: Semantic segmentation necessitates the capture of both local information and global context. The integration of these two forms of information is made easier by the encoder-decoder design. While the decoder uses skip connections to blend the acquired context with local specifics, the encoder records global context and high-level features.

The inclusion of DDC in our model allows the network to capture larger receptive fields without increasing the computational cost. This is crucial in remote sensing, where features such as urban structures, roads, and large vegetation patterns span multiple scales. DDC facilitates the extraction of spatial relationships across different scales, enhancing the model’s ability to recognize context-specific features in both fine-grained details (e.g., small objects like vehicles) and broader patterns (e.g., large urban areas). By maintaining dense connections between layers, DDC also helps the network learn long-range dependencies more efficiently, leading to improved segmentation accuracy, especially for complex land cover types.

The SSA mechanism helps the model focus on relevant spatial regions by assigning higher attention weights to important areas in the input image. This allows the model to emphasize features that contribute most to distinguishing different land cover types. For instance, in urban environments, SSA helps the model focus on buildings, roads, and other infrastructural elements that are crucial for accurate segmentation. In more natural settings, it helps the model identify vegetation, water bodies, and forested regions by focusing attention on spatial patterns specific to these land types. The improved feature selectivity through SSA enhances segmentation precision, especially in regions with complex backgrounds or mixed land uses.

Conclusion

This paper introduces an efficient and lightweight neural network for Land Cover and Land Use (LCLU) segmentation, designed to address real-time processing challenges in remote sensing applications. By integrating dense dilated convolutions with pyramid depthwise convolutions, the model effectively captures multiscale spatial and contextual features, ensuring accurate segmentation while minimizing computational overhead. Extensive evaluations on the NITRDrone and UDD6 datasets demonstrate the model’s superiority over existing state-of-the-art methods, achieving high segmentation accuracy with fewer parameters. The lightweight design enables deployment on low-end devices, making it suitable for real-time applications in resource-constrained environments. However, several limitations must be considered. First, the model’s generalization to non-urban or mixed land types remains an area for further exploration. While the model performs well on urban datasets, its effectiveness on rural or agricultural landscapes, which exhibit different spatial and spectral patterns, requires additional validation. Second, the model’s sensitivity to training data imbalance—especially in underrepresented land classes—may impact its performance in diverse real-world scenarios. Future work will explore more advanced techniques for handling class imbalance, such as domain adaptation or advanced sampling strategies. Additionally, while the compact architecture ensures efficient inference, there are trade-offs between model size and inference speed that need further consideration. Optimization techniques such as model pruning or quantization may be explored to further reduce inference time without sacrificing segmentation accuracy. In conclusion, this work demonstrates the potential of lightweight deep learning models for LCLU segmentation, offering efficient solutions for real-time remote sensing applications. Addressing the challenges of generalization to diverse land types, mitigating data imbalance, and optimizing inference speed remain key directions for future research.
