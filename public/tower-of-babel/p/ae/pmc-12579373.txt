Sex‐Specific Effects of Experience With Pups on Perineuronal Net Expression in the Biparental California Mouse (Peromyscus californicus)
Authors: Melina C. Acosta, Ian D. Warren, Wendy Saltzman, Khaleel A. Razak
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIn biparental species, repeated exposure to infants can induce parental behavior in virgin males and females. Little is known, however, about the mechanisms underlying neuroplasticity resulting from experience with infants. Perineuronal nets (PNNs), extracellular matrix structures, regulate neuroplasticity during development and adulthood. We examined the impact of pup exposure on PNN expression in the medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC), medial amygdala (MeA), and medial preoptic area (MPOA) in male and female California mice (Peromyscus californicus), a biparental rodent. We stained brain sections with the lectin Wisteria floribunda agglutinin (WFA) to examine WFA‐positive PNN expression in adult virgins exposed to pups one, two, or three times and new parents. Controls were tested with a pup‐sized object. Pup exposure altered WFA+ PNN density in the mPFC and MeA, but not MPOA, of males. Virgin males exposed to pups twice exhibited reduced WFA+ PNN density in the mPFC compared to virgin males exposed to pups only once. Pup‐exposed fathers had lower WFA+ PNN density in the MeA than virgin males exposed to pups once. In contrast, pup exposure did not alter WFA+ PNN density in females. Additionally, WFA PNN density in the mPFC was higher in males than females. PNNs preferentially associated with GABAergic cells in the mPFC of males, whereas in the MeA, PNNs associated with a similar percentage of GABAergic and glutamatergic neurons. Our results suggest that dynamic changes in PNN expression occur with repeated exposure to pups in male California mice and might contribute to the onset of paternal behavior.

Introduction

In mammals, maternal care is obligatory for offspring survival, and the onset of female parenting is associated with structural and functional changes in the mother's brain, mediated by neuroendocrine changes associated with pregnancy, parturition, and lactation (Kinsley and Lambert 2008; Leuner and Sabihi 2016; Medina and Workman 2020). Paternal care is rare in mammals, with fathers providing some form of care for their offspring in only ∼5%–10% of species (Kleiman and Malcolm 1981). Males in these biparental species often exhibit marked changes in their behavioral response to infants as they transition into fatherhood: whereas reproductively naïve males may exhibit avoidance, aggression, or affiliative behavior toward experimentally presented infants, most fathers are attracted to and nurturant toward their own offspring as well as unfamiliar infants (de Jong et al. 2009; Duclot et al. 2022; Saltzman et al. 2017; Song et al. 2010). Similar behavioral transitions have also been observed in some facultatively biparental rodents following mating and cohabitation (vom Saal 1985; Tachikawa et al. 2013; Wu et al. 2014). This change in behavioral responses to pups is accompanied by plasticity in neural circuits that mediate paternal behavior (reviewed in Leuner et al. 2010; Horrell et al. 2021; Wilson et al. 2023). However, the molecular and cellular mechanisms underlying neuroplasticity in the onset of parental behavior in males have not been elucidated.

The California mouse (Peromyscus californicus) is a rodent species that is monogamous and biparental in both the field and the lab (Dudley 1974; Gubernick and Alberts 1987; Gubernick and Teferi 2000; Ribble and Salvioni 1990). Fathers spend as much time as mothers caring for offspring (e.g., huddling, grooming, and retrieving pups) (Gubernick and Alberts 1987; A. W. Lee and Brown 2002). Under experimental conditions, fathers are typically attracted to and will care for both their own offspring and unrelated pups, while adult virgin males may either avoid, attack, or care for unrelated pups (Chauke et al. 2012; de Jong et al. 2009; Gubernick and Addington 1994; Gubernick and Alberts 1987; Horrell et al. 2017). Behavioral sensitization to pups can occur in male California mice, as repeated exposure to pups across several days facilitates the onset of pup‐affiliative behavior in virgin males (Horrell et al. 2017; Shaikh et al. 2024). However, the mechanisms that contribute to behavioral sensitization to pups in adult California mice are not known.

Increasing evidence supports the hypothesis that perineuronal nets (PNNs) regulate neural plasticity. PNNs are pericellular aggregates of extracellular matrix that surround cell bodies and proximal dendrites of neurons in the central nervous system (Celio and Blumcke 1994; Celio et al. 1998). They are highly organized, lattice‐like structures with holes allowing axonal boutons to synapse onto the underlying neurons (Wang and Fawcett 2012). Although PNNs are prevalent throughout the mammalian central nervous system, the majority of studies have focused on cortical PNNs, which preferentially envelop parvalbumin‐positive (PV+), fast‐spiking inhibitory interneurons (Härtig et al. 1992). In contrast, PNNs in subcortical regions seem to surround both excitatory and inhibitory neurons (Carstens et al. 2016; Horii‐Hayashi, Sasagawa, Hashimoto, et al. 2015; Mészár et al. 2012). PNNs have been observed in multiple brain regions in rodents, including regions implicated in motivation, reward, and reinforcement. High levels of PNN expression are present throughout the striatum and limbic system, including the amygdala, hippocampus, hypothalamus, ventral pallidum, and prefrontal cortex (Bertolotto et al. 1996; Carstens et al. 2016; Horii‐Hayashi, Sasagawa, Hashimoto, et al. 2015; Mészár et al. 2012; Seeger et al. 1994; Uriarte et al. 2020). Such high PNN expression across the circuitry mediating reward, reinforcement, and motivation suggests that PNNs may influence neuroplasticity in brain circuits underlying social behaviors, including parental behavior.

PNNs play an important role in the neural plasticity associated with sensitive periods of early development (Carulli et al. 2010; Pizzorusso et al. 2002). In addition, they can be dynamically regulated in adulthood. Manipulations that remove PNNs in the adult central nervous system can reinstate juvenile‐like states of plasticity, promote learning capabilities, and enhance cognitive flexibility (Geissler et al. 2013; Gogolla et al. 2009; Happel et al. 2014; Hirono et al. 2018; Pizzorusso et al. 2002; Romberg et al. 2013). Additionally, studies that have depleted PNNs in the cortex or amygdala have shown that they play a role in the maintenance of both auditory and visual fear memories as well as addiction‐related memories (Banerjee et al. 2017; Thompson et al. 2018; Xue et al. 2014), suggesting PNNs may provide stability to brain networks, which may be essential for the formation and recall of long‐term memories.

Recent studies in rodents have found dynamic changes in PNN expression associated with reproductive behavior. In a house mouse (Mus musculus) model of Rett syndrome, an autism spectrum disorder, female mice with the mutation for Rett syndrome exhibit impaired pup‐gathering behavior and abnormally elevated PNN expression in the auditory cortex following maternal experience (Krishnan et al. 2017). This dysregulation appears to prematurely curtail a transient window of plasticity normally engaged during adult learning. Degradation of PNNs with the enzyme chondroitinase ABC (ChABC) corrects this impairment, suggesting that excessive PNN formation interferes with experience‐dependent plasticity necessary for maternal behavior (Krishnan et al. 2017). Additionally, PNN density in female rats (Rattus norvegicus) changes across pregnancy in the medial preoptic area (MPOA), a key brain region implicated in parental behavior. Although virgin females do not exhibit any changes in PNN expression in the MPOA across the estrous cycle, PNN expression in this region increases during gestation, peaks shortly before parturition, and subsequently fades during the lactation period (Uriarte et al. 2020). Furthermore, simulating the hormonal changes of pregnancy in ovariectomized virgin female rats via systemically administering estrogen and progesterone induces PNN expression in the MPOA (Uriarte et al. 2020). Lau et al. (2020) found that PNN density was altered in primary somatosensory cortex following experience with pups in virgin female mice, in a region‐ and hemisphere‐specific manner. More recently, Carta (2023) demonstrated that degrading PNNs in the perifornical area of the hypothalamus using ChABC disrupted both caregiving and infanticidal behavior in virgin male mice, suggesting that PNN integrity in this region is critical for the expression of infant‐directed behaviors. Together, these findings suggest that interactions with pups and/or hormonal changes experienced by new mothers may facilitate neural plasticity through modulation of PNNs. Whether or not PNNs contribute to plasticity of the parental brain during the transition to parenthood in a biparental species has not been studied (Horrell et al. 2021).

Given the role of PNNs in plasticity related to experience‐dependent learning, it is possible that they may regulate plasticity resulting from experience with pups in males of a biparental species. In the present study, therefore, we investigated the effects of experience with pups on PNN expression in virgin male and female California mice. We predicted that PNN density in brain regions that play a key role in parental behavior (MPOA, medial amygdala [MeA], and medial prefrontal cortex [mPFC]; reviewed in Horrell et al. 2019; Numan 2020) would decrease during initial exposure to pups, when pup‐directed behavior is highly variable, thereby facilitating synaptic reorganization and behavioral modifications. We also examined whether PNNs preferentially associate with GABAergic or glutamatergic cells in the cortex (mPFC) and subcortically (MeA) in the California mouse to determine if cell‐type–specific associations differ from those previously described in rats and house mice.

Methods

Animals

California mice aged 161–226 days (i.e., approximately 5.5–7.5 months) were used in this study (n = 48 males and 48 females). Mice were bred at the University of California, Riverside (UCR) and housed and maintained as described previously (Shaikh et al. 2024). The breeders were descendants of mice from the Peromyscus Genetic Stock Center (University of South Carolina, Columbia, SC, USA, RRID:NCBITaxon_42520). Mice were housed in 44 × 24 × 20 cm polycarbonate cages with aspen shavings for bedding and cotton wool for nesting material and with Purina Rodent Chow 5001 (LabDiet, Richmond, IN, USA) and water available ad libitum. Humidity was approximately 60%–70%, temperature was maintained at 21°C ± 1°C, and lights were on a 14:10 light: dark cycle (lights on from 11:00 p.m. to 1:00 p.m.).

Juveniles were weaned from their parents at 27–31 days of age and housed in same‐sex groups of 3–4 age‐matched individuals in a colony room containing only virgin mice, to prevent premature exposure to stimuli from pups. At least 2 weeks before testing, virgins were pair‐housed with an unrelated, age‐matched same‐sex mouse from their original same‐sex group, and pairs were randomly assigned to experimental conditions.

All experimental procedures were approved by UCR's Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee and conform to the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. UCR is fully accredited by the Association for Assessment and Accreditation of Laboratory Animal Care (AAALAC).

Experimental Design

Each virgin mouse was assigned to one of six experimental groups and underwent data collection over a 1‐ to 5‐day period using a design modified from Horrell et al. (2017) (Figure 1A). Groups differed in the stimulus with which they were tested (unrelated pup or pup‐sized pebble) and the number of exposures to the stimulus (1, 2, or 3). Within each pair of same‐sex virgins, both mice experienced the same number of exposures, but one received the pup and the other the object.

Schematic of the experimental design. (A) Virgin mice were exposed one, two, or three times to either an unrelated pup or a novel pup‐sized pebble in a clean cage. Two hours after the start of the final stimulus presentation, mice were euthanized and perfused transcardially, and their brains were removed. Not pictured is a group of first‐time parents that were tested once with a novel object or unrelated pup as a positive control. N = 6 males and 6 females per group. (B) Schematic representation of the brain regions (blue shaded areas) in which the density of WFA+ cells was measured. These shaded areas represent the general anatomical locations of the brain regions analyzed in this study. Brain schematics were drawn with the aid of the Scalable Brain Atlas (Bakker et al. 2015), and regions were manually outlined according to Paxinos and Franklin 2012). The coordinates represent the distance relative to Bregma of the rostral face of each section. IL = infralimbic medial prefrontal cortex, MeA = medial amygdala, MPOA = medial preoptic area.

All mice underwent a final 60‐min test with the assigned stimulus, after which their brains were collected. Mice in the Pup‐1 and Object‐1 groups were tested only once, with this final 60‐min stimulus presentation. Mice in the Pup‐2 and Object‐2 groups received one 20‐min exposure 2 days before the 60‐min test (i.e., exposure on Day 1 and final test on Day 3; Figure 1A). Mice in the Pup‐3 and Object‐3 groups received two 20‐min exposures, occurring 2 and 4 days before the 60‐min test (i.e., exposures on Days 1 and 3, final test on Day 5; Figure 1A).

We used first‐time fathers and mothers, housed as breeding pairs with their first litter of pups, as positive controls. Five to 7 days after the birth of their first litter, breeders were tested once for 60 min with an unrelated pup (Breeder‐Pup) or novel object (Breeder‐Object). First‐time breeders were included as a positive control group to assess PNN expression in animals that were actively engaged in parental care. The timing of testing (5–7 days postpartum) was consistent with prior work in our lab examining pup sensitization in this species (Horrell et al. 2017). Although breeders had more cumulative pup experience than virgins, their inclusion was intended to contextualize plasticity and provide a neurobiological benchmark for established parental behavior. For all experimental groups, mice were perfused and their brains collected two hours after the beginning of the 60‐min test with a pup or novel object (see below).

Pup and Novel‐Object Exposures and Tests

For each 20‐min stimulus exposure and 60‐min test, virgin same‐sex cage mates or first‐time parents were removed from their home cage between 8:00 a.m. and 10:15 a.m. and placed individually in clean cages, identical to the home cages, containing aspen shavings, food, and water. Animals were tested in new cages to allow testing of both cage mates around the same time under identical conditions. Each cage was placed on an isolated shelf in a colony room housing only virgin mice. After a 10‐min habituation period, an unfamiliar, unrelated, 2‐ to 5‐day‐old pup or a pup‐sized pebble was introduced into the corner of the cage farthest from the subject for 20 (stimulus exposure) or 60 (stimulus test) minutes. If a subject attacked a pup, the exposure or test was immediately concluded and the pup was euthanized with pentobarbital (Fatal‐Plus solution, Vortech Pharmaceuticals, Dearborn, MI, USA). The pup‐aggressive mouse then remained alone in the test cage and was either returned to its home cage 20 min after the start of the pup presentation (pup exposures) or perfused 2 h after the start of pup presentation (pup tests). Some stimulus pups were used for multiple tests but were used in no more than one test per day; subjects were never tested with the same pup more than once.

To examine the relationship between density of PNNs and pup‐directed behavior, all 60‐min pup tests were videotaped and subsequently scored by a single observer blind to the experimental condition using Behavioral Observation Research Interactive Software (BORIS, Friard and Gamba 2016, RRID:SCR_025700). The parameters scored were latency to approach the pup, latency to initiate parental behavior (grooming and/or huddling the pup), total time spent sniffing the pup, and total time spent in parental behavior. Pup retrieval was not scored or analyzed because pup‐carrying behavior is not common in this species (Harris et al. 2011). For novel‐object tests, behaviors scored from videotapes were latency to approach the object and total time spent sniffing the object (Acosta et al. 2022; Nguyen et al. 2020). Behavioral data are not available for the Pup‐3 group.

Parental behavior was defined as pup‐directed licking/grooming and/or huddling, consistent with established ethograms for California mice (Gubernick and Alberts 1987; Gubernick and Nelson 1989; Gubernick et al. 1994; Gubernick and Laskin 1994). Huddling was scored when the subject maintained close physical contact while crouched over the pup, typically covering it with the torso. Sniffing pup was scored separately and was not included in the parental behavior category, as prior studies have shown it does not reliably distinguish parental from nonparental animals in this species (de Jong et al. 2009; Horrell et al. 2017). Virgin mice that attacked or avoided the pup did not engage in any licking/grooming or huddling during the 60‐min test.

Histology

Two hours after the beginning of the 60‐min test with a pup or novel object (i.e., between 10:00 a.m. and 12:00 p.m., at least 1 h before lights‐off), mice were anesthetized with sodium pentobarbital (Fatal‐Plus solution, Vortech Pharmaceuticals, Dearborn, MI, USA) and perfused intracardially with cold phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS, 0.1 M, pH 7.4) and 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA). PNN remodeling has been detected as soon as 90 min following the onset of stimulus exposure in prior studies (Slaker et al. 2015), so we selected 120 min as an appropriate time point to potentially capture both acute and sustained remodeling effects. Brains were removed and stored overnight in 4% PFA at 4°C and then immersed in a 30% sucrose solution for 24–48 h. The brains were stored in a freezing solution (30% ethylene glycol, 1% polyvinylpyrrolidone‐40, 30% sucrose in 0.1 M PBS) at −20°C until further processing. Brains were sectioned coronally at 40 µm thickness using a cryostat (Leica Biosystems, Deer Park, IL, USA; CM 1860). For each brain, an average of 8–10 slices of each region of interest was obtained for histochemistry and stored at 4°C in 0.1 M PBS with 0.05% sodium azide. For each animal, three to four slices containing the infralimbic mPFC (+1.73 mm from bregma), MPOA (+0.03 mm from bregma), and MeA (−1.67 mm from bregma), respectively, were processed for each batch of immunohistochemistry (Figure 1B). Brain regions selected for quantification were identified by cross‐referencing a standard mouse brain atlas (Paxinos and Franklin 2012) and images of Nissl‐stained California mouse sections (http://brainmaps.org). Within each sex, each of the eight exposure × stimulus treatment groups (Pup‐1, Pup‐2, Pup 3, Object‐1, Object‐2, Object‐3, Breeder‐Pup, and Breeder‐Object) was represented in every batch of histology.

To visualize PNNs, we used fluorescent lectin staining procedures with fluorescein‐labeled Wisteria floribunda agglutinin (WFA; 1:500; Vector Laboratories, Burlingame, CA, USA, Cat# FL1351, RRID:AB_2336875), following procedures adapted from Reinhard et al. (2019). WFA binds to terminal N‐acetylgalactosamine residues on chondroitin sulfate chains, particularly those associated with aggrecan in PNNs, and is widely used as a histological marker for PNNs in the mammalian brain (Härtig et al. 1992; Nadanaka et al. 2020; Härtig et al. 2022). Therefore, we use the term “PNNs” specifically to refer to WFA‐positive (WFA+) PNNs. Free‐floating sections were washed in 0.1 M PBS (3 × 5 min), quenched in 50 mM ammonium chloride in 0.1 M PBS for 15 min, washed in 0.1 M PBS (3 × 5 min), incubated with WFA overnight at 4°C, washed in 0.1 M PBS (3 × 5 min), mounted onto slides, and coverslipped using EMS Shield Mount with DABCO mounting medium (Electron Microscopy Sciences, Hatfield, PA, USA).

To determine whether PNNs preferentially associate with GABAergic or glutamatergic cells in the cortex (mPFC) and subcortically (MeA), we performed colocalization studies with the GABAergic neuron marker glutamic acid decarboxylase 67 (GAD67) and the glutamatergic neuron marker Ca2+/calmodulin kinase II alpha (CaMKII‐α). The GAD67 and CaMKII‐α primary antibodies used in this study have been validated for use in mouse brain tissue and, in this study, showed expected cellular localization patterns consistent with previous studies (Sabbagh et al. 2018; Ma et al. 2021; Green et al. 2020; Hammack et al. 2023). Additionally, the GAD67 antibody (MAB5406) has also been used successfully in the Syrian hamster (Mesocricetus auratus), producing strong and widespread labeling consistent with known GABAergic distributions (Bonet‐Fernández et al. 2023). To our knowledge, no studies have used the CaMKII‐α antibody (ADI‐KAM‐CA002) in other nontraditional lab rodents; however, in our research, both antibodies revealed labeling patterns consistent with expected neuroanatomical distributions, supporting their specificity in the California mouse brain. We did not perform colocalization studies in the MPOA because we did not observe group differences in PNN expression in this region (see Section 3) and because PNN density was low in the MPOA.

For colocalization of WFA and CaMKII‐α, free‐floating 40 µm sections were washed in 0.1 M PBS (3 × 10 min), quenched in 50 mM ammonium chloride for 15 min, and washed again in 0.1 M PBS (3 × 10 min). The slices were incubated in a blocking solution with 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA, Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and 0.1% Triton X‐100 (Sigma‐Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) for 6 h at room temperature and then incubated overnight at 4°C with the primary mouse antibody against CaMKII‐α (1:1500; Enzo Life Sciences, Farmingdale, NY, USA, Cat# ADI‐KAM‐CA002, RRID:AB_10617228) in the blocking solution. The day following incubation with primary antibody, sections were washed in 0.1 M PBS (4 × 10 min) and incubated with goat anti‐mouse Alexa Fluor 555 secondary antibody (1:500; Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA, Cat# A28180, RRID:AB_2536164) in blocking solution for 2 h at room temperature. Sections were then washed in 0.1 M PBS (4 × 10 min), incubated with WFA (1:500) overnight at 4°C, washed in 0.1 M PBS (3 × 10 min), mounted onto slides, and coverslipped.

We processed another subset of mPFC and MeA slices for WFA staining and GAD67 immunoreactivity. Free‐floating sections were washed in 0.1 M PBS (3 × 10 min), quenched in 50 mM ammonium chloride for 15 min, washed in PBS (3 × 10 min), and incubated in a blocking solution (3% BSA, 5% normal goat serum, and 0.1% Triton X‐100) overnight at 4°C. The next day, tissue samples were rinsed with 0.1 M PBS (1 × 5 min) and then incubated with mouse anti‐GAD67 primary antibody (1:750; MilliporeSigma, Burlington, MA, USA, Cat# MAB5406, RRID:AB_2278725) in blocking solution for 48 h at 4°C. After primary incubation, sections were washed in 0.1 M PBS (4 × 10 min) and then allowed to incubate with goat anti‐mouse Alexa Fluor 555 secondary antibody (1:500; Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA, Cat# A28180, RRID:AB_2536164) in blocking solution for 4 h at room temperature. Next, sections were washed with 0.1 M PBS (4 × 10 min) and incubated with WFA (1:500) overnight at 4°C. On the final day, sections were washed in 0.1 M PBS (3 × 10 min), incubated with the nuclear stain NucBlue (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA, Cat# R37606) for 5 min at room temperature, washed three times in 0.1 M PBS, mounted onto slides, and coverslipped.

Imaging and Quantification

An inverted Zeiss LSM 880 confocal microscope (Carl Zeiss Microscopy, White Plains, NY, USA) equipped with Airyscan Fast was used for imaging. Imaging of WFA+ PNNs in the mPFC, MeA, and MPOA was performed at 10×, and co‐expression of WFA around GAD67+ or CaMKII‐α+ neurons in the mPFC and MeA was performed at 20×. Imaged z‐stacks covering 30 µm (1 µm step size) were acquired. Imaging parameters (exposure, laser power, and gain) were held constant across samples and not auto‐adjusted. Each z‐stack was imported into ImageJ software (NIH), collapsed into a single image by projection, and then converted to a TIFF file.

For each 10× image, a standardized region of interest (ROI) of fixed dimensions was cropped from the same anatomical subregion across all animals (mPFC: 800 × 1000 µm; MeA: 600 × 1200 µm; MPOA: 650 × 1200 µm). ROIs were bilaterally positioned based on consistent anatomical landmarks to ensure that an equivalent area was quantified across slices and animals. For 20× colocalization images in the mPFC and MeA, a smaller standardized ROI was cropped from the same anatomical subregions across animals (mPFC: 300 × 300 µm; MeA: 300 × 300 µm). These ROIs were used to assess WFA co‐expression with GAD67+ or CaMKII‐α+ neurons.

These images were then imported into Pipsqueak Pro (https://rewire.ai/pipsqueak‐pro, RRID:SCR_022148) software from Rewire AI (Portland, OR, USA) to identify and quantify the number of PNN‐labeled cells, PNN/GAD67 colocalization, and PNN/CAMKII‐α colocalization. A consistent background threshold was set and applied to all images for comparison. Pipsqueak was run in semi‐automatic mode to select ROIs to identify PNNs and individual GAD67+ or CAMKII‐α+ cells, which were then verified by a trained scorer who was blinded to the condition during cell quantification. Stained cells were counted bilaterally for each brain region. Data for each region for each animal were averaged from three to four sections, and these averages were compared across groups.

Statistical Analyses

Data were tested for assumptions of normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test and for homogeneity of variance using Levene's test. WFA+ PNN density data were normally distributed; thus, they were compared among groups within each sex and stimulus treatment (pup, novel object) by one‐way ANOVAs, with Bonferroni multiple‐comparison tests performed following significant (p < 0.05) ANOVAs. This approach was used to test our primary hypothesis that exposure to pups modulates PNN expression within each sex. Two‐way ANOVAs were performed to examine sex differences in WFA+ PNN density in pup‐ and object‐exposed mice, with sex and stimulus or sex and exposure as between‐subject factors. These analyses were conducted post hoc to explore potential sex differences, which were not part of our original predictions but became relevant given the full dataset. Data are presented as mean ± SE.

Correlations between PNN expression and behavior across stimulus exposure conditions were analyzed separately within each stimulus condition. Using the Shapiro–Wilk test, we determined that the behavioral data did not follow a normal distribution, so Spearman's correlation coefficient was used. All data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism version 10.0.0 for Windows (San Diego, CA, USA) with alpha set at 0.05 (two‐tailed).

A total of three virgin males (one each in the Pup‐1, Pup‐2, and Pup‐3 conditions) exhibited aggressive behavior toward foster pups. The male in the Pup‐2 condition attacked during his 20‐min pup exposure but not during his 60‐min pup test. The male in the Pup‐3 condition attacked during each of his two 20‐min pup exposures as well as during the 60‐min test. Histological and behavioral data from males that were aggressive during pup tests were included in the analyses.

Results

Pup Exposure Altered PNN Density in the mPFC and MeA of Male, but Not Female, California Mice

To investigate the influence of pup exposure on PNN expression, we analyzed WFA+ PNN density in the mPFC, MeA, and MPOA of male and female mice with different amounts of pup experience (Pup‐1, Pup‐2, Pup‐3, and Breeders).

Males

In the infralimbic (IL) mPFC of pup‐exposed males, a one‐way ANOVA revealed a statistically significant difference in PNN expression between exposure groups (F [3, 20] = 3.12, p = 0.049, η 2 = 0.32). Post hoc analyses indicated that WFA+ PNN density was significantly lower in the IL mPFC of virgin males that were exposed to pups twice (Pup‐2) than virgin males exposed to pups only once (Pup‐1; Figure 2A,B). PNN expression in the IL mPFC did not differ significantly between any other groups of males (p > 0.05).

Post hoc analyses indicated that WFA+ PNN density in the MeA was significantly lower in first‐time fathers than in virgin males exposed to pups only once (Figure 3A,B). Females

In females, one‐way ANOVAs found no differences between pup‐exposure groups for WFA+ PNN density in the IL mPFC, MeA, or MPOA (all p > 0.11; Figures 2A,C, 3A,C, and 4A,C). Thus, pup exposure changes PNN density in the IL mPFC and MeA of male but not female California mice.

Novel‐Object Exposure Did Not Alter PNN Density in the mPFC, MeA, or MPOA of Male or Female Mice

To control for novelty, we also examined the effects of repeated exposure to a novel object (pup‐sized pebble) on WFA+ PNN density in the mPFC, MeA, and MPOA of male and female California mice (Object‐1, Object‐2, Object‐3, and Breeders). Neither males nor females showed any significant differences in WFA+ PNN density between object‐exposure groups in the mPFC, MeA, or MPOA (all p > 0.38; Figure 5).

In both sexes, PNN expression did not differ among object‐exposure groups in the mPFC (A, B), MeA (C, D), or MPOA (E, F) (n = 6 per group).

Sex Differences in PNN Density Were Observed in the mPFC, but Not MeA or MPOA, of Pup‐ and Object‐Exposed Mice

Next, we examined possible sex differences in WFA+ PNN expression using two‐way (sex × stimulus) ANOVAs. In the IL mPFC, we found a significant main effect of sex (F [1, 90] = 26.42, p ≤ 0.0001, η 2 = 0.22), where males had significantly higher PNN expression than females (p < 0.05; Figure 6A,B). PNN density in the IL mPFC did not show a significant main effect of stimulus or a sex × stimulus interaction. PNN density in the MeA and MPOA did not show any significant main effects of sex or stimulus or a sex × stimulus interaction (Figure 6C,D).

To determine whether sex differences in the IL mPFC were consistent across exposure conditions, two‐way ANOVAs were conducted separately for pup‐ and object‐exposed mice, with sex and exposure as between‐subject factors. Both pup‐ and object‐exposed mice showed a significant main effect of sex in the IL mPFC (pup‐exposed: F [1, 40] = 8.07, p = 0.007, η 2 = 0.14; object‐exposed: F [1, 38] = 18.84, p = 0.001, η 2 = 0.31), with males having higher WFA+ PNN density than females. PNN expression in the IL mPFC did not show a significant main effect of exposure or a sex × exposure interaction. Overall, these results suggest that stimulus‐exposed male mice exhibit higher WFA+ PNN density in the IL mPFC compared to females.

Pup‐Directed Behavior Correlated With PNN Expression in the mPFC and MeA of Virgin Males

Because experience with pups influenced PNN expression in male mice, we examined whether PNN expression correlated with pup‐directed behavior in males, analyzing mice from each exposure condition separately. In virgin males exposed to a pup once, WFA+ PNN density in the mPFC correlated negatively with time spent sniffing the pup (r [6] = −0.88, p = 0.044; Figure 7A) and positively with latency to approach the pup (r [6] = 0.84, p = 0.044; Figure 7B). In the same group of males, we found a significant negative correlation between WFA+ PNN density in the MeA and latency to approach the pup (r [6] = −0.87, p = 0.033; Figure 7C). No significant correlations were found between PNN expression in the MPOA and behavior in males exposed to a pup once (p > 0.05). Likewise, no significant correlations were observed between PNN expression in any region of interest (mPFC, MeA, or MPOA) and behavior in Pup‐2 or Breeder males (p > 0.05). We also evaluated relationships between object‐directed behavior and PNN expression in males. No significant correlations between PNN expression and behavior were found in the mPFC, MeA, or MPOA of object‐exposed males in any exposure condition (p > 0.05).

PNNs in the mPFC, but Not MeA, Primarily Colocalized With GAD67 in California Mice

To gain insight into how circuit function may be modified by PNN plasticity following pup exposure, we determined the cell‐type specificity of PNNs in the mPFC and MeA of adult male California mice, by measuring colocalization of WFA+ PNNs with the GABAergic neuronal marker GAD67 and the glutamatergic neuronal marker CAMKII‐α. In the IL subregion of the mPFC, approximately 38% of GAD67+ neurons, but fewer than 1% of CAMKII‐α+ neurons, were enveloped by WFA+ PNNs (Figure 8A,B). Moreover, approximately 69% of WFA+ PNNs in the mPFC colocalized with GAD67, whereas only around 6% of WFA+ PNNs covered CAMKII‐α+ glutamatergic neurons (Figure 8C).

(A) Close‐up images (20×) showing the colocalization of WFA+ and GAD67+ (left) or CAMKII‐α+ (right) cells in the infralimbic medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC, top) and medial amygdala (MeA, bottom) (n = 19). Scale bar = 20 µm. (B) Percentage (mean ± SEM) of GAD67+ and CAMKII‐α+ neurons enveloped by WFA+ PNNs. C. Percentage (mean ± SEM) of WFA+ PNNs that contained GAD67 or CAMKII‐α in the mPFC and MeA.

In the MeA, about 9% of GAD67+ cells and approximately 6% of CAMKII‐α+ cells were surrounded by WFA+ PNNs (Figure 8A,B). Similarly, the percentage of WFA+ PNNs containing GAD67 was about 23%, while ∼28% of WFA+ PNNs covered CAMKII‐α+ cells (Figure 8C). Together, these data indicate that the majority of neurons surrounded by WFA+ PNNs in the mPFC are GABAergic, while WFA+ PNNs in the MeA do not preferentially colocalize with either GABAergic or glutamatergic cell markers.

Discussion

Repeated exposure to pups across several days facilitates the onset of pup‐affiliative behavior in virgin male California mice (Horrell et al. 2017; Shaikh et al. 2024). This form of experience‐dependent behavioral plasticity, often referred to as behavioral sensitization, has been observed across several rodent species, including in virgin males (Alsina‐Llánes et al. 2025; Bridges et al. 1972; Rosenblatt 1967; Swanson and Campbell 1979) and females (Alsina‐Llánes et al. 2015; Fleming and Rosenblatt 1974; Lonstein et al. 1999; Pedersen et al. 2006; Rosenblatt 1967; Swanson and Campbell 1979). However, the mechanisms underlying the neural plasticity resulting from experience with infants are not well understood. Here, we examined the effects of experience with pups on expression of PNNs, extracellular structures that regulate synaptic plasticity, in male and female California mice. We found that the effects differed depending on brain region and sex. Compared to virgin males that were exposed to pups only once, virgin males exposed to pups twice had lower WFA+ PNN density in the mPFC, whereas first‐time fathers had lower WFA+ PNN density in the MeA. Experience with pups did not alter PNN expression in the MPOA of males, nor did it influence PNN expression in the mPFC, MeA, or MPOA of adult female California mice. Repeated exposure to a pup‐sized object did not alter PNN expression in the mPFC, MeA, or MPOA of males or females, suggesting the effects were not due to novelty. Finally, we found that PNNs in the mPFC were associated mostly with GABAergic neurons, as observed in other species, whereas PNNs in the MeA were associated with both GAD+ and CAMKII‐α + cells.

The present study is the first to show that WFA+ PNN density in the mPFC is dynamically altered with pup experience in virgin males of any species. The mPFC plays a vital role in higher executive functioning and contributes to working memory, behavioral flexibility, response inhibition, attentional processes, emotional regulation, and the modulation of social behavior (Dalley et al. 2004; Etkin et al. 2011; Kim et al. 2015; Kingsbury et al. 2019; E. Lee et al. 2016; Wirt and Hyman 2017). The mPFC has three primary subregions—the anterior cingulate cortex, the prelimbic cortex (PL), and the infralimbic cortex (IL)—and increasing evidence suggests the different subregions have specialized roles in social behavior. Notably, the PL and IL target the amygdala to differentially influence social behavior, as activation of PL projections to the basolateral amygdala (BLA) impaired social interaction in house mice, while inhibition of IL‐BLA projections impaired social interaction (Huang et al. 2020). Moreover, Huang et al. (2020) found that IL neurons were preferentially activated following social interaction compared to PL neurons, indicating that the IL mPFC, through its connections to other brain regions involved in social interaction and emotional response, may regulate social behavior.

Previous studies support a role for the IL mPFC in rodent parental motivation (Pereira and Morrell 2011, 2020); however, the changes that may occur in this region during the transition from pup‐avoidant to pup‐affiliative behavior have not been examined. Our finding that virgin males exposed to pups twice had lower WFA+ PNN density in the IL mPFC than those exposed only once might indicate that PNN reduction in the mPFC may facilitate neural plasticity underlying the transition from pup avoidance to pup affiliation. Interestingly, first‐time fathers did not differ from virgins in mPFC PNN expression despite having several days of pup experience. As noted in Section 2, breeders were included as a positive control to anchor our findings in animals with established parental behavior, rather than as an exposure‐matched comparison to virgins. Their similarity to virgins suggests that extended pup contact may not produce a simple linear reduction in PNNs, but instead points to stage‐dependent or region‐specific remodeling of plasticity. Furthermore, WFA+ PNN density in the mPFC was positively correlated with the latency to approach the pup and negatively correlated with the total time spent sniffing the pup in virgin males exposed to pups for the first time. Notably, these pup‐directed behaviors reflect social investigation rather than serving as specific indicators of parental care, and they may occur in a range of motivational contexts, including avoidance, affiliation, or aggression. It is possible that PNNs in this region initially stabilize social motivation circuits related to pup avoidance and then are dynamically reorganized with pup experience to stabilize circuits influencing pup‐approach behavior. Future studies utilizing chondroitinase ABC would allow us to degrade PNNs in the mPFC during the sensitization process to test this hypothesis.

In other rodents, PNNs in primary sensory areas preferentially surround fast‐spiking parvalbumin (PV)‐containing GABAergic interneurons (McRae et al. 2007). PV‐positive (+) interneurons play an essential role in sensory learning during development and adulthood (Lupori et al. 2023; Rupert and Shea 2022). Chemogenetic reduction of PV+ interneuron activity reinstated heightened plasticity in adult sensory cortices, and activity of PV+ interneurons can influence PNN formation (Cisneros‐Franco and de Villers‐Sidani 2019; Devienne et al. 2021). Additionally, plasticity of PV+ interneurons in the hippocampus is important for learning and memory processes in adulthood, implicating PV+ interneurons in experience‐dependent plasticity (Donato et al. 2013, Donato et al. 2015). Consistent with studies in house mice (Ueno et al. 2017), we found that in male California mice, most WFA+ PNNs in the mPFC surrounded GABAergic cells. Ueno et al. (2017) found that in the IL mPFC of adult male mice, the majority of WFA+ PNNs were located in Layers 5/6, and about 60% of WFA+ PNNs in Layers 5/6 of the IL surrounded GABAergic neurons. Notably, approximately 40% of WFA+ PNNs in Layers 5/6 of the IL mPFC enveloped PV+ neurons (Ueno et al. 2017). Given that PV+ interneurons are important for experience‐dependent plasticity and that PNNs surround PV+ cells in the mPFC (Ueno et al. 2017), it is possible that reduction of PNN density after pup exposure results in reduced inhibitory neurotransmission (Balmer 2016; Lensjø et al. 2017) and facilitates cortical disinhibition to influence learning and memory.

Results of the current study suggest that males exposed to a pup twice may have had a reduction in inhibitory tone in the IL mPFC compared to males exposed to pups only once, indicating that projection neurons in this region might have been disinhibited after the second pup exposure. In house mice, elevated activity of subsets of mPFC projection neurons occurs during social behavior (Kim et al. 2015; E. Lee et al. 2016; Murugan et al. 2017). Notably, distinct ensembles of excitatory neurons in the mPFC exhibit diverse responses during social exploration, as some excitatory populations increase their activity, while others show decreased activation, suggesting functional specialization among mPFC projection neurons (Liang et al. 2018). Therefore, dynamic reorganization of PNNs in the mPFC might facilitate pup sensitization by reducing GABAergic interneuron feed‐forward inhibition of long‐range excitatory projections that influence social behavior. An important area of future research would be to examine the contribution of different interneuron cell types to pup‐experience‐dependent plasticity. For example, parvalbumin‐, somatostatin‐, or calretinin‐expressing neurons may differentially contribute to pup experience‐dependent changes in PNNs, and future studies using broader marker panels could help determine which specific neuronal phenotypes are preferentially associated with PNN remodeling in this context.

Complementary findings in virgin male house mice suggest that PNNs help stabilize distinct pup‐directed behavioral phenotypes. Carta (2023) demonstrated that enzymatic degradation of PNNs in the perifornical area of virgin male mice disrupted both aggressive and caregiving responses to pups, leading to behavioral neglect. This dissertation provides causal evidence that PNNs in hypothalamic circuits contribute to stable infant‐directed behavior and aligns with our interpretation that PNNs may serve to stabilize behaviorally relevant circuits in a context‐dependent manner. While Carta focused on subcortical structures, our findings raise the possibility that PNNs in the mPFC may undergo similar dynamic reorganization following pup exposure, initially stabilizing aggression‐related circuits and later facilitating circuits involved in caregiving. Together, these data suggest that PNN plasticity may play a broader role in mediating experience‐dependent behavioral transitions in both cortical and subcortical regions.

The MeA is implicated in the regulation of parental behavior in male rodents (Chen et al. 2019; Kirkpatrick, Carter, et al. 1994; Kirkpatrick, Kim, et al. 1994). We found that first‐time fathers displayed reduced density of WFA+ PNNs in the MeA compared to virgin males exposed to pups for the first time. Interestingly, WFA+ PNN density in the MeA was negatively correlated with latency to approach the pup in virgin males exposed to pups for the first time. While PNNs in the BLA appear to play a role in the maintenance of fear‐ and addiction‐related memories in rodents (Gogolla et al. 2009; Xue et al. 2014), a role of amygdalar PNNs in the modulation of social interactions has not been established; however, a recent study found that PNN removal in the posterior dorsal division of the MeA (MeApd) did not significantly affect aggressive behavior in adult male mice (Ciccarelli et al. 2021). The MeApd influences pup‐directed behavior in a complex way, as affiliative and aggressive responses to pups in male house mice are regulated by GABAergic cells in an activity‐dependent manner; low‐intensity optogenetic activation of GABAergic neurons promoted pup‐affiliative behavior in virgin males, while high‐intensity activation of the same neurons promoted aggressive responses toward pups (Chen et al. 2019). In the present study, we found that while PNNs did not preferentially surround GABAergic neurons in the MeA of male California mice, a subset of MeA neurons surrounded by PNNs were GABAergic. Therefore, it is possible that PNNs regulate the activity of MeA GABAergic cells involved in pup‐directed behavior. Future immunohistochemical studies examining colocalization of PNN‐surrounded GABAergic neurons with a marker of neural activation, such as c‐Fos, within the MeA would allow us to determine if GABAergic MeA neurons surrounded by PNNs are preferentially activated following pup exposure.

We found that PNNs surrounded a subpopulation of glutamatergic cells in the MeA of male California mice. Previous studies in house mice found that glutamatergic neurons in the MeApd promote self‐maintenance behavior (self‐grooming) but not social behavior (e.g., aggressive, sexual, and parental) in adult male mice (Chen et al. 2019; Hong et al. 2014). Thus, the PNNs around the glutamatergic cells in the MeA might influence the plasticity of the asocial circuit for self‐maintenance behavior. However, the functional role of distinct neuronal subpopulations in the MeA in the control of behavior has not been explored in California mice; therefore, while glutamatergic cells in the MeA have been shown to regulate self‐maintenance behavior in other rodents, we cannot exclude the possibility that they may also play a role in parental behavior in this species.

In contrast to the mPFC and MeA, we found no effect of pup experience on WFA+ PNN density in the MPOA of male California mice. Overall, we found very low levels of PNN expression in the MPOA of pup‐exposed males (as well as all other groups in our study). These results are consistent with studies that have detected sparse WFA staining in the MPOA of male house mice (Horii‐Hayashi, Sasagawa, Matsunaga, et al. 2015; Horii‐Hayashi et al. 2017) and male rats (Uriarte et al. 2020). Thus, PNNs might not be involved in regulating structural plasticity in response to experience with pups in the MPOA.

Previous studies have found changes in PNN expression during pregnancy in rat dams and in response to interactions with pups in virgin female house mice (Lau et al. 2020; Uriarte et al. 2020). In contrast, we found no effect of pup exposure on WFA+ PNN density in the IL mPFC, MeA, or MPOA of female California mice. Notably, the effect of pup exposure on parental behavior (i.e., sensitization) has not been examined in females of this species. While some strains of inbred female house mice exhibit spontaneous parental behavior upon experimental exposure to unrelated pups (Gandelman 1973; Mann et al. 1983; Tsuneoka et al. 2013; Wu et al. 2014), most adult virgin female rodents do not exhibit parental behavior upon first exposure to foster pups of the same species and may instead avoid or attack them (Jakubowski and Terkel 1982; McCarthy and Vom Saal 1985; Rosenblatt 1967). In Mongolian gerbils (Meriones unguiculatus), virgin females are more likely than males to attack unfamiliar pups (Elwood 1980; Saltzman et al. 2009). Likewise, in prairie voles (Microtus ochrogaster), most adult females exhibit avoidant or infanticidal reactions when tested with unrelated pups, whereas the majority of adult virgin males show parental behavior when tested under the same conditions (Lonstein and De Vries 1999, 2001). Moreover, a previous study in our lab found that adult virgin female California mice are less motivated to engage in parental behavior toward experimentally presented pups compared to adult virgin males (Nguyen et al. 2020). Therefore, it is possible that virgin female California mice are more resistant to pup‐stimulated parental behavior and may require a greater number or longer duration of pup exposures to become parentally responsive. Thus, dynamic reorganization of PNNs across the sensitization period might occur in females, but only following a greater amount of pup experience than males.

In our study, males had significantly higher WFA+ PNN density in the IL mPFC than females. This pattern was observed in both pup‐exposed and object‐exposed mice, indicating that the sex difference was not specific to the pup‐sensitization paradigm. In contrast, no sex differences were observed in total PNN density in the mPFC of adult rats and mice (Gildawie et al. 2020, 2021; Page and Coutellier 2018). Interestingly, a study in lab rats reported that females exhibited lower PNN density in the mPFC at the onset of puberty compared to postpubertal females, while similar changes were not observed in males, suggesting a potential relationship between estrogen signaling and PNN expression in the mPFC (Drzewiecki et al. 2020). Gonadal steroid receptors are expressed in the prefrontal cortex of rodents, and gonadal hormones can influence synapse formation and neurotransmission in this region (Feng et al. 2010; Hajszan et al. 2007; Mitra et al. 2003; Montague et al. 2008; Nuñez et al. 2003; Yousuf et al. 2019; M. Zhang, Weiland, et al. 2021). Therefore, the observed sex differences in WFA+ PNN density in the mPFC may be related to differences in circulating hormones and central hormone signaling between males and females; however, we did not explore the relationship between gonadal steroids and PNN expression across our behavioral sensitization paradigm. Further studies characterizing colocalization of WFA+ PNNs with gonadal steroid receptors in the mPFC, along with experimental manipulation of steroid levels to assess effects on PNN expression, would help elucidate whether hormonal signaling influences PNN development around mPFC neurons in California mice.

While we found no difference in WFA+ PNN expression between males and females in the MeA, studies in other rodents have found that males exhibit greater PNN expression in the amygdala compared to females. In rats, early‐life adversity influenced PNN expression in the amygdala in a sex‐ and hemisphere‐dependent manner, where juvenile male rats exhibited increased PNN expression in the right BLA of juvenile males compared to females following early‐life adversity (Guadagno et al. 2020). Moreover, in house mice, adult males had higher PNN expression in the MeApd compared to females, and most WFA+ neurons also expressed estrogen receptors (Ciccarelli et al. 2021). Additional evidence from the hippocampus and hypothalamus further supports the role of hormones in PNN formation (Hernández‐Vivanco et al. 2022; Laham et al. 2022; Uriarte et al. 2020; N. Zhang, Yan, et al. 2021). Overall, the literature suggests that fluctuations in gonadal steroids influence cortical and subcortical PNN development; therefore, an important avenue of future research should be to explore the relationship between PNNs, gonadal steroid signaling, and behavioral sensitization to pups in male and female California mice.

Although some similarities exist between the neural circuits that govern parental care in male and female rodents (Dulac et al. 2014; Wu et al. 2014), accumulating evidence also suggests that sexually dimorphic neuronal populations exist within these shared networks (Scott et al. 2015; Chen et al. 2019). In biparental rodents, many of the regions that mediate maternal behavior are also involved in paternal behavior (Kirkpatrick, Kim, et al. 1994; A. W. Lee and Brown 2002, 2007); therefore, differences in pup sensitization might arise from the same brain regions being differently regulated by hormones and/or by differential fine‐tuned activation of neurons that regulate pup‐directed behavior in a sex‐dependent manner. Our findings suggest that one such fine‐tuning mechanism may be dynamic reorganization of PNN expression with pup experience in adult male California mice. Our findings, along with other studies that found changes in PNN expression following pup interactions (Krishnan et al. 2017; Lau et al. 2020), further suggest that pup experience may influence neuroplasticity in the parental‐care circuit through modulation of PNNs.
