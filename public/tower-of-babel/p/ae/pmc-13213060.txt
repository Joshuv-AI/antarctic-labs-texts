Mathematical modeling and analysis of the Dengue and Chikungunya co-infection
Authors: Akidul Haque, Rafiqul Islam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe Aedes aegypti mosquito is the primary vector for the transmission of Dengue and Chikungunya viruses. Initially identical signs and symptoms of these infections in all cases make disease diagnosis exceedingly challenging. This paper aims to develop a mathematical model, and explore it through stability, bifurcation analysis and Herd Immunity Threshold (HIT) to describe the dynamic transmission, their co-infection and control of these two diseases. First we analyze the biological feasibility of the proposed model and examine the existence and stability of the different equilibrium points in relation to the basic reproduction number, . The model exhibits Transcritical bifurcation at the disease-free equilibrium (DFE), which has been investigated using Sotomayor’s theorem. Furthermore, the stability of the co-infected endemic equilibrium is established through the Center manifold theorem. A normalized sensitivity analysis has been performed to identify the most influential parameters that most significantly reduce disease transmission dynamics. The proposed model is calibrated and validated with reported case data from the 2023 Brazil outbreak, and HIT is applied as a prospective control strategy. The model analysis suggests that, under the calibrated 2023 Brazil parameter setting, achieving an effective protection level of approximately through interventions such as treatment, vector control, or social awareness, may substantially reduce disease transmission intensity and overall disease burden.Keywords: Dengue, Chikungunya, Co-infection, Bifurcation behavior, Sensitivity analysis, Control, Seasonal variationSubject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Diseases, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

In recent years, mosquito-borne arboviruses, specifically Dengue, Chikungunya and Zika viruses, have emerged as leading contributors to morbidity and mortality across many regions and populations around the world. These viruses are primarily transmitted by mosquitoes of the Aedes genus, particularly Aedes aegypti and Aedes albopictus. Historically, Aedes aegypti was geographically confined to the African continent, but now it has now become widely distributed across tropical and sub-tropical regions worldwide and is recognized as the primary vector of Dengue viruses1. In contrast, Aedes albopictus was originally native to Southeast Asia and has undergone significant geographical expansions recently, including tropical to subtropical and even temperate regions, leading to major epidemics of arboviruses2. Due to the widespread distribution, lack of proven antiviral treatment3, and distinct clinical and epidemiological characteristics4, these diseases poses a significant threat to global public health.

Dengue is considered the deadliest, most widely distributed, and world’s fastest-spreading mosquito-borne viral disease, infecting an estimated million people annually and causing thousands of deaths5. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), the Americas, Africa, Southeast Asia, the Eastern Mediterranean, and the Western Pacific are among the regions severely affected by these diseases6 and approximately of the global population is living at risk of Dengue infection7. Dengue infection presents a wide range of clinical symptoms, including frontal headache, high fever, nausea, vomiting etc. The disease is caused by four distinct serotypes of Dengue virus denoted as DENV-1, DENV-2, DENV-3, and DENV-4. Individuals may also be co-infected with multiple serotypes simultaneously. While primary infection with each serotype provides lifelong immunity against that specific strain, however, it confers only temporary and partial cross-protection against the remaining serotypes. Secondary infections can cause severe outcomes such as Dengue Hemorrhagic Fever (DHF) and Dengue Shock Syndrome (DSS), both of which can be life-threatening8. As consequence, after malaria, Dengue is recognized as the second deadliest vector-borne disease (VBD) worldwide, due to its global incidents and associated mortality9.

Similar to Dengue, Chikungunya is also transmitted by Aedes mosquitoes, however, unlike Dengue, historically it is considered an acute life-threatening infection. Chikungunya was first identified during an outbreak in Tanzania in 10 and was initially confined to regions in central Africa. However, during this century, the virus has demonstrated remarkable potential for geographic expansion as it invaded other countries, including more temperate regions with periodic outbreaks. A severe outbreak of Chikungunya epidemic was reported in 2005 on the island of Réunion, in the Indian Ocean, during which approximately people were affected11. Chikungunya is classified as an encapsulated, positive-sense, single-stranded RNA virus12 and till now three genotypes of Chikungunya virus have been identified, each named according of their geographical origin: Asian, West African, and East Central South African13. According to the WHO, the clinical symptoms of Chikungunya typically include the sudden onset of high fever, headache, severe muscle pain, rash, fatigue, and joint swelling14 and once recovered, individuals generally acquire permanent immunity to the disease15. Currently, there is no proven vaccine for Chikungunya; however, VLA155, developed by Valneva, is considered as the most advanced vaccine candidate16. Overall, Chikungunya continues to pose a significant challenge to global public health.

Dengue and Chikungunya viruses have similar attributes; both pathogens are transmitted by the same Aedes mosquitoes and pose overlapping clinical symptoms and therefore making it a reasonable expectation that the epidemiology of Chikungunya and Dengue infections is temporally and spatially related17. The first Dengue-Chikungunua co-infection case was reported in Thailand in 196218 and since then, co-infections have been observed across multiple regions around the world, including Africa, South-East Asia, the Caribbean, and the South America19–21. The overlapping clinical symptoms at the early stage of infection make the diagnosis difficult. At present, there is no clinically proven treatment available to mitigate disease transmission other than controlling the spread of mosquito vectors. Therefore, it is crucial to understand the disease transmission dynamics within both human and mosquito populations and to develop effective prevention and control strategies for these diseases.

Mathematical modeling has become an important tool in understanding the infectious disease transmission and optimizing public health interventions. Numerous arboviral models have been developed to investigate single-disease transmission, including Dengue22,23, Chikungunya24,25, and Zika, with emphasis on vaccination strategies, vector control, and optimal intervention policies. These studies have provided important insights into disease persistence and outbreak control under isolated transmission scenarios.

In addition to the single-pathogen framework, several studies also explored multi-strain or multi-pathogen arboviral dynamics, including two-strain Dengue models26,27, Dengue and Chikungunya Co-infection models28,29, Dengue and Zika co-infections models30. Notably, Isea et al.28 developed one of the earliest SEIR–SEI models to investigate the co-transmission dynamics of Dengue, Chikungunya, and Zika viruses, establishing a foundational framework for future studies, epidemic monitoring, and the management of arboviral co-infections.

Despite these advances, there still remains a significant limitations with the existing Dengue-Chikungunya co-infection models. Most frameworks often simplify secondary infection pathways or omit explicit co-infected infectious compartments, focusing primarily on analysis of basic reproduction numbers and local stability results. Moreover, few studies simultaneously integrate detailed human-mosquito transmission dynamics with rigorous global stability analysis, bifurcation theory, and data-driven numerical investigation within a unified framework. As a result, the long-term effects of co-infection and vector dynamics on disease persistence and control remain poorly understood.

In this research, we seek to address these gaps by developing a robust SEIR-SEI human-vector modeling framework that integrates primary infection, secondary infection, and co-infection transmission dynamics between Dengue and Chikungunya in human populations. The proposed model is extensively analyzed using a combination of analytical methods, including feasibility and boundedness analysis, basic reproduction number derivation, local and global stability analysis, and bifurcation theory. Furthermore, the model is validated using reported Dengue and Chikungunya incidence data from Brazil, 2023, allowing us for quantitative assessment of transmission dynamics and control strategies. By incorporating advances in epidemiological modeling and numerical simulations, our proposed framework offers a comprehensive representation of co-infection transmission dynamics, disease persistence, and control strategies. These findings will advance to an in-depth understanding of co-infection dynamics and design more effective and targeted public health intervention strategies.

The rest of the manuscript is structured as follows: In Model Formulation, we present the construction of a vector-host mathematical model based on the underlying dynamics of co-infection transmission. Section Model Analysis explores the biological feasibility of the model, including the positivity and boudedness of solutions, the basic reproduction number, the existence of equilibrium points, the local and global stability properties, and the associated bifurcation behavior. Section Sensitivity Analysis presents a normalized sensitivity analysis conducted to identify the most influential parameters and further investigates the impact of Dengue transmission on Chikungunya dynamics. Section Numerical Simulations outlines the computational framework and numerical simulation approach, incorporating data fitting using Brazil 2023 data fitting, and an examining the effects of seasonality on disease dynamics. Section Control Strategy discusses potential strategies for disease prevention through control. Finally, section Conclusion summarize the findings and provides insights for future research.

It implicitly assumes homogeneous mixing and a proportional relationship between mosquito and human populations over the transmission period. Remark

In formulating the vector-host model (10-11), we have considered the only possibility of Dengue and Chikungunya co-infection, which may occur at a given time. However, a possible co-infection involving different serotypes between Dengue31, Dengue-Zika36, and Chikungunya-Zika37 has also been reported in different locations around the world. Moreover, several reports have confirmed simultaneous co-infection of Dengue, Chikungunya, and Zika viruses in regions such as Africa, Southeast Asia, the Pacific Islands, the Caribbean, and Latin America9,38,39. Unfortunately, there is currently no available vaccine or effective treatment for these diseases. Since all these diseases are transmitted through the bite of the female mosquito Aedes aegypti and share similar primary symptoms, initial diagnosis remains a significant challenge as it is often mistreated. To address co-transmission dynamics of Dengue, Chikungunya, and Zika viruses, we have proposed a vector-host disease model (see Supplementary Material Section 1).

Conclusion

This paper establishes and thoroughly analyzes a deterministic mathematical model that comprehensively considers the dynamics of both Dengue and Chikungunya, as well as their co-infection transmitted by Aedes aegypti mosquitoes. We incorporate a co-infected compartment, denoted by , with co-infection arising in two ways: Dengue-infectious individuals coming into effective contact with Chikungunya-infectious mosquitoes, and vice versa. In addition, we consider three recovery classes, namely , , and , corresponding to recovery from Dengue, Chikungunya, and co-infection, respectively. Although individuals who recovered from Dengue and Chikungunya acquire immunity against the disease they recovered from, they remain susceptible to the other infection or even to other variants (e.g., Dengue strains). We assume that only dually recovered individuals in acquire permanent immunity against both diseases. At first, we examine positivity and boundedness to demonstrate the biological feasibility of the model. Then we compute the basic reproduction number by using the next-generation matrix approach, which often plays a crucial role in characterizing disease dynamics. We show analytically that , where and denote the basic reproduction numbers of the Dengue-only and Chikungunya-only sub-models, respectively. The disease-free equilibrium point is locally stable when and unstable when . We also verified the global stability of the disease-free equilibrium by using the theorem of Castillo-Chavez. Further, we investigate the bifurcation behavior of the model. This system undergoes transcritical bifurcation at the disease-free equilibrium point, as shown in Fig. 2, and it is analytically verified via Sotomayor’s theorem using the transversality condition. This finding is biologically significant, as it depicts that the stability of the disease-free equilibrium changes as the disease transmission probability passes through a critical threshold, and . Additionally, we perform normalized sensitivity analysis to identify the most influential parameters of . The results indicate that the susceptible mosquito biting rate, , is the most sensitive parameter. Therefore, the disease transmission can be effectively reduced by taking interventions that reduce human–vector interactions, such as increasing public awareness, implementing vector control strategies, and expanding treatment facilities. To capture this effect and achieve more accurate predictions, we incorporated seasonal variation into the model. Overall, this study provides a comprehensive mathematical framework for understanding the co-transmission dynamics of Dengue and Chikungunya, offering quantitative insights into epidemic thresholds and potential control benchmarks under plausible parameter assumptions. Despite the insights provided by the proposed framework, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the proposed model is purely deterministic and does not explicitly account for the stochastic effects arising from demographic and environmental fluctuations or random contact patterns between mosquitoes and humans. Although sensitivity and uncertainty propagation analyses were utilized to address this parameter uncertainty, stochastic formulations may better capture outbreak variability, particularly in early epidemic and low-incidence settings. Second, the lack of reliable co-infection surveillance data restricts direct validation of the model and limits the identifiability of parameters associated with co-infected compartments. As a result, certain transmission and progression parameters are inferred from literature or estimated under simplifying assumptions, which may affect quantitative precision. Third, climate variability, spatial heterogeneity, and vector ecological dynamics are incorporated implicitly through aggregated parameters. Therefore, the absence of spatial structure limits the model’s ability to capture region-specific transmission trends and intervention impacts. Finally, while the herd immunity threshold is employed as a population-level control proxy, a more comprehensive assessment of specific intervention strategies such as vaccination, vector control, and behavioral measures was beyond the scope of the present study. Future work may extend this framework by incorporating stochastic and spatial dynamics, data-driven parameter estimation, environmental vector dynamics, explicit intervention strategies, and multi-pathogen interactions involving Dengue, Chikungunya, Zika, or serotype-specific co-infections. These extensions would improve the model’s predictive capability and policy relevance.
