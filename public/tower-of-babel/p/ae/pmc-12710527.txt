Five‐Year Implementation of Zero Suicide: Lessons Learnt From an Academic Health System
Authors: Seethalakshmi Ramanathan, Lianne De La Cruz, Michiko Ueda, Jay Carruthers
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThe rising rates of suicide in the United States have motivated several interventions, including among health systems. A leading quality improvement approach for health systems is the Zero Suicide (ZS) model. Despite its well‐recognized nature, implementation of ZS initiatives across health systems remains underutilized and understudied. We evaluated a 5 year‐long real‐world implementation of the ZS initiative in a behavioral health inpatient setting to understand strategies and challenges.MethodsElectronic medical record (EMR) data were used over a period of 5 years between January 2020, and June 2024, to study implementation of 3 clinician‐delivered domains of the zero‐suicide model, identify, engage, and transition. For each domain, we evaluated implementation rates and then stratified by demographic characteristics. We also estimated a logit model with individual‐level data to examine whether implementation rates varied by demographic characteristic.ResultsDuring the implementation period, 8810 individuals were hospitalized and included 5703 unique individuals. For each domain, attrition was noted. Racial and gender disparities were observed in all domains. While 100% of individuals were screened and stratified for risk level, approximately 80% of individuals identified as high risk received safety planning, and 53.3% accepted the proposed transition intervention. Refusal reasons ranged from having enough resources to feeling overwhelmed.ConclusionOur study provides useful insights for behavioral health settings planning to implement the ZS initiative and discusses strategies including EMR integration.Relevance to Clinical PracticeFor clinical systems incorporating ZS as a quality improvement implementation, this study provides suggestions about potential challenges, particularly related to sociodemographic disparities.

Suicide rates in the United States have steadily been increasing. Between 2000 and 2023, deaths by suicide increased by approximately 36% (1). Underlying every death by suicide, it is estimated that approximately 265 individuals struggle with serious suicidal thoughts (1). In 2021, this number translated to approximately 12.3 million American adults struggling with serious thoughts about suicide, 3.5 million planning a suicide attempt, and 1.7 million attempting suicide (2). These data not only highlight the problem but also highlight the need to evaluate multiple pathways and solutions to prevent suicide.

The Perfect Depression Care program at the Henry Ford Health System in Michigan, was among the first suicide prevention initiatives in a health care system (3, 4). The outcomes from this initiative, an approximately 80% reduction in deaths by suicide sustained over a period of 10 years, provided compelling evidence for the role of healthcare systems in suicide prevention (5, 6). Since then, Zero Suicide (ZS), a quality improvement and safety framework applied to suicide care, has emerged as the leading model for health system driven suicide prevention (7, 8). The model consists of 7 domains or “pillars”: involvement of committed leaders and champions (lead), improving workforce competence and confidence (train), identifying high‐risk individuals using appropriate screening tools (identify), engaging at‐risk individuals using tailored care management plans, including safety plans (engage), providing evidence‐based suicide‐specific treatments (treat), supporting transition during the high‐risk period following hospitalization (transition) and finally, engaging in continuous quality improvement to improve policies and procedures (improve). Critics of the ZS model have highlighted the difficulties with implementation and its ambitious nature (9, 10, 11). Nonetheless, health systems successes, conducted primarily in outpatient, primary care and hospital settings, highlight the importance of the seven domains of ZS in suicide prevention—3 structural (lead, train, and improve) and 4 clinical (identify, engage, treat, and transition)—and the need to evaluate ZS implementation across different behavioral health settings to both identify and address gaps (12, 13). In this paper, we summarize a 5‐year phased implementation of ZS in an academic inpatient behavioral health setting. We focus on three key clinician‐related domains of the ZS process (identify, engage, and transition) and discuss challenges in implementation and potential solutions. We also evaluate issues related to sustainability and identify demographic factors that can influence implementation.

A large hospital system affiliated with an academic health system in Central New York implemented a quality improvement initiative using ZS principles in the inpatient behavioral health settings as part of the New Yorkers Advancing Suicide Safer Care Initiative, supported by the NYS Office of Mental Health from October 2019 through July 2024. The initiative began with a yearlong engagement period with leadership as well as training of staff in the evidence‐based protocols that formed the various components of the zero‐suicide initiative. Implementation activities were disrupted or delayed by the COVID‐19 pandemic, and thus all domains of the initiative were not fully launched until 2021. Implementation of the ZS program was completed in two overlapping phases. In the first phase (09/2018 to 05/2022), individuals over the age of 25 years were included through two inpatient behavioral health units with approximately 48 beds. During the second phase of implementation (06/2019 to 06/2024), individuals between the ages of 12–25 years were included through a participating adolescent unit. In the final year (2024), there were some structural changes in the adolescent unit, which further influenced implementation.

A standardized protocol, the Suicide Care Management Pathway (14), was implemented. All individuals were screened using the Columbia Suicide Severity Rating Scale (C‐SSRS) and were offered “universal precautions” which included information on the National Suicide Prevention Lifeline, Crisis Text Line (CTL), and psychoeducation about the fluid nature of suicide risk (15). Patients assessed as having moderate or high suicide risk were further administered the Stanley Brown Safety Planning Intervention (SPI). Additionally, individuals who received a safety plan were offered the chance to participate in an intervention called Structured Follow‐Up (SFU) calls. This intervention included a set of two phone calls, the first one made 24 to 72 h after discharge and a subsequent call 72 h after their first outpatient appointment. Adult patients who refused to participate in the SFU were invited to provide reasons for not participating in the intervention.

The crisis line information provision and SPI were offered throughout the 5‐year period from the beginning of the initiative. The psychoeducation component of Universal Precautions and SFU did not fully launch until 2021 and was thus offered only in the last 4 years of the initiative (2021–2024).

METHODS

Data

Our sample included patients who were hospitalized in the inpatient behavioral health units that participated in the ZS initiative between January 1, 2020, and June 30, 2024. Although the first phase of the program launched in the 4th quarter of 2018, we defined the beginning of the study period as 2020 to include patients in all age groups in the present analysis. Individuals under 18 years of age were not included in the initiative until 2020, when the second phase of the initiative began. The current study was deemed exempt by the Institutional Review Board at Upstate Medical University under the category of secondary research for which consent is not required.

We analyzed patient‐level electronic medical records (EMRs) maintained by the hospital system. The records contained information on age, sex, and race. We categorized patients into three age groups: under 18 years (hospitalized in an adolescent unit and included in the second phase of implementation), 18–24 years (hospitalized in the adult unit and included in the second phase of implementation), and over 24 years (hospitalized in the adult unit and included from the first phase of implementation). Patients' sex was recorded as Male or Female, with missing values recorded as unknown. Using the race information recorded in the EMRs, we classified race as White, Black, or Other. The “Other” category included various racial groups, such as American Indian/Alaska Native and Pacific Islanders. The number of patients in these groups was too small to form distinct categories.

We also classified patients by the total number of admissions during the study period, as implementation rates may differ for those with repeat encounters. We created a dichotomous variable indicating whether a patient had multiple admissions.

Reasons for refusing SFU were collected only from patients who were in the adult inpatient unit, and were categorized as: uninterested in treatment, feeling overwhelmed, having enough resources or support, not having access to a phone, language and speech barriers, transferring to another unit, and other.

Analysis

For our analyses, we categorized the three key clinician‐related domains into three steps and analyzed each step separately. The first step consisted of screening, provision of information on crisis line information, and Universal Precautions (identify). In the analysis, “crisis lines” refer to both the National Suicide Prevention Lifeline and the CTL because their information was always presented together. The second step involved safety planning (engage), and the final step was the opt‐in SFU (transition). Note that the second and third steps were offered only to individuals who were identified as moderate to high risk for suicide in the first step.

For each step, we analyzed if the intervention was implemented. The intervention was considered “implemented” if it was offered to the patient. There was no opt‐in element for screening, crisis line information provision, Universal Precautions, and safety planning. Thus, every patient received these interventions if they were offered. However, the SFU was provided with an opt‐in feature, and hence, implementation does not necessarily mean that the patient received the intervention. We mainly focused on whether the intervention was offered to the patient, as our main interest lies in understanding whether clinical staff delivered the intervention in accordance with the protocol. However, to gain insights into ways to improve transition care, we also analyzed the rates of refusal and the reasons for refusing SFU.

For each intervention, we first calculated overall implementation rates and then stratified by demographic characteristics. We then estimated a logit model with individual‐level data to further examine whether implementation rates varied by demographic characteristic. In the logit model, dichotomous outcome variables were used separately for Universal Precautions, safety planning, and SFU. The independent variables included sex, race, and age group. The reference group for each category was Male, White, and those aged 25 and older, respectively. We also included a dichotomous variable for suicide risk status (positive screen for moderate to high suicide risk) when we analyzed Universal Precautions as the outcome. All models included year fixed effects to account for yearly variations in implementation status. We used robust standard errors, and the coefficients were reported as adjusted odds ratios (aOR). The analysis was conducted with Stata (16.1, StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX).

In all analyses, the data from 2020 were excluded when analyzing Universal Precautions or SFU as outcomes, as these interventions were not fully implemented until 2021.

RESULTS

Over the 5‐year period of the implementation of the ZS initiative, 8810 individuals were hospitalized in the three participating units. This included 5703 unique individuals. The mean age was 36.46 (SD = 16.13), with a range of 12 to 91. 849 (9.64%) of the patients were minors at the time of admission. The number of female patients was 4855 (55.11%). There was one patient whose sex was not specified, and we excluded the patient in the subsequent analysis. The majority of patients were White (73.52%), followed by Black (17.64%). Among these individuals, 5583 (80.3%) received a SPI. Implementation rates for safety planning varied by sex, race, and age group (Column E, Table 1). As with Universal Precautions, female and White patients were more likely to receive safety planning, compared to those with other characteristics. DISCUSSION

The continually rising trends in suicide have prompted a number of public health measures at various touchpoints. Inpatient behavioral health settings serve as an important point of intervention for individuals who are hospitalized for a suicidal crisis. Among various approaches, the data‐driven ZS model has established itself as a leading framework for suicide prevention in healthcare systems (14). Replicating the original observations from the Henry Ford experience, a recent multisite study that evaluated ZS implementation across four large outpatient mental health systems in the United States showed a significant reduction in suicide attempt rates following implementation (3, 4, 11). However, despite its preferred status in suicide prevention, concerns about implementation, including ease of implementation and expectations of outcomes remain (9, 10, 11). In a recent study, Chitavi and colleagues found low reported rates in ZS engage and transition domains among nearly 350 hospitals who responded to a questionnaire (16). In this paper, we evaluate a real‐world implementation of ZS measures in an inpatient system associated with an academic health care system. The experience provides a unique opportunity to understand feasibility, and challenges of implementation of ZS initiatives in inpatient behavioral health settings where patient suicide risk is often elevated. We focused on 3 of the 4 clinician‐centered domains—Identify, Engage, and Transition—of ZS. Among these, we were able to achieve a high, nearly universal implementation rate for screening and for provision of information on crisis line. Implementation rates were lower for safety planning (80%), and lowest for SFU. Several factors could have contributed to this variability in implementation. Screening and information on crisis line were embedded into the EMR, making it universal for all patients. However, both safety planning and SFU were clinician driven efforts that require skill and competency, and had no built‐in automated reminders. For both these activities, we actively involved and collaborated with the various discipline leaders. Additionally, for safety planning, specific clinicians were identified and systematic training on the safety planning process was provided, safety planning was included into unit protocols and policies, and finally the teams engaged in periodic quality audits and follow‐up education. Implementation of SFU was offered as an opt‐in choice, which likely influenced implementation rates as noted by the reasons for refusal. Considering the importance of the transition domain, future implementation studies can offer and test an opt‐out option, which has been shown to have higher consent rates (17). A second strategy to increase acceptance of SFU, could be combining the choice of SFU with improved psychoeducation and strategies such as motivational interviewing to address ambivalence, particularly among participants reporting reasons such as not being interested, feeling overwhelmed or having enough resources, to improve the transition phase (18).

Our observations highlight that in domains where interventions can be seamlessly incorporated using EMR, such as the provision of crisis line information, the implementation and delivery of intervention is near universal. However, in other domains, such as safety planning and the opt‐in component of follow‐up calls, where implementation is dependent on clinical staff, rate of implementation was variable. Our observations suggest that females were more likely to receive the clinician‐delivered steps such as safety planning. We also found that implementation rates tended to be lower for Black patients and for those under 18 years of age.

Several factors can be considered and addressed in a systematic manner in future implementations. One factor, “train and pray,” relates to complementing staff training with appropriate systems‐based support. This can include regular trainings, and EMR‐based supports, in addition to leadership and policy and procedural support (19, 20, 21). A second factor relates to both patient and clinician factors influencing clinical decision‐making (22, 23, 24). As an example, while it is not immediately clear why the implementation rate was higher for female patients, it is possible that the severity of suicide risk varied by sex prompting the clinician to deliver the intervention. However, we were not able to incorporate this detail in the current analysis. Similarly, for Black inpatients, the lower implementation rate might be attributable to their higher likelihood of multiple admissions, which could have influenced the clinician's decision to offer the intervention. On the other hand, the age‐related differences noted in implementation could have been driven by systemic factors such as variations in training, implementation, the need to involve parents or guardians for minors, and staff engagement across the two systems (adolescent vs. adult). Regardless of the reasons, our data indicates that implementation was variable across certain demographic groups. One potential solution to mitigate these deficiencies stemming from clinician‐driven variables could be the use of appropriately and collaboratively structured prompts and advisories through the electronic health system. However, care needs to be taken to ensure that the utilization of EMR tools is complemented by appropriate clinical and human components.

The transition period following hospitalization is often considered a high‐risk period for suicidal individuals (25, 26). This implementation offered an opportunity to evaluate acceptance of interventions that mitigate risks related to transition from inpatient to outpatient settings. These reasons, which range from feeling overwhelmed, being uninterested in treatment to not having an accessible phone number, provide useful insights into the distinct difficulties in navigating this critical period of transition. We found that male and Black patients were more likely to cite lack of phone access when they declined the SFU, which suggests that a different mode of delivery might be useful for these groups of patients.

This study is limited in that observations related to patient outcomes concerning overall influence on suicidality, outpatient follow‐up and patient experience have not been evaluated. While we were able to quantify the number of individuals who received the intervention, we were not able to assess the quality of intervention in a systematic manner. Our assessment of quality was limited to periodic audits of safety plans, followed by targeted trainings. We also did not evaluate the clinician driven treat step of the ZS model. The treat domain was difficult to evaluate for several reasons. Patients are discharged from the inpatient units when their current acute risk level on C‐SSRS is safe for discharge. We were unable to assess whether patients received suicide‐specific treatments, such as Dialetical Behavior Therapy (DBT), Cognitive Behavior Therapy (CBT), or Collaborative Assessment and Management of Suicidality (CAMS), that target enduring risk.

Despite these limitations, the experience of this implementation provides useful insights for systems that are launching ZS initiatives (27). The study highlights gaps related to clinical implementation, and racial and gender related challenges associated with implementation. One of the key tenets of ZS is incorporation of the seven domains into ongoing operations of the clinical, educational and administration of the health system (28). As health systems start exploring the incorporation of ZS in their clinical workflows, they should consider multiple innovative strategies including utilizing electronic health records to support these strategies effectively and to address bias related to implementation (29).
