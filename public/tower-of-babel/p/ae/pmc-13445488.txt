New Iflavirus identified in Chiroxiphia pareola birds from an ecotone area in northeast Brazil
Authors: Lizandra Caroline dos Santos Souto, Flávio Kulaif Ubaid, Leonardo Henrique Almeida Hernández, Sandro Patroca da Silva, Beatriz Bacelar Barbosa, Luana da Silva Soares Farias, Taciana Fernandes Sousa Barbosa Coelho, Ana Cecília Ribeiro Cruz, Joana D’Arc Pereira Mascarenhas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Complete genome of a novel iflavirus from wild bird feces.•Phylogenetic analyses place virus within Iflaviridae.•Likely dietary-derived virus detected in Chiroxiphia pareola.•Expands knowledge of iflavirus diversity in Brazil.Keywords: Iflaviridae, Avian virome, Wild birds, Viral metagenomics, Bioinformatics

Introduction

Birds represent one of the most diverse groups of vertebrates, with >11,000 species described worldwide (Gill and Donsker, 2020). This taxonomic diversity, associated with their wide geographic distribution and the broad variety of habitats they occupy, contributes to birds playing an important role in the ecology of several microorganisms, including viruses (Wille and Holmes, 2020). In addition, behavioral characteristics such as migration and the sharing of ecological niches favor interspecific interactions, thereby contributing to the circulation and dispersal of viral particles on both local and continental scales (Wille and Holmes, 2020).

Furthermore, the feeding ecology of birds directly influences the composition of their virome, since dietary habits determine the degree of exposure to viral particles present in the environment or associated with consumed organisms. For example, insectivorous birds, through the consumption of insects, are more likely to harbor insect-specific viruses (ISVs) within their virome, particularly those belonging to the order Picornavirales (Shi et al., 2016; Li et al., 2015). In this context, the avian gastrointestinal tract represents an important ecological convergence point among environmental viruses, intestinal microbiota, and diet-associated viruses, making fecal samples valuable tools for metagenomic studies aimed at investigating viral diversity in natural environments (Wille et al., 2018).

In studies involving wild birds, recent discoveries of novel viruses belonging to the order Picornavirales in fecal samples reinforce the hypothesis that this viral group is widely disseminated in natural populations, including migratory and aquatic species. Studies using samples from bar-headed geese, penguins, and other bird species have demonstrated a high diversity of RNA viruses, including members of the family Picornaviridae and other viruses associated with diet and the microbiome. These findings indicate that birds may act as hosts, reservoirs, ecological sentinels, and even vehicles for viral diversity dispersal, depending on the species analyzed, feeding ecology, and environmental context in which these animals occur (Wille et al., 2018).

The family Iflaviridae, belonging to the order Picornavirales, comprises a group of positive-sense single-stranded RNA viruses with icosahedral particles. These viruses are non-enveloped and measure approximately 22 to 30 nm in diameter. Their genome ranges from approximately 9 to 11 kb in length and encodes a single polyprotein. According to the International Committee on Taxonomy of Viruses (ICTV), the family Iflaviridae belongs to the order Picornavirales and currently includes 16 recognized species, all classified within the genus Iflavirus (International Committee on Taxonomy of Viruses (ICTV), 2025). All members of the genus Iflavirus infect arthropods and may cause either asymptomatic or symptomatic infections (International Committee on Taxonomy of Viruses (ICTV), 2025; van oers, 2010).

Thus, the recurrent detection of arthropod-associated viruses in avian fecal samples reinforces the importance of trophic interactions in shaping the avian virome and highlights the role of birds in the indirect dispersal of ISVs in natural environments.

The objective of this study was to describe the complete genome of a new iflavirus found in fecal samples from blue-backed manakin birds (Chirophixia pareola) inhabiting transitional areas in north-central Brazil, in the state of Maranhão.

Materials and methods

The samples used in the present study were obtained from the research project entitled “Investigation of viral pathogens in arthropods, birds, and bats in areas of the Middle North region of Brazil” and were collected in accordance with ethical principles for animal experimentation. The project was approved by the Brazilian System of Authorization and Information on Biodiversity (SISBIO) under permit no 68,045–1 and by the Animal Ethics and Experimentation Committee (CEEA) of the State University of Maranhão under approval no 025/2019.

The sample collection area of the present study was located in forested environments near peridomestic areas in the municipality of Caxias, in the state of Maranhão, specifically in the Buriti Cortado district, under the coordinates 5°13′61.4″ S and 43°19′96.89″ W (Fig. 1).

Map of the sampling site for avian fecal samples. The red dot indicates the sampling location in the municipality of Caxias, Maranhão state, Brazil.

Bird capture was performed using fine-mesh nylon nets, known as mist nets or ornithological nets, which were continuously deployed and supported by aluminum poles measuring 2.5 m in height and 12 m in length. During each sampling campaign, the nets were operated for three consecutive days, being opened early in the morning and closed after five hours, with inspections conducted at intervals of 30 to 40 min.

All captured birds were individually placed in porous cloth bags with drawstring closures until processing. Each bird was examined for taxonomic identification, with data recorded on species and genus, as well as sex and age whenever possible. Following identification, the birds were banded with aluminum rings provided by the National Center for Research and Conservation of Wild Birds (CEMAVE), an institution of the Chico Mendes Institute for Biodiversity Conservation (ICMBio).

In October 2021, seven fecal samples were collected from the bird species C. pareola in the municipality of Caxias, Maranhão state, Brazil, and subsequently pooled, resulting in a single fecal sample pool.

Fecal samples were collected by stimulation of the rectal ampulla using a zaragatoa-type swab. After sampling, the birds were identified and released at the same site of capture. The fecal specimens were placed in labeled cryogenic vials, stored in liquid nitrogen, and subsequently transported and processed to the Rotavirus Laboratory of the Evandro Chagas Institute (IEC).

RNA extraction and cDNA synthesis. For the extraction and purification of genetic material, 300 µL of fecal suspension was processed following the protocol described by Boom et al. (1990) (Boom et al., 1990). RNA concentration was subsequently determined using the Qubit RNA HS Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) on a Qubit 2.0 fluorometer, according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Complementary DNA (cDNA) preparation from RNA began with first- and second-strand cDNA synthesis using the SuperScript™ IV VILO™ Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and the Second Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific), respectively. cDNA purification was performed using the PureLink® PCR Purification Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). All procedures were carried out in accordance with the manufacturers’ protocols. The synthesized cDNA was quantified using the Qubit DNA HS Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) on a Qubit 2.0 fluorometer.

Genomic libraries were prepared according to the Nextera XT DNA Library Preparation Kit guidelines (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) and sequenced on the Illumina NextSeq 500 platform using the NextSeq 500/550 High Output Kit v2.5 (300 cycles) in paired-end mode, following the manufacturer’s recommendations, in the Arbovirology and Hemorrhagic Fevers Department of the IEC.

Analysis and processing of raw sequencing data.Initially, the quality of the sequencing reads was assessed using Fastp version 0.23.4 (Chen et al., 2018), with the removal of short reads (<50 nt), adapter-derived fragments, and reads containing ambiguous bases (reads with >10% of undetermined nucleotides )N)). Subsequently, SortMeRNA version 2.1 (Kopylova et al., 2012) was employed to remove ribosomal RNA (rRNA) sequences.

De novo assembly.The files generated during the data processing step were used for de novo assembly using MEGAHIT version 1.2.9 (Li et al., 2015). Assemblies were performed using k-mer sizes of 21, 31, 41, 51, 61, 71, 81, 91, and 99 for MEGAHIT. The resulting contigs were compared using DIAMOND version 2.1.8 (Buchfink et al., 2015) through BLASTX analysis against the viral non-redundant (nr) protein database, considering an e-value threshold of 1e−4 and amino acid identity.

The contig files generated for the sample were visualized using MEGAN version 6.21.1 (Huson et al., 2007), applying an e-value filter of 1e−10. Contigs showing similarity to viral sequences of interest were further inspected using Geneious Prime version 2025.1 (Kearse et al., 2012), where map-to-reference and raw-data-to-contig alignment analyses were also performed.

Phylogenetic analysis.Amino acid sequences of viruses belonging to the order Picornavirales were selected, including representatives of the families Picornaviridae, Iflaviridae, Dicistroviridae, Marnaviridae, and Secoviridae, as well as sequencespreviously reported or identified in studies of a similar nature (Remnant et al., 2021; Thekke-Veetil et al., 2020), and retrieved from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) database. For taxonomic inference at the order level, analyses were based on the translated RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (RdRp) domain, whereas inference within the family Iflaviridae was based on the translated polyprotein.

The dataset generated, together with the sequence obtained in this study, was subjected to multiple sequence alignment (MSA) using MAFFT version 7 (Katoh and Standley, 2013).

Using IQ-TREE 2 version 2.2.5 (Minh et al., 2020) the aligned dataset was subjected to phylogenetic signal testing and model selection to identify the best-fitting amino acid substitution model. Phylogenetic trees were then reconstructed using the Maximum Likelihood (ML) method (Myung, 2003), with branch support assessed by bootstrap analysis based on 1000 replicates to provide robust confidence estimates for clade support (Felsenstein, 1985).

Phylogenetic trees were visualized using FigTree version 1.4.4 (Rambaut, 2018) Because no outgroup sequence was selected for the dataset, midpoint rooting was applied using the rooting tool available in the visualization software. Following tree evaluation and editing, the phylogeny was exported in Scalable Vector Graphics (SVG) format for figure editing and manipulation using Inkscape version 1.3.2 (Inkscape, 2025).

Functional domain analysis.The sequence obtained in this study and other phylogenetically related sequences were analyzed for functional domains using the online tool InterPro version 98.0 (Paysan-Lafosse et al., 2023) The output files generated by the analysis, in “.gff” (SignalMap Gene-Finding Format), were imported into Geneious Prime for visualization. Subsequently, the resulting annotations were exported in Scalable Vector Graphics (SVG) format and edited using Inkscape version 1.3.2.

Results and discussion

For the sample analyzed, a total of 59,122,606 sequencing reads were generated. Following contig inspection, it was possible to recover the complete genome of a novel picorna-like virus comprising 9043 nucleotides, containing a single open reading frame (ORF) encoding a polyprotein that spans positions 331 to 9021 and consists of 2896 codons.

After alignment of the dataset, the sequence showing the highest similarity to the sequence obtained in this study was QQP18689 (Soybean thrips picorna-like virus 8), exhibiting 61.53% nucleotide identity.

Given that the reference sequence is not currently classified within any family of the order Picornavirales, despite exhibiting genetic features characteristic of this taxon, we performed a phylogenetic inference including viral sequences grouped across multiple families of the order (Picornaviridae, Marnaviridae, Secoviridae, Dicistroviridae, and Iflaviridae), based on the RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (RdRp) region, in order to determine the phylogenetic placement of both sequences. The dataset used for this inference exhibited a positive phylogenetic signal (80.7%) and served as the basis for phylogenetic reconstruction using the amino acid substitution model Q.PFAM+F+R5. The sequence identified in this study, designated Chiroxiphia pareola iflavirus (CpIV), clustered within the clade corresponding to the family Iflaviridae (Fig. 2A).

Phylogenetic tree of the family Iflaviridae. The Maximum Likelihood (ML) method was applied based on the complete translated polyprotein open reading frame (ORF). The sequence identified in this study is highlighted in red.

To further confirm its placement within this family, a second phylogenetic inference was performed based on the translated polyprotein sequences of members of the family Iflaviridae, as recommended by the International Committee on Taxonomy of Viruses (International Committee on Taxonomy of Viruses (ICTV), 2025). In this second analysis, the dataset exhibited a stronger phylogenetic signal (94.5%), and CpIV consistently clustered among iflaviruses, forming a specific clade with the reference sequence QQP18689 and grouping within the same major clade as other classified sequences (Fig. 2B).

Corroborating the phylogenetic findings, functional domain analyses of CpIV and three other phylogenetically related sequences revealed four shared types of functional domains (Fig. 2C), indicating the presence of conserved regions, including the superfamily 3 helicase of positive-sense single-stranded RNA viruses and the RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (RdRp).

Iflaviruses are traditionally regarded as arthropod-associated viruses, infecting a wide range of hosts such as mosquitoes, ants, bees, wasps, and other arthropod groups (Thekke-Veetil et al., 2020; Guimarães et al., 2024; Mattia et al., 2025). However, these viruses have also been detected in samples derived from different animal groups, including birds, with such occurrences generally attributed to the feeding habits of these hosts rather than to direct viral infection (Juergens et al., 2022; Wille et al., 2018).Some studies have reported that iflaviruses can be lethal to insects, leading to behavioral alterations or increased mortality (van Oers, 2010). Understanding the virome of arthropods is crucial for elucidating their evolutionary history, genetic diversity, modes of transmission, and mechanisms of persistence, thereby contributing to viral surveillance and their potential use as biological strategies for the control of arboviruses of medical and economic relevance (Guimarães et al., 2024).

In the present study, we describe the detection of an iflavirus in fecal samples from C. pareola birds, a finding that suggests a possible association with the feeding habits of this species. It belongs to the order Passeriformes, family Pipridae, and subfamily Ilicurinae. Commonly known as the blue-backed manakin, is an endemic Brazilian species characterized by small body size and a diet primarily composed of fruits, insects, and other small invertebrates (WikiAves, 2025). This ecological trait supports the hypothesis that viral acquisition may occur through dietary intake, thereby constituting a likely component of the bird’s transient virome, which emphasizes that the importance of the analysis of intestinal and fecal samples can also provide insights into the viral diversity of insects and plants that constitute the dietary chain of birds and other vertebrates.

Furthermore, C. pareola is recognized as an important seed disperser, playing a relevant ecological role in the maintenance and regeneration of natural ecosystems. This species exhibits a wide geographic distribution, occurring across different Brazilian biomes, including the Amazon, Caatinga, and Cerrado. It is frequently found in forested areas, understories, and Cerrado formations, contributing significantly to the dispersal of native plant species and to the regeneration of areas impacted by environmental degradation processes (WikiAves, 2025).

Thus, birds in general play a relevant role in the dispersal of microorganisms, particularly viruses, acting as important biological carriers between different environments and hosts. In this context, several bird-associated viruses exhibit zoonotic potential, reinforcing the importance of epidemiological surveillance of these organisms, especially in regions subject to environmental change and intense interactions among wildlife, domestic animals, and human populations (Carroll et al., 2018).

The municipality of Caxias, in the state of Maranhão, is located in the Mata dos Cocais region, which is considered a ecotone area between the Amazon, Caatinga, and Cerrado biomes. This region exhibits high biological diversity, resulting from its geographic position and the presence of distinct vegetation formations, including forests, grasslands, and mangrove areas (De Jesus Silva And Da Conceição, 2011). However, over recent decades, the region has undergone substantial land-use changes, particularly associated with advancing deforestation and the expansion of agricultural activities (Ferreira et al., 2024), thereby intensifying ecological imbalance and favoring the dispersal of wildlife and their associated pathogens to other regions.

Consequently, intensified surveillance of viruses circulating in birds and other animals in the region is essential to expand knowledge of local viral diversity and to better understand the mechanisms involved in the circulation and maintenance of these agents in this ecotone area.

Conclusions

This study describes, for the first time, the genome of an iflavirus detected in samples from Chiroxiphiapareola. The results presented here provide important insights into the behavior of these viruses in natural environments and reinforce the relevance of epidemiological surveillance studies focused on wildlife, particularly in areas subject to environmental and anthropogenic pressures.
