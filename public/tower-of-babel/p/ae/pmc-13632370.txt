Tirzepatide and risk of newly diagnosed aortic stenosis in patients with obesity: a multi-institutional real-world cohort study
Authors: Jheng-Yan Wu, Keng-Wei Lee, Sheng-Chi Huang, Hsuan-Yuan Chang, Yu-Min Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundObesity is a major risk factor for the development of degenerative aortic stenosis (AS), yet no pharmacologic therapy has been proven to prevent AS onset. Tirzepatide, a dual GIP/GLP-1 receptor agonist, provides greater metabolic and anti-inflammatory benefits than conventional GLP-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1 RAs). Whether tirzepatide reduces the risk of incident AS remains unknown.ObjectivesTo evaluate the association between tirzepatide use and the risk of newly diagnosed AS or aortic valve replacement (AVR) compared with GLP-1 receptor agonists in adults with obesity.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study used the TriNetX global federated database. Adults ≥ 18 years with obesity who initiated tirzepatide or GLP-1 RAs between 2022 and 2025 were identified. An active-comparator, new-user design with 1:1 propensity score matching (PSM) was applied. The primary endpoint was a composite of newly diagnosed AS or AVR. Secondary outcomes included individual AS, AVR, and all-cause mortality. Cox proportional hazards models estimated hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Robustness was assessed using negative-control outcomes and E-values.ResultsAfter PSM, 584,236 patients were included. During follow-up, tirzepatide users had a significantly lower risk of AS or AVR compared with GLP-1 RA users (0.2% vs. 0.6%; HR 0.90, 95% CI 0.81–0.98; P = 0.022). Tirzepatide was also associated with reduced risk of newly diagnosed AS (HR 0.90, 95% CI 0.81–0.99; P = 0.025) and lower all-cause mortality (HR 0.88, 95% CI 0.81–0.95; P = 0.001). No association was observed for the negative-control outcome of skin cancer. Subgroup findings were generally consistent, with stronger protective associations observed in women and adults ≥ 65 years. In a sensitivity analysis using a no-treatment comparator, tirzepatide was also associated with lower risks of AS/AVR, AS, AVR, and all-cause mortality; however, the mortality association was interpreted cautiously because of potential healthy-user and treatment-selection biases.ConclusionsAmong adults with obesity, tirzepatide use was associated with a significantly lower incidence of newly diagnosed AS or AVR compared with GLP-1 RAs during follow-up, suggesting a potential association between tirzepatide use and a lower rate of newly diagnosed AS/AVR. Because AS may remain asymptomatic for years and echocardiographic progression data were unavailable, these findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating. Prospective studies are needed to confirm causality and elucidate underlying mechanisms.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12872-026-06269-8.Keywords: Tirzepatide, GLP-1 receptor agonists, Aortic stenosis, Obesity, Incretin therapy, Real-world evidence

Lay summary

Aortic stenosis is a common and serious heart valve disease that mainly affects older adults and people with obesity. Currently, there is no proven medication that can prevent its development, and treatment usually requires valve replacement once symptoms appear. Using real-world health data from more than half a million adults with obesity, we compared the risk of developing aortic stenosis among people treated with tirzepatide versus other commonly used GLP-1 receptor agonists. During follow-up, tirzepatide use was associated with a lower risk of newly diagnosed aortic stenosis or valve replacement, as well as lower overall mortality. These findings suggest that more intensive metabolic treatment may be linked to a reduced risk of heart valve disease, although further prospective studies are needed to confirm whether this relationship is causal.

Introduction

Degenerative aortic stenosis (AS) predominantly affects older adults, with an estimated prevalence of 2–7% among individuals aged over 65 years and rising to nearly 10% in those older than 80 years [1, 2]. Traditionally considered a degenerative process, AS is now recognized as an active, inflammation-driven condition characterized by lipid infiltration, valvular interstitial cell activation, and progressive calcification of the aortic valve [3–5]. The prevalence of AS increases sharply with age and cardiometabolic comorbidities, particularly obesity, which contributes to systemic inflammation, oxidative stress, and endothelial dysfunction [6]. Recent epidemiologic data suggest that obesity not only accelerates aortic valve calcification but also predisposes individuals to earlier onset of clinically significant AS [7, 8].

Beyond mechanical valve replacement or transcatheter intervention, no pharmacologic therapy has been shown to prevent the onset or progression of AS [9]. However, accumulating evidence suggests that metabolic dysfunction and chronic inflammation may contribute to the initiation and progression of valvular degeneration [10, 11], raising interest in whether metabolic interventions could modify this process. Glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1 RAs) have demonstrated cardiovascular benefits in patients with type 2 diabetes and obesity, including reduction in major adverse cardiovascular events and improvement in systemic inflammation and endothelial function [12, 13]. Although these therapies have demonstrated cardiovascular and metabolic benefits, their potential role in preventing valvular disease development has not been established. Tirzepatide, a novel dual glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide (GIP) and GLP-1 receptor agonist, has shown superior efficacy in weight reduction and glycemic control compared with conventional GLP-1 RAs [14, 15]. Beyond metabolic improvements, tirzepatide also exerts favorable effects on lipid metabolism, systemic inflammation, and vascular stiffness, pathophysiologic mechanisms closely linked to aortic valve calcification and stenosis [16–18]. Taken together, these observations provide a biologically plausible rationale for investigating whether tirzepatide may influence the development of AS beyond its established metabolic effects.

Despite this biologic rationale, clinical evidence evaluating the association between tirzepatide use and incident AS remains limited. Given the growing prevalence of obesity and the rising use of incretin-based therapies, understanding the long-term cardiovascular and valvular implications of these agents is clinically important. Using a large, multi-institutional, real-world database, this study aimed to evaluate whether tirzepatide use is associated with a lower incidence of newly diagnosed AS or AVR during follow-up compared with GLP-1 RAs in obese patients.

Methods

Data source

This retrospective cohort investigation utilized the TriNetX platform, which is a global federated research network containing electronic health records for approximately 188 million individuals from 164 healthcare institutions. The database provides access to aggregated patient counts and de-identified statistical outputs, including data on diagnoses, procedures, medications, laboratory measurements, and genomic information. Because the platform offers only non-identifiable summary data and does not allow access to protected personal information or researcher-initiated interventions, the study qualified for exemption from review by the Western Institutional Review Board. In addition, the Institutional Review Board of Chi Mei Hospital approved the study protocol (IRB No. 11402-E02), and the requirement for informed consent was waived due to the retrospective study framework and reliance on aggregated de-identified records. The conduct and reporting of this work followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) recommendations [19].

Study design

We enrolled adults aged 18 years or older who had a recorded diagnosis of obesity (ICD-10-CM codes E66.0, E66.2, or E66.9, or documented body mass index ≥ 30 kg/m²) and subsequently initiated tirzepatide or GLP-1RAs between January 1, 2022, and September 30, 2025 [20, 21]. Those with any previous or overlapping exposure to either medication before the index date were excluded. The index date was defined as the first documented prescription or administration of the assigned therapy. An active-comparator new-user design was employed, contrasting initiators of tirzepatide with initiators of GLP1-RAs to mitigate confounding by indication and to ensure comparable baseline timing. To restrict analysis to incident outcomes, patients with documented study endpoints prior to follow-up were removed. In addition, individuals who received the comparator drug within one year before or up to one year after the index date were excluded. Comprehensive definitions and coding procedures for all variables are detailed in Table S1.

Covariates and propensity score matching

After defining cohorts, index dates, study outcomes, and confounding variables, a baseline covariate matrix was constructed using patient data collected during the 12 months prior to the index date. Propensity scores were generated through logistic regression to estimate the likelihood of initiating tirzepatide for each participant based on pre-treatment characteristics. A 1:1 greedy nearest-neighbor matching approach was then applied without replacement, using a caliper width of 0.1 of the pooled standard deviation of the logit-transformed propensity score. Covariate balance was deemed acceptable when standardized mean differences (SMD) were below 0.1 [22].

Baseline covariates for propensity score matching (PSM) were determined with reference to established evidence, aiming to account for demographic, comorbidity, and biochemical characteristics that may influence treatment allocation and outcomes [23–27]. Included variables comprised age (years, mean [SD]); sex (female and male, n [%]); race (White, Black or African American, and Asian, n [%]); and comorbidities including ischemic heart diseases, chronic kidney disease, nicotine dependence, type 2 diabetes mellitus with neurological, kidney, circulatory, and ophthalmic complications (all n [%]). Anthropometric and laboratory parameters consisted of body mass index (kg/m², mean [SD] and proportion with ≥ 35 kg/m²), estimated glomerular filtration rate (mL/min/1.73 m², mean [SD] and ≥ 15 mL/min/1.73 m²), serum calcium (mg/dL, mean [SD] and ≥ 10.5 mg/dL), serum phosphate (mg/dL, mean [SD] and ≥ 5 mg/dL), and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (mg/dL, mean [SD] and ≥ 160 mg/dL). Detailed coding definitions for all covariates are provided in Table S2. Comorbidity categories were not mutually exclusive; therefore, patients with diabetes-related kidney complications could also be included in the chronic kidney disease category if both conditions were recorded.

Outcomes and follow-up

The primary endpoint was a composite measure capturing newly diagnosed AS or aortic valve replacement (AVR), identified using diagnostic and procedural codes. Because echocardiographic data, AS severity, and prior aortic sclerosis status were unavailable in TriNetX, this endpoint should be interpreted as the first recorded diagnosis of AS or AVR during follow-up rather than the true biological onset or progression of AS.

Secondary endpoints consisted of the individual incidence of AS, AVR, and all-cause mortality. To evaluate the stability of our results, we additionally analyzed skin cancer as a negative control outcome. Follow-up commenced on the day after the index date and continued until the first occurrence of an outcome event, death, the last available clinical encounter, or the end of the observation period, whichever occurred first, with a maximum follow-up duration of up to 3 years. Detailed definitions and coding criteria for all outcomes are presented in Table S3.

Subgroup analysis

For the primary outcome, subgroup analyses were conducted as prespecified, stratifying patients by sex, age category (18–64 or ≥ 65 years), and the presence or absence of major comorbidities. Comorbidity-based subgroups included chronic kidney disease (CKD), diabetes mellitus (DM), heart failure (HF), and coronary artery disease (CAD).

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were summarized as means with standard deviations, whereas categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. PSM was used to balance baseline covariates between the treatment cohorts prior to the primary, subgroup, and sensitivity analyses. Cox proportional hazards models were applied within the propensity score–matched cohorts to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). PSM was performed to balance measured baseline characteristics before outcome analyses. Residual confounding was explored using negative-control outcome and E-value analyses; however, these approaches cannot exclude unmeasured or incompletely measured confounding [28]. Subgroup analyses were prespecified and considered exploratory. Because multiple subgroup comparisons were performed, Interaction between subgroups was assessed using the CI overlap method, which should be interpreted cautiously and were not adjusted for multiplicity. All analyses were performed using the TriNetX analytics environment.

Sensitivity analysis

To evaluate the robustness of the findings, we performed an additional sensitivity analysis comparing patients who initiated tirzepatide with those who did not receive tirzepatide or any GLP-1RA during the study period. Also, we performed an additional sensitivity analysis restricted to patients in both treatment groups who had at least one subsequent prescription record for the index medication between 6 and 12 months after treatment initiation. Additionally, to explore potential heterogeneity within the GLP-1RA class, additional analyses were performed using semaglutide, liraglutide, and dulaglutide as separate active comparators.

Results

Study cohort

A total of 71,398,784 patients had at least two healthcare encounters between January 1, 2022, and September 30, 2025. After excluding criteria, 1,399,535 eligible patients with obesity treated with tirzepatide or GLP1RAs were retained for analysis. Of these, 292,118 were identified as new users of tirzepatide and 1,107,417 as new users of GLP1RAs. Characteristics of study subjects

Table 1 summarizes the baseline characteristics of patients receiving tirzepatide or GLP1RAs before and after matching. Prior to PSM, individuals initiating tirzepatide were slightly younger on average compared with GLP1RA users (51.4 vs. 52.7 years), with a higher proportion identified as White and a lower proportion identified as Black or African American. Several comorbidities, including diabetes with neurological, kidney, circulatory, or ophthalmic complications, were less frequently observed among tirzepatide users, whereas ischemic heart disease, chronic kidney disease, and nicotine dependence were marginally more common in the GLP1-RA group. Primary and secondary outcomes

During follow-up, the primary outcome of AS or AVR occurred in 644 patients (0.2%) in the tirzepatide group and 1,708 patients (0.6%) in the GLP1RA group, corresponding to a significantly lower risk among tirzepatide users (HR 0.90; 95% CI 0.81–0.98; P = 0.022; E-value 1.5; Table 2). For secondary outcomes, newly diagnosed AS alone occurred less frequently in the tirzepatide group (HR 0.90; 95% CI 0.81–0.99; P = 0.025). AVR events were rare in both groups, with 37 events in the tirzepatide group and 134 events in the GLP-1RA group, and the association did not reach statistical significance (HR 0.77; 95% CI 0.53–1.13; P = 0.185). Given the very low number of AVR events and the absence of baseline AS severity information, this endpoint was considered exploratory. Tirzepatide use was also associated with lower all-cause mortality compared with GLP1RAs (0.3% vs. 0.9%), with a HR of 0.88 (95% CI 0.81–0.95; P = 0.001; E-value 1.5; Table 2). To assess the potential impact of residual confounding, a negative control outcome was examine. No significant association was observed between Tz use and the risk of skin cancer (HR, 1.03; 95% CI, 0.95–1.21; P = 0.458, Table S4). Although the relative association was statistically significant, the absolute risk difference between groups was modest. The absolute risk reduction for newly diagnosed AS/AVR was approximately 0.4% over up to 3 years of follow-up, corresponding to an estimated number needed to treat of approximately 250.

Subgroup analysis

Figure 2 presents the results of prespecified subgroup analyses for the association between tirzepatide use and the risk of AS or AVR. By sex, females had 348 vs. 987 events (HR 0.82; 95% CI 0.72–0.93; p = 0.002), and males had 338 vs. 827 events (HR 0.97; 95% CI 0.85–1.11; p = 0.657). Among adults aged 18–64 years, events were 241 vs. 491 (HR 1.07; 95% CI 0.91–1.26; p = 0.387), and in those ≥ 65 years, 445 vs. 1,228 (HR 0.86; 95% CI 0.76–0.96; p = 0.007). In the CKD subgroups, events were 505 vs. 1,346 without CKD (HR 0.92; 95% CI 0.83–1.03; p = 0.150) and 181 vs. 402 with CKD (HR 0.89; 95% CI 0.74–1.07; p = 0.206). Among individuals without DM, 212 vs. 531 events were observed (HR 0.97; 95% CI 0.82–1.14; p = 0.678), and with DM, 474 vs. 1,062 events (HR 0.95; 95% CI 0.85–1.07; p = 0.387). HF subgroups showed 487 vs. 1,374 events without HF (HR 0.92; 95% CI 0.82–1.02; p = 0.116) and 199 vs. 393 with HF (HR 0.90; 95% CI 0.75–1.07; p = 0.218). For CAD, events were 411 vs. 1,144 in patients without CAD (HR 0.96; 95% CI 0.85–1.08; p = 0.446) and 275 vs. 506 with CAD (HR 0.99; 95% CI 0.85–1.15; p = 0.847). Prespecified subgroup analyses demonstrated generally consistent associations across clinically relevant subgroups. Although the interaction test for age reached nominal statistical significance (P for interaction = 0.041), this finding should be interpreted cautiously because multiple subgroup comparisons were performed and no adjustment for multiplicity was applied. No statistically significant interactions were observed according to sex, CKD, DM, HF, or CAD (all P for interaction > 0.05).

Sensitivity analysis

In the sensitivity analysis comparing tirzepatide users with individuals who did not receive tirzepatide or any GLP-1RA during the study period, tirzepatide use was associated with a lower risk of the primary composite outcome of AS or AVR (HR 0.73; 95% CI 0.65–0.83; P < 0.001; Table 3). Similar associations were observed for AS alone (HR 0.75; 95% CI 0.66–0.84; P < 0.001) and AVR (HR 0.32; 95% CI 0.21–0.47; P < 0.001). Tirzepatide use was also associated with a substantially lower risk of all-cause mortality compared with no treatment (HR 0.41; 95% CI 0.37–0.45; P < 0.001). These findings provide additional contextual support for the active-comparator analysis and argue against the possibility that the lower AS/AVR risk observed with tirzepatide compared with GLP-1RAs was solely due to a harmful association of GLP-1RAs relative to no treatment. When analysis requiring at least one subsequent prescription record for the index medication between 6 and 12 months after treatment initiation in both treatment groups, the association remained consistent. Tirzepatide use was associated with a lower risk of AS or AVR compared with GLP-1RAs (HR 0.86; 95% CI, 0.76–0.97; P = 0.012; Table S5). When individual GLP-1RAs were analyzed separately, tirzepatide remained associated with a lower risk of AS or AVR compared with semaglutide (HR 0.90; 95% CI 0.83–0.97; P = 0.005). In contrast, no statistically significant differences were observed when tirzepatide was compared with liraglutide (HR 0.98; 95% CI 0.88–1.10; P = 0.751) or dulaglutide (HR 0.95; 95% CI 0.87–1.03; P = 0.215) (Table S6).

Discussion

In this large, multi-institutional real-world cohort of 584,236 adults with obesity, treatment with tirzepatide was associated with a significantly lower incidence of newly diagnosed AS or AVR compared with GLP-1 RAs during follow-up. Secondary analyses further demonstrated that tirzepatide use was linked to reduced incidence of newly diagnosed AS and lower all-cause mortality. This association persisted after rigorous propensity score matching, across multiple sensitivity and subgroup analyses, and was further supported by negative control outcome testing, supporting a potential association between dual GIP/GLP-1 receptor agonism and lower observed incidence of AS. To our knowledge, this study provides early real-world evidence regarding long-term association between incretin-based therapies and incident AS in a large, real-world population. The sensitivity analysis comparing tirzepatide users with individuals without exposure to tirzepatide or GLP-1RAs provides important context for interpreting the active-comparator findings. In the primary analysis, tirzepatide was associated with a lower incidence of AS/AVR compared with GLP-1RAs. However, without an untreated comparator group, it would remain theoretically possible that both incretin-based therapies were associated with increased AS risk, with GLP-1RAs having a stronger association. The no-treatment sensitivity analysis argues against this interpretation, as tirzepatide was also associated with lower risks of AS/AVR and AS compared with individuals receiving neither tirzepatide nor GLP-1RAs. These findings support the consistency of the observed association across comparator strategies, although they should not be interpreted as definitive evidence of causality. The magnitude of the mortality association observed in the no-treatment comparison warrants careful interpretation. Tirzepatide users had a substantially lower risk of all-cause mortality compared with individuals without exposure to tirzepatide or GLP-1RAs. Although this finding is directionally consistent with the broader cardiovascular and metabolic benefits reported for incretin-based therapies, its magnitude is larger than that reported in randomized cardiovascular outcome trials of GLP-1RAs. In the SELECT trial, semaglutide reduced all-cause mortality more modestly in adults with overweight or obesity and established cardiovascular disease [14]. Moreover, randomized morbidity and mortality outcome evidence for tirzepatide in broad obesity populations is still being evaluated in outcome trials. Therefore, the observed mortality association in the present real-world analysis should be regarded as hypothesis-generating rather than causal. Several factors may have amplified the apparent survival benefit, including healthy-user bias, treatment selection bias, greater healthcare engagement among tirzepatide users, unmeasured frailty, contraindications to incretin therapy, socioeconomic differences, and residual imbalance in baseline disease severity despite propensity score matching.

An important methodological consideration is that the present study evaluated newly recorded AS diagnoses rather than true biological AS onset or echocardiographic AS progression. Degenerative AS develops slowly and may remain asymptomatic for a prolonged period. Therefore, a diagnosis recorded during follow-up may reflect incident disease, previously unrecognized asymptomatic AS, or delayed diagnosis of more advanced disease. In this context, our findings should be interpreted as an association between tirzepatide use and a lower rate of newly diagnosed AS/AVR, rather than evidence that tirzepatide prevents the initiation or progression of AS. Because serial Doppler echocardiographic measurements were unavailable, we could not assess temporal changes in peak aortic jet velocity, mean pressure gradient, or aortic valve area, which remain the most reliable measures of AS progression. Therefore, prospective studies with systematic echocardiographic follow-up are required to determine whether tirzepatide modifies the natural history of aortic valve disease. Additionally, the magnitude of the primary association was modest, and the E-value suggested that relatively modest unmeasured confounding could potentially explain the observed association. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted cautiously and considered hypothesis-generating.

Previous epidemiologic studies have firmly established obesity as an important risk factor for the development and progression of AS. In a large Swedish registry study of 1,722,625 women followed for a median of 19.5 years, 2,488 cases of AS were identified (0.14%). The age-adjusted risk of AS increased progressively with body mass index (BMI), being 2.82-fold higher (95% CI, 2.44–3.25) among women with BMI 30–<35 kg/m² and 3.72-fold higher (95% CI, 2.95–4.70) among those with BMI ≥ 35 kg/m² compared with normal-weight individuals [29]. Similarly, in the Copenhagen General Population Study including 108,211 participants, each 1 kg/m² increase in BMI was associated with a 6% higher risk of both incident aortic valve stenosis and valve replacement, respectively [8]. These findings provide strong epidemiologic evidence linking excess adiposity with accelerated valvular degeneration and highlight obesity as a modifiable risk factor in the pathogenesis of AS. However, no prior clinical study has evaluated whether pharmacologic metabolic modulation can mitigate this risk [30–32]. While conventional GLP-1 RAs have been shown to reduce atherosclerotic cardiovascular events and inhibit inflammatory markers and oxidative stress, which might contribute to AS progression, their effects on valvular calcification remain largely unexplored [16, 33, 34]. These findings extend the cardiometabolic benefits of incretin-based therapies by suggesting that tirzepatide, which induces greater weight loss and metabolic improvement than GLP-1 RAs, may be associated with mechanisms linked to reduced valvular degeneration [15, 35].

Several biologically plausible mechanisms may potentially explain the observed protective association. First, tirzepatide induces substantial weight reduction, which may lower valvular mechanical stress and reduce left ventricular outflow turbulence, both key contributors to valvular injury and fibrocalcific remodeling [36, 37]. Given that obesity is a strong risk factor for AS and tirzepatide induces greater weight reduction than conventional GLP-1RAs, the lower rate of newly diagnosed AS/AVR observed among tirzepatide users may be mediated partly or substantially through weight loss rather than a direct effect on valvular biology. Reduction in adiposity may decrease systemic inflammation, improve metabolic health, reduce hemodynamic load, and attenuate obesity-related pathways involved in valvular degeneration [38]. Future studies incorporating serial body weight, metabolic biomarkers, inflammatory markers, and echocardiographic valve assessments are needed to determine whether the observed association is mediated by weight reduction or by other mechanisms.

Second, tirzepatide exerts more pronounced anti-inflammatory and anti-oxidative effects than GLP-1 RAs, as evidenced by reductions in proinflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 [39]. These cytokines are central to the initiation and progression of valvular calcification, and their attenuation could plausibly slow disease onset [40, 41]. Third, emerging experimental evidence indicates that activation of the GIP receptor may inhibit the osteogenic transformation of vascular and valvular cells, thereby exerting a potential anti-calcific effect mediated through GIP receptor signaling [42]. Collectively, mechanisms including improved glycemic control, lipid metabolism, systemic inflammation, and potentially GIP-related effects on vascular or valvular calcification pathways, may also contribute. However, these hypotheses remain speculative in the absence of biomarker and echocardiographic data.

These findings highlight a previously unrecognized potential of tirzepatide as a cardiometabolic therapy with valvular benefits, beyond its established glucose- and weight-lowering effects. Given that AS is the most common valvular disease in older adults and currently lacks proven medical therapy, early metabolic intervention in high-risk obese individuals may warrant further investigation as a potential preventive approach. However, as this study is observational in nature, prospective studies and randomized controlled trials are warranted to confirm the causal relationship and elucidate the underlying mechanisms.

The clinical significance of this association should be interpreted in light of the modest absolute effect size. Although the relative hazard reduction was statistically significant, the absolute risk reduction for newly diagnosed AS/AVR was approximately 0.4% over up to 3 years, corresponding to an estimated number needed to treat of approximately 250. This modest absolute difference is expected in part because AS/AVR is a relatively infrequent outcome over short- to intermediate-term follow-up, particularly when newly diagnosed AS rather than echocardiographic progression is assessed. Therefore, the present findings should not be interpreted as supporting tirzepatide use specifically for AS prevention. Instead, they may suggest a potential additional valvular association among patients receiving tirzepatide for established metabolic indications. The balance between potential valvular benefit, drug cost, tolerability, gastrointestinal adverse effects, treatment discontinuation, and long-term safety requires further evaluation in prospective studies.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, despite comprehensive propensity score matching and well-balanced measured baseline characteristics, residual confounding from unmeasured or incompletely measured variables cannot be excluded. Additional multivariable adjustment within the matched cohort was not performed, and therefore residual bias may remain. This limitation is particularly relevant because the observed effect size for the primary outcome was modest, and the E-value of 1.5 indicates that a relatively modest unmeasured confounder associated with both treatment selection and AS/AVR risk could potentially explain the observed association. In addition, obesity severity and duration may not be fully captured by a single baseline BMI measurement. Although BMI and severe obesity status were included in the propensity score model, we could not assess lifetime obesity burden, weight trajectory, duration of obesity, waist circumference, intentional weight loss before treatment initiation, or other measures of adiposity and metabolic health. These unmeasured factors may influence both incretin therapy selection and AS risk. In addition, although the relative association was statistically significant, the absolute risk reduction was small, and the estimated number needed to treat was high over the available follow-up period. Therefore, the clinical significance of this association remains uncertain, particularly given the cost, treatment burden, and potential adverse effects of tirzepatide.

Second, because TriNetX is a registry-based database, patient misclassification and underrepresentation may occur, particularly among individuals with milder disease, asymptomatic disease, or limited healthcare engagement, which could affect the generalizability and outcome ascertainment of the findings. Reliance on diagnostic and procedural codes to define exposures, covariates, and outcomes introduces the possibility of coding-related bias. This limitation is particularly important for AS because degenerative AS usually progresses slowly and may remain asymptomatic for years. Therefore, AS recorded during follow-up may represent newly diagnosed disease, previously undetected asymptomatic AS, delayed recognition of more advanced AS, or incidental detection during auscultation or echocardiographic evaluation, rather than true biological disease onset. In addition, the accuracy of ICD-10 coding for AS has not been specifically validated in the TriNetX network used in this analysis.

Furthermore, TriNetX does not provide systematic echocardiographic parameters, including peak aortic jet velocity, mean transvalvular gradient, aortic valve area, valve calcification severity, or serial Doppler measurements. We therefore could not evaluate baseline aortic sclerosis, AS severity, AS-related symptoms, or echocardiographic progression, nor could we distinguish incident mild AS from progression to clinically significant AS. The maximum follow-up duration of three years may also be insufficient to fully capture the long-term development or progression of AS. Accordingly, our findings should be interpreted as associations with newly diagnosed AS/AVR rather than evidence of reduced AS onset or progression. To explore the potential influence of coding-related bias, negative-control outcome analyses using clinically unrelated conditions were performed and showed no significant associations.

AVR should also be interpreted cautiously as an exploratory secondary outcome. Time to AVR can serve as a surrogate for AS progression only when baseline AS severity, symptoms, procedural eligibility, and treatment access are comparable between groups. These factors were unavailable in the present database, and AVR events were rare, indicating limited statistical power to evaluate this endpoint. Third, information regarding treatment duration and medication adherence was unavailable, preventing assessment of dose–response relationships and the effects of sustained exposure to tirzepatide or GLP-1 receptor agonists. Fourth, we were unable to determine whether the observed association between tirzepatide use and lower risk of newly diagnosed AS/AVR was mediated by greater weight reduction compared with GLP-1RAs. Although baseline BMI and severe obesity status were included in the propensity score model, TriNetX did not provide sufficiently complete longitudinal weight or BMI trajectories, timing of weight change, waist circumference, body composition, medication dose, treatment persistence, or adherence data. Therefore, formal mediation analysis could not be performed, and we could not distinguish whether the observed association reflected weight-loss–mediated effects, improved metabolic status, reduced systemic inflammation, or a direct effect on valvular biology. Future prospective studies with serial anthropometric, metabolic, inflammatory, and echocardiographic measurements are needed to clarify these pathways. Fifth, although the no-treatment sensitivity analysis provided useful contextual information, this comparison is more vulnerable to confounding by indication, healthy-user bias, and differential healthcare utilization than the active-comparator new-user analysis. Individuals not receiving tirzepatide or GLP-1RAs may have differed from treated patients in frailty, socioeconomic status, access to care, contraindications to incretin therapy, baseline disease severity, and health-seeking behavior. These differences may partly explain the large mortality association observed in the no-treatment analysis; therefore, this finding should be interpreted cautiously and should not be considered a causal estimate of the survival benefit of tirzepatide. Moreover, differential detection or surveillance bias may have influenced AS ascertainment, because early AS is often asymptomatic and may be incidentally diagnosed during echocardiographic or cardiovascular evaluation. More frequent healthcare encounters among tirzepatide users would be expected to increase detection of subclinical AS and bias the association toward the null or harm, whereas greater cardiovascular monitoring among GLP-1RA users could increase AS detection in the comparator group. Because echocardiography frequency, imaging indications, and AS severity were unavailable in TriNetX, the direction and magnitude of this potential bias could not be fully assessed. Finally, cause-specific mortality data were not available, limiting the ability to distinguish cardiovascular from non-cardiovascular causes of death and introducing potential outcome misclassification.

Conclusion

In this large, real-world cohort of patients with obesity, treatment with tirzepatide was associated with a significantly lower incidence of newly diagnosed AS or AVR compared with GLP-1 RAs during follow-up. These findings suggest that dual GIP/GLP-1 receptor agonism may confer additional cardiovascular and valvular benefits beyond metabolic control. Given the absence of proven preventive therapy for AS, tirzepatide may warrant further investigation as a potential metabolic approach for high-risk individuals. However, as this study is observational, prospective studies and randomized controlled trials are needed to validate these associations and clarify their causal pathways.
