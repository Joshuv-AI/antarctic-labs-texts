GOLD-concordant inhaled maintenance therapy at hospital discharge after hospitalisation for severe COPD exacerbation: a multicentre retrospective observational study
Authors: Catharina Peukert, Stefan Kwast, Sven Hohenstein, Victoria Pradler, Sebastian König, Torsten Bauer, Thomas Armbrust, Nikolaus Büchner, Kristina Probst, Anika Laing, Dörte Anderson, Kathrin Kahnert, Andreas Bollmann, Ralf Kuhlen, Michael Gediga, Kurt Rasche
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundChronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is a leading cause of global morbidity, with frequent exacerbations resulting in hospitalizations, increased healthcare burdens, and mortality. The GOLD report emphasizes dual or triple inhaler therapy for high-risk (GOLD group E) patients, yet real-world guideline adherence during hospitalization remains unclear. This study aimed to evaluate concordance with GOLD-recommended maintenance inhaler therapy at hospital discharge in patients with severe exacerbations of COPD and to identify factors associated with treatment rates.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective, multicentre, observational study of 3928 COPD inpatients across 85 hospitals within the German Helios group (January 2022–September 2024). The primary outcome was the proportion of patients discharged on GOLD-concordant inhaler therapy, defined as dual long-acting bronchodilation (LABA/LAMA), or triple therapy (LABA/LAMA/ICS) in patients with eosinophils ≥300/μL or high exacerbation risk. Secondary outcomes included diagnostic procedures, pharmacological management, hospital characteristics, in-hospital outcomes and predictors of guideline-concordant prescribing assessed using multivariable logistic regression models.FindingsThe mean age of the cohort was 70.1 years (SD 10.1), 45% (n = 1784) were female, 75% (n = 2952) had cardiovascular comorbidities and 45% were treated on pneumology wards. Eosinophil counts were measured in 49% (n = 1895) of patients. During hospitalisation, 14% (n = 560) of patients required intensive care, mean length of stay was 6.6 days, and in-hospital mortality was 1.7% (n = 66). In patients with available documentation, at admission, 61% (1344 of 2205) received dual (21%; 455) or triple (40%; 889) maintenance inhaler therapy. At discharge, 77% (2573 of 3326) of patients were prescribed GOLD-concordant inhaler therapy (dual: 28%; 939 and triple: 52%; 1720). In multivariable analyses, guideline-concordant prescribing at discharge was independently associated with longer length of stay (odds ratio [OR] 1.17/day; 95% CI 1.06–1.29), male sex (OR 1.45; CI 1.23–1.71), treatment on a pneumology ward (OR 1.77; 1.42–2.20), and older age (OR 0.87 per standard deviation increase; CI 0.79–0.95).InterpretationIn this multicentric large, real-world, hospital-based cohort of patients with severe COPD exacerbations, more than one in five patients were discharged without guideline-concordant maintenance inhaler therapy. Specialist pneumological care and structured inpatient management were strongly associated with improved adherence. Hospital care substantially increased maintenance therapy rate. Systematic implementation of eosinophil risk stratification, enhanced specialist involvement, and standardised discharge pathways could be opportunities and may substantially improve evidence-based COPD care and reduce future exacerbation risk.FundingThis study and the preparation of the manuscript were financially supported by AstraZeneca.Keywords: COPD, Guideline adherence, GOLD, Hospital care, Inhaler therapy

Research in context

Evidence before this study

We searched PubMed and Embase for studies published up to May 18th, 2026, using the terms “COPD”, “exacerbation”, “hospitalisation”, “guideline adherence”, “GOLD”, “inhaler therapy”, and “real-world”. Previous studies have demonstrated substantial gaps between GOLD guideline recommendations and real-world pharmacological management of COPD, particularly in outpatient and claims-based settings. Evidence on inhaler therapy adherence following hospitalisation for severe exacerbations is limited, often restricted to single centres, selected hospital types, or specific care settings, and rarely accounts for differences in hospital level or geographic setting, degree of specialisation, or patient characteristics. Data linking inpatient care structures to guideline-concordant maintenance therapy at discharge across heterogeneous hospital levels and the presence of a pneumological ward remain scarce.

Added value of this study

This study provides a large, multicentre, real-world evaluation of guideline adherence in hospitalised COPD patients with severe exacerbations across 85 hospitals within a nationwide hospital network. By including primary, secondary, tertiary, and specialist hospitals, as well as both pneumology and non-specialist wards in urban and rural settings, our analysis captures the full spectrum of inpatient COPD care. The study uniquely assesses maintenance inhaler therapy at hospital discharge, when patients are clinically stabilised and classified as GOLD group E, while considering GOLD recommended eosinophil assessment, hospital characteristics, and care pathways. This comprehensive approach allows identification of diagnostical, structural and clinical factors associated with guideline-concordant prescribing beyond individual patient characteristics.

Implications of all the available evidence

Taken together with existing evidence, our findings highlight that substantial variability in guideline adherence persists across hospital types and care settings, even within a high-income European healthcare system. The strong association between specialist pneumological care and guideline-concordant therapy suggests that organisational factors play a critical role in translating evidence into practice. Given the inclusion of diverse hospital levels and urban–rural settings, these results are highly relevant to hospital-based COPD care across Europe. Systematic implementation of biomarker-guided treatment strategies and their translation into the hospital setting, structured discharge processes, and targeted support for non-specialist wards are opportunities to improve adherence to evidence-based recommendations and reduce preventable exacerbations and rehospitalisation.

Introduction

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) remains a leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide with high socioeconomic and individual care burden. It is estimated that 3 million people die from COPD each year, and the global burden is projected to increase due to ageing populations and persistent exposure to risk factors (World Health Organization, 2020). Despite advancements in understanding and managing COPD, significant gaps in diagnosis, treatment adherence, and clinical outcomes persist, leading to frequent exacerbation-linked hospital readmissions and mortality.1,2 The 2023 GOLD update introduces several pivotal changes, including revised definitions for COPD and exacerbations, the introduction of the ABE initial assessment tool, and the emphasis on mortality reduction as a long-term treatment goal.3

Recent studies have highlighted the discrepancies between real-world data of clinical practice and guideline recommendations, underscoring the need for improved adherence to these guidelines.4, 5, 6, 7 Real-World Data for COPD patients in Germany showed a pharmacological undertreatment in more than 50% of deceased patients.8 Routine data of general practitioners (GPs) in COPD treatment also showed a significant undertreatment.9 These data from routine care support the increased pharmacological therapy recommended in the GOLD report, especially for prevention and treatment of exacerbated patients.8,10 In addition, real-world data have demonstrated significant undertreatment following hospitalization of COPD patients.11,12 Current data on in-hospital treatment of high-risk COPD patients and pharmacological management are scarce or have not been evaluated in relation to GOLD report, particularly regarding guideline-adherence.12 By introducing the class of GOLD E it was highlighted that the optimal pharmacological therapy of these patients should focus on previous hospitalizations and eosinophil count. These recommendations and the associated clinical indication for the optimal pharmacological treatment remain under debate,13,14 especially for high-risk patients often treated in the hospital setting. In-hospital treatment and discharge management itself have a significant impact on the long-term outcome of COPD patients for the prevention of exacerbations and mortality.12,15, 16, 17

Therefore, hospitals play a crucial role in the treatment of severe courses of COPD, including the pharmacological treatment and prevention of severe exacerbations, pneumonia or long-term oxygen therapy.12,18 Constant evaluation of medication and guideline adherence are important factors.9 Moreover, the coordination with general practitioners (GP) and pneumologists should be evaluated. Admission and discharge medication represent the medical treatment link between the hospital and outpatient setting and should be evaluated in the context of hospitalized COPD patients regarding guideline concordance. In addition to the individual patient clinical health care burden, guideline concordance have demonstrated in various countries substantial clinical and economic benefits.19,20 Nevertheless, in-hospital care focuses on acute treatment of COPD-related exacerbations, whereas the maintenance therapy is primarily handled by GPs or pneumologists. Current guidelines incorporate the interaction of acute and maintenance in a less amount. At discharge where patients are in stabilized condition, the hospital physicians have to adapt medical therapy in accordance to the changed patient conditions and/or change of GOLD stage.

This retrospective, observational, real-world study primarily evaluates the adherence to the GOLD 2023–2025 guideline and pharmacological management of hospitalized COPD patients. The secondary objectives were to describe the in-hospital clinical course and analyses diagnostic and treatment rates and assessing the influence of patient and hospital settings on guideline concordance in pharmacological treatment and selected patient outcomes.

Methods

Study design

In this retrospective, multicentre study, we analysed administrative data and electronic medical record data from 3928 inpatient cases in 85 hospitals within the national Helios group in Germany (primary to tertiary care centres, including university hospitals). The study was conducted and reported in accordance with the STROBE guidelines for observational studies.21

Data source

Helios Safe Medical Data (HeSaMeDa) is a broad consent-based platform for collecting routine data within the Helios hospital network, compliant with ethical, regulatory, and data protection requirements.22 The HeSaMeDa platform was reviewed in accordance with the Declaration of Taipei and Helsinki by the Ethics Committee of the Medical Faculty of the University of Leipzig (068/25-EK). The dataset comprises reimbursement data and electronic medical records, including medication and laboratory results. Patient data were stored in a double-pseudonymized form, if the patient provided his written informed consent to the data processing and storage during their hospital stay. The study protocol for this observational, retrospective study was reviewed by the local ethics committee of the Ethical Committee of the University Witten/Herdecke (S-147-2024).

Patient cohort

All inpatient cases recorded in the HeSaMeDa platform admitted between January 2022 and September 2024 with a main diagnosis of COPD (ICD-10-GM-2025 code J44) were identified (n = 5989). All patients aged ≥18 years who received full inpatient treatment for an acute severe COPD exacerbation were included to analyse a cohort as representative as possible to routine care. Structured information of a mild or moderate exacerbation history in the outpatient setting, despite hospital admission medication, was not available in our dataset. Acute exacerbations were identified using a combined diagnostic and treatment-based approach, defined by ICD-10-GM code J44.1 or ICD-10-GM J44 in combination with acute systemic corticosteroid therapy (n = 3928). Systemic corticosteroid therapy was defined as oral or intravenous administration of Prednisolone (ATC: H02AB06), Methylprednisolone (ATC: H02AB04), Hydrocortisone (ATC: H02AB09), and Dexamethasone (ATC: H02AB02). This combined ICD-10-coding and treatment-based approach allowed for a comprehensive identification of exacerbation-related hospitalizations. Fig. 1 illustrates the patient identification process.

Study aims and guideline concordance

The primary objective of this study was to evaluate adherence to maintenance inhaler therapy recommendations according to the Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease (GOLD) report3,23,24 in hospitalized patients classified as GOLD group E due to a severe exacerbation. Because hospitalization primarily aims to manage acute deterioration, treatment decisions were assessed at hospital discharge, when patients were clinically stable and the index severe exacerbation had led to classification as GOLD group E. Our approach and definitions for GOLD concordance are consistent with definitions used in prior guideline-assessment studies.25 The GOLD recommendations 2023–2025 advised that all patients in this high-risk group should receive dual bronchodilation therapy, consisting of a long-acting beta-agonist (LABA) and a long-acting muscarinic antagonist (LAMA) or triple therapy consisting of LABA, LAMA and inhaled corticosteroids (ICS): In cases where eosinophil counts are available and exceed 300 cells/μL, escalation to triple therapy (LABA, LAMA, ICS) is recommended and considered as guideline concordance, as these patients derive greater benefit from ICS in exacerbation prevention. For eosinophil counts between 100 and 300 cells/μL, both dual and triple therapy were guideline concordance, reflecting graded benefit with increasing eosinophil levels. For patients with an eosinophil count below 100 cells/μL, the GOLD report favours dual inhaler therapy. Triple therapy is not favoured; however, it was also classified as guideline concordance when clinically justified, acknowledging the suppression of eosinophil counts by systemic corticosteroids during acute exacerbation management. Treatment regimens involving ICS showed better results in patients at higher risk of exacerbation, defined as two or more exacerbations per year or one severe exacerbation leading to hospitalization.

Given that all patients in the cohort experienced a severe exacerbation requiring hospitalisation and acknowledging clinical diagnostic practice, dual and triple inhaler therapy were classified as guideline concordance in maintenance treatments in patients without eosinophil assessment. A guideline-adherence analysis stratified for eosinophil counts and testing will be reported. Guideline adherence was additionally assessed using a pragmatic definition based on eosinophil counts only: ≥300/μL requiring triple therapy, 100–299/μL allowing dual or triple therapy, and <100/μL or unavailable eosinophils allowing dual therapy only. Adopting a strict eosinophil-only standpoint, the use of ICS when the eosinophil count is below 100/μL may be considered potential overtreatment, while the absence of triple therapy when the eosinophil count is 300/μL or above could be considered potential undertreatment. These two patient cohorts guided by eosinophil count will therefore be analysed separately (see the Method section for statistics).

Outcome and endpoints

The primary outcome was the proportion of patients discharged on guideline concordance in inhaler therapy, encompassing the general recommendation for dual therapy and the conditional recommendation for triple therapy based on eosinophilic inflammation markers.

Secondary objectives were to examine variation in treatment patterns and patient characteristics across clinical and healthcare contexts and to identify factors associated with guideline concordance at discharge. Prespecified secondary analyses focused on care delivery and disease severity. Care-related characteristics included admission type (emergency vs. scheduled referral or inter-hospital transfer), geographic setting (urban vs. rural), hospital level (general, specialist, or tertiary), and treatment on a pneumology ward. Disease severity was assessed using lung function impairment (forced expiratory volume in 1 s, FEV1) and the presence of cardiovascular comorbidity. Treatment on a pneumology ward and FEV1 severity were defined as key secondary outcome characteristics and are therefore presented in dedicated tables and stratified analyses in the manuscript.

Cohort characteristics were reported for age, sex, and body mass index (BMI, kg/m2). Respiratory function was classified by FEV1 using the ICD-10-GM-2025 spirometric grading system: not impaired (≥70%), moderate impaired (≥50%, <70%), severe impaired (≥35% to <50%), and very severe impaired (<35%). The FEV1 diagnostic rate is also reported, as valid diagnostic assessment is often not feasible in the acute clinical setting; therefore, it should be performed prior to discharge to allow for better symptomatic classification of patients. Accordingly, we analyse the rate of spirometric assessment as a parameter of quality of care. Comorbidity burden was assessed using the AHRQ-weighted Elixhauser Comorbidity Index and supplemented by the prevalence of key conditions for COPD (pneumonia, asthma, respiratory failure, and CVD). Comorbid diagnosis of Asthma (ICD-10 J45) was reported for clinical contextualization. Linkage to outpatient care was assessed via analyses for referring physician specialty (pneumologist, cardiologist, general practitioner, or other).

To further examine the clinical course and healthcare burden, we assessed the in-hospital outcomes via patient clinical complexity level (PCC level), length of hospital stay (LoS), and treatment on intermediate or intensive care units (ICU), including ICU length of stay, need for mechanical ventilation, administration of long-term oxygen therapy (LTOT), and occurrence of severe acute respiratory infections (SARI) and in-hospital mortality.

Diagnostic procedures via evaluated both standard laboratory parameters and advanced pneumological assessments, including body plethysmography, pulmonary function testing, diffusion capacity (DLCO), residual volume measurement, bronchial reactivity testing, functional capacity via spiroergometric or 6-min walk test (6 MWT), and computed tomography (CT) imaging.

Laboratory values relevant to COPD management and differential diagnosis26 were evaluated: leukocyte count, absolute eosinophil count (cells/μL), arterial oxygen and carbon dioxide partial pressures (pO2 and pCO2), N-terminal pro-brain natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP), haemoglobin concentration, C-reactive protein (CRP), and procalcitonin (PCT). The first available blood sample collected post-admission was utilized to mitigate the impact of hospital therapy.

In all analyses, inhaled therapy is reported according to the highest level of maintenance inhaler therapy received by the patient. Medication use were analysed at hospital admission and discharge, categorized into inhaler therapy (SABA, SAMA, LAMA, LABA, ICS, and their relevant combinations), oral COPD medications and biological agents and concomitant medications for CV comorbidities. All separate drug classes are reported in the Supplementary Material for admission and discharge (Supplementary Tables S7.2 and S8.2).

Statistical analysis

Inferential statistics were based on (generalized) linear models. Effects were estimated in the R environment for statistical computing (version 4.0.2, 64-bit build) (R Core Team 2020). For all tests we apply a two-tailed 5% error criterion for significance.

For the description of categorical variables with more than two levels, we employed χ2-tests for categorical variables. We report p values.

For the comparison of proportions of selected treatments and outcomes in the different cohorts, we used logistic regression models. We report proportions, odds ratios (OR, calculated by exponentiation of the regression coefficients) together with confidence intervals and p values.

The analysis of numeric variables was performed via linear regression models. We used linear regression for LOS and ICU LOS. Skewed variables were transformed them via inverse hyperbolic sine in order to roughly approximate normal distributions (Burbidge, Magee, and Robb 1988). We report statistics for Elixhauser comorbidity index as well as its components for the weighted Elixhauser comorbidity index, the AHRQ algorithm, ranging from −19 to +89, was applied (Moore et al., 2017; Gasparini 2018). We report means, standard deviations, medians, interquartile ranges, and p values. Descriptive statistics for the overall cohort and predefined strata were reported unadjusted and presented separately for each subgroup to allow transparent comparison. Stratification was based on clinically relevant variables to reflect differences in patient characteristics and care pathways within routine practice.

Multivariable analyses of guideline concordance for clinical and contextual factors of outcomes were performed via logistic regression. Categorical variables entered the analyses as simple-coding contrasts while the continuous variables were centred at their means and scaled to unit variance (SD). To account for cardiopulmonary interactions, a composite cardiovascular disease (CVD) variable, which also serves as stratification factor, was prespecified and included alongside the Elixhauser comorbidity index in the multivariable analyses. Dedicated analyses of potential eosinophil-discordant triple therapy prescribing regarding ICS prescribing for potential over- and undertreatment was analysed using the same multivariable analyses as for the primary endpoint. This was done separately within the overtreatment group for patients with a low eosinophil count (less than 100/μL) and within the undertreatment group for patients with a high eosinophil count (more than 300/μL). The analyses assessing the potential for undertreatment were adjusted for comorbidities and clinical conditions that are considered to be potential contraindications to ICS treatment. These include a history of pneumonia, osteoporosis, type 2 diabetes, cataracts, glaucoma, malignancy, bronchiectasis, mycobacterial infections and tuberculosis.

Results

Results are presented for the overall cohort and key prespecified subgroups, covering endpoint relevant cohort characteristics and clinical care pathways. The reporting focused on the total cohort and the key secondary outcomes for the influence by treatment on a specialized pneumological ward and FEV1 severity. Therefore, we are reporting patient demographics and comorbidities, pharmacological treatments at admission and discharge, laboratory diagnostics, clinical course and healthcare utilization, as well as adherence to guideline-recommended therapy. Additional stratifications for clinical characteristics relevant to the secondary endpoints can be found in the Supplementary Material with concordant labelling (admission, geographic setting, hospital level, and presence of cardiovascular comorbidity).

Patient characteristics and stratification factor

A total of 3928 hospitalised patients with COPD admitted for severe exacerbation were included in the analysis. Each hospital contributed a mean of 1.54% of the cohort (SD 1.71%), indicating a broad distribution of cases across participating hospitals. The mean age was 70.1 years (SD 10.1, median 70.0 years); 45% (n = 1.784) were female, and the mean BMI was 26.5 kg/m2 (SD 7.0) (Table 1). 1786 patients (45%) were treated on pneumological ward, while 2142 (55%) were treated at other departments. More than half of patients (59%; 2329) had a severe airflow obstruction. Emergency admissions accounted for 68% (2652) of cases, 18% of which (485) were initiated by a physician. Most emergency patients presented directly to the emergency ward, either by walking in or by ambulance. By geographic setting, 54% (2112) of all patients were treated in urban hospitals, and 46% (1816) in rural hospitals. Regarding hospital care level, 822 patients (21%) were admitted to tertiary care centers, 1469 patients (37%) to secondary care or specialist hospitals, and 1637 patients (42%) to general primary care hospitals. Overall, 90.3% (n = 3004) of patients in our cohort with acute exacerbated COPD received systemic corticosteroid therapy.

Most patients overall were referred from a general practitioner (n = 988; 60%) or a pneumologist (n = 378; 23%). General practitioners primarily referred patients to general hospitals and non-pneumology wards, whereas pneumologists more frequently referred to tertiary care centres and specialized pneumology departments.

Patients with higher FEV1 values (≥70% predicted) were most treated in general hospitals (n = 233; 70%), whereas those with severe and very severe airflow limitation were more often managed in specialist hospitals (severe 39%, n = 338 of 877 and very severe 43%, n = 629 of 1452) or tertiary centres (severe 21%, n = 182 and very severe 25%, n = 361). Laboratory diagnostics

Laboratory diagnostic rates and values were reported in Supplementary Table S5. The leukocyte counts were assessed for 100% of the cohort. Eosinophil counts were measured in 49% of patients (n = 1.569) with a mean of 0.2 ± 0.3 103/μL and among these, 20.1% (n = 316) had values ≥ 300 cells/μL, 34.6% (n = 544) between 100 and 299/μL, and 45.2% (n = 709) below 100/μL. Arterial or capillary oxygen (pO2) and carbon dioxide (pCO2) partial pressures were measured in 71% and 68% of patients. CRP as a marker of systemic inflammation was frequently elevated: the median C-reactive protein (CRP) was 9.4 mg/L [IQR 3.1–32.2]. The mean haemoglobin level was 13.8 ± 2.2 g/dL. NT-proBNP was measured in 41% of patients and showed substantial variability in testing rates.

Patients treated in tertiary centres and on pneumology wards had higher rates of complete blood count and gas exchange measurements compared to those in general medical wards or lower-level hospitals.

In-hospital treatment

The in-hospital course, including severe in-hospital outcomes, is reported in Table 3 and in the corresponding table of the Supplementary Material. In our exacerbated COPD cohort, 14% (n = 560) of patients needed to be treated at an intensive care unit and the overall in-hospital mortality was 1.7% (n = 66), with higher utilization or need for mechanical ventilation in patients treated on a pneumological ward compared to care on other wards (21% (378 of 1786) vs. 11% (227 of 2142), p < 0.001). Clinical severity and resource utilisation varied by admission pathway and care setting.

Discussion

In this large, multicentre real-world study of hospitalised patients with severe COPD exacerbations, we found that guideline concordance in maintenance inhaler therapy at discharge was prescribed in 77% of patients at discharge. Despite overall high rates of inhaled therapy at discharge, substantial variability in guideline adherence persisted across hospital types, admission pathways, and levels of respiratory specialisation.

Our study extends previous outpatient- and claims-based research by providing a comprehensive evaluation of long-term inhaler outpatient treatment before exacerbation, the hospital diagnostics and the discharge management of COPD patients, across the full spectrum of hospital levels and urban–rural settings. Although conducted within a single European healthcare system, the inclusion of diverse hospital structures enhances the transferability of our findings to comparable health systems across Europe. Treatment on a pneumology ward emerged as the strongest independent predictor of guideline concordance. In addition, exacerbated COPD patients were treated in every hospital setting, in comparable proportions in specialty pneumological as well as non-specialty wards. In all hospital levels the patients were mainly referred as emergency cases and only partial involving outpatient physicians. Similar analyses of discharge inhaler treatment are lacking for Europe. For US hospitals12 significantly lower rates for triple therapy are reported at discharge (18%) compared to our observed rates, demonstrating relevant regional and care provider differences.

The GOLD report and the German national care guideline for exacerbated COPD is focusing on the handling of the acute symptoms. Especially severe exacerbated COPD patients, defined by hospitalization, were primarily managed in in-patient settings partially via intensive care measures. The German national care guideline recommends an acute laboratory setting based on a clinical consensus that primarily considers the acute treatment needs. According to the GOLD report, a hospitalized exacerbation leads to a classification staging as category E. After stabilization of the initial condition, the chosen medication for maintenance inhaler therapy should be selected upon discharge to prevent a timely exacerbation as best as possible. For this purpose, the clinical perspective and the eosinophil count are used as guides. This is not yet a guideline recommended laboratory standard parameter for acute exacerbation and should be measured directly upon admission, before the effect of corticosteroids influences the eosinophil count.

This requirements for acute care during hospitalization, on the one hand, and maintenance inhaler therapy, with adjustment of long-term therapy upon discharge, are demonstrated by our retrospective, observational study in a real-world setting and underline the need for adapted patient pathways in hospitals and dedicated guideline sections.

Our severe exacerbated COPD cohort is characterized by older age, significant comorbidity, and advanced airflow limitation, our findings closely mirror national registry data such as COSYCONET,27 which similarly document high multimorbidity and symptomatic burden in German COPD populations. Hospitalized exacerbated COPD in our cohort was clinically heterogeneous, with substantial multimorbidity (e.g., CHF 32%) and wide variability in gas exchange and NT-proBNP levels, supporting the multidimensional concept underlying the Rome criteria. However, several required primary data elements (standardized dyspnoea quantification, respiratory rate/accessory muscle assessment, and uniformly timed arterial blood gases) were not consistently available, precluding formal Rome classification in our dataset. Notably, the mean age in our cohort was 70.1 years, which is higher than the 65.1 years reported in COSYCONET. This difference likely reflects our focus on patients hospitalized for acute exacerbations, in contrast to the broader COSYCONET inclusion of stable outpatient COPD populations, highlighting the need for intensified adjustments of maintenance inhaler therapy in the hospital setting. Vogelmeier et al. reported an inhaler maintenance therapy rate of 63.4% for severe exacerbating patients 12 month before death.8 We observed a comparable maintenance dual or triple therapy in 59% of our exacerbated COPD cohort. Therefore, our cohort can be considered as a representative cohort for severe exacerbated cases regarding medication rates. Regarding the in-hospital outcome, our cohort demonstrated a significant increase in the need for intensive care, with an ICU admission rate of 14% and a mortality rate of 1.7% and covered severe cases as well. Mortality was lower for pneumological treatment than for non-pneumological treatment (1.2% vs. 2.1%). Patients in pneumological wards received higher diagnostic and treatment rates. Differential diagnostics are essential for guided treatment decisions and interdisciplinary decision-making in high-comorbidity cohorts. For example, our COPD cohort exhibited clinically meaningful increases in NT-proBNP (Median 385 ng/L) and CRP (Median 9.4 mg/mmol) values. The interpretation of NT-proBNP in acute COPD exacerbations is challenging due to the close interaction between pulmonary and cardiovascular disease. Both exacerbations and concomitant heart failure can elevate natriuretic peptide levels through increased ventricular pressure–volume load, making a clear clinical distinction difficult. Previous studies show that NT-proBNP is often elevated in COPD, even without heart failure, and reflects disease severity and prognosis. Accordingly, the levels observed in our cohort may represent overall cardiopulmonary stress rather than isolated cardiac dysfunction, highlighting the need for careful clinical interpretation. In addition to laboratory-based evaluation, oxygen therapy constitutes another important component of routine clinical care in AECOPD. In our cohort, long-term oxygen therapy (LTOT) was initiated during hospitalization in a notable proportion of patients, particularly on pneumology wards (22% vs. 6% on non-specialized wards). This reflects an attempt to achieve more rapid clinical stabilization and facilitate earlier discharge. According to the GOLD guideline recommendations, LTOT should be prescribed only in stable patients after reassessment of hypoxemia, as oxygen requirements during acute exacerbations may be transient. Therefore, initiation during hospitalization should be interpreted with caution and requires structured outpatient follow-up to confirm the indication.

As referred above, blood eosinophils have gained recognition as a pivotal biomarker guiding inhaled corticosteroid (ICS) use, particularly for patients with frequent or severe exacerbations or type-2 inflammatory features. As recognized in the GOLD report, exemplary the post-hoc analyses of the IMPACT trial revealed that patients with baseline eosinophil counts ≥310 cells/μL derived substantially greater exacerbation reduction from triple therapy compared to LAMA/LABA alone (rate ratio ∼ 0.56 vs. ∼ 0.88 at <90 cells/μL). Yet our real-world observation showed no dependency of eosinophil count and prescription rate for dual or triple therapy. Triple therapy was not more commonly prescribed in the group >300 cells/μl (53%) vs. the group <300 cells/μl (58%). In our cohort, eosinophil testing was documented in just 49%, despite leukocyte measurement in all patients and partially initiated after systemic corticosteroid application. This highlights both a gap in routine biomarker assessment and an opportunity to improve guideline-based, phenotype-driven management. While incomplete eosinophil data may limit the interpretation of our findings, the large cohort size supports robustness; in addition, we addressed potential bias by reporting across all available eosinophil strata and applying both a pragmatic and a more stringent definition of guideline adherence. Nevertheless, triple therapy was prescribed at discharge for 48%. For dual therapy with LAMA/LABA, we could not observe major variance influenced by testing or level of eosinophil count (range of means: 26–29%). As higher eosinophil testing rates were observed in patients treated at the pneumological ward than in non-pneumological ward (70% vs. 30%), a high proportion of our COPD cohort may not have been tested due to operational or timing constraints therapy. Notably, the inhaler therapy rate increased from 78% to 97% during the hospital stay. Even with potential in maintenance inhaler therapy, other patients were mainly treated for acute short-term symptom controls with short acting inhaler therapy, which is guideline-recommended in all severe exacerbations as minimum therapy approach. Recent European data support our finding that discharge after severe COPD exacerbation remains a missed opportunity for maintenance GOLD-concordant therapy optimization. Ronconi et al. showed high 1-year mortality and rehospitalization rates, while many patients remained untreated or received delayed treatment escalation after discharge.28 Białas et al. emphasize structured, multidisciplinary discharge management, but provide limited concrete guidance on immediate pharmacological adjustment.29 Similarly, Mihara et al. focused on discharge medication after acute COPD exacerbation and found relevant undertreatment, although their analysis was limited by a single-centre design, smaller sample size, and focus on triple therapy.30 This evidence also supports the need for explicit discharge algorithms linking acute exacerbation care with in-hospital guideline-based maintenance inhaler therapy.

In the adjusted multivariable logistic models, older age, male sex, longer hospital length of stay, and treatment in a pneumology ward showed significant associations with guideline-adherence. Pneumological ward treatment revealed the greatest odds ratio of 1.77 and emerged as major independent predictors of guideline-adherent prescribing. This is in line with the higher COPD-related examination rate and eosinophil diagnostic rate by dedicated pneumologists. This underscores that specialist respiratory care improves adherence to biomarker-based recommendations. Hospital level or urban/rural location did not independently influence prescribing behaviour. Guideline for COPD exacerbation treatment therefore should recognize, that half of the patients were not treated by pneumologist and provide an “easy-to-use” guide for the management of acute exacerbated cases, including the handling of maintenance therapy at discharge. Higher age was associated with a lower prescription rate in the multivariable analyses, which could be influenced by more clinical contra-indications, expected device compliance or by the partial co-linearity of variables within the multivariable analyses, like the Elixhauser Comorbidity Index. Our data highlights that older patients should be intensively evaluated to avoid undertreatment.

Operational adjustments and science implementation measures for specific components of structured management could improve discharge medication concordance, particularly on non-pneumological wards: (1) mandatory pre-corticosteroid eosinophil testing, (2) protocolised pre-discharge spirometry, (3) pharmacist-assisted medication reconciliation and (4) standardised COPD discharge checklists. As the current data analysis focused on exacerbation events and the use of GOLD-recommended long-term inhaler therapy during a single hospital stay, long-term outcomes (e.g., rehospitalization rate and re-exacerbation) cannot be assessed and should be evaluated in future studies.

Notable strengths of the multi-centric, retrospective, observational trial are the large, representative inpatient cohort and linkage to guideline adherence by eosinophil testing. A major limitation is the identification of guideline-adherence due to clinical reasons, which is an individual therapy adjustment by physicians. The guideline recommendations themselves are more useful for the outpatient setting and therefore have limitations by acute care, such as corticoid therapy, the need for intensive care and influence by other therapy options like long-term oxygen therapy. As 90.3% of patients received systemic corticosteroids at or before admission, eosinophil counts in the first post-admission sample may have been suppressed, potentially leading to misclassification into lower eosinophil strata and underestimation of the proportion of patients with eosinophils ≥300 cells/μL. Inhaler-device adherence, education information and patient acceptance of multi-pharmacological treatment also influences prescription rates and could not be reflected in analyses of clinical data. Unmeasured confounders such as socioeconomic status or prior prescription history may also influence prescribing. In addition, HeSaMeDa is a consent-based data platform, which cannot include patient with fatal hospital care course, already admitted in very severe health conditions and at least half of our patient cohort were treated by specialists. Therefore, the reported mortality rate of 1.7% in our cohort is significantly lower than those reports for Germany for all ICD-10 J44.0 COPD cases (6.4%) of the federal institute for reimbursement 2024. As the primary focus for acutely admitted patients is to reduce mortality, adjusting long-term maintenance inhaler therapy is not the focus, and they are often transferred directly to rehabilitation clinics for more dedicated therapy adjustments. In addition, some routine diagnostics were unable to capture data outside of the EMR, e.g., chest X-rays were not captured. However, the relevant diagnostic results for chest X-rays, mainly infections or pneumonia, are reported. The use of EMR and claims-based data may be subject to coding inaccuracies and limited clinical granularity, while this study represents, to our knowledge, the first to comprehensively combine claims data with laboratory parameters and medication utilization. Admission medication data were not available for all cases due to variable emergency room digitalization. Given the homogeneous distribution across hospitals, a clinically relevant bias is unlikely. Regarding the patient cohort, our real-world cohort included 20 patients younger than 40 years, potentially reflecting alternative aetiologies such as genetic lung disease, occupational exposure, or bronchiectasis, only two had no documented pulmonary function testing, making an influence on the overall results unlikely.

This study highlights substantial variability in inhaler therapy adherence following COPD exacerbation hospitalization across German hospitals. While the majority of patients received some form of maintenance inhaler therapy, guideline concordance has a potential for optimization, particularly in relation to eosinophil-guided ICS use. The eosinophil testing rate as guidance for maintenance should be established in patients admitted for typical symptoms of COPD. Nevertheless, we observed only minor influence of eosinophil count on prescription rate. Discharge management and implementation science represent key opportunities for future strategic research. Treatment on pneumology wards and longer hospital stays were strong predictors of adherence, underscoring the importance of specialist involvement and adequate diagnostic time. To enhance the translation of GOLD report and NVL COPD guideline into clinical practice, systematic eosinophil testing, structured decision support, and targeted education efforts should be prioritized.
