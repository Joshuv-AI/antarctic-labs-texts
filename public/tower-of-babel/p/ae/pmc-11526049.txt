The interference and elimination of nitrite on determination of total urinary protein by Pyrogallol Red–Molybdate method
Authors: Shijian Fan, Jiaqing Liu, Yu Wei, Jie Yao, Juan Cheng, Yang Tong, Qiang Zhou, Yuanhong Xu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSome urine samples showed negative values of total urinary protein by Pyrogallol red–molybdate (PRM) method. Interestingly, these samples showed notably high levels of nitrite in the urine dipstick test.MethodsA total of 120 urine samples were collected and categorized into four groups (0 - < 100, 100-<500, 500-<1000, ≥1000 mg/L) based on total urinary protein concentration. Various concentrations of nitrite (2, 10, 50, 100, and 200 mg/L) were added to urine samples to investigate potential interference of nitrite in total urinary protein measurement by the PRM method. Additionally, different concentrations of L-ascorbic acid (100, 500, 1000, and 2000 mg/L) were added to urine-nitrite mixtures to explore the possibility of reversing the interference effects.Results2 mg/L nitrite had no impact on the test results.10 mg/L nitrite only showed significant effects on the detection results among the groups with 0 - < 100 and 100-<500 mg/L (P < 0.05). 200 mg/L nitrite caused a noticeable decrease in the urinary protein detection results of the four groups (0 - < 100, 100-<500, 500-<1000, ≥1000 mg/L), and the concentrations were reduced to 2.7 %, 26.85 %, 75.22 %, and 89.33 % of their original levels, respectively. 500 mg/L L-ascorbic acid effectively eliminated the interference from 200 mg/L nitrite and almost had no effect on detection.ConclusionsNitrite had negative effect on the detection of total urinary protein by PRM method and L-ascorbic acid was effective in counteracting the interference caused by nitrite.

Introduction

The Pyrogallol red–molybdate (PRM) method was commonly used for the quantification of total protein in urine or cerebrospinal fluid due to its rapidity, cost-effectiveness, and high detection efficacy [[1], [2], [3], [4]]. Nevertheless, recent research has indicated that the PRM method may be susceptible to interference from aminoglycosides and quinolone antibiotics during total protein detection in urine [[5], [6], [7], [8]], as well as from heparin during total protein detection in cerebrospinal fluid [9]. In the daily work of the hospital laboratory department, it was observed that some urine samples exhibited negative values for total urinary protein when analyzed using the PRM method, while concurrently displaying positive outcomes for nitrite in the urine dipstick test. The objective of this study is to explore the impact of nitrite on the measurement of total urinary protein when utilizing the PRM method.

Materials and methods

Instruments and reagents

AU5800 Clinical Chemistry Analyzers (Beckman Coulter, USA), Urinary/CSF Protein reagent and Calibrator (PRM method, Beckman Coulter, USA), Urine Chemistry Control (Bio-Rad, USA), Urine dipstick (Arkray, Japan), Nitrite and L-ascorbic (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China). The main parameters for the analyzer included adding 1.2 μl of urine sample and 114 μl of reagent to the reaction cup. The absorbance value was then measured at a wavelength of 600 nm after 10 min. The principle of the PRM method was to measure the complex formed by protein and PRM under acidic conditions at 600 nm absorbance [3].

Urine samples

Fresh random urine or 24-h urine samples were collected from the outpatient clinic. Samples testing positive for nitrite were excluded using the urine dipstick test. A total of 120 urine samples were collected and classified into four groups (0 - < 100, 100-<500, 500-<1000, ≥1000 mg/L) according to the concentration of urinary protein, with 30 samples in each group. Before proceeding with further experiments, the urinary samples were centrifuged at 400 g for 10 min.

Nitrite interference experiment

Nitrite was dissolved and diluted to create a range of concentrations (20, 100, 500, 1000, and 2000 mg/L) using pure water. Subsequently, either 50 μl pure water or 50 μl nitrite solution of nitrite solution with varying concentrations was added separately into 450 μl urinary sample. The final nitrite concentrations used for evaluating nitrite interference were 0, 2, 10, 50, 100, and 200 mg/L. Each sample was tested three times and then averaged.

Elimination of nitrite interference experiment

50 μl various concentrations of L-ascorbic acid solutions were added separately into 450 μl of a urine-nitrite mixture (400 μl urine and 50 μl nitrite solution). The final concentration of nitrite in the mixture was 200 mg/L, and L-ascorbic acid were 100, 500, 1000 and 2000 mg/L, respectively. The urine- L-ascorbic mixture (400 μl urine, 50 μl L-ascorbic solution and 50 μl pure water) was used as a blank control. Each sample was tested three times and then averaged.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using Prism 8 software. Data are presented as mean ± s. d, One-way ANOVA for multiple group comparisons. P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Interference of nitrite on measured total urinary protein

2 mg/L nitrite did no impact the urinary protein detection of the four groups. However, at the groups of lower urinary protein concentrations (0 - < 100, 100-<500 mg/L), 10 mg/L nitrite began to affect the detection results significantly (P < 0.05). As the nitrite concentration increased to 50, 100, and 200 mg/L, the impact on the detection results became even more pronounced (P < 0.0001). When the urinary protein concentration was in the range of 500-<1000 mg/L, nitrite concentrations of 50, 100 and 200 mg/L resulted in decreased the detection results (P < 0.05). Elimination of nitrite interference through the use of L-ascorbic acid

The inhibition of 200 mg/L nitrite on the detection of urinary protein was quite significant. The concentration of urinary protein in each group (0 - < 100, 100-<500, 500-<1000, ≥1000 mg/L) decreased to 2.7 %, 26.85 %, 75.22 %, and 89.33 % of the original concentration, respectively (Fig. 1). 100 mg/L L-ascorbic acid failed to reverse the inhibition. However, 500, 1000, and 2000 mg/L L-ascorbic acid were successful in significantly eliminating the interference caused by the 200 mg/L nitrite. The mixture of 500 mg/L L-ascorbic acid and urine samples was tested as a blank control. Discussion and conclusion

Urinary nitrite was one of the comprehensive items of urinary chemical detection, which was mainly used for rapid screening of urinary tract infection. Nevertheless, in clinical practice, there were instances where urine samples were not stored properly or sent for testing promptly, leading to a significant increase in nitrite production by bacteria. To avoid potential additional interference caused by heightened nitrite levels in the urine samples themselves during experiments, urine dipstick test was employed to screen for and exclude positive nitrite samples. In this study, it was found that a concentration of 2 mg/L nitrite did not impact the detection of urinary protein. The urine dipstick test had a lower limit of detection for nitrite of approximately 0.8 mg/L. This suggested that even if the urine samples used in the experiment contain small amounts of nitrite, it had minimal impact on the nitrite inhibition test. The impact of 10 mg/L nitrite on urinary protein detection was found to be statistically significant for groups with a protein concentration of 0 - < 100 mg/L and 100 - < 500 mg/L. Specifically, there was a noticeable percentage difference of 7.31 % for the former group, whereas the latter group only showed a slight difference of 1.54 %. This suggested that the presence of 10 mg/L nitrite had minimal effect on the detection of urinary protein when the concentration exceeded 100 mg/L. If the percent difference was assumed to be greater than 10 % to indicate effective interference, it was only considered when the concentration of nitrite surpasses 50 mg/L in the groups of 0 - < 100 mg/L and 100 - < 500 mg/L, and exceeds 200 mg/L in the group 500-<1000 mg/L in order to form effective interference (Table 1).

The addition of protamine to the cerebrospinal fluid helped alleviate the interference of heparin in detecting total protein using PRM [9]. Furthermore, a reduction in dye and SDS, coupled with an increase in sodium oxalate, successfully mitigated the interference caused by aminoglycoside antibiotics when detecting urinary proteins with PRM [10]. In this study, we found when the urinary protein concentration was in the range of 0–200 mg/L, 100 and 200 mg/L nitrite often reduced the urinary protein detection results to negative values, which caused problems for clinical reports. In the course of our research, we discovered when the urinary protein concentration was within the range of 0–200 mg/L, the nitrite levels of 100 and 200 mg/L frequently resulted in negative detection values. This discrepancy presented challenges for accurate clinical reporting. In the experiment to eliminate nitrite interference, we tested a variety of concentrations of L-ascorbic acid along with the highest concentrations of nitrite (200 mg/L) to evaluate the potential for reversal. At a concentration of 100 mg/L, L-ascorbic acid increased urinary protein levels in four groups by varying degrees, from 2.7 %, 26.85 %, 75.22 % and 87.33 %–4.82 %, 54.96 %, 86.6 % and 89.45 %, respectively, and the reversal effect was not ideal (Fig. 1). When the concentration of L-ascorbic acid added was ≥500 mg/L, it effectively neutralized the effects of nitrite and returned urine protein levels to their original range (97.42 %–101.4 %). In our testing, a mixture of 500 mg/L L-ascorbic acid and urine samples was used as the blank control group. The results indicated that 500 mg/L L-ascorbic acid did not impact the detection of urinary protein (P > 0.05). The impact of L-ascorbic acid concentrations of 1000 mg/L and 2000 mg/L on the experiment was not investigated. We also attempted to use sulfanilic acid and sodium cyclamate which could react with nitrite in the elimination test. However, we found that the reverse effect of these substances was not significant. Additionally, using them as blank controls would result in serious interference.

In conclusion, total urinary protein was an important indicator of renal function impairment in clinical medicine. This study found that sodium nitrite had a negative interference on the determination of urinary total protein through the PRM method. The level of interference increased with higher concentrations of nitrite, and was most pronounced when the total protein concentration in urine was low. L-ascorbic acid could be used to eliminate the interference of nitrite without affecting the detection of total urinary protein.
