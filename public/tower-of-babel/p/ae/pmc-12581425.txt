Curcumin-incorporated edible hydrogel films based on potato starch/κ-carrageenan/poly(vinyl alcohol) for cultured meat scaffolding
Authors: Kannan Badri Narayanan, Rakesh Bhaskar, Sung Soo Han
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Edible polymeric composite hydrogel films offer a promising solution for cultured meat production. These films are made by incorporating natural polysaccharides, synthetic biocompatible polymers, and antioxidants within the scaffolds. This approach can help combat global climate change and meet the increasing demand for sustainable food sources. The utilization of edible polysaccharides in the fabrication of hydrogels is a cost-effective and sustainable approach, which serves as effective scaffolding in the cultivation of meat. The polymeric composite hydrogel films, designated as “CSCP” (curcumin–starch–carrageenan–PVA) with varying concentrations of polymers, consist of curcumin (an antioxidant and coloring agent), starch (potato), kappa (κ)-carrageenan, and poly(vinyl alcohol) (PVA), with PVA being classified as generally recognized as safe (GRAS) for use in food applications. These edible polymeric composite hydrogel films were prepared with glycerol, serving as a plasticizer, and succinic acid, a crosslinker, through solvent casting and thermal treatment methods. Analytical techniques, including field-emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM), X-ray diffraction (XRD), Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy, and tensile strength testing, were employed to evaluate the morphology, crystalline nature, composition, and mechanical properties of the fabricated CSCP scaffolds. The incorporation of glycerol and succinic acid facilitates the plasticizing and cross-linking of the polymeric materials via hydroxyl and carboxyl group interactions during film formation. Increasing the potato starch content in the CSCP-2 composite hydrogel film reduced its mechanical strength. This is because the starch disrupted the polymer’s crystalline regions. The resulting amorphous structure improved the film’s flexibility and elasticity. Nevertheless, the increased potato starch content adversely affects interfacial adhesion, reducing tensile strength. The swelling ratio of the CSCP-2 composite hydrogel film slightly decreases with higher potato starch content, which limits hydrogen bonding interactions with water. Notably, the CSCP composite hydrogel films support adhesion and proliferation of bovine muscle satellite cells (MuSCs) with good cytocompatibility for up to 21 days. However, a slight decrease in metabolic activity on CSCP-2 films was observed. This was likely due to nutrient depletion and limited oxygen diffusion caused by cell multilayering. Overall, the starch-based edible CSCP composite hydrogel films exhibit significant potential as scaffolds for culturing bovine muscle satellite cells (myosatellite cells), paving the way for large-scale production of three-dimensional (3D) cultured meat.

Introduction

The global population is projected to progressively rise and reach 9.7 billion people by 2050 [1]. According to a United Nations study, reducing the consumption of animal products is essential to mitigate the severe impacts of climate change. The study urges the global adoption of veganism in place of meat- and animal-product-based diets [2]. Furthermore, it also emphasizes that animal-based food production is unsustainable given the growing population demands. However, the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) of the United Nations indicates that an additional 200 million tons of meat will be needed annually to meet the unprecedentedly rising demand for protein. In this context, cultured meat offers a scalable and sustainable alternative to conventional meat, which does not rely on traditional animal farming and the need for animal slaughter, aligning with animal welfare principles.

Cultured meat, also referred to as clean meat, is an emerging technology focused on producing meat from cell cultures on a large scale [3]. This artificial meat is generated through the mass culturing of muscle cells, combined with other cell types, without the necessity of livestock slaughter. Efficient development of cultured meat techniques for large-scale production can significantly reduce greenhouse gas emissions attributed to traditional livestock farming, a major contributor to climate change. This approach also offers a potentially sustainable alternative for next-generation meat with minimal environmental effects associated with water and land use [4]. Moreover, cultured meat processes are inherently eco-friendly and ethical for animal protein production [5]. Importantly, cultured meat has the potential to improve food safety by preventing the spread of microbial pathogens and potentially reducing or eliminating the need for antibiotics [6].

To produce cultured meat, primary muscle satellite cells are harvested from animals and cultured in a bioreactor containing an artificial growth medium supplemented with nutrients and growth factors [7]. These muscle satellite cells (myosatellite cells) serve as an inoculum for cell growth on various scaffolds that promote differentiation into mature muscle tissue, which closely resembles natural muscle [8]. The composition of meat muscle consists of approximately 90% muscle fibers and 10% connective and adipose tissues, along with a few vascular and nervous tissues. The connective tissue binds together bundles of elongated muscle fibers (myofibers) within the muscle and attaches muscle to bone, while fat surrounds the muscles. The primary challenge in cultured meat production is the development of three-dimensional (3D) constructs as edible, mechanically robust, and biologically functional scaffolds that mimic the original skeletal muscle architecture, characterized by unidirectionally aligned juxtaposed myotubes appearing uniformly striated [9]. For this purpose, scaffolds should mimic the extracellular matrix (ECM) of natural tissue, using collagen-rich materials to support cell attachment and tissue assembly via integrin interactions [10]. Myoblasts, which are adherent cells, undergo proliferation and differentiation into myotubes. Orella et al. [11] developed a microstructured edible film to facilitate muscle fiber growth for in vitro meat production [11]. Commonly, ECM-based proteins (collagen and collagen-derived gelatin) [12], biopolymers such as alginate and agarose [13, 14], chitosan, cellulose [15], and decellularized plant matrices [16] are used as scaffolds for in vitro meat production due to their non-toxicity, low antigenicity, biocompatibility, and biodegradability [17]. Despite these advancements, the mechanical properties of scaffolds remain a significant concern. To enhance mechanical strength, flexibility, and texture, natural polymers such as starch and carrageenan, along with synthetic food-grade poly(vinyl alcohol), can be incorporated into the scaffold design.

Interestingly, Bodjrenou and colleagues demonstrated that purple sweet potato starch (PSPS) cross-linked with biocompatible 3% sodium trimetaphosphate (STMP) exhibited increased paste viscosity, higher resistant starch content, and a reduced glycemic index (GI). The cross-linked PSPS showed slower enzymatic digestion and delayed glucose release, resulting in a lower GI. These low-GI starch materials have the potential for large-scale production of functional foods and as scaffolding materials for cultured meat production [18]. In 2024, Kumar and colleagues demonstrated maleic acid cross-linking of starch/PVA blend films by solvent casting for food packaging applications [19]. The novelty of this study is the fabrication of edible starch-based polymeric composite hydrogel films integrating food-grade PVA with starch and carrageenan for improving both mechanical strength and biological functionality for cultured meat production. Previous studies primarily used ECM proteins, alginate, agarose, chitosan, cellulose, or decellularized plant matrices as materials for scaffold fabrication, which have weak mechanical properties, poor flexibility, and limited scalability. Gurler and Ertekin (2024) demonstrated the fabrication of antibacterial and antioxidant eco-friendly gelatin–PVA blend films with Cinnamomum zeylanicum extract (CZE) [20]. Similarly, an antibacterial active composite of starch foam incorporated with PVA and carboxymethyl chitosan (CMCS) exhibited enhanced mechanical properties for various applications [21]. Many of these scaffolds lacked cell compatibility for cell culturing and suitability for direct consumption or large-scale industrial production. Therefore, in our study, we fabricated and characterized edible starch-based polymeric composite hydrogel films as scaffolds, evaluating their physicochemical and mechanical properties, as well as their in vitro cytocompatibility for culturing bovine muscle satellite cells (MuSCs) in cultured meat production.

Results and discussion

Fabrication of CSCP composite hydrogel films

The use of sustainable materials in developing edible polymeric composite hydrogel films for cultured meat scaffold fabrication helps reduce environmental pollution in the food industry [22, 23]. These biodegradable and non-toxic edible polymeric composite hydrogel films are based on natural polymers, specifically starch (potato) and κ-carrageenan, and a synthetic polymer, poly(vinyl alcohol) (PVA). Curcumin, a natural plant metabolite, is also incorporated into polymeric hydrogel films. Glycerol and succinic acid are added as plasticizers and cross-linking agents, respectively, during the gelation process [24]. Starch, a natural plant polysaccharide composed of amylose and amylopectin (with linear and branched chains, respectively), contains glucose units with three reactive hydroxyl (–OH) groups, allowing for various derivatizations [25, 26]. Upon heating in an aqueous environment, starch undergoes gelation, characterized by granular swelling, structure breakage, and solubilization. Starch-based edible polymeric composite hydrogel films are of significant interest in the food industry due to their natural polymer characteristics, biodegradability, and biocompatibility [27]. Kappa (κ)-carrageenan, a natural marine polysaccharide from red seaweeds, contains sulfated galactan with repeating disaccharide units, including d-galactose and 3,6-anhydro-d-galactose, linked by alternating α-(1 → 3) and β-(1 → 4) glycosidic bonds. It has one sulfate group per disaccharide unit, with the first sugar unit esterified to form d-galactose-4-sulfate. κ-carrageenan is widely used as a gelling agent, stabilizer, and thickener in the food industry [28]. Compared to natural polymers, poly(vinyl alcohol) (PVA) is a water-soluble, synthetic polymer prepared by polymerizing vinyl acetate and subsequently hydrolyzing the acetate groups. Its physicochemical properties depend on the degree of polymerization and hydrolysis [29]. It is generally recognized as safe (GRAS) by the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) [30], and the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) has evaluated PVA as a food additive used as a film coating agent for food supplements. The safety of PVA was confirmed by dietary toxicity studies, including a 90-day oral toxicity study [31] and a two-generation reproductive or developmental toxicity study in rats [32]. Additionally, genotoxicity assays, both in vitro and in vivo, have shown no evidence of toxicity at the dose levels of 5000 mg/kg body weight per day [33]. PVA is neither mutagenic nor genotoxic, and no carcinogenic activity has been observed [34, 35]. Consequently, PVA is used in film coating and food supplements in the United States, Europe, and Japan. Typically, PVA constitutes 1.8% w/w of film coating formulations [36]. The use of approximately 2% (w/v) PVA in fabricating the edible CSCP (curcumin/starch/carrageenan/PVA) composite hydrogel films is well below the no-observed-adverse-effect-level (NOAEL) in experimental animals. The incorporated natural compound curcumin is a bright yellow color compound and the principal curcuminoid of turmeric (Curcuma longa) from the Zingiberaceae family. It has well-documented pharmacological properties, including anticancer, antioxidant, neuroprotective, and anti-inflammatory effects [37]. In this study, curcumin is added as a nutraceutical, flavoring, coloring, and preservative agent in edible CSCP composite hydrogel films. Huang et al. [38] assessed the thermal stability and bioactivity of microencapsulated curcumin using porous starch for food processing applications [38]. Non-microencapsulated curcumin rapidly undergoes browning at oven temperatures above 160 °C, whereas microencapsulated curcumin retains its color and bioactivity at high oven heating temperatures of 150–170 °C. Additionally, the antioxidant activity of curcumin increases after oven heating [38]. Lee et al. [39] produced plant-based film scaffolds using alginate and corn starch for the culture of bovine myoblasts for cultured meat production [39].

The investigation on the impact of various additives of glycerol and succinic acid showed that hydroxyl and carboxyl groups of these additives can be used to prepare starch/PVA blend polymeric composite hydrogel films (Fig. 1). Tensile strength and elongation studies revealed that the presence of glycerol with three hydroxyl groups and succinic acid with two carboxyl groups enhanced the flexibility and strength of the polymeric composite hydrogel films [40]. The development of starch/PVA composite hydrogel films with glycerol and succinic acid exhibited a lower degree of swelling by influencing their molecular interactions. The incorporation of both hydroxyl and carboxyl groups found in glycerol and succinic acid improves the physicochemical and mechanical properties of the films. In addition, drying films at low temperatures may enhance hydrogen bonding [41, 42].Fig. 1Schematic diagram of the fabrication of edible CSCP composite hydrogel films

Characterization of CSCP composite hydrogel films

UV–Vis spectroscopy and X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis

UV–Vis diffuse reflectance spectra (DRS) absorption spectra provide insights into the optical properties and electronic transitions of solid materials [43]. Figure 2a shows UV–Vis DRS absorption spectra of CSCP-1 and -2 samples along with their individual components, such as potato starch, κ-carrageenan, PVA, and curcumin, ranging between 200 and 1100 nm regions. Both potato starch and carrageenan do not show significant absorption in the visible region and a broad, weak absorption in the UV region, which is due to the presence of polysaccharide structure, lacking conjugated systems [44, 45]. However, PVA showed relatively low absorbance in the visible region and significantly increased absorption around the UV regions of 200–300 nm due to the inherent electronic transitions within the PVA molecule. The maximum absorption peaks (λmax) at 220, 275, and 320 nm belong to the π ← π* electronic transitions, namely syndiotactic-PVA, atactic-PVA, and isotactic-PVA, respectively [46, 47]. Curcumin exhibits significant absorbance in the UV and visible regions; the absorbance peak at 470 nm corresponds to the π → π* electronic transition in the conjugated system of curcumin, whereas the peak at 328 nm is attributed to the n → π* transition arising from the non-bonding oxygen electrons, which are excited to the π* orbital [48]. The presence of curcumin in the polymeric mixture can improve the UV-barrier properties of the composite hydrogel film [49]. Nevertheless, both CSCP-1 and -2 polymeric composite hydrogel films showed weak absorption along the entire visible region and a broad absorption peak, which concurred with the presence of all components in the polymeric mixtures. The higher starch content in the polymeric composite of CSCP-2 hydrogel films appears to improve the optical properties compared to CSCP-1 films, particularly in the visible region [50]. The increased intensity of UV–vis absorbance in the polymeric composites compared to the individual components of potato starch, carrageenan, and PVA shows that curcumin interacts well with the polymeric matrix in enhancing UV absorbance centered at 350 nm with proper distribution in the polymeric matrices of the composites. Along with the application of edible films, these CSCP composite hydrogel films with UV-absorbing properties can be used in the fabrication of UV-barrier, environmentally friendly protective coatings, and various packaging applications. [51] demonstrated the eco-friendly strategy in the development of water-resistant, UV-blocking, and thermally stable composite films using oxidized hydroxypropyl starch and carrageenan [51].Fig. 2A UV–vis diffuse reflectance spectra (DRS), and B X-ray diffraction patterns of edible CSCP composite hydrogel films a CSCP-1 and b CSCP-2, c potato starch, d κ-carrageenan, e PVA, and f curcumin

The X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis is used to analyze the crystalline and amorphous nature of various composite materials and other components by measuring their diffraction pattern [52]. Figure 2b shows the XRD patterns of edible CSCP-1 and -2 composite hydrogel films and their individual components. The XRD pattern of potato starch shows broad peaks at low angles of 10–25°, which indicates the semi-crystalline nature with both amorphous and crystalline phases, whereas carrageenan is mostly amorphous with weak diffraction peaks [53, 54]. In the case of the PVA spectrum, it shows a strong peak around 20°, indicating a semi-crystalline nature [55]. Curcumin spectra show multiple sharp peaks with a high crystallinity nature [56]. The XRD patterns of CSCP composite hydrogel films show that both CSCP-1 and -2 films exhibited decreased sharp peaks, thereby reducing crystallinity, suggesting the interaction of components. The increased potato starch concentration in the CSCP-2 films exhibited a more amorphous nature by disrupting the crystallinity than in CSCP-1 films [57]. The amorphous nature of these CSCP composite hydrogel films improves their flexibility and hydrophilicity, which are required in the fabrication of cultured meat scaffolds [58].

Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy and swelling ratio measurement

FTIR is an analytical technique used to identify and characterize materials by providing information about the molecular structure, functional groups, and chemical composition [59]. Figure 3a shows the FTIR spectra of CSCP-1 and -2 composite hydrogel films along with the polymers of potato starch, carrageenan, and PVA, as well as curcumin. Potato starch shows peaks at 3200–3600 cm− 1 belonging to the –OH stretching, indicating hydrophilic groups. The peak around 1150 cm− 1 is assigned to C–O–C stretching vibrations of the glycosidic linkage. The peak at 1638 cm− 1 is due to the O–H bending or H2O deformation, and the peak around 995 cm− 1 belongs to the C–OH stretch vibration, and the peak at 850 cm− 1 is attributed to the ring mode stretching vibration of CH2–O–CH2 bonds in starch [60]. The κ-carrageenan spectrum shows a broad peak around 3200–3400 cm− 1 due to –OH groups stretching, and the peaks near 1220 and 1030 cm− 1 are attributed to S=O stretching in sulfate groups. The peak at 920 cm− 1 belongs to the C–O–C stretching vibration of 3,6-anhydro-d-galactose, whereas 840 cm− 1 belongs to the C–O–SO3− stretching vibration of galactose-4-sulfate [61, 62]. PVA shows absorption slight broad peaks at 3340 cm− 1 belonging to the –OH stretching from hydroxyl groups, 2919 cm− 1 is due to the C–H stretching from aliphatic CH2 and CH3 groups, 1720 cm− 1 is due to the C=O stretching of the acetate group from the residual polyvinyl acetate, 1422 cm− 1 is due to the C–H bending from the CH2 group in the polymer backbone, 1375 cm− 1 is due to the C–H wagging of CH2 and CH3 groups, 1235 cm− 1 is due to the C–O stretching in alcohol groups, 1070 cm− 1 is due to the C–O stretching of the PVA backbone, and 835 cm− 1 is due to the CH2 bending vibrations [63]. Curcumin shows characteristic peaks at 3500 cm− 1 (O–H stretching), 1628 cm− 1 (C = O stretching vibration of the β-diketone moiety), 1597 cm− 1 (C = C stretching vibrations in the benzene rings), 1428 cm− 1 (C–H bending vibrations), 1278 cm−1 (aromatic C–O stretching), as well as 1024 cm−1 (C–O stretching vibration of the enolic group) [64]. Both CSCP-1 and -2 composite hydrogel films show strong peaks at 3330 cm− 1, indicating the –OH stretching from hydrophilic groups, and 2920 cm− 1, indicating the C–H stretching vibrations of CH2 groups. The peak at 1720 cm− 1 could have arisen due to the oxidation of starch, forming carboxyl (–COOH) and ester (–COO–) groups exhibiting strong C=O stretching. The intensity of the 1720 cm− 1 peak with CSCP-2 films is higher than with CSCP-1 films, which shows that more potato starch is involved in the additional chemical interactions and new functional group formation. Swelling ratio analysis determines the water absorption capacity and swelling behavior of CSCP composite hydrogel films, which is essential for their applications in various applications, including cultured meat production [65]. Figure 3b shows the swelling behavior of CSCP composite hydrogel films of CSCP-1 and -2. Initially, both films exhibit rapid swelling within 5 min, which is subsequently followed by a more gradual increase until equilibrium is attained. After 12 h, the swelling ratio of CSCP-1 stabilizes at approximately 1.15, whereas CSCP-2 films stabilize at a slightly lower value of approximately 1.05. The rapid initial swelling is attributed to the presence of sulfated polysaccharide carrageenan and its ionic nature [66]. While an increased concentration of potato starch in CSCP-2 films should be more hydrophilic due to the enhanced amorphicity, the reduced swelling is likely due to the increased cross-linking density within its polymeric network, which diminishes the free volume available for water absorption [67]. Furthermore, the increased hydrogen bonding between starch and PVA polymeric chains in CSCP-2 films limits the interaction, uptake, and swelling behavior of water molecules within the polymeric hydrogel film. Liu et al. [68] showed that the PVA and starch mixture decreased water absorption compared to pure PVA due to hydrogen bonding between PVA and starch, due to the decrease in the number of free hydroxyl groups [68].

Field emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM) analysis

FE-SEM shows high-resolution imaging to assess the surface morphology and cross-sectional structure of biomaterials [69]. Figure 4 shows the surface morphology, topography, and cross-sectional images of both CSCP-1 and -2 composite hydrogel films. The CSCP-1 films, which have a lower starch concentration in the polymeric matrix, exhibit a homogenous, smooth surface with uniformly dispersed starch molecules throughout the film structure with minimal defects. In contrast, the CSCP-2 films, characterized by a higher starch concentration, show greater variability and structural defects attributed to the flocculation or aggregation of starch molecules resulting from improper blending with other polymers. The cross-sectional SEM micrographs of the CSCP-1 and -2 hydrogel films (Figures E–H) revealed well-defined and homogeneous structures with consistent thickness across the sections (dotted lines). Both films exhibited compact morphologies without any evidence of open or closed pores, interconnected channels, or phase separation. No cracks, wrinkles, or fractures were observed, indicating that the fabrication process preserved the structural integrity of the films. In vitro degradation and water contact angle measurement

The degradation of CSCP composite hydrogel films study evaluates the functional performance of these biomaterials as scaffolds for cultured meat production. It also describes the compatibility of the material with the biological systems and environment [70, 71]. Figure 5a shows the weight loss of CSCP-1 and -2 composite hydrogel films over several weeks. Both CSCP-1 and -2 composite hydrogel films exhibit rapid degradation in the first week, indicating a significant decrease in the percentage of remaining weight. However, the rate of degradation slows in subsequent weeks. After 4 weeks, the CSCP-1 films retained approximately ~ 50% of their initial weight, while the CSCP-2 films retained a lower percentage of around 40%. The increased weight loss in CSCP-2 films, which contain a higher concentration of potato starch, may be attributed to the enhanced biodegradability of these films. The higher starch content provides more sites for degradation, leading to greater weight loss [72]. Furthermore, the increased amorphous nature of the CSCP-2 films contributes to their accelerated degradation compared to CSCP-1 films, a finding consistent with the XRD results.Fig. 5A In vitro degradation and B water contact angle (WCA) measurements of edible CSCP composite hydrogel films

A In vitro degradation and B water contact angle (WCA) measurements of edible CSCP composite hydrogel films

Water contact angle (WCA) analysis is used to measure the hydrophilicity or hydrophobicity of the surface of the biomaterial by measuring the angle formed between a water droplet and the surface [73]. Several factors influence surface wettability, including surface topography, surface chemistry, polymeric type and composition, porosity, pore size and distribution, pH, and ionic strength [74]. WCA analysis was employed to analyze the surface hydrophilicity and wettability of CSCP-1 and -2 composite hydrogel films. The CSCP-1 film exhibited a WCA of 73.6 ± 4.5°, while the CSCP-2 film showed an angle of approximately 70 ± 2.1°. The surface topography of CSCP-2 composite hydrogel films is uneven, featuring more clusters and ridges, thereby increasing the surface area and hydrophilicity of CSCP-2 films compared to CSCP-1 (Fig. 5b). Increased surface roughness enhances surface area, leading to higher surface energy and improved wettability [75]. Furthermore, a higher concentration of potato starch provides a higher hydrophilic surface with more hydroxyl groups (–OH), which interact with water molecules, thus enhancing wettability and resulting in lower contact angles. A WCA angle between 60 and 90° indicates a hydrophilic surface with relatively limited wetting properties [76]. Pooja et al. [77] showed that both corn and potato starch biopolymeric films exhibited a WCA ranging from 32.11° to 48.73°, indicating that the films are hydrophilic [77]. The highly hydrophilic nature of starch is due to the presence of amylose chains in the starch molecules, which contain numerous hydroxyl groups along their chains. These hydroxyl groups can interact strongly with the water molecules through hydrogen bonding [77]. Consequently, the CSCP-2 film exhibited a slight improvement in surface hydrophilicity and wettability compared to CSCP-1 due to the higher content of potato starch.

Mechanical properties and PrestoBlue® metabolic activity assay

The mechanical properties of CSCP-1 and -2 composite hydrogel films were evaluated using tensile strength, which is a crucial parameter for cultured meat production [78]. The mechanical properties of the biomaterial directly influence the texture, durability, and overall quality of the artificial meat [79]. The texture of the cultured meat relies on the stiffness and elasticity of the scaffold structure, mimicking the animal meat [80]. The stress–strain (SS) curves were used to evaluate the mechanical performance of CSCP-1 and -2 composite hydrogel films. The CSCP-1 composite hydrogel film showed a maximum tensile strength of 20 MPa at 42% strain, whereas the CSCP-2 composite hydrogel film showed a maximum tensile strength of 2.13 MPa at 60% strain (Fig. 6a). Thus, the CSCP-1 film demonstrated higher tensile strength compared to the CSCP-2 films, while the CSCP-2 films showed greater elongation at break (ductility). Notably, tensile strength and elongation at break are inversely related [81]. With an increased concentration of potato starch in the polymeric matrix of the CSCP-2 film, the starch was unevenly distributed, forming clusters as evidenced by FE-SEM images. These clusters weaken the interactions between starch molecules and reduce the stiffness and rigidity of the polymeric composite hydrogel films [82]. Conversely, the amorphous regions within the starch matrices of CSCP-2 films, which are loosely packed due to irregular distribution, can stretch under stress. This increases flexibility and elongation at the break by enhancing the molecular mobility of the starch polymeric chains [83]. At higher starch concentrations, the polymer chains of CSCP-2 films tend to form a more viscous, gel-like structure with fewer cross-links, allowing free movement of starch molecules that results in increased flexibility and reduced rigidity [84]. In contrast, the CSCP-1 films, which have lower starch concentrations, exhibit a more orderly arrangement of starch molecules with slightly higher crystallinity. This organized structure enhances the intermolecular interactions between starch and other polymeric chains, thereby increasing tensile strength (rigidity under stress) and reducing ductility (elongation at break) due to restricted chain mobility [85]. Therefore, the lower starch concentration leads to a more homogeneous and cross-linked network that is stronger but less ductile, whereas higher starch concentrations in the CSCP-2 films diminish tensile strength due to the disruption of the crystalline regions by heat treatment, resulting in greater amorphous areas that improve flexibility and elasticity.Fig. 6A Stress–strain curves, and B PrestoBlue assay of metabolic activity of bovine muscle satellite cells (MuSCs) of edible CSCP composite hydrogel films

A Stress–strain curves, and B PrestoBlue assay of metabolic activity of bovine muscle satellite cells (MuSCs) of edible CSCP composite hydrogel films

The metabolic activity of bovine muscle satellite cells (MuSCs) on CSCP-1 and -2 composite hydrogel films over several weeks was evaluated using the PrestoBlue assay [86]. The metabolic activity of MuSCs on biomaterials is correlated to the viability of cells, enzyme activity, ATP production, and glucose utilization over time [87]. Generally, in CSCP-1 films with a homogenous surface and composition, consistent nutrient and oxygen diffusion resulted in sustained cell proliferation and survival. However, CSCP-2 films, due to the irregular surface topography and aggregation (structural irregularities), lead to inconsistent nutrient distribution, causing localized cell stress or inhibition of metabolic pathways, leading to lower cell viability or enzymatic activity over time [88]. On Day 7, the composition and surface topography of the CSCP-1 film show slow early-stage growth lower than that of CSCP-2 films due to low cell adhesion and proliferation. On Day 14, there was a gradual increase in the metabolic activity of the proliferating, surviving, and migrating cells over time for both films, with the same pattern, which is in agreement with the higher surface hydrophilicity (lower WCA) of CSCP-2 films [89]. In contrast, there was a decrease in the metabolic activity of CSCP-2 films between days 14 and 21 compared to CSCP-1 films, which is attributed to the insufficient supply of nutrients and oxygen to the multilayered cells formed on the surface of CSCP-2 films (Fig. 6b). This relationship between higher potato starch content and the metabolic activity of MuSCs could be due to the surface topography, morphology, and composition of CSCP composite hydrogel films [90]. In contrast, the control group on all days shows increased cell metabolic activity over time, which confirms that the 2D culture plate provides more favorable and optimal growth conditions for MuSCs in the 2D culture conditions compared to the CSCP composite hydrogel films due to the lack of adhesion molecules on the surface of the composite hydrogel films [91]. However, these cultured films can be stacked to form three-dimensional (3D) meat-like constructs for artificial meat production.

Live/dead cell staining and fluorescence microscopy

The live-dead staining was employed to visualize cell viability, as well as the proliferation and survival rates of cultured mammalian cells on the surfaces of biomaterials [92]. Live cells exhibited green fluorescence, whereas dead cells displayed red fluorescence. On day 7, cell adhesion on the CSCP-1 and -2 films was observed, though it was comparatively lower than that of the control group consisting of 2D cultured cells. By day 14, there was an increase in green fluorescence intensity for both CSCP-1 and -2 films, indicating higher levels of proliferation and survival. Additionally, the migration of cells resulted in the multilayering of cells on both films. Increased hydrophilicity and a higher potato starch content may have contributed to surface topographies that promote enhanced adhesion and proliferation of MuSCs on the CSCP-2 films compared to the CSCP-1 films. However, by day 21, both CSCP-1 and -2 films exhibited a notable increase in cell proliferation, migration, and survival, characterized by multilayering and confluence, likely due to sustained nutrient availability and reduced cell crowding. Thus, CSCP-1 demonstrated greater cell viability (green color) over time when compared to CSCP-2 (Fig. 7). This difference in cell viability suggests that the starch-based polymeric composite hydrogel films of CSCP-1 favored a more conducive environment for cell growth and survival. FE-SEM analysis of cell-seeded scaffolds

Figure 8 shows the FE-SEM images of bovine MuSCs cultured on CSCP-1 and -2 films at 7, 14, and 21 days. On day 7, MuSC adhesion to the CSCP-1 films was lower, with dispersed adhesion and reduced cell–matrix interactions on the film surface, compared to CSCP-2 films. In contrast, the cells on CSCP-2 films exhibited filopodia with focal adhesion on the surface. This increased cell adhesion on CSCP-2 films may be attributed to the increased surface roughness and hydrophilicity of the film surface. By day 14, both films showed confluence and layering of MuSCs on the surface, accompanied by ECM deposition. However, visible cracks and surface irregularities in the cell confluence suggest incomplete confluence due to stress conditions. These observations indicate that while cells were proliferating and forming layers, the structural integrity of the cell layer was compromised, possibly due to mechanical stress or nutrient limitations [93]. Moreover, on day 21, there was multilayering of MuSCs on the surface of both films, along with increased ECM deposition. Conclusions

In conclusion, the preparation of CSCP-1 and -2 edible polymeric composite hydrogel films, composed of potato starch, κ-carrageenan, and poly(vinyl alcohol) incorporating curcumin (CSCP), offers a promising and sustainable scaffold for bovine muscle satellite cells (MuSCs) culture, which is essential for artificial meat production. The increased starch content in CSCP-2 hydrogel films enhances optical transparency in the visible range but reduces mechanical strength. Surface characterization indicates that CSCP-2 films possess a rougher, more heterogeneous morphology with a higher surface area and hydrophilicity compared to the smoother, more uniform CSCP-1 films. Water contact angle measurements confirm slightly improved wettability in CSCP-2 (∼70°) relative to CSCP-1 (73.6°). Despite the increased hydrophilicity, CSCP-2 exhibits a marginally lower swelling ratio, likely due to higher cross-linking density and starch aggregation, which restricts water uptake. The metabolic activity assessment via the PrestoBlue assay over several weeks confirmed the cytocompatibility of CSCP composite hydrogel films, supporting MuSCs proliferation in vitro. Fluorescence microscopy revealed MuSCs proliferation and multilayer formation, indicating a conducive environment for cell growth.

However, certain limitations must be addressed. CSCP hydrogel films may limit nutrient and oxygen diffusion, particularly in multilayered cultures, which could hinder the development of meat-like thickness in the final construct. These factors need further optimization to enhance the efficacy of CSCP films for large-scale cultured meat production. Future research should focus on improving the mechanical properties, nutrient delivery capabilities, and oxygen diffusion of CSCP hydrogels to better support the maturation and differentiation of MuSCs, thereby enhancing the scalability and overall quality of cultured meat production.

Preparation of curcumin-incorporated starch (potato)/κ-carrageenan/poly(vinyl alcohol) (CSCP) blend edible polymeric composite hydrogel films

Edible polymeric composite hydrogel films were produced by solvent-casting methods. Poly(vinyl alcohol) (PVA) at a concentration of 2% (w/v) was dissolved separately in deionized water at 90 °C for 3 h. For the preparation of starch (potato) (S) and carrageenan polymeric blend, 3.0 or 5.0 g of starch (potato) and 2.0 g of carrageenan were dissolved individually in 50 mL of deionized water by stirring at 90 °C, with the addition of glycerol and succinic acid (SA) additives. Subsequently, 50 mL of the PVA solution with 0.01% (w/v) of curcumin was added to the starch and carrageenan polymeric mixture and blended at 90 °C for 2 h using an overhead stirrer. Morphological and mechanical properties of edible CSCP composite hydrogel films

The morphology of CSCP-1 and -2 composite hydrogel films was analyzed using field-emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM). Lyophilized films were mounted on aluminum stubs using carbon tape, followed by platinum coating through sputter deposition in a vacuum. FE-SEM imaging was performed using S-4800 FE-SEM (Hitachi Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) with a probe current of 10 µA and an accelerating voltage of 10 kV. The tensile strength (TS) and elongation (E) of the films were evaluated with an MCT-2150 instrument (A&D Company Ltd., Tokyo, Japan). The CSCP films were cut into dimensions of 1 cm × 2 cm (width x length) and subjected to tensile testing at a probe speed of 3 mm/min. The resulting data were used to plot the stress–strain curve [94].

Swelling ratio and in vitro degradation studies

The swelling ratio (Sg/g) and degradation of CSCP composite hydrogel films were determined following immersion in aqueous phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; pH 7.4) at 37 °C. Prior to testing, samples were preconditioned in ethanol for 48 h and air dried. Subsequently, films were immersed in PBS and allowed to swell for 16 h at 37 °C. At regular intervals (every 30 min for the first 4 h, then every hour for the next 12 h), swollen samples were carefully removed, excess liquid was blotted with tissue paper, and their weights were measured [55].

Isolation of bovine muscle satellite cells (MuSCs) and cell culture

Bovine muscle satellite cells (MuSCs; myosatellite cells) were isolated from fresh beef skeletal muscle tissue obtained from a slaughterhouse in Jeonju, South Korea. The bovine muscle tissue was sterilized with 2% povidone-iodine for 5 min, briefly treated with 70% alcohol, and washed with sterilized deionized water and phosphate-buffered saline (1X PBS, pH 7.4). Approximately 4 g of beef tissue was chopped, and connective tissues and fat cells were removed. The tissue was then minced using thin blades and incubated with Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) containing 0.1% collagenase, 1000 U/ml dispase, and 50 µg/ml gentamycin sulfate (antibiotics) at 37 °C for 1 h. The resulting cell clumps were mechanically dissociated by passing the mixture gently through a 30-gauge blunt-tipped needle. The cell suspension was subsequently filtered through 100-µm, 70-µm, and 40-µm mesh filters, and the filtered cell suspension was centrifuged at 400 × g for 5 min. Cells were then counted using a hemocytometer and cultured in T75 tissue culture flasks at a density of 100,000 cells/cm2 in a complete DMEM medium supplemented with 20% FBS, 5 ng/ml human fibroblast growth factor (hFGF), 10 µM p38i, and 1% antibiotic–antimycotic, in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2 at 37 °C. After 24 h, the culture medium containing non-adherent cells was transferred to a new flask and cultured for an additional 24 h. Using multiple pre-plating steps, non-adherent bovine muscle satellite cells (MuSCs) were enriched and further cultured on laminin-coated dishes (1 µg/cm2). After incubation, the confluent cells were detached by trypsinization using 1X trypsin–EDTA and collected by centrifugation at 1000 rpm for 5 min, resuspended in a complete DMEM medium, and counted with a hemocytometer [95].

PrestoBlue assay

As previously reported by Narayanan et al. [96], the in vitro cytocompatibility/metabolic activity of edible CSCP-1 and -2 composite hydrogel films was evaluated using bovine muscle satellite cells (MuSCs). CSCP films were circularly cut to a diameter of 11 mm, sterilized in a graded ethanol series, washed in DPBS, immersed in complete DMEM medium overnight, and placed in a 24-well plate. Approximately 5000 cells were then seeded onto the CSCP films and cultured in a complete DMEM medium containing 20% FBS, 5 ng/ml hFGF, 10 µM p38i, and 1% antibiotic–antimycotic in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2 at 37 °C. At various incubation periods, the metabolic activity of the proliferating MuSCs was assessed using the PrestoBlue assay. Cells cultured on a standard 2D polystyrene dish well served as controls. For visualizing the morphology of the cultured and proliferating MuSCs on CSCP films, scaffolds with MuSCs grown for different durations were fixed with glutaraldehyde, freeze-dried after dehydration in an ethanol series, mounted on aluminum stubs, coated with platinum using an ion sputter coater (Model: MC1000; Hitachi Ltd., Tokyo, Japan), and imaged using FE-SEM.

Live/dead cell viability assay

Live/dead cell viability was used to qualitatively assess the viability of satellite cells on the CSCP composite hydrogel films. After culturing cells on these polymeric composite hydrogel films for various periods, the media was discarded, the cells were washed with DPBS, and 20 µl of live/dead solution was added. The cells were incubated at 37 °C in the dark for 30 min and observed under an inverted fluorescent microscope (Nikon Eclipse Ti, Japan) [97].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using a two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-test assuming unequal variances (Welch’s test) to assess significant differences between control and experimental groups, using GraphPad Prism version 5.0. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical significance was considered at p < 0.05 (*), p < 0.01 (**), and p < 0.0001 (****).
