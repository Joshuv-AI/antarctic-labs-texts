Microwave ablation synergizes with LPS to drive abscopal tumor regression associated with ferroptosis in HCC
Authors: Yajuan Sui, Jiahui Peng, Wenyi Meng, Renjie Li, Yang Kang, Lujing Li, Zuofeng Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryHepatocellular carcinoma recurrence after microwave ablation commonly results from residual micrometastases and an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment. To address these dual barriers, this study developed a combined strategy using ultrasound-guided microwave ablation alongside Klebsiella pneumoniae lipopolysaccharide. Through multimodal experimental approaches, the combination therapy induced interconnected therapeutic cascades: immunogenic remodeling via M1 macrophage polarization with anti-angiogenic effects; features of ferroptosis, including NRF2 suppression, glutathione depletion, and cytoplasmic lipid peroxidation; and Hsp70-mediated proteostasis counteracting copper toxicity despite modulation of the FDX1-LIAS-DLAT axis. Adaptive stress responses limited copper-dependent cell death, while prominent cytoplasmic lipid peroxide accumulation was observed spatially. This work establishes a distinctive paradigm combining localized thermal stress and macrophage reprogramming to effectively suppress post-ablation recurrence, transforming microwave ablation into a platform for systemic immunotherapy in hepatocellular carcinoma management.Subject areas: Radiology, Cancer

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) remains one of the predominant causes of cancer-related mortality on a global scale, and conventional therapies are often undermined by metastatic recurrence and microenvironment-mediated resistance.1,2 Microwave ablation (MWA), while achieving surgical efficacy in early stage HCC, fails to eliminate residual micrometastases.3,4 Although MWA-induced immunogenic cell death releases tumor antigens, the resultant immune activation is transient and incapable of establishing durable systemic antitumor effects.5,6,7 Clinically, infectious complications following percutaneous thermal ablation of liver cancer are relatively rare, with Escherichia coli and Klebsiella pneumoniae being the predominant pathogens isolated.8,9 Curiously, a subset of cases with concurrent infections exhibited potential for unanticipated therapeutic efficiency,10 characterized by significant local effects and better long-term outcomes (Figure S1). This paradoxical observation suggests that the associated robust immune activation—whether stemming from a subclinical bacterial presence or, as is more common, a potent sterile inflammatory response to necrosis—may potentiate the antitumor response. Our retrospective analysis (Figure S1) and literature8,9 identified Klebsiella pneumoniae as a predominant pathogen in this context. We therefore reasoned that its key virulence factor and a quintessential pathogen-associated molecular pattern, lipopolysaccharide (LPS), which is a known potent inducer of innate immunity, could be harnessed to mimic and amplify this thermal synergy. LPS, a key virulence factor of Klebsiella pneumoniae, demonstrates biphasic immunomodulation.11,12,13 Chronic LPS exposure sustains tumor-promoting inflammation, whereas acute administration polarizes macrophages toward antitumor M1 phenotypes via TNF-α induction and enhances antigen presentation.14,15,16 This duality underscores the utility of LPS as a strategic adjuvant to transfer immunologically “cold” HCC into an inflamed tumor microenvironment.17 We hypothesized that ultrasound-guided MWA combined with LPS would generate synergistic therapeutic effects primarily through a profound remodeling of the tumor immune microenvironment, polarizing macrophages toward an antitumor M1 phenotype. This central immunomodulatory effect is proposed to simultaneously amplify local oxidative stress (via ROS induction) and trigger specific tumor cell death pathways, namely ferroptosis and cuproptosis. Furthermore, the repolarized macrophages would contribute to suppression of tumor angiogenesis. This integrated mechanism aims to convert the post-ablation niche into a durable, systemically effective antitumor immune response.

This study utilized integrated in vitro and in vivo models to systematically assess the abscopal efficacy of synergistic therapeutic approach. The predictive mechanisms might be tumor cell death pathway engagement and immune microenvironment remodeling, bridging critical knowledge gaps in thermal immunotherapy integration. By investigating the LPS-mediated conversion of the ablated tumor microenvironment into immunogenic targets, our findings aimed to establish a robust platform for overcoming post-ablation recurrence in the management of HCC.

Results

Thermal sensitivity profiling in H22 and RAW264.7 cell lines

Thermal exposure induced divergent responses in H22 and RAW264.7 cell lines. Short time exposure for 10 min paradoxically enhanced H22 proliferation, such that the viability at 41°C was 117.2%, while 99.8% of control (p < 0.001). While extended 15 min exposure caused progressive H22 death (2% viability at 49°C vs. 99.8% of control, p < 0.0001) (Figure 1A). Particularly, a temperature of 45°C (27% and 5% residual viability of H22 and RAW264.7, p < 0.0001) or above significantly reduced the viability of H22 and RAW264.7 (Figures 1A and 1B). Another test validated the disparity at 45°C, revealing 26% H22 viability (p < 0.001) versus 11% in RAW264.7 (p < 0.001) (Figures 1C and 1D). Live/dead staining revealed temperature dependence from another perspective (Figures 1E and 1F). H22 cells exhibited incremental dead cell accumulation (red fluorescence) with temperature, while RAW264.7 showed marked lethality at ≥45°C (Figures 1E and 1F). Signal loss at 49°C suggested membrane rupture and dye detachment post-cell death. Therefore, based on reproducibility, 45°C for 10 min was selected for subsequent studies.

Synergistic induction of pro-inflammatory polarization by MWA and LPS

H22 cells exposed to LPS (0.1, 0.5, 1 μg/mL) maintained >90% viability at 24 h (96.2 ± 3.7%, 91.2 ± 7.7%, 98.2 ± 4.8%) and 48 h (103.2 ± 1.6%, 99.4 ± 5.0%, 97.0 ± 5.0%) without statistical significance vs. controls (p > 0.05), indicating minimal direct cytotoxicity of LPS (Figure 1H).

To investigate the interplay between thermal treatment and LPS on macrophage polarization, four experimental groups were analyzed. The concentration of LPS was 100 ng/mL, previously optimized for M1 polarization.18,19,20 MWA combined with LPS was associated with increased production of proinflammatory cytokines. TNF-α levels increased by 22-fold (663.3 ± 152.5 vs. 30.7 ± 12.7 pg/mL, p < 0.0001), while IL-6 levels surged 112-fold (817.3 ± 331.0 vs. 7.3 ± 2.5 pg/mL, p < 0.001) compared to the control group (Figure 2A). Interestingly, LPS group showed even higher TNF-α (813.7 ± 52.3 pg/mL) and IL-6 (1,425 ± 51.5 pg/mL) levels than the MWA + LPS group. The combination of MWA and LPS uniquely enhanced M1 polarization commitment, as evidenced by western blot (WB) analysis (Figure 2B). CCR7 expression was upregulated 1.9-fold (p < 0.01) and iNOS induction was upregulated 2.7-fold (p < 0.001) relative to the control group. Concurrently, M2 polarization markers were suppressed; ARG1 decreased to 0.4-fold (p < 0.001), and EGR2 declined to 0.7-fold (p < 0.05). In particular, MWA monotherapy did not significantly alter cytokine profiles or polarization markers. These findings suggest that dual thermal immune stimulation may contribute to macrophage reprogramming. All full WB images in this study are provided in Data S1.

Thermal-immune co-modulation triggers oxidative stress and hierarchical activation of regulated cell death in vitro

Combined MWA and LPS was associated with amplified oxidative stress and changes in regulated cell death markers. The indirect co-culture system showed that the viability of H22 cells was synergistically suppressed to 20.1% of control levels (p < 0.0001), significantly surpassing individual treatments (MWA: 51.7%; LPS: 49.7%) at 48 h (Figure 2D). Simultaneously, this process is accompanied by severe oxidative imbalance, characterized by GSH depletion and ROS overproduction. At 48 h, intracellular GSH levels dropped to 0.4-fold of the control group, from 32.4 μmol/gprot to 11.6 μmol/gprot (p < 0.0001), The combined treatment (MWA + LPS) led to the most severe GSH depletion, which was significantly greater than that caused by thermal treatment alone (18.1 μmol/gprot, p < 0.001) or LPS alone (16.33 μmol/gprot, not significant vs. control). (Figure 2E). ROS accumulation was time-dependent across the groups (Figure 2F). At 24 h, the MWA + LPS group showed a 1.4-fold increase in ROS levels compared to the control group (p < 0.001), while the LPS group (1.1-fold, p = 0.55) and MWA group (0.9-fold, p = 0.46) showed no significant difference. After 48 h, ROS levels increased dramatically. The MWA + LPS group was 6.9-fold higher than the control group (p < 0.0001). The LPS group showed a 1.6-fold increase (p = 0.59), whereas the MWA group showed a sharp increase (7.7-fold, p < 0.0001). In parallel, LPO measurements using BODIPY 581/591 C11 probe revealed a stepwise increase at 48 h (Figure 2G), with the LPS group (1.17-fold, p < 0.0001), MWA group (1.27-fold, p < 0.0001), and MWA + LPS group (1.55-fold, p < 0.0001). Collectively, during the above process, MWA combined with LPS triggered early ROS activation and peak lipid peroxidation, whereas, MWA monotherapy induced delayed but extreme oxidative stress and LPS monotherapy exhibited limited effects.

These changes were further supported by WB analysis. The combination of MWA and LPS markedly modulated ferroptosis-related markers, as demonstrated by the downregulation of GPX4 (0.3-fold, p < 0.0001) and SLC7A11 (0.3-fold, p < 0.01), along with upregulation of ACSL4 (6.8-fold, p < 0.0001) and PTGS2 (9.1-fold, p < 0.0001), as well as suppression of Nrf2 (0.4-fold, p < 0.0001) (Figure 2H). The observed alterations in ferroptosis-related markers (GPX4, SLC7A11, ACSL4, and PTGS2) are consistent with ferroptosis activation.

In contrast, markers associated with cuproptosis—FDX1 (0.3-fold, p < 0.0001), LIAS (0.4-fold, p < 0.0001), and dihydrolipoamide acetyltransferase (DLAT) (0.3-fold, p < 0.0001)—were uniformly downregulated following LPS + MWA treatment (Figure 2I). The reduction in DLAT monomer levels (0.4-fold compared to control) limited the substrate availability necessary for oligomerization, thereby impeding the formation of functional oligomers. This pattern suggests limited engagement of cuproptosis under these conditions. Moreover, the marked overexpression of Hsp70 (6.7-fold, p < 0.0001) appeared to primarily alleviate oxidative and proteotoxic stress rather than directly regulate cuproptosis, further supporting that the copper-dependent cell death pathway was not fully activated. Together, these results indicate that ferroptosis-associated pathways may play a prominent role in the observed antitumor effects.

Abscopal antitumor efficacy and systemic safety profile

Ultrasound-guided unilateral MWA combined with LPS intra-tumoral injection demonstrated significant abscopal effects in bilateral tumor-bearing mice (Figures 3A and 3B). The combination therapy group exhibited a 40.8% reduction in terminal contralateral tumor volume compared with the control group (608 ± 72 vs. 1,029 ± 150 mm3, p < 0.0001), with growth inhibition becoming statistically significant from post-procedural day 10 (Figure 3C). Thus, whereas MWA monotherapy failed to inhibit the growth of the untreated contralateral (left-side) tumor, the MWA + LPS combination elicited a significant abscopal effect, resulting in a 40.8% reduction in its volume. Physiological parameters remained stable across groups, as evidenced by <10% variation in body weight trajectories and hepatorenal biomarkers, without obvious abnormalities (Figures 3D and 3E). Histopathology confirmed preserved organ architecture across all treatment groups with no evidence of treatment-related damage or metastasis (Figure 3F).

Tumor microenvironment reprogramming mechanisms in vivo

The combinatorial strategy induced preferential M1 macrophage polarization, elevating CCR7 expression 2.0-fold over the control group (p < 0.0001), while suppressing the M2 markers ARG1 (0.6-fold, p < 0.05) and EGR2 (0.4-fold, p < 0.001) (Figure 4A). Cytokine profiling revealed LPS-driven TNF-α (2.0-fold, p < 0.001) and IL-6 hyperactivation (9.8-fold, p < 0.0001) compared with the control group. Similarly, the MWA + LPS group exhibited elevated levels of TNF-α (1.6-fold, p < 0.01) and IL-6 (5.3-fold, p < 0.0001). The inflammatory response in the MWA + LPS group was slightly milder than that in the LPS group (Figure 4B). Tumor-associated vasculature was predominantly localized to the neoplastic edge, characterized by an aberrantly branched and tortuous morphology (Figure 4C). Angiogenesis suppression was evidenced by an 81.4% reduction in CD34-positive vessel density (p < 0.0001) and a corresponding 0.5-fold decrease in CD34 protein expression (p < 0.001) (Figures 4C, 4D).

Ultrasound-guided unilateral MWA combined with LPS modulates distal tumor ferroptosis and cuproptosis in vivo

Unilateral MWA combined with LPS was associated with changes in regulated cell death markers in abscopal tumors, with ferroptosis-related features being more prominent than cuproptosis-related changes. WB analysis demonstrated that compared to the control group, potent Nrf2 suppression (0.2-fold, p < 0.0001) critically impaired antioxidant defenses through coordinated downregulation of GPX4 (0.5-fold, p < 0.0001) and SLC7A11 (0.6-fold, p < 0.001), which depleted GSH biosynthesis. ACSL4 upregulation (2.9-fold, p < 0.001) enhanced membrane LPO susceptibility, whereas PTGS2 elevation (1.7-fold, p < 0.001) amplified oxidative signaling (Figure 5A). IF localized ACSL4 to the cytoplasmic domains and revealed PTGS2 accumulation in the peri-nuclear regions, creating spatial hotspots for LPO (Figure 5C).

Cuproptosis pathway analysis revealed paradoxical suppression despite copper stress induction. FDX1 decreased by 0.6-fold (p < 0.01), blocking Cu2+ reduction, while LIAS depletion (0.5-fold, p < 0.0001) disrupted lipoic acid synthesis. DLAT monomer levels dropped by 0.4-fold (p < 0.001), preventing functional oligomerization (Figure 5B). IF confirmed cytoplasmic FDX1/LIAS attenuation and DLAT oligomeric aggregates, indicating protein misfolding (Figure 5D). Compensatory Hsp70 upregulation (6.68-fold, p < 0.01) established chaperone-mediated proteostasis, thus mitigating copper toxicity (Figure 5E).

These findings suggest that ferroptosis-associated pathways may contribute to tumor suppression, while Hsp70 upregulation may mitigate copper-mediated stress.

Discussion

To improve the outcomes following ablation therapy for HCC, we established a multimodal mechanism for HCC treatment combining MWA with LPS, integrating localized thermal ablation and systemic immune activation. The therapeutic efficacy of this scheme mainly stems from macrophage-associated immune reprogramming and redox collapse-driven ferroptosis.

In vitro studies demonstrated that combined thermal treatment with LPS significantly suppressed the viability of H22 cells. Thermal stress can induce intracellular protein denaturation and oxidative stress, thereby inhibiting cell proliferation and triggering cell death.21,22,23 Similarly, it exerts cytotoxic effects on RAW264.7 macrophages. These findings suggest that thermal exposure may simultaneously impair tumor cell viability and compromise the immunoregulatory function of macrophages. However, in vivo investigations revealed that MWA and LPS combination therapy not only achieved local tumor ablation but also induced systemic immune-mediated abscopal effects, significantly suppressing growth in unablated contralateral tumors. Several compensatory mechanisms may be responsible for this effect. Although thermal ablation eliminates most macrophages, LPS can activate the remaining cells toward the M1 phenotype, promoting tumor ferroptosis via pro-inflammatory signals and ROS.24,25 The critical role of macrophages as antigen-presenting cells in linking innate and adaptive immunity. The specific mechanisms include: (1) processing of phagocytosed and degraded tumor fragments into tumor-specific antigens (Tsas), which are presented to the cell surface by Major histocompatibility complex class I (MHC-I) molecules; combined with the costimulatory molecule CD80CD86, naive CD8 + T cells are activated to differentiate into cytotoxic T lymphocyte (CTL) 0.2 at the epigenetic regulatory level, the M6A reader YTHDF2 can accelerate the degradation of STAT1 mRNA by recognizing the M6A site of STAT1 mRNA, which may be a potential target for further research, inhibition of IFN γ-STAT1 pathway activation, while knockout of YTHDF2 enhanced macrophage antigen cross-presentation ability, promoting IFN γ and granzyme B production by CD8 + T cells.26 In vivo, MWA may recruit peripheral monocytes to replenish macrophages unaffected by heat, thereby preserving the immune responsiveness. Moreover, ultrasound-guided MWA minimizes off-target damage, and local LPS delivery limits systemic immune exposure.25 In vivo experiments further demonstrated that M1-polarized macrophages suppressed tumor angiogenesis via TNF-α-mediated endothelial apoptosis and IL-6-induced vascular senescence, leading to a significant downregulation of CD34 expression. This dual mechanism not only restricted the tumor nutrient supply but also established a self-reinforcing antitumor loop through positive feedback regulation, suggesting a dual mechanism of direct tumor killing and immune reprogramming. Notably, while LPS alone strongly induces M1 polarization in vitro, it fails to suppress abscopal tumor growth in vivo unless combined with MWA. This discrepancy suggests that macrophage reprogramming is necessary, but not sufficient, for systemic antitumor responses, which require the multidimensional synergy of MWA and LPS. Interestingly, MWA monotherapy did not significantly alter cytokine profiles or macrophage polarization markers in our models. This could be attributed to the transient nature of the initial pro-inflammatory response, which may have subsided by our measurement time point, and the potential concurrent induction of immunosuppressive mechanisms that counterbalance initial activation, particularly under sublethal thermal conditions. This highlights that the immunomodulatory effect of thermal stress alone is context-dependent and may require an additional adjuvant, such as LPS, to lock the immune microenvironment into a stably activated, antitumor state.

Meanwhile, the combinative regimen exhibited a favorable in vivo safety profile, with no significant alterations in body weight, hepatic and renal function, or histopathological integrity of the major organs, as evidenced by HE staining. These results provide a critical experimental foundation for further investigation of MWA and LPS combination therapy in HCC management.

Our integrated in vitro and in vivo studies revealed changes consistent with ferroptosis activation under combined MWA and LPS therapy, associated with redox imbalance and lipid peroxidation. These observations provide a molecular perspective on potential cell death mechanisms following MWA + LPS treatment. In vitro analyses examined the effects of the combined regime on ferroptosis and cuproptosis in H22 cells through multilevel assessments of oxidative stress, LPO, antioxidant systems, and key protein expression patterns. These investigations systematically elucidated the molecular mechanisms underlying the induction of programmed cell death by combinatorial treatment. WB and IF analyses further revealed significant alterations in ferroptosis-relative protein expression within unablated contralateral tumors in vivo. GPX4 downregulation impaired LPO clearance, while SLC7A11 suppression reduced cystine uptake, depleted GSH synthesis, and further inhibited GPX4 activity.24,27,28,29 These collective changes promoted LPO accumulation and ROS generation. Concurrently, ACSL4 upregulation enhances esterification of polyunsaturated fatty acids, supplying substrates for peroxidation, whereas PTGS2 elevation corroborates ferroptosis activation.30,31,32,33 Furthermore, Nrf2 downregulation compromises cellular antioxidant defenses, thereby accelerating ferroptosis progression.34,35,36,37 These findings suggest that the combination may modulate ferroptosis-associated pathways, potentially increasing susceptibility to ferroptosis.

Interestingly, changes in cuproptosis-related markers (FDX1, LIAS, and DLAT) were observed, but the absence of direct copper measurements limits conclusions about cuproptosis activation. The downregulation of these markers may represent an adaptive response to stress. Cuproptosis, a mitochondrial copper-dependent death process that involves DLAT, triggers proteotoxic stress and mitochondrial dysfunction.38 In the copper death regulatory network, DLAT acts as a core catalytic subunit of the mitochondrial pyruvate dehydrogenase complex, which is responsible for the regulation of copper death, it is the key effector molecule of copper ions (especially Cu+, such as Cu+ released from atom-dispersed s-Cu (I)-S sites in CuNTD) that triggers copper death. Its molecular mechanism can be carried out around the core link of “Copper ion targeted binding → DLAT conformation abnormality → oligomerization out of control → mitochondrial function collapse → copper death initiation,” and there is a synergistic regulation relationship with the “photothermal enhanced cascade catalysis” characteristics of CuNTD.39

Our findings demonstrated that FDX1 downregulation compromised Cu2+ reduction to cytotoxic Cu+, whereas LIAS suppression impaired lipoic acid biosynthesis, collectively resulting in insufficient copper accumulation to trigger DLAT-dependent proteotoxicity. DLAT, a core subunit of the pyruvate dehydrogenase complex, exhibited concurrent downregulation and oligomerization, a hallmark of partial cuproptosis activation; however, constrained oligomerization due to FDX1 and LIAS defects attenuated copper mediated cytotoxicity.40,41,42 Notably, in line with the findings of Zhu J et al., Hsp70 upregulation was observed in the MWA + LPS group, coinciding with reduced aberrant DLAT aggregation and enhanced M1 macrophage polarization, suggesting a potential role in stress adaptation and immune modulation.43 Ferroptosis-preferred cuproptosis underscored LPO vulnerability as a critical therapeutic target in HCC while highlighting the need for optimized copper delivery strategies to circumvent microenvironmental buffering mechanisms that currently limit cuproptosis efficacy.

Among current enhanced MWA technologies, nanosensitizers such as MOFs-Ni-Ru@COFs and Au@PMO@DOX-Lac, as well as non-ionic sensitizers like EF-DOX-Lips, mostly rely on the physical/chemical properties of materials to enhance local thermal effects or integrate chemotherapy and chemical ablation for “functional superposition,” but they do not directly intervene in the tumor immunosuppressive microenvironment and depend on complex nanomaterial fabrication; meanwhile, MWA-combined immunotherapy strategies (e.g., Gd-MOF@aPD-1@CM, sPD-1-15&15R@VNP) can activate systemic immunity but carry risks of immune-related adverse events or require sophisticated engineering modifications. In contrast, the MWA + LPS strategy described here does not rely on exogenous nanomaterials or systemic immune intervention, and its effects are associated with local thermal stress and LPS-mediated immunomodulation. MWA may induce tumor oxidative stress and create a microenvironment permissive for LPS activity; in turn, LPS was associated with polarization of tumor-associated macrophages toward an M1-like phenotype. This shift coincided with anti-angiogenic effects (an 81.4% reduction in CD34-positive vessel density) and changes consistent with ferroptosis in tumor cells (downregulation of GPX4 and SLC7A11), potentially mediated by cytokines from M1 macrophages. These observations suggest a possible feedback loop involving immune activation and ferroptosis-related pathways. Additionally, local LPS injection may limit systemic exposure, and the combination was associated with an abscopal effect (a 40.8% reduction in contralateral tumor volume). This strategy thus demonstrates unique value in mechanistic innovation (filling the gap in the “MWA-immunity-ferroptosis” mechanism chain), clinical applicability (easy integration into existing MWA workflows), and therapeutic universality (addressing both insufficient thermal effects and immunosuppressive microenvironments), providing a promising direction for the clinical translation of MWA.44,45,46,47,48

Limitations of the study

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, while we observed changes consistent with ferroptosis, we did not perform functional validation using ferroptosis inhibitors or iron chelators; thus, the causal role of ferroptosis remains to be established. Second, although cuproptosis-related markers were modulated, we did not measure intracellular copper levels or use copper chelators/ionophores, preventing definitive conclusions about cuproptosis involvement. Third, the contribution of T cells, particularly CD8+ cytotoxic T cells, to the abscopal effect was not assessed; future studies with T cell depletion or adoptive transfer are needed. Fourth, we did not evaluate other forms of cell death such as apoptosis or pyroptosis, which may also contribute. Finally, long-term efficacy and safety were not evaluated. Addressing these gaps will be essential to confirm the mechanistic insights suggested by our data.
