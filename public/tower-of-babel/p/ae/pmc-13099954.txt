Analysing cell death patterns to predict outcomes and treatment options in patients with high-grade serous ovarian carcinoma
Authors: Xiao-Ning Li, Li Wei, Shu-yi Wang, Yan-Kun Yu, Yeernaer Hazaisihan, Xiang-Ting Gao, Li-Juan Huang, Qing-Hua Meng, Yu-qing Zheng, Wei Jia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHigh-grade serous ovarian carcinoma (HGSOC) is a gynaecological malignancy that is often fatal. Poor prognosis of HGSOC patients is primarily attributed to concealed initial symptoms, diagnostic challenges, postsurgical recurrence , and chemoresistance. Distinct programmed cell death (PCD) patterns play a pivotal role in tumour progression, serving as valuable predictors for postoperative intervention outcomes in HGSOC. Additionally, they provide insights into HGSOC’s pathogenesis and the exploration of immunomodulatory therapeutic mechanisms. Transcriptome and clinical data were collected from TCGA-OV and the GSE26193 databases. We constructed an ovarian carcinoma death score intervention model using eight genes and machine learning algorithms based on 13 PCD modes (apoptosis, necroptosis, pyroptosis, cuproptosis, ferroptosis,entotic cell death, netotic cell death, parthanatos, lysosome-dependent cell death, autophagy, alkaliptosis, oxeiptosis, and disulfidptosis). Three molecular subtypes of HGSOC with different biological processes were identified using unsupervised clustering models. A nomogram was constructed by combining the cell death index (CDI) with clinical features, which exhibited high predictive performance. The correlation between CDI and immune checkpoint genes, components within the tumour microenvironment, and drug therapy sensitivity was analysed. After multiple dataset validation, the prognosis of HGSOC patients with high CDI was relatively poor. CDI and immune checkpoint genes were related to components of the tumour microenvironment. Patients with HGSOC and high CDI may have resistance to standard adjuvant therapy; therefore, targeting these genes could be a potential therapeutic strategy. Finally, we found that our model had better predictive ability than published models. We conducted a comprehensive analysis of 13 PCD patterns and established a novel CDI model, which can evaluate the prognosis of HGSOC and provide a theoretical basis for its clinical treatment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-42628-3.Keywords: HGSOC, Programmed cell death, Genotyping, Immune microenvironment, Drug sensitivitySubject terms: Cancer, Computational biology and bioinformatics, Biomarkers, Medical research

Background

Ovarian Carcinoma (OC) is a common and highly malignant tumor of the female reproductive system. It ranks second in incidence and first in mortality among malignant tumors of the female reproductive system1.Studies have confirmed that this is a highly heterogeneous tumour2. High-grade serous ovarian cancer (HGSOC), the most aggressive and common histological subtype of epithelial ovarian cancer (EOC), has an unsatisfactory overall survival (OS) rate of 30% (5-year survival rate) post-diagnosis. This makes early testing crucial3,4. The primary contributing factor to high mortality is the late stage of the disease at the time of diagnosis. In addition, the recurrence rate of EOC after surgery is high, which often leads to chemotherapy resistance5,6, requiring the use of biomarker diagnosis, treatment targets, and intervention measures for patients.

Heterogeneity within the tumour microenvironment (TME) significantly influences differences in intervention and treatment responses among patients with cancer, with factors, such as cell death status of the tumour tissue closely related to tumour progression heterogeneity, prognosis, and treatment response. Cell death occurs in two main ways: accidental cell death and programmed cell death (PCD). Accidental cell death is caused by accidental damage, and the molecular mechanism is uncontrollable, whereas PCD is a predetermined process of finely regulated cell death, including apoptosis, necroptosis, pyroptosis, cuproptosis, ferroptosis, endogenous cell death, reticulocyte death, dependent cell death, lysosome-dependent superficial cell death, autophagy-dependent cell death, intracellular salt-induced death, oxygen radical-induced death, and disulfide bond-induced death7–9. These subroutines eliminate cells in different ways, causing characteristic morphological changes, signalling cascade changes, and different immunological outcomes.

Recently, PCD has become a key cancer research topic. Avoiding PCD is crucial for tumours to escape immune responses and metastasise10,11. Various cancer treatments can promote tumour cell death12. Conversely, immunosuppressive cell death enhances the effect of immunotherapy13.PCD regulates immunosuppressive TME-induced swelling and determines clinical outcomes for cancer14. Apoptosis is the process of eliminating damaged or unnecessary cells to achieve the normal development and function of multicellular organisms. Withered cells die in an orderly manner, exhibiting coagulation, nuclear division, and nucleolar dissolution. The vesicles are swallowed by giant phagocytic cells, and adjacent cells are not disturbed, which does not trigger inflammatory reactions15. Apoptosis can prevent metastatic communication by killing cells in an inappropriate location16. Cell death is usually caused by certain pathological conditions, such as physical or chemical damage and lack of oxygen and nutrients17. This kind of non-programming cell death can prevent damaged cells from affecting healthy cells. The most critical feature of necrosis is the formation of necrotic bodies, which is a multistep process18,19. Cellular pyroptosisais a highly inflammatory cell death mechanism. It is an immune defence response caused by the body after pathogenic microorganism infection. It is characterised by cell swelling, cracking, and the release of inflammatory factors20. Ferroptosis is a regulatory cell death process induced by iron-mediated oxidative damage21. Cuproptosis occurs when copper forms aggregates by directly binding to the acylated components of the tricarboxylic acid cycle, leading to cytotoxicity and, ultimately, cell death22.

Endogenous cell death, a non-apoptotic pathway, involves one cell engulfing and killing another. This process is characterised by changes in intracellular structure without requiring apoptosis activation. The active invasion of living cells occurs in only living cells and neighbouring areas23. Regulated cell death (RCD) is caused by the release of neutrophil extracellular traps. The neutral granulocyte extracellular trap is a mesh structure released by cell release during infection or injury24. Stress-induced DNA injury activates PARP1-dependent cell death25. Lysosome-dependent shallow cell death is an RCD mode regulated by tissue protein (LMP) or iron and is characterised by the rupture of microwave shallow cells26. Autophagy is a type of RCD driven by the molecular mechanism of autophagy and is characterized by self-biting vacuolization27. It is related to multi-step solution degradation, which supports metabolic adaptation and nutrient cycling28. Saline-induced cell death is driven by the accumulation of salt in cells29. The characteristic of oxygen-free radical-induced cell death is that the caspase induced by reactive oxygen species is not dependent on withering, as in cell death. It has a unique injury induction pathway (reactive oxygen species), key genes, and pathways (KEAP1-PGAM5-AIFM1) that differ from other cell death modes, such as necrosis and apoptosis30. Disulfide bond death is a recently discovered form of PCD. The abnormal accumulation of intracellular disulfides (such as cysteine protease inhibitors) can induce disulfide stress and is highly toxic to cells9. In tumour cells with high SLC7A11 expression, the redox state changes, and the accumulation of disulfides destroys the normal binding of disulfide bonds between histone backbone chains, leading to the detachment of other cell plasma membranes and ultimately cell death31,32. Therefore, there is a close relationship between disulfide bond death and cancer33. The advancement in PCD research has led to the development and use of numerous related drugs in clinical practice. By regulating the apoptotic process, selective BCL-2 inhibitors (Venetoclax/Vitamin B/AbbVie) have been approved by the US Food and Drug Administration for treatment of lymphoma34. GSDME-mediated cell death is a novel antitumour immunotherapy method35. Additionally, inhibiting iron-deficiency anaemia can induce cell resistance to PD-1/PD-L136.

Previously, PCD has been shown to play a fundamental role in the development and metastasis of malignant tumours. It can inhibit the occurrence and development of tumours by mediating cell death and beneficial immune responses, and its highly inflammatory state favours the microenvironment for tumour growth and metastasis. If various forms of cell death cannot be tolerated, malignant tumour cells will not be able to develop further. Nonetheless, our grasp of the link between PCD and HGSOC is limited, with minimal in-depth functional studies on PCD in HGSOC. Thirteen programmed death-related genes were screened from databases to construct a predictive model for high-grade serous ovarian carcinoma (HGSOC). This model can effectively evaluate the efficacy of therapeutic interventions and patient prognosis, providing a reference for the clinical formulation of optimal individualized treatment strategies. Consequently, our study’s goal was to develop a clinical prognostic framework and elucidate genetic characteristics to foresee the outcomes of HGSOC treatment and its prognosis. Our primary objective was to discern the heterogeneity among patients with HGSOC and assess their clinical prognosis, aiding in the selection of suitable treatments and lowering the recurrence rate in these patients.

Methods

Data collection

From the GeneCards database (https://www.genecards.org/) and review articles, the manual collection of 13 key regulatory genes for PCD patterns was generated. In total, 24,435 genes related to PCD were identified (supplementary Table 1). Among them, there were 13,198 apoptosis genes, 590 pyroptosis genes, 989ferroptosis genes, 8522 autophagy genes, 745 necroptosis genes, 26 entotic cell death genes, 30 copperptosis genes, 36 parthanatos genes, 10 netotic cell death genes, 14 alkaliptosis genes, 438 lysosome-dependent cell death genes, 22 oxeiptosis genes, and 13 disulfidptosis genes.

This study was conducted separately from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database (https://cancergenome.nih.gov/). GTEx was used to download the transcriptome, mRNA expression matrix, corresponding clinical data, and somatic mutation data from 375 EOC-related tumours and normal ovarian tissue samples. Ovarian cancer microarray datasets (including GSE26193) were retrieved from the GEO database of NCBI, with the corresponding expression matrices and GPL platform information downloaded. After preprocessing the data and sorting out sample information using the “GEOquery” R package, GSE26193 was selected for in-depth analysis due to its sufficient sample size and relatively uniform sample staging. From the Gene expression Omnibus (GEO; GSE26193) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/), we downloaded the transcriptome, mRNA expression matrix, and corresponding clinical data of 107 tumour samples from patients with HGSOC. The inclusion criteria were a diagnosis of EOC, a complete expression profile, and prognostic follow-up data. We merged and summarised the samples from TCGA and GTEx based on gene consistency, then normalised them using the ‘limma’ and ‘sva’ packages in R language and converted them into TPM data. The R package ‘sva’ was used to correct the batch effects of the TCGA-EOC and GTEx datasets via the ComBat method, and a unified merged dataset was created. Subsequently, the merged dataset was normalized using the R package ‘limma’, which included probe annotation and normalization procedures. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was performed on the expression matrix before and after batch effect removal to verify the effectiveness of batch correction.The log2 converted sequence number data chip and clinical features of 107 patients with HGSOC were downloaded from the GEO Database (ID: GSE98281). The probe was mapped using the ‘AnnoProbe’ R package. To ensure accuracy, we calculated the average value of multiple probes using the ‘limma’ R package.

Identification of expression and variation levels of PCD-related genes

We prepared raw transcriptome statistics from 375 patients with EOC in TCGA-OV and GTEx databases and normal tissue from controls. Subsequently, the ‘edgER’ package was used to adjust standard screening to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs; p < 0.05 and |log2FC| > 1). We used the ‘maftools’ software package to explore the hidden somatic mutation information in the EOC. From the copy number variation (CNV) values of PCD-related genes, values > 0 were considered ‘increases’, whereas values as small as 0.2 were considered ‘losses’. Using the ‘circle’ R software package, various features of PCD-related genes were displayed in the land blocks of the circus.

Unsupervised clustering of PCD-related model genes

We used the negative matrix factor analysis software package for consistency clustering (CC) to identify previously unidentified EOC subtypes. The CC parameter ‘maxK’ was selected as ‘ten’, ‘clusterAlg’ was selected as ‘km’, and ‘distance’ was selected as ‘pearson’. This analysis was based on the expression of DEURG associated with prognosis. Subsequently, survival rate analysis was conducted to compare the intervention outcomes of the three groups of patients.

Construction of PCD-related gene markers

Univariate Cox regression analysis was used to evaluate whether these genes affected the survival status of patients with EOC. We adjusted the cut-off p-value to 0.01. The dataset was randomly divided into training and predictive testing sets. Furthermore, using the LASSO Cox regression method to construct the most suitable signature, we selected the ‘λ. min’ value (R package ‘glmnet’). Based on the median CDI, we divided the patients with EOC into low-CDI and high-CDI groups. We used the ‘stats’ package for principal component analysis and the ‘survival’ and ‘surviviner’ packages for Kaplan–Meier analysis to investigate the correlation between OS time and CDI in the training, validation, and overall sets.

Functional enrichment analysis

According to the median risk score, patients were divided into low- and high-risk groups. We used the ‘clusterProfiler’ R software package to identify potential biological pathways based on DEGs. Gene set variation analysis was used to analyse the different biological functions between high-CDI and low-CDI groups using the ‘c2.cp.act.v7.four.symbols.gmt’ database (R packages ‘GSVA’ and ‘GSEABase’). The filtering criteria were normalised enrichment fraction > 1, p-value < 0.05, and FDR < 0.25.The differential pathways were filtered according to adjusted P-value < 0.05 and |log2FC|> 0.25.

Independent prognosis of CDI and establishment of a nomogram

We collected clinical data (age and grade) of patients with EOC in TCGA cohort and analysed them with CDI through univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses. We combined clinical features (age and grade) and CDI and established a prognostic line chart using multivariable Cox and stepwise regression analyses. The nomogram was displayed using the ‘regplot’ software package. Calibration chart and decision curve analysis (DCA) were used to evaluate treatment effectiveness (R packages ‘caret’ and ‘rmda’). Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was conducted using the ‘timeROC’ R software package. A dynamic nomogram was constructed using the ‘rsconnect’ and ‘DyNoom’ packages to predict the probabilities of 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival. Calibration and ROC curves were used to evaluate the clinical treatment efficacy and discriminative accuracy of the column chart model.

Research analysis of TME and Tumor mutation burden (TMB)

We analysed the relationship among CDI, immune checkpoint genes, and immune cells. A generalised diversity selection combining (GDSC) scheme was constructed using oncoPrecdict to predict tumour drug sensitivity (https://www.cancerrxgene.org/). The tumour immune dysfunction and exclusion algorithm was used to predict the immune therapy response between CDI groups. We investigated the relationship between prognosis and TMB.

Tumour immune single-cell hub (TISCH) and Human Protein Atlas (HPA)

TISCH (http://tisch.compGenomics.org) is a large online database that focuses on the TME for single-cell RNA sequencing. This database was used for systematic research on TME heterogeneity across different datasets and cell types. Immunohistochemical data were obtained from the HPA website (http://www.proteinatlas.org/) for verification purposes.

Comparison verification of different queue characteristics

To test the stability of the signature, we replaced it with the GSE26193 and TCGA datasets. We searched PubMed for four models of HGSOC within 1 year37–40 and compared it with our signature.

RNA extraction and quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction analyses

Total RNA was extracted from the cells using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Extracted RNA was converted to cDNA using a miScript II RT kit (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany), and quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) was performed using a miScript SYBR Green PCR kit (QIAGEN). Quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) was performed using a QuantiFast SYBR Green PCR kit (QIAGEN) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. Unsupervised clustering of genes related to three PCD models

To better understand the role of PCD-related genes in EOC, we conducted CC of TCGA-EOC cohort based on the expression of DEGs (p < 0.05) and divided the samples into different basic molecule subtypes. We found that the differences between subtypes were most pronounced when k = 3, indicating that the 374 individuals with EOC could be classified into three types (Fig. 2E and F). We included 99, 36, and 239 samples for the first, second, and third types, respectively. The thermal image showed that genes were expressed at high levels in typing, such as PDEC1, PTGFR, and KIF26B, which were highly expressed in type 1; BCL2L14, ACACB, and TPH1, which were highly expressed in type 2; and WFDC12, which was highly expressed in type 3 (Fig. 2G).Overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS) analyses showed statistically significant differences in prognosis among the three subtypes (p < 0.05) (Fig. 2H and I). The third type was associated with a good prognosis, whereas the first type was associated with a poor prognosis. The immune microenvironment characteristics varied among the subtypes (p < 0.001, Fig. 2J). Additionally, there were significant differences in the immune cell infiltration levels (Fig. 2K). Among all clusters, the proportions of T-cells, CD8T cells, and NK cells in cluster 3 were very low (Fig. 2L). Overall, the consistency between the prognosis and TME features observed in the three gene clusters indicated the reliability and effectiveness of this classification.

Construction of prognostic genes as markers for the HGSOC

The 374 patients with TCGA-EOC were divided into training and validation groups in a 7:3 ratio. Single-variable Cox regression analysis was used to screen for survival-related genes. The 17 PCD-related genes were associated with survival rates (p < 0.05; Supplementary Table 3). The eight gene models were constructed using LASSO and multivariate Cox regression analyses (Fig. 3A–C). BCL2L14 and FMN2 were involved in apoptosis and autophagy. PADI2 was found to be an apoptosis, autophagy, and reticulocyte death gene. TPH1, RLN3, and SLFN5 were related to apoptosis, whereas SLFN5 and NBEAL1 were related to autophagy. The model was formulated as follows: CDI = (-0.2126 * BCL2L14 exp.) + (0.2103 * HIF3A exp.) + (0.2778 * PADI2 exp.) + (0.1760 * FMN2 exp.) + (0.4192 * TPH1 exp.) + (0.2326 * RLN3 exp.) + (0.2577 * SLFN5 exp.) + (0.7004 * NBEAL1 exp.). Based on the median CDI calculated using the above formula, we divided the 375 patients with HGSOC in TCGA cohort into high- and low-CDI groups. CDI was significantly associated with clinical features, such as fustat (live or dead), age, and cluster (Fig. 3D–F).CDI was not associated with clinical stage (Supplementary Fig. 2).

Internal training and external validation of the five gene feature prediction model

The 374 patients from TCGA-EOC were divided into training and validation groups in a 7:3 ratio. We compared the OS rates of patients with HGSOC with different CDI values. Our results showed that the survival rates of patients with a high CDI were lower than those of patients with a low CDI in the entire dataset, training, and validation groups (Fig. 4A–C). ROC curves were used to evaluate the predictive performance of 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS in risk-scoring models. The results indicated that in the training and validation sets, the area under the curve values of the ROC curve for the entire dataset were > 0.60, with a maximum of 0.745 (Fig. 4E–G). The GSE26193 dataset was used as the validation queue. According to the median CDI of the validation queue, 107 patients with HGSOC in GSE26193 were divided into two groups, and a high CDI resulted in a poor survival time (Fig. 4D). The ROC curve was used to evaluate the predictive performance of the risk-scoring model for OS at 1, 3, and 5 years, with area under the curve values all > 0.5 (Fig. 4H). Patients were categorised into high- and low-risk groups based on the median of their risk scores, with more patients dying as their risk increased. Additionally, FMN2, HIF3A, PADI2, TPH1, RLN3, SLFN5, and NBEAL1 were significantly overexpressed in the high-risk group, whereas BCL2L14 was significantly overexpressed in most of the low-risk group samples (Fig. 4I–L).

Establishing and validating nomograms based on clinical features

We conducted single-factor analyses (Fig. 5A) and found that the risk score was an independent risk factor (p < 0.05, HR: 1.72, 95% CI: 1.52–1.96). Multi-factor Cox regression analyses showed risk score remained an independent prognostic factor (p < 0.05, HR: 1.67,95% CI: 1.46–1.89, Fig. 5B). Overall, the model used in this study performed well in predicting the survival rates of patients with HGSOC. We quantitatively predicted survival probability and constructed a comprehensive risk score and clinical feature nomogram (Fig. 5C). Each change was rated based on the HR. By adding the total scores of each variable and positioning them on the total score table, we obtained the probabilities of 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS. The C-index of the permutation chart was 0.682, indicating excellent prediction performance for 1, 3, and 5 years. The calibration curve showed a high degree of overlap between the line of the nomogram model and the white reference line, confirming the model’s high accuracy (Fig. 5D). The decision curve analysis (DCA) curve demonstrated the clinical decision-making benefits of multiple models, with the nomogram model yielding substantial clinical utility(Fig. 5E). The results of ROC curve analysis showed that the column charts were highly accurate in predicting the 2-, 3- and 5-year survival in patients with HGSOC (Fig. 5F). Based on this, the nomogram showed a good predictive performance.

Differences between TME and TMB in risk scores

We investigated the correlation between risk models and the TME. The risk score was negatively correlated with NK cells and cytotoxic lymphocytes (Fig. 6C). Most immune checkpoint-related genes were significantly upregulated in the low-risk groups (Fig. 6D and Supplementary Fig. 3). Based on the median mutation burden of the tumour, we divided the 374 patients with HGSOC in TCGA cohort into high- and low-mutation-burden groups. The correlation among TMB, microsatellite instability, and immune cell infiltration is shown in Fig. 6E. Based on the median mutational load of the tumours, we divided the 374 patients with HGSOC in TCGA cohort into high- and low-mutational load groups. There was a difference between the two groups; the high-mutation group had a better prognosis than the low group after the intervention (Fig. 6F). Subsequently, we performed a joint survival analysis using risk scores and mutation burden. There was a difference between the high- and low-expression groups in the model risk score and the high- and low-expression groups in the mutation load (Fig. 6G). We assessed the relationship between the stemness index and the risk model. Results showed that the risk scores were negatively correlated with the stemness index (Fig. 6H). The high- and low-expression group risk scores can be used to distinguish different HGSOC molecular subtypes (Fig. 6I).

Correlation and immunotherapy analysis between human model genes and drug sensitivity

To evaluate the effectiveness of risk scoring in clinical treatment, we used the GDSC to analyse the sensitivity of the subtype groups to therapeutic drugs. The results showed that patients in the low-risk group had higher IC50 values for acetalax and WIKI4 (p < 0.001, Fig. 7A and B). The IC50s of AGI-6780, AZD6482, Eg5_9814, and PF-4,708,671 were higher in the high-risk group than in the low-risk group (Fig. 7C–F). These findings are of great significance; for example, the low-risk group may exhibit higher sensitivity to molecular-targeted and common therapeutic drugs. However, acetalax has demonstrated therapeutic significance in high-risk populations. These findings indicated that risk models can predict drug sensitivity in patients with HGSOC. Immunotherapy analysis revealed that the high-risk group had a higher probability of an inept response to immunotherapy and worse immunotherapy effectiveness than the low-risk group (Fig. 7G–J). Next, we explored the detailed distribution of risk model genes in patients with HGSOC through single-cell RNA transcriptome data (GSE118828). Discussion

HGSOC is a highly invasive female reproductive system tumour. Its incidence rate is rising annually, and it is the primary cause of death from malignant female reproductive system tumours. It is usually diagnosed at an advanced stage and has a high recurrence rate41. To our knowledge, this is the first study to comprehensively analyse 13 different PCD patterns, construct cell death features in TCGA cohort, and validate their excellent performance in other external cohorts (GSE26193). A nomogram was designed and established, including the clinical features and CDI, and the results showed good performance in various areas. Further, we found that CDI is related to the immune regulatory dose, TME, and drug sensitivity.

PCD regulates various mechanisms and plays an important role in biological learning. Recently, cell death has been established as an important anti-cancer defence mechanism and therapeutic target. Avoiding cell death is crucial for malignant transformation and tumour occurrence. Exploring the mechanisms and functions of cell death, particularly the form of PCD and the steps involved in regulating cell death, will help elucidate tumour development and anti-cancer treatments11. We created a signature with eight PCD-related genes (BCL2L14, FMN2, HIF3A, PADI2, TPH1, RLN3, SLFN5, and NBEAL1) as features that could be used to predict OS and disease-free survival of patients with HGSOC. BCL2L14 is associated with systemic lupus erythematosus42, colon cancer43, and osteosarcoma44. Studies have shown its overexpression in medullary breast cancer45. FMN2 is an important member of the formin protein family46, expressed in the central nervous system and tumours, such as parathyroid tumours, malignant gliomas, retinal cells, tumours, and chondrosarcoma47,48. A study on lymphocytic leukaemia found that abnormally high FMN2 expression may serve as an important carcinogen by promoting cell proliferation and malignant transformation49. In colon cancer, FMN2 is an important tumour suppressor gene closely related to the occurrence and development of colorectal cancer, serving as an important prognostic indicator50. It regulates tumour cell proliferation and apoptosis by regulating cell cycle inhibitors51. HIFs are complex proteins composed of heterodimers of alpha subunits (HIF-1 α, HIF-2 α, or HIF-3 α) and beta subunits (VII)52. The expression of HIF-3 begins with chronic hypoxia in the human endothelium. Reportedly, HIF3A expression is downregulated in papillary and lung adenocarcinomas53–55. PADI2 is an important member of the peptidylarginine deiminase (PADI) family56 and has been implicated in chronic inflammatory diseases and cancers. It is upregulated in various human tumours, including breast cancer, prostate cancer, hepatocellular carcinoma, glioma, skin cancer, and colon cancer57–62. There are six members of the human SLFN5 (Schlafen) gene family, with the SLFN5 gene being highly expressed in lung, kidney, breast, and pancreatic cancers and other tumour tissues63–66. TPH1 is responsible for 5-hydroxytryptamine synthesis in enterochromaffin cells. According to some study results, the expression of 5-hydroxytryptamine may be related to the activation of survival-promoting signalling pathways and the regulation of tumour progression67. The findings of Ge et al. demonstrated that the trp hydroxylation induced by TPH1 plays an essential role in prostate cancer development68. In addition, Zhang et al. found that RLN3 may be a key gene affecting the progression of hepatocellular carcinoma69. Liu et al. found that RLN3 is differentially expressed and correlated with prognosis in patients with KRAS-mutated colorectal cancer70. Compared with that in normal brain tissue, NBEAL1 was expressed at higher levels in glioma tissue for the first time, indicating its association with glioma (clustering)71. Some studies have reported its association with stroke, cerebrovascular disease, and hereditary breast cancer72–74.

We utilised PCD-DEGs to construct the prognostic features of HGSOC based on TCGA database. Each HGSOC sample exhibited different PCD levels. Based on the heterogeneity of this expression, we divided TCGA samples into three subgroups to display the three subtypes of cell death in question, which were closely related to HGSOC intervention. Our results indicated a short survival time in type 1. We further utilised machine learning algorithms to screen for PCD-DEGs related to HGSOC and successfully constructed a signature associated with HGSOC prognosis. We calculated the risk score for each sample and divided all the patients into high- and low-risk groups based on the median value. Survival analysis showed that patients in the high-risk group had relatively poor OS. The PCD-DEG mutual intervention prognostic model showed that stage, age, and risk score were independent prognostic factors for OS, particularly related to the high-risk rating of diseases based on the PCD-DEG assessment. Tumour cells survive because the TME allows them to evade immune monitoring and drug interference75. Theoretically, in the high-CDI group, the expression levels of checkpoint and antitumour infiltrating immune cells decreased, indicating an overall impairment of immune function76,77. According to our analysis, the high- and low-risk groups exhibited different immune landscapes, including immune cell infiltration and immune checkpoints. Furthermore, results showed that the low-risk group may have stronger immune activity than the high-risk group. Moreover, we analysed possible immune-related pathways, and the results showed a significant correlation between high- and low-risk patients in multiple immune pathways.

The research results of standardised therapeutic drugs in the GDSC database indicate that the low-risk group may exhibit higher sensitivity to standardised therapeutic and molecular-targeted drugs. However, AGI-6780 is therapeutically significant for high-risk patients. This study proposes a new approach for chemotherapeutic drug treatment in high-risk populations.

A difference exists in survival status between the high- and low-mutation load groups; the high-mutation group has a higher survival status than the low-mutation group. This is because the more mutations a tumour has, the higher the number of antigens it carries on its cell surface. Such tumour cells are more easily recognised by the immune system. Therefore, detecting the tumour mutation load of a malignant tumour makes it possible to predict the patient’s response to immunotherapy. In addition, its stimulation of the immune system can indirectly improve the survival prognosis of tumour patients. In our study, the low-risk group exhibited a higher TMB. A previous study suggested a close relationship between somatic mutations in solid tumours and immunotherapy76. The survival effect observed in the TMB groups with high and low expression remained consistent overall in both FDA-approved groups77, in agreement with our research findings. Despite the recent approval of anti-PD-1 accelerated drug therapy for solid tumour TMB pembrolizumab by the US Food and Drug Administration and progress in evaluating blood tests for TMB78, further development of TMB as a clinical biomarker still faces several challenges. We expect to incorporate composite biomarkers using multiple variables, and this treatment approach can be used for EOC. This integration rate improved the accuracy of prediction results and fully utilised the potential benefits of immunotherapy79.

Although our model showed excellent performance in the training and validation queues, there were still some limitations.First, the results were derived from retrospective public databases for bioinformatics analysis, which may introduce bias and errors. Second, the prognostic model was established based on a limited sample size; thus, more datasets and clinical samples are needed to validate the prognostic model, and further experimental and clinical studies are required to explore the functions of the model-related genes. Finally, the biological functions of some programmed cell death-related genes (TPH1 and RLN3) included in the model remain unclear in high-grade serous ovarian cancer (HGSOC), which is critical for subsequent experimental research. Therefore, high-quality studies with large sample sizes and long-term follow-up are still needed for further validation.

Conclusions

The PCD-related gene features proposed in our study provides a theoretical foundation and valuable reference for the research and clinical treatment of ovarian cancer. We conducted an in-depth analysis of the clinical significance and characteristics of the TME features associated with PCD-related genes in patients with EOC. We established PCD-related feature risk models, including BCL2L14, FMN2, HIF3A, PADI2, TPH1, RLN3, SLFN5, and NBEAL1, and conducted an analysis based on the clinical features to validate and enrich pathways for immune status mRNAsi, EREG mRNAsi, TMB, and drug sensitivity. Our findings highlight the critical role of PCD in ovarian cancer progression and its potential as a biomarker and therapeutic target. Further experimental and clinical studies are warranted to fully exploit the potential of PCD-related genes in ovarian cancer management.
