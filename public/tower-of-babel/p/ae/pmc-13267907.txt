Modeling the effects of flight itinerary on jetlag duration
Authors: Shuzheng Huang, Yu Sun Bin, Corinne Caillaud, Svetlana Postnova
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractStudy ObjectivesJetlag affects millions of travelers daily, yet influences of flight schedules on circadian disruption remain unclear. We aimed to quantify the effects of flight itinerary on jetlag duration at the group-average level and to identify underlying physiological mechanisms.MethodsWe used biophysical modeling to analyze how flight departure and duration times impacted jetlag across 55 296 simulated flights. Simulated flights spanned time zone differences, ΔT, from −12 to 12 h and departed between 00:30 and 24:00 with durations from 0.5 to 24 h. Two cases were compared: (1) free sleep and (2) forced wakefulness during daytime at the destination. In-flight sleep followed the physiological need for sleep.ResultsFlight itineraries affected jetlag duration for all ΔTs and determined the direction of circadian adaptation for a subset of ΔTs depending on behavior at the destination. The highest variability of jetlag duration was observed for ΔT >0 h, peaking at ΔT = 9 h. Overall, for 1 ≤ ΔT ≤ 9 h, jetlag was shortest for flights departing or arriving around habitual wake time and longest when departing or arriving near habitual sleep onset. When in-flight sleep was restricted, the shortest jetlag for these ΔTs was instead observed for flights arriving during the circadian day. This pattern reversed for other ΔTs.ConclusionsFlight departure and arrival times contribute to the variability of jetlag duration and can reverse the direction of circadian adaptation post-flight. These dynamics are mainly governed by light exposure and sleep timing during and around flights. Statement of SignificanceThis study provides a quantitative analysis of how flight itineraries interact with circadian and sleep physiology to determine jetlag duration. By simulating over 55 000 flights across all time zone transitions, we reveal systematic patterns linking flight timing with adaptation time and the opportunities to minimize circadian disruption through itinerary design and behaviors during and around flights. These findings advance the mechanistic understanding of jetlag and offer actionable insights for travelers and airlines to reduce jetlag impact through optimized flight schedules and personalized strategies. Keywords: jetlag, sleep, flight itinerary, entrainment, light, modeling, quantitative, circadian misalignment

Introduction

Rapid travel across time zones causes jetlag, a temporary misalignment between endogenous biological clocks and environmental and behavioral time cues [1]. Symptoms of jetlag include insomnia, fatigue, impaired performance, gastrointestinal upset, and mood alterations [2]. These result in reduced wellbeing and productivity and an increased risk of accidents in the days following the flight until adaptation to the destination time is achieved [3, 4]. Given that 4.5 billion people fly every year [5], reducing jetlag duration by just 1 day each could significantly reduce the number of fatigue-related accidents and improve productivity and health.

Studies on jetlag have so far focused on evaluating the effects of flight direction (i.e. eastward flights versus westward flights) [6–8], the number of time zones crossed [9], in-flight sleep quality [10], and the impact of interventions, such as scheduled light exposure and avoidance [11, 12], on the severity and duration of jetlag. The severity of jetlag is generally characterized by the degree of circadian misalignment, the number and intensity of jetlag symptoms, and the speed of recovery [2]. Jetlag is considered to be over when symptoms are no longer present, and the circadian clocks are entrained to the destination time zone, so jetlag duration is defined as the amount of time from arrival until recovery. The severity and duration of jetlag increase with the number of time zones crossed [2, 9], and eastward flights induce worse jetlag symptoms than westward flights [7, 13]. Adaptation to destination time after eastward flights is 50% slower than after westward flights [14, 15]. This is because eastward flights generally lead to adaptation via a phase advance of the endogenous circadian clocks, while westward flights lead to a phase delay, which is easier to achieve [16, 17].

The entrainment of circadian rhythms to the destination time zone is achieved through Zeitgebers [14], i.e. external time givers, including both photic and non-photic inputs to the central and peripheral circadian clocks. Light is the strongest Zeitgeber for the central circadian clock in the suprachiasmatic nuclei (SCN) of the brain [18], which controls the timing of most physiological processes and synchronizes the peripheral circadian clocks in other organs. Non-photic stimuli, such as sleep–wake and activity schedules, have a weaker but independent effect on the SCN, also contributing to circadian entrainment [19, 20]. Other non-photic Zeitgebers, such as meal and exercise times, entrain peripheral circadian clocks in the liver and muscles [21, 22].

Exposure to the local light–dark cycle at the destination is the main driver entraining the central circadian clock to the local time zone. However, the effects of light depend on the timing of light relative to the circadian clock’s phase [16, 17] as well as light’s irradiance [23] and spectrum [24, 25]. The time dependence of light exposure is characterized by a phase response curve (PRC), and the impact of light irradiance is characterized by a dose–response curve (DRC) [16, 23, 25]. Light exposure and avoidance, scheduled to facilitate either advance or delay based on the PRC, accelerate adaptation [11, 12]. Conversely, light exposure at inappropriate times according to the PRC prevents adaptation and lengthens jetlag duration [26, 27]. Flight itineraries and in-flight lighting schedules generally do not account for physiological effects of light on passengers’ circadian clocks, which may inadvertently affect jetlag duration.

The timing of departure and arrival is expected to influence the environment and behavior during and around the flight, including changes in light exposure and sleep schedules. For example, departure or arrival during the circadian night would require extended wakefulness and introduce light exposure during the sensitive part of the PRC. Conversely, it may be dark in flight during the circadian day. Such changes shift the circadian clocks and increase homeostatic sleep drive [1, 2], thereby impacting jetlag duration. However, the effects of flight durations and departure/arrival times on jetlag have not yet been systematically studied despite more than a century of commercial aviation and the large number of existing flight schedules, which may have different effects on sleep and circadian clocks.

The in-flight lighting of short- and medium-haul flights (flight duration ≤6 h) is generally aligned with day–night cycles; however, in-flight light levels are lower than typical light levels in daily life due to the low intensity of aircraft artificial lighting and limited access to outdoor light. In-flight lighting levels during long- and ultra-long-haul flights (flight duration >6 h) are generally low, averaging less than 80 lx according to Wan et al. [28] and less than 40 lx according to our own unpublished data. Depending on the flight duration and departure time, lower in-flight light exposure can contribute to a phase shift during the flight, which in turn impacts adaptation to the destination, depending on the time difference with the origin. Changes in sleep patterns during and around flight can affect light exposure and recovery from jetlag, as sleep is an opportunity to avoid light.

Considering the variety of light and activity changes introduced by different flight departure and arrival times, we propose that jetlag duration depends on flight itinerary. However, given the large number of possible flight itineraries and time zone crossings, the effects of different itineraries on jetlag are hard to assess experimentally.

Mathematical modeling has been instrumental in the systematic investigation of the effects of flight direction [29–31] and the number of time zones crossed [29, 32, 33] on jetlag dynamics and in developing effective interventions [26, 27, 34, 35]. However, a common limitation of modeling [18, 25, 28–32] and, sometimes, laboratory [11, 36] studies of jetlag is that they assume an immediate transition between the time zones of the origin and destination. This scenario does not accurately represent reality and overlooks the effects of light and sleep during and around the time of flight, which impact post-flight adaptation.

In this study, we use our model of arousal dynamics [37] to systematically study the effects of flight itinerary on jetlag duration. The model is calibrated to predict a group-average (or standard individual) dynamics and is based on the neurobiology of sleep–wake cycles, accounting for the effects of light on the master circadian clock according to the experimentally reported PRC and DRC. The model combines the sleep–wake switch between sleep- and wake-active neuronal populations in the hypothalamus and brainstem, along with the dynamic circadian oscillator that receives both photic and non-photic inputs, to predict sleep–wake times, circadian phase, and alertness. The model has been validated against laboratory and field data for young healthy adults, including sleep deprivation, forced desynchrony, and circadian misalignment [25, 37–40]. The objectives of this study are to (1) systematically investigate the effects of flight durations and departure times on the duration of jetlag and (2) study the effects of common post-flight behaviors (social schedules), namely free sleep and daytime forced wakefulness, on jetlag duration. The findings were then applied to characterize existing direct flights worldwide.

Materials and Methods

Model

A biophysical model of arousal dynamics [37] was applied to simulate sleep–wake cycles, alertness, and melatonin dynamics before, during, and after travel across time zones. The model accounts for the circadian phase response and dose response to light, including that of different spectra [25], and was previously validated against experimental data for sleep deprivation, shiftwork (field and laboratory), and forced desynchrony [37–40], as well as studied for stability and synchronization properties [41–43]. The full list of equations and parameter values was provided in the earlier papers [37, 40] and is included in the supplementary material for completeness. Therefore, we provide only a brief description here.

The model schematic in Fig. 1 illustrates the key model components and the interactions introduced by flight itineraries and social schedules. Both flight itineraries and social schedules impact forced wake times and light exposure, which, in turn, affect the sleep–wake switch and the homeostatic and circadian drives of the model. The circadian drive in the SCN controls the timing of melatonin synthesis, which is used to assess the circadian phase every day of the simulation and calculate the duration of jetlag.

Schematic of the model and the key interactions due to flight itineraries and social schedules. Both flight itineraries and social schedules affect forced wake schedules and light profile. Forced wakefulness is implemented by keeping wake-active neurons in the monoaminergic nuclei (MA) in the wake state, which increases homeostatic drive, H, and the sleep pressure acting on the sleep-active ventrolateral preoptic nucleus (VLPO). MA and VLPO constitute a mutually inhibitory flip-flop switch, so only one population is active at a time. When there is no forced wakefulness, the flip-flop dynamics are governed by the interaction of H and the circadian drive C originating in the suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN). Light affects the circadian oscillator in the SCN through circadian phase- and dose-dependent responses, shifting the circadian phase and melatonin profile. Circadian phase is also affected by non-photic inputs according to the sleep–wake cycle tracked by the MA dynamics. Jetlag duration is calculated from model dynamics when melatonin synthesis onset is entrained to the destination time zone.

Simulation of flights

Flights were simulated by setting light and forced wakefulness schedules during and around the flights based on realistic expectations. Wakefulness was forced for 3 h before and 1 h after the flight to reflect travel to/from the airport and the time spent at the airport, including for security and border checks. The light level during this time was set to 300 lx to reflect relatively bright airport lighting [46]. Wakefulness was also forced for 0.5 h at light levels of 80 lx at the start and end of the flight to simulate boarding and preparation for landing, which requires passengers to be awake. Sleep was permitted at all other times during the flight, following the physiological need for sleep. To isolate the effects of flight itinerary on jetlag duration, in-flight light exposure was modeled as a constant low-intensity profile of 50 lx across all simulated flights. While the real-life in-flight light exposure may vary depending on seat and flight duration, this level is representative of typical cabin illuminance during cruise ([28] and our unpublished data). Light was set to 0 lx when asleep. To investigate the effects of sleep disruption during flights, we repeated all simulations with forced wakefulness during the flights—these results are included in the supplementary material for comparison.

To simulate different flight itineraries, the flight departure time was varied from 00:30 to 24:00, and the flight duration from 0.5 to 24 h. Their increments are both 0.5 h. The time difference between destination and origin was simulated by shifting the ambient light profile and varied from −12 to 12 h.

Ambient light conditions and social schedules at the origin and destination

Two sleep–wake conditions were simulated at the origin and destination, which were free sleep and social schedules. For the free sleep condition, unconstrained dynamic sleep–wake cycles following the physiological need for sleep were allowed at the origin and destination. For the simulation of social schedules, forced wakefulness was applied at the destination from 10:00 to 17:00 to reproduce the real-life scenario where air travelers stay awake during daytime at destination. Ambient light at the origin and destination was set to 1000 lx during the daytime [47] (from 08:00 to 20:00, local time) and 40 lx during the nighttime [48] (from 20:00 to 08:00). During sleep, the light was gated to 0 lx.

Jetlag duration

Jetlag duration, , was calculated as the time between flight arrival time and the moment when DLMO was less than 0.5 h away from being fully entrained to the destination light–dark cycle. The nonzero threshold was selected because, in practice, people are likely to consider their jetlag to be over before complete entrainment is achieved [8]. To better present results, we round the duration of jetlag to days because people generally think of jetlag in days instead of fractions of days.

Feature band and band center

The feature band in the dynamics of jetlag duration was defined as the departure or arrival time that resulted in a longer or shorter duration of jetlag than other times, regardless of the flight duration. To identify the center of the feature band, we first filtered the duration of jetlag in each band and excluded the data that intersected with other bands. The mean of jetlag duration with the same departure or arrival time was calculated. For a given time zone difference, ΔT, the band center was defined as the departure or arrival time that caused the shortest or longest jetlag, depending on the total effect of this band. The band center across all ΔTs was defined as the median of the band centers for all ΔTs.

Identifying existing flights

The data of existing direct flights worldwide were extracted using the Aviationstack API (https://aviationstack.com), and 338 694 flight schedules were obtained in July and August 2024. Duplicate flights or flights lacking critical information, such as origin, destination, and departure and arrival times, were excluded. The final dataset included 326 005 flights. To align with simulation results, the existing flight data were binned into 0.5-h bins according to their flight departure and duration times.

Results

Jetlag duration depends on the flight itinerary

During stable, entrained, sleep–wake cycles, the model predicts a typical sleep onset time at 23:40, wake onset time around 08:00, and a dim light melatonin onset at 21:40. We consider two behavioral scenarios at the destination: (1) free sleep following the physiological need for sleep regardless of time of day and (2) forced wakefulness during typical daytime hours of 10:00–17:00 at the destination.

On average, a lower number of time zones crossed results in a shorter duration of jetlag (Fig. 2a, Pearson’s r = 0.88, p < .001). The Advance and Advance/Delay groups result in an average duration of jetlag that is 1.5 times longer than that of the Delay and Delay/Advance groups, 14 ± 5 days versus 9 ± 3 days (mean ± SD).

The spread of the distributions in Fig. 2a is the narrowest for the Delay group, with SD varying from 0.5 to 1.2 days and decreasing with the number of time zones crossed (Pearson’s r = −0.82, p = .0127), indicating the weaker effects of flight itineraries on jetlag duration for these flights. This is consistent with more than 75% of flights for this group delaying the circadian clock, Fig. 2c, which facilitates adaptation to the destination time zone. The spread is larger for the Advance/Delay, Delay/Advance, and Advance groups, with SD varying from 0.9 to 5.0 days, showing high variability due to flight durations and departure times. This is associated with a larger percentage of advance and no-shift phase changes during the flight, as shown in Fig. 2c.

Generally, the long upper tails (long jetlag) in the distributions in Fig. 2a result from adaptation via phase-advancing at the destination or the counteraction between the phase shift induced by the flight, Fig. 2c, and the direction of adaptation at the destination, Fig. 2b. The long lower tails (short jetlag) are achieved via alignment of the phase shift during the flight and the adaptation direction at the destination.

The shortest average duration of jetlag occurs for ΔT = 0 h. However, contrary to common expectation, it is not 0 days but 2 ± 1 days, with a range from 0 to 7 days. This is because the majority of all possible flights themselves result in either a delay (63%) or an advance (22%) of the circadian clock, with only 15% producing no phase shift, as shown in Fig. 2c. This phase shift introduces a misalignment of the endogenous clock with the destination time zone, which has to be compensated for after arrival. In most cases, this misalignment can be corrected within the first day after arrival (70%), so their direction of adaptation is denoted as “no-shift” in Fig. 2b. Other flights require more than 1 day to entrain. The long upper tail of the distribution at ΔT = 0 h in Fig. 2a is caused by flights leading to particularly large phase shifts in either delay or advance, which require several days to adjust.

The longest average jetlag duration of 21 ± 5 days occurs for ΔT = 9 h with a range between 4 and 49 days, Fig. 2a. For this ΔT, the percentage of flights leading to adaptation via phase advance and delay at the destination is 65% and 35%, Fig. 2b, resulting in a bimodal distribution of jetlag durations. The higher peak at 23 days corresponds to adaptation via phase advance of 9 h, and the lower peak at 16 days corresponds to the phase delay of −15 h. As seen in Fig. 2c, 68% of all flights at ΔT = 9 h produce phase delay on arrival, and only 23% produce phase advance. This means that some phase-delaying flights result in final adaptation via phase advance and vice versa, thus increasing jetlag duration.

This pattern of jetlag duration distributions is preserved when sleep is not allowed during flights while keeping free sleep at the destination, as shown in Fig. S1. The main difference compared to free in-flight sleep is the smaller percentage of flights resulting in “no-shift” response for ΔT from −1 to 1 h.

Existing flights worldwide

Of the 55 296 possible flights (48 × 48 departure/duration pairs across 24 ΔTs) in Fig. 2a–c, only a subset exists in the real world, e.g. having a 1-h flight for ΔT = 9 h is currently impossible. We identified 326 005 existing direct flights globally across all ΔTs with a mean of 13 584 ± 42 812 flights per ΔT, ranging from 121 for ΔT = 11 h to 208 960 for ΔT = 0 h (larger number for ΔT = 0 due to domestic flights). Once these are binned into 0.5-h bins by departure and duration for direct comparison with the above simulations, we are left with 8821 distinct itineraries across all ΔTs with a mean of 368 ± 243 flights per ΔT, ranging from 44 for ΔT = 10 h to 814 for ΔT = 0 h.

Figure 2d–f show the predicted jetlag duration distributions and phase shift percentages for all existing direct flights worldwide, assuming free sleep at the destination. Compared to Fig. 2a, jetlag duration distributions are narrower in Fig. 2d, and the upper tails for Advance/Delay and Delay/Advance groups are less dense, indicating fewer flights with very long jetlag. Same as the case of all possible flights, the duration of jetlag for ΔT = 0 h is 2 ± 1 days, ranging from 0 to 7 days, where 19% of existing flights result in no phase shift, while 58% and 23% of existing flights produce a delay or an advance of the circadian clock (Fig. 2f). The distribution of jetlag duration for ΔT = 9 h is still bimodal, indicating that the flight itinerary, together with the individual’s behavior and physiology, will determine the direction of adaptation and duration of jetlag.

Remarkably, 19% of all existing direct flights worldwide (60 991 out of 326 005) result in the direction of phase shift due to flight being opposite to the direction of adaptation at the destination (e.g. advance vs. delay), 67% of all existing flights have negligible effect on phase shift, and only 14% result in the same direction of phase shift during the flight as at the destination, thus reducing jetlag duration. This suggests that the duration of jetlag for at least 19% of all direct flights may be reduced by adjusting light and sleep schedules during and around the flight or at the destination.

Daytime social schedules (forced wakefulness) at the destination

Many passengers are likely to follow social schedules at the destination and stay awake during the daytime despite an increased sleep drive. We simulate this by keeping the model awake between 10:00 and 17:00 at the destination, which also ensures exposure to daytime light.

Existing flights worldwide

The subset of predictions for the existing direct flights is shown in Fig. 3d–f. Similar to the full set of flights, the jetlag duration for ΔT greater than 0 h on existing flights is lower in the presence of forced wake compared to free sleep at the destination, Fig. 3d versus Fig. 2d, and the proportion of phase advance due to flight is increased, Fig. 3f versus Fig. 2f.

Similar to the free sleep case, 24% of all existing flights (77 477 out of 326 005) result in the direction of phase shift due to flight being opposite to the direction of adaptation at the destination, 62% of all existing flights have no effect on phase shift, and only 14% result in the same direction of phase shift during the flight as at the destination, thus reducing jetlag duration. This suggests that the duration of jetlag for at least 24% of all direct flights may be reduced by adjusting light and sleep schedules during and around the flight or at the destination.

Cognitive performance

The reduction in jetlag duration for ΔT > 0 h in the case of forced wakefulness during daytime at the destination, compared to free sleep, comes at the cost of reduced cognitive performance in the first week after arrival. The larger the reduction in jetlag duration, the higher the number of cognitive lapses during wakefulness, Fig. 4. Across all possible flights and the existing direct flights, the worst reduction of cognitive performance occurs for ΔT = 7 h, with 5 ± 1.5 more PVT lapses observed on day 1 after flight under the social schedules condition compared to free sleep. This difference in lapses reduces with days since arrival, dropping to approximately 3.4 ± 1.0 on day 3, approximately 1.7 ± 1.8 on day 5, and is negligible by day 7.

Mechanisms of the effects of flight itinerary on jetlag duration and dynamics

The bands #1–4 that are observed in Fig. 5 and schematized in Fig. 6a are explained by the timing of light exposure immediately before and after flight, according to the light PRCs in Fig. 6b. Example raster plots are provided in Fig. S5.

For all ΔTs, bands #1 (arrival around 06:30, origin time) and #2 (departure around 09:00, origin time) are associated with the strongest phase advance due to flights. This is because they maximize light exposure in the phase-advancing part of the PRC on either the day of arrival (band #1) or the day of departure (band #2). Our simulations assume 3 h of forced wakefulness at 300 lx of airport lighting before departure, followed by 0.5 h at 80 lx immediately after departure. For the 09:00 departure, this means that 300-lx light exposure starts at 06:00, which results in a maximum phase advance according to the 3-h PRC in Fig. 6b. For the arrival, we simulate 0.5 h of forced wakefulness at 80 lx immediately before arrival and 1 h of airport lighting at 300 lx immediately after. For the 06:30 arrival time (in the origin time), the 300-lx exposure starts at 06:30, but the 1-h PRC in Fig. 6b does not demonstrate the maximum phase advance at this time. The maximum phase advance is achieved due to light exposure after the period of forced wakefulness, as it occurs close to the physiological wake time. This results in 1–2 h of extra light exposure compared to ambient and allows for maximizing phase advance as seen with the 3-h PRC in Fig. 6b.

Conversely, bands #3 (arrival around 00:30, origin time) and #4 (departure around 00:30, origin time) are associated with the strongest phase delay due to flights. It is worth noting that while PRCs are obtained for light pulses scheduled during periods of darkness, in our flight simulations, the extra light exposure due to flight is overlaid with ambient lighting. As a result, flights leading to light exposure after typical sleep onset or between sunset and habitual sleep, as demonstrated by the shaded areas in Fig. 6b, have a stronger effect because they add extra light. Flights arriving around 00:30, shortly after the typical sleep onset, enable the maximum phase delay due to the extra light after the typical sleep onset time, as seen with 1-h PRC in Fig. 6b. Flights departing around 00:30 allow for similar light exposure after the typical sleep onset but also increase the light in the evening before sleep due to the longer duration. The interplay with the ambient light in the evening is also the reason why bands #3 and #4 in Fig. 5 are broader and less well defined compared to #1 and #2.

The phase advance for bands #1 and #2 and phase delay for bands #3 and #4 are observed for all ΔTs, as these dynamics are determined by the flight itinerary and behavior around the flight. However, the effect of the bands on jetlag duration differs for different ΔTs because of the interplay with the direction of adaptation at the destination. Thus, flights within bands #1 and #2 result in the shortest jetlag for ΔTs dominated by adaptation via phase advance at the destination (1 ≤ ΔT ≤ 9 h) and the longest jetlag for ΔTs dominated by phase delay at the destination. Similarly, flights within bands #3 and #4 result in the shortest jetlag for ΔTs with adaptation via phase delay at the destination and the longest jetlag for adaptation via phase advance.

Discussion

Our study predicts, for the first time, that the duration of jetlag is strongly affected by flight departure and duration (or arrival) times. This suggests that flight times can be strategically selected to reduce jetlag duration. For flights that require a phase delay of the circadian clock, the difference across flights is relatively small. However, for the flights requiring phase advance, there is at least a 10-day difference between the best and worst itineraries in terms of jetlag duration. We find that the shortest jetlag for a given ΔT is observed when the flight departure or arrival times enable the largest phase shift due to flight in the same direction as the adaptation at the destination. Conversely, the longest jetlag is predicted when the largest phase shift due to flight is in the direction opposite to that at the destination. This variance in jetlag duration can be reduced by staying awake and getting exposed to light during the daytime at the destination (10:00–17:00 local time in our simulations). However, this comes at the cost of decreased cognitive performance until the adaptation is achieved because of being awake during the time when circadian drive signals sleep.

Importantly, we demonstrate that flight itineraries not only change the duration of jetlag but also determine the direction of adaptation at the destination. In conditions of free sleep at the destination, this is seen for ΔTs of [−12, −10], [−1, 1] [8, 12] hours, while for forced wake, the selection is narrower: [−1, 1] [7, 11] hours. The mechanisms of the change in direction of phase shift for the short and long ΔTs are different. For the short ΔT between −1 and 1 h, the change in direction is due to some flights leading to a phase shift that overshoots the required ΔT. For example, a −2-h phase shift due to a flight for ΔT = −1 h would result in adaptation via a phase advance of 1 h. For the long ΔT, where the circadian oscillator is already at the boundary between phase advance and delay, the phase shift due to flight pushes adaptation at the destination to be in the same direction as the shift due to flight.

These results expand on earlier modeling and experimental studies. Modeling studies [30, 49] predicted that crossing more than eight time zones, i.e. |ΔT| at least 8 h, can result in antidromic adaptation. In field studies, antidromic adaptation was observed for eastward flights of 8 and 11 h [50, 51]. In this case, the rate of adaptation through phase delay is still faster than through phase advance [50, 51], as also predicted by our model in the case of free sleep. It is important to note, however, that these studies reported the different direction of adaptation for different individuals on the same flight, not for different flight itineraries as in our study.

Light exposure immediately before and after flight strongly affects the duration of jetlag at the destination. We assumed identical in-flight light conditions for all flights: 50 lx light throughout the flight except for during sleep (0 lx) and 0.5 h at the start and end of the flight with 80 lx. This in-flight light exposure has a minimal effect on the circadian clock because we allow sleep during flight according to the physiological need for sleep and the light level is low. Conversely, wakefulness is forced for 3 h before departure and for 1 h after arrival, assuming airport lighting of 300 lx. When this light exposure coincides with the strong phase-advancing or phase-delaying parts of the light PRC, as shown in Fig. 6b, it enables the shortest and the longest jetlag durations depending on the alignment with the adaptation at the destination.

Practical implication from these findings would be to schedule eastward flights to depart or arrive in the morning in the origin time zone and to schedule westward flights to depart or arrive in the evening in the origin time zone. Using the origin, as opposed to the destination, time zone for this scheduling is critical because the circadian clock is typically still aligned with time at the origin during and immediately after flights. However, for any real-world translation, our modeling findings first need to be confirmed by field experiments.

Typically, recommendations for reducing jetlag include light and sleep schedules in the days before and after the flight [1, 52–57]. Our study demonstrates that jetlag can be further reduced by selecting flight itineraries that minimize jetlag duration for their destination time zone and/or adjusting light exposure during and around the flight to shorten jetlag. For example, if an eastward flight departs in the evening, which would typically be associated with longer jetlag due to delaying the circadian clock, the advice would be to avoid light before the flight by wearing sunglasses and staying in the areas of the airport with dimmer light.

The effects of suboptimal flight itineraries on jetlag duration can be mitigated by adjusting behavior and light exposure at the destination, especially the light exposure and avoidance around the time of core body temperature minimum [26, 27]. We demonstrated that staying awake and exposing oneself to light during the daytime at the destination (10:00–17:00, daily)—a common practice among travelers—reduces both the duration and variance of jetlag for eastward flights; however, this has almost no effect on westward flights. This reduction in jetlag duration also comes at the cost of impaired performance on the first days after arrival for all flights with ΔT greater than 0 h, with the effect continuing for up to a week for ΔT from 5 to 9 h. This is expected because forced wakefulness during daytime means staying awake during circadian low and trying to sleep when the wake-promoting circadian signal is high, resulting in reduced performance due to both circadian timing and increased homeostatic pressure until adaptation is achieved [1, 40, 58].

In practice, this means that air travelers should be more mindful when engaging in higher-risk daytime activities (e.g. driving) on arrival after eastward travel as the risk of accidents would be higher [59]. This reduction in performance may also be relevant for work-related travel or athletes who travel to compete and need to be at their best performance during specific times of competition [4, 60]. In this case, following the general advice of staying awake during the daytime may be counterproductive for performance [61, 62]. Instead, customized schedules are needed to optimize performance based on their schedule, as suggested in previous studies [3, 57, 63].

The average jetlag duration for flights with ΔT greater than 0 h and free sleep at the destination is predicted to be longer than that for the flights with the equivalent absolute but negative ΔT. This is due to the asymmetry in the circadian PRC to light [16, 17] and a larger maximum phase delay that can be achieved per day compared to phase advance [14]. However, this is not the case when wakefulness is forced during daytime at the destination. In this case, jetlag duration is predicted to be similar for both directions, but performance is worse on eastward flights. Together, these likely contribute to the common perception of eastward flights being associated with more severe jetlag than westward flights [7, 13, 64].

Consistent with previous studies, we show that the duration of jetlag increases with the number of time zones crossed [2, 14] and the shortest jetlag is observed for ΔT = 0 h [9, 32]. It is accepted that traveling within the same time zone results in travel fatigue, i.e. general weariness associated with any long journey, rather than jetlag, caused by circadian misalignment [65]. However, our results support the notion that jetlag, as opposed to travel fatigue, occurs when traveling within the same time zone. This was also previously predicted by one modeling study [29] and observed in a large wearable-based field study [8]. This is because passengers must adapt to the circadian phase shift resulting from activities during and around the flight, even if the day–night cycle is the same. The circadian phase shift could be reduced by selecting a flight that departs and arrives at the weaker part of the PRC or by manipulating the light exposure during and around the flight.

To allow for a systematic study of the effects of flight times, we made several assumptions that introduced limitations to our study:

First, we used standard individual (group average) model parameters for all simulations. These parameters describe the most common scenario corresponding to a young healthy individual with typical sleep between 23:30 and 08:00. For more morning types, the entire pattern in Fig. 5 is expected to shift to the left, while it would shift to the right for the more evening types. Other differences in individuals’ physiology, such as sensitivity to light or typical sleep times, would also affect the quantitative model outcomes. However, the qualitative results are expected to be the same.

Second, we assumed the same ambient light profiles at the origin and destination, thus neglecting seasonal changes in sunrise and sunset. This is justified because light exposure typically follows social schedules in urban environments [66]. However, future studies should consider the effects of seasons, especially since one field study showed that sleep time does not return to baseline even after 15 days at destination [8]. This may be caused by differences in light–dark cycles or social schedules.

Despite our systematic study of all possible direct flights with durations up to 24 h, this constitutes only a subset of possible flights and environmental conditions. In reality, there is more variability in flight itineraries (e.g. connecting flights), environmental factors (e.g. light levels), and individuals’ physiology, as well as sleep history and behavior (e.g. prior shiftwork). The effects of these factors and their combinations can be modeled and predicted, but a systematic study would be challenging. As such, it is more convenient to use modeling to make personalized predictions and interventions [38, 67–69].
