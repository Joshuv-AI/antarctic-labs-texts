Molecular markers associated with elevated colorectal cancer risk: a mini review
Authors: Marcin Szuman, Marek Kazimierczyk, Natalia Grot, Marta Kaczmarek-Ryś, Alicja Kryszczyńska, Iga Dziechciowska, Monika Knaur, Andrzej Hnatyszyn, Szymon Hryhorowicz, Andrzej Pławski
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractColorectal cancer (CRC) is the third most common cancer worldwide, with 70% of cases attributed to sporadic mutations and the remaining linked to inherited genetic predispositions. This mini-review focuses on low-penetrance genetic variants that modestly influence CRC risk, categorizing them by mutation type - single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) and non-SNP variants. Missense mutations in genes such as TP53, APC, CHEK2, and MUTYH are highlighted for their varying associations with CRC risk across populations. Additionally, silent mutations, untranslated region variants, and promoter modifications, such as those in PLA2G2A, XPA, and DNMT3B, are discussed for their potential, albeit modest, roles in CRC predisposition. Non-SNP variants, including deletions and insertions in genes like CHEK2, NOD2, GSTM1, and GSTT1, are explored for their frameshift effects and influence on CRC susceptibility. The review underscores the complexity of CRC risk, shaped by genetic, environmental, and lifestyle factors, and advocates for comprehensive, population-specific research to enhance genetic counseling and advance personalized medicine in CRC prevention and treatment.Keywords: Colorectal cancer, Cancer predispositions, Low-penetrance variants

Background

Cancer is among the most common causes of death worldwide, with only cardiovascular diseases taking more lives [1]. Colorectal cancer (CRC) is the third most occurring cancer, accounting for 10.7% of neoplasms and with an annual death toll of 935,000 (data from 2020) [2]. It is known that most CRC cases are sporadic (70% of cases), caused by spontaneous mutations in genes involved in DNA repair and tumor suppressing pathways [3–5]. Remaining cases are an effect of inherited mutations; 5–6% of cases worldwide being caused by well-known mutated gene variants leading to development of predisposition syndromes, both polyposis and non-polyposis, and 24–25% cases being a result of low-predisposing gene variants [6, 7]. Whilst the former, smaller group, has been researched relatively well, the latter is still not properly characterized, due to it comprising a much wider range of weaker associations. Such germline mutations usually do not result in any noticeable symptoms before tumorigenesis occurs, and genetic background and environmental factors modify the level of penetration of these variants. In this minireview we intend to present examples of most important low penetrance gene variants thought to influence lifetime CRC risk categorized by mutation type and shortly comment on the discourse surrounding this topic.

To find relevant gene variants, PubMed and Google Scholar databases were searched for papers investigating associations between CRC risk and gene mutations (original studies and meta-analyses) published between 1998 and 2024. We applied specific search terms, including “colorectal cancer susceptibility gene”, “CRC susceptibility gene”, “colorectal cancer low penetrance susceptibility allele”, and “CRC low penetrance susceptibility allele”. Then we proceeded to include only those variants we considered important, based on our judgement, to keep the paper in the intended mini-review scope.

Genetic variants

Single nucleotide polymorphisms

Single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) is a substitution of a single nucleotide at a specified position in the genome. It is widely agreed that such mutation must concern more than 1% of the entire human population to be considered an SNP. Vast majority of SNPs are not associated with any genetic disorders or their significance is yet to be known [8–10].

SNP variants - missense mutations

Nearly all known SNP variants associated with CRC risk can be classified as missense mutations - it means that resulting codon codes for a different amino acid and thus the protein product has altered sequence (Table 1). Depending on properties of new amino acid, this can severely affect protein folding and structure.

TP53 is the best known tumor suppressor gene, with its somatic mutations often leading to cancerogenesis - it was reported that nearly 30% of all cancers harbor TP53 mutations [11]. Its most analyzed variant is c.215 C > G (rs1042522, p.Pro72Arg) and it is reported that it might be associated with increased CRC risk [12]. On the other hand, it is believed to not be a CRC risk factor in the Iranian Azari populations [13]. A different tumor suppressor gene - APC - is known for its germline mutations causing familial adenomatous polyposis [14–16]. Not all variants will cause the syndrome, however. Polymorphism c.3920T > A (rs1801155, p.Ile1307Lys), prevalent in Ashkenazi Jews with Russian/Eastern European ancestry, might be moderately associated with CRC risk [17–20].

Genes responsible for DNA repair mechanisms are crucial for the proper functioning of cells and tissue. It is then not surprising that among cancer-associated variants, many are of those genes. CHEK2, a gene highly involved in DNA damage response pathways, can harbor a pathogenic substitution c.470T > C (rs17879961, p.Ile157Thr) known to increase CRC risk 1.5 to 2-fold [21–27]. A gene playing a role in the base excision repair pathway, MUTYH, is known for its variants strongly predisposing to CRC causing MUTYH-associated polyposis syndrome. Notwithstanding, many mutated heterozygous genotypes predispose very weakly, if at all. Ma et al. calculated the risk associated with c.22G > A (rs3219484, p.Val8Met) and c.930G > C (rs3219489, Gln310His) to be very low, with odds ratio (OR) of 1.17 [28]. DNA mismatch repair gene MLH1 also can harbor variants that cause strong CRC predisposition syndrome - Lynch syndrome [29–31]. There are, however, mutations which do not result in such high risk. Polymorphism c.655 A > G (rs1799977, p.Ile219Val) was found to moderately increase susceptibility to CRC in the Spanish population, both in cases of hetero- and homozygosity, increasing lifetime risk up to 3-fold, which is still a way lower value compared to Lynch syndrome but significant nonetheless [32]. Notwithstanding, a meta-analysis performed in 2015 did not corroborate those findings, but the study in question was not included in its database [33].

Most discovered polymorphisms are believed to be benign, whilst some are suggested to be pathogenic. Nevertheless, there also exist variants with a protective effect. The CYP1B1 gene’s protein product is highly involved in metabolism of drugs and endogenous compounds, and multiple known variants were investigated for CRC susceptibility. A study on Czech population scrutinized two alleles: c.1294 C > G (rs1056836, p.Leu432Val) and c.1358 A > G (rs1800440, p.Asn453Ser). The former’s influence was deemed insignificant, but the latter was found to be protective against CRC (in dominant model). A diplotype containing both changes was reported to have an even stronger effect, reducing CRC risk nearly by half, compared to wild type individuals [34]. A similar scale study on Polish population also examined c.1294 C > G allele, as well as two additional ones: c.142 C > G (rs10012, p.Arg48Gly) and c.355G > T (rs1056827, p.Ala119Ser). Variant c.355G > T was regarded as a CRC risk factor, and when paired with c.142 C > G the association was stronger. What is interesting, substitution c.1294 C > G (which previous study deemed insignificant) seemed to increase c.355G > T’s carcinogenic effect [35]. A recent study, meta-analysis conducted on Caucasians with a combined number of participants greatly exceeding populations of aforementioned studies (15- and 18-times, respectively), ultimately found no association of c.1294 C > G variant with CRC, neither negative nor positive [36].

SNP variants - other

Not all SNPs in the coding sequence change the amino acid sequence of resulting protein products. If a nucleotide substitution occurs and the new codon is synonymous (genetic code redundancy), the mutation is silent. It is believed that silent mutations may also be involved in cancer pathogenesis [37]. PLA2G2A, a gene responsible for inflammatory response and fatty acid metabolism, was investigated in search of variants associated with CRC risk. Variant c.132 C > T (rs4744, p.Tyr44Tyr) scored an OR = 1.17, after comparing heterozygous and homozygous genotypes to homozygous wild type [38]. The authors did not list this polymorphism as predisposing, but perhaps more research might shed new light onto it.

Untranslated 5’ regions (5’UTR) can harbor SNPs, too, and many such variants are reported to influence risk of cancerogenesis. This regards gene promoter sequences and other regions, sometimes of yet unknown functions. The already mentioned PLA2G2A gene can harbor 5’UTR polymorphism c.−859 C > G (rs11573156), but it was not proclaimed pathogenic, with OR = 1.20 [38]. XPA, gene responsible for nucleotide excision repair pathway, also had its untranslated variant assessed - c.−4 A > G (rs1800975). According to Ma et al., it has a weak protective effect [28], but a more recent meta-analysis stated it to be not associated [39]. A promoter substitution c.−579G > T (rs1569686) of DNMT3B gene is an example of a highly prevalent, pathogenic variant; 85% of the Chinese population possess it. It was proven to moderately increase CRC and gastric cancer risk [40–44].

Even intronic SNPs, not included in the final transcript’s nucleotide sequence, might present some effects. Allele c.444 + 1G > A (rs121908698, IVS2 + 1G > A) of the CHEK2 gene is sometimes implicated to raise cancer risk, due to it modifying the splicing acceptor site. It was investigated in Polish patients, but no correlation with CRC risk was found [23, 26].

Non-SNP variants

Other, non-SNP gene variants comprise mostly insertions, deletions and duplications. They often result in frameshifts - the reading frame gets disrupted and as result the protein product is characterized by different amino acid sequence and length. One of the most studied CHEK2 mutations is c.1100delC (rs555607708, p.Thr367fs), a single nucleotide deletion resulting in frameshift and protein truncation. It is predominant in families afflicted by CRC, and thus it was thought to be a major risk factor [45]. However, most studies found only weak or moderate link, whilst also lacking statistical power [26, 46–51]. It is most likely a low-penetrance variant, but more evidence is needed. NOD2, a gene playing a role in defense against bacteria, is also known for a CRC-associated frameshift mutation. Insertion c.3020insC (rs2066847, p.Leu1007fs), also referred to as a duplication c.3019dup, was reported to weakly increase CRC risk and is overrepresented in ulcerative colitis patients. On the other hand, comparing mutated homozygous genotype to wild type yielded higher OR values and so low-penetrance status is no longer applicable. Nevertheless, heterozygous genotype can be considered weakly associated [52–54].

Genes GSTM1 and GSTT1, involved in neutralization of some carcinogenic compounds from tobacco smoke, can be targets of large exonic deletions [55]. Such deletions result in null alleles and there is no protein product. One study deemed GSTM1 to be weakly associated with CRC risk, and the risk slightly grew if both genes were deleted [34]. A different study reported moderate to strong association for both homozygous genotypes, and estimated the CRC risk to be over twenty times higher (compared to wild type) for individuals with double homozygous genotype [56]. A very recent meta-analysis corroborated those findings, but only in case of Asian population [57].

Conclusions

Presented work is a cursory, concise look at gene variants reported to be associated with CRC risk and excludes strongly predisposing mutations. Low predispositions to CRC can stem from a wide array of different gene mutations and often highly depend on eating habits, environmental factors, individual’s ethnicity, which explains the disarray and debates surrounding this topic. Such variety requires thorough research, with a focus on ethnic groups, but dietary habits and environmental factors have to be taken into consideration as well. Only properly planned research can make it possible to create advanced guidelines for CRC prevention and treatment, by incorporating more genetic testing and personalized medicine into the practice. Colonoscopy is a very important prevention measure, although for most of these variants there is no official recommendation for earlier surveillance. The American Cancer Society recommends to start regular screenings at age 45, and recent study upholds this statement, but suggests earlier surveillance for individuals harboring APC c.3920T > A and CHEK2 c.1100delC variants [58]. In genetic counseling for low-penetrance variants, the patient interview and a number of broadly understood results of other tests, should be taken into account.

It is important to truly elucidate roles of these variants. Only then it will be possible to group them based on importance and apply genetic testing where needed. Until then, such tests results will not be very informative and may prove to be an excess use of funds.
