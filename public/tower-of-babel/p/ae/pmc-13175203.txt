Neonatal Blood Count Data of Piglets in Modern and Traditional Breeds following Spontaneous or Hormonally Induced Parturition
Authors: Michael Oster, Heinke Lickfett, Andreas Vernunft, Henry Reyer, Eduard Muráni, Hartwig Bostedt, Klaus Wimmers
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Traditional pig breeds experience a renaissance in public perception to favor biodiversity and resilience to diseases. Moreover, the routine use of parturition induction with exogenous hormones is questioned, as it may compromise the welfare of both dams and piglets.Aims and Objectives:In this study, neonatal complete blood counts were analysed regarding the effects of genotype, i.e., traditional German Saddleback versus modern German Landrace, and the implementation or omission of parturition induction by hormonal intervention.Materials and Methods:Blood samples of n = 296 newborn piglets from 28 farrowings were taken within the first 6 h of life to study hematological traits.Results:The population showed 22% of neonates being anaemic with hemoglobin <11.0 g/dl (P < 0.01). The Saddleback piglets exhibited higher red blood cell count and lower mean corpuscular volume and mean corpuscular hemoglobin levels than Landrace piglets (P < 0.001). The Landrace piglets exhibited higher white blood cell count, neutrophil count, and neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio than Saddleback piglets (P < 0.001). The hormonal parturition induction revealed no effects for the analysed indices except for red cell distribution width-coefficient of variation with higher values in spontaneously born piglets compared with piglets that experienced an induced parturition (P < 0.001).Conclusion:The pronounced differences between Saddleback and Landrace neonates suggest a breed-specific setting of the hematological activity at birth, which might be partially modulated by hormone application due to labor induction in pigs.

Introduction

The success of pig husbandry largely depends on generating a sufficient number of healthy piglets. Piglet losses of around 20% are observed in both conventional and organic systems.[123] These losses are not only an economic but also an ethical issue for agriculture and may require adjustments to current breeding and management approaches.[456]

As pigs are born with limited iron stores, iron deficiency or even iron deficiency anemia in suckling pigs is a long-known phenomenon[7] and one of the major causes of piglet mortality when left untreated. This also applies for modern, fast-growing genetic lines, as up to 56% of porcine neonates[8] and 32% of suckling piglets[9] appeared to be anemic in previous studies. After birth, the erythrocyte cell count and hemoglobin content of the piglets decline due to the increase in plasma volume after ingestion of colostrum (Thorn 2000);[710] It was suggested that the physiological postnatal drop in hemoglobin content after colostrum intake is due to a corresponding influx caused by the increase in oncotic pressure.[1112] Because of this dynamic situation in the first hours of life, it is difficult to compare observations from different studies, as results are based on blood samples taken before or after ingestion of colostrum from piglets up to 36 h of age.[891314]

Iron supplementation applies as standard treatment in piglet husbandry.[1415] Depending on the supplementation regimen, however, the corresponding deficiencies are not consistently prevented.[161718] Moreover, iron supplementation has also been questioned due to its potential toxicity, following, e.g. formation of hydroxyl radicals and as an interference with innate immunity with negative effects on resistance to disease.[141920]

While pigs may serve as a valuable animal model for iron deficiency anemia to approximate the human situation, large differences exist in peripheral lymphocyte numbers at birth, with a relatively high number in human neonates[2122] but an initially low number in pigs[13] when compared with adult counterparts. In neonatal piglets, the adaptive immunity is immature with a low number of effector/memory T cells and mainly unprimed B cells due to limited or absent external antigenic stimuli during fetal life, as reviewed elsewhere.[23] To compensate for the neonatal immunodeficiency of piglets, the intestinal passage of immune components derived from colostrum (i.e. maternal immunoglobulins, antibodies, cytokines, and leucocytes) is required for the acquisition of passive immunity.[242526] Compared with adults, porcine neonates have a higher intestinal permeability, which also accounts for other mammalian species, including humans and cattle.[272829] Pig milk exosomes show breed-specific differences in concentration and miRNA content, with Bamei pig colostrum being particularly rich in immune-related miRNAs that may influence piglet development.[30] To achieve a sufficient number of immunocompetent cells, the intrinsic hematopoietic activity in porcine neonates is significant and comprises liver, thymus, and bone marrow.[26] As neonatal piglets exhibit high levels of plasma cortisol compared with weaned piglets of modern and traditional breeds[3] or adult pigs,[13] immunomodulatory implications by endocrine routes suppressing inflammatory pathways in the first days of life are conceivable. Moreover, piglets born at spontaneous parturitions appeared to develop a higher physiological maturity and a more efficient utilization of nutrients than piglets born at induced parturitions.[31] Indeed, sow management practices, including parturition induction, often follow a one-size-fits-all approach. There may be a need to evaluate and tailor these strategies to improve piglet outcomes.

This study hypothesizes that sow genetics and hormone-induced parturition affect blood traits related to anemia and immunity in newborn piglets. Complete blood counts are used to evaluate whether newborn piglets from traditional breeds (Saddleback) are more robust at birth than those from modern breeds (Landrace). This study also assesses how hormone-induced parturition impacts these blood traits.

Materials and Methods

Ethics

The trial was carried out in accordance with the German Animal Welfare Act, approved by the Animal Welfare Committee of the FBN. The study complies with the ARRIVE guidelines and was approved by the Ethics Committee of the federal state of Mecklenburg-Western Pomerania, Germany (LALLF M-V/TSD/7221.3-1-030/21).

Animals and experimental groups

To investigate the influence of breed and induction on complete blood count data of the newborn piglets, three experimental groups of multiparous sows were formed. Nulliparous sows were not considered in this study. Sows used in this study were healthy individuals with known pedigree, as documented in the FBN herd books. These comprised (i) German Saddleback (GS) sows with hormonally induced parturition (n = 14; parity: 4.1 ± 2.27; mean ± SD), (ii) German Landrace (GL) sows with hormonally induced parturition (n = 6; parity: 4.3 ± 1.62), and (iii) GL sows with spontaneous, noninduced parturition (n = 8; parity: 3.9 ± 1.22). This arrangement was used to compare breed effects (GS vs. GL) and the influence of partus induction within the GL breed (induced vs. natural, spontaneous parturitions).

Sow management

The sows were owned by the Research Institute of Farm Animal Biology (FBN) and were kept at the FBN pig facility. Sows were synchronized and artificially inseminated before group housing until gestation day 105, and were then transferred to farrowing units. The piglets were provided with a heating plate (76 cm × 60 cm) and a heating lamp in the creep area (temperature of 30°C). The farrowing compartments were kept at a computer-controlled temperature of 22°C with regulated ventilation. From gestation day 105, the sows were fed a corn-soybean-based commercial gestation diet twice daily for a total of 3.2 kg/day with an energy content of 12.2 MJ metabolizable energy (ME) per kg dry matter (DM) [Supplementary Table 1]. At gestation day 109, the diet was switched to a commercial lactation diet with a higher energy density of 13.2 MJ ME/kg DM, while maintaining the daily feed rate at 3.2 kg/day. On gestation day 112, the daily feed quantity was reduced to 2 kg. Throughout the trial, the sows had unlimited access to water.

The gestation period of GL sows was around 1.2 days longer compared with GS sows.[3233] Partus induction was performed while taking into account the different gestation lengths of GS and GL sows. The induction protocol used a combination of prostaglandin F2α (PGF) and carbetocin (a long-acting synthetic oxytocin analogue). In GS sows, 0.175 mg cloprostenol (a synthetic analogue of prostaglandin F2α; 2 ml PGF Veyx forte; Veyx, Schwarzenborn, Germany) was administered on day 112 of gestation, while GL sows received the same dose on day 114. Subsequently, all GL sows received 70 μg carbetocin (1 ml Depotocin; Veyx) 24 h after the first injection. In total, 28.5% of the GS sows received carbetocin because farrowing had already started. Both drugs were administered by intramuscular injection. The peripartal period was monitored for all sows as described previously.[3] If the birth interval was more than 60 min, the parturition process was supported by manual obstetrics without hormonal intervention.

Piglet management and blood sampling

For all newborn piglets, information is available on parentage and parturition (litter weight, farrowing length, time of birth, birth order, and any obstetrics performed). The individual time of parturition was recorded for each individual piglet. In addition, the time from birth to first suckling was recorded for each piglet. To minimize stress and ensure physiological stability, blood sampling was conducted only after the first colostrum intake. This approach was taken as an animal welfare consideration to reduce neonatal challenges and ensure piglets had received essential nutrients before handling. Blood samples were taken from the jugular vein of all viable piglets, and the exact time of each sample collection was documented. K-EDTA tubes from Sarstedt (Nümbrecht, Germany) were used for blood sampling.

Hematology analyses

A total of 299 EDTA whole blood samples, which were taken within 240 min (4 h) after first colostrum intake, were used to generate a complete blood count using the ProCyte Dx device, utilizing laser flow cytometry and optical fluorescence (IDEXX Laboratories Inc., Westbrook, Maine, USA). Retrieved data comprised relative and absolute quantifications of red blood cells (RBC), hemoglobin (HGB), hematocrit (HCT), mean corpuscular volume (MCV), mean corpuscular hemoglobin (MCH), mean corpuscular hemoglobin concentration (MCHC), red cell distribution width (RDW.SD, RDW.CV), reticulocytes (RET), immature reticulocyte fraction (IFR), low fluorescence ratio, medium fluorescence ratio (MFR), high fluorescence ratio (HFR), reticulocyte hemoglobin (RET-He), red blood cell hemoglobin (RBC-He), platelet count, platelet distribution width, mean platelet volume, plateletcrit, white blood cells (WBC), neutrophils (NEUT), lymphocytes (LYPMPH), monocytes, eosinophils, and basophils. The neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), defined by the absolute counts of NEUT and LYPMPH, was calculated.

Data analyses

Data points identified as outliers via the Grubbs test (P < 0.01) were skipped from downstream analyses (R language v4.2.2; package outlier, v0.15; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). To account for hypervolemia due to colostrum intake, the difference between age at sampling (min) and first colostrum intake (min), representing the individual duration for digestion, was calculated. For each blood count parameter, a linear regression model was fitted using time of digestion (min) as a covariate (R package stats, v3.1-1). The slope and intercept were extracted for each blood count parameter and were used for normalization according to the individual time at blood collection since the first colostrum intake. Therefore, the time-dependent component was subtracted from each data point, effectively removing the confounding influence of hypervolemia and returning the data to baseline level before colostrum intake, i.e., fasting status. Data was evaluated via a linear model with either breed or birth induction as fixed effects (R package stats). The ANOVA function was applied to analyze the differences among means. A correction for multiple testing was done with the Bonferroni procedure,[34] and differences were considered significant at an adjusted P < 0.05. The results were displayed in graphical form via GraphPad Prism (v8.4.3 for Windows, GraphPad Software, Boston, MA, USA). Results are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM).

Results

The data from individual neonatal piglets comprised n = 296 complete blood counts for downstream analyses, as data from three animals have been skipped following Grubbs outlier detection (P < 0.01). In view of the experimental design for blood sampling within a maximum of 240 min after first colostrum intake, analyses showed a time-dependent negative correlation, e.g. RBC (P < 0.001), and a time-dependent positive correlation, e. g., WBC (P = 0.001), as exemplarily presented in Figure 1. Results of other traits are presented in Supplementary Table 2. Neonates with low HGB (<11.0 g/dl) represent 22.0% of the population (65 of 296 piglets). Results of the analyses of the hematological traits comparing the three experimental groups are shown in Figure 2 (related to the RBC profile), Figure 3 (related to the platelet profile), and Figure 4 (related to the white blood cell profile). Specifically, data comprise n = 136 for GS piglets that experienced an induced parturition, n = 58 for GL piglets that experienced an induced parturition, and n = 102 for GL piglets born via a spontaneous, noninduced parturition. In addition, for all analyzed hematological traits, the mean values ± SEM are shown in Supplementary Table 3. The data show significant differences between modern and traditional pig breeds, with higher values for RBC, MCHC, and RDW.CV, RET count, MFR, and percentage of LYMPH, and lower values for MCV, MCH, and RDW.SD, HFR, RET-He, RBC-He, WBC, NEUT count, percentage of NEUT, and NLR in induced GS compared with induced GL neonates. The analyses revealed higher levels in RDW. CV in spontaneous, noninduced parturitions compared with hormonally induced parturitions of GL neonates.

Discussion

The study involved piglets from two pig breeds that differ fundamentally in terms of performance, body composition, and breeding history.[3536] The GS is a noncommercial, traditional breed, while the GL represents a modern, commercial line with pronounced maternal traits. The GS and GL pigs also differ in gestation length and birth characteristics,[3233] as well as in performance traits such as litter size.[337]

A well-established hematopoietic system is essential for successful neonatal adaptation, as it supports crucial functions such as oxygen transport, immune response, and circulatory stability during the transition to extra-uterine life. In this study, a number of parameters related to the hematopoietic activity differed between GS neonates compared with GL neonates. Effects due to parturition induction were largely absent. The time-dependent normalization carried out in this study reflects hematological characteristics of the as-is state before the first colostrum intake. Results are largely consistent with published data from piglets in early postnatal adaptation, although referring to blood samples taken before suckling,[13] within 18 h after birth,[8] after 24 h;[938] or at 1 d in general.[14] Consequently, the analyzed neonates exhibited values referring to erythropoiesis (e.g. MCH, MCV, MCHC, RET) that do not match reference ranges of weaned piglets or adult animals.[3940] Neonatal adaptation is one of the most vulnerable stages of life for piglets.[1641] The range of hematological parameters observed in particular, in neonates underlines the complex dynamics that occur at the beginning of postnatal life in pigs, which have to be managed alongside the development of cardio-respiratory function, thermoregulation, the establishment of acid-base balance, and neurological and muscular characteristics.[16]

In this study, about 22.0% of the piglets were born anemic (HGB <11.0 g/dl), i.e. 65 of 296 piglets. It documents the persisting problem in both traditional and modern breeds, and appears to be independent of management measures such as hormonal parturition induction. In fact, early postnatal iron deficiency is a prevalent phenomenon in pigs,[42] although low HGB levels at birth are considered primary physiological. Taking into account reference values[40] specifically characterizing porcine neonates as for MCH (19.5–23.7 pg) and MCV (56–72 fl), 25 animals or 8.4% of the population showed hypochromic anemia (MCH <19.5 pg), and 23 animals or 7.8% of the population showed macrocytic anemia (MCV >72 fl). The higher RBC and MCHC levels might be considered as a breed-specific compensation for the lower MCV and MCH levels in GS piglets compared with GL piglets. Consistently, a study on hematology traits of the local Krškopolje pig breed has revealed a distinct pattern within their population.[43] Interestingly, the iron status and RBC count of neonatal piglets might be independent of litter size, which was reported by transferring different numbers of embryos in Polish Landrace.[38]

The results for RET-He, which reflect the reticulocyte hemoglobin content, showed a range of 11.9–20.4 pg in the neonatal piglets. The RET has been suggested to assess the iron status.[44] In combination with other hematology parameters, such as the HGB and the RDW, it has been reported that conclusions can be drawn about the prevalence of iron deficiency anemia. However, this requires reliable cutoff criteria for the diagnosis of iron deficiency in pigs, which need to be established for different phases of life.

The absolute number of RET indicates efficiency of erythropoiesis, which seems to be higher in GS compared with GL neonates. On the contrary, the RBC and hemoglobin synthesis of RBC and RET were observed to be higher in GL compared with GS neonates, indicating hypoxia in GL. Indeed, litter size is higher in GL compared with GS,[3] which increases resource competition. The values for low fluorescence ratio and IRF, which reflect the mature RET and immature RET fractions for assessing the regeneration of erythropoiesis, did not differ between the experimental groups. This indicates effective erythropoiesis in both traditional and modern breeds, with some compensatory effects regarding HFR and MFR.

There are discussions about supporting the iron stores of piglets by adjust supplements for pregnant sows. In fact, HGB concentrations in sows decrease during pregnancy, which might be compensated for with iron supplements.[45] In this study, the GS and GL sows received the same rations, which included iron supply via a vitamin and mineral premix. However, breed-specific requirements referring to iron are conceivable due to genetic variations in iron absorption or utilization. When analyzing the metabolic status of piglets throughout early postnatal adaptation, considerable breed effects were observed in GS piglets compared with GL piglets.[3]

For the differential white blood cell counts, this study showed several breed effects following induced parturitions, including lower absolute values of WBC and NEUT, lower relative values of NEUT, higher relative values of LYPMPH, and a lower NLR in GS piglets compared with GL neonates. Short-term shifts in leukocyte fractions, including NEUT and LYMPH in the blood of pigs[46] and rats[47] has been documented following elevated blood glucocorticoids, but also adrenaline and noradrenaline. However, early postnatal plasma cortisol levels in GS versus GL were not significantly influenced by breed.[3] On the other hand, studies in parturient sows showed pronounced breed effects, including increased ante partum and sub partu levels of serum cortisol, plasma adrenaline, and plasma noradrenaline in GS compared to GL sows throughout parturition,[33] which points to a breed-specific neuroendocrine regulation with implications on leucocyte distribution and trafficking of blood cells from or into tissues. The observed differences in cell number between the breeds resulted in an altered NLR (GS < GL), which might suggest differences in immune status. This could involve an active innate immune response in GL, which might protect against bacterial infections but could also predispose them to excessive inflammation and immune dysregulation. In contrast, lower NEUT and higher LYPMPH in GS piglets suggest a more developed adaptive immune response and better-regulated immune homeostasis, which may reduce excessive inflammatory reactions but could also delay early-life pathogen clearance. These differences might also affect long-term immunity and vaccine efficacy.[48] Genetic variations influence the balance between innate and adaptive immunity, which could have important implications for disease resistance, susceptibility to neonatal infections, or responses to environmental stressors, such as early-life microbial colonization, and overall health in pig populations.[4950]

Conclusion

Early anemia is a physiological phenomenon in both traditional and modern pig breeds. However, as hematological activity is crucial for neonatal vitality and health, strategies concerning various parameters reflecting erythropoiesis and immunity differ between traditional Saddleback pigs and modern Landrace pigs at birth, with the most pronounced differences in RBCs, MCV, NEUT, and LYPMPH. The results show the dynamics of the traits represented by the blood count in the first hours of life, which can be analyzed after normalization to the fasting state. The data on hematological traits indicate that both age- and breed-specific reference values are required to assess the welfare and vitality of newborn pigs in particular.
