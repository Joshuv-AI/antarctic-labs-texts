Factors Associated With Alcohol Intake in Patients With Newly Diagnosed Breast Cancer
Authors: Sanjna Rajput, Robert A. Vierkant, Nicole L. Larson, Daniela L. Stan, Dawn M. Mussallem, Stacy D. D'Andre, Fergus J. Couch, Janet E. Olson, Ciara C. O'Sullivan, Kathryn J. Ruddy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundAlcohol intake is a modifiable risk factor for breast cancer, yet patterns of alcohol intake in women with breast cancer remain underexplored. This study evaluates demographic and clinical predictors of alcohol intake in patients with breast cancer.MethodsPatients at Mayo Clinic Rochester diagnosed with breast cancer (Stage 0–3) between July 2014 to March 2022 were surveyed about their average alcohol intake over the 10 years prior to their diagnosis. Alcohol intake was categorized as < 1, 1–4, 5–14, and ≥ 15 drinks per week. Demographic, behavioral, and clinical data were analyzed using univariate Fisher exact tests and multivariate multinomial logistic regression.ResultsOf 2030 participants (mean age at diagnosis: 59 years; 99.3% female; 95.9% White), 29% consumed < 1 drink/week, 48% reported 1–4 drinks/week, 20% consumed 5–14 drinks/week, and 3% reported ≥ 15 drinks/week. Older age at diagnosis (≥ 60 years), non‐White race, financial difficulty, and never smoking were associated with less alcohol intake. Patients who reported higher levels of mild–moderate intensity exercise reported more alcohol intake. Tumor stage, tumor receptors, and BRCA mutation status were not associated with alcohol intake. PROMIS‐10 physical health scores correlated with alcohol intake in univariate but not multivariate models.ConclusionAlcohol intake is more common prior to a breast cancer diagnosis for patients who are younger, White, financially secure, exercising, and smoking. These findings may inform alcohol intake‐related breast cancer prevention efforts and highlight patient subgroups who could benefit from targeted counseling at the time of diagnosis.

Introduction

Breast cancer is the most common cancer in women; in 2023, 300,590 people were diagnosed with invasive breast cancer in the United States, and 55,720 were diagnosed with ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS) (99% of whom were women). Since the mid‐2000s, incidence rates of invasive breast cancer, particularly for younger women, have been rising [1]. Alcohol intake increases the likelihood of multiple malignancies including those of the oral cavity, gastrointestinal tract, liver, and breast. Alcohol intake is also associated with other adverse health conditions including liver disease, hypertension, and cardiomyopathy [2]. The International Agency for Research on Cancer has classified ethanol and acetaldehyde in alcoholic beverages as carcinogens to humans [3]. Studies indicate that ethanol, when metabolized into acetaldehyde in the liver, may contribute to cancer development by causing DNA damage. This process can trigger free radical production, reduce folate levels, increase hormone levels, and potentially act as a tumor promoter, all of which may play a role in the formation and growth of tumor cells [4].

Several studies have established the association between alcohol intake and increased primary breast cancer risk, with approximately 4%–10% of breast cancer cases in the United States and Europe attributable to alcohol intake [5, 6, 7]. This increased risk has been shown to occur in a dose–response manner with alcohol intake, with each drink per day increasing risk by 7%–10%, with even light levels of drinking translating into elevated risk [8, 9]. Alcohol intake in adult life has been shown to be independently associated with breast cancer risk [10]. As alcohol is a major modifiable risk factor, it has been emphasized by the American Society of Clinical Oncology as important to address in cancer prevention efforts and research [11].

Notably, the American Cancer Society's (ACS) Guideline for Diet and Physical Activity recommends no alcohol intake, further stating that if alcoholic drinks are consumed, alcohol should be limited to 1 drink per day for women and 2 drinks per day for men [12]. Studies have shown that adherence to ACS or World Cancer Research Fund/American Institute for Cancer Research guideline recommendations, as well as reducing alcohol intake from heavy, moderate, or light drinking to cessation or a lower category, is associated with a lower risk of developing breast cancer [13, 14]. Despite this, approximately half of women in the US are current drinkers, with one in twenty engaging in heavy drinking of > 7 drinks per week [15, 16]. Many people are unaware of the connection between alcohol intake and cancer. Surveys conducted in 2016 and 2017 in both England and the United States revealed that only 18% to 50% of participants correctly identified alcohol as a risk factor for breast cancer [17, 18]. This lack of knowledge may encourage higher levels of alcohol intake among individuals who might otherwise reduce their intake. Public messaging on alcohol risks is complicated by several studies suggesting that light to moderate alcohol intake may benefit cardiovascular health, contributing to confusion, despite evidence that any benefits are minimal in comparison to the risks of alcohol intake [19].

While sociodemographic and lifestyle predictors of alcohol intake have been described in the general population, it is less clear whether these patterns apply to women who go on to develop breast cancer. Understanding whether established population‐level predictors of alcohol intake apply in this clinical context is important, as alcohol is directly implicated as a modifiable risk factor in both breast cancer incidence as well as in outcomes after diagnosis. Identifying subgroups of the population with higher alcohol intake may help inform prevention and patient education at time of diagnosis and later in survivorship.

This study therefore aimed to assess demographic and clinical characteristics that are associated with higher alcohol intake at the time of breast cancer diagnosis by leveraging a clinic‐based cohort with detailed tumor and treatment data, validated patient‐reported physical and mental health measures, and retrospective reporting by participants of their alcohol intake during the 10 years preceding diagnosis.

Methods

Patients seen at least once at Mayo Clinic Rochester who provide informed consent are prospectively enrolled in the Mayo Clinic Breast Disease Registry (MCBDR) following an initial diagnosis of DCIS or breast cancer within the year prior (at age 18 or older). A baseline survey was mailed to MCBDR participants who were diagnosed with breast cancer between July 2014 and March 2022. The survey included a question about alcohol use, asking about the average number of alcoholic drinks consumed per week over the past 10 years with answer choices of none, 1–4 drinks, 5–9 drinks, 10–14 drinks, 15–19 drinks, 20–29 drinks, 30–39 drinks, and more than 39 drinks. One standard drink was defined as 5 oz of wine, 12 oz of beer, or 1 oz of liquor. Self‐reported alcohol intake was classified into 4 categories of alcohol intake per week: none or less than < 1, 1–4 (low), 5–14 (moderate), and 15+ (high). Patients were additionally asked about demographic and clinical characteristics including education level, financial situation, smoking status, exercise levels, menopausal status, and PROMIS‐10 scores. The PROMIS Global‐10 is a 10‐item measure of health, well‐being and distress that produces global physical health (GPH) and global mental health (GMH) scores. PROMIS measures use a T‐score metric that is converted from raw sum scores. The T‐score of 50 is standardized to the mean United States general population with a standard deviation (SD) of 10 points (11–12). A higher T‐score demonstrates more of what is being measured, such as greater global physical or mental health than the general population. Nurse‐abstracted data on race, gender, age at cancer diagnosis, tumor stage, tumor characteristics and cancer treatments received after diagnosis (surgery, radiation, chemotherapy, and/or endocrine therapy) and BRCA mutation status were collected from the electronic health record and used for this analysis.

Participants were excluded from the analysis if they returned the survey with missing information on alcohol intake, if they had any known breast cancer recurrence, if they had metastatic (stage IV) disease at the time of diagnosis, or if their survey was collected more than 24 months after diagnosis.

Statistical Analyses

Data were summarized using frequencies and percents for categorical variables and means and standard deviations (SDs) for continuous variables. Univariate associations of weekly alcohol consumption with categorical demographic and clinical characteristics were examined using Monte Carlo‐based Fisher exact tests. For a given comparison, a cross‐tabulated table was created using the observed data and the Fisher test statistic was extracted. Following this, 10,000 random samples of cross‐tabulated tables were generated conditional on the same total sample size, row totals, and column totals as the observed table. The Monte Carlo p‐value was then calculated as the proportion of the resulting 10,000 tables that yielded a test statistic at least as large as the one observed. This approach was chosen due to the existence of sparse cells in some categories, which violates the large sample approximation assumptions of the chi‐square test, and because the sheer number of possible combinations to consider for variables with many categories made calculations for standard Fisher exact tests impractical.

Following these univariate associations, we fit a nominal, multivariate, multi‐categorical logistic regression model to determine variables independently associated with alcohol use, after adjusting for the effects of all other variables in the model. Alcohol intake was fit as the outcome, and all variables that were associated with alcohol use (p < 0.05) in the univariate models were included in the multivariate model, using a multinomial distribution and a generalized logit (glogit) link function. Estimated marginal percents of patients falling within a given alcohol category for each level of each characteristic of interest were calculated using an approach similar to calculating least squares means when using a continuous outcome variable. First, predicted probabilities of alcohol consumption were calculated for each combination of all variables in the model. Then, for a given level of a characteristic of interest, we calculated the mean of the predicted probabilities of alcohol consumption for that level by averaging all predicted probabilities for that level across all combinations of the other variables in the model. These marginal predicted probabilities were multiplied by 100 to arrive at predicted percents. All statistical tests were two‐sided, and all analyses were carried out using the SAS system (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC).

Results

In all, 2230 patients with Stage 0–3 breast cancer returned questionnaires. Of these, we excluded 88 patients who had a known breast cancer recurrence, 73 who did not respond to the alcohol question, and 39 for whom time from diagnosis to questionnaire return was greater than 1 year, resulting in a final sample size of 2030 patients. Mean age at cancer diagnosis was 59.0 years (SD 12.4), 2016 (99.3%) were female, and 1946 (95.9%) were of White race. Seventy‐nine percent of female patients were post‐menopausal. Of patients with non‐missing tumor stage, 17% were stage 0%, 50% were stage 1%, 26% were stage 2%, and 8% were stage 3. Slightly more than half of the patients (53%) underwent lumpectomies, while the remainder underwent mastectomies. Fifty‐seven percent underwent radiotherapy, 31% underwent chemotherapy, and 67% received endocrine therapy. Forty‐seven patients (2.3%) had a pathologic germline BRCA1/2 mutation. Eighty‐five percent had estrogen receptor (ER)‐positive, 78% progesterone receptor (PR)‐positive, and 12% HER2‐positive breast cancer. Twenty‐nine percent of patients reported consuming < 1 alcoholic drink/week, 48% reported 1–4 drinks/week, 20% reported 5–14 drinks/week, and 3% reported 15 or more drinks/week.

Univariate associations of alcohol with demographic and clinical characteristics are provided in Table 1. Lower alcohol consumption was associated with later age at diagnosis, such that < 1 alcoholic drink/week was reported by more patients aged 60 and older at diagnosis (33%) than those aged < 40 (19%), 40–49 (28%), and 50–59 (24%, p < 0.001). Fifty percent of non‐White patients reported < 1 drink/week compared to 28% of White patients (p < 0.001). Compared to patients not reporting financial difficulty, those reporting financial difficulty were more likely to consume < 1 drink/week (34% vs. 27%) and less likely to consume 15+ drinks/week (1% vs. 4%, p = 0.002). Ten percent of current smokers reported consuming 15+ drinks/week, compared to 6% of former smokers and 1% of never smokers (p < 0.001). Compared to patients with higher levels of strenuous exercise, those reporting 0 min of strenuous exercise per week were more likely to abstain from alcohol (32% reported < 1 drink/week vs. 21%) and less likely to consume smaller amounts of alcohol (46% reported 1–4 drinks/week compared to 54%, p = 0.002). Patients reporting 0 min of moderate exercise per week were more likely to abstain from alcohol than those reporting any moderate exercise (35% vs. 25%). Conversely, patients reporting 240 or more minutes of moderate exercise per week were more likely to consume 15+ drinks/week than those reporting lower levels of moderate exercise (5% vs. 2%, p < 0.001). Similar to moderate exercise, patients reporting 0 min of weekly mild exercise were more likely to abstain from alcohol (36%) than those reporting at least some mild exercise (26%, p = 0.01). Alcohol consumption was not associated with gender, education, menopausal status, tumor stage, cancer treatment characteristics, BRCA mutation status, or breast cancer subtype (p > 0.05 for each).

In multivariate analyses, older age at diagnosis was again significantly associated with alcohol abstinence (34% of those aged 60 and older reported < 1 drink/week compared to 19% for those aged < 40%, 28% for those aged 40–49 and 24% for those aged 50–59, p < 0.001, Table 3 and Figure 1a). Discussion

The present study aimed to assess demographic and clinical predictors of alcohol consumption among women diagnosed with stage 0–3 breast cancer. In a sample of 2030 patients, we identified several key demographic and behavioral factors that were significantly associated with alcohol use. These findings are important for understanding patterns of alcohol consumption in this patient population and for developing tailored alcohol intake‐reduction interventions.

Our results indicate that older age at diagnosis is strongly associated with lower alcohol consumption. Women aged 60 and older at diagnosis were significantly more likely to report consuming less than one drink per week compared to younger age groups. This trend aligns with previous studies suggesting that older adults often reduce alcohol intake due to health concerns or comorbidities [15, 20, 21]. And women who begin drinking alcohol during their teenage years tend to maintain similar or higher levels of consumption into adulthood [22]. Given that nulliparous breast tissue is particularly vulnerable to cancerous changes, especially in the critical period between menarche and first pregnancy, alcohol consumption during adolescence and early adulthood may play a significant role in the development of breast cancer [23]. This underscores the need for breast cancer prevention efforts to target adolescent girls and young women.

Race was another significant predictor of alcohol use. Non‐White patients were more likely to abstain from alcohol than White patients, consistent with broader population‐level trends, with less alcohol consumption among racial and ethnic minorities [15, 24]. This may reflect cultural differences in drinking behavior, access to alcohol, and/or other socio‐economic factors that influence drinking patterns in different communities [25]. Gaining insight into individual perceptions of alcohol consumption, along with the impact of cultural and social influences, will enhance our ability to evaluate how drinking affects various populations in order to help craft public health messages specifically targeted at the most vulnerable subgroups.

Financial difficulty also emerged as a strong predictor of lower alcohol consumption. Patients who reported financial hardship were more likely to abstain from alcohol and less likely to engage in heavy drinking. It is possible that patients facing financial difficulties do not have money to purchase alcohol or are discouraged from drinking due to other health concerns [26, 27].

Additionally, smoking status was closely linked with alcohol consumption, with current smokers significantly more likely to engage in heavy drinking compared to former or never smokers. This relationship between smoking and alcohol use is well‐documented in the literature, with the two behaviors often co‐occurring due to shared psychosocial and behavioral risk factors [10, 24, 28].

Exercise patterns were also associated with alcohol consumption. Patients who engaged in moderate and mild intensity exercise were less likely to abstain from alcohol and more likely to report light to moderate drinking, even after accounting for PROMIS‐10 global physical health scores. These findings may reflect broader cultural or socioeconomic impacts on lifestyle. Social engagement, greater financial stability, and more leisure time may encourage both physical activity and social drinking [29]. This association should be interpreted with caution as potential residual confounding by lifestyle factors that were unmeasured cannot be excluded. Interestingly, strenuous exercise was not associated with alcohol consumption in multivariate analyses, suggesting that more intense physical activity may not share the same relationship with drinking behaviors.

Importantly, tumor stage, tumor receptor status, and BRCA mutation status were not statistically significantly associated with alcohol consumption. Notably, while some studies have identified a stronger association between alcohol consumption and hormone receptor‐positive (ER+/PR+) breast cancer [6, 8], possibly due to the impact of alcohol on circulating hormones, we did not find this, similar to some other studies [10]. Furthermore, existing evidence indicates that alcohol intake is not associated with an increased risk of breast cancer in germline BRCA mutation carriers compared with non‐BRCA mutation carriers. Ethanol's possible role in downregulating the BRCA1 gene, leading to increased ER activity and cell proliferation, may contribute to the complexity of these relationships [30]. Importantly, our results should be interpreted with caution; the number of patients with pathogenic germline BRCA variants in our study was small, which limits statistical power and precludes definitive conclusions in this subgroup.

Physical health, as measured by the PROMIS‐10, was associated with alcohol consumption in univariate analyses, with lower physical health scores linked to less alcohol intake. People with poor physical health may consume less alcohol due to concerns that alcohol could worsen their existing health problems. However, given that this association was not statistically significant in multivariate models, other factors, such as age at diagnosis or financial difficulty, may contribute substantially to the relationship between patient‐reported physical health and drinking behaviors.

One key strength of our study is the large size of the cohort, which includes 2030 patients. The comprehensive data collection, encompassing a broad range of demographic, clinical, and behavioral variables such as tumor stage, menopausal status, BRCA status, and exercise patterns, allows for a detailed analysis and clinical contextualization of factors associated with alcohol use. The stratification of weekly alcohol intake into four categories captured important gradations of use that are relevant to cancer risk but often obscured in broader categorizations. The use of PROMIS‐10 physical and mental health scores offered a patient‐reported measure of global health in relation to alcohol intake. Findings from a real‐world, clinic‐based cohort enhance translational relevance for oncology settings where risk communication and behavior change occur.

This study has several limitations. Our data‐gathering relied on self‐reported surveys, allowing for possible recall bias and underreporting of alcohol use, especially as we asked our patients to report average daily or weekly alcohol use over the last 10 years. Recall bias may have led to misclassification of exposure, with potential for underestimation of or inconsistencies with reporting of prior intake. This concern is especially pertinent for higher consumption amounts, as standard quantity‐frequency methods have been shown to underestimate prevalence of harmful drinking in comparison to more detailed graduated frequency measures, with the most notable discrepancies observed in those with higher alcohol consumption [31]. This in turn may have affected observed associations between alcohol use and other factors. However, retrospective decade‐based questionnaires have demonstrated moderate‐to‐good test–retest reliability and concordance with prospectively collected data in older adults [32]. Additionally, recall of alcohol intake from 10 years prior has correlated well with other recall periods across diverse populations, supporting the use of such measures in epidemiologic research [33]. Our study also lacks data on the specific types of alcohol consumed (e.g., beer, wine, liquor) and the context in which alcohol was consumed (e.g., with meals or binge drinking). Absence of these details may obscure an important nuance in characterizing alcohol use, as patients with similar average weekly intake may have substantially different consumption behaviors (i.e., some may consume small amounts of daily while others binge drink once per week), without distinction in our analyses. Additionally, without information on type of alcohol consumed, it is not possible to assess how that may differ by age, socioeconomic status, or cultural background. Future studies should incorporate more detailed measures of alcohol consumption to facilitate a comprehensive characterization of alcohol use patterns among breast cancer survivors. Our definition of one standard drink of liquor was specified as 1 oz of distilled spirits, which deviates from the NIAAA standard of 1.5 oz of 80‐proof spirits. This discrepancy may affect the comparability of our alcohol use estimates with those of studies employing the NIAAA definition and may have resulted in a slight overestimation of the number of standard drinks consumed among participants who reported liquor intake. Additionally, our study population was predominantly White and recruited from a single institution, limiting the generalizability of our findings. Patients of racial and ethnic minority groups may have different patterns and cultural norms surrounding alcohol use, and may receive different counseling regarding alcohol consumption. Sexual and gender minority status was not assessed in our survey; given evidence that sexual orientation and gender identity may be associated with alcohol use, future studies should assess these factors to better characterize alcohol use across populations. Our single institution cohort may not represent patients with breast cancer from other regions within the U.S. or globally. To better assess the relationship between race, ethnicity, and alcohol intake, a more diverse cohort should be studied.

Overall, our findings suggest that age at diagnosis, race, financial status, smoking, and exercise are significant predictors of alcohol consumption prior to a breast cancer diagnosis. While our current analysis is limited to pre‐diagnosis intake, prior literature suggests that pre‐diagnosis drinking behaviors may persist after cancer diagnosis [24]. Thus, our findings have practical implications for clinical care at time of cancer diagnosis, potentially informing the development of targeted behavioral interventions [34], particularly for high‐risk subgroups such as younger individuals, those with greater financial security, those who engage in mild to moderate intensity exercise, and smokers. Building on the present analysis, our group is actively pursuing the longitudinal follow‐up needed to evaluate changes in drinking patterns during and after breast cancer treatment.
