Effectiveness of Dual-System Endotracheal Suction on Cardiopulmonary Functions in Mechanically Ventilated Adult Patients at Ismailia City, Egypt: Nursing Practice
Authors: Mohamed Goda Elbqry, Eman Ragab Elsayed, Fatma Mohamed Elmansy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Tracheal suctioning is an essential procedure used to maintain airway patency by removing secretions. However, it is a critical nursing skill that carries potential risks. This study aims to evaluate the effects of the dual-system endotracheal suction on cardiopulmonary function in mechanically ventilated adult patients.Materials and Methods:A quasi-experimental design was employed in this study. A purposive sample of 74 patients was recruited from the intensive care unit of a teaching hospital in the city of Ismailia, Egypt. The patients were assigned to two equal groups, with 37 patients in each group: Group I received closed-system suction, while Group II received open-system suction. Data were collected using a single structured tool consisting of three sections: demographic information, health history, and a hemodynamic parameters sheet used to assess cardiopulmonary function before and after suctioning.Results:The mean (SD) ages of the studied patients were 49 (6.70) years in Group I and 47.2 (7.10) years in Group II. There was a highly significant difference between the use of closed- and open-suction systems for cardiopulmonary function during (t = 4.23; P = 0.01) and directly postsuction (t = 4.23, P = 0.01). However, no statistically significant differences were observed between the study groups in the presuction phase.Conclusions:Patients managed with a closed-suction system demonstrated improved cardiopulmonary function compared to those who received open suction. Therefore, the use of a closed-suction system is an effective intervention for minimizing cardiopulmonary instability and supporting the overall well-being of mechanically ventilated adult patients.

Introduction

Mechanical ventilation (MV) is a standardized treatment commonly used in adult patients for a range of clinical and physiological indications. It is increasingly recognized as a life-saving intervention, particularly in cases of reversible respiratory failure.[1] MV supports gas exchange, reduces or replaces the work of breathing, lowers systemic and cardiac oxygen consumption, improves lung expansion, facilitates sedation and anesthesia, promotes muscle relaxation, and stabilizes the thoracic wall. However, despite its benefits, MV is associated with several complications that may prolong its use, including ventilator-associated pneumonia, sepsis, and ventilator-induced lung injury. These risks necessitate comprehensive nursing and medical care to mitigate potential harm.[2] Endotracheal suctioning (ETS) is one of the most used airway management techniques in mechanically ventilated patients. The presence of an artificial airway often leads to tissue irritation and increased secretion production. Therefore, regular cleaning and suctioning of the airway are essential to maintain effective ventilation. Nonetheless, ETS, like other invasive procedures associated with MV, can result in various complications.[3]

ETS poses several serious risks and side effects, including hypoxemia, tissue hypoxia, significant fluctuations in blood pressure or heart rate, cardiac dysrhythmias, and even cardiac or respiratory arrest. Additional complications may include trauma to the tracheal or bronchial mucosa, infection, pulmonary hemorrhage, bronchoconstriction or bronchospasm, elevated intracranial pressure, and disruption of MV.[34] Traditionally, tracheal suctioning (TS) is performed using an open tracheal suction system (OTSS), which involves disconnecting the patient from the ventilator and inserting a single-use suction catheter.[5] In contrast, the closed tracheal suction system (CTSS) utilizes a multiuse catheter that is introduced into the airway through a one-way valve, allowing suction without disconnecting from the ventilator.[6] This technique enhances patient safety by maintaining ventilator support throughout the procedure. Compared to OTSS, CTSS offers several advantages, including improved oxygenation, reduced incidence of clinical hypoxemia, preservation of positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP), and decreased contamination risks. Additionally, CTSS helps prevent lung volume loss. As a result, the use of CTSS in ETS has significantly minimized associated risks and complications.[7]

Several studies have compared CTSS and OTSS in terms of mortality, secretion removal, and VAP incidence.[58] CTSS offers benefits such as reduced infection risk, ease of use by a single nurse, and repeated catheter use. However, evaluating suction system efficacy requires a comprehensive approach beyond isolated parameters like secretion volume or hemodynamic changes.[9] Effective suctioning should optimize airway clearance while maintaining cardiopulmonary stability, with improvements in tidal volume and oxygen saturation, especially under pressure-controlled ventilation.[10] To achieve optimal ventilation, clinicians may adjust ventilator settings to a volume-controlled mode with pressure support. This leads to reduced airway pressure as respiratory system compliance and dynamic lung compliance increase. Compared to pressure-controlled modes, volume-controlled ventilation facilitates more efficient adjustment of tidal volume, minute ventilation, and end-expiratory lung volume (EELV).[1112] Determining the most effective suctioning method requires a comprehensive evaluation that considers secretion volume, suction frequency, and hemodynamic parameters together. However, few studies have assessed all these factors simultaneously, so cardiopulmonary function indicators are commonly used to monitor patient stability during MV and suctioning.[11] To support evidence-based practice, nurses must be proficient in both traditional and advanced suctioning techniques. They play a critical role in monitoring respiratory status and determining appropriate interventions.[23] Therefore, the aim of this study is to evaluate and compare the effects of OTSS and CTSS on cardiopulmonary functions in mechanically ventilated adult patients.

Materials and Methods

The study was conducted in adult intensive care units (ICUs) from February to July 2022 using a quasi-experimental design affiliated with Suez Canal University hospitals in the city of Ismailia, Egypt. Each patient served as both the control and experimental group and was examined twice using two distinct techniques. In the dynamic ICU, the clinical state of patients could fluctuate daily. The fundamental premise of the study design was that the patient’s clinical state would remain stable for a single day. The inclusion and exclusion criteria were selected with this assumption in mind, as well as to eliminate and standardize confounding variables resulting from patient characteristics. The study population consisted of hospitalized patients who were placed on MV at the ICU. As there was no patient acuity chart in the unit, all patients with acute admissions were included.

The sample included 74 patients, recruited as a purposive sample in two equal groups. Group I consisted of patients admitted to the ICU with OTSS from February 1 to April 15, 2022, and Group II consisted of mechanically ventilated patients admitted with CTSS from April to June 2022. G*Power 3 software was used to calculate the sample size based on the independent sample t-test, with an error probability α of 0.06, a power of 0.81, and a dropout rate of 10%.[1314] According to these criteria, the sample size was 37 patients per group.

The inclusion criteria were age ≥18 years, continuous hemodynamic monitoring, MV support, platelet counts ≥50,000/mm3, an international normalized ratio ≤3.0, and oxygen saturation ≥90%. The exclusion criteria were unstable hemodynamic status; treatment with anticholinergic drugs (except routine inhalers), mucolytic agents, or sedation; pulmonary bleeding; extubating; reintubation; tracheostomy; or a debilitating medical condition with refractory hypoxemia (oxygen saturation <90% despite FiO2 ≥60%).

A single structured tool, adapted by the researcher based on reviews of clinical textbooks and previous related studies,[51012] was used for data collection. It comprised three parts: a demographic attributes sheet to record age, sex, working conditions, and body mass index; a health history sheet to document medical data, diagnosis, length of hospital stay, time of intubation, sedation status, and ETT size; and a cardiopulmonary monitoring sheet to assess cardiac function (mean arterial pressure, pulse rate, blood pressure) and pulmonary function (breathing cycles, tidal volume, oxygen saturation, and arterial blood gas (ABG) results pre- and postsuction). Data were collected daily using the questionnaire. ETS was performed as part of routine care, with each session lasting 15–20 s per suction pass, and the full assessment was completed within 3–5 min per patient. On average, three to five intubated adult patients were recruited each week.

The validity and reliability of the tool were evaluated by seven experts in medical-surgical nursing, critical care nursing, and anesthesia medicine, with minor modifications made based on their feedback using a three-item Likert scale, which showed high acceptance. Reliability was further confirmed with Cronbach’s alpha of 0.89. A pilot study was conducted on 10% of the sample (eight intubated adult patients) to assess readability, applicability, and clarity, and participants in the pilot were subsequently excluded from the main study after essential modifications were incorporated.

The 74 intubated adult patients who met the inclusion criteria were divided into two equal groups: 37 patients underwent closed-system suctioning (Group I), and 37 underwent open-system suctioning (Group II). Group assignment was based solely on admission dates, with the first 37 eligible patients assigned to Group I. No other factors influenced allocation. After meeting with patients’ relatives and reviewing medical records, demographic characteristics were collected. Before suctioning, all patients received 100% oxygen for 2 min via MV. In the open-suction group, the endotracheal tube was disconnected from the ventilator, and a disposable suction catheter was inserted until resistance was encountered, then withdrawn by 0.50 cm. In the closed-suction group, a catheter appropriate for the endotracheal tube size was positioned between the Y-piece and the tube following established guidelines.

The water irrigation port was kept closed, and the suction catheter was initially locked. It was then unlocked for suctioning, inserted into the endotracheal tube using a regulated thumb valve, and patients immediately received 100% oxygen for 2 min following suctioning. The researcher observed cardiopulmonary functions pre-, during, and immediately postsuction for 3–5 min per patient. A total of 74 data collection sessions were completed over an average of eight hours. After data collection, the researcher verified the accuracy of all recorded information. Consent for participation was obtained from healthcare providers and patients’ relatives through direct communication or appropriate ethical procedures. The outcomes of the suction procedures were recorded and reflected in the study groups.

The Statistical Package for Social Sciences (IBM SPSS Statistics, v. 25) for Windows 11 was used to organize, tabulate, and analyze the data. Normality was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Collected data were presented as numbers, percentages, and mean (SD). The chi-square test and Fisher’s exact test were applied for qualitative data. The Kruskal–Wallis test was used to compare nonparametric quantitative data, while the independent sample t-test was applied for parametric quantitative data. A P-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethical considerations

The study’s agreement to progress was attained from the institutional Research Ethics Committee (REC), Faculty of Nursing, Suez Canal University, Ismailia city, Egypt. The acceptance was achieved in February 2022 with No. (49), and code (149/1-2022). Official consent was obtained from the adult ICU director at the start of the study. Healthcare providers and patients’ relatives received clarification of the study’s goal, and written informed consent was obtained from the relatives who could leave and receive the same care. The data was saved and kept confidential.

Discussion

The existing study aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of dual-system endotracheal suction on cardiopulmonary function in mechanically ventilated adult patients. Invasive MV in critically ill patients often necessitates TS to clear airway secretions and prevent obstruction of the endotracheal tube. This procedure is typically conducted using either an open- or closed-suction system.[15] Numerous studies have evaluated these methods, indicating that closed-suction systems may offer benefits such as better oxygenation, preserved PEEP levels, and reduced infection risk, especially in patients requiring long-term ventilation.[1216] When secretions are not removed, the ETT may become clogged or occluded. If left untreated, this can worsen ventilation, oxygenation, and gas exchange, which may result in cardiac arrest. Bradycardia, atelectasis, hypertension, hypoxemia, and cardiac arrest are among the known side effects of ETS, despite its necessity. High-risk young people may be more susceptible to these consequences.[1216]

Regarding demographic traits [Table 1], the existing study exposed that less than half of the current studied patients in both studied groups were aged ≥45 years. Furthermore, more than half of the participants were male (Group I), while less than two-thirds were male (Group II). Approximately three-quarters more workers were in Group I than in Group II. Moreover, the mean (SD) body mass index was 63 (10.70) in Group I compared to 65.27 (9.01) in Group II. However, the findings showed that age and sex were significantly different between the studied groups (χ2 = 2.39, 3.01; P = 0.01, 0.05, respectively). This validates that these two groups were comparable during the study implementation and that there was a high prevalence of adult males in ICUs compared to females. Moreover, more than half of the studied patients in both groups were intubated due to inadequate oxygenation [Figure 1]. Most of the studied patients’ length of hospital stay in Group I was 10–14 days, while less than half of the patients in Group II had a length of hospital stay within 7–10 days [Table 2].

The duration of intubation was more than or equal to seven days in Group I compared to more than one-third (7 to <10 days) in Group II. Additionally, there were significant differences (χ2 = 3.31, 1.25; P = 0.01, 0.05, respectively) in the duration of hospital stay and intubation time between the groups using closed and open-suction systems. This outcome was in line with studies conducted by Stilma et al. and Mohamed et al.,[1217] which showed that patients who used an open-suction system stayed longer than those who used a closed-suction system. Moreover, patients with a closed suction system spent less time in the intensive care unit than patients with an open-suction system, according to Ebrahimian et al.[18] The current study’s findings were incongruent with those of Thabet et al. and Ardehali et al.,[1920] who found no discernible differences in the length of ICU stay or intubation time between the open and closed-suction groups.

The two suction system groups’ studied patients’ cardiac parameters were compared. The results of this investigation showed that the suction group and the group that was immediately following the suction had significantly different heart rates, mean arterial pressures, arterial blood pressures, and pulse pressures (t = 11.92; P = 0.05). Furthermore, there were no differences in presuction between the two study groups that were statistically significant. Furthermore, compared to the Group II open-suction system group, the results showed that the mean heart rate, arterial pressure, arterial blood pressure, and pulse pressure were all lower in the closed-suction system Group I [Table 3]. The findings of this study were in line with those of Alavi et al., who observed that following airway suctioning, the mean heart rate, mean systolic blood pressure, and mean arterial blood pressure increased initially before decreasing in both the open suction group and the closed suction group. These changes were less noticeable in the closed suction group and were significant immediately following airway suctioning.[21] The results were corroborated by Zohary et al., who established a significant difference over time in heart rate, systolic blood pressure, and diastolic blood pressure between the groups using an open and closed suctioning system at distinct stages.[22]

Similar findings were made by Mengar and Dani, who noted that while heart rates rose during both open and closed endotracheal suction, they did so more in the open-suction group than in the closed suction group.[23] According to the author, these effects could be caused by the tube being disconnected from the ventilator, which causes desaturation and the induction of hypoxia. The adrenergic nervous system, which regulates hemodynamic and cardiovascular responses like tachycardia, is stimulated by hypoxia, and Group II (open-suction system) patients’ hypertension is a compensatory response to their low blood oxygen saturation. When compared to before tracheal suction, the HR increased more in the open suction group than in the closed suction group, and both groups’ HR returned to baseline after 15 min. According to the authors, the tracheal suction technique itself may cause anxiety, discomfort, and worry in addition to irritation brought on by suction tube activities. The current investigation found that during suction and right after suction, there were significantly different mean arterial pressure, diastolic blood pressure, and systolic blood pressure between the open and closed suction groups. This result contrasts with that of Afshari et al., who found no appreciable differences in mean systolic, diastolic, or mean arterial blood pressure between the two suctioning techniques.[24]

Pulmonary parameters were compared between the study groups. The results of this investigation demonstrated that, with (t = 5.23; P = 0.05), there were significantly different respiratory rates, oxygen saturation, lung tidal volume, and PEEP between the suction group and the group that was immediately after suction. However, no statistically significant differences were detected between the two study groups. Furthermore, the results of the present study showed that the mean respiratory rate, oxygen saturation, lung tidal volume, and PEEP were greater in the closed-suction system (Group I) during and immediately after suction than in the OTSS (Group II), with a highly statistically significant difference between the two study groups [Table 4]. This result is also consistent with the findings of Ahmed and Mengar and Dani, who found that during suction and right after suction, patients in the closed-suction system group had higher mean oxygen saturation than those in the open group.[2325] A study by Ebrahimian et al.,[18] which found significant variations in the mean oxygen saturation values during and right after suction, also supports this conclusion.

This result was consistent with that of Thabet and Sayed, who reported that there were highly significant differences between the two methods of suctioning in terms of mean oxygen saturation, respiratory rate, and tidal volume among the studied patients during and immediately after closed and open suctioning.[19] The researcher believes this is because the catheter in a closed-endotracheal suction system is part of a ventilator circuit, there are highly qualified nurses, proper facilities and equipment, and a closed suctioning system. Furthermore, MV support is continuous, so PEEP can be maintained with minimal changes in fraction of inspired oxygen (FiO2). This prevents lung volume loss and reduces changes in oxygenation and ventilation during suctioning. Disconnecting the patient from the ventilator in an open suction causes a reduction in PEEP and tidal volume. These changes have the potential to significantly reduce signs of hypoxemia while also improving oxygenation and pulmonary parameters. This finding is supported by Alshahrani, who found that the loss of lung volume during OSS was significantly greater than during CSS.[14] This observation contradicts the findings of other studies, Cardoso et al. and Gahan et al., who reported that there was an increase in respiratory rate only with open suction, with no significant differences.[2627]

Furthermore, ABG parameters were compared across the study groups. The two study groups showed no significant differences in ABG parameters immediately after suction (t = 12.32; P ≥ 0.55). Furthermore, no statistically significant differences were observed between the two study groups [Table 5]. According to the authors, this could be due to a partial restriction in the exchange of oxygen and carbon dioxide during MV disconnection and forward manipulation of the aspiration catheter. The decrease in PaO2 and hypoxemia could also be attributed to endotracheal suction, which causes the drainage of oxygenated air and secretion through the respiratory tract. As PaO2 levels drop, PaCO2 levels rise.

This finding is compatible with Özden and Görgülü, Dastdadeh et al., who noticed that there is no significant difference between both suction groups in conditions of ABGs partial pressure of oxygen (PO2), power of hydrogen (pH), and partial pressure of carbon dioxide (PCO2).[2829] The mean PaCO2 increased when aspiration was halted and decreased following tracheal suction in individuals who underwent open tracheal suction. This finding is supported by the findings of Alavi et al.,[21] who revealed no significant differences in pH or PaCO2 between suction system groups. Additionally, Raimundo et al. and Cocca and Mincolelli[3031] statistically revealed no significant differences between pre- and posttracheal aspiration values of PO2, pH, and PCO2 in the open- and closed-suction system groups. The findings of the extant study showed that both the open and closed suction groups had higher levels of HCO3. This could be connected to decreased pH and increased HCO3 caused by an increase in PaCO2 as soon as aspiration was stopped during both open and closed tracheal suction. The study’s limitations: the use of the direct observation technique, which may have an impact on the authors’ behavior, was one of the study’s limitations. The researcher made two observations and stayed present for multiple work shifts to significantly reduce this impact.

Conclusion

The study concluded that both the open- and closed-suction systems achieved comparable cardiopulmonary outcomes; however, the closed-suction system demonstrated greater effectiveness by supporting more critical cardiopulmonary functions. Therefore, the closed-suction system is recommended for routine use in clinical practice to help minimize cardiopulmonary instability. Suctioning procedures should strictly follow established standards, and further research involving diverse populations is needed. It is essential that all intensive care units have access to closed-suction systems and that healthcare providers are professionally trained in their use according to standard operating procedures.

This study contributes valuable knowledge to the field of nursing by highlighting the clinical advantages of the closed-endotracheal suction system over the open system, particularly in maintaining cardiopulmonary stability in mechanically ventilated adult patients. From an educational standpoint, the findings reinforce the importance of evidence-based suctioning practices and emphasize the need to incorporate updated suctioning protocols into nursing curricula and training programs. At the bedside, the study supports the integration of the closed-suction system as a standard practice in intensive care settings. It empowers nurses with practical insights to minimize patient complications, such as hypoxemia, infection, and cardiopulmonary instability during suctioning procedures. Furthermore, the findings underscore the critical role of nurses in selecting and implementing the most appropriate suctioning technique, promoting patient safety, and improving clinical outcomes. By conducting this research, new knowledge was added to existing nursing practices regarding the effectiveness, safety, and practical application of suctioning systems. It also highlighted the need for ongoing education, training, and resource availability to ensure that best practices are followed consistently across healthcare settings.
