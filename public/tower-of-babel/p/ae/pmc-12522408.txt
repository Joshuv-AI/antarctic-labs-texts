Anaemia of inflammation and cancer risk according to comorbidity in patients aged 40–89 years: a Danish cohort study
Authors: Astrid Boennelykke, Lene Sofie Granfeldt Østgård, Alina Zalounina Falborg, Peter Vedsted
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAnaemia of inflammation represents a common form of anaemia. It is often clinically linked to pre-existing comorbidities but can also be a sign of cancer. This challenges the diagnostic evaluation, making it difficult to determine whether anaemia of inflammation results from existing comorbidity or indicates an underlying malignancy. However, the cancer risk remains poorly investigated in patients with new-onset anaemia of inflammation in relation to pre-existing comorbidity. Therefore, we aimed to assess the cancer risk in patients with new-onset anaemia of inflammation in a general practice setting according to comorbidity status.MethodsThis observational population-based cohort study was based on data from laboratory systems in the North- and the Central Denmark Region combined with data in selected national healthcare registers. We included patients aged 40–89 years with new-onset anaemia of inflammation between January 2013 and December 2018. Patients with a prior history of cancer were excluded. The primary outcome measure was the cancer incidence within 12 months of the anaemia index date, stratified by comorbidity.ResultsA total of 2,640 patients (median [interquartile interval] age 70 [62–79] years; 61.1% men) were included. In total, 16.9% (95% confidence interval (CI): 15.5–18.3) of patients with new-onset anaemia of inflammation were diagnosed with cancer within 12 months. Notably, patients without pre-existing comorbidities had the highest cancer incidence at 20.8% (CI: 18.7–22.8), while those with pre-existing comorbidity also demonstrated a significant cancer incidence of 11.7% (CI: 9.8–13.5). This represents a cancer incidence proportion ratio of 1.78 (CI:1.48–2.15). In the adjusted Cox regression analysis, the cancer risk was significantly higher in patients without pre-existing comorbidity compared to patients without comorbidity (hazard ratio 1.89, CI:1.54–2.32).ConclusionThis study showed a significant cancer incidence linked with new-onset anaemia of inflammation, particularly in patients without pre-existing comorbidities, which doubled the cancer incidence compared to patients with pre-existing comorbidity. It is crucial to remain aware of the cancer risk when assessing patients with new-onset anaemia of inflammation, especially in those without pre-existing comorbidity, to ensure appropriate detection of potential underlying malignancy in this high-risk population.Keywords: Anemia, Cohort studies, Comorbidity, Denmark, Early detection of cancer, General practice, Inflammation

Introduction

Anaemia is a common condition. It is associated with impaired quality of life, increased morbidity and higher mortality rates [1–3]. Anaemia affects over 1.6 billion people globally, with a prevalence of 24% among the elderly [4]. The demographic development means that the prevalence of anaemia is increasing [5], which marks anaemia as an important health concern requiring attention.

Anaemia of inflammation is one of the most prevalent types of anaemia. It is defined by a ferritin level above 100 µg/L in the presence of systemic inflammation [6]. Anaemia of inflammation is frequently recognised as ‘chronic anaemia’, which is associated with comorbidities [6, 7]. However, anaemia of inflammation is also seen as a nonspecific indicator of potential undetected serious diseases, including cancer. Therefore, it is crucial to carefully evaluate patients with anaemia of inflammation to uncover potentially underlying cancer and reduce missed diagnostic opportunities [8]. As the first-line access to health care and gatekeepers to secondary care, general practitioners have a crucial role in diagnosing and classifying patients with anaemia of inflammation to ensure relevant diagnostic investigation and treatment of underlying disease.

A previous study from our group found a 15–18% cancer risk in patients with new-onset anaemia of inflammation detected in general practice [9]. This is more than a 10-fold increase in the overall cancer incidence compared to the general population. Further, these patients had a 10- to 30-fold higher incidence of some specific cancer types [9]. However, no evidence exists on the cancer risk in patients with new-onset anaemia of inflammation in relation to pre-existing comorbidity. Anaemia of inflammation is highly associated with comorbidities, which may constitute a reasonable clinical explanation for the anaemia. However, anaemia of inflammation is also characterised by an underlying inflammation, which may also be seen in cancer [10]. Moreover, certain comorbidities are associated with an increased risk of cancer, such as diabetes [11–13], hypertension [12, 14], chronic kidney disease [12, 15] and inflammatory bowel disease [16]. Thus, patients with anaemia of inflammation may present complex disease mechanisms that need careful consideration.

Therefore, we aimed to explore the cancer risk in patients with new-onset anaemia of inflammation in relation to comorbidity in an unselected general practice population.

Methods

Setting

Denmark has a population of approximately 5.8 million inhabitants and consists of five healthcare regions with similar healthcare services [17]. This study was conducted in the North Denmark Region (0.6 million inhabitants) and the Central Denmark Region (1.3 million inhabitants) [18]. Denmark has a public, tax-funded healthcare system, and general practice and hospitals offer services free of charge [19, 20]. Nearly all (99%) Danes are registered with a general practice, which they must consult for medical advice. General practitioners (GPs) are gatekeepers to specialised healthcare, except for emergencies [20].

Design and data sources

We conducted an observational population-based study using data from the regional laboratory information systems and the Danish health registers. The data included information on healthcare services provided, and these data can be linked at the individual level through the unique civil personal registration (CPR) number allocated to all Danish residents at birth or immigration [21]. All laboratory tests requested by general practice or hospital departments and analysed at hospital-based clinical biochemistry laboratories are registered in the electronic regional laboratory information systems [22]. Point-of-care tests (POCTs) analysed in general practice laboratories (e.g. C-reactive protein and haemoglobin) are not included [22].

Study population

We used the regional laboratory information systems to identify the source population, which consisted of all patients aged 40–89 years with anaemia detected in general practice or at Hospitals in the period from 1 January 2013 until 31 December 2018 (Fig. 1). According to the Danish reference intervals, anaemia was defined as a haemoglobin level below 134 g/L (8.3 mmol/L) for men and below 118 g/L (7.3 mmol/L) for women [23]. We restricted the study cohort to patients with new-onset anaemia of inflammation detected in general practice. We classified anaemia of inflammation based on measurements of haemoglobin, ferritin (> 100 µg/L) and C-reactive protein (increased level) requested by GPs within 31 days of the anaemia index date, as defined in the guideline for unexplained anaemia by the Danish Society for Gastroenterology and Hepatology [25, 26]. We defined new-onset anaemia as no anaemia in the preceding 15 months registered in the laboratory information system (to exclude patients with chronic anaemia having annual consultations). We defined the date of new-onset anaemia in the inclusion period as the index date and did not allow patients to re-enter the cohort. Further, we excluded patients with a prior history of cancer (all cancer types were included (using ICD-10 codes C00-C96) except non-melanoma skin cancers (ICD-10 code C44) registered in the Danish Cancer Registry) [24] as we aimed to investigate the risk of a first-time diagnosis of cancer.

Exposure

The exposure in this study was comorbidity (recorded in the Danish National Patient Register [27] or the Psychiatric Central Research Register [28] within the ten years preceding the anaemia index date).

Outcomes

The main outcome measure was a cancer diagnosis. We included all cancer types, except for non-melanoma skin cancers, as these are usually non-fatal. We grouped the specific cancer types into eight categories: breast, gastrointestinal, gynaecological, haematological, kidney or urinary tract, male genital, respiratory system, and other cancers. We measured all outcomes within 12 months of the anaemia index date.

Characteristics of study population

Variables used to characterise the study population were age, sex, anaemia severity, comorbidity, educational level, disposable income and cohabitation. Age was categorised into five groups (40–49, 50–59, 60–69, 70–79 and 80–89 years). Educational level was categorised into three groups (‘low’, ‘medium’ and ‘high’) according to the International Standard Classification of Education (ISCED). Disposable income was categorised into tertiles (‘low’, ‘medium’ and ‘high’). Cohabitation was categorised into two groups (‘living alone’ and ‘living with a partner’). Anaemia severity was defined according to WHO’s guideline and categorised into three groups (‘mild anaemia’ with a haemoglobin value between 110 g/L (6.8 mmol/L) to normal value, ‘moderate anaemia’ with a haemoglobin value between 110 g/L (6.8 mmol/L) and 80 g/L (5.0 mmol/L) and ‘severe anaemia’ with a haemoglobin value below 80 g/L (5.0 mmol/L)). Comorbidities were included if recorded in the Danish National Patient Register [27] or the Psychiatric Central Research Register [28] (i.e. comorbidities registered at hospitals) within the ten years preceding the anaemia index date. Comorbidities were categorised into equally weighted chronic disease groups: cardiovascular disease, hypertension, diabetes, mental illness, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, neurological disorders, kidney disease, liver disease, inflammatory bowel disease and arthritis [29–31]. An overview of the chronic disease groups, the included diseases, and ICD-10 codes are displayed in the supplemental material in a previous paper [32].

Statistical analysis

Cohort characteristics according to comorbidity were summarised in percentages. We followed the patients with new-onset anaemia of inflammation from the anaemia index date until the date of a first cancer diagnosis, death, moving out of included regions or end of 12-month follow-up, whichever came first. We assessed the hazard ratios (HRs) by applying a multivariable cause-specific Cox regression model, treating competing risk (death) as censored. We detected no violations of the proportional hazard assumptions evaluated from log-minus log plots. We adjusted the analyses for age. Further, we used the Aalen-Johansen estimator to calculate the cumulative incidence proportion of patients diagnosed with cancer within 12-month follow-up. We chose the Aalen-Johansen estimator over the Kaplan-Meier estimator to account for death as a competing event because Kaplan-Meier estimates can be biased in the presence of competing risks [33]. We assessed the time to a cancer diagnosis within 12 months of the anaemia index date. We stratified the analyses by comorbidity (yes/no) and sex. Further, we estimated the cancer incidence proportion stratified by comorbidity (yes/no) and age group and according to anaemia severity. We also estimated the cumulative cancer incidence proportion among comorbid patients stratified by comorbidity groups. The difference in cancer incidence between groups of interest was evaluated using the cancer incidence proportion ratio (CIPR), which is based on the pseudo-values approach [34].

This study followed the Strengthening the reporting of observational studies in epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guideline. All analyses were performed in Stata®, version 16.

Results

We included 2,640 patients with new-onset anaemia of inflammation in the analyses (Fig. 1). The median age was 70 years (interquartile interval (IQI) 62–79) (men: 69 years, IQI 61–77, women: 72 years, IQI 64–82), and 61.1% were men (1,612). Overall, 75.2% (1,984) had mild anaemia, 42.6% (1,124) had comorbidities; the most frequent being cardiovascular disease (23%, 606) (Table 1).

A total of 16.9% (CI: 15.5–18.3) of patients with new-onset anaemia of inflammation were diagnosed with cancer within 12 months. The cancer incidence was significantly higher among patients without pre-existing comorbidity (20.8%, CI: 18.7–22.8) compared to patients with pre-existing comorbidity (11.7%, CI: 9.8–13.5) giving a CIPR = 1.78 (CI:1.48–2.15) (Fig. 2). In the adjusted Cox regression analysis, patients without comorbidity had a significantly higher cancer risk compared to patients with a pre-existing comorbidity; with a HR of 1.89 (CI:1.54–2.32) for overall cancer. Further, of the specific cancer types, significant values were seen for gastrointestinal cancer (HR 1.47, CI: 1.00-2.15), respiratory system cancer (HR 1.77, CI: 1.16–2.70), and for haematological cancer (HR 3.06, CI:1.70–5.50).

Of the patients without pre-existing comorbidity, 21.3% (CI: 18.7–23.9) of men and 19.9% (CI: 16.7–23.2) of women were diagnosed with cancer within 12 months. Respiratory system cancer was the most frequent cancer type in men (6.4%, CI: 4.8-8.0), and gastrointestinal cancer (5.7%, CI: 3.8–7.6) was the most frequent cancer type in women.

Of the patients with pre-existing comorbidity, 13.2% (CI: 10.7–15.8) of men and 9.3% (CI: 6.6–12) of women were diagnosed with cancer within 12 months. Gastrointestinal cancer was the most frequent cancer type in both men (4.0%, CI: 2.5–5.5) and women (3.1%, CI: 1.5–4.7).

Across the age groups in patients with anaemia of inflammation, the highest cancer incidence was observed in patients aged 70–79 years, in both the population without pre-existing comorbidity (24.1%, CI: 20.2–28.0) and in the one with pre-existing comorbidity (15.0%, CI: 11.4–18.6) (Fig. 3).

The cancer incidence in patients with anaemia of inflammation increased according to anaemia severity; the cancer incidence was 15.1% (CI: 13.6–16.7) in patients with mild anaemia, 21.5% (CI: 18.2–24.7) in patients with moderate anaemia, and 36.1% (CI: 20.4–51.8) in patients with severe anaemia. Among patients without pre-existing comorbidity, the cancer incidence was 18.0% (CI: 15.8–20.3) in mild anaemia, 27.9% (CI: 23.2–32.6) in moderate anaemia, and 55.0% (CI: 33.2–76.8) in severe anaemia. Among patients with pre-existing comorbidity, the cancer incidence was 11.1% (CI: 9.0-13.3) in mild anaemia, 13.2% (9.2–17.3) in moderate anaemia, whereas the cancer incidence in severe anaemia could not be determined due to insufficient events to protect confidentiality.

In patients with pre-existing comorbidity, the highest cancer incidence was observed in patients with diabetes (15.2%, CI: 10.2–20.4) (Fig. 4). Gastrointestinal cancer was the most frequent cancer type seen in patients with cardiovascular disease (3.8%, CI: 2.3–5.3), hypertension (3.6%, CI: 2.0-5.3) or diabetes (6.9%, CI: 3.3–10.5). Respiratory system cancer was the most frequent cancer type seen in patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (4.1%, CI: 1.1–7.1) and in patients with mental illness (2.6%, CI: 0.4–4.9).

Discussion

Principal findings

This cohort study, including 2,640 patients with new-onset anaemia of inflammation, revealed a 12-month cancer incidence of 16.9% in an unselected general practice population. Patients without pre-existing comorbidity had a higher cancer incidence (20.8%) compared to patients with comorbidity (11.7%), corresponding to nearly twice the incidence (cancer incidence proportion ratio of 1.78). The highest cancer incidence was observed for patients aged 70–79 years, both in those without pre-existing comorbidity (24.1%) and in those with comorbidity (15.0%). In the group with pre-existing comorbidity, patients with diabetes had the highest cancer incidence (15.2%).

Strengths and limitations

This large-scale population-based cohort study utilised comprehensive data from the Danish healthcare system, which is known for its accuracy, completeness and possibility to link laboratory and register data at an individual level [22, 35]. This robust linkage facilitates near-complete follow-up and reduces the risk of selection bias and information bias. The setting in general practice, embedded within a universal healthcare system, enhances the real-world applicability and generalisability of the findings to similar healthcare systems globally.

However, as with any observational study, residual confounding and selection bias may be a concern. We accounted for key variables in our analyses. However, we investigate whether a new-onset anaemia of inflammation is a sign of cancer. Thus, the cancer is not caused by the anaemia. Therefore, it would not be correct to adjust for other variables as e.g. medication use would not confound the real link between anaemia as a sign of cancer. Thus, our analysis provides exploratory insights rather than definitive causal conclusions.

Additionally, the study lacks specific information on the clinical reasons for GP consultations. This could have introduced confounding by indication, as those consulting their GP might be more ill.

As we used laboratory-based data, we did not obtain the results from POCTs analysed in general practice. Although hospital laboratory tests would subsequently have verified many cases, this exclusion may have led to an underrepresentation of (most likely) mild cases of anaemia. POCTs are not widely used to measure haemoglobin level [36, 37]. However, this does not entirely exclude the risk of misclassification of new-onset anaemia (as previous anaemia could have been undetected by POCTs). As such, these aspects could potentially have resulted in an overestimation of the overall incidence.

Furthermore, detection bias might have occurred since clinicians may be more aware of referring patients with anaemia to further diagnostic investigations. Nevertheless, the real-world data and broad population representation will likely reflect clinical practice. These methodological considerations underscore the exploratory nature of our findings, which calls for further research to validate and expand upon these results.

Comparison with other studies

To our knowledge, this is the first large-scale study exploring the risk of specific cancer types in patients with new-onset anaemia of inflammation in relation to comorbidity in a general practice population. One previous small-scale study showed that 23% of patients with anaemia of inflammation in general practice had underlying cancer [38], which is higher compared to our findings. However, that study used various durations of follow-up within six years of inclusion and excluded patients with pre-existing comorbidity [38]. Our results align with previous studies indicating that patients with anaemia of inflammation and combined inflammatory iron deficiency anaemia (CIIDA), which are both characterised by underlying inflammation, exhibit a higher cancer risk than patients with iron deficiency anaemia (IDA) [9]. Underlying inflammation is associated with an increased cancer risk, and the risk increases as the level of inflammation rises [10]. Thus, the simultaneous presence of anaemia and inflammation can be seen as a red flag for potential underlying malignancy and should receive clinical attention. Our findings on ageing and increased cancer incidence support existing evidence that ageing is linked to an increased cancer risk, and this may also reflect the vulnerable and complex biological processes that accompany ageing [39, 40].

Increasing anaemia severity was linked with a higher cancer incidence, which is in line with other studies [41]. This could indicate more pronounced symptoms and disease progression, which might lead to detection bias.

Conclusions and implications

We found a significant cancer incidence linked with new-onset anaemia of inflammation in general practice, with a notably high incidence in patients without pre-existing comorbidities (twice the incidence compared to patients with pre-existing comorbidity). This underscores an important clinical implication that these patients require comprehensive diagnostic evaluation to uncover potential underlying malignancy. At the same time, patients with pre-existing comorbidities still face a significant cancer risk in case of new-onset anaemia. Assessing patients with both anaemia and pre-existing comorbidities may be challenging, as it can be difficult to determine whether anaemia is attributable to existing comorbidity or an undiagnosed malignancy. Increased awareness and careful consideration are essential to ensure that these high-risk patients with new-onset anaemia of inflammation, especially those without pre-existing comorbidity, receive sufficient evaluations of potential underlying malignancy to ensure timely cancer diagnosis, which may enhance treatment options and prognosis.
