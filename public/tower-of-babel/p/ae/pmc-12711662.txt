Trilaciclib triggers a neutrophil-related immune response and sensitizes non-small cell lung cancer to anti-PD-1 therapy
Authors: Yuan Gao, Yuchao He, Chengmeng Wang, Ran Zuo, Xiangdong Tian, Duo Zuo, Yi Luo, Wei Liu, Yuanying Feng, Aomei Ling, Ping Yue, Wenchen Gong, Yu Wang, Liwei Chen, Zhiyong Liu, Peng Chen, Hua Guo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryImmunotherapy-based combination approaches have improved treatment efficacy in advanced non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), but progressive disease remains a challenge. Trilaciclib is a cyclin-dependent kinase 4/6 inhibitor approved for myelopreservation in extensive-stage small cell lung cancer (ES-SCLC). Our results demonstrate that trilaciclib has antitumor potential in NSCLC without significant toxicity. It reprograms the tumor immune microenvironment by primarily increasing antitumor neutrophils and CD8+ T cells. Trilaciclib induces tumor cell senescence and the senescence-associated secretory phenotype in a cGAS-STING-dependent manner, which further facilitates the infiltration and activation of CD177+ neutrophils with anti-tumor properties. These neutrophils enhance CD8+ effector T cell activation and promote antitumor immunity. Additionally, activated CD8+ T cells recruit and activate neutrophils, forming a positive feedback loop. Combining trilaciclib with anti-PD-1 antibodies presents a promising strategy for NSCLC treatment.Keywords: non-small cell lung cancer, trilaciclib, neutrophils, senescence, cGAS-STING pathway, immune checkpoint blockade

Introduction

Lung cancer, particularly non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), remains a leading cause of cancer-related mortality.1,2 Although immunotherapy improves survival in advanced NSCLC, the overall objective response rate to single-agent immune checkpoint inhibitors remains less than 20%.3,4,5,6 Based on these considerations, the combination of immunotherapy with chemotherapy, targeted therapy, or other immunotherapies has further improved efficacy,7,8 but are limited by toxicity and resistance.7,9,10,11,12 Therefore, promising combination approaches are urgently required to increase response rates and minimize adverse events.

The tumor microenvironment (TME) plays a critical role in the response of immunotherapy.13 Mounting evidence has shown that the recruited and reprogrammed immune cells in the TME can affect the efficacy of immunotherapy.14,15,16 Tumor-associated neutrophils (TANs) are among the most important immune cells in TME. Although TANs have been characterized as short-lived and have a differentiated (non-proliferative) status,17 recent studies have revealed the heterogeneity and diversity of neutrophils within tumors.18,19 TANs have either classical pro-tumoral (N2) or anti-tumoral (N1) phenotypes.20,21,22 Therapeutic strategies have been proposed to counteract the pro-tumor effects of TANs, mainly by blocking neutrophil recruitment23 and reprogramming TANs from the N2 to the N1 phenotype.21,24,25,26 Therefore, targeting TANs and facilitating their reprogramming have become the primary aims to allow the TME to overcome immunotherapy resistance.

Increasing evidence indicates that targeting oncogenic driver kinases can reprogram the TME and serve as a promising combination therapy with immunotherapy.27,28 CDK4/6 inhibitors (CDK4/6i) have been approved for treating hormone receptor-positive breast cancer.29 Recently, multiple studies have reported that, in addition to inducing tumor cell cytostasis,30 CDK4/6i can also play an immunomodulatory role in the TME. Several recent studies have demonstrated that CDK4/6i upregulates major histocompatibility complex (MHC)-I expression, activates T cells, induces T cell memory, and suppresses the proliferation of regulatory T cells (Tregs).31,32

Trilaciclib, a potent CDK4/6 inhibitor approved for ES-SCLC, preserves hematopoietic stem cells during chemotherapy.33,34,35 Clinical trials in advanced breast cancer indicate it may also improve overall survival,36 suggesting additional antitumor mechanisms such as immune modulation. Recent work shows trilaciclib remodels the TME by altering CD8+ T cells/Tregs ratios.37 However, its impact on NSCLC and potential to induce immune-favorable TME reprogramming remains unexplored.

In this study, we found that the antitumor efficacy of trilaciclib in immunocompetent mice was superior to that in immunodeficient mice. Interestingly, we found that trilaciclib induces cellular senescence and activates the cGAS-STING pathway, leading to a senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP). It facilitates the infiltration and activation of CD177+ TANs, activates CD8+ T cells, and remodels the TME. Combining trilaciclib with an anti-PD-1 antibody may further improve the treatment efficacy. Thus, our preclinical results provide a rationale for further clinical treatment and offer novel therapeutic strategies for NSCLC.

Results

Trilaciclib suppresses progression of NSCLC

To evaluate the antitumor effects of trilaciclib in NSCLC, we first analyzed The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) dataset and found frequent alterations in the CDK4/6–retinoblastoma (RB) pathway—such as CDKN2A deletion and CDK4/6 amplification—with RB1 intact in more than 90% of cases (Figure S1A), suggesting potential sensitivity to trilaciclib therapy.

Currently, CDK4/6i is approved by the Food and Drug Administration for patients with hormone receptor-positive (ER+) and human epidermal growth factor receptor 2-negative (HER2−) breast cancer.29 To explore the antitumor potential of trilaciclib, we selected MCF-7 cells (an ER+ breast cancer cell line) as a positive control and NSCLC cell lines (A549, H1299, and Lewis lung carcinoma [LLC]) to conduct in vitro clone formation. We found that trilaciclib inhibited proliferation of NSCLC cells in a dose-dependent manner (Figures 1A and 1B). We subcutaneously inoculated the murine NSCLC cells into immunocompetent and immunodeficient mice to further test their antitumor activity in vivo (Figure 1C). The tumor volume and weight were significantly decreased in immunocompetent mice bearing subcutaneous LLC (Figures 1D–1F) and CMT167 tumors (Figures S1B–S1D) after treatment with trilaciclib for 14 days, and survival was prolonged (Figure 1I). However, in immunodeficient mice, the trilaciclib group showed minor reduction in tumor volume and weight, but there was no statistical difference compared with the control group (Figures 1G, 1H, and S1E). Moreover, trilaciclib treatment induced consistent, concentration-dependent growth inhibition across eight well-characterized NSCLC patient-derived organoid models (6 adenocarcinomas, 1 squamous cell carcinoma, and 1 adenosquamous carcinoma) within 72 h (Figures S1I and S1J; Table S1). Collectively, these data demonstrate that trilaciclib inhibits NSCLC progression.

The tumors of immunocompetent mice were sectioned and subjected to hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining. We found a significant increase in inflammatory cells infiltration and lower tumor viability after trilaciclib treatment (Figures 1J and S1K). A TUNEL assay of the tumor tissues showed greater induction of apoptosis by the trilaciclib treatment than by the vehicle (Figures 1K and S1L). In addition, no obvious decrease in body weight was detected in either group of mice before or after drug administration (Figures S1F–S1H). No noticeable histological changes were observed with H&E staining of the major organs such as the heart, liver, spleen, lung, or kidney after trilaciclib treatment (Figure S2A). Peripheral blood was drawn from mice, and routine blood count parameters and biochemical changes were collected for analysis. The levels of biochemical parameters, including creatinine (Cr), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) in the serum were not significantly different from those in the control group (Figures S2B–S2E). Similarly, blood analysis showed no differences in the levels of white blood cells, red blood cells, hemoglobin, or platelets between the two groups (Figures S2F–S2I). Thus, we speculate that trilaciclib may promote immune cell infiltration to achieve better antitumor effects without significant adverse reactions. These results prompted us to further explore the specific modulatory effects of trilaciclib on the immune response.

Trilaciclib promotes recruitment of neutrophils into the TME

To investigate the mechanism of trilaciclib’s antitumor effects, we performed single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) on subcutaneous LLC tumors harvested before exceeding 2,000 mm3 (typically 25 days post-implantation). (Figures 2A and S3A–S3C). Following rigorous quality control and unsupervised clustering, we delineated seven major cellular clusters encompassing critical immune components (neutrophils, macrophages, T cells, and B cells), which were manually assigned based on the expression of known cell type-specific markers. The observed immune cell enrichment implies trilaciclib’s potential to potentiate antitumor immunity through enhanced immune infiltration, thereby directing our subsequent investigation toward the tumor immune microenvironment (Figures 2B and S3D).

Single-cell analysis revealed that trilaciclib treatment shifted the immune landscape from a macrophage-dominated state (F4/80 and CD68) to a neutrophil-enriched one (Ly6g, S100a8, and Csf3r) (Figure S3E). These tumor-infiltrating neutrophils exhibited upregulated pathways related to chemotaxis, killing, and apoptosis, indicating a mature and activated phenotype (Figures S3F and S3G). We estimated the proportion of infiltrating immune cells in the TME and found that neutrophils comprised a larger proportion after treatment with trilaciclib (Figure 2C). Because immature and mature neutrophils can infiltrate tumoral tissue,38 we investigated the relationship between the maturation and functional states of neutrophil populations. The transcriptional levels of Cxcr2 versus Cxcr4 in regions of the UMAP (Figures S3F and S3H) suggest that trilaciclib-treated neutrophils are more closely associated with mature neutrophils.

We performed multicolor flow cytometry and found that treatment with trilaciclib resulted in an increase in the proportion of CD45+ immune cells (Figures S4C, S5A, and S5B), in which neutrophil counts were significantly increased (Figures 2D, S4A, and S7A). Immunohistochemical (IHC) staining of mouse tumor tissues was evaluated to determine the proportion of Ly6g+ cells, a marker for neutrophils, which were found to be markedly increased (Figure 2E). We further analyzed the proportion of immune cell populations and identified increased T cells and B cells (Figures S4B and S5C–S5F) and decreased macrophages, including M1 macrophages and M2 macrophages, but an increased M1/M2 macrophage ratio after trilaciclib treatment (Figures S4C and S5G–S5M). Simultaneously, we orthotopically implanted murine luciferase-expressing LLC cells into the lungs of syngeneic mice (Figures S6A and S6B). The response trends and immune infiltration patterns of subcutaneous and in situ models to trilaciclib treatment were consistent. Trilaciclib also suppressed the proliferation of tumor cells (Figures S6C and S6D) and induced a similar immune remodeling profile: expanded T cell infiltration with increased CD8+ T cells and CD4+ T cells coupled with reduced Tregs frequency (Figures S6E–S6K); marked elevation of cytotoxic TNF-α+ CD8+ T cells and a non-significant trend toward increased IFN-γ+ CD8+ T cells and GZMB+ CD8+ T (Figures S6L–S6Q), indicative of CD8+ T cell activation; no significant changes in B cell populations (Figures S6R and S6S); and substantial neutrophil recruitment accompanied by decreased total macrophage infiltration, without detectable shifts in M1/M2 polarization (Figures S6T–S6Z). These data suggest that while absolute immune cell densities may vary, the functional immune landscape is conserved across implantation sites. These results were consistent with those of the scRNA-Seq analysis (Figure 2C).

Collectively, our data indicate that trilaciclib remodels the TME into an immune-active state characterized by robust neutrophil infiltration, aligning with reports of therapy-induced neutrophils exhibiting anti-tumor functions through increased antigen presentation and T cell co-stimulation.39,40,41 After trilaciclib treatment, there was a substantial reduction in macrophages, including M1-type macrophages, in the TME, whereas strong infiltration of neutrophils was observed. It has been proposed that TANs have phenotypic heterogeneity and functional plasticity, and that their production and mobilization from the bone marrow to the blood is tightly controlled.18 Therefore, we further investigated the origin of the infiltrated neutrophils in the TME after trilaciclib treatment. Flow cytometry revealed a specific increase of neutrophils within the TME, but not in the peripheral blood or bone marrow, ruling out enhanced myelopoiesis (Figures 2D, 2F and S7A). In comparison with the control group, the expression level of chemokines and cytokines known to regulate chemotaxis of neutrophils was significantly elevated, while the expression of immunosuppressive cytokines was downregulated after treatment (Figure 2G). Overall, these results indicate that trilaciclib treatment can remodel the TME, promoting chemotaxis and neutrophil infiltration, rather than stimulating bone marrow hematopoiesis toward myeloid cell lineage differentiation.

Trilaciclib induces tumor cell senescence and promotes neutrophil infiltration in the TME

To identify the driver of neutrophil infiltration, we analyzed our scRNA-seq data. CellChat analysis revealed that macrophages-tumor cell interactions decreased, while neutrophil-tumor cell interactions significantly increased (Figures S8A and S8B). This was also consistent with changes in the proportion of infiltrating immune cells when trilaciclib remodeled TME (Figure 2C). Therefore, we hypothesized that the effect of trilaciclib on tumor cells may play an important role in regulation of TME. The pathways related to cell senescence were significantly enriched in tumor cells following trilaciclib treatment (Figure 3A). Previous studies have shown that in the context of anticancer therapy, tumors can enter therapy-induced senescence to trigger antitumor immunity, which is similar to immunogenic effects.16,31,42 Increased senescence-associated β-galactosidase (SA-β-gal) activity is a hallmark of senescence.43 We performed SA-β-gal staining on tumor tissues and NSCLC cell lines, and confirmed a marked increase in SA-β-gal+ cells following trilaciclib treatment (Figures 3B, 3C, and 3E). Stable cell-cycle arrest is a hallmark of cellular senescence.43 Thus, the cell cycle was analyzed by flow cytometry, and we established that trilaciclib induced G1 cell-cycle arrest (Figures S8C and S8D). These findings suggested that trilaciclib induces tumor cell to enter a state of therapy-induced senescence.

Cellular senescence is frequently accompanied by the secretion of cytokines, chemokines, and other molecules, a phenotype termed the SASP, which mediates the different effects of senescence on the tissue microenvironment.43 Notably, after trilaciclib treatment of A549 and H1299 cells, the mRNA expression levels of SASP-related factors43 were elevated (Figures S8E and S8F). Analysis of conditioned media from A549 and H1299 cells revealed increased secretion of SASP-related cytokine proteins in response to trilaciclib treatment (Figures 3D, 3F, and S8G). These results suggest that trilaciclib can induce cell senescence that accompanies the SASP. Several studies have shown that SASP factors secreted by senescent cells enhance the infiltration and activation of immune cells.44,45 Therefore, we compared the effects of the different treatment conditions on neutrophil chemotaxis in vitro. The neutrophil chemotaxis assay showed greater chemotaxis by the tumor cell-conditioned medium (CM) than by treatment with vehicle or trilaciclib (Figures 3G, 3H, S8H, and S8I). It has been speculated that tumor cells play an important role in neutrophil chemotaxis. Moreover, exposure of neutrophils to trilaciclib-treated A549 and H1299 cell CM resulted in increased chemotactic capacity compared to those exposed to CM from control cells (Figures 3G and 3H).

Trilaciclib promotes neutrophil infiltration mediated by SASP, dependent on tumor cell cGAS-STING pathway activation

Next, we identified different markers of cellular senescence to further investigate the mechanism of trilaciclib-induced senescence. Senescence-associated proliferation arrest is a hallmark of senescent cells, moreover the p53/p21 and p16INK4a/RB pathways are critical mediators.46 p16INK4a directly interacts with and inhibits CDK4/6, and p21 suppresses CDK2-cyclin E activity, both of which retain RB in its hypophosphorylated state to prevent progression from G1 to S of the cell cycle.43,47 Thus, the levels of p53, p21, p16, RB phosphorylated at serine 807 and 811 (p-RBS807–811), and RB were determined in A549 cells using western blot analysis. We found that p53, p21, and p16 expression levels did not increase, whereas RB hyperphosphorylation was abrogated (Figure 4A). Comparable results were obtained with H1299 cells (Figure S9A). These results suggest that trilaciclib-induced cell-cycle arrest is independent of p21 and p53 and is mediated through the inhibition of CDK4/6, as H1299 cells are deficient in p53,48 retaining RB hypophosphorylation.

Senescent cells frequently exhibit nuclear structural alterations, including the formation of cytoplasmic chromatin fragments (CCFs) due to nuclear membrane blebbing, often associated with reduced lamin B1 expression.43,49,50 In trilaciclib-treated A549 and H1299 cells, lamin B1 downregulation was observed by western blotting and immunofluorescence microscopy (Figures 4A, 4B, S9A, and S9B). Next, we observed nuclear chromosomes in the form of DAPI+ blebs budding from the nucleus, resulting in CCFs in trilaciclib-treated A549 cells, while no significant CCFs were observed in the control group (Figure 4B). A similar result was obtained with H1299 cells (Figure S9B). Studies have shown that senescence-related CCFs can act as an immune stimulator to rapidly initiate the innate immune response, while cGAS has attracted much attention as an important DNA sensor in innate immunity.51 The type I interferon (IFN) response is an important characteristic of the cGAS-STING pathway.52 GO enrichment analysis revealed that several IFN response-related pathways were enriched following trilaciclib treatment (Figure 4C). The cGAS immunofluorescence was co-localized with DAPI+ nuclear bubble budding areas in trilaciclib-treated A549 and H1299 cells, indicating that cGAS might be involved in sensing CCFs (Figures 4B and S9B). Persistent cell-cycle arrest often induces a DNA damage response and aggravates cell senescence.43 H2AX phosphorylation is a sensitive marker of double-stranded breaks in response to DNA damage.53 Western blotting confirmed that the level of γH2AX in A549 and H1299 cells increased after trilaciclib treatment (Figures 4A and S9A). The innate immune response is triggered by cytosolic DNA (cytoDNA) following DNA damage. Immunofluorescence assays of both A549 and H1299 cells showed that cytoDNA co-localized with cGAS in the trilaciclib group, indicating that cytoDNA might be sensed by cGAS (Figures 4B and S9B). Therefore, we hypothesized that cGAS is activated by recognizing cytoDNA, thus promoting the conduction and activation of downstream signaling pathways. As a key downstream effector of the cGAS-STING pathway, IFN-β critically activates immune responses.54,55 The STING-IFN-β axis mediates CD4+ T cell-cDC1 crosstalk to enhance CD8+ T cell priming through cross-presentation, bridging innate and adaptive immunity to create an immunostimulatory TME.56 Notably, we detected the levels of IFN-β and IFN-γ in tumor tissues and found that their secretion levels significantly increased after treatment with trilaciclib (Figures 4D and 4E). Meanwhile, cGAS-STING pathway components were detected by western blotting, and it was found that the phosphorylation of RB was inhibited in a time-dependent manner, whereas the phosphorylation of STING, TBK1, IRF3, p65, as well as ISG15 and ISGylated proteins expression significantly increased in the trilaciclib group and changed dynamically with time in A549 and H1299 cells (Figures 4F and S9C–S9E). These findings indicate that the activation of the cGAS-STING pathway.

Previous reports have suggested that the activation of cGAS-STING promotes type I IFN production and pro-inflammatory responses, which is also consistent with our findings of trilaciclib-induced SASP. After combined treatment with trilaciclib and the cGAS inhibitor G140, the SA-β-gal+ population of A549 cells was decreased (Figures 4G and 4H). Similar results were observed in H1299 cells (Figures S9F and S9G). Next, the quantitative reverse-transcription PCR (RT-qPCR) assay demonstrated that the mRNA expression levels of SASP-related factors were partially counteracted by the combined treatment with trilaciclib and G140 in A549 cells (Figure 4I), with similar results in H1299 cells (Figure S9H). Furthermore, chemotaxis assays of neutrophils showed that exposure of neutrophils to A549 cell CM following the combined treatment with G140 and trilaciclib resulted in decreased chemotactic capacity compared to those exposed to CM from trilaciclib-treated cells alone (Figures 4J and 4K). Similar results were observed for H1299 cells (Figures S9I and S9J). These results confirm that trilaciclib induces senescence and the SASP in a cGAS-STING-dependent manner, which further promotes neutrophil chemotaxis.

Trilaciclib primes neutrophils to amplify CD8+ T cell antitumor immunity

Several studies have demonstrated that neutrophil activation enhances adaptive antitumor immune responses and systemic antitumor immunity.40,57 CD8+ T cells are key players in the adaptive immune response and have received considerable attention. To investigate T cell responses at single-cell resolution, we clustered T cells into three subsets: CD4+ T, CD8+ T, and Treg, based on established marker expression (Figures 5A and S10A). The results suggested that reduced infiltration of Treg cells was accompanied by increased infiltration of CD4+ T and CD8+ T cells (Figure 5B). Single-cell sequencing results were confirmed by flow cytometry (Figures 5C–5E, S10B, S10C, and S11A). CellChat analysis showed that the interaction between CD8+ T cells and neutrophils was significantly enhanced after treatment (Figures 5F and S10D). CD8+ T cell activation was promoted by the interaction of the receptor CD8A/B1 (CD8 subunit) on T cells with MHC class I antigen ligands on neutrophils (Figure S10E). Single-cell analysis consistently showed that CD8+ T cells had elevated effector function, and the exhausted CD8+ T cells were reduced after trilaciclib treatment (Figures 5G and 5H). Multicolor flow cytometry was used to assess CD8+ T cell activity. The ability of CD8+T cells to secrete tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α, granzyme B (GZMB) and IFN-γ was significantly enhanced in the trilaciclib group (Figures 5I–5K, S10F–S10H, and S11A). These results indicate that trilaciclib treatment promoted the effector function of CD8+ T cells and alleviated their exhaustion, in which neutrophils might play an important role.

The role of neutrophils in trilaciclib antitumor activity and its effect on CD8+ T cells was further investigated. We evaluated the role of neutrophils in mediating the antitumor effects of CD8+ T cells. Immunocompetent mice were randomly divided into four groups (control, trilaciclib, anti-Ly6g, and combination groups) for neutrophil depletion experiments in vivo. Neutrophil depletion in the untreated group reduced the tumor volume and weight of immunocompetent mice compared to the control group, whereas tumor volume and weight were significantly increased in the combination-treated tumors compared to the trilaciclib group (Figures 5L, 5M, and S12A). Body weight showed no obvious loss after treatment (Figure S12B). Our results showed diverse functions of neutrophils in the treatment and control groups, which agreed with previous reports in which TANs were a heterogeneous cell population. Flow cytometry confirmed successful neutrophil depletion (Figure S12F). We found an increase in the CD45+ immune cell population in the tumor tissue of neutrophil-depleted mice in the untreated group compared to the control mice, whereas a slight decrease was observed in the combination group compared to the trilaciclib group (Figure S12C). We did not observe any difference in T cells in neutrophil-depleted mice in the untreated group (Figure S12D), and there was a slight increase in CD8+ T (Figures 5N and S12E) and CD4+ T cells (Figure S12E); however, the difference was not statistically significant when compared with the control group. Furthermore, the proportions of CD3+ T cells, CD4+ T cell and CD8+ T cells were lower in the combination treatment group than in the trilaciclib group (Figures 5N, S12D, and S12E). Upon neutrophil depletion in the untreated group, we found that GZMB+ CD8+ T cells significantly increased, PD-1+ CD8+ T cells decreased, and there were no significant changes of IFN-γ+ CD8+ T cells and TNF-α+ CD8+ T cells, compared with the control group (Figures 5O, 5P, S11A, S11B, and S13A–S13D). Compared with the trilaciclib group, PD-1+-CD8+ T cells significantly increased, TNF-α+ CD8+ T cells decreased, and IFN-γ+ CD8+ T cells and GZMB+ CD8+ T cells were not significantly changed in the combination treatment group (Figures 5O, 5P, S11A, S11B, and S13A–S13D).

To further study the role of the augmented activation of T cells induced by TANs in vitro models, both T cells and neutrophils were extracted and activated from peripheral blood. As outlined previously, our results demonstrated that CM from tumor cells treated with trilaciclib sensitized tumor cells to T cell death after the addition of TANs (Figures 5Q and S14A). Previous studies have demonstrated that IFN-stimulated neutrophils induced by immunotherapy play a crucial role in activation of cytotoxic T cells,60 and a similar neutrophil subset was also identified in our study. Therefore, in vitro experiments, we neutralized IFN-γ and examined the impact of neutrophils on T cell activity and therapeutic efficacy. We found that CM from neutrophils treated with tumor CM or tumor CM with anti-IFN-γ antibody did not significantly alter T cell GZMB expression. However, CM from neutrophils exposed to trilaciclib-treated CM significantly increased T cell GZMB expression. Crucially, CM from neutrophils exposed to trilaciclib-treated CM and anti-IFN-γ antibody resulted in a significant decrease in T cell GZMB expression compared to the group without anti-IFN-γ antibody (Figure S14B). This experiment demonstrates that IFN-γ present in the trilaciclib-treated tumor cell CM is vital for priming neutrophils to acquire the ability to subsequently activate T cells, as measured by GZMB upregulation. Meanwhile, using CM from trilaciclib-treated tumor cells, we set up co-culture killing assays. We found that IFN-γ blockade directly impairs T cell cytotoxicity even without neutrophils. Similarly, T cells and neutrophils cultured in CM from trilaciclib-treated tumor cells exhibited the strongest tumor cell killing, confirming the synergistic enhancement provided by neutrophils in this therapeutic context, while, IFN-γ blockade showed significantly weaker tumor cell killing (Figures S14C and S14D). These results show that neutralizing IFN-γ directly impairs T cell cytotoxicity and partly abrogates the synergistic enhancement of tumor cell killing provided by neutrophils. Previous studies have shown that IL-12b is a critical cytokine linking neutrophil activity to cytotoxic T cell responses.60,61 Thus, we collected CM from trilaciclib-treated or untreated tumor cells, incubated it with healthy donor-derived neutrophils, and measured IL-12b secretion by ELISA. Our results demonstrated that neutrophils exposed to trilaciclib-treated tumor cell CM exhibited significantly elevated IL-12b levels compared to those stimulated with control CM (Figure S14E).

In summary, trilaciclib reprograms neutrophils into potent activators of CD8+ T cells, enhancing T cell effector function, alleviating exhaustion, and promoting tumor cell killing partly through a cytokine-dependent priming mechanism.

Trilaciclib increases the proportion and activation of neutrophils with antitumor phenotype

Trilaciclib mediates the recruitment of neutrophils to the TME and promotes T cell activation. TANs have varied functions and were analyzed and categorized into three clusters: C0–C2 (Figures 6A and S15A). Clusters C0 and C2 were enriched, whereas cluster C1 was significantly decreased in the trilaciclib group (Figure 6B). Functional enrichment analysis revealed that C0 was associated with the innate immune response, cell killing, IFN signaling, and antigen presentation. Hypoxia, glycolysis and angiogenesis were found to be significantly associated with cluster C1. Cluster C2 was significantly enriched in many signaling pathways relevant to the IFN signaling, cytokine-mediated signaling, and the inflammatory response (Figure S15B). Previous studies demonstrated that neutrophil function is associated with cell differentiation. Single-cell differentiation states were predicted using the CytoTRACE and Monocle 3 packages. Cluster C0 was likely to be in an early stage of differentiation compared with clusters C1 and C2 (Figure S15C). Gungabeesoon61 and Leslie et al.62 found that the early stages of neutrophil differentiation showed functional plasticity and drove them to differentiate into a cell subset with enhanced antitumor capabilities. We used the previously identified gene signatures from GSE101584 to distinguish the C0, C1, and C2 neutrophil subsets and found that clusters C0 and C2 were related to neutrophils (N1) with antitumor properties, especially cluster C0 (Figure 6C). The functions of TANs were further characterized, and killing, chemotaxis, and neutrophil extracellular trap formation were found to be significantly upregulated in clusters C0 and C2 (Figure 6D). Interaction analysis showed that cluster C0 had the most significant interaction with CD8+ T cells after trilaciclib treatment (Figure S15I). Peripheral blood was collected, and neutrophils were purified and co-cultured with tumor cells in vitro to monitor tumor killing. Neutrophils were capable of killing tumor cells after treatment with CM from trilaciclib-treated tumor cells, which led to the most prominent neutrophil-mediated tumor cell killing (Figures 6E, 6F, and S16A). Trilaciclib-mediated neutrophil activation is strongly dependent on its therapeutic effect on tumor cells rather than the direct effects of trilaciclib on neutrophils. The scRNA-seq sequencing revealed dramatic increases in chemotaxis, killing, NETs, and IFN responses in neutrophils after treatment with trilaciclib (Figure S15D). We performed immunofluorescent staining for NETs from mouse tumor tissue sections and observed increased formation of NETs in the trilaciclib group (Figures 6G and 6H). Accordingly, our results suggest that neutrophils are heterogeneous in TME and trilaciclib therapy facilitates neutrophil activation and functional remodeling.

Next, we compared the trilaciclib and control group through unsupervised clustering and differential gene expression analysis of the three groups of neutrophils. We found that CD177 expressions in clusters C0 and C2 significantly increased after trilaciclib treatment (Figures S15E and S15G). It was detected primarily in neutrophils after trilaciclib treatment (Figure S15H). It was also augmented by chemotaxis, killing, NETs, and IFN responses in CD177+ neutrophils (Figure S15F). Recent studies have shown that the expression of programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1), also known as CD274, on the surface of TANs is associated with tumor formation.26 Neutrophil identities were further confirmed by analyzing the expression of CD274, which was mainly expressed in the C1 and C2 neutrophil populations, especially in cluster C1 (Figure S15G). The three neutrophils’ clusters can be characterized by the expressions of CD177 and PD-L1. CD177+-CD274− is cluster C0, CD177−-CD274+ is cluster C1, and CD177+-CD274+ is cluster C2. Multicolor flow cytometry further revealed that infiltration of cluster C0 and C2 neutrophils increased, whereas cluster C1 neutrophil infiltration was decreased in the TME (Figures 6I–6K and S16B). Hence, we speculated that trilaciclib therapy might induce a transition from a protumor phenotype to the antitumor in neutrophils.

We confirmed that trilaciclib treatment induced senescence and contributed to the SASP in a cGAS-STING-dependent manner, which promoted the infiltration and activation of TANs. Our results suggest that type I IFN secretion is increased in senescent tumor cells and tissues (Figures 3D, 3F, 4D, and 4E). We speculate that the type I IFN response plays an important role in the effector functions of TANs. By further investigating the scRNA-seq data of TANs, we found an increased IFN response in clusters C0 and C2 (Figure S16C). Prior research has identified Ly6Ehi neutrophils with IFN-stimulated signatures in circulation as a baseline biomarker predictive of anti-PD-1 response in mice.60 Furthermore, we analyzed Ly6E expression in neutrophils from our scRNA-seq dataset. Strikingly, Ly6E was markedly upregulated in the treatment-induced C0 and C2 subsets (Figures S16D and S16E), with the highest expression in C0 neutrophils. Notably, the C0 subset also exhibited enhanced anti-tumor properties, supporting its functional relevance. To further explore its clinical relevance, the TAN subset gene signatures were used to group patients from TCGA with lung adenocarcinoma. The patients with high signature scores of clusters C0 and C2 had a better prognosis, and those with high signature scores of the cluster C1 had a worse prognosis (Figure 6L). In summary, trilaciclib promoted neutrophil reprogramming toward an antitumor phenotype, potentially in a type I IFN-dependent manner.

Trilaciclib induces antitumor neutrophil phenotypes to enhance the efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitors

The PD-1/PD-L1 axis plays a central role in checkpoint inhibitor-based cancer immunotherapy, transforming the landscape of cancer treatment and improving patient survival in several cancer types.65,66 Effector T cells play a crucial role in immunotherapy.67 However, it remains unknown whether innate immune cells, particularly neutrophils, are associated with the efficacy of immunotherapy. Our study showed that trilaciclib triggered an increased infiltration and activation of CD177+ neutrophils, enhancing antitumor immune responses by promoting cellular interactions. In contrast, we observed a proportion of neutrophil subsets with high PD-L1 expression during treatment (Figures 6J, 6K, and S15G). The expression of PD-L1 in neutrophils has been associated with T cell suppression and has been predicted to impair T cell immunity and obstruct immunotherapy.26 We further investigated the role of neutrophils with antitumor properties in common T cell-targeted immunotherapy. To this end, a single-cell transcriptomic gene signature of neutrophils was applied to patients with lung adenocarcinoma from GSE126044. We found a significant enrichment of neutrophil gene sets (antitumorigenic/cluster C0) after trilaciclib treatment, which was associated with the immune response (Figure 7A). Immunocompetent mice were randomly divided into four groups (control, trilaciclib, anti-PD-1, and combination) for combination therapy in vivo (Figure 7B). Anti-PD-1 antibody monotherapy demonstrated mild antitumor activity in NSCLC in vivo, and when combined with trilaciclib treatment, significantly reduced the tumor volume and weight (Figures 7C–7E), without observable toxicity (Figure S17A). Using these tumor tissues, tumor-infiltrating immune cells were characterized using flow cytometry. Interestingly, we found the most pronounced increase in the proportion of neutrophil infiltration in the combination treatment group compared to that in the other treatment groups (Figures 7F and S17B). In-depth analysis of neutrophil subsets showed that the proportion of the C0 cluster significantly increased with the combination treatment (Figures 7G and S17C), and the PD-L1 expression of clusters C1 and C2 was the lowest (Figures 7H, 7I, and S17C) compared to other treatment groups. Surprisingly, the characterization of T cell infiltration by flow cytometry demonstrated no increase in the proportion of CD4+ T or CD8+ T cells after combination therapy (Figures S17D–S17F). Wu et al.68 showed that tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes in the TME are composed of tumor-specific T cells and bystander T cells, in which PD-1+ CD25+ CD8+ T cells are the principal antitumor effector cells that respond to anti-PD-1 antibody. Based on this, we further investigated the expression levels of PD-1+ CD25+ CD8+ T cells in the individual groups. Compared to the control group, there was no significant change in the anti-PD-1 group; however, there was an increasing trend in the trilaciclib monotherapy group, and the expansion was obvious in the combined treatment group (Figures S17G and S17H).

Similarly, seven independent freshly resected NSCLC tumor tissue samples were used to construct in vitro organoid models. We performed embedding and sectioning of the mature organoids, as well as immunofluorescence staining to confirm the presence of T cells within the organoids (Figure 7J; Table S2). Subsequently, we conducted a drug sensitivity assay. The dead organoids had a scorched black center with a fragmentary appearance. The results showed that compared with the control group, the cell viability of the organoid model decreased after trilaciclib monotherapy, and the cell viability was further decreased after combined treatment with the anti-PD-1 antibody (Figures 7K and S18A). To further explore the role of neutrophils in combination therapy, peripheral blood neutrophils were extracted and added to the combination therapy group, and the results showed that the cell viability of the organoid model reached the lowest level compared to the control and other treatment groups (Figures 7K and S18A). LIVE/DEAD immunofluorescence staining corroborated these results, revealing the highest proportion of dead cells and the lowest viability in the neutrophil-supplemented combination group (Figure S18B). The aforementioned results suggest that anti-PD-1 antibody combined with trilaciclib therapy further promotes antitumor neutrophil infiltration and activation, resulting in a higher proportion of tumor-specific T cells, rather than affecting bystander T cells to initiate an antitumor immune response.

Discussion

Immunotherapy activates antitumor immunity, improving outcomes, particularly in high PD-L1 expression patients.69 Nevertheless, most PD-L1-negative patients do not benefit significantly from immunotherapy. Furthermore, only 29.8% of patients with advanced NSCLC show high PD-L1 expression,70 which greatly limits the application of immunotherapy. Although combination strategies can improve clinical outcomes, response rates remain below 20% due to toxicity and resistance,71 underscoring the need for optimized regimens to broaden applicability.

Increasing evidence indicates that signaling from oncogenic driver kinases, in addition to their role in promoting the biological behavior of tumor cells, could alter the TME to promote immunosuppression, and targeting these oncogenes using small-molecule drugs could contribute to immune re-activation.27,28 Currently, CDK4/6i is approved for the treatment of patients with ER+/HER2- breast cancer, and multiple clinical trials have been performed with the drug in a variety of solid malignancies.29 CDK4/6i plays an important immunoregulatory role, including enhancing antigen presentation via type III IFN induction,31 favoring memory T cell formation,32 and stimulating NFAT activity to promote T cell activation,72 thereby reshaping the TME. Trilaciclib is a highly potent, selective, and reversible CDK4/6i that protects hematopoietic stem cells from damage caused by cytotoxic chemotherapy in ES-SCLC.33,34,35 Interestingly, previous studies have indicated that trilaciclib may improve survival in advanced triple-negative breast cancer,36 indicating antitumor effects beyond hematopoietic protection.

In this study, trilaciclib inhibited proliferation and clonogenicity in RB-proficient NSCLC cells by inducing G1 cell-cycle arrest. In immunocompetent mice, it significantly reduced tumor growth and extended survival, whereas in immunodeficient mice, the antitumor effect was attenuated, suggesting an immune-dependent mechanism. Increased inflammatory infiltration was observed in treated tumors, supporting immune activation. Cellular senescence plays a complex role in the development of tumorigenesis, and its function is environment- and phase-dependent, indicating that senescence is a double-edged sword in cancer. Cellular senescence is often accompanied by a hypersecretory phenotype for cytokines, chemokines, metalloproteases, and growth factors, referred to as SASP.43,73,74 The antitumor mechanisms of cell senescence mainly involve cell-intrinsic and cell-extrinsic roles.43 The cell-intrinsic mechanism is mainly related to the stable cell cycle exit phenomenon, which contributes to senescence-associated proliferation arrest.75 The extrinsic mechanism is mainly related to the ability of the SASP to trigger the inflammatory TME in both cellular autonomic (autocrine effect) and non-cellular autonomic (paracrine effect) ways.76

Inflammation may be triggered by the innate immune system pattern recognition receptors. The cGAS/STING pathway has also been implicated in cellular senescence, which is the main driver of senescence-related inflammation, and further links senescence to cytoplasmic DNA.77,78,79 Canonical pathway activation leads to TBK1–IRF3 signaling and type I IFN production, while non-canonical signaling promotes nuclear factor κB (NF-κB) activation and inflammatory cytokine release.52,54 In senescent cells, cGAS-STING activation drives SASP factor secretion, facilitating immune cell recruitment and antitumor immunity.80,81 Previous work shows that CDK4/6 inhibitors can induce RB-mediated senescence and NF-κB-dependent SASP, promoting natural killer cell-mediated tumor clearance.42 Additionally, some studies have shown that the cGAS-STING pathway plays an important role in the induction of senescence. IRF3-RB interaction attenuates CDK4/6-mediated RB hyperphosphorylation and induces cell-cycle arrest, thereby driving cells senescence.82 Our study further indicated that trilaciclib triggers cell-cycle arrest and senescence, accompanied by increased cytoDNA and cGAS colocalization, enrichment of IFN-related pathways, and secretion of cytokines including IFN-β, TNF-α, and IFN-γ. These effects promoted neutrophil chemotaxis and were attenuated by the cGAS inhibitor G140, confirming the pathway’s role in SASP regulation. Several studies have shown that IFN-γ and/or type I IFNs (IFN-α and IFN-β) play an important role in granulocytogenesis and induction of antitumor phenotype neutrophils.60,83,84 Moreover, recent studies indicated that neutrophils mediate a novel IFN-γ-dependent antitumor immunity, which could drive the polarization of CD4−-CD8− unconventional αβ T cells and the type I immunity response.57 These data were consistent with our findings that trilaciclib reshaped the TME by triggering cell senescence and the SASP, thereby converting a “cold” tumor into a “hot” one, which provides a foundation for effective cancer immunotherapy.

The TME comprises diverse stromal and immune cells that critically influence tumor progression and therapy response.85 For example, in hepatocellular carcinoma, neutrophil-macrophage-dendritic cell interactions promote antigen presentation and CD8+ T cell activation.86 In early-stage NSCLC, appropriate levels of IFN-γ and GM-CSF in the TME change the differentiation program of TANs and give rise to a subset of antigen-presenting cell (APC)-like hybrid TANs exhibiting composite characteristics of neutrophils and APC, which upregulates MHC molecules and induces expression of stimulatory ligands such as CD86, OX40-ligand, and 4-1BB-ligand. Thus, neutrophils are capable of presenting antigens to CD8+ T cells, thereby promoting antigen-specific CD8+ T cell activation and IFN-γ production.39,40,87 In this study, trilaciclib enhanced MHC-I-CD8A/B interactions between neutrophils and CD8+ T cells, promoting T cell activation. The scRNA-seq and flow cytometry analyses confirmed increased effector function and reduced exhaustion in CD8+ T cells following treatment. Neutrophils express the two known TNF-α receptors (TNFR1 and TNFR2), and stimulation with TNF-α has been shown to promote the infiltration and activation of neutrophils, leading to the formation of NETs and the production of reactive oxygen species.88 Activated neutrophils also promote the production of TNF-α, resulting in upregulation of MHC class I on T cells,89 inducing activation and differentiation into effector cytotoxic T cells.90 Similarly, IFN-γ increases neutrophil antigen-presentation capacity via molecules such as CD86 and 4-1BBL. Thus, antigen presentation by neutrophils further promotes the activation of CD8+ T cells and the production of IFN-γ.39,40,87 Therefore, we speculate that trilaciclib promotes the activation and antigen presentation of neutrophils, which could improve the CD8+ T cell immune response and enhance the indirect antitumor effects of neutrophils. In addition, CD8+ T cells can recruit and activate neutrophils, promoting the formation of antitumor positive feedback loops.

While prior studies indicate that trilaciclib may transiently suppress T cell proliferation in the TME, it promotes a more rapid recovery of CD8+ T cells than Tregs, thereby increasing the CD8+/Treg ratio and remodeling immune contexture.37 Our study demonstrated that trilaciclib has durable antitumor activity in NSCLC in vivo, and its combination with an anti-PD-1 antibody further reduced tumor volume and weight. Flow cytometry revealed the most pronounced increase in the proportion of infiltrating neutrophils, especially in the C0 cluster. PD-1+-CD25+-CD8+ T cells, the main antitumor effector cells that respond to anti-PD-1 checkpoint therapies, were also increased in the combined treatment group. Similarly, in vitro drug susceptibility assays using organoid models showed that the addition of neutrophils to trilaciclib combined with an anti-PD-1 antibody resulted in the lowest cell viability. Altogether, our study suggests a novel combination of immunotherapy (immune checkpoint inhibitors) and targeted therapy (small molecule kinase inhibitors) provides a promising candidate strategy for treatment of NSCLC.

Limitations of the study

Our study has several limitations. Although scRNA-seq of the tumor model in mice provided high-resolution insights into CD177+ neutrophil heterogeneity and their association with CD8+ T cells, this approach does not fully capture the spatial organization and dynamic cellular interactions within the TME. Therefore, future studies incorporating spatial transcriptomics, lineage-tracing mouse models, or multiparametric imaging are warranted to elucidate the precise mechanisms by which CD177+ neutrophils modulate antitumor immunity and interact with other immune cell types.

In vivo administration (mouse model):

Although Trilaciclib is clinically administered orally, intraperitoneal (i.p.) injection was chosen in our study to ensure precise dosing and bioavailability in the experimental context. The i.p. route avoids potential variability in oral absorption (e.g., fasting effects, gut metabolism) and aligns with preclinical protocols in oncology studies.37 Mice received 100 mg/kg Trilaciclib (or 100 μL PBS as control) via daily i.p. injection, based on prior studies demonstrating efficacy at this dose.37

Animal experiments

We purchased 6-8-week-old female C57BL/6 mice and BALB/c nude mice from Nanjing GemPharmatech Co. Ltd. and housed them under specific pathogen-free conditions. We strictly adhered to animal care principles and ethics and received approval from the Research Ethics Committee of the Tianjin Medical University Cancer Institute and Hospital (approval number AE2023023). LLC cells (7 × 105) and CMT167(5 × 105) were injected subcutaneously at day 0 into the right flank. The mice were randomly grouped and treatment initiated when tumor size reached 5 to 20 mm3 (normally 3 to 5 days after tumor cell transplantation). Tumor growth and body weight was monitored every other day. Tumor volume was calculated using the formula: (length × width × width)/2. The two groups were: the control group and the Trilaciclib group. The mice were treated with trilaciclib (100mg/kg) or PBS (100μL per mouse) by intraperitoneal injection daily. The survival rate of mice was observed over time. The four groups were: control, trilaciclib, Anti-PD-1 antibody and combined treatment groups. Anti-PD-1 antibody was purchased from Selleck at a dose of 10 mg/kg per mouse every other day via intraperitoneal injection. The control group received vehicle intraperitoneal injection. For orthotopic tumors, we performed a transverse skin incision along the left lateral axillary line at the level of the xyphoid process. Then, we injected the LLC cells (5×105) containing the same volume of Matrigel (Corning) through the chest wall into the left lung, and the incision was closed. For neutrophil depletion, anti-Ly6g (clone 1A8, Selleck) or rat IgG2a (isotype control, Selleck) was given with intraperitoneal injection at a dose of 100 μg/mouse every other day. The anti-Ly6G antibody was administered 6 h before trilaciclib injections in combination therapy group. Tumor growth was examined at 5, 11, and 18 days after inoculation via bioluminescence imaging. For bioluminescent imaging of in vivo tumors, the mice were injected intraperitoneally with d-luciferin (Promega, P1041) and images were acquired using a Xenogen IVIS Lumina II Imaging System and Living Image software.

Patient samples

Patient samples were collected from Tianjin Medical University Cancer Institute and Hospital, Tianjin, China. Patients who met the following criteria were considered eligible for inclusion in this study: aged 20–75 years; clinical stage I to III disease according to the American Joint Committee on Cancer Lung Cancer Staging Manual, 8th edition (2017); an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status score of 0 or 1; histologically confirmed NSCLC; and no prior anticancer therapy. All participating patients provided written informed consent, and the use of patient specimens was approved by the Ethics Committee of Tianjin Medical University Cancer Institute and Hospital (approval number E20231392).

Method details

H&E staining

Freshly isolated tumor, heart, liver, kidney, spleen, and lung were collected and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, paraffin-embedded and sectioned. The sections underwent manual dewaxing in Xylene, graded alcohol until distilled water. Then, sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin. Slides were then dehydrated with graded ethanol washes followed by xylene wash, covered with a coverslip and observed using bright-field microscopy.

TUNEL staining

Paraffin-embedded tumor sections were dewaxed with xylene, rehydration in graded series of ethanol and washing with distilled water. TUNEL staining was performed to detect the apoptosis of cells using DeadEnd Fluorometric TUNEL System (Promega, USA), according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

Flow cytometry

Freshly excised tumor tissue, lung tissue and spleen were cut into small pieces (2–3 mm diameter). Tumor tissue and lung tissue were digested using Tumor Dissociation Kit, mouse (MACS, Germany), at 37°C for 30 min in a shaking incubator at 200 rpm. Blood samples from the orbital sinus of mice were collected in tubes containing anticoagulant. Bone marrow was aspirated or isolated from cleaned tibias and femurs of mice. The cells were passed over 70μm and 40μm filters to generate a single cell suspension. Red blood cells were lysed with ACK lysis buffer on ice for 5 min, and samples were centrifuged and resuspended in PBS supplemented with 2% FBS. Mouse cells were then incubated with anti-Fc blocking mAb (anti-CD16/32, BioLegend) followed by staining of viability dye Zombie NIR (BioLegend) for 30min. For membrane proteins, antibodies labeling was performed shielded from light at 4°C for 30 min. For intracellular staining, cells were stimulated with Leukocyte Activation Cocktail (BD Biosciences) for 5 h. After centrifugation, cells were resuspended in Fixation/Permeabilization solution and intracellular cytokine staining was performed (BD Cytofix/Cytoperm Kit; BD Biosciences). CellQuest software (BD FACS Aria flow cytometer) and CytExpert Software (CytoFLEX LX, Beckman Coulter) were used for data collection. Samples were analyzed using CytExpert software and FlowJo software.

For an apoptosis assay, an Annexin V-APC/DAPI Apoptosis Kit was used according to the manufacturer’s protocol (Elabscience). A549 and H1299 cells were treated with DMSO or Trilaciclib (1 μM) for 48 h at 37°C. Flow cytometry was performed to detect cell cycle progression. The cells were stained with propidium iodide (PI; BD Biosciences) keeping away from light for 30min. The data were analyzed with CytExpert software and FlowJo software.

Immunohistochemistry

Mouse tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, dehydrated, embedded in paraffin and sectioned. Tissue sections were incubated at 65°C overnight followed by de-paraffinized and rehydrated through serial graded xylene and alcohol washes. Tissue sections were incubated in EDTA pH 9.0 for antigen retrieval and then blocked for endogenous peroxidase activity. Cancer tissues were stained for anti-Ly6g (Cell Signaling, 87048S, 1:100) by immunohistochemistry and the staining intensity of the IHC referred to the positive cell frequency.

β-Galactosidase staining

Cells and tumor tissues staining were performed with the Senescence-β-Galactosidase Staining Kit (Beyotime Institute of Biotechnology). A549 and H1299 cells in media treated with the indicated time of trilacicilib (1 μM). Mouse xenograft tumor tissues were prepared for frozen sectioning. Cells and tumor tissues were then fixed with Fixative solution provided with the Kit during 15 min at room temperature. Following fixation, cells and tissues were washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and then incubated in the working staining solution at 37°C for 24–48 h. SA-β-Gal-positive cells were analyzed under light microscopy and then calculated for the average of five individual fields in each group.

Immunofluorescence

A549 and H1299 cells were treated with DMSO or 5 trilacicilib (1 μM) for 6 days. The coverslips were placed in a twelve-well plate, after indicated treatment, cells were seeded at a density of 4×104 cells/well and incubated overnight at 37°C. The next day, cells were washed three times with PBS, fixed for 10 min with 4% paraformaldehyde at room temperature and rinsed with PBS. Cells were permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X-100 for 10 min at room temperature and blocked with 5% normal goat serum for 1 h. Samples were incubated with primary antibodies: Anti-Lamin B1(1:100, Santa Cruz, sc-377000), Anti-cGAS (1:100, Proteintech, 26416-1-AP), Anti-dsDNA (1:200, Santa Cruz, sc-58749) at 4°C overnight. Paraffin embedded sections were incubated at 65°C overnight, and sequentially rehydrated by Xylene and graded concentrations ethanol, which was followed with a rinsed in water and washed in PBS. Antigen retrieval was performed with EDTA (pH = 8.0), blocked for 1 h and incubated with primary antibodies: Anti-Myeloperoxidase (1:20, R&D Systems, AF3667), Anti- H3cit (1:100, Abcam, ab219407) at 4°C overnight. The following day, sections were incubated with fluorescently conjugated secondary antibodies (Invitrogen) for 1 h at room temperature. The samples were washed three times with PBS, stained with DAPI (1:100 in PBS; SIGMA-ALDRICH) for 10 min at room temperature followed by twice washes with PBS. Anti-quenching agent was sealed to the slides and observed under a fluorescence microscope to collect images.

PDO were collected and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Sigma-Aldrich, P6148) for 20 min following dehydrated, embedded in paraffin and sectioned. PDO sections were permeabilized with 1% Triton X-100 in PBS for 30 min, and blocked with 10% goat serum for 30 min. PDO sections were then incubated with primary antibodies, including anti-CD3, anti-CD8, anti-PanCK at 4°C overnight followed by staining with Alexa 488,Alexa 647 and Alexa 555-conjugated secondary antibody. Nuclei were stained with DAPI (1:100 in PBS; SIGMA-ALDRICH) for 10 min at room temperature followed by twice washes with PBS. Images were obtained with Nikon Eclipse Ti2 laser scanning confocal microscopy (Nikon).

Clonogenic assay

Cells were evenly seeded at 1000 cells/well of six-well plates. After culturing for 2 days, cells were then cultured in the absence or presence of drug with indicated concentration for 10–14 days. Cells were stained and fixed and then colonies counted using ImageJ software.

Toxicity analysis

For drug toxicity evaluation, the body weights of the mice were monitored every other day throughout the dosing period. We assessed toxicity of this therapy by treating mice with either PBS or Trilacicilib. Blood samples were collected in tubes containing EDTA from the orbital sinus using a microhematocrit tube at the end of treatment and analyzing their blood for white blood cells, red blood cells, hemoglobin and platelets using a laser based hematologic analyzer (Beckman Coulter). The blood was collected following the protocol described above after each treatment. The samples subjected to biochemical analysis for liver marker enzymes ALT and AST and kidney marker by-products creatinine and BUN to evaluate treatment toxicity by COBAS 8000 analyzer series (Roche Diagnostics, Rotkreuz, Switzerland).

Quantitative real-time PCR

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol Reagent (Ambion, USA) from mouse tumor tissues and tumor cells after indicated treatments. Then the cDNAs were synthesized using the PrimeScript RT Master Mix (Takara, Tokyo, Japan) and amplification reaction was performed using the SYBR Green Supermix (Takara, Tokyo, Japan) following the manufacturer’s protocol. The 2–ΔΔCt method was used to calculate relative expression of the targeted genes.

Western blotting

Protein extraction and WB were performed as previously described.91 Primary antibodies against phospho-RbSer807/811(8516T,1:1000), RB (9313T,1:1000), P21(2947T, 1:1000), Lamin B1(17416T, 1:1000), Phospho-NF-κB p65 Ser536(3033T, 1:1000), Phospho-TBK1Ser172(5483T, 1:1000), Phospho-IRF-3Ser396(29047T, 1:1000), Phospho-IRF-3Ser396(29047T, 1:1000), Phospho-STINGSer366(50907,1:1000), TBK1(38066T, 1:1000), IRF3(4302T, 1:1000), STING(50494,1:1000) were obtained from Cell Signaling Technology. Antibodies against P53(60283-2-Ig: 1:1000), P16(10883-1-AP, 1:1000), P65(66535-1-Ig, 1:1000), ISG15 (15981-1-AP, 1:2500) were purchased from Proteintech. Antibodies against p-Histone H2A.XSer139(sc-517348, 1:500) were obtained from Santa Cruz. Antibodies against GAPDH(BN20621,1:1000) were purchased from Bioss.

Lentiviral infection

Knockdown cell lines were generated by lentiviral infection. Briefly, HEK293T cells were co-transfected with these plasmids: psPAX2, pVSV.G and pLKO.1_shRNA-Cxcl2 containing a shRNA sequence targeting Cxcl2 mRNA. On the second day, the supernatant was replaced with fresh medium. The lentiviral supernatants were collected and filtered through a 0.22 μm filter at 48 and 72 h after transfection. Tumor cells were infected with shNC or shCxcl2 lentivirus for 48 h. Stably Cxcl2 knockdown tumor cells lines were selected with puromycin. The efficiency of knockdown was validated using Western blot. LLC cells were transfected with lentivirus carrying the luciferase gene (GeneChem, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Then the positive cells after transfection were screened and harvested by puromycin selection for future assays.

Conditioned medium (cell-conditioned medium)

Tumor cells CM: After treatment with PBS or Trilaciclib (1μM, 6 days), A549 and H1299 cells were replaced with serum-free medium further treatment for 2–3 days. Then, the CM of tumor cells was collected and treated or untreated with 1mg/ml anti-IFN-γ antibody (Selleck, A2041).

TANs CM: After treatment with PBS or Trilaciclib (1μM, 6 days), A549 and H1299 cells were replaced with serum-free medium further treatment for 2–3 days. Then, the conditioned medium (CM) of tumor cells was collected and treated or untreated with anti-IFN-γ antibody to stimulate neutrophils isolated from the peripheral blood of healthy volunteers for 24 h to generate different CM of TANs.

Cytokine array

Conditioned media samples (collected in complete RPMI 1640) from Trilacicilib treated NSCLC cell lines were normalized based on cell number by diluting with complete RPMI 1640 and centrifuged at 3500 rpm for 10 min. A 12-plex cytokine detection kit from NovaStar was validated to analyze aliquots (100 μL) of the conditioned media using multiplex immunoassays following manufacturer’s protocol. The experiment was repeated thrice.

ELISA

The protein expression levels of CXCL2 in tumor cells CM and IL-12b in TANs CM were measured by using human enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kit (Elabscience, Cat. #E-EL-H1904, Cat. #E-EL-H0151) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The protein expression levels of IFN-β and IFN-γ in tumor tissue lysate supernatant were measured by using mouse ELISA kit (Elabscience, Cat. # E-EL-M0033, Cat. # E-EL-MO048) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The protein level was calculated based on the standard curve.

In vitro chemotaxis and killing assay of neutrophils

Neutrophils were isolated from the peripheral blood of healthy volunteers using the Polymorphprep (Serumwerk Bernburg AG) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The cells were suspended in a culture medium containing RPMI Medium 1640 with 10% fetal bovine serum. The purity and cell viability of neutrophils were assessed by Trypan blue (MP Biomedicals, LLC) exclusion and Wright/Giemsa staining, respectively.

Neutrophil chemotactic assay was used to evaluate neutrophils chemotaxis under different conditions. A total of 1 × 106 neutrophils in RPMI-1640 with serum-free were seeded onto 3-μm pore size inserts for 24-well plates. By contrast, the lower chamber contained tumor cell CM or RPMI Medium 1640, both treated with or without Trilacicilib and supplemented with 5% FBS. After incubation, the neutrophils that had migrated were fixed using 4% paraformaldehyde, followed by staining with crystal violet. The samples were observed under a microscope.

Cancer cells were plated in RPMI Medium 1640 with 10% fetal bovine serum in 12-well or 6-well plates. Purified neutrophils were treated with either conditional medium or Trilacicilib and incubated with cancer cells at a 15:1 ratio for overnight. Then, the cells were washed twice with cold PBS followed by apoptosis assay using flow cytometry.

T cell functionality assays

Blood mononuclear cells were isolated by density gradient centrifugation using Lymphoprep (Solarbio,P8610). Naive CD8+ T cells were subsequently isolated by positive selection using the EasySep Human CD8 Positive Selection Kit II (Stem Cell Technologies, #17813) according to the manufacturer. The cells were stimulated with Dynabeads Human T-Activator CD3/CD28 (Invitrogen, 11161D) at 37°C in 5% CO2 at a cell concentration of 0.25–1.0×106 cells/ml for the times indicated at a bead to cell ratio of two to five. The activated CD8 cells were cultured in complete RPMI media supplemented with IL-2 (20 ng/mL; Stem Cell Technologies), 10% FBS and 100 IU/mL penicillin/streptomycin for expanding. The purified CD8+ T cells (0.5×106/mL) were cultured with CM of TANs for 24 h, after which the cells were washed and analyzed by flow cytometry for the evaluation of GZMB+ cells. Then, purified CD8+ T cells were incubated with NSCLC cell lines at a 5:1 ratio with or without the addition of neutrophils treated with different tumor cells CM at a 1:1 ratio for 16–24 h. The killing of tumor cells was measured by apoptosis assay using flow cytometry.

Tissue preparation and organoid establishment

Patient tumor tissue sample of NSCLC were separated and transported to the laboratory on ice within 1 h in cold Hank’s balanced salt solution with antibiotics (Lonza, Basel, Switzerland). Then, tumor tissues were processed into 4-mm-diameter pieces and washed with ice-cold PBS. The sectioned samples were dissociated into single cells with 0.001% DNase (Sigma-Aldrich, MO, USA), 1 mg/mL collagenase (Roche, IN, USA), 200 U/ml penicillin, 200 mg/mL streptomycin, and 0.5 mg/mL amphotericin B (2% antibiotics, Sigma) in DMEM/F12 medium (Gibco) at 37°C for 30min with gentle shaking. After incubation, the suspensions were repeatedly triturated by pipetting and passed through 70 μm cell strainers (BD Falcon, CA, USA). Cells were counted and resuspended in Matrigel (VWR), plated in 24-well and 96-well tissue culture plates as Matrigel domes and maintained in 37°C 5% CO2 for 3h. Then, the Matrigel dome were overlayed by the special media (Invitrogen). Organoid growth was monitored weekly for the detection of initiated organoids, and organoids were kept in the same passage for no longer than four weeks.

Co-culture system of non-small cell lung cancer organoid and neutrophils

Mature NSCLC tumor spheroids were pretreated with a combination of trilaciclib and PD-1 monoclonal antibody for 48 h prior to co-culture. Drug-pretreated tumor spheroids were enzymatically dissociated from Matrigel by incubation with Cell Recovery Solution (Corning, #354253) at 4°C for 15 min. Extreme care was taken during all manipulation steps to preserve spheroid integrity. Primary human neutrophils were freshly isolated from peripheral blood of healthy donors using density gradient centrifugation followed by red blood cell lysis. Cell viability (>95%) was confirmed by Trypan Blue exclusion. Neutrophils and recovered spheroids were cocultured at a 2:1 effector-to-target ratio in RPMI-1640.The combination therapy (trilaciclib + PD-1 monoclonal antibody) was maintained throughout the 24-h co-culture period. The coculture system was utilized for drug sensitivity testing and Calcein-AM/PI double staining.

Cell viability of non-small cell lung cancer organoid

Cell viability was quantified using the Cell Counting Kit- 8 (Beyotime Biotechnology). The cell viability was determined by measuring the absorbance at 450 nm in a microplate reader and cell viability rate was calculated using GraphPad Prism 8.0 software. The live or dead staining cells were measured by Live/Dead kit (Cat: #04511, Sigma) according to the manufacturers standard protocol. The images were captured using an inverted Nikon ECLIPSE Ti fluorescence microscope system.

Preparation and analysis of single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq)

Mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation, tumors were harvested from mice, dissociated to single cell suspension with Tumor Dissociation Kit (Miltenyi Biotec, Germany), incubated in ACK red blood cell lysis buffer (Invitrogen), and filtered through a 40-μm sterile cell strainer. Sample viability was assessed using a Trypan blue meter (ThermoFisher) and a hemocytometer (ThermoFisher), meanwhile the appropriate volume of each sample was calculated so that each sample contained approximately 10,000 cells. Subsequently single-cell experiments (10x Genomics, USA) were performed. The primary sequencing data generated by 10x Genomics were processed for alignment and quantification through the utilization of the Cell Ranger software package (10x Genomics, V.6.0). Genes detected in less than 3 cells and cells in which detected transcripts were either fewer than 200 genes or >6000 genes and >50% of transcripts derived from mitochondrial genes were filtered out and excluded from the subsequent analysis. And Doublets were removed by DoubletFinder function.92 Dimension reduction and unsupervised clustering were performed according to the standard workflow in Seurat (v5.0.1).93 To integrate cells from different samples into a shared space for unsupervised clustering, we used the harmony algorithm in the R package (v0.1.0)94 to perform batch effect correction. For clustering and visualization, we applied the “FindCluster” function in Seurat to obtain cell clusters at various resolutions and reduced the dimensionality of the data using UMAP implemented in the “RunUMAP” function with the following settings: reduction = ‘harmony’, dims = 1:30. The second round of clustering T cells and neutrophils was the same as abovementioned. To validate the trajectory, we next used monocle3,95 CytoTRACE96 to separately infer the pseudotime of neutrophil subsets.

Cell‒cell communication analysis

To investigate cell-cell interactions among clusters from each cellular module, the R package CellChat (v1.5.0) was employed to analyze cell-to-cell communication between different cell types.97 First, a CellChat object was created by grouping defined clusters. The ligand‒receptor interaction database we used for analysis was “CellChatDB_mouse”, without additional supplementation. Preprocessing steps were all conducted with default parameters. The functions computeCommunProb and computeCommunProbPathway were applied to infer the network of each ligand‒receptor pair and each signaling pathway separately. A hierarchy plot, circle plot and heatmap were used as different visualization forms. The CellChat analysis evaluated the differences in ligand-receptor interactions among two treatment groups.

Pathway analysis

DEGs were detected by the “FindAllMarkers” function in Seurat. GO enrichment analysis on DEGs in this study were performed using the clusterProfiler package.98 GSVA was conducted using the GSVA package.99 Differences between different cell groups were calculated by the “FindMarkers” function in the Seurat package.

Identification and calculation of signature genes

The signature genes of different subcluster of neutrophils were estimated using the the “FindAllMarkers” function in Seurat. signature scores calculated by “AddModulScore” function with default parameters in Seurat. Score expression plots were generated with the “VlnPlot” function in Seurat package. Considering the different transcriptomic distribution between bulk RNA-seq and scRNA-seq, signatures derived from the former were calculated by ssGSEA.100 The signature genes of mouse were firstly translated to their human genes. All signature scores were calculated from the normalized matrix based on the official tutorials.

Survival analysis

RNA-seq and clinical data of LUAD samples were obtained from TCGA to evaluate the prognostic effects of gene signatures derived from specific cell states. To assess the impact of specific differentially expressed marker genes on LUAD cancer progression, the tumor samples were divided into two groups based on the optimal cutoff point. Survival curves were performed by the Kaplan–Meier method with the Survival package v.2.44, and visualized using the ‘ggsurvplot’ function of the survminer package. Significance was assessed by the log rank test statistics (p values) between two groups.

Quantification and statistical analysis

All statistical tests were performed in R (v4.3.2) and GraphPad Prism 8.0 software. At least 5 mice per group were used to reach statistical power. For in vitro studies, at least three biological repeats were carried out, and shown as the means ± SDs unless otherwise mentioned. Two-tailed unpaired Student’s t test was used to compare the differences between two different treatment groups, whereas analysis of experiments with more than two groups was performed using one-way ANOVA. Survival and time to response were evaluated by constructing Kaplan–Meier curves and using the log rank test. Statistical significance was defined as p values less than 0.05: ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001, and ∗∗∗∗p < 0.0001 and n.s. indicates no significant difference.
