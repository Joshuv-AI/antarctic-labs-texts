Exercise‐Induced Cardiac Troponin I Elevation Is Associated With Regional Alterations in Left Ventricular Strain in High‐Troponin Responders
Authors: Christine Bjørkvik Erevik, Øyunn Kleiven, Vidar Frøysa, Magnus Bjørkavoll‐Bergseth, Monica Chivulescu, Lars Gunnar Klæboe, Lars Dejgaard, Bjørn Auestad, Øyvind Skadberg, Tor Melberg, Stig Urheim, Kristina Haugaa, Thor Edvardsen, Stein Ørn
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe implications of exercise‐induced cardiac troponin elevation in healthy individuals are unclear. This study aimed to determine if individuals with a high exercise‐induced cardiac troponin I (cTnI) response have alterations in myocardial function following high‐intensity endurance exercise compared with normal‐cTnI responders.Methods and ResultsStudy individuals were recruited from previous participants in a 91‐km mountain bike cycling race (the North Sea Race) and were classified as high‐ (n=34) or normal‐cTnI responders (n=25) based on maximal cTnI values after the recruitment race. The present study exposed all participants to 2 prolonged high‐intensity exercises: a combined lactate threshold and cardiopulmonary exercise test and repeated participation in the North Sea Race. Echocardiography was performed before, immediately after, and 24 hours following exercise. All study individuals (n=59) had normal coronary arteries, and were aged 51±10 years; 46 (74%) were men. There were no differences in baseline characteristics between the high‐ and normal‐cTnI responders. Maximal cTnI levels 3 hours after exercise were significantly higher in the high‐ compared with normal‐cTnI group (P<0.001–0.027). Following exercise, there were no differences in global ventricular function between the 2 groups. In contrast, high‐cTnI responders had significantly lower regional strain in the anteroseptal segments following exercise, with more profound changes after the race.ConclusionsHigh‐cTnI responders had lower anteroseptal segmental strain shortly after exercise than normal‐cTnI responders. However, there were no permanent alterations in myocardial strain, indicating no short‐ or long‐term adverse consequences of these exercise‐induced alterations in myocardial function.RegistrationURL: https://www.clinicaltrials.gov; Unique identifier: NCT02166216.

Cardiac troponins (cTns) are biomarkers of myocardial injury strongly associated with mortality and morbidity. 1 , 2 However, prolonged high‐intensity exercise may cause cTn elevations in healthy individuals. 3 , 4 The underlying mechanisms and clinical significance of exercise‐induced cTn elevation in healthy individuals remain unclear. 5

Prolonged high‐intensity exercise is associated with alterations in myocardial function detected by echocardiography. 6 , 7 There are variable reports on whether these changes are associated with exercise‐induced cTn levels. 8 , 9 , 10 Some studies indicate segment‐specific alterations in left ventricular function, with reductions in strain in the septal myocardium. 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 However, these studies did not explore postexercise regional strain abnormalities and cTn release, and the clinical significance of these alterations remains unknown.

A recent publication suggests that the exercise‐induced cardiac troponin I (cTnI) response is reproducible within individuals, indicating that high‐cTnI responders have a consistently higher cTnI response to exercise than normal responders. 15 It is unknown if the cTnI levels in high‐troponin responders are related to myocardial function alterations before or after exercise. This study aimed to determine if there are differences in regional and global myocardial function in high‐ compared with normal‐cTnI responders before and after endurance exercise of different workloads.

METHODS

Study Individuals

The present study was conducted in 2018, recruiting individuals without obstructive coronary artery disease from the coronary computed tomography angiography (CCTA) cohort (n=133) of the north sea race endurance exercise study (NEEDED) studies in 2013/2014 3 , 16 (Figure 1). All participants had no history, signs, or symptoms of significant coronary artery disease (>50% stenosis). Study participants were categorized as either high‐ or normal‐cTnI responders based on their maximum cTnI values in the NEEDED 2013 and 2014 studies. High‐cTnI responders were defined as individuals with a postexercise maximal cTnI value >200 ng/L, corresponding to the 92.5 percentile of the 1099 participants of the main NEEDED 2013/2014 studies. Normal‐cTnI responders were defined as individuals with maximal cTnI values <200 ng/L. 17

Flowchart of the study.

Study individuals in the NEEDED 2018 study were recruited from 133 study participants assessed by coronary computed tomography angiography (CCTA) in 2013 or 2014. Only study individuals without obstructive coronary artery disease were eligible for participation in the present study: 93 from the high– and 40 from the normal–cardiac troponin I (cTnI) responders. Finally, 34 individuals from the high‐ and 25 individuals from the normal‐cTnI responders were included in the study. CAD indicates coronary artery disease; and MB, myocardial bridging.

Study Design

Echocardiographic assessments, GE Vivid E 95 ultrasound systems, and 4V probes (GE HealthCare, Horten, Norway) were acquired before exercise, immediately after exercise, and 24 hours following 2 episodes of high‐intensity endurance exercise in 2018 (Figure 2). The first exercise was a lactate threshold and cardiopulmonary exercise test (La‐CPET) with assessments of power outputs, lactate threshold, maximal oxygen consumption, and other physiological parameters (Data S1). The second exercise was participation in the 91‐km North Sea Mountain Bike Race 2018. The same bikes (the athletes' own bikes) were used for both the La‐CPET and the race to ensure that the assessment of power output was comparable between the exercises. Before the race, power meters (Stages Power Meters, Boulder, CO) were fitted to the participants' bikes and connected to the Garmin Forerunner 935 Sport Watch (Garmin, Kansas). All power meters were calibrated before starting and at the end of the study (24 hours following the race).

Study flow for NEEDED 2018.

Echocardiography was performed before, immediately after, and 24 hours after 2 bouts of exercise: the lactate threshold and cardiopulmonary exercise test (La‐CPET) and the 91‐km North Sea Race. The La‐CPET included both assessments of lactate threshold and maximal oxygen uptake (VO2max). The 2 exercises were separated by 2 to 3 weeks. Biomarkers of myocardial injury (cardiac troponin I) were acquired 24 hours before and at 3 and 24 hours following exercise. A repeated coronary computed tomography (CT) angiography was performed 2 to 3 weeks after the race in 2018 to ensure no coronary artery obstruction.

During the race, blood pressure was measured at 4 pit stops. Measurements were performed manually on the right arm with different models of manual aneroid sphygmomanometers manufactured by Heine Opotechnik: Heine G5, G7, or XXL LF‐T (Heine, Herrsching, Germany). The position of the pit stops was based on the race profile, at the top and bottom at the 2 largest hills of the race after 34, 41, 69, and 76 km. Heart rate (HR) was measured continuously by chest straps during both the La‐CPET and the race. HR and geopositioning data were downloaded and analyzed for the whole race, and mean and maximal HR values were calculated. Work performed during the race was measured continuously with the power meters.

Blood samples were acquired before (baseline) and at 3 hours (the expected maximum cTnI elevation) and 24 hours (to assess recovery) following both exercises. CCTA was performed 2 to 3 weeks after the race in 2018 to ensure the absence of new coronary artery obstruction potentially influencing the echocardiographic assessments and cTnI response. Informed consent was obtained from all study participants before study inclusion. The Regional Ethics Committee approved the study (REK number 2018/63). The data supporting this study's findings are available from the corresponding author on reasonable request.

Echocardiographic Image Acquisition

Three physicians were responsible for acquiring images after the La‐CPET, and 6 physicians were responsible for acquiring images immediately after participants finished the race. Subjects were scanned in the left lateral decubitus position under relaxed conditions. All recordings and measurements were made according to the European Association of Cardiovascular Imaging. 18 , 19 Comprehensive imaging protocols were applied with full coverage of both atria and ventricles, including parasternal and apical views of 3 cardiac cycles triggered to the QRS complex. Two‐dimensional apical and 3‐dimensional apical triplan of the 4‐, 3‐, and 2‐chamber view and a focused 2‐dimensional apical 4‐chamber view of the right ventricle were obtained, with care to avoid foreshortening. Appropriate frame rates (60–70 frames/second) allowed for high‐quality postprocessing later, including speckle‐tracking strain analysis of the left and right ventricles and the left atrium. Three‐dimensional volume acquisitions of the left and right ventricles were obtained from the apical 4‐chamber view over 6 cardiac cycles using multibeat full‐volume acquisition during breath hold and encompassing the entire left ventricular (LV) and right ventricular (RV) cavity in the data sets.

Analysis of Myocardial Function and Morphology

All echocardiographic analyses were performed offline on EchoPAC software, V202 version (GE HealthCare) by a researcher (CBE) blinded to clinical data and exercise information. The Devereux formula was used to determine LV mass indexed to body surface area. Mitral valve inflow was assessed using pulsed‐wave Doppler at the mitral valve leaflets; pulsed‐wave tissue Doppler imaging was performed to assess septal mitral annular velocity. LV volumes and ejection fraction were assessed using 3‐dimensional imaging, and stroke volume was calculated by subtracting the end‐systolic volume (ESV) from the end‐diastolic volume (EDV). Two‐dimensional speckle‐tracking imaging was used to study LV deformation. LV global longitudinal strain was calculated as an average longitudinal strain value based on apical 2‐, 3‐, and 4‐chamber views at a frame rate of 60 to 70 frames/second, and endocardial borders were readjusted manually until satisfactory tracking was achieved. Segmental strain was assessed in each of the 18 myocardial segments. In cases of poor tracking quality, even after manual adjustment, these segments were excluded from the final analysis. Peak strain was the peak negative longitudinal strain during the cardiac cycle. Mechanical dispersion was defined as the SD of time to peak negative strain in 18 LV segments. The RV global longitudinal strain was measured as the mean of the RV lateral basal, middle, and apical segments. RV fractional area change was obtained from the apical 4‐chamber view and was calculated as the difference in the end‐diastolic area and the end‐systolic area divided by the end‐diastolic area. Left atrial volume was measured using the biplane method and indexed to body surface area. The left atrial strain was measured in the apical 4‐chamber view and assessed as global longitudinal strain, using ventricular end diastole as the baseline. Peak 1 represents the peak strain during the reservoir phase (left atrial reservoir function), and peak 2 represents the peak strain during the atrial contraction phase (left atrial booster pump function).

cTnI Measurements

The high‐sensitivity cTnI assay (STAT) from Abbott Diagnostics (Abbott Park, IL) was used to measure cTnI before exercise and at 3 and 24 hours following exercise. The assay was analyzed on an Architect SR2000i (Abbott Diagnostics). The 99th percentile is 26 ng/L (men: 34 ng/L; and women: 16 ng/L). 20

Statistical Analysis

Normally distributed continuous variables are reported as mean±SD, whereas continuous variables with markedly skewed distributions are reported as the median and interquartile range (25th–75th percentile). The Shapiro‐Wilk test was used to test for normality. The Mann‐Whitney U test was applied to compare continuous or ordinal variables between the 2 groups (2 independent samples). For comparison of categorical variables, the Pearson χ2 test was used to compare 2 independent groups. Spearman analysis was used to calculate bivariate correlations. The intraobserver and interobserver variability was determined by analyzing the same subject (a total of 10 subjects) a second time by the same operator and by a different operator in a blinded manner. Reproducibility was assessed by the intraclass correlation coefficient, and reliability was assessed by the coefficient of variation. A 2‐tailed P<0.05 was considered significant. SPSS, version 26.0 (IBM, New York) was used for statistical analyses.

RESULTS

A total of 59 healthy, well‐trained, recreational athletes, aged 51±10 years and 73% men, without obstructive coronary artery disease (>50% stenosis) verified by computed tomography scans, were included in the study. The 2 groups comprised 34 high‐cTnI responders and 25 normal‐cTnI responders. Study baseline characteristics, maximal oxygen consumption, training experience, and echocardiographic parameters at baseline are outlined in Table 1. There were no abnormal ECG findings before or after the 2 exercises.

At baseline, there were no differences between the 2 groups on demographics, cardiac risk factors, maximal oxygen consumption, use of medications and supplements, or conventional echocardiographic parameters. A total of 25 participants had nonobstructive coronary artery disease (CAD). There was no significant difference in the presence of nonobstructive CAD between the high‐ and normal‐cTnI responders (Table 1). Prior cardiac conditions were present in 2 of the high‐cTnI responders (atrial septal defect closure and mild aortic and mitral regurgitation) and 1 of the normal‐cTnI responders (1 previous episode of atrial fibrillation). The normal‐cTnI group had significantly more training experience than the high‐cTnI group, with more prior competitions (P=0.042) and more years with endurance exercise training (P=0.002). Baseline echocardiographic parameters were within normal ranges for both groups.

Postexercise cTnI Response

At baseline, cTnI levels were significantly higher in the high‐cTnI group compared with normal‐cTnI responders: 4.5 (2.5–12.3) versus 2.6 (1.5–4.3) ng/L (P=0.016). Further maximal cTnI levels 3 hours after exercise were significantly higher in the high‐ compared with normal‐cTnI group: La‐CPET: cTnI 13 (9–32) versus 9 (5–17) ng/L (P=0.027); and race: cTnI: 101 (76–198) versus 37 (25–69) ng/L (P<0.001). At 24 hours after the exercises, the high‐cTnI group still had significantly higher cTnI levels compared with the normal‐cTnI responders: cTnI: La‐CPET: 5.8 (3.4–14.6) versus 3.7 (2.1–8.2) ng/L (P=0.05); and race: cTnI 22.3 (14.4–61.7) versus 9.8 (6.1–16.3) ng/L (P<0.001) (Figure 3; Table S1).

During the race, the high‐cTnI group maintained an HR above the HR at the lactate threshold for 2270 seconds (38 minutes) and the normal‐cTnI group for a median of 1900 seconds (32 minutes). This difference was nonsignificant (P=0.98). Duration of HR >150 beats per minute was insignificant between the 2 groups (Table 2). However, significant bivariate correlations between duration of HR >150 beats per minute during the race and regional strain in the anteroseptal segments following the race were evident: basal anteroseptal segment (ρ=0.257, P=0.049), mid anteroseptal segment (ρ 0.402, P=0.002), and apical anteroseptal segment (ρ=0.346, P=0.007).

Echocardiographic Parameters After Exercise

Immediately following both exercises, left atrial volume and ratio of early/late diastolic transmitral blood flow velocities were significantly reduced compared with baseline findings in both groups. There were reductions in LV EDV and LV ESV immediately following the race in both groups; however, the reductions were only significant in the high‐cTnI group. There were no significant differences in LV EDV and LV ESV following the La‐CPET. The decrease in LV EDV was more pronounced than the reduction in LV ESV, resulting in a significantly reduced stroke volume in both groups following the race. At 24 hours following the race, both LV EDV and LV ESV were larger than preexercise values for both groups. There were nonsignificant alterations in several other indexes of LV and RV function after the race compared with baseline measures. These echocardiographic parameters are presented in Table S2.

Comparing the 2 groups following the La‐CPET and the race, the 2 groups showed no significant differences on LV diastolic function, LV global systolic function, RV global systolic function, atrial function, or ventricular and atrial volumes (Table 3).

Regional Longitudinal Strain Following Exercise

In the high‐cTnI group, all the anteroseptal segments showed lower peak strain values from baseline to following the exercises, except for the apical anteroseptal segment following the La‐CPET. The reductions in strain values were significant for the basal anteroseptal segment following the La‐CPET (P=0.004) and the mid anteroseptal segment following the race (P=0.0037). No significant changes were seen in the anteroseptal segments in the normal‐cTnI group following the exercises. The Δ values (differences between baseline and postexercise values) were significantly larger for all the anteroseptal segments in the high‐cTnI group compared with the normal‐cTnI group following the race (basal anteroseptal segment P=0.042, mid anteroseptal segment P=0.05, and apical anteroseptal segment P=0.025). After both exercises, significant bivariate correlations were evident between postexercise cTnI levels and postexercise peak strain in the basal anteroseptal segment (La‐CPET: ρ=0.282, P=0.037; and race: ρ=0.385, P=0.003).

Comparing the 2 groups' postexercise regional longitudinal strain values, the high‐cTnI group had significantly lower regional strain values in the anteroseptal segments following both exercises (Figure 4). Following the La‐CPET, the high‐cTnI group had significantly lower peak longitudinal strain values in the apical anteroseptal segment (P=0.034, 8% relative difference), mid anteroseptal segment (P=0.041, 13% relative difference), and mid septal segment (P=0.037, 9% relative difference). Following the race, the high‐cTnI group had significantly lower peak strain values in all anteroseptal segments: apical (P=0.007, 11% relative difference), mid (P=0.005, 14% relative difference), and basal anteroseptal segment (P=0.011, 9% relative difference). No other significant differences were seen between the remaining segments compared with the 2 groups. At 24 hours following both exercises, there were no significant differences in strain values in any segments comparing the 2 groups (Table S3).

Reproducibility

Intraobserver and interobserver variability analysis confirmed high reproducibility of the parameters LV global longitudinal strain, 4‐chamber strain, 3‐chamber strain, and 2‐chamber strain analyses (Table S4).

DISCUSSION

This study demonstrates that in the present group of high‐cTnI responders, exercise‐induced cTnI release is associated with a temporal reduction in regional LV function. High‐cTnI responders had lower regional myocardial function in anteroseptal segments than subjects with normal‐cTnI response. The differences between the 2 groups were only detectable a short time following exercise and were not present at assessments made before or the day after exercise. Notably, the differences were most profound following the highest workloads. This study suggests that high exercise‐induced cTnI levels reflect focal myocardial alterations in response to increasing workloads but argues against permanent alterations in myocardial function at rest. These findings support that the exercise‐induced cTnI elevation in healthy individuals represents a transient response to increased workload without evidence of persisting adverse alterations in myocardial function.

Exercise‐induced cTn elevation in healthy athletes has been extensively studied and is well documented, 4 but the underlying mechanisms and consequences of exercise‐induced cTn elevations are unclear. 5 Increased cardiomyocyte membrane permeability and apoptosis have been suggested as potential mechanisms for transient and mild cTn release in assumable healthy subjects without cardiac symptoms. 4 Only a few studies have addressed the prognostic value of the postexercise cTn release in healthy athletes without cardiovascular risk factors, 21 , 22 and there is an unclear relationship between postexercise cTn elevations and global cardiac dysfunction. 8 , 23

Transient reductions in the global RV and LV function following prolonged endurance exercise have been reported by many studies. 6 , 7 , 24 The mechanisms responsible for these alterations are multifactorial. 24 Because cardiac volumes and myocardial deformation are influenced by exercise‐induced volume changes and autonomic tone, 25 the postexercise reduction in myocardial function may be hard to determine. However, some studies have shown that high‐intensity endurance exercise evokes a focused segment‐specific cardiac perturbation at the septal portion of the myocardium. 11 , 12 , 14 Alterations in loading conditions are unlikely to be causally related to segmental abnormalities, 14 suggesting that other mechanisms may be responsible for the alterations in septal function. Birat et al 26 recently observed significant cTnI release alongside a decline in regional myocardial function following a prolonged race, whereas global ventricular function remained unaltered. The normalization of cTnI levels within 24 hours suggested that these findings reflected an acute physiological response to intense exercise. However, because most previous studies have not investigated the association between postexercise regional myocardial function and cTn release, this association remains unknown.

Previous studies on postexercise cTn release and cardiac performance have mainly assessed global ventricular function. In the present study, the significant differences in anteroseptal segments between the 2 groups were consistent following the La‐CPET and the race, making the results more reliable. The regional strain differences and magnitude of postexercise cTnI release between the 2 groups were more profound following the race, highlighting that the cTn response depends on the physical strain's total workload (duration and intensity). Following both exercises, significant correlations were evident between postexercise cTnI and postexercise peak strain in the basal anteroseptal segment (La‐CPET: ρ=0.282, P=0.037; and race: ρ=0.385, P=0.003). This finding indicates that a larger release of cTnI following exercise is linked to reduced peak strain values in the basal anteroseptal segment. Although significant differences in regional strain values were observed immediately after exercise comparing the 2 groups, this did not result in significant differences in stroke volume at any time point comparing the 2 groups, nor were there significant differences in global systolic or diastolic cardiac function between the 2 groups. Therefore, the differences in regional strain did not appear to influence overall cardiac function.

Several potential mechanisms may explain the relationship between cTnI levels and the reduced myocardial function in LV anteroseptal segments. The reduced strain in the interventricular septum may be attributable to restricted septal deformation generated by the tethering of the right ventricle. 27 The complex architecture of the ventricles may explain the heterogeneous strain response observed across myocardial segments. 11 Decreased wall stress is seen toward the apex, which may result in a higher magnitude of longitudinal strain in the apex compared with the base, 28 and a different composition of the myocardium with more cross‐fiber shortening is seen in the apex compared with the base. However, these mechanisms may not explain the difference between the high‐ and normal‐cTnI groups.

The anteroseptal myocardial segments represent the site of the upper RV insertion point, where the right ventricle hinges to the left ventricle. The RV insertion points are sites known for increased tension during exercise. 29 In athletes, myocardial fibrosis is frequently located at the RV insertion points. 30 These regions are exposed to high myocardial stress levels, which may explain the increased risk of developing fibrosis. 9 Interestingly, Eijsvogels et al 31 found that minor focal myocardial fibrosis in this region did not influence global ventricular function. However, in some fibrotic regions, there was reduced regional longitudinal strain. Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging studies have demonstrated myocardial edema following strenuous exercise, 32 , 33 which may be localized, potentially contributing to regional cardiac function alterations. 34 , 35 The increased regional myocardial workload may explain the pronounced cTnI release and reduced regional strain in the high‐cTnI group in the present analysis. Unfortunately, because it was not possible to perform cardiac magnetic resonance imaging following the 2 exercises in the present study, it is impossible to determine if the focal alterations were associated with local edema or fibrosis. However, postexercise myocardial edema is fully reversible and without evidence of permanent cardiac dysfunction or structural abnormalities. 35 Furthermore, insertion point fibrosis in athletes is the only form of benign fibrosis, 30 emphasizing that these focal alterations are not necessarily equivalent to pathology.

Another possible mechanism for the observed regional systolic differences in the present study could be RV‐LV interaction; increased tension on the insertion points and interventricular septum could be caused by the disproportionate wall stress on the right ventricle during exercise, and the altered RV shape, compliance, and afterload could be translated to the LV interventricular septum, affecting regional LV performance. 9 However, in the present study, there were no significant differences in RV longitudinal strain or RV fractional area change after exercise comparing the 2 groups, nor were there any significant differences in RV dimensions. Therefore, our work does not support the idea that this is a potential mechanism for the observed regional strain differences. However, we cannot exclude that the observed reduction in LV anteroseptal strain in the high‐cTnI group may provide a sensitive marker that precedes RV dilatation and reduced RV function.

There were no differences in the lactate threshold, oxygen consumption, watt values, or HR measurements following the exercises comparing the 2 groups (Table 2). These findings suggest that the causes of the differences in cTnI levels in high‐ versus normal‐cTnI responders are related to other factors than those measured in Table 2. The high‐cTnI group had significantly less training and competitive experience than the normal‐cTnI group. However, the cTnI values at baseline and 3 and 24 hours following exercise were not significantly correlated to years of endurance training. These findings contrast previous studies that have demonstrated an association between lower training levels and increased exercise‐induced cTnT levels. 10 Although we did not identify a direct significant correlation between years of endurance training and postexercise cTnI levels, this does not eliminate the potential relationship between training experience and exercise‐induced cTnI release. The higher postexercise cTnI values observed between the 2 groups in the present study may reflect excessive myocardial stress on the interventricular septum in individuals with lower training levels. A previous study has demonstrated a relationship between HR duration >150 beats per minute and exercise‐induced cTnI elevation. 36 In line with the previous study, the present study showed that HR duration >150 beats per minute was significantly correlated to strain values in the anteroseptal segments following the race. Although we failed to find a significant difference in HR duration >150 beats per minute comparing the 2 groups, we cannot exclude that the present findings may indicate a more sensitive response to variations in the duration of high HR in the high‐cTnI group.

In the present work, 16 of 59 participants were women, and women were more likely to be high‐cTnI responders (68% of women were in the high‐cTnI group). However, when excluding women from the final analyses, peak strain values in anteroseptal segments were still significantly lower for the high‐cTnI versus normal‐cTnI group after exercise. Why a higher proportion of women exhibited higher cTnI responses in this cohort remains unclear. Different geometry of the ventricles with accompanying different distribution of wall stress in men and women may explain some differences in postexercise cTnI response, but this was not assessed in the present work and will remain speculative.

Regional strains can be impaired in myocardial segments supplied by stenotic coronary arteries 37 , 38 and in nonobstructive coronary artery disease, 39 which may only be evident during stress when oxygen demand increases. None of the participants in the present study had obstructive CAD, but some exhibited nonobstructive CAD. There were no significant differences between the high‐ and normal‐cTnI group on nonobstructive CAD in 2018 or progression of CAD (increase ≥10 Agatston units in coronary artery calcification) from 2013/2014 to 2018. Because echocardiography was not performed in 2013/2014, it remains unknown whether there were regional strain differences between the high‐cTnI and normal‐cTnI groups at that time. CCTA was last conducted in 2018; no follow‐up data have been available. We cannot exclude that the regional strain differences between the 2 groups after exercise represent myocardial vulnerability because of subclinical CAD or are indicative of future significant CAD. However, normalizing regional strain differences at 24 hours following the exercises contradicts this notion.

Finally, baseline cTnI values significantly differed between the 2 groups before both exercises (Figure 3). Figure 3 demonstrates that individuals with the highest cTnI response have higher cTnI levels 24 hours after exercise. This finding indicates that the postexercise cTnI levels remain higher for a more extended period in high versus normal responders. This finding may explain the higher cTnI levels before exercise in high responders, reflecting a higher cTnI elevation following training before cTnI sampling. All study participants were instructed not to perform high‐intensity training during the last 48 hours before cTnI sampling. In the last 48 hours before the race, the study participants reported only light‐ to moderate‐intensity exercise (mostly cycling). There was no significant difference in the number of individuals performing exercise: the high‐cTnI group: 17 subjects (50%); and the normal‐cTnI group, 10 subjects (42%). Among those who exercised before the race, there was no significant difference in baseline cTnI comparing the 2 groups. However, given the scarcity of information on the specifics of the exercise performed before the La‐CPET, the significance of this observation is constrained. On the basis of these findings, we hypothesize that the higher baseline cTnI values before exercise most likely reflect the higher exercise‐induced cTnI response in the high‐cTnI group compared with the normal‐cTnI group.

Despite the significantly higher cTnI values, there were no significant differences in global cardiac function and dimensions comparing high‐ and normal‐cTnI responders in 2018. Notably, there were no significant differences in regional strain between the 2 groups at baseline or 24 hours following the exercises. This argues against permanent injury in these regions and strengthens the assumption that exercise‐induced cTnI elevation in healthy individuals represents a transient response to increased workload without indicating persisting alterations in myocardial function.

Limitations

No cardiac magnetic resonance imaging was performed to exclude the presence of myocardial fibrosis or myocardial edema. It is impossible to determine whether the findings are related to fibrotic or other focal myocardial alterations. The present study addresses biochemical and cardiac functional changes in a group of healthy, well‐trained recreational middle‐aged athletes, and the current findings may not apply to a younger population with a higher functional capacity or to an age‐matched group of less well‐trained individuals. Regional strain values are less validated in clinical practice, and there is a lack of sufficiently validated reference values for a regional strain to decide if the anteroseptal strain values in the high‐cTnI group are abnormal.

CONCLUSIONS

High‐cTnI responders had significantly lower strain in anteroseptal segments than normal‐cTnI responders shortly after exercise. The differences in regional strain were most profound following the highest workload (the race). However, comparing the 2 groups, there were no differences in the regional strain at rest, neither before nor 24 hours after exercise, suggesting that elevated cTnI levels in healthy high‐cTnI responders may be a marker of regional myocardial stress, not associated with permanent alterations in myocardial strain, indicating no short‐ or long‐term adverse consequences in myocardial function.
