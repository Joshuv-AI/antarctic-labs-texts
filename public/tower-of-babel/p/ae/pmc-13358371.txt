A Case of Silicone Allergy Induced by a Subcutaneous Implantable Cardioverter Defibrillator
Authors: Wataru Shinkai, Jun Kishihara, Megumi Toraiwa, Yuki Arakawa, Shuhei Kobayashi, Hironori Nakamura, Naruya Ishizue, Jun Oikawa, Hidehira Fukaya, Junya Ako
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundAllergic reactions associated with subcutaneous implantable cardioverter‐defibrillator (S‐ICD) are extremely rare, and silicone‐related cases are scarcely reported.Methods and ResultsA 54‐year‐old man with a transvenous implantable cardioverter‐defibrillator (TV‐ICD) for idiopathic ventricular fibrillation underwent removal of an infected TV‐ICD system, followed by S‐ICD implantation. Seven months later, a granulomatous lesion developed at the xiphoid incision site without signs of infection. Conservative management was initially selected; however, progressive wound deterioration and eventual device exposure necessitated complete system removal. Patch testing demonstrated a positive reaction to silicone, a device component corresponding to the lesion site.ConclusionsThis case highlights that silicone‐related hypersensitivity, although extremely rare, should be considered when evaluating noninfectious wound complications after S‐ICD implantation.Keywords: allergy, infection, patch test, S‐ICD, silicone

Introduction

Allergic reactions associated with cardiac implantable electronic devices (CIEDs) are relatively rare but represent clinically significant complications that must be distinguished from device‐related infections. The subcutaneous implantable cardioverter‐defibrillator (S‐ICD) eliminates the need for a transvenous lead and has been associated with fewer lead‐related complications requiring surgical intervention compared with transvenous systems [1, 2]. Nevertheless, device‐related allergic reactions can occur with S‐ICDs, similar to those reported with transvenous implantable cardioverter‐defibrillators. While cobalt‐related contact dermatitis after S‐ICD implantation has been described [3], silicone‐related hypersensitivity associated with S‐ICD systems is exceedingly uncommon.

Case Presentation

A 54‐year‐old man was admitted for device removal due to exposure of a previously implanted S‐ICD system. Fourteen years earlier, he had developed idiopathic ventricular fibrillation during a subarachnoid hemorrhage and underwent implantation of a transvenous implantable cardioverter‐defibrillator (TV‐ICD). Seven years later, the entire TV‐ICD system was removed because of device‐related infection, and an S‐ICD was implanted within the same year.

Seven months after S‐ICD implantation, granulomatous changes at the xiphoid incision site (Figure 1) were observed. Laboratory findings showed a white blood cell (WBC) count of 6.1 × 109/L, C‐reactive protein (CRP) of 0.56 mg/dL, neutrophils of 64.8%, and eosinophils of 3.0%. There were no findings suggestive of active infection. Culture of the granulomatous lesion yielded methicillin‐susceptible Staphylococcus aureus (MSSA) with a semi‐quantitative result of < 3, indicating scant bacterial growth. Oral cefaclor (750 mg/day, divided into three doses) was administered for 2 weeks primarily to prevent secondary infection. Thereafter, only topical application of Povidone‐Iodine was continued. The blood cultures were ultimately confirmed negative. Two months later, laboratory findings showed a WBC count of 5.5 × 109/L, CRP of 0.28 mg/dL, neutrophils of 54.5%, and eosinophils of 4.0%, and no fever was observed. These findings strongly suggest the absence of active infection at that time. In the absence of clinical signs of infection, a device‐related allergic skin ulcer was suspected. Although complete system removal was considered, the patient initially refused surgical intervention. Because no inappropriate shocks or sensing abnormalities had occurred, conservative management with topical steroids and close follow‐up was selected. Despite the absence of infection during follow‐up, skin ulceration with intermittent fistula formation persisted at the xiphoid incision site.

(a) Magnified view of the boxed area in panel A, showing a granulomatous lesion that developed 7 months after S‐ICD implantation, without clinical signs of infection. A silicone‐containing suture sleeve was anatomically located at this site.

Six years after S‐ICD implantation, exudate from the left lateral dorsal region increased, and the exudate was sent for culture. Similar to the findings at 7 months post‐implantation, MSSA was detected, but the semi‐quantitative result was < 3, indicating only minimal bacterial growth. Oral minocycline (200 mg/day, divided into two doses) was administered primarily to prevent secondary infection. One month later, generator exposure occurred at the left lateral dorsal region (Figure 2), prompting hospitalization for complete system extraction. Blood tests performed on admission showed a WBC count of 7.4 × 109/L, CRP of 0.14 mg/dL, neutrophil percentage of 69.3%, and an eosinophil percentage of 3.5%. Throughout the entire clinical course from the initial S‐ICD implantation to its eventual extraction, the patient never developed a fever. Furthermore, there was no localized pain or purulent discharge at the affected sites.

At the time of S‐ICD extraction, wound exudate culture yielded MSSA with a semi‐quantitative result of < 3, indicating minimal bacterial growth. Taken together, the absence of systemic inflammatory responses, consistently negative blood cultures, and only minimal/scant bacterial growth (semi‐quantitative result < 3) on multiple localized cultures provide strong evidence against a true device‐related infection, supporting a non‐infectious, allergic etiology.

The patient had never experienced appropriate or inappropriate ICD therapies. Echocardiography and electrocardiography were unremarkable, and there was no family history of sudden cardiac death. The ventricular fibrillation recorded at the time of the initial TV‐ICD implantation was considered to have occurred under the specific physiological conditions associated with the subarachnoid hemorrhage; therefore, device reimplantation was not performed.

Patch testing performed after system removal revealed a positive reaction to the silicone suture sleeve. Silicone is also present in the generator header and lead cap, and these components anatomically corresponded to the lesion sites. Based on these findings, the patient was diagnosed with silicone‐induced allergic skin ulceration associated with the S‐ICD system. During a 3‐month follow‐up period after extraction, no recurrence of granuloma or fistula formation was observed.

Discussion

Because the localized features of device‐related allergy closely resemble those of device‐related infection at first glance, differentiating between the two conditions can be challenging. Furthermore, due to the lack of clear indications or established guidelines for device‐related allergy, its diagnosis and management can be extremely difficult [4]. However, employing a structured approach may help differentiate hypersensitivity reactions from device‐related infections.

First, clinical features should be carefully evaluated. Unlike device‐related infections, hypersensitivity reactions are frequently localized and are not typically accompanied by fever, leukocytosis, or elevated inflammatory markers such as CRP. Negative or minimal bacterial growth on repeated cultures, particularly in the absence of systemic signs of infection, strongly supports a non‐infectious etiology. Hypersensitivity reactions to device components can manifest as pruritus, pain, cutaneous eruption, erythema, and swelling at the pacemaker (PM) insertion site. Chronic inflammation caused by these hypersensitivity reactions can eventually lead to granuloma formation, pseudo‐capsule formation, and other serious adverse effects. These reactions are most commonly mediated by type IV (delayed‐type) allergic mechanisms, which can manifest over a wide chronological spectrum. Previous reports have demonstrated that the onset of symptoms can vary widely, ranging from a few days to up to 9 years after initial implantation [4, 5, 6]. In the present case, the marked difference in the timing of lesion development between the xiphoid incision site and the lateral dorsal generator region likely reflects differences in local mechanical stress and chronic exposure to silicone‐containing components. The xiphoid region is more susceptible to repetitive mechanical friction caused by trunk movement, which may accelerate local inflammation and earlier manifestation of hypersensitivity reactions. In contrast, the generator was implanted within a deeper intermuscular pocket where mechanical stress was initially limited, potentially resulting in a more gradual progression of chronic inflammation and delayed tissue breakdown. In addition, because highly biocompatible materials such as medical‐grade silicone are generally considered low‐allergenic, hypersensitivity reactions may remain clinically unapparent for several years before becoming evident [6].

Second, once infection has been meticulously excluded, patch testing can serve as a valuable tool for identifying the responsible allergen. Reported allergens include titanium, nickel, silicone, polyurethane, and cobalt. However, it is critical to note that a negative patch test result does not definitively exclude contact dermatitis, particularly when caused by titanium [4].

Regarding management, although conservative treatments such as topical or systemic corticosteroids have been reported [7], they are frequently associated with high rates of recurrence or treatment failure. Based on currently available evidence, complete device removal remains the most definitive treatment, especially in cases presenting with progressive tissue damage, fistula formation, or device exposure. Following extraction, the use of alternative materials or switching to devices made of hypoallergenic substances can be considered. Management strategies documented in the literature include covering the allergenic components with hypoallergenic materials or implanting gold‐coated devices [6, 8, 9, 10].

Allergic skin ulceration associated with S‐ICD systems is exceedingly uncommon. Among the few reported cases, only one identified a specific allergen—cobalt [3]. In the present case, patch testing demonstrated hypersensitivity to silicone. Within the S‐ICD system, silicone‐containing components include the suture sleeve and the generator header [11, 12], both of which were anatomically localized near the patient's skin lesions. Continuous exposure to these components likely resulted in a persistent hypersensitivity reaction, contributing to progressive tissue damage and eventual device exposure.

Although silicone is widely regarded as a biocompatible material and is used broadly in implantable medical devices, it can—albeit rarely—function as an allergen [13]. This case underscores the importance of considering device‐related hypersensitivity reactions, including those involving silicone, in patients who present with noninfectious wound abnormalities following S‐ICD implantation.

In the present case, although patch testing strongly suggested silicone hypersensitivity, histopathological examination of the wound tissue was not performed; therefore, definitive pathological confirmation could not be obtained, which remains a limitation of this case. In addition, advanced imaging modalities for excluding occult infection, such as FDG‐PET/CT, were also not performed, representing another limitation of this report.

Conclusion

Allergic skin ulceration related to subcutaneous implantable cardioverter‐defibrillators is an exceedingly rare complication. However, in patients presenting with noninfectious wound abnormalities after S‐ICD implantation, device‐related hypersensitivity—including reactions to silicone‐containing components—should be considered in the differential diagnosis.
