CluVar: clustering of variants using autoencoder for inferring cancer subclones from single cell RNA sequencing data
Authors: Chae Won Kim, Heewon Park, Dohyeon Kim, Yuchang Seong, Minhae Kwon, Junil Kim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractTumor tissues are composed of malignant subclones with diverse genetic profiles. Reconstructing the evolutionary trajectory of these subclones is crucial for understanding how tumors acquire malignant traits. However, current approaches to subclonal tree reconstruction are limited either by their reliance on single-cell DNA sequencing (scDNA-seq) that involve a small number of cells and thus yield low-resolution results, or using single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data, which despite including larger cell populations, remain susceptible to bias from high dropout rates and technical noise. Here, we introduce CluVar, an autoencoder-based framework for inferring the phylogeny of cancer subclones from scRNA-seq data using mutation profile analysis. To address the extensive missing variant information inherent in scRNA-seq datasets, CluVar incorporates a customized loss function and multiple hidden layers optimized for clustering. CluVar demonstrated superior performance in reconstructing phylogenetic trees of cancer subclones under a range of erroneous conditions. When applied to cancer scRNA-seq data, the phylogenetic tree predicted using CluVar aligned well with the transcriptomic profiles. These findings highlight its utility for tracing evolutionary trajectories and identifying novel variants associated with cancer progression.

Introduction

The cancer cell evolution model describes how mutations and selective pressures drive the development of cancer cell lineages that inherit malignant characteristics, and it serves as a foundation for understanding intratumor heterogeneity [1, 2]. These lineages are shaped by genetically distinct subclones within the same tumor that can drive similar phenotypic traits or exhibit diverse malignant behaviors accelerating tumor growth rate, therapeutic resistance, and risk of disease relapse [3, 4]. Identifying such cancer cell lineages allows a deeper characterization of tumor heterogeneity and cancer-specific traits, thereby facilitating precision medicine through the development of subpopulation-targeted therapeutic strategies.

In recent years, single-cell sequencing (SCS) has emerged as a powerful approach for dissecting cancer heterogeneity [5, 6]. Unlike traditional bulk sequencing, which averages genetic information across heterogeneous cell populations with varying tumor purity, SCS captures genomic and transcriptomic profiles at the resolution of a single-cell [6–8]. This enables a more precise inference of tumor evolution by leveraging the diversity of individual cancer cells. Consequently, numerous studies have used SCS to identify distinct subclonal populations with greater accuracy.

However, most lineage reconstruction studies have relied on single-cell DNA sequencing (scDNA-seq); this typically profiles only a few hundred or thousand cells, which is significantly fewer than that achievable with single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) [9–13]. This suggests that utilizing a large number of cells is important for providing a more accurate and high-resolution inference of cancer cell lineages [14] and that analyses using scRNA-seq data, which typically include relatively large cell populations and are widely available as public datasets, may serve as a promising alternative. Furthermore, scRNA-based mutation profiling enables the mapping of genotypes and gene expression profiles in single cells [15]. In recent years, several studies have used scRNA-seq data to identify cancer cell lineages [16–18]. Nevertheless, the intrinsic limitations of scRNA-seq data, including high dropout rates resulting in missing values and noise associated with lowly expressed mRNAs, remain significant challenges [19–21].

To address the various challenges associated with scRNA-seq data, deep learning approaches have gained significant attention owing to their ability to model complex biological systems [22–24]. Among these, autoencoders have emerged as powerful tools for dimensionality reduction by capturing meaningful latent representations while minimizing reconstruction error [13, 25, 26]. This approach enables the analysis of a large number of cells while effectively reducing noise by compressing the input data and subsequently reconstructing it [27]. Therefore, autoencoders are effective for extracting essential features from high-dimensional and noisy scRNA-seq datasets [27]. However, while the decoder output is typically regarded as denoised data, the latent layer, which contains a more informative and compact representation, is commonly used in most autoencoder-based studies for downstream analyses, such as prediction or clustering [13, 25, 28]. Additionally, dropout-induced missingness continues to pose challenges for model training and applications, highlighting the need for models that can address these issues [29].

In this study, we present CluVar, an autoencoder-based framework that extracts robust mutational signals from scRNA-seq data to reconstruct high-resolution subclonal phylogenies. To address the sparsity and noise inherent in scRNA-seq variant calls, CluVar employs a masked loss function that excludes missing values during training, and it leverages decoder-derived representations to capture denoised biologically informative patterns. This approach enables the accurate and scalable inference of genetically distinct subclones directly from transcriptomic data. We further validated the resulting subclonal phylogenies by examining their transcriptomic profiles, revealing distinct functional identities and temporal shifts in biological programs that may underlie tumor relapse mechanisms driven by cancer stem cell dynamics. To enhance reproducibility and accessibility, we implemented the entire workflow, from preprocessing to clustering, as a snake-make pipeline utilizing singularity (available on GitHub (https://github.com/h2w1/CluVar)), allowing streamlined end-to-end execution without the need to manually integrate multiple tools.

Methods

Single-cell RNA-seq preprocessing, re-alignment, and variant-calling pipeline for cancer samples

A publicly available scRNA-seq dataset was preprocessed for analysis. Table S1 provides the detailed sample metadata, and an overview of the preprocessing pipeline is presented in Fig. S1. FASTQ files from each sample were mapped to the GRCh38 reference genome using CellRanger v7.1.0 with the default parameters. To reliably detect cancer cell–specific mutations, quality control (QC) and, for in vivo datasets, cell type annotation were performed using Scanpy v1.10.3. The QC cut-offs and cell type marker genes were adopted from the original studies and applied to publicly available datasets [30, 31]. Only barcodes that passed the QC thresholds and were identified as malignant cell were used to create a new filtered BAM file. To distinguish different samples, we gave sample specific numbers behind the ‘-’ in cell barcodes and bam files.

Due to its handling of splicing, soft clipping, and indel penalties, the default RNA-seq alignment method (STAR) used by CellRanger may introduce false-positive variants and reference bias in variant calling [32]. To address this, we remapped the BAM file to the reference genome using minimap2 v2.28 with the same parameters described in a previous study [32]. We created a fastq file from the filtered BAM file while placing the UMI and cell barcode information in the read name. After remapping the BAM file, cell barcodes and UMI tags were restored. Next, we followed the GATK best practices for variant calling: sorting, joining read groups, marking PCR duplicates, splitting spliced reads, recalibration, applying base quality score recalibration, mutation calling using HaplotypeCaller, and joint genotyping [33]. After variant calling, to remove known normal and RNA editing sites, we used panels of normal (PoN), RNA editing, 1000 Genome reference variants [33, 34]. After obtaining the filtered variants, BAM files, and barcode information, we employed Vartrix v1.22 (https://github.com/10XGenomics/vartrix) to generate a cell-by-mutation matrix.

Variants were encoded as −1, 0, or 1, where −1 denotes a missing call (neither reference nor alternative reads detected), 0 denotes reference-only reads detected, and 1 denotes alternative reads detected. For variant selection, we ranked the variants by their missing values and retained 300 variants with the lowest proportion of missing values; i.e. the most frequently detected variants. A list of these variants is provided in Table S2. We also removed cells with more than 95% missing values. Variants were annotated to gene symbols using ANNOVAR, based on the RefSeq gene database [35, 36].

Clonal type profiling and parsimony tree construction

To define a clonal genotype for each cluster, we utilized a majority voting approach, while excluding genotype entries labeled −1 (missing values). For each variant within a cluster, the genotype was binarized according to the majority value present among the cells. Variants that exhibited identical genotypes (i.e. consistently alternate or reference across all clusters) were subsequently removed to ensure that only informative mutations (i.e. mixed genotype patterns across clusters) contributed to the downstream analysis. The genotype patterns of each variant are shown in Table S3.

Using the resulting cluster-by-variant binary genotype matrix, a phylogenetic tree was reconstructed using the parsimony approach. Specifically, the binarized cluster-by-variant genotype matrix was first converted into a phyDat object, specifying ‘0’ and ‘1’ as the character states [38]. Hamming distances were then computed and an initial unrooted tree was generated using the neighbor-joining (NJ) algorithm. Parsimony optimization was performed on this initial tree to minimize the total number of mutation events required to explain the observed data. The optimized tree was subsequently rooted in a designated cluster. Finally, the rooted tree structure was visualized alongside genotype matrix to facilitate interpretation of clonal relationships.

In cells harboring the simulated mutation, one parental allele of the chosen SNP was randomly designated as ‘mutant,’ and its read count was retained; in wild-type cells, the mutant-allele count was set to zero. A binomial noise parameter (default P = .001) was then applied to the mutant counts to mimic sequencing error, yielding mutation matrices that preserved scRNA-seq stochasticity while reflecting the desired clonal structure.

The simulation procedure was modified in two ways. First, we augmented the lineage tree by adding additional trunk cells to better capture the early stages of cancer cell evolution. Second, to better match the characteristics of human variants counts in the scRNA-seq data, we replaced the mouse reference with a variant matrix obtained from the Vartrix analysis of 10x scRNA-seq data from a breast cancer cell line. The simulated data were generated to follow a similar distribution, thereby preserving the sparsity and allele imbalance characteristics of the real datasets.

To evaluate whether the actual tree structure could be accurately reconstructed, we used ground-truth cluster labels from the simulated dataset to reconstruct the phylogeny using a parsimony tree algorithm combined with majority voting. Tree similarity between the reconstructed and true topologies was assessed using the normalized Robinson–Foulds (RF) distance implemented in the phangorn R package (v2.12) [38].

scRNA-seq data analysis

scRNA-seq data analysis was performed using Scanpy (v1.10.3) framework [40]. Following standard preprocessing steps, including normalization and highly variable gene selection, we conducted principal component analysis to reduce the dimensionality of the data. To correct for batch effects and integrate multiple datasets, we applied harmony integration using the HarmonyPy (v0.0.10) package, aligning cells across different experimental conditions while preserving biological variability. To compute the expression-based clusters, we applied the Leiden algorithm for unsupervised clustering.

The evolutionary trajectory of cancer subclones was reconstructed using Monocle3 (v1.3.7) [41] with default parameters. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, with selection criteria differing by cluster type: in expression-based clusters, genes with a log fold change (logFC) greater than 1 and a p-value less than 0.05 were selected (Table S4), while in CluVar clusters, the threshold was set to logFC >0 and p-value <0.05 (Table S5).

Enrichment analysis

To characterize the biological functions of each cluster, DEGs from each cluster were subjected to Gene Ontology (GO) and pathway enrichment analysis using Enrichr [42]. KEGG_2021_Human, Reactome_2022, MSigDB_Hallmark_2020, GO_Biological_Process_2023, and WikiPathway_2024_Human databases were used for pathway enrichment. For each cluster, the top five pathways with the lowest p-values were selected to generate a pathway enrichment heatmap.

Results

Overview of CluVar framework

CluVar was developed to reconstruct cancer subclonal trees from mutation profiles derived from scRNA-seq data. An overview of the CluVar framework is presented in Fig. 1. CluVar begins with 10x Genomics scRNA-seq data from cancer samples. For tissue sample datasets that typically include multiple cell types, an additional annotation step is required to isolate cancer cell-specific BAM files. The resulting BAM files undergo remapping, variant calling, filtering, and other preprocessing procedures to produce a variant matrix, where each cell is assigned a value of 1 (alternative), 0 (reference), or − 1 (missing). Although raw variant counts showed reasonable performance, the wide range of input scales negatively affected the stability of the autoencoder training. To address these issues and ensure robust model performance, we ultimately chose to use a binarized format that simplified the input representation (Fig. S2). This matrix served as the input to the CluVar autoencoder, which learned latent representations by combining the embeddings from the encoder and outputs from the lth layers of the decoder. This design enabled the robust extraction of key mutational features, even in the presence of high error rates and dropouts in scRNA-seq data [16, 18]. BGMM clustering was subsequently applied to the integrated feature vectors, with each resulting cluster representing a distinct cancer subclone. A voting-based method was used to infer consensus variants within each subclone. Finally, a parsimony algorithm was employed to reconstruct a subclonal phylogenetic tree.

Overview of CluVar framework reconstructing subclonal phylogenies from scRNA-seq data. CluVar begins with 10x genomics scRNA-seq data from cancer samples. Variant calling and filtering are performed to generate a genotype matrix, where each cell is assigned a value of 1 (alternative), 0 (reference), or − 1 (missing data). This matrix is used as input to an autoencoder, which extracts latent representations. These latent vectors, combined with the decoder’s l-th layer output, are clustered using BGMM to identify cancer subclones. Clonal structure is visualized via a mutation heatmap and phylogenetic tree using a parsimony algorithm. Schematics were created using BioRender (https://biorender.com).

CluVar shows a superior performance on simulation datasets

The simulation datasets provided ground-truth cluster labels, and they were thus rigorously applied to evaluate the CluVar framework. These datasets allowed us to assess the relationship between the autoencoder training performance and clustering quality, compare different clustering algorithms, and test robustness under increasing error rates.

Across 300 different hyperparameter configurations (varying learning rates, activation functions, and initialization methods), we observed a strong negative correlation between the autoencoder training loss and clustering performance (Fig. 2a). Each point represented a different configuration. This trend suggests that in the absence of ground-truth labels in real datasets, selecting hyperparameters yields a low training loss to achieve reliable clustering performance.

CluVar reveals trunk–branch subclonal architecture and functional shifts in breast-cancer cell line

To validate CluVar using real data, we analyzed an ER-positive breast cancer cell line dataset containing a drug-naive sensitive population and a resistant population generated by prolonged ribociclib exposure (6–9 months) (Table S1) [30]. As resistance is expected to increase the mutational burden, the dataset provides a robust test case for detecting clonal expansion. We assessed the sensitivity to key hyperparameters using this dataset of breast cancer cell line. CluVar maintained a low reconstruction loss across a range of learning rates and latent dimensions (Fig. S4), confirming the robustness of the model to moderate changes in hyperparameter settings.

After QC, 30 978 cells remained. Using the 300 most frequently detected mutation sites, CluVar partitioned the cells into seven subclones, which were visualized as a clonal heat map and a phylogenetic tree (Figs. 3a, S5, and Table S6). Subclone numbers were assigned in ascending order of mutational burden. Subclones 0 and 1, which harbored few mutations and resided near the tree root, constituted the trunk, whereas subclones 2–6, with higher mutation counts, formed branches. No additional bifurcation was observed among branch subclones.

To relate the subclone structure to transcriptional states, we projected all cells onto Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) embedding and performed unsupervised expression-based clustering (Fig. 3b). Coloring of the UMAP by CluVar subclones revealed spatially segregated, non-random distributions (Fig. 3c); sensitive cells were dominated by trunk subclones, whereas resistant cells were enriched in branch subclones with greater mutational loads (Fig. 3d). Ordering the expression-based clusters by their fraction of trunk subclones produced a smooth gradient across UMAP (Fig. 3b, d). A Benjamini-Hochberg-corrected hypergeometric test confirmed an almost linear correspondence between these ordered expression-based clusters and mutation-defined subclones (Fig. S6a). The same analysis across experimental conditions showed that subclones 1, 4, and 6 were specifically overrepresented in the resistant state (Fig. S6b). Monocle3 pseudo-time analysis reproduced the consecutive arrangement, underscoring the link between mutational hierarchy and transcriptional dynamics (Fig. 3e).

Finally, a pathway enrichment analysis of both the expression clusters and CluVar subclones revealed a temporal shift in biological programs (Fig. 3f). With the expression-based clusters reordered using CluVar information as a proxy for pseudo-time, early states were enriched for estrogen-response genes and pathways involving nitric-oxide/cGMP signaling, whereas late states showed marked upregulation of aggressive tumor signatures such as hypoxia and epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), and cell-cycle programs including the G2–M checkpoint, DNA replication, and E2F targets. Consistently, the branch subclones that emerged as mutations accumulated were also enriched in cell cycle pathways (Fig. 3g). Subclones sharing key mutations displayed convergent functions; subclones 3 and 5 were enriched for oxidative phosphorylation, whereas subclones 1, 4, and 6 shared a Myc-target signature. These findings demonstrate that CluVar not only resolves variant-based trunk-branch evolutionary relationships in real tumor data but also captures associated transcriptional and functional programs.

Unlike traditional Gaussian Mixture Model (GMM), the BGMM can estimate the optimal number of clusters by penalizing the model complexity during inference. We adopted the number of clusters selected by the model as the default and validated their transcriptomic distinctiveness in subsequent analyses. Although we also tested manually specified cluster numbers in real cancer samples, both fewer and greater than the default, the overall interpretation remained largely unchanged (see Fig. S7).

CluVar delineates trunk–branch subclonal evolution and lineage-specific pathway programs in tissue samples of pancreatic cancer

To test CluVar using more heterogeneous samples, we analyzed scRNA-seq data from three patients with pancreatic cancer (Table S1) [31]. The dataset included paired primary tumor and liver metastasis specimens, which incorporated evolutionary trees and a greater mutational burden in the metastatic samples.

After QC, 45 463 cells were retained. In contrast to the breast cancer cell line dataset, these samples contained non-malignant cell types. Therefore, we annotated the cell types using literature-derived marker genes and restricted the subsequent analyses to 16 951 malignant cells. Application of CluVar to the pooled malignant cells from all three patients yielded a clear trunk–branch architecture of the seven subclones (Figs. 4a, S8, and Table S6). In the parsimony tree, subclones 0 and 1 formed the trunk, whereas the remaining subclones split into two major lineages: subclones 2, 4, and 5 on one branch, and subclones 3 and 6 on the other.

We mapped the subclone structure onto gene expression profiles. Coloring the UMAP by clinical metadata—patient identity and anatomical site (primary versus metastatic)—highlighted the pronounced patient-specific transcriptomic heterogeneity (b). The unsupervised expression-based clusters and CluVar-defined subclones exhibited extensive overlaps (Figs. 4c, d, and S9a). For example, subclone 5 was almost exclusive to patient 3, whereas subclone 6 was largely restricted to patient 2 and enriched in metastatic lesions (Fig. 4d); a Benjamini–Hochberg-corrected hypergeometric test further confirmed that trunk and branch subclones map to distinct expression clusters and identified subclone 6 as metastatic-enriched (Fig. S9b). Next, we inferred the trajectories using Monocle3. Although the CluVar phylogeny revealed a clear bifurcation of cell lineages, Monocle3 arranged these cells along a linear path (Fig. 4e). These results suggest that CluVar not only identifies the root of the trajectory but also provides relationships of bifurcated branches.

Reordering of the expression-derived clusters by CluVar subclone composition revealed a stepwise shift in the enriched pathways (Fig. 4f). The early state, Cluster 0 was enriched for DNA damage responses, showing significant activation of the p53 pathway and UV response signatures. Intermediate states, Clusters 1, 2, 3, and 4 exhibited a stress-adaptation/inflammatory profile with marked upregulation of HSF1 activation, KRAS, IL-17 signaling, and hypoxia pathways. The late states comprised two genetically divergent expression-based clusters, Clusters 5 and 6, each adopting a distinct phenotype. Cluster 5 exhibited strong enrichment for hypoxia, metal detoxification, EMT, and Staphylococcus aureus-associated inflammatory pathways, indicating a highly aggressive, metastatic, and stress-adapted state [46–48]. These features suggest enhanced invasiveness and potential for chemoresistance. In contrast, cluster 6 retained epithelial junction gene expression while mounting a robust type I/II interferon and antiviral response, reflecting an immunologically engaged yet immune-evasive and non-invasive phenotype [49]. The preservation of epithelial integrity may confer resistance to chemotherapy by maintaining a physical barrier that limits drug penetration [50].

CluVar subclones that shared key mutations displayed common biological functional enrichments (Fig. 4g). Subclones 3 and 6 were both enriched for EMT, S. aureus infection, TNF-α/NF-κB signaling, metallothionein-mediated metal binding, and interferon-γ responses. Conversely, subclones 4 and 5 shared programs related to epithelial structure and gastrointestinal epithelial maintenance. These data suggest that, while subclonal evolution drives a common temporal progression of stress-adaptation pathways, it also seeds lineage-specific functional trajectories within heterogeneous cancers.

CluVar shows superior performance than other tools in various simulations

To evaluate CluVar performance, we compared it with two current approaches, SCClone and bmVAE, which cluster cells based on variant information derived from scRNA-seq data. SCClone utilizes a probabilistic mixture model to cluster single cells into subclones, whereas bmVAE employs a deep-learning-based variational autoencoder, similar in architecture to our method [12, 13]. For the evaluation, we used the ARI and Robinson-Foulds (RF) distance. A higher ARI indicates better clustering performance, whereas an RF distance closer to zero reflects greater similarity between the reconstructed subclonal tree and the ground-truth phylogenetic tree (Fig. S10).

In scenario A, which involved 10 000 cells structured in a five-branch subclone tree, both CluVar and SCClone exhibited high ARI scores, whereas bmVAE showed significantly lower performance (Fig. 5a). Furthermore, except for bmVAE, all methods achieved an RF distance of zero, demonstrating perfect concordance between the reconstructed and ground-truth phylogenetic trees (Fig. 5b). Even as the error rate increased from 0% to 5%, CluVar and SCClone maintained high performance. In contrast, bmVAE showed variations in the accuracy of the reconstructed subclonal tree across these scenario datasets, except at an error rate of 1%. In particular, the discrepancy between the reconstructed subclonal tree and the ground-truth phylogenetic tree was the greatest in datasets with a 0% error rate and high ARI variability.

Comparison of CluVar with existing methods across simulated scenarios. (a, c, e) Clustering performance measured by ARI under varying error rates (0%–5%) across three simulation scenarios. (b, d, f) Subclonal tree reconstruction performance measured by generalized Robinson-Foulds (RF) distance under the same error rates and scenarios. (a, b) 10 000 cells with 5 subclones; (c, d) 10 000 cells with 7 subclones; (e, f) 5000 cells with 7 clusters. CluVar consistently outperforms SCClone and bmVAE across all conditions. (g) Runtime comparison of CluVar, SCClone, and bcVAE across the three simulation scenarios. CluVar exhibits the shortest runtime, demonstrating both efficiency and scalability.

In Scenario B, which involved 10 000 cells and a more complex seven-branch tree, the ARI scores for all tools decreased with increasing error rates. However, CluVar consistently outperformed the other tools under all error conditions (Fig. 5c). Moreover, the RF distance of CluVar consistently reached zero in most simulation datasets, indicating a strong concordance with the ground-truth phylogenetic tree (Fig. 5d). This suggests the robustness of CluVar in more heterogeneous settings, where a greater number of subclones emerge.

In Scenario C, consisting of 5000 cells and seven branches, CluVar consistently outperformed both SCClone and bmVAE across error rates ranging from 0% to 5% (Fig. 5e). In addition to clustering accuracy, CluVar consistently outperformed the other methods in accurately reconstructing the phylogenetic tree, showing greater concordance with the ground-truth tree (Fig. 5f). Interestingly, bmVAE outperformed SCClone in this lower-cell-count scenario, suggesting that when the number of cells is limited, deep-learning methods with dimensionality reduction may be more effective than probabilistic models, which can struggle with optimization under such constraints.

Across all three scenarios, CluVar consistently exhibited superior clustering performance. Additionally, we evaluated the runtime of each tool’s clustering process by measuring the time required to obtain clustering results from the input binary matrix (Fig. 5g). To provide a more comprehensive assessment of runtime, we further measured execution times not only in simulation scenarios but also in real datasets (breast cancer cell line and pancreatic cancer), where the runtimes for all steps from raw data preprocessing to clustering are summarized in Table S7. Although the performance of each tool varied depending on the size and complexity of the input data, CluVar consistently achieved shorter execution times under most tested conditions, with the differences becoming more pronounced as the number of cells and overall complexity increased, thereby highlighting its scalability. Taken together, these results suggest that CluVar is particularly well-suited for handling complex tumor heterogeneity and is robust against various sources of technical error, and all CluVar analyses can be reproduced in an automated manner through the CluVar pipeline.

Discussion

The intrinsic heterogeneity of cancer and its clinical implications necessitate accurate reconstruction of cancer cell lineages. While traditional approaches using scDNA-seq or multiome technologies have been informative, they often suffer from limitations such as low cell throughput and a lack of transcriptomic context. In contrast, scRNA-seq captures transcriptomic profiles at a single-cell resolution, offering a means of inferring cellular hierarchies [51]. However, the high error rate and technical noise inherent in these data make them challenging to utilize effectively. To mitigate such issues, numerous AI-based approaches—particularly autoencoder-based techniques—have been developed to remove noise [22–24]. Despite these advances, their application remains limited due to difficulties in processing large-scale single-cell datasets and managing missing values resulting from dropout.

In this study, we present an integrative framework for identifying functional subclusters of cancer cells using both variant information and transcriptomic profiles derived from scRNA-seq data. By incorporating a modified loss function and a decoder layer into a tailored autoencoder architecture, we robustly classified heterogeneous cancer cell populations into subclusters. These subclusters exhibited distinct evolutionary trajectories based on their expression patterns. Subsequent pathway enrichment analyses revealed subcluster-specific biological functions, providing insights into the functional diversification of cancer cells.

We redesigned the autoencoder loss function to mitigate the influence of missing values during training. This approach improved the quality of the latent embeddings by focusing only on the observed data. Many deep-learning-based methods do not explicitly address the issue of missing values in scRNA-seq data [13, 25, 26], often incorporating them into training and introducing noise and biases. Our customized loss function allows CluVar to better capture meaningful variations, leading to improved clustering accuracy.

Notably, the clustering performance improved when latent embeddings were combined with the outputs of the hidden layers of the decoder. Existing autoencoder-based methods typically focus solely on latent embeddings and ignore the informative patterns encoded in the decoder. Because the encoder’s latent output already integrates the upstream encoder information, additional encoder-derived features offer limited benefits. By contrast, the decoder layers progressively reconstruct the input data, filter out noise, and emphasize consistent patterns. By incorporating decoder-derived features, we enhanced the model’s capacity to capture robust structural signals in noisy data, ultimately improving the clustering performance.

Traditional approaches for cancer cell subtyping using scRNA-seq often rely solely on transcriptomic profiles [52, 53]. However, cancer is fundamentally driven by genomic alterations and the integration of mutational information can offer a more comprehensive view of tumor heterogeneity [54, 55]. CluVar addresses this gap by directly incorporating variant information into the representation learning process.

Our results indicate that transcriptional states and mutational backgrounds jointly shape the functional heterogeneity of tumors. Pathway enrichment analysis underscores this point; while subclone-based pathways illuminate lineage-specific processes tied to mutational burden, expression-based clusters capture functional signatures across tumor populations. Together, these findings suggest a molecular mechanism by which the accumulation of genomic alterations selectively reinforces proliferative programs in breast cancer cell lines, while promoting EMT, maintenance of epithelial junctions, and antiviral responses in pancreatic cancer. Integrating these two perspectives provides a more comprehensive understanding of how genetic alterations and transcriptomic features jointly govern tumor cell behavior.

CluVar-derived subclones revealed underlying mutational lineages associated with distinct metabolic and proliferative phenotypes in breast cancer cell lines. Notably, non-synonymous exonic mutations in EIF2S2 and NDUFS8 clearly delineated these subclones. Subclones 3 and 5, which lacked NDUFS8 mutations, exhibited enriched oxidative phosphorylation and depleted expression of cell cycle pathways; these results are consistent with previous reports showing that tumors displaying elevated respiratory enzyme activity tend to be metabolically differentiated and less aggressive [56–58]. Conversely, subclones 1, 4, and 6, which showed increased proportions in resistant cells and harbored both EIF2S2 and NDUFS8 mutations, progressively exhibited enhanced activity in the translational, ribosomal, and MYC target pathways, with subclones 4 and 6 showing strong proliferative signatures. EIF2S2 directly interacts with lncRNA LINC01600, which stabilizes MYC protein, and activates the EIF2S2–LINC01600–MYC [59], thereby promoting proliferation, migration, and invasion via Wnt/β-catenin pathway [59]. The observed upregulation of MYC targets indicates a resistance mechanism in which tumor cells redirect their growth dependency from estrogen-driven pathways inhibited by CDK4/6 blockade instead of activating the CDK2-mediated MYC pathway [60, 61]. Meanwhile, subclone 2, which lacked mutations in both EIF2S2 and NDUFS8, was enriched in the estrogen response and p53 pathways and absent in proliferative expression cluster 6, indicating a favorable prognosis.

CluVar analysis revealed two bifurcating mutational lineages in pancreatic cancer, each associated with a distinct phenotypic trait. Subclones 3 and 6 shared nonsynonymous mutations in GSTP1, whereas LGALS1 mutations were unique to subclone 6. GSTP1 is involved in TNF-α/NF-κB signaling, antioxidant defense via metallothioneins, and IFN-γ–mediated stress responses, while LGALS1 acts as an immunomodulator by suppressing NF-κB and regulating host immune defense [62–64]. However, these protective roles may be co-opted by tumor cells; GSTP1 can support survival and therapeutic resistance through ROS neutralization and apoptosis inhibition, while LGALS1 may promote immune evasion by impairing cytotoxic T cell function and expanding regulatory T cells [65–67]. Subclone 5 harbored mutations in GSDMD, NEAT1, and NCL, which are key regulators of antiviral immunity that are increasingly being recognized for their paradoxical roles in cancer. Although GSDMD typically mediates pyroptosis to eliminate infected cells, its inactivation may suppress immune-mediated cell death in tumors [68, 69]. NEAT1, a paraspeckle-associated lncRNA critical for interferon responses, is frequently overexpressed in cancers, driving proliferation and EMT through Wnt/β-catenin signaling [70, 71]. NCL, a viral RNA-binding protein, promotes tumor growth and immune evasion [72, 73]. These findings suggest that antiviral pathways can be rewired to support immune escape and malignant transformation of cancers.

Although CluVar advances cancer subtyping by integrating mutational data, it has several limitations. We limited the number of variants to the number of cells because of the stability constraints inherent in training autoencoder-based models. Therefore, we employed a filtering strategy to retain only the most confidently detected variants, which may have introduced bias by excluding rare or less reliably detected mutations. Furthermore, instead of applying imputation, we used a majority-voting approach to address dropout events. Although this method is simple and computationally efficient, it may be susceptible to bias in datasets with high levels of missing data. Finally, given the low-read-depth characteristics of scRNA-seq data, the accuracy of mutation calling using GATK is limited. This highlights the need for more robust mutation callers specifically optimized for low-depth single-cell transcriptomic data, such as SCcaller [74] and Monovar [75].

Future studies should explore the application of CluVar to longitudinal and spatially resolved datasets to better capture the temporal dynamics and spatial architecture of tumor evolution. The application of single-cell multimodal omics methods, such as genomic DNA-mRNA sequencing, may also enable orthogonal validation of CluVar-identified variants. To assess the generalizability of our approach, broader applications across diverse tissue types and disease contexts are required. In addition, linking CluVar-defined subclones to clinical annotations, such as therapy resistance or patient outcomes, may reveal predictive biomarkers and guide personalized treatment strategies. Finally, by embedding sparse mutation data into a scalable deep learning framework, CluVar provides a powerful tool for dissecting how genetic alterations and transcriptional programs jointly shape cancer evolution and progression.
