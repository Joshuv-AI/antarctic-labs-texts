Blood Pressure Response Index and Mortality in Patients With Cardiogenic Shock: A Multicenter Retrospective Cohort Study
Authors: Xiaodong You, Yi Zhu, Zhongman Zhang, Yanlong Chen, Xinyi Mao, Jiahui Chen, Zheng Zhou, Wei Li, Yong Mei, Peipei Huang, Xufeng Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe blood pressure response index (BPRI) is an easy, rapid, precise dose–effect index to reflect the cardiovascular response of patients with septic shock. However, the relationship between BPRI and clinical outcomes in patients with cardiogenic shock (CS) has not been elucidated.MethodsWe extracted the clinical data of patients with CS admitted to the ICU from the MIMIC‐IV (v3.1) database and the eICU Collaborative Research Database (eICU‐CRD). The predictive ability for in‐hospital mortality was evaluated using the receiver operating characteristic curve. The relationship between BPRI and in‐hospital mortality was explored utilizing restricted cubic splines, segmented logistic regression, and subgroup analysis. A new prediction model for CS was established and assessed by receiver operating characteristic curves and decision curve analysis.ResultsThere were 1456 and 386 patients in the MIMIC‐IV and eICU‐CRD cohorts, respectively. BPRI had a better area under the receiver operator characteristic curve for predicting in‐hospital mortality than sequential organ failure assessment (SOFA) score and BOS, MA2 risk score in both MIMIC‐IV and eICU‐CRD cohort. We found an L‐shaped association between BPRI and in‐hospital mortality (P for nonlinear <0.001), with the cutoff values at 2.4 in both cohorts. The newly developed model utilizing BPRI demonstrated a good predictive ability for in‐hospital mortality among patients with CS (area under the curve=0.799).ConclusionsBPRI is a powerful predictor for in‐hospital mortality among patients with CS admitted to the ICU. The application of BPRI can help clinicians achieve early warning of mortality risk and early decision‐making, guide the use of vasoactive drugs, and assist the risk stratification.

Cardiogenic shock (CS) is a common cause of intensive care unit (ICU) admission characterized by a low cardiac output, which results in systemic hypoperfusion, persistent hypoxia, and multiple organ dysfunction, ultimately leading to death. 1 , 2 , 3 Despite advances in the treatment and management of CS, 4 , 5 patients continue to face a substantial risk of in‐hospital mortality, with rates ranging from 30% to 50%. 6

The application of vasoactive drugs, such as catecholamines and phosphodiesterase inhibitors, plays a crucial role in improving the hemodynamics and prognosis of patients with CS. 3 , 7 , 8 , 9 However, the response to vasoactive drugs is highly variable among patients with CS due to individual differences. 10 , 11 Previous studies have shown that early changes in vasoactive drug burden are associated with the risk of mortality among patients with CS. 12 The blood pressure response index (BPRI) devised by Du et al. has been proven to be a precise indicator of the response of patients with septic shock to vasoactive drugs and a powerful predictor for prognosis of septic shock. 13 BPRI is calculated by dividing the mean arterial pressure (MAP) at a given time point by the vasoactive‐inotropic score (VIS) as follows: BPRI (t)=MAP (t)/VIS (t). 14 Although it exhibited an excellent predictive ability in septic shock, its utility in predicting the risk of mortality of patients with CS remains unclear. No conclusive evidence exists on the relationship between BPRI and clinical outcomes in patients with CS admitted to the ICU.

The primary objective of this study was to validate the ability of BPRI to predict in‐hospital mortality in patients with CS admitted to the ICU. The secondary objective of this study was to investigate the dose–effect relationship between BPRI and risk of mortality and determine a cutoff value to provide a reference for dynamically adjusting the dosage of vasoactive drugs in patients with CS. Additionally, a novel prognostic model incorporating BPRI was developed for patients with CS admitted to the ICU.

METHODS

Study Population

This is a multicenter study utilizing 2 cohorts from the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care‐IV (MIMIC‐IV) database (version 3.1) 15 and the eICU Collaborative Research Database (eICU‐CRD). 16 Patients with CS admitted to the ICU were included in this study. The diagnosis codes for CS following the International Classification of Diseases, Ninth Revision (ICD‐9), and International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD‐10) are 78 551 and R570. Patients meeting the following criteria were excluded: (1) <18 years old; (2) lacking any BPRI data within 1 week after ICU admission (Figure 1). According to the Society for Cardiovascular Angiography and Interventions (SCAI) cardiogenic shock classification system, 17 patients with SCAI Shock Stage C or greater constituted the target population for this study because all of them received vasoactive agents after diagnosis of CS.

Flowchart of the study population selection.

ICU indicates intensive care unit.

Because of the sensitive nature of the data collected for this study, access to these data sets from qualified researchers trained in human subject confidentiality protocols can be obtained by the policies listed on PhysioNet (https://physionet.org/about/database/). MIMIC‐IV and eICU databases are available to registered users after completing the credentialing process and signing a Data Use Agreement. Given that all data on patients in these data sets were anonymized and deidentified, review approval and informed consent were not required.

Data Extraction and Preprocessing

Data extraction was conducted using Navicat Premium software (version 16.3.8) via structured query language. We collected the following variables: (1) demographic characteristics, encompassing age and sex; (2) comorbidities, including hypertension, coronary heart disease (CHD), atrial fibrillation, diabetes, cerebrovascular disease, liver disease, and renal disease; (3) vasoactive agents and dosage used within 1 week after ICU admission, consisting of dopamine, dobutamine, epinephrine, norepinephrine, vasopressin, and milrinone; (4) procedures, including mechanical ventilation and mechanical circulatory support (MCS) such as ventricular assist devices or intra‐aortic balloon pump; and (5) measurements, such as heart rate, systolic blood pressure, diastolic blood pressure, MAP, respiratory rate, saturation of peripheral oxygen, white blood cell, platelet, hemoglobin, glucose, albumin, blood urea nitrogen (BUN), serum creatinine, sodium, potassium, chlorine, anion gap, prothrombin time, activated partial thromboplastin time, arterial partial pressure of carbon dioxide, lactate, and Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score.

The percentage of missing values was calculated for all continuous variables to minimize bias. Variables with missing values exceeding 20% were excluded, and for variables with missing values <20%, random forest imputation was used. 18

Comparison of BPRI and SOFA Score at Different Given Time Points

BPRI was defined as dividing the MAP at a given time point by the VIS, as described in the previous study. 13 The formula for calculating BPRI is as follows: BPRI (t)=MAP (t)/VIS (t). VIS was calculated using the following formula: VIS=dopamine dose (μg/kg per minute)+dobutamine dose (μg/kg per minute)+epinephrine dose (μg/kg per min)×100+milrinone dose (μg/kg per minute)×10+vasopressin (U/kg per minute)×10 000+norepinephrine dose (μg/kg per minute)×100. Hourly VIS and MAP were used to calculate BPRI for each time point within the 7‐day period after ICU admission.

SOFA score is a commonly used score to assess organ dysfunction in the ICU. 19 The maximum value of SOFA score was recorded within different time spans (24/48/72/168 hours).

The primary outcome of this study was in‐hospital mortality. To fairly compare the predictive ability of BPRI and assess its clinical value, we recorded the minimum value of BPRI, which represents the worst condition, within different time spans (24/48/72/168 hours). The area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) was used to compare the performance of the minimum value of BPRI and the maximum value of SOFA score at different given time points (24/48/72/168 hours) for predicting in‐hospital mortality.

Comparison of BPRI and BOS, MA2 Risk Score

BOS, MA2 risk score is a simple, clinically interpretable risk score that can better predict in‐hospital mortality than existing cardiogenic‐shock risk scores and guide clinical decision‐making at the bedside for patients with CS in the ICU, which was developed using 2 large real‐world data sets (n=8815 for training, n=2237 for validation). 20 It was calculated as previously reported. Briefly, a patient would receive a point for each of the following criteria during the first 24 hours of ICU stay: (1) maximum BUN ≥25 mg/dL; (2) minimum oxygen saturation <88%; (3) minimum systolic blood pressure <80 mm Hg; (4) any use of mechanical ventilation; (5) age ≥60 years; and (6) maximum anion gap ≥14 mmol/L.

To further validate the predictive value of BPRI, we compared the ability to predict in‐hospital mortality between BPRI (168 hours) and BOS, MA2 risk score utilizing receiver operating characteristic curves.

The Relationship Between BPRI and In‐Hospital Mortality

The natural logarithm of BPRI (Log BPRI) was calculated. Restricted cubic spline curves based on a logistic regression model were used to explore the relationship between the minimum value of BPRI within 7 days and in‐hospital mortality and find the cutoff value (odds ratio=1) of the curve if a nonlinear relationship was observed. Then a segmented logistic regression analysis was performed to analyze the association between BPRI and in‐hospital mortality in different groups divided by the cutoff value. Moreover, subgroup analysis was conducted to investigate the effect of BPRI on in‐hospital mortality in different subgroups of hypertension, coronary heart disease, atrial fibrillation, diabetes, cerebrovascular disease, liver disease, renal disease, and MCS in the MIMIC‐IV cohort.

Development of a New Prognostic Model Utilizing BPRI

Considering the good performance of BOS, MA2 risk score for predicting in‐hospital mortality among patients with CS as previously reported, we combined BPRI and the individual components of BOS, MA2 risk score, including age, BUN, saturation of peripheral oxygen, systolic blood pressure, use of ventilation, and anion gap, to develop a new prognostic model for CS. Nomogram was used to display the new model. Receiver operating characteristic curves and decision curve analysis were used to assess the performance of the new model.

Statistical Analysis

The normality of continuous variables was determined using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Kruskal–Wallis test was used to compare continuous variables, and χ2 test was used for categorical variables. Continuous variables are expressed as median (interquartile range). Categorical variables are presented as counts (%). DeLong's test was used to compare the AUROCs of BPRI and SOFA score at different given time points, as well as those of BPRI and BOS, MA2 risk score for predicting in‐hospital mortality. 21 Different spline knots ranging from 3 to 5 were chosen to construct restricted cubic spline regression models to achieve the best fit between BPRI and in‐hospital mortality according to the Akaike information criterion. A 2‐tailed P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.3.3).

In the MIMIC‐IV cohort, within 24 hours, 48 hours, 72 hours, and 168 hours after ICU admission following CS diagnosis, the AUROCs (95% CI) of BPRI for predicting in‐hospital mortality were 0.70 (0.67–0.72), 0.72 (0.69–0.75), 0.74 (0.71–0.76), and 0.75 (0.73–0.78), respectively. DeLong's test showed that BPRI had a superior predictive ability for in‐hospital mortality than SOFA score within different time spans.

In the eICU‐CRD cohort, the AUROCs (95% CI) of BPRI at different time points (24/48/72/168 hours) were 0.75 (0.69–0.80), 0.75 (0.70–0.80), 0.76 (0.72–0.81), and 0.79 (0.75–0.84). Relationship Between BPRI and In‐Hospital Mortality

Restricted cubic spline revealed an L‐shaped association between BPRI and in‐hospital mortality (P for nonlinear <0.001), with the cutoff values at 2.4 (Log BRPI=0.89) in both the MIMIC‐IV cohort and the eICU‐CRD cohort (Figure 3). After adjusting for age, sex, mechanical ventilation, systolic blood pressure, saturation of peripheral oxygen, BUN, and anion gap, segmented logistic regression revealed that the risk of in‐hospital mortality increased significantly with decreasing BPRI (odds ratio=1.98 [95% CI, 1.50–2.63]; P <0.001) among patients with BPRI <2.4, and decreased significantly with increasing BPRI (odds ratio=0.94 [95% CI, 0.90–0.97]; P <0.001) in the MIMIC‐IV cohort. Similar results were seen in the eICU‐CRD cohort (Table S3).

DISCUSSION

BPRI is a new, rapid, and noninvasive dose–effect index to reflect the cardiovascular response of patients with septic shock. 13 However, its role in predicting clinical outcomes in patients with CS remains unclear. In this study, we investigated the relationship between BPRI and in‐hospital mortality of patients with CS admitted to the ICU. Our study has 3 important findings: (1) BPRI exhibited a better predictive capability for in‐hospital mortality in patients with CS compared with SOFA score and BOS, MA2 risk score. (2) We found an L‐shape relationship between BPRI and risk of mortality, with cutoff values at 2.4 in both the MIMIC‐IV cohort and the eICU‐CRD cohort. (3) Combining BPRI and the measured variables of BOS, MA2 risk score could be a useful tool for predicting in‐hospital mortality of patients with CS.

The central feature of CS is an inability to maintain an adequate stroke volume despite sufficient preload, leading to reduced cardiac output. 1 Low cardiac output causes organ hypoperfusion and tissue hypoxia, impairing cellular metabolism and resulting in the accumulation of metabolic toxins such as lactate, which further exacerbate coronary ischemia, and impair myocardial function and cardiac power index. 2 , 6 , 8 Additionally, systemic inflammatory response syndrome often accompanies CS. 22 It is triggered by tissue hypoxia, ischemia–reperfusion injury, and the release of pro‐inflammatory mediators. These factors can finally lead to multiple organ dysfunction, affecting organs such as the brain, liver, kidneys, and lungs. 23 Furthermore, systemic inflammatory response syndrome impairs vascular tone elasticity and contributes to microcirculatory dysfunction, exacerbating the overall condition. 24 , 25 , 26 Therefore, the achievement of hemodynamic stability is crucial for improving the prognosis of patients with CS. 8 Vasoactive drugs are commonly used to manage hemodynamic instability by stabilizing blood pressure and improving cardiac output. However, individual variations in baseline cardiac function, comorbidities, and responses to clinical interventions can lead to diverse responses to these medications. 10 , 11 Thus, an easy‐to‐use indicator for dynamically assessing the reactivity of vasoactive drugs is necessary for patients with CS to objectively quantify the degree of hemodynamic support.

Although MAP and VIS have been proven to be valuable indicators for the prognosis of patients with CS, 10 , 27 relying solely on MAP or VIS to evaluate the circulatory status seems to be unreasonable. For patients with CS, individual MAP can be influenced by vasoactive drugs or other different interventions, which may limit its reliability as an independent marker. 28 Similarly, while VIS provides insight into the cumulative dose and intensity of vasoactive drugs, the lack of standardized protocols for titrating these drugs in clinical practice can result in overdoses due to variations in individual drug responsiveness. BPRI, which was recently proposed by Chen et al., exhibited an excellent performance in the assessment of individual response to vasoactive drugs. 13 Building on this foundation, we aimed to investigate its applicability in patients with CS. Our results demonstrated that BPRI was a powerful predictor for in‐hospital mortality among patients with CS admitted to the ICU. Considering that BPRI is a noninvasive marker that reflects real‐time changes of vasoactive response to vasoactive drugs, it could provide clinical workers with a more precise bedside tool for the management of patients with CS. Early warning of mortality risk can be achieved in patients with CS who are administered vasoactive drugs by calculating BPRI at the bedside quickly, without waiting for laboratory tests. This is superior to conventional prognostic tools such as SOFA score and simplified acute physiology score II, which are dependent on laboratory tests and unable to be accessed in a short time.

When investigating the relationship between BPRI and in‐hospital mortality, we observed a distinct L‐shaped relationship, with cutoff values at 2.4 in both 2 cohorts. The identification of the cutoff value is of clinical significance because it could guide early decision‐making, provide a reference for considering the initiation of MCS, and optimizing the dosages of vasoactive drugs dynamically, and assist the risk stratification. BPRI provides a quantitative, dynamic, real‐time measure that can achieve early warning of adverse outcomes. If BPRI is <2.4, it indicates a poor cardiovascular responsiveness and circular status, thereby assisting in early identification of high‐risk patients. When BPRI is persistently <2.4 despite the high burden of vasoactive drugs, we may initiate MCS as soon as possible, including intra‐aortic balloon pump and extracorporeal membrane oxygenation, rather than continue escalating doses or increasing numbers of vasoactive drugs. Currently, the SCAI shock stage classification has been widely used for risk stratification, prognostic assessment, and decision‐making guidance for adult patients with CS. According to SCAI shock stage classification, if patients with stage C develop worsening hemodynamics (requiring escalating doses or increasing numbers of vasoactive drugs), they are classified into stage D, indicating a worse prognosis for them. 29 However, SCAI shock stage classification does not provide a definite criterion for escalating doses or increasing numbers of vasoactive drugs; therefore, clinicians often judge it subjectively. The cutoff value found in our study may provide a reference for quantification of worsening hemodynamics, and help assist the risk stratification of CS combined with SCAI shock stage classification.

The nonlinear L‐shaped relationship between BPRI and in‐hospital mortality has a certain physiologic and clinical rationale. MAP is determined by cardiac output and systemic vascular resistance, and cardiac output equates to the product of heart rate and stroke volume. 30 Vasoactive drugs maintain the MAP through boosting cardiac output by strengthening heart rate and myocardial contractility, and regulating the systemic vascular resistance. 31 Due to the physiologic reserve, MAP can be maintained by relying on very small doses of vasoactive drugs. Extensive clinical practice has found that low doses of vasoactive drugs can significantly improve circulatory function in patients with shock. 9 Additionally, due to compensatory limit, further increases in heart rate, myocardial contractility, or systemic vascular resistance no longer elevate cardiac output and MAP, so increased VIS is unable to maintain MAP for these patients with poor compensatory capacity. 32 Therefore, as VIS increases, MAP demonstrates a pattern of rapid initial rise followed by a gradual increase and ultimately stabilizes, leading to a distinct L‐shaped relationship between BPRI and in‐hospital mortality.

In recent years, researchers have focused on developing new, simple, sensitive indicators or tools for predicting clinical outcomes in patients with CS, such as diastolic blood pressure, dipeptidyl peptidase 3, venous‐to‐arterial CO2 difference, VIS, and BOS, MA2 risk score. 20 , 33 , 34 , 35 , 36 In this study, we developed a new prediction model combining BPRI and the measured variables of BOS, MA2 risk score. As mentioned above, in addition to hemodynamic alterations, a key characteristic of CS is systemic organ hypoperfusion caused by pump failure. Therefore, relying solely on BPRI to assess the prognosis of patients with CS may be insufficient, even though it demonstrated strong predictive capability for in‐hospital mortality in our study. Existing general ICU mortality risk scores, such as the acute physiology and chronic health evaluation II score and simplified acute physiology score II, are frequently complex to calculate and hard to achieve rapid bedside evaluation. It is reported that BOS, MA2 risk score achieves higher discrimination than the CS‐specific CardShock score and Intra‐Aortic Balloon Pump in Cardiogenic Shock II risk score. Also, compared with SOFA score and Oxford Acute Severity of Illness Score, it exhibits similar discrimination with better calibration. Considering that the measured variables of BOS, MA2 risk score provides a comprehensive evaluation of multiple aspects of patients with CS and can be quickly implemented at the bedside, 20 we combined BPRI with the measured variables of BOS, MA2 risk score to develop a novel prognostic tool for CS. Subsequent analysis validated the predictive ability and clinical utility of the new tool. The novel prognostic tool combining BPRI and the measured variables of BOS, MA2 risk score holds promise for aiding clinicians in early risk stratification of patients with CS, guiding tailored interventions, and optimizing their management.

Several limitations to this study should be considered. Firstly, our study was a retrospective cohort study; thus, it could not be as convincing as prospective studies. Secondly, not all potential confounders were included in this study due to the limitations of the database, such as physical activities, tobacco use, alcohol abuse, and history of long‐term medication use. This may affect the accuracy of the results. Thirdly, CS was defined solely according to ICD codes in our study, which may introduce selection bias and lead to the omission of some patients with CS. Finally, the sample size of the eICU‐CRD cohort was relatively small, which may lead to potential biases or limit the generalizability of the findings. Therefore, further studies are needed to confirm our findings.

CONCLUSION

Our study demonstrated that BPRI was a rapid, noninvasive, powerful predictor for in‐hospital mortality among patients with CS admitted to the ICU. It also offers valuable guidance for clinicians to dynamically optimize the dosages of vasoactive drugs. Combining BPRI with the measured variables of BOS, MA2 risk score further enhances the ability to predict in‐hospital mortality, providing a robust tool for risk stratification and informing clinical decision‐making in the management of patients with CS.
