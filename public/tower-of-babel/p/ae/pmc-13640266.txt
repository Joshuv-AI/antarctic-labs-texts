Whole-genome sequences of Enterobacter cloacae H5 and Enterobacter bugandensis H6 isolated from patients in Khartoum State, Sudan
Authors: Hiba A. A. Mahgoub, Mogahid M. Elhassan, Hind H. Ahmed, Hisham N. Altyab, Mohamed E. Hamid
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveEnterobacter species are important nosocomial pathogens, mainly due to multidrug resistance (MDR). This study reports the whole-genome sequences of Enterobacter cloacae H5 and Enterobacter bugandensis H6.Data descriptionDigital DNA-DNA hybridization (dDDH) confirmed species identity: H5 (91.8% with E. cloacae ATCC 13047) and H6 (89.3% with E. bugandensis EB-247). H5 has 46 contigs, 4,867,245 bp, GC 54.97%; H6 has 27 contigs, 4,616,569 bp, GC 56.05%. Phylogenetic analysis grouped H5 within E. cloacae and H6 within E. bugandensis. Both genomes harbor multiple antimicrobial resistance (AMR) genes. H5 includes β-lactamase CMH-16, FosA2, efflux pumps (oqxA, emrB), and regulators (ramA, baeR, baeS). H6 includes β-lactamase ACT-146, FosA2, efflux pumps (oqxA, emrB, msbA), regulators (baeR, baeS), and target modification gene ArnT. These genomes highlight the clinical significance of these strains and provide essential resources for understanding the genomic basis of antimicrobial resistance in Sudanese Enterobacter isolates.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12863-026-01471-1.

Objective

Enterobacter, a genus of Gram-negative, facultative anaerobic, rod-shaped bacteria, is recognized as a significant cause of nosocomial infections worldwide [1]. These pathogens can produce a wide range of infections, including bacteremia, respiratory tract infections, and urinary tract infections, particularly in immunocompromised patients [2]. Enterobacter species, for example E. aerogenes, E. cloacae, and E. hormaechei, are part of the ESKAPE pathogens, recognized for their multidrug-resistant phenotypes and ability to thrive in hospital environments [3].

The increase of antibiotic resistance in Enterobacter has been promoted by the common use of broad-spectrum antibiotics, leading to many outbreaks and difficulties in efficient treatment [4]. These organisms regularly display intrinsic resistance mechanisms, comprising the production of AmpC β-lactamases (Mezzatesta et al., 2012) and extended-spectrum β-lactamases (ESBLs) [1], reducing treatment options.

In spite of extensive research into their resistance profiles, the pathogenic mechanisms of Enterobacter remain poorly known [2, 5]. The introduction of genome sequencing technology offers a hopeful means for solving the genetic basis of resistance and virulence [6, 7]. This study aims to investigate whether the whole-genome sequences of Sudanese Enterobacter isolates differ from global reference genomes and to identify any novel mutations associated with antimicrobial resistance. Understanding these genetic variants is essential for tackling the public health threat caused by multidrug-resistant Enterobacter species in resource-limited settings.

This study presents a genomic analysis of Enterobacter cloacae strain H5 and Enterobacter bugandensis strain H6, both isolated from patients in Khartoum State, Sudan, as part of a surveillance initiative. An overview of all datasets generated in this study is provided in Table 1.

These strains’ taxonomic location was explained by the use of dDDH, which confirmed that they were E. cloacae (aligning with E. cloacae ATCC 13047) and E. bugandensis (aligning with E. bugandensis EB-247), respectively (Fig. 1) which is available in the associated data repository. The dDDH values, along with genome sequencing, serve as robust methods for bacterial species delineation [15].

The finding of multiple antimicrobial resistance genes in both genomes is a significant worry, indicating the global increase in antibiotic resistance among Enterobacteriaceae [16].

Antimicrobial resistance genes were identified using CARD and RGI under strict criteria. In E. cloacae H5, β-lactamase (CMH-16), FosA2, and efflux pump and regulatory genes (oqxA, emrB, ramA, baeR, baeS) were detected. In E. bugandensis H6, class C β-lactamase (ACT-146), FosA2, efflux pump and regulatory genes (oqxA, emrB, msbA, baeR, baeS), and ArnT, involved in peptide antibiotic target modification, were identified. All AMR genes for H5 and H6 were summarized in the AMR genes file, available in the associated data repository. The identified resistance mechanisms, including antibiotic efflux pumps, antibiotic inactivation enzymes, and target alterations, highlight the diverse strategies employed by these bacteria to evade the effects of antimicrobial agents. These findings feature the clinical relevance of these strains, especially taking into account the rising prevalence of multidrug-resistant Enterobacteriaceae in hospital sites [5].

The study’s findings support existing literature on the role of Enterobacter species as opportunistic pathogens capable of causing a range of infections, including bacteremia, lower respiratory tract infections, and urinary tract infections. The presence of multiple antimicrobial resistance genes in these strains agrees with the established capacity of Enterobacteriaceae to acquire and spread resistance genes through horizontal gene transfer [16]. This draws attention to the value of continued surveillance and monitoring of antimicrobial resistance patterns in Enterobacteriaceae to address appropriate treatment plans and infection control measures [17, 18].

E. cloacae and E. bugandensis have emerged as clinically significant nosocomial pathogens, predominantly in immunocompromised patients. An E. cloaca is known to cause a range of infections, including pneumonia, septicemia, and urinary tract infections, while E. bugandensis has been associated with neonatal sepsis [17]. The identification of specific resistance genes, such as beta-lactamase genes (bla genes), colistin-resistant genes, tetracycline-resistant genes, and aminoglycoside-resistant genes, provides valuable information for understanding the mechanisms of resistance in these strains and for developing strategies to combat them [19].

Limitations

Two isolates from a specific geographic area were the sole focus of the investigation, which might not accurately reflect the genetic diversity of these species [20]. This Data Note describes genome sequences from two clinical isolates obtained from a single geographic location, which may not reflect the broader genomic diversity of Enterobacter species circulating in other regions. Although the study found several genes linked to antibiotic resistance, it lacked functional tests to verify the genes’ expression and activity. Phenotypic antimicrobial susceptibility testing data were not available for these isolates; therefore, direct correlations between resistance genotypes and phenotypic resistance profiles could not be assessed. In addition, plasmid localization of resistance genes was not experimentally validated, and resistance determinants were identified based on in silico predictions. However, this limitation does not affect the validity of the genomic data presented, and plasmid localization can be investigated in future studies. The precise role that these genes play in the resistance phenotypes that have been reported requires more investigation. Other elements that might contribute to the virulence and pathogenicity of these strains were not thoroughly examined in the study, which mainly concentrated on genes linked to antibiotic resistance. Future studies integrating phenotypic data, plasmid characterization, and larger isolate collections will further enhance the interpretation of these genomic findings. The entire spectrum of virulence factors and their function in infection require more investigation. Notwithstanding these drawbacks, this research offers insightful information about the genomic traits of E. cloacae and E. bugandensis.
