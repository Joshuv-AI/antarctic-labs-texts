The association between systemic immune-inflammatory index and cardiac biomarkers among US population: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Xinmeng Liu, Shihao Jin, Mei Ma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe systemic immune-inflammatory index (SII), calculated from platelet, neutrophil, and lymphocyte counts, is a novel marker of inflammation potentially relevant to cardiac disease. While cardiac biomarkers are used clinically, the relationship between SII and these markers in the general population is unclear.MethodsWe analyzed data from 3206 US adults (aged 20–85 years) in the 1999–2004 National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES). Multivariable linear regression models, adjusted for demographics, comorbidities, and lifestyle factors, assessed cross-sectional associations of SII with cardiac biomarkers. Nonlinearity was examined using restricted cubic splines. Subgroup analyses explored effect modification.ResultsSII showed a consistent positive association with C-reactive protein (CRP) levels across all models (All P < 0.001). In contrast, the positive association of SII with NT-pro BNP observed in the unadjusted model (β = 0.20; 95% CI 0.11, 0.29; P < 0.001) was attenuated and became non-significant after full adjustment in the general population. Subgroup analyses showed significantly positive associations between SII and NT-pro BNP specifically in individuals aged > 55 years (β = 0.17; 95% CI 0.014, 0.33), and in those with myocardial infarction (MI) (β = 0.82; 95% CI 0.22, 1.43) or angina pectoris (AP) (β = 0.73; 95% CI 0.19, 1.47). The robust SII-CRP association was confirmed in subgroups. Dose–response relationships were supported by spline analyses.ConclusionsSII is persistently associated with CRP levels, supporting its role as an inflammatory indicator. Significant associations between SII and NT-pro BNP were observed only in specific high-risk subgroups (aged > 55 years, MI or AP patients), but not in the fully adjusted general population. These findings suggest SII may have particular relevance in specific cardiovascular contexts.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40001-025-03143-9.

Introduction

Cardiovascular diseases (CVD), including coronary heart disease (CHD), heart failure (HF), and myocardial infarction (MI), remain the leading global cause of mortality and morbidity, imposing a significant public health burden [1, 2]. Inflammation is a well-established key driver in the pathogenesis and progression of CVD [3]. Consequently, various inflammatory indices derived from routine blood counts, such as the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) and platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR), have been developed and linked to CVD risk, severity, and prognosis [4–8].

The systemic immune-inflammatory index (SII), introduced in 2014, evaluates an individual’s inflammatory status based on platelet counts, neutrophils, and lymphocytes, and has gained widespread acceptance [9]. Recent research has linked SII to the incidence of CHD [10], the risk of HF [11, 12], establishing it as a cost-effective and clinically relevant indicator of heart health [10]. Despite its promise, previous studies have primarily examined the relationship between SII and heart diseases, overlooking its association with a range of sensitive biomarkers. Notably, the correlation between SII and cardiac biomarkers remains unconfirmed.

Recent studies have shown that cardiac biomarkers can effectively identify individuals at high risk of developing heart disease, even within the general population [13]. Various cardiac biomarkers and related indicators are utilized in clinical settings to evaluate heart damage and cardiovascular workload, including CRP [14], homocysteine (HCY) [15], high-sensitivity cardiac-specific troponin [16] and N-terminal-pro B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-pro BNP) [17]. The cardiovascular metabolic index (CMI) is a metabolism-related indicator that has gradually attracted the attention of researchers for its relationship with heart health [18]. However, the association between SII and these indicators in the general population remains unexplored. To address this gap, we conducted a hypothesis-generating, cross-sectional analysis to investigate the relationship between SII and cardiac biomarkers, as well as cardiac-related indicators, among participants in the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES).

Methods

Study population

NHANES is a comprehensive cross-sectional survey conducted by the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS) under the auspices of the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) in the United States. NHANES employs a complex multistage probability sampling design to procure a nationally representative sample of the civilian population, enabling accurate estimation of health and nutritional status indicators across all age groups within the U.S. populace [19]. This encompassing study spans three survey cycles (1999–2000, 2001–2002, and 2003–2004). Excluding 281 pregnant participants, our analysis ultimately comprised 3206 individuals aged 20–85 years from NHANES data collected between 1999 and 2004. Ethical considerations are paramount in NHANES, with informed consent procedures meticulously adhering to rigorous ethical standards and regulatory guidelines to safeguard participants’ rights and confidentiality. Comprehensive details regarding NHANES study designs and data are readily accessible at https://wwwn.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/Default.aspx.

Measurement of exposure and outcome variables

Blood and urine samples underwent processing and were preserved at frozen temperatures (− 20 ℃) before being dispatched to the University of Washington for analysis. SII, a pioneering biomarker, captures the systemic inflammatory response by integrating peripheral blood cell counts, including lymphocytes, neutrophils, and platelets. SII is computed as the product of platelet count and neutrophil count divided by the lymphocyte count [9]. NT-pro BNP (Roche Diagnostics) was assayed in serum using the Roche Cobas e601 autoanalyzer. CRP levels were determined through latex-enhanced nephelometry. Routine biochemical markers such as high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDLC) and triglycerides (TG) were quantified using a Hitachi Model 704 multichannel analyzer (Boehringer Mannheim Diagnostics, Indianapolis, IN). Four distinct high-sensitivity troponin (hs-troponin) assays were analyzed: hs-troponin T (Roche Cobas e601 with Elecsys reagents) and hs-troponin I (Abbott ARCHITECT i2000SR, Siemens Centaur XP [TNIH], Ortho Vitros 3600). In this study, these four high-sensitivity troponins were labeled Hs-troponin T, Abbott, Siemens, and Ortho. Each hs-troponin was treated as an independent outcome variable in separate regression models. HCY levels were determined using Abbott Diagnostics’ Fluorescence Polarization Immunoassay (FPIA) on an Abbott IMX analyzer. CMI is derived from biochemical and anthropometric indicators [20], with the formula: CMI = TG (mmol/L)/HDLC (mmol/L) × WHtR, where WHtR represents the ratio of waist circumference (cm) to height (cm).

Variables

The covariates encompassed factors previously demonstrated or theorized to correlate with SII or cardiovascular well-being, comprising demographic parameters, clinical markers, inflammatory elements, dietary supplementation, medication records, and comorbidities. Demographic factors encompassed gender, height, weight, age, race, education, alcohol intake, body mass index (BMI), household income to poverty ratio (PIR), systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), serum cotinine levels, and various biochemical parameters, including total cholesterol (TC), TG, HDLC, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDLC), total protein (TP), albumin (ALB), globulin (GLB), glycohemoglobin, alanine aminotransferase (AST), glutamate aminotransferase (ALT), gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT), lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), total bilirubin (TB), platelet count (PLT), neutrophils, lymphocytes, and mean platelet volume (MPV). Hypertension was delineated by criteria such as systolic blood pressure ≥ 130 mmHg, diastolic blood pressure ≥ 80 mmHg, or self-reported physician diagnosis [21]. Diabetes was defined as either a self-reported physician diagnosis or HbA1c levels exceeding 6.5% [22]. Information regarding dietary supplement usage, hypertension, high cholesterol, and diabetes medication, along with a medical history encompassing HF, AP, CHD, and MI, was obtained through a structured questionnaire. The questionnaire assessed drinking frequency through the inquiry: “In the past 12 months, on those days that you drank alcoholic beverages, on the average, how many drinks did you have?” Metabolic equivalent (MET) data were gathered utilizing the Global Physical Activity Questionnaire. Following the WHO analysis guidelines, physical activity (PA) was converted into MET minutes of moderate to vigorous activity per week. This calculation was based on MET values derived from the type, frequency, and duration of activities weekly, formulated as: PA (MET-min/wk) = MET × weekly frequency × duration of each activity [23].

Statistical analysis

We accounted for the complex survey design, incorporating survey weights in all analyses to ensure the extrapolation of estimates to the 1999–2004 US adult population. The baseline characteristic table of the study population was divided into four groups based on the SII. Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviations (Mean ± SD), while categorical variables were presented as proportions. After ln-transformation to approximate a normal distribution, then using Spearman correlation to observe the correlation between SII and other clinical indicators. We investigated the relationship between SII and the cardiac biomarkers using multivariate linear regression with three models: model 1 (unadjusted variables), model 2 (adjusted for gender, age, race, and education), and model 3 (adjusted for gender, age, race, education, PIR, BMI, MET, drinking habits, comorbidities, dietary supplement, medication use, and dyslipidemia). Comprehensively consider the classification of TC, TG, HDLC, and LDLC to determine whether dyslipidemia have occurred [24]. Restricted cubic spline (RCS) regression was used to verify the relationship between SII and cardiac biomarkers. Subsequently, a subgroup analysis was conducted to divide the population into different levels, including gender, age, hypertension, MI, HF, CHD, AP, and added interaction terms to test for heterogeneity among subgroups. All analyses were performed using R version 4.1.0 (http://www.R-project.org), with statistical significance set at a two-sided P < 0.05.

Association between SII and cardiac biomarkers

Initially, we examined the relationships among various clinical indicators (Fig. S1). Our findings showed that SII exhibited the most robust positive correlation with neutrophils count (r = 0.71), followed by PLT (r = 0.52), CRP (r = 0.26), and NT-pro BNP (r = 0.14). Conversely, the most substantial negative correlation was observed between SII and lymphocyte count (r = − 0.41). Furthermore, hs-troponin T showed significant positive correlations with CRP (r = 0.088), hs-troponin I (r = 0.055–0.146) and NT-pro BNP (r = 0.51).

Three multivariate linear regression models were employed to assess the correlation between SII and cardiac biomarkers, as shown in Table 2. While SII showed an initial positive association with NT-pro BNP, this relationship attenuated substantially after comprehensive adjustment. In contrast, SII maintained a robust positive association with CRP across all adjustment levels. Analysis of restricted cubic spline regression

In this study, we employed RCS to explore the relationship between the SII and various cardiac biomarkers, controlling for demographic and physiological variables including gender, age, race, education level, PIR, and BMI. This analysis showed a significant nonlinear association between SII and most cardiac biomarkers (All P-for-nonlinear < 0.05, Fig. 1), with the exception of HCY. Figure 1A illustrates a U-like relationship between SII and NT-pro BNP, characterized by a decline followed by a subsequent increase in NT-pro BNP as SII levels rise. Conversely, Fig. 1H depicts an inverted U-shaped curve for the relationship between SII and CMI, where an initial increase in CMI is succeeded by a decrease with higher SII levels. Relationships of SII with cardiac biomarkers in subgroups

Table S2–S9 presents the relationship between SII and cardiac biomarkers across diverse demographic and clinical subgroups. In the initial unadjusted model, a notable linear correlation was evident between SII and NT-pro BNP as well as CRP across all subgroups (All P < 0.05; Table S2-S3). After adjusting for selected covariates, significant positive associations were found between SII and NT-pro BNP in the age > 55 years, the MI, and the AP subgroup, the positive correlation between SII and CRP was found in almost all models.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this study represents the first investigation into the correlation between the SII and cardiac biomarkers. While the SII-NT-pro BNP association attenuated after full adjustment, strong subgroup associations (> 55 years/MI/AP patients) and consistent SII-CRP relationships constitute key findings.

In recent decades, there has been a growing recognition of the profound impact of the immune system and inflammation on cardiac development and health [25]. Cells within the immune system, along with their secreted factors, assume pivotal roles in the initiation, progression, and resolution of inflammation subsequent to cardiac disease [26]. The immune system is the primary driver of the protective inflammatory response, vital for safeguarding against infections, facilitating wound healing and tissue regeneration, and preserving immune tolerance to self-antigens [27]. Notably, the association between inflammation and alterations in peripheral blood cells [28] has prompted an exploration into the potential utility of hematologic pro-inflammatory markers. Given the cost-effectiveness and accessibility of blood biochemical markers derived from routine tests, they offer valuable insights into systemic inflammation status, fueling a trend towards assessing the diagnostic and prognostic significance of these markers across various chronic diseases [5].

Inflammation emerges as a pivotal contributor to the genesis and progression of cardiovascular disease, with evidence indicating its utility as a risk indicator and prognosticator for adverse events [29]. While inflammatory hematological ratios (e.g., NLR, PLR) offer valuable insights into the unique pathophysiology of CVD [30], our research suggests the association between the SII and cardiac biomarkers across distinct disease subgroups. SII’s tri-parametric design captures broader inflammatory activity than dual-parameter indices like NLR or PLR. SII exhibits greater stability compared to individual blood counts, which are susceptible to physiologic conditions or exercise [31, 32]. Yang observed its better predictive capacity for major cardiovascular events post-coronary intervention compared to traditional risk factors [33]. Furthermore, other studies also affirm its efficacy in prognosticating the severity, complications, and mortality associated with CVD [34, 35]. Crucially, the SII-NT-pro BNP association observed exclusively in MI/ AP patients provides clinical validation for SII as a targeted surrogate marker in contexts where conventional biomarkers may lack sensitivity.

While no direct evidence is available, several studies have explored the association between SII and heart diseases. A meta-analysis revealed that elevated SII significantly correlates with an increased risk of heart diseases, including myocardial infarction [34]. While initial analyses suggested an association between SII and NT-pro BNP, this relationship attenuated substantially after comprehensive adjustment. The attenuation suggests that metabolic factors (e.g., TC, TG, LDL-C), lifestyle variables (e.g., MET, drinking), and medication use accounted for the observed crude association, as indicated by β-value reduction from 0.20 to 0.064. Notably, clinically meaningful associations persisted in high-risk subgroups. This indicates that SII’s relationship with NT-pro BNP may become apparent in contexts of established cardiac stress. NT-pro BNP, a member of the natriuretic peptide family, serves as a cardiogenic hormone released in response to myocardial strain [36]. Epidemiological studies have consistently shown that higher levels of natriuretic peptides are positively and significantly associated with cardiovascular mortality or major adverse cardiovascular events [37, 38]. Additionally, we identified a positive correlation between SII and CRP. CRP, a well-studied inflammation indicator, is recognized as a risk marker for heart diseases. The persistent and dysregulated inflammatory response is acknowledged as a contributor to heart disease pathogenesis. Although our findings only showed a crude model’s positive association between SII and CMI, this may offer insights for future studies. CMI, a novel metabolic index calculated as the product of waist circumference/height and TG/HDLC, can evaluate an individual’s cardiovascular disease risk. Elevated CMI may indicate the presence of cardiac pathophysiological processes such as atherosclerosis [39]. Although no studies directly examined the relationship between SII and CMI, epidemiological evidence indicates that SII is positively associated with other cardiometabolic risk biomarkers like HDLC and non-HDLC [40]. While data on the relationship between SII and cardiac biomarkers are limited, our findings potentially support the notion that SII is linked to the development and prognosis of heart disease [34].

In the analysis using RCS, it was observed that SII had a U-shaped relationship with most of the cardiac markers studied, while it had an inverted U-shaped relationship with CMI. Low levels of SII may reflect a weaker inflammatory state, characteristic of reduced cardiac stress. There appears to be an intrinsic protective mechanism within the body wherein a certain degree of low-grade inflammation fosters cardiac well-being [41]. Hs-troponin T and hs-troponin I are pivotal indices of cardiac insult [42]. Elevated inflammation levels precipitate augmented cardiac strain or myocardial injury, thereby leading an escalation in cardiac biomarker levels. The elevated inflammatory state exhibited a negative correlation with CMI, potentially indicative of mid-range SII potentially stimulates adipose lipolysis, whereas severe inflammation suppresses metabolic homeostasis through glucocorticoid signaling and insulin resistance [43].

Through subgroup analysis, a stronger effect of the SII and NT-pro BNP association was observed in the > 55 years age group, as well as in patients diagnosed with MI and AP. Consistent with our results, Xu reported a robust stronger positive correlation between SII and total cardiovascular disease among individuals aged ≥ 65 years, hypertensive, or diabetic [44]. The elderly population are commonly featured with chronic, low-grade inflammation, often resulting in tissue deterioration or degeneration, strongly linked to increased morbidity and mortality [45]. MI and AP, being pivotal cardiac pathologies, have been increasingly associated with heightened inflammation and oxidative stress within cardiomyocytes, precipitating cellular injury, endothelial dysfunction, and vascular remodeling, thereby exacerbating vascular impairment [46]. Furthermore, our study comprised a smaller proportion of patients with MI (5.7%), HF (3.4%), CHD (5.9%), and AP (4.8%), potentially limiting our ability to uncover additional associations.

Our study has some strengths. Firstly, it stands out as the first investigation to find the correlation between SII and cardiac biomarkers, controlling for potential confounders such as serum cotinine levels, drinking habits, comorbidities, medication use, and physical activity, thus minimizing residual confounding effects. Secondly, our study focused on a diverse array of cardiac biomarkers, rather than a single indicator, thereby providing a comprehensive picture of the complex interactions among factors influencing cardiac health. Thirdly, this study conducted a stratified analysis and found that SII had a stronger correlation with cardiac biomarkers in the patients with AP or MI, thereby enhancing the relevance and applicability of our findings in clinical practice and public health interventions aimed at mitigating heart disease burden. Nevertheless, several limitations of this study remain. Firstly, the inherent cross-sectional design of our study precludes elucidation of the temporal dynamics or establishment of causality between SII and cardiac biomarkers. Secondly, the unavailability of detailed participant data, including New York Heart Association (NYHA) classification and echocardiographic parameters, impeded the stratification of the SII-cardiac biomarker relationship based on heart disease severity. Thirdly, the relatively limited number of cardiac cases may have affected the statistical power required to accurately estimate the association between SII and cardiac biomarkers. Fourth, although stronger point estimates emerged in high-risk subgroups, the absence of significant interaction terms suggests these observations require validation in larger cohorts to exclude random variability. To address these gaps in knowledge, future longitudinal studies with a larger sample size are imperative to provide a more comprehensive understanding of these associations.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study elucidated a positive correlation between SII and cardiac biomarkers. Specifically, the SII showed significant associations with NT-pro BNP levels, with particularly correlations observed among patients with AP or MI. However, further validation through larger prospective cohort studies is warranted to consolidate these observations and establish predictive utility for cardiac health.
