Development and Physical Characterization of Injectable Nanocrystals Derived from Euphorbia Fractiflexa Stem SAP and Antibacterial Efficacy
Authors: Sivakumar Sivagurunathan Moni, Santhosh Joseph Menachery, Ahmad Salawi, Remesh Moochikkal, Mohamed Eltaib Elmobark, Aamena Jabeen, Rawan Mohsen Ail Mashi, Lojain Abdulqder Adawi, Lama Abdullah Mohammed Alhazmi, Rafa Khalid Mohammed Humadi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:Despite its ornamental value, Euphorbia fractiflexa (E. fractiflexa) is still little explored for pharmaceutical applications. Plant-based nanoparticles, especially nanocrystals (NCs), are of great interest due to their ability to improve drug delivery and combat antibiotic resistance. This study presents a novel nanotechnological approach by using E. fractiflexa, a plant native to Abu Arish in the Jazan province of Saudi Arabia, for the first time in pharmaceutical research. The natural occurrence and sustainability of E. fractiflexa in desert climates make it a viable green resource for the development of NCs.Objective:This study investigates the pharmaceutical potential of plant-derived nanoparticles, specifically NCs produced from the stem SAP of E. fractiflexa, a desert plant from Abu Arish, Saudi Arabia.Methods:The milky SAP from the stem of Euphorbia fractiflexa was collected and processed to produce NCs. The NCs were analyzed by dynamic light scattering (DLS) for their zeta potential, particle size and polydispersity index (PDI). Morphological analysis was performed using scanning electron microscopy (SEM). The antibacterial activity of the NCs was evaluated against Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacterial strains using the agar well diffusion method, with ciprofloxacin as a standard reference. A statistical analysis was performed to compare the antibacterial activity of the NCs with the standard.Results:The NCs exhibited a zeta potential of − 7.62 ± 8.83 mV, indicating moderate stability, with particle sizes ranging from 100 to 200 nm, making them suitable for injectable applications. SEM revealed irregular, inhomogeneous crystal structures. The NCs showed significant antibacterial activity and inhibited both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria with inhibition zones of 18 - 21.3 µm varied with individual bacteria. Although E. fractiflexa derived NCs exhibit slightly lower antibacterial efficacy than ciprofloxacin, their sustainability and natural origin make them promise. Further optimization of formulation methods is needed to optimize the injectable formulation for future antibacterial applications.Conclusion:This study highlights E. fractiflexa as a valuable natural resource for the development of innovative nanotechnology formulations. The prepared NCs showed significant antibacterial activity and suitable physicochemical properties for injectable drug delivery systems. These NCs are gaining increasing attention as they can improve drug delivery and combat antibiotic resistance through plant-based, environmentally friendly formulations. However, a notable limitation is their slightly lower efficacy compared to ciprofloxacin, indicating that the formulation needs to be further optimized to increase antibacterial efficacy. Overall, this work lays the foundation for the future development of nanomedicines based on E. fractiflexa and highlights the value of underutilized desert plants for pharmaceutical nanotechnology.

INTRODUCTION

The emergence of bacterial resistance and multiple antibiotic resistance is a growing global health problem. This phenomenon, which results from treatment failures, contributes to increased morbidity and mortality.[12] The therapeutic world is currently facing major challenges in the treatment of infectious diseases, especially those caused by bacteria. This struggle is most pronounced in the case of hospital-acquired (nosocomial) infections. Researchers around the world are working on new antibiotics to fight infections, but these are no guarantee of preventing resistance. The current challenge is to deliver drugs effectively at the cellular level to overcome resistance. This is driving the development of advanced drug delivery systems, particularly using nanotechnology.[34] Nanoparticle drug delivery systems improve the treatment of human diseases.[5] The use of plant-derived nanoparticles is emerging as an innovative strategy to improve the delivery and efficacy of biomolecules.[6] These nanoparticles improve the penetration of cell membranes and trigger a wide range of biological activities. The design and development of plant-derived nanoparticles has become a cutting-edge field of research in nanotechnology, focusing on the development of advanced formulations for improved therapeutic outcomes.[4567]

Euphorbia fractiflexa S. Carter and J. R. I. Wood is a hardy, leafless stem succulent native to the Arabian Peninsula, growing mainly in the arid regions of Abu Arish, Jazan Province, Saudi Arabia. The plant belongs to the diverse family Euphorbiaceae, the species is highly adapted to extreme environmental conditions, indicating the presence of unique bioactive compounds with potential therapeutic applications.[8] This plant is characterized by its twining, thorny stems, and its ability to thrive in harsh, arid environments. Its fleshy, segmented stems often look twisted or bent, likely an adaptation to its native arid habitat, making it a particularly interesting species for studying plant survival strategies under extreme conditions. While it is widely known for its ornamental value, its pharmaceutical potential remains largely unexplored. This widespread availability in local ecosystems reduces dependence on cultivated or imported materials and provides a sustainable and cost-effective source for pharmaceutical research and nanomaterial development. The plant’s dominance in these environments also reflects its ecological adaptability, which may correlate with the presence of unique secondary metabolites that are valuable for drug discovery and therapeutic applications. As an abundant and sustainable resource, E. fractiflexa offers economic opportunities for pharmaceutical development, particularly in antibacterial research. The sustainability of E. fractiflexa as a pharmaceutical raw material lies in its natural resilience, its low maintenance requirements and its ability to grow on nutrient-poor, dry soils without the need for intensive agricultural intervention. As a result, plants such as E. fractiflexa are likely to contain unique bioactive compounds with significant therapeutic potential, making them valuable candidates for pharmaceutical research and the development of new drugs. These characteristics make it an environmentally friendly and cost-effective option for large-scale harvesting. Its perennial growth habit ensures continuous availability, eliminating the need for seasonal replanting or artificial cultivation systems. From an economic perspective, the local occurrence of E. fractiflexa in regions such as Jazan offers a strategic advantage as transport and procurement costs are minimized. This study aims to exploit the untapped potential of the plant through the development and evaluation of NCs developed from its stem SAP as injectable antibacterial formulations, making it a promising candidate for environmentally friendly therapeutic applications.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

A sample of the plant was obtained from the hilly region of Abu Arish, Jazan province, Saudi Arabia. Plant is Common and widely distributed in Jazan province. The plant sample was collected along with the team of taxonomists of Jazan University Herbarium in connection with the National coordinating project on diversity, vegetation and biomass analysis across Kingdom of Saudi Arabia, funded by Ministry of environment, agriculture and water. The plant species is not classified under any special conservation category, ensuring compliance with regulatory guidelines. The plant sample was transported to the laboratory in perforated polyethylene bags. The stem portions were rinsed several times with ordinary water, and then with Millipore water to eliminate contaminants, followed by air-drying. Identification of the sample was done by a taxonomist, and a sample of the plant (reference number: JAZUH 1637) was deposited in the Jazan University herbarium. The SAP was collected by cutting the stem longitudinally using a sterile blade. Then, the viscous SAP was collected in a clean sterile glass tube by keeping the stem in an inclined position.

Preparation of NCs

A novel and modified nanoprecipitation method has been developed to produce NCs, based on the previous publication.[4] The study utilized an extensive pre-formulation and optimization approach was used in the study to accurately determine the optimal concentration ratio of SAP to tripolyphosphate. The method was developed as a modified approach based on the results of previous studies to achieve the ideal SAP and tripolyphosphate concentrations and ensure optimal nanocrystal formation.[4] Based on pre-formulation studies, the concentration of E. fractiflexa stem SAP was optimized as 5% v/v by diluting in Millipore water. This concentration was found to provide an ideal balance between phytochemical content and viscosity for stable nanocrystal formation. The solution was continuously stirred with a magnetic stirrer on a hot plate to ensure uniform mixing. Sodium tripolyphosphate (TPP) at a concentration of 1% (w/v) was selected as the crosslinking agent as initial trials showed effective nanocrystal formation without aggregation or instability. The TPP solution was added at specific time intervals with constant stirring for up to one hour to facilitate the formation of a stable reaction mixture (RM). During stirring, sonication was performed at intervals of 2 min at 100% amplification using a CPX ultra sonicator (Cole Parmer Instruments Co, USA) to enhance the dispersion and formation of NCs. After completion of the mixing process, the resulting nanocrystal mixture was filtered using a Millex-GV syringe filter unit (0.2 μm, PVDF, Merck KGaA, Darmstadt, Germany).

Characterization

Dynamic Light Scattering (DLS) analysis

The physical characteristics of the NCs, including zeta potential, particle size (z-d.nm) and polydispersity index (PDI), were analyzed using a Zeta Sizer Nano ZS (Malvern Instruments, UK). Measurements were performed according to the standard operating procedures described in the Zeta Sizer Nano ZS user manual to ensure accurate and consistent results.[9]

Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) analysis

The morphological features NCs and the particle size in diameter were studied using high resolution SEM. The samples were analyzed using an Inspect F50 field-emission scanning electron microscope (Zeiss, Zaragoza, Spain). The microscope operated at an accelerating voltage of 30.0 kV. The particle size distribution was analyzed from the SEM images using Image J software.

Determination of antibacterial effect of NC

Bacterial experiments were conducted in accordance with the institutional biosafety and ethical guidelines for microbial research. The in vitro antibacterial screening was performed as established by Moni et al., 2018.[9] A known concentration of Muller Hinton agar media was prepared and plated under aseptic conditions. The agar well diffusion technique was used for the antibacterial susceptibility test of NCs whereas agar disc diffusion method was applied in determining the antibacterial susceptibility test of the standard ciprofloxacin disc (5 μg/disc).

Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis was done using the Prism 9, Graph Pad Instat software system, USA. Statistical analyses were performed using one-way ANOVA, followed by Tukey’s test (post hoc test). Values of P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all analyses.

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

Widespread bacterial resistance significantly impairs the therapeutic efficacy of conventional antibiotic treatments. In response to this growing challenge, there is a clear trend towards nanotechnology drug delivery systems that have the potential to improve the efficacy of antibiotics. Nanotechnology enables targeted drug delivery, improved bioavailability and mechanisms that can circumvent bacterial resistance.[10] The development of antibiotic resistance during standard therapy not only compromises current treatments but also poses a serious threat to the future treatment of infectious diseases. Innovative approaches are therefore needed to effectively combat resistant pathogens.[11] Nature has endowed man with a plethora of plants and shrubs with great potential medicinal values.[12] The Kingdom of Saudi Arabia is rich in rare herbs that have been utilized as folk medicine, but their scientific significance has yet to be discovered. Figure 1 demonstrates the collection of SAP from the stem of Euphorbia fractiflexa. The color of SAP was milky white, and it had a thick and uniform consistency, with a textural smooth-ness that was uniform and consistent. The highly viscous SAP was solidified after exposure to the open air. The pharmacokinetic profile of nanoparticles for biodistribution is determined by their surface charge and particle size.[13] The study revealed that the NCs had a zeta potential of -7.62 ± 8.83 mV. The PDI value of 0.105 with 68.4% polydispersity indicates that although the nanocrystal formulation had good uniform particle distribution, its stability was not particularly high [Figure 2]. In our previous study, we successfully demonstrated the extraction and formulation of NCs from the exudate gel (EG) of Caralluma retrospiciens. DLS analysis provided important insights into the physical properties of the NCs, with the previous formulation exhibiting a zeta potential of -5.58 ± 4.27 mV, which was moderately stable.[4] A previous study indicated that nanoparticles with a zeta potential of more than +30 mV or less than −30 mV are generally considered stable due to the strong electrostatic repulsion that prevents aggregation.[14]

The crystals were showing potent anti-bacterial activity against screened in this study. An important factor which is critical in bacterial cell targeting, that is, EPR effect, is controlled by the size of the nanoparticles and ZP.[15] The ZP and particle size of NCs indicate how readily they diffuse across bacterial membranes. Thus, the diffusion of NCs into bacteria can be accomplished by optimizing the size of nanoparticles and surface charges. Anionic particles have been demonstrated to be less toxic than cationic NPs which can easily pass through the bacterial membrane pores.[16] It has been reported that NPs of sizes of 50 to 200 nm readily permeate the bacterial membrane, making them very suitable for use in targeting.[17] Interestingly, the size of NCs prepared in the current research ranged from 100 to 200 nm, which indicates their suitability as injectable forms, as was reflected in antibacterial studies. The efficacy of NC was found to have a wide range of action against Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria. The antibacterial efficacy was observed against Bacillus subtilis (20.16 ± 1.6 mm), Staphylococcus aureus (20 ± 3 mm), Streptococcus pyogenes (18 ± 2 mm), Escherichia coli (21.3 ± 2.5 mm), Pseudomonas aeruginosa (18.6 ± 2 mm), and Klebsiella pneumoniae (19.6 ± 1.5 mm). The different inhibition between Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria can be attributed to the differences in the composition of their cell walls. Gram-negative bacteria, known for their intrinsic resistance mechanisms, showed remarkable susceptibility to NC, indicating its potential efficacy in destroying their cell structure. To gain a deeper understanding of the antibacterial potential of NC, further studies are needed to clarify its exact mechanism of action. Although NC showed significant activity, its efficacy was significantly lower than that of the standard drug ciprofloxacin (50 μg/mL) with P < 0.05. The further development of NC towards a clinical application as a viable product can be advanced; however, the study is still at a very early stage and requires further research and optimization. To ensure suitability for human use, the NC formulation needs to be further optimized to improve its antibacterial efficacy and stability. In addition, comprehensive toxicity studies are crucial to determine the safety profile and minimize potential adverse effects. Rigorous in vivo studies, pharmacokinetic evaluations and large-scale clinical trials are required to confirm the therapeutic potential and long-term effects. Only after these critical evaluations can NC be considered a viable alternative for clinical antimicrobial therapy, offering a safer and more effective approach to combating resistant bacterial infections.

CONCLUSION

The development of nanoparticles of plant origin represents a promising and innovative strategy to improve drug delivery systems for the treatment of bacterial infections. Injectable NCs offer a novel therapeutic approach that has the potential to improve treatment outcomes by efficiently targeting bacterial cells. NCs derived from E. fractiflexa exhibit unique physicochemical properties that enhance cell penetration and trigger various biological activities, contributing to their antibacterial efficacy. Optimizing their size and surface charge enables better diffusion through bacterial membranes, further enhancing their therapeutic potential. This study highlights the value of desert plants as natural resources for the development of advanced formulations that are effective against various bacteria. While these results demonstrate their potential, further research is needed to move from experimental results to practical applications. Comprehensive evaluations of their safety, efficacy, and large-scale production are crucial to establishing their viability in modern medicine. Future studies should focus on evaluating the in vivo efficacy and biocompatibility of NCs to ensure their suitability for clinical use. The absence of toxicity and long-term stability data limits conclusions about clinical applicability.

Furthermore, the scalability of the nanoprecipitation method needs to be evaluated to determine its feasibility for industrial production. Addressing these factors through in vivo safety and efficacy testing, stability studies, and process optimization will be essential for advancing these plant-based nanocrystals from laboratory research to real-world therapeutic applications. Additionally, investigating the scalability of the nanoprecipitation method is critical for large-scale production and commercial viability. While the nanocrystals demonstrated significant antibacterial activity, their effectiveness was somewhat lower compared to the standard antibiotic ciprofloxacin. This gap could be closed by further optimization of the formulation process, including increasing the concentration of active phytochemicals, improving the uniformity of the particles or integrating synergistic bioactive agents. Future studies will focus on addressing these limitations to advance the development of a safe, stable and effective plant-based nanotherapeutic agent.
