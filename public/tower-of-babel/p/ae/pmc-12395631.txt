Use of MRI-double inversion recovery sequence for early diagnosis of multiple sclerosis: case series
Authors: Dan Zhu, Yongjia Shao, Nana Zhang, Zhourui Wu, Liming Cheng, Hongyu Zhou, ShuGuang Chu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The choice of additional Double Inversion Recovery (DIR) Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) sequences may affect the judgment of the presence and nature of lesions. Herein, we reported three cases initially presenting with non-typical multiple sclerosis (MS) symptoms, in which the final diagnosis of MS was confirmed after incorporating spinal cord and brain DIR MRI sequence. The sequence revealed more conspicuous lesions compared to conventional T2-weighted imaging or T2-weighted fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (T2WI/FLAIR) sequences. Consequently, the patients received an earlier definitive diagnosis and timely treatment. Our cases demonstrated the additional DIR sequences for the patients with non-typical MS symptoms were reasonable and may be helpful.

Introduction

Multiple sclerosis (MS) is a prevalent yet pathologically complex inflammatory demyelinating disorder, characterized by multifocal lesions in the central nervous system (CNS), including the brain, spinal cord, and optic nerve, which may develop sequentially or simultaneously [1]. It is the second leading cause of disability in young adults, therefore, early diagnosis and treatment are crucial for MS patients, as they can significantly improve outcomes.

Due to the lack of specific biomarkers and definitive histopathological findings, the diagnosis of MS is often delayed. Therefore, imaging plays a crucial role in the diagnostic process [2]. The diagnostic criteria for MS include lesions in both the brain and spinal cord as evidence of spatial dissemination. However, in clinical practice, these regions are not always examined simultaneously during the initial assessment, particularly across different countries and healthcare settings [3]. Meanwhile, different Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) sequences detection vastly affects the doctor’s judgment on the existence and nature of lesions. On the other hand, non-MS physicians, such as orthopedic surgeons and neurosurgeons, are not familiar with the characteristics of the MS spinal cord lesions, probably resulting in the incorrect diagnoses in patients with initial spinal cord symptoms.

For patients presenting with atypical MS form, such as non-classical clinical symptoms or atypical imaging findings, identifying appropriate imaging or laboratory biomarkers to enhance the diagnostic accuracy of MS has been a persistent goal for researchers. Double Inversion Recovery (DIR) is an advanced MRI sequence first developed in 1994 that enhances white matter lesion contrast through a dual inversion recovery technique. This method employs two strategically timed inversion pulses to simultaneously suppress signals from both free water and white matter, thereby improving lesion detection sensitivity. Studies have shown that brain DIR sequences are better than conventional T2-weighted imaging or T2-weighted fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (T2/FLAIR) in detecting proximal cortical lesions [4]. However, according to current research, there are different data regarding the advantages or disadvantages of DIR in detecting demyelinating lesions of the spinal cord. Some recent studies have limited sample sizes [5]. Therefore, DIR has not been included in the clinical routine examination at present.

Case series

Case 1

(Figure.1): a 33-year-old male suddenly felt numbness at the base of thigh at both sides while playing with his child on January 20, 2021 and the numbness ascended to his waist the next day. Lumbar spine MRI scanned on February 9, 2021 (Fig. 1A) showed a herniated disc at lumbar 1–2 level, and intervertebral disc surgery was subsequently performed. Numbness was partially relieved after operation. Three months later, the patient experienced numbness in the palms of both hands. MRI of the cervical spine showed a lesion (Fig. 1B). The patient was again admitted to the orthopedic department. At consultation, because of the cervical intramedullary hypersignal lesion shown on conventional T2WI didn’t appear in the context of disc herniation, we added the cervical spine DIR, which showed short-segment uniform hyperintense lesions, suggesting inflammatory demyelinating lesions. Considering the ambiguous history of lumbar disc surgery, and the possibility of MS, a brain DIR was added for the patient (Fig. 1C). (Fig. 1D) demonstrated lesions in corresponding axial FLAIR sequence. The results showed multiple lesions in the brain. The patient was ultimately diagnosed with MS clinically and is currently on regular treatment with stable condition.

Case 2

(Figure. 2): A 16-year-old female complained of bilateral hand numbness on March 27, 2021. Subsequent MRI of the cervical spinal cord found a single short-segment lesion without enhancement (Fig. 2A), which was considered an inflammatory demyelinating lesion. The patient then underwent further cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) examination, which was CSFOB (+). Simultaneously, conventional brain MRI did not reveal any definitive lesions, and the patient was diagnosed clinically isolated syndrome. The numbness was partially relieved after oral steroid therapy. On December 13, 2021 (nine months later), the patient complained of discomfort in the hip and lower back. The thoracic and lumbar spinal cord MRI were examined and a short-segment lesion in thoracic spinal cord without enhancement was found (Fig. 2B). In suspicion of MS, brain DIR sequences were added and multiple small lesions were detected (Fig. 2C). The patient was ultimately diagnosed with MS according to McDonald’s diagnostic criteria. The patient is currently on regular treatment and under continuous follow-up, with intermittent disease flare-ups.

Case 3

(Figure. 3): A 33-year-old male presented to the hospital following a rear-end motor vehicle collision and underwent standard brain MRI as part of a medicolegal injury assessment. Multiple cerebral lesions were identified, prompting consultation with a neurologist. Additional brain DIR revealed a more conspicuous subcortical lesion with enhancement in this patient. The DIR scan also detected a brainstem lesion which was not shown on the T2/FLAIR scan (Fig. 3), highly suggestive of Radiologically Isolated Syndrome (RIS), which referred to incidental MRI findings highly suggestive of demyelination (typically fulfilling McDonald MRI criteria for MS), but lack corresponding clinical symptoms of MS. Although the patient had no related spinal symptoms, in order to make a more definitive diagnosis, cervical and thoracic spinal cord DIR were also added, and one short-segment lesion of cervical spinal cord was detected, which not only supported the diagnosis, but also added the information for the assessment of the condition. The patient was followed longitudinally and ultimately received a definitive MS diagnosis after 1 years, based on integrated clinical, radiological, and CSF findings. The patient is currently undergoing treatment and remains clinically stable.

Discussion

We reported three cases of MS with atypical initial presentations, where expedited diagnosis was achieved through supplemental brain and spinal cord DIR sequences. In comparison to conventional T2-weighted and FLAIR imaging, DIR sequences demonstrated superior lesion conspicuity and more definitive characterization of demyelinating plaques.

MRI is the preferred method for diagnosing MS, as it can display areas of abnormality in the brain and spinal cord, helping doctors observe the presence of demyelinating plaques, which are a typical characteristic of MS [6]. The 2017 McDonald Criteria for MS diagnosis require demonstration of both temporal dissemination (DIT) and spatial dissemination (DIS) of CNS lesions, which may be established through either clinical evidence or neuroimaging findings. The criteria identified four MS-specific sites, including periventricular, juxtacortical, infratentorial and spinal cord [7]. Therefore, when radiologists identify lesions in these specific locations, they might consider the possibility of MS according to the diagnostic standards. Different with the first three sites, the spinal cord MRI scans need additional time, expense, and even scan coil replacement. Therefore, in most instance, patients are not scanned for both the spinal cord and brain at the initial visit. When a patient complains of spinal cord symptoms to a non-neurologist, he may be misdiagnosed with other diseases such as spinal degeneration, and treated inappropriately.

The DIR sequence demonstrates superior tissue contrast by effectively suppressing signals from both cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) and white matter. This selective suppression enhances gray matter-white matter differentiation, significantly improving the detection of demyelinating lesions, particularly within cortical and deep gray matter structures [8]. Research indicated that the DIR sequence was highly sensitive to inflammatory demyelinating lesions, especially cortical lesions of MS [9]. However, DIR is an optional but not recommended sequence in MS patients in actual work. In the current diagnostic criteria for MS in 2024 [6], the DIR sequence is still not included in the recommended sequences. Our experience indicated that the DIR sequence was particularly useful in specific situations. Correspondingly, other clinical imaging sequences such as Susceptibility Weighted Imaging (SWI) can also assist in MS diagnosis. SWI can detect the specific central vein sign present in some MS patients, which helps differentiate MS from other white matter lesions and improves diagnostic specificity [10]. However, this sign is not present in all MS patients, particularly in spinal cord imaging, the detection positivity rate is even lower [11]. Therefore, the limitations of SWI in spinal cord imaging restrict its application across all MS cases.

In our three cases, the brain DIR sequences showed more conspicuous lesions than conventional MRI, especially for small lesions below 5 mm. In first of reported three MS cases, conventional T2 images of the spinal cord showed suspicious lesions with obscure signal in the cervical cord. While DIR identified short segments, focal areas, and ultrahigh signal intensity of the lesions, increasing the confidence of diagnosis. This proved that DIR has obvious advantages in detecting short-segment small lesions and increases the accuracy of the overall assessment of patient. In the second and third cases, DIR more clearly delineated the lesions. Additionally, the DIR examinations of the brain and spinal cord complemented each other, enhancing the confidence in the diagnosis of MS. More importantly, the DIR sequence could also detect some lesions that were difficult to show on conventional T2WI or FLAIR sequences. Specifically, the third patient was diagnosed with RIS. Although immediate initiation of disease-modifying therapy (DMT) is not currently recommended for RIS patients [12], early detection can prompt closer clinical follow-up and regular monitoring. The 2021 MAGNIMS-CMSC-NAIMS consensus has also not yet recommended the routine DIR sequences into standard brain and spinal cord MRI examinations, but only recommending the use of DIR in experienced centers [12]. Our experience has demonstrated that DIR images have outstanding resolution and might be used for diagnosis when MS diagnosis was suspected.

The case series presented in this study demonstrated that the DIR sequence could assist in the diagnosis of MS, enhanced diagnostic confidence, and potentially expedited the diagnostic process. However, its value in differential diagnosis remains limited. In clinical practice, MS requires careful differentiation from other autoimmune disorders, infectious diseases, and vascular conditions. However, it reminded that for non-MS specialists encountering short-segment lesions of the spinal cord, adding brain MRI, especially DIR sequences could help to confirm the diagnosis more quickly; a spinal cord DIR sequence could be also added when multiple lesions were observed in the head.

This study had several limitations. Firstly, the DIR sequence included a longer scanning time and artifacts in spinal cord MR imaging, therefore, in the future, we will be dedicated to optimize the DIR sequence to enhance its application in clinical practice. Secondly, the small sample size might limit the generalizability of the findings, and future studies with larger cohorts were needed to validate these findings. However, clinicians should be aware that for patients with MS who presented with atypical clinical and imaging findings, the DIR sequence may provide additional diagnostic information.

Conclusion

MRI is an important imaging choice to diagnose MS currently. Although the DIR sequence is not a routine sequence for the diagnosis of MS at present, our experience showed that: Firstly, DIR sequence could show the lesions more conspicuous than conventional T2WI or Flair sequences; secondly, when MS was clinically highly suspected, DIR sequences could be added to the brain or spinal cord mutually; finally, additional DIR sequences may assist in clinically establishing the diagnosis of MS, thereby giving patients earlier treatment.
