Steroid-Resistant Idiopathic Nephrotic Syndrome Reveals a Distinct Maladaptive Molecular State
Authors: Sonia Spinelli, Sofia Gaudiano, Andrea Garbarino, Francesca Lugani, Edoardo La Porta, Andrea Petretto, Martina Bartolucci, Chiara Lavarello, Nicole Grinovero, Ilaria Musante, Paolo Scudieri, Antonella Trivelli, Giorgio Piaggio, Alberto Magnasco, Maria Ludovica Degl’Innocenti, Gabriele Mortari, Simona Granata, Enrico Verrina, Gianluigi Zaza, Giovanni Candiano, Maurizio Bruschi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionIdiopathic nephrotic syndrome (iNS) is clinically classified by treatment response; however, the molecular basis of multidrug resistance remains poorly defined. We hypothesized that multidrug-resistant disease represents a distinct biological state rather than a more severe form of active disease.MethodsSerum proteomic profiling using liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) was performed in 165 pediatric and young adult patients with iNS, stratified as steroid-dependent nephrotic syndrome (SDNS), multidrug-dependent nephrotic syndrome (MDNS), or multidrug-resistant nephrotic syndrome (MRNS), and compared with healthy donors. Differential expression, pathway enrichment, and multivariate modeling were used to identify discriminative proteins. Selected biomarkers were validated using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) and independently replicated in a separate validation cohort. Functional effects of patient-derived sera were assessed in cultured human podocytes and peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs).ResultsOf 810 quantified proteins, 322 differed significantly across clinical groups. A 35-protein signature discriminated MRNS from MDNS and SDNS phenotypes. Integrated analyses identified dystroglycan 1 (DAG1), CD44 antigen (CD44), and RISC-loading complex subunit (TARBP2) as principal contributors to phenotype separation. ELISA confirmed increased DAG1 in MRNS, preserved TARBP2 in remission, and increased CD44 in active disease. Sera from patients with MRNS induced coordinated alterations in cytoskeletal organization, proteostasis, autophagy, and survival signaling in podocytes. An inverse distribution of endoplasmic reticulum (ER) chaperone BIP (GRP78) and phosphorylated AKT (pAKT) was observed in both serum-treated podocytes and patient PBMCs.ConclusionMRNS is associated with a distinct proteomic and functional profile characterized by coordinated stress-response dysregulation, supporting biologically informed disease stratification.Keywords: idiopathic nephrotic syndrome, mass spectrometry, multidrug resistance, podocyte injury, proteome

iNS encompasses a heterogeneous group of glomerular disorders characterized by heavy proteinuria and variable therapeutic response.1 Although most patients initially respond to corticosteroids, a substantial subset develops steroid dependence or multidrug resistance.2 These divergent clinical trajectories are associated with recurrent disease activity, cumulative treatment toxicity, and an increased risk of progressive kidney damage. Current classifications based on therapeutic response guide clinical management but do not fully capture the biological heterogeneity of iNS or the mechanisms that determine treatment resistance.

Podocyte injury represents a central event in iNS pathogenesis, integrating immune-derived signals with intrinsic stress responses that disrupt cytoskeletal organization, slit diaphragm integrity, and cellular homeostasis.3 Experimental and human studies suggest that specific molecular alterations may influence whether podocyte injury remains reversible or progresses toward maladaptive, treatment-refractory states. However, the molecular programs distinguishing active disease from stable remission, as well as steroid-sensitive from multidrug-resistant forms, remain incompletely defined.

High-resolution mass spectrometry enables quantitative profiling of large-scale proteomic datasets across clinically heterogeneous populations.4 When coupled with advanced bioinformatics and multivariate modeling approaches, serum proteomics allows identification of coordinated protein signatures and molecular networks associated with distinct clinical phenotypes. This systems-level strategy provides an opportunity to interrogate the biological states underlying therapeutic response and resistance in iNS.

In this study, we performed large-scale LC-MS/MS–based serum proteomic profiling in a well-characterized cohort of pediatric and young adult patients with iNS, encompassing SDNS, MDNS, and MRNS phenotypes, sampled during both active disease and complete remission. Integrative univariate and multivariate analyses were applied to identify a restricted proteomic signature associated with MRNS within this cohort, followed by Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment to define the biological pathways underlying this signature.

To assess biological plausibility and independent reproducibility, key proteins identified through proteomics were validated using ELISA and independently replicated in a separate cohort. Functional relevance was further explored by exposing human podocytes to patient-derived sera and evaluating cytoskeletal integrity, stress signaling, autophagy, inflammatory activation, and survival pathways. Selected signaling alterations were additionally examined in PBMCs from the independent cohort.

Together, this integrated strategy was designed to test the hypothesis that multidrug resistance in iNS reflects a biologically distinct state rather than a simple amplification of proteinuric activity. By linking systemic proteomic signatures to functional cellular responses and independently validating biomarkers, this study provides a framework for refined biological stratification and generates hypotheses for future biomarker-driven therapeutic strategies.

Proteome Profile

LC-MS/MS profiling identified 810 proteins, 770 (95.1%) of which were detected across all study groups. Forty proteins were unique to iNS, whereas none were exclusive to healthy donors. Functional annotation assigned the identified proteins to 9 major categories, mainly immune-related proteins (n = 186), proteases and their inhibitors (n = 108), complement and coagulation components (n = 78), extracellular matrix proteins (n = 77), and cytoskeletal components (n = 32), consistent with the inflammatory and structural context of iNS.

Unsupervised principal component analysis revealed no outliers, and hierarchical clustering showed no intrinsic sample stratification independent of clinical phenotype (Supplementary Figure S1).

One-way analysis of variance identified 320 proteins differing significantly among study groups after Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate correction (adjusted P ≤ 0.05) (Supplementary Table S1); expression profiles are shown in Supplementary Figure S2.

To define contrasts related to disease presence, activity, and therapeutic phenotype, predefined pairwise comparisons were performed. Independent groups were analyzed using unpaired t tests, whereas the comparison between active disease (SDNS-p/MDNS-p) and complete remission (SDNS-r/MDNS-r) was restricted to patients with paired longitudinal samples (n = 40) and assessed using paired t tests. In all analyses, P values were adjusted by Benjamini–Hochberg correction.

These analyses identified 160, 149, 157, and 191 proteins differing between iNS, SDNS, MDNS, or MRNS and healthy donors, respectively. Direct comparison of SDNS and MDNS identified only 2 proteins, dysferlin and family with sequence similarity 76 member B, irrespective of treatment category or disease activity.

Paired longitudinal analysis identified 87 proteins differing between active disease and remission after pooling SDNS and MDNS. In contrast, 91 proteins differed between MRNS and SDNS/MDNS overall, whereas 40 differed between MRNS and SDNS-p/MDNS-p only (Supplementary Figure S3 and Table S1).

No significant associations between global proteomic variation and sex, age strata, or estimated glomerular filtration rate were detected after false discovery rate correction, and exploratory stratified analyses showed no systematic effects of these variables. Therapy-specific comparisons were not performed because of limited sample sizes within treatment categories. To identify a minimal discriminative protein set, a Random Forest classifier was applied in stratified 70% training and 30% internal hold-out test sets, with paired longitudinal samples kept in the same partition to prevent information leakage.

All preprocessing steps were performed only on the training set. Hyperparameter optimization by repeated cross-validation (5-fold, 10 repeats) identified a 35-protein core signature with optimal discrimination across clinical groups (Table 2; Figure 1b and Supplementary Figure S4). Independently, sparse Partial Least Squares Discriminant Analysis using the same training or test partition supported partial separation of MRNS, active disease, remission, and healthy donors (Figure 1c). In the internal 30% hold-out test set, overall accuracy was 82.7% and macroaverage balanced accuracy was 81.8%. Misclassifications occurred mainly between steroid-dependent and multidrug-dependent phenotypes within the same disease state, with none between healthy donors and patients with iNS (Supplementary Figure S5).

These findings indicate that the identified signature captured biologically relevant differences among phenotypes within this cohort, although class separation remained incomplete. Integration of Random Forest mean decrease in Gini and Sparse Partial Least Squares Discriminant Analysis variable importance in projection scores identified DAG1, CD44, and TARBP2 as the main contributors to phenotype separation.

DAG1 was preferentially elevated in MRNS, and the detected DAG1 peptides mapped to amino acids 227–302 of the precursor, within α-dystroglycan rather than the β-subunit (Supplementarty Figure S6).

CD44 was elevated in active disease (SDNS-p, MDNS-p), whereas TARBP2 was elevated in remission (SDNS-r, MDNS-r). Bootstrap resampling (100 iterations) retained proteins selected in ≥ 70% of iterations. Independent approaches, including multinomial LASSO regression, yielded convergent panels and similar classification performance, supporting the robustness of the signature.

GO Enrichment Analysis

PathfindR GO analysis of the 320 proteins differentially expressed across clinical groups identified 15 biological processes grouped into 3 macrocategories (Figure 1d; Supplementary Table S2): inflammation and complement, cytoskeletal organization and extracellular matrix remodeling, and protein quality control and cellular homeostasis. The latter included proteasome function, ER-Golgi stress responses, glycoprotein processing, vesicle trafficking, autophagy, and PI3K/AKT/mTOR and MAPK signaling. Overall, these pathways indicate coordinated alterations in inflammatory, structural, and stress-response programs across iNS phenotypes.

Validation of Proteomics-Derived Biomarkers by ELISA

To validate the proteomic findings and assess clinical relevance, DAG1, CD44, and TARBP2 were quantified using ELISA. These proteins were selected for their contribution to phenotype discrimination in both Random Forest and Sparse Partial Least Squares Discriminant Analysis models. ELISA confirmed the proteomics-derived patterns in the discovery cohort and independently replicated them in a separate, non-overlapping validation cohort (Supplementary Table S3).

ELISA of DAG1

Serum DAG1 levels differed significantly across clinical groups (P < 0.0001). Levels were comparable among healthy donors, IgA nephropathy, and membranous nephropathy controls (Figure 2a). Within iNS, DAG1 levels tended to increase across clinical states, with intermediate levels in remission, higher levels in active disease, and the highest levels in MRNS. Patients with MRNS exhibited significantly higher DAG1 levels than all other groups in post hoc analyses. Accordingly, DAG1 accurately discriminated MRNS from other iNS phenotypes, with an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve of 0.96 (95% confidence interval: 0.92–0.99; P < 0.0001). No significant differences were detected between SDNS and MDNS within the same activity state. Although active phases showed numerically higher DAG1 levels than remission within SDNS and MDNS, these differences were not significant after correction for multiple testing.

ELISA of TARBP2

Serum TARBP2 levels differed significantly across clinical groups (P < 0.0001) (Figure 2b). Levels were comparable among healthy donors, IgA nephropathy, membranous nephropathy, and remission samples, but reduced in active iNS and lowest in MRNS. MRNS levels were significantly lower than those in the remission groups, but not lower than those in active disease groups. No significant differences were detected between SDNS and MDNS within the same activity state. Accordingly, TARBP2 discriminated remission from the other iNS phenotypes (area under the receiver operating characteristic curve: 0.94, 95% confidence interval: 0.91–0.97; P < 0.0001).

ELISA of CD44

Serum CD44 levels differed significantly across clinical groups (P < 0.0001). Levels were comparable between healthy donors and remission samples, whereas higher levels were observed in active iNS, IgA nephropathy, and membranous nephropathy (Figure 2c). Within iNS, serum CD44 was increased in SDNS-p, and MDNS-p compared with remission, whereas MRNS levels overlapped with active disease and were higher than SDNS-r, but not MDNS-r. No significant differences were detected between SDNS and MDNS within the same activity state. Consistent with this pattern, serum CD44 discriminated active disease from remission (area under the receiver operating characteristic curve: 0.95, 95% confidence interval: 0.91–0.98; P < 0.0001).

Urinary CD44 showed a similar distribution (Figure 2d), with levels comparable between healthy donors and remission samples, higher in active disease, and MRNS values overlapping with active disease and exceeding those in remission groups. Urinary CD44 discriminated active iNS from remission with comparable performance (area under the receiver operating characteristic curve: 0.95, 95% confidence interval: 0.91–0.98; P < 0.0001). Serum and urinary CD44 levels were positively correlated (Spearman R = 0.65, P < 0.05), and urinary CD44 levels correlated with proteinuria in active iNS (Spearman R = 0.75, P < 0.001; Supplementary Figure S7).

In Vitro Modeling of Proteomics-Derived Pathways in Human Podocytes

To evaluate whether proteomic signatures were associated with measurable cellular alterations, immortalized human podocytes were exposed to patient-derived sera stratified by therapeutic response and disease activity. Based on GO–enriched pathways, markers of cytoskeletal organization, survival signaling, podocyte identity, cellular stress, cell-cycle checkpoint regulation, autophagy, and inflammasome-related signaling were assessed across clinical phenotypes.

Cytoskeletal Organization

Exposure to MRNS sera was associated with marked cytoskeletal remodeling in human podocytes. Calponin-2 expression was increased compared with healthy donors, with intermediate levels in SDNS-p and MDNS-p and healthy donor–like levels in remission (Figure 3a–g). Phalloidin staining showed preserved actin organization in healthy donors and remission conditions, partial disruption in SDNS-p and MDNS-p, and greater loss of filamentous actin after MRNS exposure. This was supported by phalloidin intensity and entropy analysis, with the lowest entropy values in MRNS-treated podocytes, intermediate values in SDNS-p and MDNS-p, and values in remission comparable to healthy donors, consistent with a graded loss of stress-fiber organization (Figure 3h–n).

Survival Signaling

pAKT at ser473, a mediator of prosurvival signaling, was preserved in podocytes exposed to healthy donor and remission sera but reduced following MRNS treatment, with intermediate levels in active SDNS and MDNS; the pAKT/AKT ratio paralleled this pattern (Figure 3o–u), as did total AKT (Supplementary Figure S8).

Podocyte Identity

Expression of slit diaphragm and podocyte identity markers (nephrin, synaptopodin, CD2-associated protein) was maintained in remission, partially reduced in active disease, and further reduced in MRNS-treated cells (Supplementary Figure S9).

Cellular Stress and Cell-Cycle Checkpoint Regulation

GRP78 fluorescence was increased in podocytes exposed to MRNS sera, with intermediate levels observed in SDNS-p and MDNS-p and values comparable to healthy donors in remission samples (Figure 4a–h).

Autophagy-Related Markers

Autophagy markers, including sequestosome-1 (p62), autophagy protein 5 (ATG5), and microtubule-associated protein 1 light chain 3 beta (LC3B), showed higher signal intensity in healthy donors and remission samples, partial reductions in SDNS-p and MDNS-p, and lower levels in MRNS-treated podocytes (Figure S10).

Inflammasome-Related Signaling

Inflammasome-related markers exhibited a graded pattern. NACHT-LRR and PYD domain-containing protein 3 (NLRP3), caspase-1, and interleukin-1β signals were not uniformly regulated across conditions. NLRP3 and caspase-1signals were reduced in MRNS-treated podocytes, showed intermediate values in active SDNS and MDNS conditions, and were largely preserved in remission and healthy donor sera. Interleukin-1β showed the opposite pattern, with the highest signal in MRNS-treated podocytes, intermediate levels in SDNS-p and MDNS-p, and low signal in healthy donors and in remission conditions. NLRP3 immunostaining showed greater overlap with DAPI in MRNS-treated podocytes and a more diffuse pattern in healthy and remission conditions, consistent with a higher NLRP3 nuclear-to-cytoplasmic fluorescence ratio in MRNS-treated cells. (Supplementary Figure S11).

Quantitative nuclear morphometry revealed phenotype-associated differences. MRNS-treated podocytes showed fewer nuclei per field than healthy donors, whereas remission samples were comparable with healthy donors, and SDNS-p and MDNS-p showed intermediate reductions (Figure S12a). Nuclear area was increased in MRNS-treated podocytes, with intermediate enlargement in active SDNS-p and MDNS-p and healthy donor–like values in remission (Supplementary Figure S12b). Together, these findings indicate that reduced cell count number in MRNS-treated podocytes is part of a broader morphological phenotype.

Collectively, remission sera were associated with cellular profiles similar to healthy donors, whereas MRNS sera were associated with coordinated alterations in cytoskeletal organization, proteostasis, autophagy, inflammatory signaling, and survival pathways. Active SDNS and MDNS showed intermediate phenotypes.

In Vivo Assessment of Proteostasis and Signaling Pathways in PBMCs

To assess whether the stress-response patterns observed in vitro were detectable in patient-derived cells, GRP78 and the pAKT/AKT ratio were evaluated by western blot in PBMCs stratified by therapeutic response and disease activity. GRP78 was highest in MRNS, intermediate in active SDNS and MDNS, and similar to healthy donors in remission (Figure 5a and b). In contrast, the pAKT/AKT ratio showed the opposite pattern, with the lowest levels in MRNS, intermediate levels in active disease, and higher levels in remission (Figure 5c and d). These findings indicate that the GRP78/AKT axis in PBMCs parallels the patterns observed in serum-treated podocytes.

Discussion

The pathogenesis of iNS has traditionally been attributed to circulating permeability factors that trigger podocyte dysfunction and proteinuria.1 However, the absence of a universally validated causative molecule suggests that multidrug resistance may reflect not only circulating factors but also cell-intrinsic mechanisms. Our findings support a model in which resistance reflects impaired restoration of podocyte homeostasis after injury. By integrating large-scale serum proteomics with biomarker validation and functional modeling, we identified a restricted molecular signature that distinguished MRNS from SDNS and MDNS, independent of disease activity. Multivariate analyses supported partial separation of MRNS from active and remission states.

Exploratory analyses did not reveal significant associations between global proteomic variation and sex, age strata, or estimated glomerular filtration rate, suggesting that these distinctions are unlikely to be driven by demographic or clinical variables. Several apolipoprotein-related proteins were increased during active disease, consistent with the dyslipidemic milieu of proteinuric states. However, they did not emerge as discriminative features across MRNS, SDNS, and MDNS, suggesting that lipid-related alterations may contribute to the disease milieu without driving the proteomic separation observed in this cohort.

GO enrichment converged on coordinated pathways involving cytoskeletal organization, proteostasis, autophagy, inflammatory signaling, survival regulation, glycoprotein processing, and ER-Golgi–associated trafficking, providing a coherent framework for functional investigation.

Among the discriminative proteins, DAG1 emerged as a principal contributor to the MRNS phenotype in multivariate models. DAG1 encodes dystroglycan, a structural component of the dystrophin–glycoprotein complex anchoring podocyte foot processes to the glomerular basement membrane. Experimental and human studies have linked dystroglycan disruption to foot process effacement and detachment.12,13 In inflammatory and proteolytic environments, dystroglycan can undergo cleavage and posttranslational modifications that destabilize podocyte-matrix adhesion and promote shedding of extracellular fragments.14 Recent laser microdissection–based proteomics in human biopsies demonstrated reduced podocyte DAG1 expression across minimal change disease and multiple focal segmental glomerulosclerosis forms, but not membranous nephropathy, despite similar proteinuria.15 These observations suggest that DAG1 loss represents a feature of podocytopathy rather than a nonspecific consequence of proteinuria. Earlier biopsy-based studies of dystroglycan in human glomerular disease have yielded partly heterogeneous results depending on the cohort, the α- versus β-dystroglycan epitope examined, and the analytical approach.16,17 This heterogeneity argues against a simple binary loss model and is more consistent with context-dependent alterations in dystroglycan expression and processing. In addition, the circulating DAG1 signal detected by mass spectrometry was supported by peptides mapping to amino acids 227–302 of the precursor, within α-dystroglycan (amino acids 30–653) rather than the β-subunit (amino acids 654–895), supporting the interpretation that the circulating species predominantly reflects the extracellular portion of dystroglycan.

In contrast, TARBP2 displayed the opposite distribution, being reduced in MRNS and preserved in remission. As a core component of the RNA-induced silencing complex, TARBP2 regulates microRNA-mediated stress adaptation and posttranscriptional gene control.18,19 Although its role in podocytes has not been directly characterized, preservation of TARBP2 in remission is consistent with the possibility that intact RNA-regulatory capacity contributes to adaptive resilience and reversibility of injury.

CD44 behaved as a marker of disease activity rather than multidrug resistance. Its elevation during proteinuric phases and normalization in remission, together with concordant serum–urine correlations, aligns with its established role in epithelial activation, inflammatory signaling, and glomerular injury.20, 21, 22, 23 CD44 thus appears as a dynamic biomarker of ongoing injury, distinct from DAG1, which characterized the treatment-refractory state in this cohort. Although CD44 downstream signaling was not interrogated here, experimental evidence links CD44 induction in parietal epithelial cells to ERK1/2 activation by albumin, CSF-1/CSF-1R, C5a–C5aR1, MIF/SDF1–CXCR4, and podocyte-derived Wnt4/β-catenin,24, 25, 26, 27, 28 with genetic ablation attenuating experimental FSGS.29 Its mechanistic role remains to be defined.

More broadly, emerging molecular markers in pediatric iNS include circulating autoantibodies directed against slit diaphragm proteins, particularly nephrin. Recent studies and our published observations in an independent iNS cohort indicate that antinephrin positivity defines a limited subset of patients associated with active disease and remission-dependent fluctuations, rather than a uniform feature across therapeutic phenotypes.30, 31, 32 Although such markers were not assessed in the present study, they may further refine biological classification in future longitudinal studies.

Functional modeling in human podocytes recapitulated these molecular distinctions. MRNS sera were associated with coordinated alterations in structural integrity, proteostasis, autophagy, and survival signaling, whereas remission sera were largely comparable to healthy donors. Active SDNS and MDNS samples exhibited intermediate phenotypes, supporting a graded biological continuum that parallels clinical activity but does not fully overlap with the MRNS profile. These observations suggest that factors in MRNS sera perturb multiple homeostatic pathways under experimental conditions. A central axis emerging from both proteomic and functional analyses was the inverse regulation of GRP78 and pAKT. GRP78, a master regulator of the unfolded protein response, was elevated in MRNS-treated podocytes and in PBMCs from MRNS patients, consistent with sustained ER stress.33, 34, 35 Conversely, pAKT, which supports podocyte survival and slit diaphragm integrity, was reduced in MRNS and preserved in remission.

AKT phosphorylation at Ser473 represents a biologically relevant readout in podocytes, as PI3K/AKT signaling is recruited downstream of nephrin/CD2AP-dependent slit diaphragm signaling and contributes to podocyte survival and cytoskeletal homeostasis.36,37 GRP78 is not only a canonical marker of ER stress but has also been shown to directly modulate AKT signaling under sustained stress conditions through formation of a GRP78-AKT complex associated with reduced Ser473 phosphorylation.38

This opposing GRP78/AKT configuration is therefore compatible with a shift toward a stress-survival imbalance that may reflect impaired adaptive signaling.

Nuclear morphometry further aligned with this interpretation. MRNS sera were associated with reduced cell counts and increased nuclear size in podocytes, whereas remission conditions resembled healthy donors. Nuclear enlargement has been linked to stress-induced arrest and senescence-like programs,39,40 and reduced cell count recalls podocyte depletion observed in progressive glomerular disease.41,42 Although causality cannot be inferred, these structural alterations are consistent with compromised cellular resilience and may reflect impaired podocyte attachment and/or partial cell loss under stress conditions.

Replication of the GRP78/AKT pattern in PBMCs suggests that the stress-survival imbalance associated with MRNS may extend beyond podocytes and reflect a broader systemic signature. Several limitations should be acknowledged. Although the overall cohort size was substantial for a discovery-phase serum proteomics study, patient distribution across therapeutic phenotypes was unbalanced, with a relatively small number of MRNS cases, reflecting real-world prevalence. Subgroup analyses by immunosuppressive regimens were not statistically powered, and residual treatment-related confounding cannot be entirely excluded. In addition, the machine-learning analyses were designed as exploratory tools to assess discrimination among predefined therapeutic phenotypes rather than to develop an outcome-predictive classifier. Classification performance remained imperfect in the internal hold-out test set, with most misclassifications occurring between steroid-dependent and multidrug-dependent phenotypes within the same disease state. Accordingly, these models should be viewed as tools for biological stratification within this cohort rather than as robust or externally validated classifiers, and confirmation in independent external cohorts will be essential.

The cross-sectional design precludes inference regarding temporal dynamics or causality between proteomic alterations and the development of multidrug resistance. Prospective longitudinal studies across treatment phases will be required to determine whether the biomarkers identified here, particularly activity-associated markers such as CD44, predict subsequent changes in proteinuria, kidney function decline, or other clinically relevant outcomes, and whether these signatures precede, accompany, or follow resistance.

Finally, although in vitro experiments in podocytes and PBMCs support biological plausibility, such models cannot fully recapitulate the complexity of the in vivo glomerular microenvironment. External validation in multicenter cohorts will be necessary to confirm robustness and generalizability.

Overall, these findings should be interpreted within a hypothesis-generating framework. The convergence of differential proteomics, multivariate modeling, independent biomarker replication, and functional assays identifies a coherent molecular program associated with MRNS within this cohort. Although causality cannot be established, the data are consistent with the possibility that multidrug resistance reflects a biologically distinct and potentially maladaptive state characterized by convergent disturbances in proteostasis and survival signaling, rather than a simple amplification of proteinuric activity.

The generalizability of these findings should be considered in light of the study population, which consisted primarily of pediatric and young adult patients managed at tertiary referral centers. Extrapolation to broader adult or community-based populations requires caution. Future multicenter studies including diverse age groups and ethnic backgrounds will be necessary to establish external validity.
