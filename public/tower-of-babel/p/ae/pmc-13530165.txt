Base-catalyzed regioselective C(sp3)–Si bond activation of silacyclobutanes and benzosilacyclobutenes
Authors: Yuanfang Kong, Delong Mu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The development of a green, sustainable, and environment-friendly activation model is of great significance in catalytic chemistry. Herein, a transition-metal- and ligand-free, base-catalyzed regioselective C(sp3)–Si bond activation of silacyclobutanes (SCBs) and benzosilacyclobutenes (BSBs) has been developed. The protocol features a simple catalytic system that proceeds smoothly under air atmosphere without requiring inert gas protection. A variety of SCBs and BSBs are competent substrates, along with a range of nucleophiles as coupling partners. Furthermore, the strategy provides a straightforward platform for the late-stage functionalization of various bioactive molecules, enabling access to diverse quaternary silicon-containing scaffolds. Preliminary mechanistic investigations indicate that the C–Si bond cleavage is involved in the rate-determining step, and the hydrogen source originates from the nucleophile. In addition, the proposed mechanism is consistent with our experimental results and further supported by DFT calculations.

Introduction

The development of green and sustainable processes has become a cornerstone of modern chemistry. Over the past decades, significant efforts have been devoted to catalytic chemistry in the pursuit of generality, high atom economy, and sustainable processes1–3. Transition metals usually exhibit high catalytic activity and play a dominant role in contemporary synthetic chemistry, especially in the construction of C–C and C–heteroatom bonds from readily available starting materials4–7. Although the strategy has achieved tremendous progress, the overall abundance of transition metals in the Earth’s crust remains low, and their enrichment process has a significant impact on the environment. Therefore, exploring an alternative approach and developing a green, sustainable, and environment-friendly activation model will be of great significance.

Since the pioneering contribution of Kipping reported the first example of silacyclobutane (SCB) in the 1920s, this class of compounds has attracted considerable attention in synthetic chemistry8–10. As a family of four-membered ring compounds, silacyclobutanes exhibit remarkable reactivity and have proved to be versatile synthons to access a diversity of organosilicon compounds11–15. Taking advantage of their unique properties, the inherent C–Si bonds can be activated due to their ring strain and Lewis acidity16,17. The functionalization of the scaffold by transition metals insertion into C–Si bonds is a powerful protocol for generating highly active [C-M-Si] species, which undergo further transformations to afford ring-opening18–24 or ring-expansion products25–43 (Fig. 1a). It is worth mentioning that selective cleavage of C(sp2)–Si bonds over C(sp3)–Si bonds is widely observed44–46. Recently, a great breakthrough has been made in activation of the inert C–Si bonds by modulation of the reaction parameters (e.g., electronic and steric characteristics of the ligand or additive), and the regioselectivity can be precisely regulated47–50. Despite the robustness, versatility, and high efficiency that the activation model has achieved, the inherent complexity of these systems has severely restricted C–Si bond activation from becoming a general tool for realizing diverse transformations. So, developing a simple catalytic system and exploring a novel activation model are in high demand.Fig. 1Protocols for activation of SCBs and BSBs.a Transition-metal-catalyzed protocol for activation of SCBs and BSBs. b Base-catalyzed C(sp3)–Si bond activation of SCBs and BSBs.

As is well known, inorganic and organic bases are crucial bulk chemicals with widespread applications in synthetic chemistry. Although numerous breakthroughs have been achieved in base-mediated processes51–53, employing bases as catalysts to promote C–Si bond activation remains a challenging concept. Compared with transition metals, bases are inexpensive, readily available, and structurally diverse. We therefore envision that bases could potentially replace transition metals while retaining high reactivity. In order to establish a general base-catalyzed protocol, the following issues should be seriously taken into consideration: first, the cleavage of the C(sp3)–Si bond by using base as the catalyst remains far less explored, raising the question of how to complete the catalytic cycle without external additives; second, while regioselectivity, chemoselectivity, and stereoselectivity can be well controlled in transition-metal-mediated systems, base exhibits no inherent bias towards any specific site, making the control of regioselectivity highly challenging; finally, a viable activation model tailored for functionalization of the inert C(sp3)–Si bonds should be designed. With our continuing interest in silicon chemistry54–57, herein, a base-catalyzed strategy for activation of silacyclobutanes (SCBs) and benzosilacyclobutenes (BSBs) has been developed, and the protocol provides a green and sustainable activation model that circumvents the use of transition metals and ligands (Fig. 1b). The system is simple and exhibits a remarkable catalytic activity, providing a convenient strategy to access a variety of organosilicon compounds. In addition, the protocol provides a versatile and practical platform for the late-stage modification of various bioactive molecules. Preliminary mechanistic investigations are performed and a concerted pathway is proposed to demonstrate the transformations.

Results

Optimization of reaction conditions

The investigation was commenced with using S1 as starting material and S1′ as coupling partner to explore the reaction parameters at 60 oC under air atmosphere. In the absence of a base, no desired product was observed (Table 1, entry 1). Next, several widely used organic bases were evaluated; however, using Et3N, DMAP, or DABCO as the catalyst resulted in complete recovery of the starting materials (Table 1, entries 2–4). In contrast, DBU could significantly promote the transformation, delivering compound 1 in nearly quantitative yield (Table 1, entry 5). The exciting results inspired us to further evaluate the performance of inorganic bases. To our delight, inorganic bases also proved to be competent catalysts for driving the C(sp3)–Si activation, and K2CO3 stood out to be another efficient catalyst, affording compound 1 in 99% 1H NMR yield and 96% isolated yield (Table 1, entries 6–11). Screening of solvents revealed a pronounced solvent effect, with acetonitrile being uniquely effective (Table 1, entries 12–16). Remarkably, when the loading of the catalyst was lowered to 5 mol%, 2.5 mol%, or even 1 mol%, the reaction also proceeded smoothly to afford the desired product in nearly quantitative yields, demonstrating that the system exhibited a powerful catalytic activity (see Supplementary Information for more details). Scope of SCBs

With the standard conditions in hand, the generality and substrate scope of SCBs for K2CO3-catalyzed C(sp3)–Si bond activation were evaluated (Fig. 2). A variety of asymmetric, symmetric, and bis-silicon-derived SCBs proved to be competent substrates, affording the corresponding silyl ethers in excellent yields. In general, the steric hindrance of substituents had no obvious effect on the efficiency, whereas the electronic properties exerted a slight influence on the ring-opening process. The experimental results revealed that aryl groups bearing electron-neutral, electron-donating, and electron-withdrawing functional groups at different positions reacted smoothly, providing the corresponding products in excellent yields (2–17). Besides, alkyl, allyl, benzyl, and cyclic vinyl substituents were well tolerated (18–21). Heterocyclic scaffolds bearing thiophene, benzofuran, benzothiophene, indole, and pyridine moieties also underwent C(sp3)–Si bond activation efficiently, delivering the corresponding products in excellent yields (22–26). Linear, branched, and cyclic alkyl, as well as allyl, vinyl, and aryl-derived motifs underwent the ring-opening transformations smoothly (27–35), with cyclopropyl, cyclopentyl, and cyclohexyl groups remaining intact (33–35). Furthermore, the scaffolds of SCBs with symmetric structures also exhibited high compatibility (36–40). Scope of nucleophiles

To demonstrate the versatility of this protocol, a wide range of coupling partners was investigated (Fig. 3). Under the standard conditions, silanols and disilanols were excellent coupling partners that underwent facile C(sp³)–Si bond activation, furnishing siloxanes in good to excellent yields (43–52). Simple alcohols such as methanol, ethanol, and butanol were successfully converted into silyl ethers in excellent yields (53–55). Notably, fatty alcohols with poor solubility also exhibited high compatibility (56–60). Besides, various primary alcohol derivatives were also competent coupling partners (61–64). The cyclic substituents were stable under the reaction conditions, and no ring-opening side reactions were detected (65–67). Benzyl alcohol and methanol derivatives bearing electron-donating, electron-withdrawing, or heterocyclic substituents made no obvious influence on the activation process, delivering the corresponding products in good to excellent yields (68–79). Allyl and propargyl alcohol derivatives exhibited comparable tolerance (80–81). Complex natural products and drug derivatives also participated smoothly in the catalytic cycle (82–89). In addition, steric hindrance made no obvious impact on the C(sp3)–Si bond cleavage (90–91). This strategy also provided an alternative platform for protecting various phenol derivatives (92–96). When water was employed as the nucleophile, a subsequent Si–O bond formation occurred, affording disiloxane in excellent yield (97). Terminal alkynes were also found to be viable coupling partners, providing the corresponding products in moderate yields (98–107). As is well known, N–Si bond is significantly weaker and highly sensitive to ambient conditions, making its construction a persistent challenge58. To our delight, our system provided a straightforward protocol for N–Si bond formation using NaOMe as the catalyst (108, see Supplementary Information for more details). Scope of BSBs and nucleophiles

Having established the remarkable catalytic activity of the base in SCB-mediated ring-opening reactions, we therefore turned our attention to further evaluating the generality of the protocol towards the challenging scaffold of benzosilacyclobutenes (BSBs). As the inherent dissociation energies of the C(sp2)–Si and C(sp3)–Si bonds are similar, achieving the regioselective functionalization of the scaffold remains a formidable challenge. To our delight, the developed system could well regulate the regioselectivity, and the C(sp3)–Si bond cleavage was observed with a variety of nucleophiles as coupling partners (Fig. 4). Under the standard conditions, silanol and phenol, even the simple methanol were suitable coupling partners, affording the corresponding silyl ethers in excellent yields (110–112). Generally, benzyl alcohols bearing either electron-donating (Me, Et, OMe, SMe, Vinyl, OAc) or electron-withdrawing (COOMe, BPin, F, CF3) groups at various positions afforded the desired products in comparable yields, whereas CN and NO2 substituents afforded only moderate yields (113–124). Various methanol and ethanol derivatives containing benzofuran, benzothiophene, carbazole, alkynyl, oxyalkyl, and cyclopropyl groups all served as effective coupling partners, and the ring-opening reactions proceeded efficiently (125–131). Furthermore, steric hindrance exhibited a negligible effect on the Si–O bond formation, allowing secondary and even tertiary alcohols to be successfully converted into silyl ethers (132–137). It is worth noting that the strategy provided an alternative protocol for protecting bioactive molecules such as L-menthol and cholesterol (138–139). Mechanistic investigations

To get insight into the reaction mechanism, several control experiments were conducted (Fig. 5). First, a deuterium-labeling experiment was performed using S1′ as coupling partner, and d-1 was isolated in 86% yield with >99% deuterium incorporation, demonstrating that the hydrogen source in the system originated from the nucleophile (Fig. 5a). Next, a designed KIE experiment was performed via parallel C(sp3)–Si bond cleavage of S1 with S1′ and d-S1′, respectively. A large kinetic isotope effect value (KIE = KH/KD = 2.0) was observed, suggesting that in the system C–Si bond cleavage was involved in the rate-determining step (Fig. 5b). Besides, the rate law of the system was further evaluated with different initial concentrations of S1, S1′, and K2CO3. Analysis of the corresponding reaction profiles revealed a zero-order dependence on both S1 and S1′, but a first-order dependence on K₂CO₃59 (Fig. 5c). In addition, the formation of a hydrogen bond between base and nucleophile was also unambiguously observed by stoichiometric 1H NMR analysis of a mixture of DBU and S1′ at 60 oC for 30 min (Fig. 5d).Fig. 5Mechanistic investigations.a Deuterium labeling experiment. b KIE effect. c Kinetic experiments. d Formation of a hydrogen bond between base and nucleophile.

Proposed mechanism and DFT calculations

Based on the above preliminary experimental results and literature report60, a proposed pathway was assumed to demonstrate the transformations (Fig. 6). Initially, the formation of a hydrogen bond between base and S1′ generated the stabilized complex I. Next, a concerted route coordinated with S1 to form complex II. Subsequently, a synergistic process released one molecule of Base–H to afford complex III, which then underwent ring opening to yield complex IV. Finally, rapid protonation of complex IV furnished product 1 and concurrently regenerated the catalyst, thereby completing the catalytic cycle.Fig. 6Proposed mechanism.Base-catalyzed pathway for the synthesis of compound 1.

Next, the proposed catalytic cycle was further investigated by density functional theory between S1 and S1′ using DBU as catalyst (Fig. 7). The calculations were performed using Gaussian 16 at the M06-2X-D3/6-311 + G(d,p)/SMD (acetonitrile)//M06-2X/6-311 + G(d,p)/SMD (acetonitrile) level of theory (see Supplementary Information for more details). The coordination of DBU with S1′ to form Int-1 was found to be thermodynamically favorable with a stabilization energy of 1.4 kcal/mol. Next, a concerted route of forming Int-2 passed through transition state Ts-1 with an energy barrier of 12.3 kcal/mol. The formation of Int-3 was endergonic with 0.5 kcal/mol of energy barrier. The ring opening of Int-3 to form Int-4 passed through transition state Ts-2 with an energy barrier of 13.4 kcal/mol. The result also indicated that C–Si bond cleavage was the rate-determining step, which was also consistent with our experimental result (Fig. 5b). Besides, all species preceding Ts-2 were found to be in rapid equilibrium, a conclusion that aligned with both the computed energetics and our mechanistic investigations. Finally, the protonation of Int-4 proceeded quickly, releasing 56.1 kcal/mol of energy to drive the reaction forward.Fig. 7DFT calculations.Computations were performed at the M06-2X-D3/6-311 + G(d,p)/SMD (acetonitrile)//M06-2X/6-311 + G(d,p)/SMD (acetonitrile) level of theory.

Applications

To elucidate the synthetic utility of the protocol, a gram-scale synthesis was performed using 8 mmol of S1, providing 3.52 g of compound 1 in 98% yield without any loss of efficiency (Fig. 8a). Incorporating silicon atom into organic frameworks is an emerging trend in medicinal chemistry and materials science. Carbon–silicon switching effect makes a great significant on functional molecules for improving bioactivity and modifying material property. Under the standard conditions, the protocol provided a straightforward platform for the late-stage functionalization of various bioactive molecules, including Estrone, Desloratadine, (+)-Nootkatone, Testosterone, Cholesterol, and Ezetimibe, delivering a series of silicon-containing frameworks in moderate to excellent yields (Fig. 8b).Fig. 8Applications.a Gram-scale synthesis. b Derivatization of bioactive molecules.

Discussion

In summary, we have developed a base-catalyzed strategy for highly regioselective functionalization of silacyclobutanes and benzosilacyclobutenes. The protocol establishes a green and sustainable activation model that circumvents the use of transition metals and ligands. The system is simple and exhibits a powerful catalytic activity, which provides a convenient route to access a variety of organosilicon compounds with a series of nucleophiles as coupling partners. Gram-scale synthesis is performed smoothly without erosion in yield, and its utility is further highlighted by the late-stage derivatization of complex bioactive frameworks. Mechanistic investigations and DFT calculations collectively support a concerted catalytic pathway. Further applications of this environment-friendly activation model are underway in our laboratory.

Methods

General procedure for base-catalyzed C(sp3)–Si bond activation of silacyclobutanes and benzosilacyclobutenes

To a 10 mL microwave tube were in order added silacyclobutanes or benzosilacyclobutenes (0.1 mmol, 1.0 eq), NuH (0.15 mmol, 1.5 eq), K2CO3 (1.4 mg, 10 mol%), and MeCN (1 mL) under air atmosphere. The tube was capped and stirred at 60 oC for 12 h. The reaction mixture was filtered and concentrated under reduced pressure. The residue was purified by flash chromatography on silica gel to afford the desired products.
