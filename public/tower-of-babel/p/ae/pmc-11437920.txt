The importance of high total body water/fat free mass ratio and serial changes in body composition for predicting hospital mortality in patients with severe pneumonia: a prospective cohort study
Authors: Chia-Cheng Tseng, Kai-Yin Hung, Huang-Chih Chang, Kuo-Tung Huang, Chin-Chou Wang, Yu-Mu Chen, Chiung-Yu Lin, Meng-Chih Lin, Wen-Feng Fang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
PurposeThis study aimed to investigate the impact of body composition variables on hospital mortality compared to other predictive factors among patients with severe pneumonia. Additionally, we aimed to monitor the dynamic changes in body composition variables over the course on days 1, 3, and 8 after intensive care unit (ICU) admission for each patient.MethodsWe conducted a prospective study, enrolling patients with severe pneumonia admitted to the medical intensive care unit at Kaohsiung Chang Gung Memorial Hospital from February 2020 to April 2022. We collected clinical data from all patients and assessed their body composition at 1, 3, and 8 days post-ICU admission. On day 1, we analyzed clinical and body composition variables to predict in-hospital mortality.ResultsMultivariate analysis identified the Modified Nutrition Risk in the Critically Ill (mNUTRIC) score and the ratio of total body water to fat-free mass (TBW/FFM) as independent factors associated with in-hospital mortality in severe pneumonia patients. Receiver operating characteristic analysis determined that the TBW/FFM ratio was the most reliable predictive parameter of in-hospital mortality, with a cutoff value of 0.74. General linear regression with repeated measures analysis showed that hospital non-survivors displayed notable fluctuations in body water, fat, and muscle variables over the course of days 1, 3, and 8 after ICU admission.ConclusionsThe mNUTRIC score and TBW/FFM ratio emerged as independent factors for predicting hospital mortality, with the TBW/FFM ratio demonstrating the highest reliability as a predictive parameter.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12890-024-03302-4.

Background

Malnutrition diminishes physical activity and weakens immune systems [1, 2], increasing the risk and severity of pneumonia episodes and often leading to treatment failure and substantial mortality in severe cases [3, 4]. It is a significant cause of in-hospital death among severe pneumonia patients. Under the influence of malnutrition, changes in body composition can occur, leading to a loss of body cell mass and an expansion of extracellular water [5, 6].

Severe pneumonia contributes to high mortality rates in patients with elevated severity scores, such as the Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation (APACHE II) score, Pneumonia Severity Index (PSI), CURB-65 (new onset confusion, blood urea > 19 mg/dL, respiratory rate ≥ 30/min, systolic blood pressure < 90 mmHg and/or diastolic blood pressure ≤ 60 mmHg, age ≥ 65), Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA), Quick SOFA (qSOFA), and Charlson comorbidity index (CCI) score [7–10]. Disease severity and underlying chronic conditions exacerbate malnutrition due to imbalances in dietary intake and energy expenditure, compounded by impaired digestion and absorption [11]. High comorbidity indices correlate positively with malnutrition, prevalent among severe pneumonia patients [12, 13].

Clinicians assess nutritional status through recent weight loss, body mass, and daily food intake [14–16], crucial for evaluating clinical prognosis in severe pneumonia. The Modified Nutrition Risk in the Critically Ill (mNUTRIC) score, while an independent predictor of hospital mortality, lacks nutrition-related factors such as body mass index and body composition [8]. Therefore, integrating specific body composition assessments may enhance nutritional evaluation [17].

Malnutrition can lead to compromised immunity, muscle wasting, poor wound healing, and worsened infectious processes. Our previous study identified the mNUTRIC score as an independent predictor of hospital mortality in severe pneumonia [8]. However, interrelated body composition variables predicting clinical outcomes remain unidentified. Hence, our study aimed to identify predictive body composition variables for hospital mortality and compare them with established scoring systems in severe pneumonia. Additionally, we investigated serial changes in body composition among hospital survivors and non-survivors.

Material and methods

Setting and study design

This prospective study was conducted in three medical intensive care units (ICU) with a total of 34 beds at Kaohsiung Chang Gung Memorial Hospital, a 2,700-bed tertiary teaching hospital in southern Taiwan. The study took place between February 2020 and April 2022 (ClinicalTrials.gov Identifier: NCT04396522, date of registration: May 20, 2020). We prospectively enrolled patients with severe pneumonia, including those with both community- and hospital-acquired pneumonia. All patients who met the criteria for severe pneumonia were admitted to the ICU. The survival status was defined and recorded based on the patient’s condition at hospital discharge. According to this definition, we divided the patients into two groups: hospital survivors and non-survivors.

Definitions

Pneumonia was defined according to the 2019 ATS/IDSA guidelines [18]. The diagnosis of pneumonia was made according to new or progressive radiographic pulmonary infiltration/consolidation in patients with two or more features: cough, hyperthermia, hypothermia, purulent sputum, or respiratory secretion, and pathological lung auscultation. Community-acquired pneumonia was defined as pneumonia acquired outside the hospital setting, and hospital-acquired pneumonia was defined as pneumonia acquired in an in-hospital setting. Severe pneumonia was defined as pneumonia that required supportive therapy in a critical care setting. The ATS guidelines of 2001 modified the definition of severe pneumonia [19] to include the presence of two or more minor criteria (respiratory rate ≥ 30 breaths per min, ratio of arterial oxygen tension to inspired oxygen fraction < 250, bilateral or multilobar pneumonia, systolic blood pressure ≤ 90 mm Hg, and diastolic blood pressure ≤ 60 mm Hg) or the presence of one major criterion (the need for mechanical ventilation, septic shock or the need for vasopressors for > 4 h, an increase in the size of infiltrates by > 50% within 48 h, and acute renal failure). The exclusion criteria are defined as follows: any condition that does not meet the above criteria will result in exclusion from the study. Hospital survivors were defined as those alive after discharge. Hospital non-survivors comprised those who experienced mortality for any reason during their hospital stay.

Data record and evaluation

Clinical characteristics such as age, gender, body weight (BW), body height (BH), body mass index (BMI), ICU stay, hospital stay, pneumonia types, mechanical ventilation status, and inotrope administration status were recorded. Biomarkers such as C-reactive protein (CRP), procalcitonin (PCT), lactate, and albumin were prospectively evaluated and recorded. To understand the persistence of infective inflammation, we followed up on CRP levels three days after the administration of antibiotics. The decline or elevation of CRP levels was recorded for the analysis of survival outcomes. Other markers, such as the oxygen index and the ratio of PaO2/FiO2, as well as underlying comorbidities including congestive heart failure (CHF), chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), cancer, chronic liver disease (CLD), chronic kidney disease (CKD), and cerebrovascular accident (CVA), were also documented for survival outcome analysis.

Scoring indices

The data needed to calculate the severity scores were evaluated on the first day of ICU admission. The pneumonia scoring systems (PSI and CURB-65) were calculated from the evaluated patient databases. The ICU scoring systems (qSOFA, SOFA, and APACHE II) were assessed during the ICU stay. Information regarding underlying comorbidities was collected from medical records and patient histories to calculate the CCI score. The mNUTRIC score data were calculated using the evaluated variables and patient databases [17].

Bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA)

As in our previous study, we analyze body composition using InBody S10 [20]. The InBody S10 device (Biospace Co., Ltd., Cheonan, Korea) uses hand-to-foot BIA that sends varying frequencies of alternating current through the body. These impedance values are then used to predict several variables, including the total body water (TBW), intracellular water (ICW), extracellular water (ECW), visceral fat area (VFA), fat-free mass (FFM), skeletal muscle mass (SMM), skeletal muscle index (SMI), soft lean mass (SLM), body cell mass (BCM), basal metabolic rate (BMR), bone mineral content (BMC), body fat mass (BFM), and percent body fat (PBF). The InBody S10 analyzer utilizes a tetra-polar 8-point tactile electrode system. The InBody S10 takes 30 impedance measurements with six frequencies (1, 5, 50, 250, 500, and 1000 kHz) and three different frequencies of phase angle measurement (5, 50, and 250 kHz) at each of the five-segment parts (left upper limb, right upper limb, trunk, left lower limb, and right lower limb) within a measurement duration of approximately 5 min. Before measuring the protocols, the age, sex, and height were entered into the software for each patient lying supine on the bed. Before contact with the electrodes, the fingers and ankles of the patients were cleaned with antibacterial tissue. The electrodes were then clamped to the patient’s fingers and ankles. The arms were held wide enough to avoid contact between the arms and torso. This posture was maintained throughout the measurement. Once the assessment was completed, the finger and ankle electrodes were removed. Body composition was evaluated using the InBody S10 on days 1, 3, and 8 after ICU admission.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were analyzed using the chi-square test, and continuous variables were analyzed using Student’s t-test. Multivariate logistic regression analysis was performed to identify independent factors for in-hospital mortality and treatment outcomes. The multivariate model analysis included body composition factors, all clinical prediction rules, and other clinical factors that showed statistical significance in the univariate analysis. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were plotted, and the area under the curve (AUC) was compared to the clinical prediction rules measured in this study. The cutoff values of all factors for predicting hospital mortality among patients with severe pneumonia were analyzed according to the ROC curves.

To evaluate serial changes in body composition variables, a two-way analysis of variance using a general linear regression with repeated measures and the Huynh–Feldt method was employed at each body composition follow-up evaluation to examine the differences between hospital survivors and non-survivors.

The results are expressed as absolute numbers (percentages) or mean ± standard deviation. Adjusted odds ratios and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated using multivariate logistic regression. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 22; SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA).

Result

Population characteristics

A total of 2,122 patients were admitted to the ICU during the study period. Of these, 1,928 patients were excluded because they were ineligible for a pneumonia diagnosis, leaving 194 patients with definite severe pneumonia enrolled in the study. The participants included 127 male and 67 female patients, with a mean age of 70.87 ± 13.37 years. Among them, 149 patients were hospital survivors, and 45 were hospital non-survivors, resulting in an in-hospital mortality rate of 23.2%. A total of 136 patients were intubated and mechanically ventilated, while 58 patients were treated with non-invasive ventilation. Sixty patients were observed to have profound shock status and required the administration of inotropic agents.

Comparison of scoring systems score and clinical factors for hospital mortality

In this study cohort, we observed no statistically significant associations between hospital mortality and variables such as age, sex, BW, BH, BMI, hospital stay, CRP, PCT, albumin level, PaO2/FiO2 ratio, oxygen index, or underlying comorbidities including CHF, COPD, CLD, CKD, and CVA. However, we found that high scores on APACHE II, PSI, CURB-65, qSOFA, SOFA, CCI, and mNUTRIC were significant predictors of hospital mortality. Additionally, a prolonged ICU stay, high lactate levels, intubation status, elevated CRP level, administering inotropic agents and the presence of underlying malignant disease were also associated with in-hospital mortality (Table 1). Comparison of predictive accuracy for hospital mortality

Pneumonia severity scoring systems such as APACHE II, PSI, CURB-65, CCI, and mNUTRIC scores were significantly higher in hospital non-survivors compared to hospital survivors. Additionally, body composition factors, specifically ECW/TBW and TBW/FFM, were notably elevated in hospital non-survivors. Consequently, our objective was to identify the most predictive factor for hospital mortality, and ROC curves were plotted for analysis. Serial change in body composition between survivors and non-survivors

Body composition was assessed on days 1, 3, and 8 after ICU admission. General linear regression with repeated measures was employed to analyze the differences in body composition factors between survivors and non-survivors. TBW, ICW, ECW, FFM, SMM, SMI, SLM, BCM, BMR, BMC, BFM, PBF, and VFA exhibited significant linear slopes between survivors and non-survivors. TBW, ICW, ECW, BCM, BMR, BMC, BFM, PBF, and VFA displayed increasing slopes, while FFM, SMM, SMI, and SLM showed decreasing slopes in non-survivors. Discussion

In our research, we have highlighted the significant role of body composition analysis in predicting mortality among pneumonia patients. Compared to traditional pneumonia severity indices, the TBW/FFM ratio demonstrated superior predictive accuracy. We observed distinct trends in body composition variables during the initial eight days of hospitalization, distinguishing survivors from non-survivors among severe pneumonia cases.

Clinical predictors such as APACHE II score, PSI, CURB-65, SOFA score, and CCI are critical for assessing survival outcomes in severe pneumonia patients [7, 21, 22]. Our recent study reaffirmed the mNUTRIC score as a pivotal predictor of in-hospital mortality in severe pneumonia, comparable in effectiveness to other clinical factors [8]. Nutritional support has been shown to potentially reduce mortality in critically ill patients with high mNUTRIC scores [23, 24], highlighting nutrition's crucial role in these cases. Our study aimed to identify body composition variables indicative of nutritional status and their impact on clinical outcomes in severe pneumonia. We discovered that a high TBW/FFM ratio independently predicted hospital mortality and outperformed the mNUTRIC score.

Body composition analysis using devices like InBody S10 categorizes body weight into fat, protein, minerals, and body water components. This approach provides insights into health and nutritional status beyond BMI and weight [14, 25]. Critically ill patients often experience hypermetabolism leading to malnutrition, affecting body composition variables. Previous studies noted an increase in BMI accompanied by muscle mass loss and increased fat percentage in these patients [26]. Our study similarly observed higher body water (TBW, ICW, and ECW) and fat levels (BFM, PBF, and VFA) among non-survivors, whereas survivors tended to maintain or increase muscle mass.

Regular fluid resuscitation is a standard practice in the care of critically ill patients with shock. It's important to note that acute kidney injury may arise as a complication during the treatment of severe pneumonia, potentially impacting the management of fluid balance [27]. This may have contributed to the increased TBW. Fluid accumulation is mostly found in the third body space, with a depletion of intravascular volume due to albumin consumption in the hypermetabolic and malnourished state. In addition, the inflammatory process initiates endothelial damage, leads to vascular permeability, and shifts water from the intracellular to the extracellular space [28]. This leads to a poor hemodynamic state, and a poor survival outcome is anticipated. Based on a literature review, high ECW/TBW predicts the 30-day mortality in patients with septic shock [29]. In our study, a high ECW/TBW ratio also predicted hospital mortality independently, with a defined cutoff point.

The TBW/FFM ratio emerged as a stronger predictive parameter of hospital mortality than ECW/TBW, the mNUTRIC score, or other clinical factors in severe pneumonia. Loss of fat-free mass (FFM) due to increased energy expenditure is linked to adverse outcomes like ICU-acquired weakness, characterized by muscle protein loss and sarcopenia [30, 31]. A previous study reported that 60 ventilated ICU patients had a cumulative energy deficit during ICU admission, independently associated with FFM loss. Most patients were diagnosed with ICU-acquired weakness [32]. ICU-acquired weakness is highly positively correlated with FFM depletion. It is a sequela of sepsis that may generate numerous inflammatory factors (TNF-α, IL-1, and IL-6) [33] and excessive superoxide and reactive oxidative species in tissues and further result in massive loss of myosin and myoglobin-related proteins in limb and trunk muscles [34, 35]. ICU-acquired weakness is strongly associated with adverse clinical outcomes and increases the risks of short- and long-term mortality, prolongs hospital stay, increases medical expenses, and diminishes the long-term quality of life [36]. Serial evaluations revealed declining FFM slopes in non-survivors, highlighting its prognostic relevance.

Increased basal metabolic rate (BMR) and body cell mass (BCM) reflect higher energy needs and are associated with poor outcomes, observed more frequently in non-survivors in our study [37, 38]. Infectious diseases exacerbate inflammation and FFM depletion, emphasizing the predictive value of the TBW/FFM ratio in severe pneumonia.

Effective management strategies for ICU-acquired weakness include early rehabilitation and nutritional support to enhance muscle mass and reduce fluid accumulation and TBW [39–41]. Future studies should explore the TBW/FFM ratio's utility in predicting ICU-acquired weakness.

Limitations include the lack of documented daily nutrient intake and physical activity levels, crucial factors influencing body composition. Patients with severe pneumonia and related conditions typically experience limited mobility during initial ICU admission. Ongoing research aims to elucidate the relationship between body composition changes and rehabilitation efforts in these patients.

In conclusion, body composition analysis, particularly the TBW/FFM ratio, provides valuable insights into the prognosis of critically ill patients, including those with severe pneumonia. Understanding these dynamics can optimize nutritional and rehabilitation strategies, potentially improving patient outcomes.
