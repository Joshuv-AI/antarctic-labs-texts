Whole Abdominal Radiotherapy in Bone and Soft Tissue Sarcomas: Indications, Techniques, Clinical Outcomes, and Future Directions
Authors: YuanYou Yang, Lu Xie, Xin Sun, Jie Xu, Gang Ren
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Opinion StatementManaging bone and soft tissue sarcomas at high risk of intra-abdominal spread remains clinically challenging, and whole abdominal radiotherapy (WART) has become a pivotal locoregional component of multimodal therapy. For desmoplastic small round cell tumor (DSRCT)—the primary indication for WART—our practice prioritizes integrating WART with complete cytoreductive surgery and alkylator-based chemotherapy, with intensity-modulated radiotherapy (IMRT) or helical tomotherapy strongly recommended as preferred techniques due to their significant reduction in hematologic and gastrointestinal toxicity versus conventional 2D radiotherapy while preserving target coverage. For other subtypes (peritoneal rhabdomyosarcoma, Ewing sarcoma, myxoid liposarcoma), WART should be individualized: considered for confirmed peritoneal dissemination, residual disease post-chemotherapy, or incomplete resection, but avoided in extensive distant metastases. Future paradigms must optimize benefit-to-toxicity ratios, including routine proton/carbon ion therapy for pediatric/young adult patients to minimize late effects (e.g., growth abnormalities, infertility), exploration of WART combinations with targeted agents (e.g., IGF-1R inhibitors) or immunotherapies (e.g., PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors) in prospective trials given promising preclinical/early clinical data, and use of molecular profiling and circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) monitoring to guide patient selection and timely treatment adjustments. Long-term follow-up is essential for all patients to monitor late toxicities (e.g., small bowel obstruction, radiation nephritis) and secondary malignancies. In summary, WART is a cornerstone of curative-intent treatment for selected intra-abdominal sarcomas, but requires rigorous patient selection, advanced planning, and integration with systemic therapies, with continued international collaboration and trials critical to refining its role and improving outcomes for these rare, aggressive malignancies.Keywords: Whole abdominal radiotherapy (WART), Desmoplastic small round cell tumor (DSRCT), Sarcoma, Peritoneal dissemination, Intensity-modulated radiotherapy (IMRT)

Introduction

Bone and soft tissue sarcomas constitute a heterogeneous group of malignancies with diverse clinical behaviors and treatment requirements. While localized disease often responds to surgical resection combined with adjuvant or neoadjuvant chemotherapy, certain subtypes exhibit a high propensity for intra-abdominal spread, peritoneal carcinomatosis, or local recurrence following initial treatment. For such cases, whole abdominal radiotherapy (WART) has emerged as a valuable locoregional treatment modality designed to target microscopic residual disease, prevent peritoneal recurrence, and improve long-term disease control.

Desmoplastic small round cell tumor (DSRCT) is the most well-characterized sarcoma for which WART is routinely recommended as part of multimodal therapy. First described in 1989 [1], DSRCT predominantly affects adolescent males and young adults, with over 90% of cases presenting with multiple abdominopelvic nodules and peritoneal dissemination at diagnosis [2, 3]. The aggressive biological behavior of DSRCT, characterized by early systemic spread and high recurrence rates, necessitates intensive multimodal treatment combining chemotherapy, cytoreductive surgery, and WART [4, 5]. Other rare sarcomas with intra-abdominal involvement, such as peritoneal rhabdomyosarcoma (RMS), Ewing sarcoma variants, and myxoid liposarcoma, may also be considered for WART in selected clinical scenarios [6, 7].

The primary rationale for WART in sarcoma management lies in its ability to deliver homogenous radiation dose to the entire abdominal and pelvic cavities, targeting microscopic peritoneal metastases and residual tumor foci that are inaccessible to surgical resection or inadequately addressed by chemotherapy alone [2, 8]. Despite its potential benefits, WART is associated with significant acute and late toxicities, requiring careful patient selection, advanced treatment planning, and supportive care strategies [9, 10]. This review synthesizes the available literature identified through a systematic search of PubMed, Cochrane Library, and Scopus databases using keywords including ‘Whole abdominopelvic radiotherapy’, ‘Whole abdominal irradiation’, and ‘sarcoma’. It provides a detailed analysis of WART in bone and soft tissue sarcomas, with a focus on DSRCT as the primary indication while incorporating relevant data for RMS, Ewing sarcoma, and myxoid liposarcoma.

Rationale for Whole Abdominal Radiotherapy in Sarcomas

Disease Biology and Peritoneal Dissemination

The decision to utilize WART in sarcoma treatment is primarily driven by the biological behavior of the tumor subtype. DSRCT, the most common sarcoma treated with WART, is characterized by a unique chromosomal translocation t(11;22)(p13;q12) resulting in the EWSR1-WT1 fusion gene [11, 12]. This fusion oncoprotein drives the formation of multiple intra-abdominal nodules and extensive peritoneal dissemination, which are hallmark features of the disease [2, 3]. Even after aggressive cytoreductive surgery, microscopic residual disease persists in the peritoneal cavity in a significant proportion of patients, leading to high local recurrence rates [5, 7].

Clinical studies have demonstrated that peritoneal recurrence is the most common pattern of treatment failure in DSRCT, occurring in up to 88% of patients without adequate locoregional control [9]. WART addresses this unmet need by delivering a uniform radiation dose to the entire peritoneal surface, eradicating microscopic residual tumor cells and reducing the risk of local recurrence [2, 8]. Additionally, DSRCT often presents with synchronous extraperitoneal metastases (in approximately 50% of cases), but WART remains a critical component of treatment for patients with localized or regionally disseminated disease to improve progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS) [12, 13].

For other sarcoma subtypes, the role of WART is increasingly recognized. Peritoneal RMS, though rare, exhibits similar patterns of peritoneal dissemination, making WART a potential therapeutic option to enhance local control [6, 14]. Ewing sarcoma with extensive intra-abdominal involvement, particularly when surgical resection is incomplete, may benefit from WART to target residual disease [6, 14]. Myxoid liposarcoma, the most common STS metastasizing to the peritoneal cavity, has shown responsiveness to WART as salvage therapy for chemotherapy-refractory peritoneal seeding [7]. These tumors share the propensity for diffuse peritoneal spread, justifying WART as a locoregional control strategy in selected cases. Synergy with Multimodal Therapy

WART is rarely used as a standalone treatment for sarcomas; instead, it is integrated into a multimodal approach combining chemotherapy and cytoreductive surgery. Preclinical and clinical evidence supports the synergistic effects of these treatment modalities. Neoadjuvant chemotherapy reduces tumor burden, making cytoreductive surgery more feasible, while WART eliminates residual microscopic disease following surgery [4, 17].

Several retrospective studies have demonstrated that the combination of chemotherapy, complete cytoreductive surgery, and WART is associated with superior outcomes compared to bimodal therapy (chemotherapy plus surgery or chemotherapy plus radiotherapy) [5, 15]. For example, a study by Lal et al. [5] reported a 3-year OS rate of 55% in DSRCT patients who received trimodal therapy (chemotherapy, surgery, and WART) compared to 27% in those who missed one modality. Similarly, a French multicenter study [15] found that postoperative WART was the only variable associated with longer peritoneal recurrence-free survival and disease-free survival (DFS) in DSRCT patients after complete cytoreductive surgery. In pediatric and young adult sarcoma patients with peritoneal dissemination, trimodal therapy including WART has achieved 100% relapse-free survival (RFS) and OS at a median follow-up of 4.0 years, highlighting the critical role of WART in this setting [18].

Technical Aspects of Whole Abdominal Radiotherapy

Treatment Planning and Dose Fractionation

The goal of WART planning is to deliver a homogenous radiation dose to the entire abdominal and pelvic cavities while minimizing exposure to critical organs at risk (OARs), including the kidneys, liver, small bowel, and bone marrow [9, 10]. Traditional two-dimensional (2D) radiotherapy techniques have been largely replaced by advanced modalities such as intensity-modulated radiotherapy (IMRT) and volumetric-modulated arc therapy (VMAT), which allow for better dose conformity and OAR sparing [9, 10]. Helical tomotherapy, a specialized IMRT technique, has emerged as a promising option for peritoneal sarcomas, enabling precise targeting of intraperitoneal lesions while reducing dose to OARs [7].

The standard dose of WART for sarcomas is typically 30 Gy delivered in 1.5–1.55 Gy daily fractions [10, 16, 19]. This dose is chosen to balance efficacy against toxicity, as higher doses are associated with increased gastrointestinal and hematological toxicities [10]. For patients with macroscopic residual disease or high-risk areas, a focal boost dose of 15–20 Gy (to a total dose of 45–50 Gy) may be administered [16, 19]. However, the use of boost doses must be carefully evaluated to avoid excessive toxicity to adjacent normal tissues. In myxoid liposarcoma with peritoneal seeding, a low-fractionation schedule (1.6 Gy once daily, total 24 Gy) followed by a focal boost (16 Gy in 8 fractions) using helical tomotherapy has achieved complete metabolic response with minimal toxicity [7].

IMRT has emerged as the preferred technique for WART due to its ability to reduce dose to OARs while maintaining target coverage. A study by Desai et al. [9] demonstrated that IMRT reduced acute toxicity compared to conventional 2D radiotherapy in DSRCT patients, with no significant difference in late toxicities. Similarly, Pinnix et al. [10] reported that IMRT for WART after surgery was associated with acceptable toxicity profiles and improved dose distribution compared to 2D techniques. In pediatric sarcoma patients, IMRT with dose-painting (DP-IMRT) allows simultaneous delivery of different doses to the primary site and abdominal/pelvic region, optimizing local control while sparing normal tissues [18].

Simulation and Target Volume Definition

Accurate simulation and target volume definition are critical for the success of WART. Patients typically undergo computed tomography (CT)-based simulation in the supine position with immobilization devices to ensure reproducibility [9, 10]. The clinical target volume (CTV) includes the entire abdominal and pelvic cavities, encompassing the peritoneal surface, omentum, mesentery, and retroperitoneal lymph nodes [2, 8]. The planning target volume (PTV) is defined by expanding the CTV by 0.5–1.0 cm to account for setup errors and organ motion [10].

Special considerations are required for patients with anatomical variations or comorbidities. For example, patients with horseshoe kidney may require modification of the radiation field to avoid excessive dose to the renal parenchyma [1]. Additionally, patients who have undergone prior abdominal surgery may have altered anatomy or peritoneal adhesions, which can affect radiation dose distribution and increase the risk of toxicity [20]. In DSRCT patients, the CTV may be extended to include the liver surface (liver rind) to target peritoneal deposits, while limiting the central liver parenchyma dose to < 30 Gy to reduce hepatic toxicity [21].

Organ-at-Risk Sparing

The primary challenge in WART planning is sparing critical OARs, particularly the kidneys, small bowel, and bone marrow. The kidneys are highly sensitive to radiation, and doses exceeding 20 Gy are associated with an increased risk of radiation nephritis [1, 9]. IMRT allows for the creation of dose gradients to limit renal dose, with most plans designed to keep the mean renal dose below 18 Gy [9, 10]. In helical tomotherapy plans for myxoid liposarcoma, the average doses to the liver and kidneys were 16.9 Gy and 13.8–14.1 Gy, respectively, well below toxic thresholds [7].

The small bowel is another critical OAR, as radiation-induced enteritis can lead to acute symptoms (diarrhea, abdominal pain) and late complications (small bowel obstruction, fistula formation) [10, 21]. To minimize small bowel dose, patients may undergo bowel preparation before simulation, and the radiation field may be adjusted to exclude portions of the small bowel when possible [10]. Additionally, the use of IMRT can reduce the volume of small bowel receiving high doses (> 30 Gy) [10].

Bone marrow suppression is a common acute toxicity of WART, particularly in patients who have received prior chemotherapy [10]. To mitigate this, the radiation field may be modified to exclude the pelvic bone marrow when not required for target coverage, and supportive care with growth factors may be administered [10, 21]. IMRT has been shown to reduce mean bone dose by 21–25% compared to 2D RT, resulting in lower rates of grade 4 hematologic toxicity (33% vs. 86%) [9, 10]. Clinical Outcomes of Whole Abdominal Radiotherapy in Sarcomas

Desmoplastic Small Round Cell Tumor (DSRCT)

The majority of clinical data on WART in sarcomas comes from studies of DSRCT. Multiple retrospective and prospective studies have demonstrated that WART improves local control and survival in DSRCT patients when integrated into multimodal therapy.

A French multicenter study by Honoré et al. [2] evaluated 38 DSRCT patients treated with chemotherapy, surgery, and WART. The median OS was 37.7 months, and the 3-year OS rate was 48%. The absence of extraperitoneal metastases, complete cytoreductive surgery, and WART were identified as favorable prognostic factors. Similarly, a study by Atallah et al. [15] involving 107 DSRCT patients found that the combination of surgery and WART was associated with a median OS of 40.3 months and a 3-year OS rate of 63.1%, compared to 28.3 months and 48.5% for patients who underwent surgery without WART.

In pediatric and adolescent DSRCT patients, WART has also been shown to improve outcomes. A study by Osborne et al. [21] evaluated 32 pediatric and young adult DSRCT patients treated with multimodal therapy including WART (23 with IMRT). The median OS was 60 months, and the 3-year OS rate was 64%, demonstrating that WART is feasible and effective in this population. A larger series of 31 DSRCT patients treated with WART reported 3-year OS and PFS rates of 50% and 24%, respectively, with IMRT associated with reduced toxicity and maintained efficacy [9].

However, it is important to note that WART does not benefit all DSRCT patients. Patients with extensive extraperitoneal metastases or incomplete cytoreductive surgery have limited benefit from WART, as systemic disease progression remains the primary cause of treatment failure [2, 13]. Additionally, the efficacy of WART may be influenced by the timing of treatment, with postoperative WART typically administered 4–6 weeks after surgery to allow for recovery [8, 21]. Delays exceeding 2.1 months between surgery and WART have been associated with shorter local progression-free survival in DSRCT [21].

Other Sarcoma Subtypes

Data on WART in other sarcoma subtypes is growing, with promising results in selected cases. For peritoneal RMS, a phase I study by Modak et al. [6] included three patients who received WART after intraperitoneal radioimmunotherapy. All patients tolerated the treatment well, and two remained alive without recurrence at 29.4 and 97.4 months of follow-up. In a series of 10 pediatric patients with non-DSRCT sarcomas (7 RMS, 2 embryonal undifferentiated sarcoma, 1 Ewing sarcoma) treated with WART, 100% RFS and OS were achieved at a median follow-up of 4.0 years [18].

Ewing sarcoma with extensive intra-abdominal involvement may benefit from WART when surgical resection is incomplete. Although large-scale studies are lacking, case reports and small series have demonstrated improved local control with WART in this setting [6, 14]. Myxoid liposarcoma, a subtype with a high propensity for peritoneal metastasis, has shown significant responsiveness to WART. A case report of chemotherapy-refractory peritoneal myxoid liposarcoma treated with helical tomotherapy-based WART (24 Gy + 16 Gy boost) achieved complete metabolic response and durable intraperitoneal control [7].

Toxicity of Whole Abdominal Radiotherapy

Acute Toxicity

Acute toxicity is common with WART, primarily affecting the gastrointestinal and hematological systems. Gastrointestinal toxicity includes nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, abdominal pain, and anorexia, which occur in more than 80% of patients [10, 21]. These symptoms are typically mild to moderate (grade 1–2) and manageable with antiemetics, antidiarrheals, and supportive care [9, 10]. Grade 3–4 gastrointestinal toxicity (severe diarrhea, bowel obstruction) occurs in approximately 10–15% of patients [10]. In pediatric patients, acute gastrointestinal toxicities are mostly grade 1–2, with grade 3 diarrhea occurring in 30% of cases [18].

Hematological toxicity, including neutropenia, thrombocytopenia, and anemia, is also common, particularly in patients who have received prior chemotherapy [10]. Grade 3–4 neutropenia and thrombocytopenia occur in 10–20% of patients, and may require growth factor support or blood product transfusions [6, 10]. IMRT has been shown to reduce grade 4 hematologic toxicity from 86% (2D RT) to 33% (IMRT) in DSRCT patients [9].

Other acute toxicities include fatigue, skin reactions (erythema, dryness), and genitourinary symptoms (dysuria, frequency), which are generally mild and resolve after completion of treatment [9, 10].

Late Toxicity

Late toxicity of WART is a significant concern, particularly in long-term survivors. The most common late complications include small bowel obstruction, enterocutaneous fistula, hemorrhagic cystitis, and ureteral fibrosis [10, 21]. Small bowel obstruction is the most frequent late toxicity, occurring in approximately 10–15% of patients, and may require surgical intervention [10]. In pediatric patients, late sequelae include partial small bowel obstruction, radiation-induced proctitis, ovarian failure, and growth abnormalities, though 50% of patients remain free of long-term toxicities [18].

Radiation nephritis is a rare but serious late toxicity, occurring in less than 5% of patients [1, 9]. To minimize this risk, the mean renal dose is typically limited to < 18 Gy, and patients are monitored for changes in renal function during follow-up [9, 10].

Other late toxicities include infertility (due to radiation to the pelvic organs), secondary malignancies, and chronic fatigue [10, 21]. The risk of secondary malignancies is low but increases with time, particularly in patients who have received high-dose chemotherapy in addition to WART [20].

Toxicity Management

Several strategies have been developed to reduce the toxicity of WART. The use of IMRT has been shown to reduce acute gastrointestinal and hematological toxicity compared to conventional 2D radiotherapy [9, 10]. Helical tomotherapy further enhances OAR sparing, allowing for delivery of WART without significant side effects in chemotherapy-refractory cases [7]. Additionally, the administration of prophylactic antiemetics (such as 5-HT3 antagonists and neurokinin-1 inhibitors) and antidiarrheals can significantly reduce gastrointestinal symptoms [10].

For patients at high risk of late toxicity, careful treatment planning and dose constraints to OARs are essential. Long-term follow-up is also critical to monitor for late complications and intervene early when necessary [20, 21]. In pediatric patients, dose-painting IMRT and strict OAR dose limits (mean kidney dose < 16 Gy, mean liver dose < 30 Gy) have minimized late toxicities [18]. Future Directions and Emerging Strategies

Novel Radiation Techniques

Emerging radiation techniques, such as proton therapy and carbon ion therapy, hold promise for further reducing the toxicity of WART. Proton therapy delivers a Bragg peak dose distribution, allowing for precise targeting of the abdominal cavity while minimizing dose to OARs [8, 9]. Although there are no large-scale studies evaluating proton therapy in sarcomas, preliminary data suggests that it may reduce the risk of late gastrointestinal and renal toxicity [9]. A case report of carbon ion radiotherapy for DSRCT demonstrated feasible delivery and potential for improved local control in radioresistant cases [22].

Combination with Targeted Therapy and Immunotherapy

The development of targeted therapy and immunotherapy has opened new avenues for combining with WART to improve outcomes. For example, the anti-B7H3 monoclonal antibody 131I-omburtamab has been evaluated in a phase I study [6] in DSRCT patients, with WART administered after intraperitoneal radioimmunotherapy. The combination was well-tolerated, and preliminary data suggests improved local control [6]. IGF-1R inhibitors, such as ganitumab, have shown clinical activity in DSRCT and may synergize with WART to enhance tumor cell kill [23].

Immunotherapy agents, such as PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors, may also be combined with WART to enhance antitumor immunity. Preclinical studies have demonstrated that radiation can induce immunogenic cell death, and combining WART with PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors may synergistically improve tumor control [24, 25]. However, clinical data on this combination in sarcomas is limited, and further studies are needed.

Personalized Medicine and Risk Stratification

The development of personalized medicine approaches, such as molecular profiling and liquid biopsy, may allow for better patient selection for WART. For example, circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) analysis can detect minimal residual disease after surgery, identifying patients who are at high risk of recurrence and may benefit from WART [26, 27]. Additionally, molecular markers such as CD99 expression and DDR pathway alterations may predict response to WART and guide treatment decisions [12, 17].

Risk stratification models, such as the one proposed by Saltsman et al. [17], which incorporates imaging findings (liver involvement, ascites) to predict survival, may also help identify patients who are most likely to benefit from WART. By selecting patients based on individual risk factors, we can optimize the benefit-to-toxicity ratio of WART. In DSRCT, the absence of extraperitoneal metastases, complete cytoreductive surgery, and early initiation of WART (< 2.1 months after surgery) are key prognostic factors for improved outcomes [21]. The integration of WART into the management of intra-abdominal sarcomas requires careful patient selection based on histologic subtype and disease characteristics. Conclusion

Whole abdominal radiotherapy is a critical component of multimodal treatment for selected bone and soft tissue sarcomas, particularly DSRCT, which exhibits a high risk of peritoneal dissemination and local recurrence. Advanced techniques such as IMRT and helical tomotherapy have improved the feasibility and safety of WART by reducing dose to critical OARs, while clinical data demonstrate that WART improves local control and survival when integrated into multimodal therapy. Emerging evidence supports the utility of WART in other sarcoma subtypes, including peritoneal RMS, Ewing sarcoma, and myxoid liposarcoma, in selected clinical scenarios.

However, WART is associated with significant acute and late toxicities, requiring careful patient selection and supportive care. Future directions for WART in sarcomas include the development of novel radiation techniques (proton, carbon ion therapy), combination with targeted therapy and immunotherapy, and personalized medicine approaches to optimize patient selection. International collaboration and clinical trials are needed to further evaluate the efficacy and safety of these strategies, particularly in rare sarcoma subtypes. By continuing to refine the use of WART, we can improve outcomes for patients with aggressive intra-abdominal sarcomas.
