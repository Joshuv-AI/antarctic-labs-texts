Oxidative stress and fertility studies of selected plants using female Drosophila melanogaster
Authors: H. O. Alaka, T. O. Ajayi, A. O. Abolaji
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveLow-to-moderate oxidative stress (OS) plays physiological roles in reproduction. However, excessive production of OS markers has been implicated in infertility, and the use of medicinal plants for its management is based on a paucity of scientific confirmation. Therefore, this study investigated the effects of methanol extracts of five (5) selected medicinal plants (Kigelia africana, Spondias mombin, Anacardium occidentale, Senecio biafrae, and Corchorus olitorius) on biochemical markers, together with fertility index in the Drosophila melanogaster model.ResultsThe plant extracts were found to be associated with a decrease in flies’ mortality, while the fertility index increased significantly. The OS markers [Hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) and Nitric oxide (NO)] were elevated in varying degrees with a decrease in the antioxidant parameters [total thiols (T-SH), and non-protein thiol (NPSH)] revealed in flies exposed to the medicinal plants.

Introduction

Oxidative Stress (OS) arises from the disruption of the balance between reactive oxygen and nitrogen species (RONS) generated from cellular metabolism and the defending antioxidants [1]. At the physiological level, RONS are involved in various transduction pathways controlling reproductive processes such as ovulation and implantation [2]. However, excessive OS is undesirable, as the excess RONS generated can attack cellular components, leading to a cascade of inflammatory reactions, which could potentially cause reproductive diseases leading to infertility [3].On the other hand, antioxidants are defense molecules with the ability to terminate RONS-induced reactions, therefore minimizing or preventing cellular damage [4]. Therefore, antioxidants can help improve fertility in humans. Notably, examples are total thiols (T-SH), glutathione peroxidase (GPx), non-protein thiol (NPSH), vitamin C, and E.

Several key oxidative stress makers and antioxidants present in humans have been discovered in Drosophila melanogaster, hence, it can successfully be adapted to investigate oxidative stress and antioxidant capacities of medicinal plants [5]. The use of traditional medicine is quite popular among infertile couples, especially the females in Nigeria, according to ethnobotanical research conducted on Nigerian plants useful in reproductive health [6]. Several Nigerian plants have been identified as beneficial for reproductive health [6–9], and five plants from the surveys were selected for the study. The plants are;

Kigelia africana (Lam.) Benth., family Bignoniaceae, is the only species in the genus Kigelia and is highly utilized for its therapeutic, religious, and cultural values [10]. Different parts of Kigelia africana are used in Africa for the treatment of various diseases such as female reproductive disorders, sexually transmitted diseases, and peptic ulcer [10].

Spondias mombin L., also known as hog plum, is a small deciduous tree belonging to the Anacardiaceae family and is indigenous to tropical areas of America, Asia, and Africa [10]. Ethnopharmacological uses in Nigeria include the treatment of childbirth, diabetes, and malaria [11].

C. olitorius L., belonging to the family Malvaceae, is an annual herbaceous plant with a thin stem. Its leaves are widely used as a soup and contain a high content of vitamins, minerals, and phenolic compounds [12].

Senecio biafrae (Oliv. & Hiern) J. Moore (family Asteraceae), commonly known as “English spinach” or “worowo” in Nigeria, grows as undercover in tree crop plantation [13]. It is used in West Africa to treat many diseases such as rheumatic pain, female infertility, and oedemas [13].

Anacardium occidentale L., widely known as cashew, belongs to the family Anacardiaceae. Different parts of the plant are popular constituents of traditional medicine worldwide for the treatment of various ailments, including malaria, diabetes, and cancer [14].

The paucity of evidence-based research to justify traditional medicine’s indigenous claims has made it difficult for them to be officially recognized and included in Nigeria’s healthcare system as alternative medicines [8]. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the effect of the methanol leaf extract of Kigelia africana, Spondias mombin, Anacardium occidentale, Senecio biafrae, and Corchorus olitorius on the mortality rate, fertility index, oxidative stress, and antioxidant markers using the female D. melanogaster model.

Methods

Plants extracts preparation

The leaves of Corchorus olitorius, Kigelia africana, Spondias mombin, Anacardium occidentale, and Senecio biafrae were collected from Oyo, and Osun States, Nigeria, and authenticated by Dr S. A. Odewo with the voucher numbers FHI 113590, 113757, 113756, 113591, and 1142167, respectively at the Forest Herbarium, Ibadan, Oyo State. The extraction was carried out as described by [15] with some modifications.

Drosophila melanogaster strain and culture

The female gender of Drosophila melanogaster (Oregon strain, 1–3 days old) was used for the study. They were reared and maintained in Drosophila Laboratory, Biochemistry Department, University of Ibadan, Nigeria on cornmeal medium mixed with brewer’s yeast (1% w/v), agar (1% w/v), and nipagin (preservative, 0.08% v/w) at temperature (23 + 2 °C) under 12 h dark/light cycle according to [16].

Survival assay

Thirty (30) female flies were exposed to drosophila diets (10 g) already premixed with 1 mg, 2 mg, 3 mg, and 4 mg methanol extract of Corchorus olitorius, Kigelia africana, Spondias mombin, Anacardium occidentale, Senecio biafrae, and ethanol (2%) as control for 7 days. The plant extracts were dissolved in 200 µL of ethanol. Flies were transferred to new vials every 3 days. Daily scoring of the flies’ mortality was measured, and the survival rate was calculated as a percentage of live flies as described by [16].

Assessment of fertility index

Ten (10) virgin female flies, emerging from standard diet were collected within 6–8 h after eclosion, and were transferred to diets (10 g) supplemented with 1 mg, 2 mg, 3 mg, and 4 mg of Corchorus olitorius, Kigelia africana, Spondias mombin, Anacardium occidentale, Senecio biafrae, for 5 days. Five virgin female flies from each treatment group above were paired with five non-treated males (a day old, Oregon strain) in vials containing standard fly diet, labeled and kept for 48 h for mating to take place and eggs laid, afterwards the adult flies were removed. The vials (with eggs) were observed daily for 14 days for the emergence of adult flies. The total number of flies’ emergence within a 14-day duration is a measure of reproductive ability as described by [17].

Assessment of biochemical parameters

Sixty (60) female flies were fed with diet supplemented with (1 mg, 2 mg, 3 mg, and 4 mg/10 g diet) of the plant extracts, and ethanol (control) for 5 days. The flies were collected after anesthetizing in ice, weighed, homogenized in 0.1 M phosphate buffer, pH 7.4 (ratio of 1 mg:10 µL), and centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C in a Thermo Fisher Sorvall Legend Micro 17R. Subsequently, the supernatant was separated from the pellet into labeled Eppendorf tubes and used for the determination of Total Protein, Total Thiol level, Hydrogen Peroxide Level, non-protein thiol (NPSH), and Nitric Oxide (NO) level.

Total protein determination

Total protein quantification was by a standard method described [18] with Bovine Serum Albumin as standard.

Total thiol determination

The total thiol concentrations were determined as described by [19]. The reaction mixture consisting of 510 µL of phosphate buffer (0.1 M), 25 µL of sample, 35 µL of distilled water, and 30 µL of DTNB was incubated for 30 min at room temperature and absorbance read at 412 nm. Using Glutathione as a standard, a calibration curve was plotted, and the data were expressed as in μmol/mg of protein.

Determination of non-protein thiol (NPSH)

The supernatant was precipitated in 4% sulphosalicylic acid in the ratio of 1:1 at 4 °C for 1 h and then subjected to centrifugation at 5000 rpm for 10 min. The assay mixture consisted of 550 µL of 0.1 M phosphate buffer, 100 µL of centrifuged supernatant, and 100 µL of DTNB. Glutathione was used as a standard, and the data were expressed as in μmol/mg of protein [19].

Determination of hydrogen peroxide (H2O2)

The reaction mixture contained 10 µL of sample and 590 µL of FOX-1 reagent (Ferrous Oxidation-Xylenol orange). The absorbance was measured at 560 nm after 30 min of incubation at room temperature. The concentration of hydrogen peroxide generated was determined using an extinction coefficient of H2O2 and expressed as μmol/mL [20].

Determination of nitric oxide (NO)

Nitric oxide level was measured according to [21], which involved incubating 250 µL of flies’ supernatant in 250 µL of Griess reagent at room temperature for 20 min, then absorbance was measured at 550 nm. The concentration of NO in the samples was calculated using the standard calibration curve of NaNO2 and expressed as μmol/L.

Statistical analysis

Data are expressed as mean ± SD. Statistical analysis was by One-way analysis of variance using GraphPad Prism 9 (GraphPad, San Diego, CA, USA). The statistical significance is determined at p < 0.05.

Results

Effects of the selected plants on Drosophila melanogaster survival

Kigelia africana at all concentrations administered, elevated the survival rate of the flies; however, Corchorus olitorius, Senecio biafrae showed no significant alteration in the survival rate of the flies by day 7 when compared to the control. Discussion

Drosophila melanogaster can be utilized in natural product research to investigate the pharmacological activities of medicinal plants and their toxic effects. The general reduction in mortality rate of the exposed flies suggests that medicinal plants are relatively safe and may account for one of the reasons why plants are incorporated in many traditional medicine recipes worldwide for the treatment of human ailments. Overall, all concentrations of the plant extracts significantly (p < 0.05) increased the reproductive ability of the female flies. The increased fertility index of the exposed flies corroborates the efficacy of herbs in infertility management and agrees with the result of [17], where 500 mg of Moringa oleifera significantly (p < 0.05) increased fly emergence compared to Ascorbic acid supplementation. The highest fertility rate was observed in Anacardium occidentale at a concentration of 1 mg, which increases the fertility of the treated flies by 245% when compared to the control. This could be due to its richness in polyphenols, a fertility enhancer, as a study done by [22] revealed A. occidentale as the most yielding plant in terms of polyphenols among other plants in Southeast Asia.

According to [23], lowered antioxidant levels have been discovered to favor the ovulation process, wherein endogenous antioxidants activities are lowered, leading to increased RONS production that are needed as signaling molecules for various ovulation pathways. None of the plant extracts at various concentrations significantly (p < 0.05) increased the total thiols level (TSH), and there was generally a non-significant (p < 0.05) lowering effect of TSH across the groups. These results agree with the findings of [14], where treatment of D. melanogaster with ethanol extract of leaves and stem barks of Anacardium occidentale did not significantly change GSH and TSH levels in the exposed flies. Also, the oxidative stress biomarkers H2O2, NO were elevated by the plant extracts with Senecio biafrae significantly (p < 0.05), increasing H2O2, NO, except at 4 mg.

Therefore, the decrease in antioxidant markers via T-SH and NPSH and elevated oxidative stress markers (NO and H2O2) in the study could be responsible for the increased ovulation in the treated flies which could be stimulated by the plant extracts leading to a high fertility index.

In conclusion, the plants which are traditionally used for infertility management have been shown to reduce mortality, increase oxidative stress markers with a non-significant effect on antioxidant markers, and increase the fertility rate of D. melanogaster.

Limitations

The role that oxidative stress and antioxidants play in maintaining female reproduction is complex. Further research needs to be conducted on specific reproductive pathways in vertebrate models before definitive claims about the relationship between oxidative stress and reproduction can be appliedS to clinical uses.
