Infective endocarditis of tricuspid valve following septic abortion: a case report
Authors: Abinash Baniya, Prabin Duwadee, Sujata K.C., Manoj Shahi, Amir Khan, Yasoda Rijal, Bipana Gurung, Ram N. Kurmi
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Introduction:Right-sided infective endocarditis (IE) in non-intravenous drug users is a rare finding. IE of the tricuspid valve is considered an important but uncommon complication in patients with a recent history of obstetric and gynecological procedures.Case presentation:We report a case of a 28-year-old female with IE of the tricuspid valve with a prior history of dilatation and curettage. The echocardiography revealed two mobile vegetation in the septal leaflet of the tricuspid valve with severe eccentric tricuspid regurgitation. Blood culture was positive for Staphylococcus aureus. The patient was started on intravenous (i.v.) antibiotics with supportive treatments and improved over the days.Clinical discussion:Infection can get access to the venous system via pelvic veins after the septic obstetric and gynecological procedure and subsequently to the right side of the heart. Different studies have highlighted the role of prophylactic antibiotics in significantly reducing post-abortal infections. In our patient, the disease was diagnosed on the basis of clinical, echocardiographic, and blood culture findings, and the patient responded well to i.v. antibiotics and supportive care under close monitoring in the coronary care unit.Conclusion:It is important for healthcare providers to be aware of the risk factors and symptoms associated with right-sided IE for early diagnosis and treatment. Appropriate antibiotic prophylaxis and adherence to sterile techniques can help to prevent IE.

The incidence of right-sided infective endocarditis (IE) is considerably less as compared to left-sided, accounting for 5–10% of all cases of IE. It is exceptional to have right-sided IE in non-addicts as it primarily affects intravenous (i.v.) drug abusers, patients with central venous catheters, and in-situ devices in the heart1. However, in some parts of the South Asian region, puerperal sepsis and septic abortion are also reported to be some of the principal causes of isolated right-sided IE2. Septic abortions usually result from the application of nonsterile techniques for uterine evacuation, which is likely to be due to inadequate healthcare access or legal as well as personal barriers to receiving professional medical care3. In addition to this, IE is considered an important but uncommon complication in patients with a history of obstetric and gynecological procedures performed recently4. In light of this fact and much less published information on this subject, we report a case of isolated right-sided IE following dilation and curettage.

Case presentation

A 28-year-old female presented to the outpatient department with a history of fever, shortness of breath, and bilateral leg swelling for 2 months. She is a mother of two children (7 and 4 years old) born via elective lower-segment cesarean section. She had terminated her pregnancy of 9 weeks with dilatation and curettage at a nearby health clinic 9 weeks prior to admission in our tertiary care center. The patient does not remember having any blood tests or receiving any antibiotics before or after the procedure. She has no significant medical history (including HIV and hepatitis) and denied i.v. drug use or recent tattoos.

One week after the abortion, she developed a fever and malaise. Fever was insidious on onset, intermittent type; the maximum temperature recorded at home was 103°F and was associated with chills and rigor. Fever was also associated with an erythematous rash over the lower abdomen and lower limbs. Shortness of breath gradually progressed over days and she even had difficulty breathing while changing her clothes. She visited a nearby clinic with the above-mentioned complaints, where she was prescribed oral antibiotics and antipyretics for 7 days and sent home without a blood culture. The symptoms did not relieve, and she visited various hospitals before being referred to our center.

On examination, she was ill-looking, thin-built, and febrile. Her pulse rate was 127 beats per minute and regular with a blood pressure of 90/60 mmHg and a respiratory rate of 25 breaths per minute. There was no icterus and clubbing, but bilateral mild pedal pitting edema was present. Abdominal examination revealed tenderness over the right upper abdominal quadrant. Her respiratory examination revealed diminished breath sound over the bases of both lungs and diffuse crepitations heard over the lower third of both lung fields. Auscultation over the tricuspid area revealed a diastolic murmur. Blood examination revealed hemoglobin of 6.5 g%, platelet 31000/mm3, total leukocyte counts of 18 500/mm3 (neutrophils – N: 87%, lymphocytes – L: 12%, eosinophils – E: 01%), CRP (C-reactive protein) of 235 mg/l, and D-dimer 3.54 mg/l. An electrocardiogram (ECG) revealed sinus tachycardia with a heart rate of 120 bpm. An echocardiogram was done, which showed two mobile vegetation of 8.2×5.3 mm and 7.4×5.2 mm in size in the septal leaflet of the tricuspid valve with severe eccentric tricuspid regurgitation as shown in Figs 1, 2. The patient was immediately admitted to the coronary care unit for further assessment and management. Peripheral blood smear showed an impression of microcytic hypochromic anemia, thrombocytopenia (with anisocytosis), and neutrophilic leukocytosis. A blood culture report from another healthcare center (where she had been for a few days before presenting to our center) was reported to be positive for Staphylococcus aureus. She was started on i.v. ceftriaxone 2 g twice a day and i.v. vancomycin 1 g twice a day based on the early critical phase protocol of the hospital. The fever subsided on the fifth day after treatment. The patient symptomatically improved over days and was discharged after 4 weeks of in-hospital treatment.

Discussion

IE is the inflammation of the endocardium, the inner lining of the heart, as well as the valves that separate each of the four chambers within the heart. It is primarily caused by bacteria and has a wide array of manifestations and sequelae5. The most common organism isolated from IE is S. aureus, accounting for 30% of total cases. Furthermore, streptococci species and commensals of the oropharynx, like the HACEK (Haemophilus, Aggregatibacter (formerly Actinobacillus), Cardiobacterium, Eikenella, Kingella) group of organisms, also cause IE but less frequently6. Right-sided IE is an uncommon phenomenon, representing only 5–10% of total cases of IE, which itself is a rare disease. In most instances, right-sided IE mainly affects persons indulging in i.v. drug abuse, patients with pacemakers, and patients with central venous catheters7. However, this does not imply that it exclusively affects this population group, as on rare occasions, gynecological infections such as septic abortion and puerperal sepsis can also lead to right-sided IE, but this prospect has been underlooked and not been researched and published extensively. Septic abortion may be complicated by IE in ~1 in one million cases8.

Abortion is a common procedure globally as 6 out of 10 unintended pregnancies and 3 out of 10 of all pregnancies end in induced abortion. However, global estimates show that 45% of all abortions are unsafe, and unsafe abortion is increasingly concentrated in developing countries and among vulnerable or marginalized populations9. Each year, 4.7–13.2% of maternal mortality occurs due to unsafe abortion among women of reproductive-age group. An estimated 30 deaths occur for every 100 000 unsafe abortions in developed countries compared to 220 deaths for every 100 000 unsafe abortions in developing countries10,11.

In this report, we presented an otherwise healthy female without any association known risk factors of right-sided IE sparing history of dilation and curettage days prior to the onset of symptoms. Although modified Duke criteria are an important assessment tool for diagnosis, the paucity of the classic presentation can make the diagnosis of IE challenging, especially in right-sided IE12. So, as in other cases of IE, the disease was diagnosed on the basis of clinical, echocardiographic, and blood culture findings. The patient had developed prolonged high-grade fever, cough, dyspnea, tachycardia, bilateral lower lobe infiltration on chest X-ray along with positive blood cultures for S. aureus, notably one of the typical organisms causing IE and vegetation over the tricuspid valve on the echocardiography following dilation and curettage, is highly suggestive of right-sided IE7,13.

Dilation and curettage seemingly have played a pivotal role in the development of right-sided IE as the infection can get access to the venous system via pelvic veins after the gynecological procedure and subsequently to the right side of the heart and occurrence after a few days following the procedure in an otherwise healthy person without any established risk factor for right-sided IE, making the correlation between cause and effect more likely. American Heart Association does not recommend antibiotic prophylaxis in women undergoing a surgical abortion, nonetheless, if there is underlying valvular heart disease, previous IE, and congenital heart disease14. However, routine antibiotic prophylaxis for women undergoing first-trimester surgical abortion is supported by a Cochrane review (19 RCTs)15, which showed prophylactic antibiotics for surgical abortion reduced post-abortal infection by an average of 41% compared with placebo.

2019 published National Institute for Health and Care Excellence abortion care guidance (NG140)16 recommends offering antibiotic prophylaxis (3-day course of oral doxycycline) to women having a surgical abortion; however, the committee states that the choice of antibiotic for prophylaxis may vary in different settings based on cost-effectiveness, medication adherence, and local epidemiology of genital tract infections. The subject of our case was not given antibiotics prophylactically before dilation and curettage.

Thus, it is imperative not to overlook the recent history of obstetric and gynecological procedures in a patient presenting with clinical features of right-sided IE. This facilitates prompt diagnosis and aids in necessary treatment that is vital in determining the course, prognosis, and outcome of the disease.

Conclusion

Right-sided IE is an uncommon but life-threatening condition that can occur as a complication of septic surgical procedures. Although it is a rare complication, it is important for healthcare providers and patients to be aware of the risk factors and symptoms associated with this condition. For a successful outcome, early diagnosis and prompt treatment are essential. Appropriate antibiotic prophylaxis and adherence to sterile techniques during a surgical abortion can help to prevent IE. Early intervention and proper precautions can minimize the incidence and impact of IE caused by surgery.
