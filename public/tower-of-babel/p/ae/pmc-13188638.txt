High-risk anatomical features: a new perspective on refined echocardiographic diagnosis of anomalous coronary artery origin from the opposite sinus
Authors: Huidi Zhou, Shiying Li, Lin He, Jing Zhang, Lingyun Fang, Yixia Lin, Nan Cui, Jiayi Sun, Jing Wang, Li Zhang, Mingxing Xie, Yali Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe aim of this study was to explore the value of echocardiography in diagnosing anomalous origin of the coronary artery from the opposite sinus (ACAOS) and to summarize the high-risk anatomical features associated with intramural and non-intramural courses.Materials and methodsA retrospective analysis was performed on echocardiograms of 24 patients with surgically confirmed ACAOS (median age 23.5 years; 12 males and 12 females) at Wuhan Union Hospital between May 2021 and January 2025. Patients were divided into intramural (n = 11) and non-intramural (n = 13) course subgroups. Parameters including take-off angle, thickness between the coronary artery and aortic intima, and proximal segment diameter were compared.ResultsIntramural courses were identified using “intra-ring sign,” and “thin-wall sign,” while non-intramural courses were characterized by “sandwich sign” and “extra-ring sign.” The initial recognition of intramural segments was 36.36%, leading to an overall diagnostic accuracy of 62.50%. After specialized training, diagnostic accuracy improved significantly to 87.50% (p = 0.031). Comparative analysis showed that the non-intramural subgroup had significantly greater intima-to-intima wall thickness (2.63 ± 0.13 mm vs. median 1.03 mm, p < 0.001) and larger take-off angle (34.51 ± 1.24° vs. 27.76 ± 0.58°, p = 0.025), while proximal segment diameter showed no significant difference (p = 0.119).ConclusionThis study proposes specific echocardiographic signs for diagnosing high-risk anatomical features in ACAOS. Specialized training significantly improves the detection rate of malignant coronary courses, enhancing the clinical utility of echocardiography.Keywords: ACAOS, Coronary artery from the opposite sinus, High-risk anatomical, Intramural course

Introduction

Anomalous coronary artery origin from the opposite sinus (ACAOS) is recognized as the one of the leading causes of sudden cardiac death in youth and young athletes [1–3]. ACAOS can be categorized according to the course of the anomalous coronary artery, including inter-arterial, pre-pulmonic, retro-aortic, sub-pulmonic, and retrocardiac courses [4]. Among these, ACAOS with an inter-arterial course is generally regarded as potentially malignant, particularly when accompanied by high-risk anatomical features such as an intramural segment, a slit-like ostium, an acute take-off angle, or proximal coronary narrowing [5–7]. In contrast, ACAOS with non-inter-arterial courses and without high-risk anatomic features is usually considered benign. Moreover, accurate identification of high-risk anatomical features is crucial for appropriate risk stratification and clinical decision-making in patients with ACAOS. Importantly, inter-arterial courses with and without an intramural segment may require different surgical approaches, making accurate anatomical differentiation clinically important [8].

To address this critical need for accurate differentiation, various imaging modalities have been employed in clinical practice. Coronary computed tomography angiography (CTA) is the first-line imaging modality in most centers for visualizing the origin of ACAOS in adult patients [9, 10]. A previous study summarized the features about the intramural course of ACAOS on CTA [11]. However, despite its role as the initial diagnostic method for ACAOS [12], there is a lack of systematic research on characterizing high-risk features of ACAOS via transthoracic echocardiography (TTE). There are only scattered case reports describing the intramural and non-intramural courses using TTE [7, 13]. With recent advancements in high-resolution TTE technology, precise echocardiographic diagnosis of these subtypes has become feasible [14]. Building on previous case reports, this study conducted a retrospective analysis of patients from our institution to systematically summarize the specific echocardiographic signs of these two subtypes, particularly focusing on high-risk anatomical features.

Materials and methods

In the present study, the data of 24 patients diagnosed with ACAOS from May 2021 to January 2025 were reviewed. This retrospective study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Wuhan Union Hospital and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was obtained from all patients or their responsible caregivers before enrollment.

Patients

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) diagnosis of ACAOS confirmed by surgery. (2) A complete set of ultrasound images clearly demonstrate the anomalous origin of the coronary artery. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) poor echocardiographic image quality affecting subsequent image data analysis.

Echocardiography

All patients underwent a comprehensive echocardiographic examination performed with ultrasound systems, including the GE LOGIQ E9, GE Vivid E95, and Philips EPIQ 7 C. TTE scans were performed with a 2-7.5 MHz probe.

All patients had ACAOS confirmed intraoperatively and underwent surgery based on institutional practice for high-risk anatomy and/or clinical symptoms, representing a highly selected surgical cohort. Clinical presentation included exertional chest pain, syncope, or incidental findings during cardiac evaluation. Coronary CT angiography was used as part of routine preoperative imaging to further delineate coronary anatomy.

Evaluation of submitted echocardiographic images

For the echocardiographic assessment, we focused on the critical anatomical features influencing clinical management and surgical strategy in ACAOS patients. The standardized assessment protocol included four key morphological parameters: (1) spatial relationship between the coronary ostium and the sinotubular junction; (2) determination of coronary pathoanatomy including non-intramural or intramural course; (3) comprehensive ostial characterization including coronary ostial angle (Fig. 1A) and the internal diameter of anomalous coronary ostium and the space thickness between the coronary artery and the aortic wall (Fig. 1B). (4) assessment of ostial stenosis in anomalous coronary arteries using spectral Doppler echocardiography (Fig. 1C). In addition, during the analysis of image features, the TTE images of all enrolled cases were fully reviewed by at least 2 experienced sonographers.

(A) The take-off angle is defined as the angle between the aortic intimal tangent (yellow line, inner edge of the aortic wall) and the central axis of the coronary ostium (red line). (B) The intima-to-intima thickness (double white arrows) is defined as the perpendicular distance between the aortic intima and the coronary intima. (C) Ostial stenosis assessment using spectral Doppler echocardiography

When performing coronary TTE, due to the limitations of the patients’ acoustic windows, our focus was primarily on the ostium and the initial segment of the coronary arteries. The key planes included the parasternal aortic valve short-axis view and the parasternal long-axis view of the left ventricle (rotate the probe to clearly visualize the ostium of the coronary artery). The multimodal images and surgical photograph from a patient are shown in Fig. 2. After learning the novel ultrasound features, a blinded sonographer reevaluated the submitted echocardiographic images to assess inter-observer variability.

Patients were divided into two subgroups for further analysis, including inter-arterial ACAOS with an intramural segment and inter-arterial ACAOS without an intramural segment.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) for continuous data that follow a normal distribution, while for non - normally distributed continuous data, the median with range is presented, or frequency and percentage where appropriate. Independent samples t-test or Mann-Whitney U test was used to compare the thickness between the coronary artery intima and the aortic intima, the take-off angle and the diameter of anomalous coronary ostial between intramural and non-intramural subgroups, depending on whether the data followed a normal distribution. A paired McNemar’s test was used to compare the difference of diagnostic accuracy between pre- and post-learning. Inter-observer reproducibility for quantitative measurements (wall thickness and take-off angle) was assessed using the Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC) based on a two-way mixed model with absolute agreement. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS statistics software or R software (version 4.3.3; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) (version 26.0).

Echocardiographic characteristics in subgroups

In the present study, the thickness of the space between the coronary artery intima and the aortic intima was 2.63 ± 0.13 mm in the non-intramural subgroup and 1.03 mm (25%-75% IQR, 1.01–1.07 mm) in the intramural subgroup. And the space thickness between the coronary artery and the aorta was significantly greater in the non-intramural subgroup than in the intramural subgroup (p < 0.001). The non-intramural subgroup exhibited a higher take-off angle (34.51 ± 1.2°) than the intramural subgroup (27.76 ± 0.58°), and the difference was statistically significant (p = 0.025). Inter-observer reproducibility was assessed for both wall thickness and take-off angle measurements. For thickness between the coronary artery and the aortic, the agreement was excellent, with an ICC of 0.947. Bland-Altman analysis (Fig. 3) demonstrated a mean bias of 0.02 mm, with 95% limits of agreement ranging from − 0.51 to 0.55 mm. Similarly, the take-off angle measurements showed high consistency between observers (ICC = 0.956). The Bland-Altman plot revealed a negligible mean bias of 0.13°, with narrow limits of agreement (-2.83° to 3.10°). These results confirm that the quantitative measurement protocol is highly reproducible.

Bland-Altman plots assessing inter-observer reproducibility. (A) Graphs display the agreement for (A) thickness between the coronary artery and aortic (B) take-off angle. The blue solid line represents the mean bias, and the red dashed lines represent the 95% limits of agreement

Regarding the diameter of anomalous coronary ostial, there was no statistically difference between the intramural and non-intramural subgroup (median 1.56 mm [IQR 1.30–1.86 mm] vs. 1.90 ± 0.15 mm; p = 0.119). The intramural subgroup showed a higher ostial peak velocity (60.45 ± 5.62 cm/s) compared to the non-intramural subgroup (44.47 ± 3.40 cm/s). However, the difference did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.240). Anatomically, the incidence of ostial stenosis was greater in the intramural group (72.73%, 8/11) than in the non-intramural subgroup (38.46%, 5/13), a difference that did not show a statistical significance (p = 0.089).

High-risk anatomical echocardiographic signs in ACAOS

The “intra-ring sign” (Fig. 4) refers to a characteristic cross-sectional feature defined by spatial mismatch between the entry and exit points of an anomalous coronary artery through the aortic wall. This geometric discordance produces a fragmented ring-shaped morphology on short-axis ultrasound views. In contrast, the “extra-ring sign” (Fig. 4) is defined as an anomalous coronary artery maintaining full-thickness continuity with the aortic wall, exhibiting identical entry-exit orientations. This manifests as a concentric ring structure on short-axis ultrasound, serving as a key differentiator from intramural coronary anomalies.

Sketches illustrating the echocardiographic features associated with ACAOS. (A) “Intra- ring sign”: the coronary intima (red) is located within the aortic annulus; “thin-wall sign”: the origin of the anomalous coronary artery was observed to have a close relationship with the aortic root. (B) “extra-ring sign”: the coronary intima (red) is located outside the aortic annulus

The “thin-wall sign” (Fig. 4) is characterized by the proximal coronary artery and aortic lumen sharing a common thinned vascular wall. This anatomical configuration leads to ultrasonographic obliteration of the normal interface between the aortic wall and coronary origin. The “sandwich sign” refers to an anomalous coronary artery originating between the main pulmonary artery and aorta at the supravalvular level, demonstrating full detachment from both arterial walls. This creates a characteristic layered appearance resembling sandwich fillings on short- axial ultrasound views. The “small take-off angle sign” refers to a small angle formed by the intersection of the aortic intimal tangent line and the central axis of the coronary artery ostium. Ostial stenosis was defined as an abnormal spectral doppler flow velocity ≥ 50 cm/s at the coronary artery origin. Accuracy rate of echocardiographic reports in diagnosing ACAOS

In the present study, accurate classification of the presence or absence of an intramural segment was achieved in only 15 of 24 cases (62.5%) when compared with surgical findings. Among the 11 surgically confirmed intramural cases, intramural anatomy was correctly identified in 4 cases (36.36%), whereas among the 13 non-intramural cases, correct classification was achieved in 11 cases (84.62%). To evaluate the effectiveness of the specific echocardiographic signs for intramural coronary course in ACAOS, the accuracy of a single blinded sonographer was compared before and after learning the high-risk anatomic echocardiographic signs. The comparison of imaging signs between intramural and non-intramural subgroups was showed in Table 1. Significant differences in imaging sign prevalence were observed between the two subgroups. The analysis included 24 cases with confirmed diagnoses. Pre-learning accuracy was 62.50% (15/24), with a misdiagnosis rate of 37.50% (9/24). After training, accuracy improved to 87.5% (21/24), with a missed diagnosis rate of 12.5% (3/24). A paired McNemar’s test revealed a statistically significant reduction in missed diagnosis rate (p = 0.031).

Discussion

ACAOS is a rare disorder, holding significant clinical importance due to its association with sudden cardiac death [15]. The identification of high-risk anatomical features in ACAOS is crucial to determine management, especially the decision for and the type of surgical procedure [4, 16], such as the coronary unroofing procedure for ACAOS with intramural course. Moreover, according to the 2020 European Society of Cardiology Guidelines for the management of adult congenital heart disease, the presence of high-risk anatomical features plays a central role in determining whether surgical intervention is indicated [17].

Previous studies have emphasized that imaging characteristics such as a slit-like ostium, acute take-off angle, and proximal narrowing have been described as markers of increased risk, particularly in inter-arterial variants [18, 19]. Most prior investigations, however, have focused primarily on coronary CT angiography for anatomical delineation. In contrast, our study highlights transthoracic echocardiography as a modality capable of identifying intramural-related features based on dynamic visualization of the coronary–aortic wall relationship.

Previous echocardiographic studies have demonstrated that ACAOS can be detected using TTE, but they have also reported variable diagnostic performance, especially for identifying intramural coronary courses. Early echocardiographic work focused mainly on demonstrating anomalous coronary origins using color Doppler flow mapping, with limited emphasis on systematic characterization of high-risk anatomical features [20]. More recent imaging studies have highlighted substantial inter-observer variability and inconsistent sensitivity among imagers when evaluating intramural segments, even across advanced imaging modalities [21]. These limitations may be related to the subtle anatomical features of intramural segments, operator-dependent interpretation, and the absence of standardized echocardiographic criteria.

In the present study, a systematic analysis of high-risk anatomical features in a surgically confirmed ACAOS cohort is presented, extending existing echocardiographic research. By directly correlating echocardiographic findings with surgical confirmation, our results demonstrate that specific echocardiographic signs can reflect underlying high-risk coronary anatomy that has previously been described mainly using CTA or surgical inspection [22, 23]. A key contribution of the present study is the systematic description of echocardiographic signs, including the “intra-ring sign” and “thin-wall sign”. While previous studies have illustrated individual echocardiographic appearances of anomalous coronary arteries, these have largely been limited to isolated case reports or small series without standardized definitions [20, 24]. In clinical practice, the decision to proceed with surgery is primarily based on the presence or absence of myocardial ischemia [25]. Previous studies have reported considerable variability and limited accuracy in the noninvasive identification of an intramural course in ACAOS across various imaging modalities [26]. This historical challenge may be attributed to evolving diagnostic criteria and earlier-generation technology. As echocardiography is the preferred imaging examination method for suspected ACAOS patients [27], it is urgent to improve the rate of high-risk anatomical features of ACAOS detection by echocardiography. Based on our clinical experience, this study first systematically summarized the high-risk anatomical characteristics of ACAOS in echocardiography.

Among the proposed signs, the intra-ring or extra-ring sign and thin-wall sign reached statistical significance. After targeted training, the diagnostic performance of echocardiography improved substantially. These findings suggest that echocardiography, aided by intramural course specific signs, may serve as a practical noninvasive approach for evaluating ACAOS and for characterizing intramural morphology relevant to procedural planning. Although conventional wisdom posits the superiority of CTA in diagnosing an intramural coronary course [18], we observed encouraging agreement with surgical findings for intramural segment characterization in this selected cohort after focused training. Moreover, no significant difference was found between the non-intramural and intramural subgroups in the incidence of ostial stenosis or the prevalence of the sandwich sign. It demonstrates that these high-risk features may be distributed across both anatomical subtypes. These findings are consistent with the concept that an intramural course represents a specific subtype of inter-arterial course [28]. Furthermore, ostial stenosis was identified as a pathophysiological feature common to both subtypes.

Our study provided the first echocardiographic quantification of the thickness between the coronary artery and aortic intima in patients with ACAOS. According to the present study, the space between the coronary artery and aortic intima is significantly thinner in the intramural subgroup than in the non-intramural subgroup, which establishes a critical imaging marker for diagnosing intramural coronary segments. The observed morphological characteristics are consistent with our defined criteria for the thin-wall sign, as quantified in the imaging analysis. This finding likely reflects the unique anatomy of intramural coronaries, where the vessel is enveloped by the aortic media, resulting in the obliteration of the connective tissue space between the coronary adventitia and aortic intima [29]. Histopathological studies have demonstrated direct fusion of the coronary adventitia with the aortic media in the intramural segments [30], which corresponds closely to our imaging measurements. This objective parameter surpasses the traditionally subjective morphological assessments, potentially explaining the 87.50% diagnostic sensitivity of echocardiography in our cohort.

There was a statistically significant difference in coronary take-off angles between intramural and non-intramural subgroups. This explains why abnormal morphology of the intramural course is more prone to sudden cardiac death. Based on a previous study, in the symptomatic group, pressure was negatively correlated with the acute take-off angle [31].

This study has found that the diameter of the anomalous coronary ostium was not significantly different between intramural and non-intramural subgroups. Previous studies have suggested that ACAOS with an intramural course was associated with sudden cardiac death, due to ischemia and arrhythmias and ostial hemodynamic abnormalities leading to progressive stenosis [32]. In this study, although the median diameter of the intramural subgroup was lower, the sample e limitation may have masked underlying differences.

There were still a few limitations in the present study. First, this study is based on a surgically confirmed subset of ACAOS patients identified through clinical imaging pathways, rather than an unselected imaging population, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Second, although CCTA was part of routine preoperative assessment, the total number of patients undergoing CCTA and the proportion proceeding to surgery were not systematically evaluated. Finally, the performance of these signs in prospective screening settings or unselected populations remains to be validated. Future research can expand the sample size to draw more definitive conclusions.

Conclusion

This study establishes high-risk anatomical echocardiographic criteria for diagnosing ACAOS, improving the detection rate of high-risk anatomical subtypes for ACAOS in clinical work. This work further supports echocardiography not only as a screening modality but also as a valuable tool for anatomical characterization of ACAOS.
