Atomic Accuracy: Unmasking a Colonic Dieulafoy Lesion With a Nuclear Medicine Scan
Authors: Christine B Kwak, Charleston R Powell, Nicole M Harrison
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Obscure gastrointestinal bleeding (OGIB) poses a unique diagnostic challenge as diagnosis may require repeat endoscopy, enteroscopy, video capsule endoscopy, contrasted imaging, or nuclear medicine (NM) scans. Dieulafoy lesions are enlarged submucosal vessels in a background of normal mucosa, typically found in the stomach, occasionally in the small bowel, and rarely in the colon. NM scans, although less common today, are sensitive to even very slow bleeding. We present a case of a critically ill 88-year-old male with persistent, episodic bleeding in whom an NM scan significantly aided in identifying a Dieulafoy lesion in the ascending colon.

Introduction

Obscure gastrointestinal bleeding (OGIB) is gastrointestinal bleeding (GIB) that does not have an identifiable etiology following bidirectional endoscopy [1]. There are two forms of OGIB: overt and occult [1]. Overt OGIB presents with signs of visible bleeding, whereas occult OGIB does not [1]. OGIB comprises roughly 5% of all GIB [2]. Up to 56% of OGIB has an upper gastrointestinal (GI) source, 40% of which is from small bowel angioectasias [1,3]. There are many causes for OGIB, including angioectasias, nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug-induced mucosal injury, hemobilia, Meckel’s diverticulum, inflammatory bowel disease, malignancy, and Dieulafoy lesions [1,3].

A Dieulafoy lesion is a dilated, tortuous submucosal artery that erodes into the intestinal lumen, accounting for roughly 3.5% of all GIB [4]. Most Dieulafoy lesions are localized to the stomach [4]. They are generally found in elderly male patients with medical conditions such as cardiovascular disease and diabetes [4]. Additional risk factors include the use of anticoagulation and antiplatelet medications and alcohol [4,5]. Dieulafoy lesions can be diagnostically challenging due to their intermittent and excessive bleeding and small size, which obscure detection [4]. These factors lead to a miss rate of around 50% [4]. The probability of diagnostic detection increases by more than 70% of patients if the first endoscopy is completed within the first 12 hours of bleeding [4].

The initial approach to OGIB is commonly repeated endoscopic evaluation guided by clinical features [1,3]. If this is unrevealing, enteroscopy, video capsule endoscopy (VCE), computed tomography angiography (CTA), provoked angiography, or a nuclear medicine (NM) scan may be utilized [1,3]. These modalities have varying sensitivities and specificities, and not all methods allow for intervention to be done at the time of diagnosis. Of these modalities, NM scans have had a dwindling role [6]. Currently, of the American College of Gastroenterology guidelines for management of upper and lower GIB, only the guideline for management of lower GIB discusses the limited role of NM scans, recommending that these be used in the following situations: lack of availability of CTA or contraindication due to risk for contrast-induced nephropathy [6]. In contrast, the American Society for Gastrointestinal Endoscopy mentions the utility of NM scans in situations of overt OGIB with a slow bleeding rate of 0.1 to 0.4 ml/minute and in actively bleeding patients with no identifiable source of EGD or colonoscopy to provide localization of bleeding, which can be confirmed with additional endoscopic, imaging, or surgical methods [1].

We present a case of a critically ill patient with intermittent bleeding in whom a Dieulafoy lesion was identified only after using an NM scan. This case was previously presented as a poster presentation and published as a meeting abstract at the American College of Gastroenterology’s Annual Scientific Meeting on October 29, 2024, and presented as a poster presentation at the 2024 American College of Physicians Maryland Mulholland Residents Meeting on May 1, 2024.

Case presentation

An 88-year-old male with a history of atrial fibrillation on apixaban, congestive heart failure, severe pulmonary hypertension, complete heart block status-post pacemaker implantation, chronic anemia, and thrombocytopenia, with a recent visit to the emergency department for epistaxis, presented for dizziness and fatigue followed by large-volume melenic stools. The patient was admitted to the intensive care unit. Apixaban was held, and he was given packed red blood cells (pRBCs), prothrombin complex concentrate (PCC), intravenous fluids (IVF), and pantoprazole. Although he had no formal diagnosis of cirrhosis, his prior imaging studies showed a nodular contour of the liver paired with his unexplained thrombocytopenia, raising suspicion for variceal hemorrhage, so octreotide and ceftriaxone were also administered.

On evaluation, he had bright red blood per rectum. Once stabilized, an esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD) was performed, which revealed a small blood clot in the oropharynx and a 1 cm subepithelial lesion without overlying mucosal abnormality, such as ulceration or adherent clot to suggest recent bleeding, on the greater curvature of the gastric body. There was no prior EGD available for comparison. Given the concern for possible cirrhosis mentioned on prior imaging, an ultrasound was obtained, which showed no evidence for chronic structural or vascular liver disease. Ceftriaxone and octreotide were discontinued.

Given the blood clot in the oropharynx and the patient's recent episode of epistaxis, which prompted an emergency department visit and required treatment with nasal cauterization, Otolaryngology was consulted. On evaluation, the patient had no epistaxis. Flexible fiberoptic laryngoscopy was normal. Later that evening, while preparing for a colonoscopy, the patient developed low-volume epistaxis that resolved with oxymetazoline.

The patient continued to experience hematochezia with continued transfusion requirements. A CTA of the abdomen and pelvis was negative for the source of GIB. After an adequate bowel preparation, bidirectional endoscopy with placement of VCE showed mostly dark red blood products in the colon and the presence of large internal hemorrhoids. Colonic views were partially obscured by partially degraded blood products, but no evidence for active bleeding was seen.

While examining the ileocecal valve, bright red blood was seen to move into the colon. The colonoscope's looping to reach the cecum caused discomfort for the patient, resulting in small boluses of epinephrine and propofol. The ileocecal valve was not intubated due to procedural intolerance. VCE demonstrated small fragments throughout the small bowel and dark blood throughout the colon, but was otherwise normal. Then, the patient developed recurrent epistaxis. Otolaryngology was reconsulted and performed a repeat flexible fiberoptic laryngoscopy, which was notable for a vessel in the anterior inferior septum. Upon application of a hemostatic dressing and oxymetazoline, the epistaxis resolved. However, hematochezia continued. Subsequently, a repeat CTA was performed and was unremarkable. Then, the patient had episodes of both melena and hematochezia. An NM scan was performed, which localized the bleed to the right lower quadrant in the mid-abdomen (Figure 1).

After an excellent bowel preparation, repeat colonoscopy with focused examination and thorough lavage revealed a slowly oozing Dieulafoy lesion in the ascending colon (Figure 2), which was successfully treated with epinephrine and hemostatic clip and tattooed (Figure 3). An additional clip was placed on the opposite wall for localization in the event the patient re-bleeds and requires Interventional Radiology embolization. Although the patient was hemodynamically unstable and required propofol, he tolerated the procedure well.

Following the intervention, the patient had no further overt bleeding. His pantoprazole was soon discontinued, and his apixaban was restarted. Hemodynamic and laboratory parameters remained stable overall, and he was subsequently discharged. In total, he received 11 units of pRBCs during his hospitalization.

At the patient's two-week post-hospitalization primary care appointment, he was doing well with a hemoglobin of 8.1 g/dL and no further episodes of GIB. At his one-month post-hospitalization follow-up at the Gastroenterology clinic, the patient continued to be well with complete resolution of his GIB. Further workup for SEL was offered, but deferred by the patient, given overall life expectancy and the impression that this was an incidental finding, unrelated to his bleed.

Discussion

This case of OGIB demonstrates the utility of an infrequently used imaging modality to locate a subtle endoscopic finding in an unusual location. Approximately 71-75% of Dieulafoy lesions occur in the stomach, followed by 15% in the duodenum [4,7]. Of the remaining possible locations, only 2% are colonic [4,7]. Diagnosis often relies on direct visualization of an actively bleeding Dieulafoy lesion, given its characteristic lack of surrounding mucosal abnormalities. Endoscopic treatment is very successful using traditional hemostatic methods such as thermal-electrocoagulation, heat probe coagulation, argon plasma coagulation, epinephrine injection, and hemostatic clip application [4,7]. Image-based options can provide information on the location of the bleed to focus endoscopic evaluation. Both CTA and NM scans have a sensitivity of greater than 80% for acute GIB [8]. Advantages of CTA include better specificity, 95% compared to NM scan’s 84% [8], and localization to guide potential therapeutic angiography. Timeliness is key to successful imaging of GIB [3], and CTAs can be performed within minutes compared to the hours needed for NM scans. For these reasons, CTA is the preferred imaging modality for the detection of an acute GIB. The biggest advantage of NM scans is their ability to identify slower bleeds. They can detect GIB as slow as 0.05 mL/min compared to CTA’s threshold of 0.5 mL/min [3,8]. NM scans have the significant disadvantage of providing almost no information on organs of interest, except for a general location of a bleed, only when the study is positive. In this instance, the sensitivity for a slow GIB was critical to localizing the source of this patient’s recurrent GIB from a lesion difficult to visualize and reducing the exposure to invasive procedures for a high-risk patient.

Although our patient had multiple risk factors, the Dieulafoy lesion was initially missed largely due to evidence of bleeding from multiple sources, inadequate visualization from the residual blood products, and possibly the quality of the initial bowel preparation. Evidence of bleeding from multiple sources presented a significant diagnostic challenge, as it was not initially clear whether the source primarily originated from the GI tract or the nasopharynx. The initial EGD finding with a clot in the oropharynx and the patient's epistaxis served as a red herring for an oropharyngeal or nasopharyngeal etiology. This case also serves as a reminder of the significant role of bowel preparation in increasing the probability of identifying lesions. Although the initial bowel preparation was adequate, subtle findings were obscured by residual blood. A more aggressive initial bowel preparation may have aided in cleansing the old products for a clearer view. In the second bowel preparation for the repeat colonoscopy, the patient required multiple encouragements from staff to complete his bowel preparation. Use of octreotide may have also had angiospastic effects, further masking the bleed.

Conclusions

OGIB from a colonic source, especially from a Dieulafoy lesion, is rare. This case was particularly difficult given: the changing clinical presentation of GIB, evidence of bleeding from multiple locations, and the patient’s significant medical comorbidities and risk factors. The changing clinical presentation and evidence of bleeding from multiple locations created uncertainty as to where to focus the diagnostic work up. Additionally, the inadequate visualization from residual products and possibly the quality of the initial bowel preparation led to failure of endoscopic detection. This case serves as a reminder of the significant role of bowel preparation in increasing the probability of identifying lesions which can be easily overlooked by both clinicians and patients. Fortunately, by localizing the site of bleeding, the NM bleeding scan allowed a focused search which led to identification of a subtle endoscopic finding. This case illustrates well that NM scans have a role in detecting elusive sources of OGIB.
