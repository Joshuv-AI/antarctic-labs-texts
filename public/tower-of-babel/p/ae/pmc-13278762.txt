The impact of medication management interventions on healthcare utilisation in people with dementia: a systematic review with meta-analysis
Authors: Jiefei Yu, Danijela Gnjidic, Xenia Nastatos, Mouna Sawan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMedication management interventions are designed to reduce medication-related harms in older people with dementia; however, their effect on healthcare utilisation remains unclear.ObjectiveTo summarise the impact of medication management interventions on healthcare utilisation in people with dementia.Data sourcesEmbase, MEDLINE, Web of Science, Cochrane CENTRAL Register of Controlled Trials, CINAHL, PsycINFO.Study selectionInterventional studies assessing the impact of medication management interventions on healthcare utilisation in people with dementia aged ≥65.Data extraction and synthesisEligible randomised controlled trials (RCTs) were meta-analysed with risk ratios (RR), 95% confidence intervals (CIs) using a fixed-effect model. Introduction

People with dementia experience progressive and irreversible cognitive impairment, which affects their ability to perform activities of daily living [1]. In addition, they often present with two to eight additional comorbidities compared to people without dementia [2]. Managing these coexisting conditions adds complexity to dementia care and contributes to high health service utilisation [1]. Hospital admission rates in people with dementia are 1.42 times higher than those without dementia [3], with 518 hospitalisations per 1000 in the USA compared with 234 per 1000 [4]. In Australia, dementia accounts for one out of every 58 hospitalisations [5], and in the UK, ~25% of hospital beds are occupied by people with dementia [6]. While appropriate use of healthcare services is beneficial to patient outcomes, people with dementia remain at high risk of unplanned and potentially avoidable healthcare utilisation [7]. The incidence of preventable hospitalisations and emergency admissions continues to rise annually in people with dementia [7], contributing to high economic costs and placing a heavy burden on the healthcare system [8]. A previous review estimated that 40% of hospitalisations in people with dementia are avoidable, and a 25% reduction in preventable hospitalisations in this population could save over $2 billion per year [7].

Medication-related harm is the main contributing factor to 15%–22% unplanned hospital admissions and general practice visits in people with dementia [9, 10]. People with dementia are more likely to be exposed to polypharmacy (routine use of five or more medications) than those without dementia [11], and often struggle with managing medications, ensuring correct administration, adherence and monitoring for adverse drug reactions [12]. People with dementia and their carers (e.g. spouse, partner, family and friend) are provided limited support from health care professionals in medication management and involvement in decision-making which leads to significant strain [13]. Optimised medication management, defined as the process underlying the use of medications (including initiation, supply, administration, review, monitoring and cessation), is a key approach to minimising drug-related harm and improving quality of care [14, 15]. However, inadequate and fragmented communication between healthcare professionals, people with dementia and their carers can occur across various healthcare settings [16], resulting in medication-related harm and potentially avoidable use of healthcare services [17, 18]. Therefore, there is a need for medication management interventions that address the specific needs of people with dementia to reduce ineffective healthcare utilisation.

The World Health Organisation (WHO) has listed reducing avoidable hospitalisation and improving healthcare services for people with dementia as a high healthcare priority [1]. Several reviews have examined the effectiveness of chronic disease management interventions on healthcare utilisation outcomes in older adults with or without dementia [19, 20]. To date, only a few studies have tested interventions to optimise medication management in people with dementia and associated healthcare utilisation outcomes have not been systematically summarised [14]. Therefore, our systematic review aimed to summarise the impact of medication management interventions on healthcare utilisation in people with dementia, and to identify key aspects of medication management targeted in the interventions.

Methods

This systematic review was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) (Figure 1, See Appendix 1 in the Supplementary data for checklist) [21] and registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO CRD420251030113).

PRISMA flow chart showing the systematic review process with four stages: Identification, Screening, Eligibility, and Inclusion.

Search strategy and selection criteria

A consultation with a librarian from the University of Sydney was undertaken to finalise the search terms. Subsequently, searches were performed across six electronic databases (Embase [Ovid], MEDLINE [Ovid], Web of Science [Clarivate], Cochrane CENTRAL Register of Controlled Trials [Ovid], CINAHL [Ebsco], PsycINFO [Ovid]) from inception until 16 March 2026. A comprehensive description of the full search strategy was included as supplementary material for reference (See Appendix 2 in the Supplementary data for search strategies). Studies were exported from the databases and subsequently imported into Covidence systematic review software (Veritas Health Innovation, Melbourne, Australia) and duplicates were removed. Conference abstracts, reviews and protocols were excluded.

All interventional studies were included in this review, comprising randomised controlled trials (RCTs) as well as non-randomised studies (i.e. non-randomised controlled trials (non-RCTs) and before and after comparisons). Older people are commonly underrepresented in clinical trials, and as such, to provide a comprehensive review of the current evidence available, we included non-randomised studies [22]. The Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcome (PICO) model was used alongside search terms to ensure the studies exported were relevant to the research question. For this review, people ≥65 years old with a diagnosis of all types of dementia (Population) were included. Interventions of medication management delivered to healthcare professionals, people with dementia and/or their carers in any healthcare settings (Intervention) considered eligible included those involving medication supply, selection, administration, adherence, monitoring, review, reconciliation, deprescribing/cessation and patient-centred care. The population without medication management interventions or treated with normal care was considered as comparative (Comparison). Any healthcare utilisation outcomes (Outcome) were reported as defined by the authors. Studies that explored the indirect impact of an intervention on healthcare outcomes were excluded. Non-English language studies and non-human studies were also excluded.

Screening and data extraction

Two reviewers (J.Y., X.N.) independently screened abstracts and titles. Any conflicts were resolved by discussion between the two reviewers (J.Y., X.N.). Eligible studies with full text were then screened independently by two reviewers (J.Y., X.N.) and any conflicts were resolved by a third reviewer (M.S.).

Data extraction of the eligible studies was independently conducted by two reviewers (J.Y., X.N.). The details were recorded from each study, including first author, year of publication, location, settings, sample size, description of the intervention, results and outcomes of healthcare utilisation such as hospitalisation, emergency department visits, primary care and residential aged care home admission. Any discrepancies were resolved by discussion with all reviewers.

Interventions were categorised based on the intervention target (e.g. physicians, multidisciplinary team). Cochrane Effective Practice and Organisation of Care (EPOC) [23] was used to categorise the components involved in interventions. Key aspects of interventions were defined as aspects involved in medication management, including supply, selection, administration, adherence, review, reconciliation, monitoring and deprescribing of medications [15].

Quality assessment

Quality assessments were completed for all eligible studies using the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool (MMAT) by two reviewers (J.Y., X.N.) independently [24]. Methodological quality criteria 2 was applied to any RCTs and criteria 3 was applied to any non-randomised studies [24]. Any disagreements were resolved by discussion with all authors.

Statistical analysis

Healthcare utilisation outcomes were assessed and pooled for meta-analysis via fixed effect methods using Review Manager (RevMan) version 5.4 (The Cochrane Collaboration, The Nordic Cochrane Centre, Copenhagen, Denmark). The risk ratios (RR) for dichotomous outcomes were calculated, and the 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported. The heterogeneity of outcomes was measured by I2. The P-value less than .05 was considered statistically significant. Included studies that could not be pooled due to heterogeneity in outcome reporting were synthesised narratively.

Results

A total of 14 945 studies were identified, and after removal of duplicates, 12 438 studies were screened for eligibility with 40 potentially eligible full-text articles identified. Of these studies, 12 articles were included in the systematic review (Fig. 1), including seven RCTs and five non-randomised studies (Table 1). The 12 studies included were from Australia (n = 3), Sweden (n = 3), Taiwan (n = 1), and the USA (n = 5) and were conducted in the primary care [26–28, 31, 35, 36], hospital [32–35], and residential aged care homes (also known as long-term care, residential aged care facilities and nursing homes) settings [25, 28–31].

Medication management interventions

Of the 12 studies, seven tested physician-based interventions, and five tested multidisciplinary team-based interventions. Three common types of physician-based interventions were identified among RCTs and non-randomised studies, including physician education, physician and patient/carer targeted education and physician targeted prescribing reminders. The most reported key aspects of medication management targeted in these studies were deprescribing (n = 7), reconciliation (n = 5) and monitoring (n = 4) (see Appendix 3 in the Supplementary Data).

Physician education interventions focused on peer education sessions with brief follow-up meetings and knowledge evaluation regarding psychotropic medication [25, 29], as well as distribution of educational materials, including deprescribing guidelines and tapering protocols [27, 30, 31]. Furthermore, two RCTs supplemented physician education with additional patient/carer targeted education by providing information sheets outlining the indication of the medication, potential side effects and rationale for deprescribing high-risk medications, including antipsychotics, sedatives and anticholinergics to the patients [26, 27]. One RCT evaluated the impact of sending overprescribing warning letters to primary care physicians with a high prescribing rate of quetiapine compared to peers, and assessed subsequent prescribing patterns and patient clinical outcomes [28].

Three RCTs [32–34] and two non-randomised studies [35, 36] tested a multidisciplinary team-based intervention in hospital or community settings consisting of physicians, nurses, and pharmacists.

Effects of medication management interventions in randomised clinical trial studies

Hospitalisation

All-cause hospitalisation was evaluated in all seven RCTs [25–28, 32–34]. Three RCTs [26, 27, 33] were included for meta-analysis. Medication management interventions were not found to have a significant effect on all-cause hospitalisation (13 739 residents, RR = 0.94, 95% CI [0.88–1.01], P = .25, I2 = 8%).

Subgroup analysis based on intervention type (Figure 2a) showed that physician and patient/carer targeted education was associated with a significant reduction in all-cause hospitalisation (9973 residents, RR = 0.92, 95% CI [0.84–0.99], P = .04) [26, 27]. In comparison, no significant effect on all-cause hospitalisation was observed in trials testing physician education (6926 residents, RR = 1.01, 95% CI [0.90–1.15], P = .83) [27] or multidisciplinary team-based interventions (303 residents, RR = 0.80, 95% CI [0.57–1.10], P = .17) [32].

a: A meta-analysis of the effect of interventions on all-cause hospitalisation. b: A meta-analysis of the effect of interventions on all-cause emergency department visits. c: A meta-analysis of the effect of interventions on residential aged care home admissions.

An additional three studies could not be pooled due to substantial heterogeneity in outcome reporting. Physician targeted prescribing reminders showed no effect on all-cause hospitalisation in participants from residential aged care homes (95% CI [−0.7–0.1], P = .19) or community (95% CI [−0.3–0.3], P = .81) [28]. In contrast, physician education significantly reduced all-cause hospitalisation over 12 months (Incidence Rate Ratios = 0.2, 95% CI [0.05–0.77]) [25]. Multidisciplinary team-based intervention showed a lower risk of drug-related hospital readmission over 180 days (Hazard Ratio = 0.49, 95% CI [0.27–0.90], P = .02) [32–34].

Emergency department visits

All-cause emergency department visits were reported in five RCTs [25–28, 32]. Two RCTs were included for meta-analysis. Medication management interventions were not found to have a significant effect on all-cause emergency department visits (13 436 residents, RR = 0.95, 95% CI [0.90–0.99], P = .02, I2 = 52%) [26, 27].

Subgroup analysis based on intervention type (Figure 2b) showed that physician and patient/carer targeted education was associated with a significant reduction in all-cause emergency department visits (9973 residents, RR = 0.92, 95% CI [0.86–0.98], P = .007) [26, 27]. In comparison, trials evaluating physician education showed no significant effect on all-cause emergency department visit (6926 residents, RR = 0.98, 95% CI [0.92–1.05], P = .57) [27].

An additional two studies could not be pooled due to substantial heterogeneity in outcome reporting. Physician targeted prescribing reminders had no effect on all-cause emergency department visit was observed in participants from nursing homes (95% CI [−0.3–0.8], P = .31) or community (95% CI [−0.2–0.4], P = .54) [28]. In contrast, physician education significantly reduced all-cause emergency department visits over 12 months (Incidence Rate Ratios = 0.04, 95% CI [0.01–0.13]) [25].

Residential aged care homes admissions

Admissions to residential aged care homes were reported in three RCTs [27, 28, 33]. Two RCTs were included for meta-analysis. Medication management interventions were not found to have a significant effect on all-cause residential aged care homes admissions (10 728 residents, RR = 1.03, 95% CI [0.91–1.16], P = .65, I2 = 0%) [27, 33].

Subgroup analysis based on intervention type (Figure 2c) did not show significant effect on residential aged care homes admissions in trials testing multidisciplinary team-based interventions (304 residents, RR = 0.77, 95% CI [0.43–1.37], P = .37) [33], physician education (6926 residents, RR = 1.02, 95% CI [0.86–1.21], P = .81) [27] or physician and patient/carer targeted education (6961 residents, RR = 1.06, 95% CI [0.89–1.26], P = .49) [27].

An additional study could not be pooled due to substantial heterogeneity in outcome reporting. Physician targeted prescribing reminders had no effect on all-cause residential aged care homes admissions was observed in physician targeted prescribing reminders (95% CI [−0.2–0.1], P = .68) [28].

Effects of medication management interventions in non-randomised studies

Non-randomised studies of physician education and multidisciplinary team-based interventions showed mixed results, with no significant effect [29, 31, 35], reduction (25.2% annual decrease, P = .005) [36] or an unexpected increase (0.7% increased risk in the post-intervention group) [30] in all-cause hospitalisation. Results for all-cause emergency department visits were similarly inconsistent, with one study reporting no effect (P = .3) [35] and another study reporting a reduction (24% annual decrease, P = .006) [36]. Two studies [35, 36] investigated the multidisciplinary team-based interventions found no significant difference in primary care visits between intervention and control groups.

Quality assessment

The quality assessment was performed using MMAT (Table 2). Among the seven RCTs, six were identified as high to very high quality [26, 28, 31–34]. One study was categorised as moderate quality [25]. The potential issue was related to the baseline difference between groups and blinding. Among the five non-randomised studies, two were identified as high to very high quality [35, 36]. Three studies were categorised as moderate quality [29–31]. The potential issues were related to inappropriate measurement, incomplete outcome data, confounders and unintended administration of intervention. No studies were excluded due to risk of bias.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first systematic review to summarise the impact of medication management interventions on healthcare utilisation outcomes in people with dementia. Of the four intervention types we identified, physician and patient/carer targeted education focusing mainly on deprescribing were effective in reducing all-cause hospitalisation and emergency department visits, whereas multidisciplinary team-based interventions focusing mainly on medication review were effective in reducing drug-related hospital readmission. We found no evidence that any intervention type was associated with primary care visits or residential aged care home admissions, which may be due to a lack of capturing healthcare utilisation outcomes.

Our review found that physician and patient/carer targeted education can reduce all-cause hospitalisations and emergency department visits. Patient education interventions decrease re-hospitalisation in people with chronic conditions, including dementia [37, 38]. In 2024, the WHO highlighted the need for effective strategies to enhance patient medication literacy [39], such as educational materials delivered via patient portals, therapeutic patient education provided by healthcare professionals and self-management programs [40, 41]. However, the impact of these strategies on people with dementia is under-reported, and needs to be highlighted given this population is more susceptible to medication-related harm than those without dementia due to greater exposure to polypharmacy and high-risk use of medications [13]. Further, there remains a lack of patient education interventions for people with dementia that comprehensively address all aspects of medication management [42]. Lower levels of education and knowledge deficits among people with dementia may contribute to their vulnerability to low medication literacy, increasing the risk of adverse health outcomes, such as medication errors and potentially avoidable hospitalisations [43]. Therefore, future studies should develop more tailored approaches that target all aspects of medication management-related needs to improve medication literacy.

Our findings align with previous studies reporting that physician-based educational interventions have no impact on reducing hospital admissions or other outcomes in older adults with or without dementia from residential aged care homes or primary care [44, 45]. Physician education plays a critical role in the safe and quality use of medication [45]. However, the implementation of education delivered by physicians in routine practice may be influenced by contextual barriers, such as staff attrition, time constraints and high workload [25].

Although studies in our review showed no effect on all-cause hospitalisation, it did reduce drug-related hospitalisations [32–34], suggesting that medication management interventions may mitigate medication-related harm but not the broader disease burden in people with dementia. This highlights the need for multidisciplinary team-based approaches that support physicians to optimise medication management as well as address the wider clinical, cognitive, functional and acute care needs of this population to optimise healthcare utilisation outcomes [35].

Furthermore, the involvement of patients and informal carers (e.g. family, child, spouse, and partner) remains underrepresented in existing interventions. Carers are involved in all aspects of medication management [46]. The involvement and education of patients and carers is essential for improving continuity and quality of care during transition from hospital to home by bridging communication gaps, coordinating medications, screening for safety needs and supporting care planning [47]. Carers often report difficulties navigating healthcare systems and receive limited support and communication from healthcare professionals due to inefficient communication [48]. Therefore, it is important to establish clear policies and guidelines to encourage the involvement of patients and carers in medication management decisions and to support smooth care transitions for people with dementia.

Our review shows there is a lack of evidence with respect to capturing primary care visits or residential aged care home admissions outcomes. Transitions between healthcare settings are common in people with dementia. The patterns of primary care visits and residential aged care home admission may be influenced by factors such as dementia progression and comorbidities [33], making it difficult to measure the impact of interventions on these outcomes. Therefore, future trials are needed to evaluate the effect of medication management interventions and capture broader factors that impact the outcomes.

Strengths and limitations

This systematic review was conducted using a comprehensive search strategy across six databases and following a robust methodology. The included studies were assessed using validated quality assessment tools, ensuring that findings are based on reliable evidence. This review included RCTs with high-quality evidence and low risk of bias. Additionally, non-randomised studies were captured, which, despite the moderate risk of bias, provided valuable insights into developmental impacts within real-world settings.

However, this study has several limitations. Our study only included peer-reviewed, full-text, English-language articles. The results of meta-analysis from RCTs were drawn from a limited number of studies and as such, future studies are required to enhance the strength of the evidence. Additionally, the outcome measures and definitions varied between different studies included in this review. This may limit the comparability across studies and weaken the strength of evidence. Moreover, future studies should consider using a standardised approach to capture healthcare utilisation outcomes, such as the Core Outcome Set (COS), to allow comparison across studies [49]. Finally, our review did not capture interventions where medication review was included as a key component of a broader intervention, such as a comprehensive geriatric assessment. Future research should consider capturing such interventions to elucidate the impact on healthcare utilisation patterns.

Future research and implications

Medication management in people with dementia is complex and challenging. Effective medication management interventions should be multidimensional rather than delivered as isolated components, capturing essential aspects such as comprehensive medication review, multidisciplinary input and patient-centred decision making. This review highlights the need to develop interventions that actively involve people with dementia, their carers and healthcare professionals. While designing interventions to improve medication literacy in people with dementia, future studies should also consider analysing other meaningful outcomes, such as medication literacy, involvement of carers and decision making. Additionally, more robust evidence is needed to inform the effect of practical interventions and care navigation in people with dementia.

Conclusion

This systematic review found mixed impact of medication management interventions on healthcare utilisation outcomes in people with dementia, with physician and patient/carer targeted education showing the most promising effect on reducing all-cause hospitalisation and emergency department visits. More trials are needed to evaluate the impact of medication management interventions on primary care visits and residential aged care home admissions. Future research should develop tailored interventions that address key aspects of medication management to optimise healthcare utilisation in people with dementia.
