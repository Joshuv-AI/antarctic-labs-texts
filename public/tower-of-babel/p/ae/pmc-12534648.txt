Ubiquitin-specific protease 34 serves as a novel prognostic biomarker through correlating with immune responses and cell proliferation in acute myeloid leukemia
Authors: Zhihui Li, Cuijuan Shi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEmerging evidence showed that ubiquitin-specific proteases (USPs) are promising therapeutic targets for hematopoietic malignancies. The roles of USP34 on acute myeloid leukemia (AML) pathogenesis remain completely unknown.MethodsGene expression and prognostic relevance were analyzed utilizing public AML datasets from Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) and The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA), and validated with the Gene Expression Profiling Interactive Analysis (GEPIA), BloodSpot and the human protein atlas (HPA) databases. Systematical analyses are performed to characterize the immune infiltration and biological function of USP34 in AML. MTS, colony formation and EdU assays were also employed to confirm the effect of USP34 on cell proliferation in AML cells.ResultsUSP34 expression was significantly higher in AML compared to its matched normal samples. Higher USP34 expression predicted worse prognosis in AML, and USP34 acted as an independent prognostic predictor for AML patients (HR:1.365, 95%CI:1.032–1.805, p = 0.029). Moreover, the expression of USP34 was associated with several immune cells, including Treg cells, indicating its potential role in modulating immune responses. Finally, functional experiments revealed that USP34 knockdown reduced cell proliferation in Molm-13 and Kasumi cells.ConclusionsUSP34 is an independent prognostic predictor in AML and may contribute to leukemogenesis via intervening immune processes and promoting proliferation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-025-03662-1.Keywords: Acute myeloid leukemia, Ubiquitin-specific protease 34, Prognosis, Protein ubiquitylation, Immune microenvironment, Cell proliferation.

Introduction

Acute myeloid leukemia (AML) is a severe hematologic malignancy derived from the bone marrow precursors of myeloid lineage, characterized by uncontrolled proliferation and a differentiation blockade of leukemic blasts [1, 2]. Despite significant therapeutic advances that have improved prognosis for some AML patients, overall outcomes remain poor with a high incidence of relapse [3]. AML is a genetically complex disease with numerous unknown drivers and the pathologic molecular mechanisms have not been fully elucidated. Therefore, it is imperative to identify novel discerning biomarkers for AML to improve understanding of molecular basis, which may optimize the diagnostic stratification, diagnosis, and facilitate the development of targeted therapies for AML.

Post-translational modifications (PTMs) are central to numerous regulatory processes in eukaryotic cells. Protein ubiquitylation, one of the most abundant and vital PTMs, is involved in numerous physiological processes [4, 5]. Dysregulation of ubiquitination contributes to the pathogenesis of various human diseases [4]. Recently, there has been considerable progress in developing the ubiquitylation machinery as therapeutic targets [6, 7]. Protein ubiquitylation is dynamically regulated by deubiquitinating enzymes (DUBs), which remove ubiquitin from substrates, thereby altering substrate stability, localization or activity [6, 8]. Although a growing evidence demonstrates that ubiquitin-specific proteases (USPs), the largest subfamily of DUBs, are promising therapeutic targets for tumor, including hematopoietic malignancies [9–11], their prognostic significances in AML and relevance to AML pathogenesis remain largely unexplored.

Ubiquitin-specific protease 34 (USP34) ), a member of the USP subfamily, possesses deubiquitinase activity. USP34 has been reported to be involved in regulating various physiological processes, including control of Imd or Toll dependent immune signalling [12], assembly of DNA damage repair factors at double-strand break sites [13] and osteogenic differentiation [14], by regulating nuclear factor (NF)-κB [15, 16] and Wnt [17] signaling pathway. Aberrant USP34 expression is associated with various diseases [18–20]. Within hematological malignancies, USP34 overexpression has been documented in diffuse large B cell lymphoma (DLBCL) [20] and shown to play a critical role in the transformation from follicular lymphoma to DLBCL [21]. However, to our knowledge, the expression and prognostic value of USP34 in AML and its roles in leukemogenesis remain completely unknown.

In the present study, we explored the implications of USP34 in AML through systematically analyzing its expression, prognostic relevance and biological functions. We demonstrated for the first time that USP34 expression is upregulated in AML and serves as an independent prognostic predictor for AML patients. High expression of USP34 portends a dysregulated immune and proliferation landscape in AML. Our results indicate that USP34 maybe a novel prognostic biomarker for AML and is involved in AML development through immunological and proliferative regulation.

Methods

Analysis of USPs expression

Gene expression profiles (GSE37642-GPL96) of 422 AML patients with complete clinical information were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) [22–24]. The patients’ clinical characteristics were matched to the gene expression data and were used for correlation analysis. We also obtained the RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) data of 173 AML and corresponding clinical information from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database via UCSC Xena (https://xenabrowser.net/datapages/). We explored the tumor/normal differential expression of USPs in the AML using the Gene Expression Profiling Interactive Analysis (GEPIA) database (http://gepia.cancer-pku.cn), which provides the mRNA expression data of tumors and normal samples from the TCGA and the GTEx (Genotype Tissue Expression) projects [25]. Then, The expression of USPs in different subgroups were analyzed. The subgroups were categorized by French-American-British (FAB) classifications, the status of RUNX1-RUNX1T1 fusion, RUNX1 mutation, FLT3 mutation, IDH1 mutation or RAS activation in the GSE37642 and TCGA datasets.

Comparison of USP34 expression

The mRNA expression level of USP34 in the pan-cancer was analyzed via SangerBox (http://sangerbox.com/home.html), which provides the expression profile across various cancer types and the matched normal tissues from the TCGA and GTEx projects [26]. The cancers include ACC (adrenocortical carcinoma), BLCA (bladder urothelial carcinoma), BRCA (breast invasive carcinoma), CESC (cervical squamous cell carcinoma), CHOL (cholangiocarcinoma), COAD (colon adenocarcinoma), DLBC (lymphoid neoplasm diffuse large B cell lymphoma), ESCA (esophageal carcinoma), GBM (glioblastoma), HNSC (head and neck squamous cell carcinoma), KICH (kidney chromophobe), KIRC (kidney renal clear cell carcinoma), KIRP (kidney renal papillary cell carcinoma), LAML (acute myeloid leukemia), LGG (brain lower grade glioma), LIHC (liver hepatocellular carcinoma), LUAD (lung adenocarcinoma), LUSC (lung squamous cell carcinoma), MESO (mesothelioma), OV (ovarian serous cystadenocarcinoma), PAAD (pancreatic adenocarcinoma), PCPG (pheochromocytoma and paraganglioma), PRAD (prostate adenocarcinoma), READ (rectum adenocarcinoma), SARC (sarcoma), SKCM (skin cutaneous melanoma), STAD (stomach adenocarcinoma), TGCT (testicular germ cell tumors), THCA (thyroid carcinoma), THYM (thymoma), UCEC (uterine corpus endometrial carcinoma), UCS (uterine carcinosarcoma), and UVM (uveal melanoma). The differential expression of USP34 in human bone marrow-derived cells of normal hematopoietic system and hematopoietic neoplasms was evaluated by analysis of two published datasets (MILE: Microarray Innovations in Leukemia and BloodPool) from BloodSpot database (https://servers.binf.ku.dk/bloodspot/) [27].

Analysis of prognostic value

The prognostic potentials of USPs in AML patients from GSE37642-GPL96 were evaluated using Kaplan-Meier analysis and compared by the log-rank test. Five AML patients from GSE37642-GPL96 dataset, whose survival data were missing, were excluded from our prognostic analysis. Finally, 417 AML patients, whose clinical information were completely matched with the gene expression data, were selected for further analysis. Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses were established and hazard ratio with 95% confidence interval were computed to evaluate the significance of USPs in predicting overall survival (OS) in AML. Since the status of RUNX1 mutation was lacking in part of the patients, we had to remove this factor from our Cox analysis. The nomogram and calibration curve integrating five clinical features of AML patients from GSE37642-GPL96 were constructed using the R package ‘rms’. For validation of the prognostic value of USP34 in AML, survival data of AML patients for GSE10358 dataset [28] were acquired from the GEO database.

Cell culture and SiRNA treatment

Human Romas, Kasumi and THP-1 cell lines was obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, USA), and Molm-13 cell was purchased from Beyotime Biotechnology (China). Cells were cultured in Roswell Park Memorial Institute 1640 medium (RPMI-1640) supplemented with 10% FBS in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with a 5% CO2 atmosphere. The protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Tianjin Medical University General Hospital (TMUGH). All experiments were conducted following the guidelines of the Ethics Committee of TMUGH and in accordance with the ethical principles and guidelines for medical research in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Scrambled control siRNA (si-NC) and small interfering RNAs targeting USP34 (si-USP34#1 and si-USP34#2) were synthesized and transfected into Molm-13 and Kasumi cells using Lipofectamine 2000 transfection reagent according to the manufacturers’ protocols. The siRNA sequences used are listed in Supplemental Table 1.

RNA isolation and quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted using the TRIzol extraction reagent (Invitrogen, USA), and the cDNA was synthesized with M-MLV Reverse Transcript reagent (Promega, USA). USP34, CTLA4 and TNFRSF4 mRNA level was detected by qRT-PCR using SYBR Green PCR kit (TaKaRa, Japan) with glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) as the internal control. The sequences of primers used for qRT-PCR were listed in Supplemental Table 2.

Gene enrichment analysis of USP34 DEGs in AML

Gene expression data of 42 USP34 high-expressing (top 10%) and 42 USP34 low-expressing (bottom 10%) AML patients from GSE37642-GPL96 were analyzed for identifying the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) (fold change > 1.1; P < 0.05). The DEGs were displayed as a heatmap using GraphPad Prism 9 software. Gene ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genome (KEGG) enrichment analysis were performed on the protein products of DEGs using the online tool STRING (http://string-db.org) to identify the potential functional enrichments affected by USP34, and visualized by GraphPad Prism 9 software. The protein-protein interaction (PPI) network of USP34 DEGs were also constructed using STRING database.

Gene set enrichment analysis

Gene expression data of 84 AML samples aforementioned were selected for gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) to further understand the potential molecular mechanisms using the GSEA software with the following parameters: Permutation = 1000, and p-value < 0.05 [29]. Heatmaps of the expression of key genes in immune process and transcriptional regulation were provided to visualize the obtained results.

Assessment of immune cell proportion in immune microenvironment and the gene correlation

The stromal, immune and ESTIMATE scores of the high- and low-USP34 expression groups in AML patients from GSE37642 were calculated by Estimation of STromal and Immune cells in MAlignant Tumor tissues using Expression data (ESTIMATE) algorithms. The proportions of immune cells in immune microenvironment of AML patients from GSE37642 were estimated by CIBERSORTx (https://cibersortx.stanford.edu) and the ImmuCellAI (Immune Cell Abundance Identifier) database (http://bioinfo.life.hust.edu.cn/ImmuCellAI#!/) [30–32]. The relative proportion of 22 and 24 immune cell subtypes in 422 AML patients were evaluated by CIBERSORTx and ImmuCellAI, respectively. Then, patients were divided into high-expressing and low-expressing groups according to the median value of USP34 expression level for comparing the proportions of immune cells in immune microenvironment. The association scores of USP34 expression with the estimated proportion of immune cell subtypes, markers for common immune cells including CD8 + T cells, total T cells, B cells, monocytes, tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), M1 and M2 macrophages, neutrophils, NK cells, dendritic cells (DCs), T-helper 1 (Th1) cells, T-helper 2 (Th2) cells, follicular helper T (Tfh) cells, T-helper 17 (Th17), Regulatory T (Treg) cells, exhausted T (Tex) cells, and follicular dendritic cells (FDC), and immune checkpoints (including but not limited to CD40, CD44, CD80, CTLA4, TNFRSF25 and TNFRSF4) were evaluated by Pearson correlation test, and scatter plots or heatmaps were provided to visualize the obtained results.

Cell growth and proliferation assays

Cell growth was quantified using colorimetric MTS assay. Cells were seeded into 96-well plates and incubated for 24 to 96 h. The cells were detected the OD value using the CellTiter 96 Aqueous Non-Radioactive Cell Proliferation Assay (Promega) following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Colony-forming cell assay (CFC) was used to assess anchorage-dependent growth. cells were seeded in MethoCultTM H4100 (Stemcell) methylcellulose medium and incubated for 6–7 days. Colonies were counted and identified based on morphological examination.

5-ethynyl-2′-deoxyuridine (EdU) incorporation assay was used to assess cell proliferation. The proliferating cells were seeded and labeled with EdU using the Cell-Light™EdU Apollo 567 in Vitro Imaging kit (RiboBio) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Then, images were captured using a fluorescence microscope (Olympus) and the EdU-positive rates (%) were calculated as EdU-positive cell numbers/Hoechst-stained cell numbers × 100%.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using the Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS, 17.0) software and results were graphed by GraphPad Prism 9 software. Data were all presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and were assessed using two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-tests. Pearson correlation analysis was used to evaluate the correlation levels of USP34 expression with certain variables, and the correlation coefficient r and p values were used to define the intensity of correlation. Kaplan-Meier method and log-rank test were used to analyze the prognosis, and univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazards models were used to establish the impact of USP34 expression on prognosis with the hazard ratios (HR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI). For all statistical analyses, p < 0.05 was considered the significance threshold.

Results

The expression and prognosis of USPs in human AML

To explore the potential role of USPs in the pathogenesis of AML, we performed gene expression analysis of USP family, which contains 60 USPs, in bone marrow (BM) mononuclear cells of 422 samples from adult patients with AML. And finally, expression data of 39 USPs were available in GSE37642 dataset. We found that USP34 was expressed at the highest level in AML (Fig. 1A). Similarly, USP34 was most highly expressed in 173 AML patients from TCGA database (Fig. 1B). Furthermore, most members of USPs, including USP4, USP9Y, USP11, USP13, USP22, USP34, USP37, USP40, USP41, US43 and CYLD, were more highly expressed in AML compared to the corresponding normal controls using data from TCGA and GTEx databases (Fig. 1C, Supplemental Fig. 1). In contrast, the expression levels of USP2, USP14, USP20 and USP45 were significantly declined in AML (Fig. 1C, Supplemental Fig. 1). We then analyzed the correlation of USPs with the clinical characteristics of AML samples (Fig. 1D and E, Supplemental Fig. 2), and the results showed that the expression levels of most USP members were significantly associated with French-American-British classification (FAB) classifications (Fig. 1D and E).

To consequently investigate the prognostic values of USPs expression in AML, we analyzed the GSE37642 dataset using Kaplan-Meier analysis with log-rank testing. The analysis revealed that elevated expression of most USP members was significantly associated with inferior overall survival (OS). Patients with higher expression of these USPs exhibited significantly shorter OS compared to those with lower expression (p < 0.05, Fig. 2A, Supplemental Fig. 3). Importantly, univariate Cox regression analysis further identified USP6, USP18, USP32, USP34 and USPL1 as independent prognostic risk indicators for OS in AML patients (Fig. 2B). Consistently, high expression of USP6, USP18, USP32, USP34 and USPL1 was predictive of worse OS (Fig. 2A). The results all above demonstrated that the dysregulation of USPs is associated with poor prognosis and may play a crucial role in AML pathogenesis.

High expression of USP34 in human AML

Given our findings above, we concluded that USP34 might play an imperative role in the initiation and development of AML (Fig. 3A). To investigate the possible involvement of USP34 in AML, we analyzed its differential mRNA expression between tumors and normal tissues across multiple cancer types using data from the TCGA and GTEx projects. This pan-cancer analysis revealed that USP34 exhibited the highest expression in AML across all cancer types (Fig. 3B). Furthermore, analysis of the BloodPool database demonstrated that USP34 expression was significantly higher in AML and other hematopoietic neoplasms compared to its matched normal samples (Fig. 3C). We validated this differential expression pattern using MILE database (Fig. 3D). We then validated these results by quantifying USP34 mRNA in four leukemia cell lines and the result confirmed that USP34 expression was significantly higher in AML cell lines (Molm-13, Kasumi and THP-1) compared to cell lines derived from other human hematologic neoplasm (Romas, Fig. 3E).

Next, we evaluated the subcellular localization of USP34 protein in normal bone marrow tissue using the human protein atlas (HPA; https://www.proteinatlas.org) database. As shown in Fig. 3F, USP34 protein expression in normal bone marrow tissue was low with localization primarily observed within the nucleus. We then evaluated the correlations of USP34 expression with clinical characteristics in 422 AML samples from GSE37642 dataset, with a median age of 57 years. Correlation analysis suggested that USP34 expression was significantly associated with FAB (p = 0.001) and the age (p = 0.040) of patients (Table 1). Subgroup analysis comparing USP34 expression across different clinical characteristics showed that USP34 was significantly high-expressed in AML patients with FAB classification (M0-M2 type) and age ≥ 57 than other subgroups (Fig. 3G and H, Supplemental Fig. 4A and 4B). Taken together, these results demonstrated that USP34 was significantly overexpressed in AML, and the high expression of USP34 may be closely associated with leukemogenesis.

High USP34 expression predicted worse prognosis in AML patients

To evaluate the prognostic significance of USP34 in AML, we performed Kaplan-Meier survival analysis on 417 AML patients from the GSE37642-GPL96 dataset with matched survival and expression data. The results presented that high expression of USP34 was remarkably associated with poor overall survival (OS) in patients with AML (Fig. 4A), and this association was validated in an independent dataset GSE10358 (Supplemental Fig. 5). ROC curve analysis showed that the area under the curve (AUC) values exceeded 0.6 for predicting 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival, and USP34 expression had a great predictive ability of AML patient survival (Fig. 4B).

In addition, univariate Cox proportional hazards regression analysis was performed to assess the factors influencing OS in the GSE37642 dataset. Four variables with complete data were considered in our univariate analysis. The results suggested that the four variables including USP34 expression, FAB classification, age and RUNX1-RUNX1T1 fusion were all significantly correlated with OS in AML (Fig. 4C). Since variables of FAB classification and age were significantly associated with USP34 expression, these two variables were excluded in multivariate Cox regression modeling. USP34 expression and RUNX1-RUNX1T1 fusion were then included in multivariate Cox analysis with the Cox proportional hazard model. We observed that high expression of USP34 remained significantly associated with worse OS (HR:1.365, 95%CI:1.032–1.805, p = 0.029, Fig. 4D), and RUNX1-RUNX1T1 fusion was correlated with better OS (HR:0.500, 95%CI:0.274–0.915, p = 0.024, Fig. 4D). These two factors were independent prognostic factors for OS of AML patients. We then constructed a nomogram using USP34 and clinical characteristics of AML patients that could estimate the probability of 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival rates (Fig. 4E). The calibration curve, which could evaluate the accuracy of our nomogram, indicated a well prediction (Fig. 4F).

Furthermore, we stratified these AML patients according to FAB classification, age, RUNX1-RUNX1T1 fusion status and RUNX1 mutation from GSE37642-GPL96 dataset. Patients with relatively high USP34 expression also had apparently worse prognoses than those with low USP34 expression in the subgroups of FAB M0-2, FAB M3-7 (Fig. 4G), age < 57 and age ≥ 57 (Fig. 4H), without RUNX family transcription factor 1 - RUNX1 partner transcriptional co-repressor 1 (RUNX1-RUNX1T1) fusion (Fig. 4I) and without RUNX1 mutation (Fig. 4J). Take together, these findings indicated that high expression of USP34 predicted worse prognosis and it is an independent prognostic predictor for worse OS of AML patients.

Functional enrichment analysis of DEGs and gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) in AML samples

To explore the functional implication of USP34 in AML, we identified the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) by analyzing gene expression data of 42 USP34-low-expressing (top 10%) and 42 USP34 high-expressing (bottom 10%) AML patients from GSE37642-GPL96 (Fig. 5A). A total of 1170 DEGs were identified (fold change > 1.1; p < 0.05), including 932 upregulated and 238 downregulated genes (Fig. 5B). The top 50 upregulated and top 50 downregulated DEGs were displayed by the heat maps (Supplemental Fig. 6A). 1170 protein products of DEGs were then submitted to STRING to identify the possible functional enrichments affected by USP34. GO enrichment analysis revealed that immune process and immune response were most significantly enriched (Fig. 5C). Processes including regulation of gene expression and regulation of metabolic process were also functionally associated with USP34-related DEGs (Fig. 5C, Supplemental Fig. 6B and 6 C). Moreover, Reactome pathway analysis indicated that USP34 DEGs were prominently functionally enriched in immune system like neutrophil degranulation, innate immune system and adaptive immune system (Fig. 5D). The regulation of gene transcription pathways including RNA polymerase II transcription and gene expression (Transcription) were also altered (Fig. 5D). In addition, KEGG pathway analysis showed that B cell receptor signaling pathway, T cell receptor signaling pathway and transcriptional misregulation in cancer were also enriched (Fig. 5E). The protein-protein interaction network of USP34-related DEGs enriched in the immune process (red) and regulation of gene transcription (blue) were constructed using STRING database (Fig. 5F). These data suggest that USP34 might be involved in the development of AML via intervening immune process and transcriptional regulation.

To gain further insight into the potential molecular mechanisms underlying the regulation of immune process and transcription by USP34, GSEA was performed using gene expression data of 84 AML patients from GSE37642-GPL96 (Fig. 5A). Consistent with the aforementioned functional enrichment, significant differences were observed in the enrichment of immunologic signatures (c7.all.v7.5.symbols.gmt). The immune-related processes associated significantly with USP34 expression, included ‘naïve vs. memory CD8 T cell’, ‘mature vs. immature NK cell’, ‘CD8 vs. CD4 naïve T cell’ (Figs. 5G), ‘naïve vs. activated CD8 T cell’, etc. (Supplemental Table 3). Meanwhile, GSEA also showed that USP34 was involved in various gene expression-related gene sets such as ‘regulation of gene expression epigenetic’, ‘regulation of DNA templated transcription initiation’, ‘RNA polymerase I transcription’ and ‘histone ubiquitination’ (Supplemental Figs. 6D and 6E, Supplemental Table 3). Expression patterns of genes associated with immune processes (Supplemental Figs. 6 F) and transcriptional regulation (Supplemental Figs. 6G) were visualized using heatmaps. For molecular mechanistic exploration, GSEA demonstrated that USP34 participated in regulating Toll-like receptor signaling, cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction, TOR signaling and Notch signaling pathway (Supplemental Table 3). These results further indicate that USP34 is highly involved in the regulation of immune process and transcription in AML.

Relationship between USP34 expression and immune cell proportion in immune microenvironment in AML

Increasing evidence showed that AML blasts harnessed the immunological microenvironment, and immunological dysregulations also helped AML blasts evade immune surveillance and finally leaded to leukemogenesis [33, 34]. Given the results that USP34 might be involved in the regulation of immune process in AML based on the enrichment analysis above, we evaluated the immune scores (stromal score, immune score and ESTIMATE score) based on USP34 expression within GSE37642-GPL96 cohort. This analysis confirmed an association between USP34 expression and immune infiltration, indicating that high USP34 expression may influence the immune microenvironment (Figs. 6A-C). We then investigated the relationship between USP34 expression and immune cell proportion in the AML immune microenvironment. The proportions of 22 immune cell subtypes were estimated from the gene expression profiles of 422 AML patients in the GSE37642-GPL96 dataset using CIBERSORTx. The relative proportions of these 22 immune cell subtypes varied across the samples. Profiles depicting the proportions of all 22 immune cells across the 422 AML samples are shown stratified by USP34 expression (Supplemental Fig. 7A). Then, the proportion of each immune cell subtypes were further compared based on USP34 expression (Fig. 6D). This comparison revealed that the estimated proportions of thirteen immune cells were significantly differentiated between the two groups, including naive B cells, gamma delta T cells, regulatory T cells, CD8 + T cells, activated NK cells, monocytes, M1 macrophages, M2 macrophages, plasma cells, activated dendritic cells, resting and activated mast cells, and eosinophils (Fig. 6D). Among them, the estimated proportions of naive B cells (p < 0.01), plasma cells (p < 0.01), CD8 + T cells (p < 0.05), regulatory T cells (Tregs, p < 0.01), gamma delta T cells (p < 0.05), activated NK cells (p < 0.01), resting mast cells (p < 0.001) and eosinophils (p < 0.05) were significantly increased in USP34 high-expressing group, whereas those of monocytes (p < 0.001), M1 macrophages (p < 0.05), M2 macrophages (p < 0.05), activated dendritic cells (p < 0.05) and activated mast cells (p < 0.01) were decreased in USP34 high-expressing group (Fig. 6D). Moreover, we also verified the above results by evaluating the relative proportion of 24 immune cell subtypes in 422 AML patients mentioned above using ImmuCellAI (Fig. 6E, Supplemental Fig. 7B). Consistent with the findings above, estimated proportions of most of immune cell subtypes were increased in USP34 high-expressing group, expect for monocytes and macrophages (Fig. 6E). Furthermore, the relationship between prognosis of 417 AML patients and estimated proportions of immune cells was assessed by Kaplan-Meier survival analysis. The results revealed that higher fractions of naive B cells (p < 0.01) and gamma delta T cells (p < 0.05) were associated with poor OS in AML patients (Fig. 6F). Positively correlation of USP34 expression with estimated proportions of T cells in immune microenvironment of AML indicate that USP34 is implicated in the suppression of immune responses.

To further verify the relationship between USP34 expression and proportions of immune cells in immune microenvironment of AML, we also assessed the associations between USP34 expression and those of immune cell marker genes in 422 AML samples from GSE37642-GPL96 dataset. The results revealed significant correlations between USP34 expression and the majority of the selected immune cell marker genes in AML (Supplemental Fig. 8A). Among them, USP34 expression was strongly positively correlated with the expression levels of gene markers of Treg cells, CD8 + T cells, Th1 cells, Tfh cells and Tex cells, such as CD8B (r = 0.240, p < 0.001), CD3E (r = 0.302, p < 0.001), TBX21 (r = 0.260, p < 0.001), IL21 (r = 0.434, p < 0.001), FOXP3 (r = 0.336, p < 0.001), CCR8 (r = 0.303, p < 0.001), PDCD1 (r = 0.294, p < 0.001) and CTLA4 (r = 0.436, p < 0.001) (Supplemental Fig. 8B). In USP34 knockdown Molm-13 and Kasumi cells, we consistently reproduced these patterns in CTLA4 mRNA expression, which shows the strongest positive correlation with USP34 (Fig. 6G). In contrast, the expression of USP34 was negatively correlated with related gene markers of monocytes, macrophages and dendritic cells (Supplemental Fig. 8A), which were consistent with the above results of immune cell proportions in immune microenvironment.

Immune checkpoint molecules have been reported to promote immune evasion [33, 34]. We then assessed the correlation of USP34 expression with key immune checkpoints in 422 AML samples from GSE37642-GPL96 dataset. Notably, we observed that USP34 expression exhibited significant correlations with the expressions of almost all the selected immune checkpoint genes in AML (Supplemental Fig. 8C). USP34 expression was strongly positively correlated with the expressions of immune checkpoint genes like TNFRSF4 (r = 0.447, p < 0.001), TNFRSF25 (r = 0.442, p < 0.001), CTLA4 (r = 0.436, p < 0.001), CD44 (r = 0.426, p < 0.001), CD40 (r = 0.419, p < 0.001), CD80 (r = 0.412, p < 0.001), TNFSF18 (r = 0.405, p < 0.001) and CD40LG (r = 0.369, p < 0.001) (Supplemental Fig. 8C), and the expressions of these immune checkpoint genes were upregulated in USP34-high-expressing group (Supplemental Fig. 8D). These patterns were consistently reproduced in our examination of TNFRSF4 mRNA expression, which is mostly correlated with USP34, in USP34 knockdown Molm-13 and Kasumi cells (Fig. 6H). Taken together, these results indicate that high USP34 expression is correlated with the suppression of immune responses.

USP34 knockdown inhibits proliferation of human myeloid leukemia cells

Functional correlation using the single-cell CancerSEA tool was executed to assess the relevance between USP34 and 14 biological function in AML. The result showed that USP34 was extensively associated with the apoptosis, differentiation, metastasis, proliferation, quiescence and Stemness (Fig. 7A). Further biological functional analysis in AML using a single-cell dataset (ExpID: EXP0047) indicated that USP34 was positively associated with cell proliferation (Fig. 7B). To investigate the proliferation role of USP34 in AML, we employed two specific siRNAs targeting USP34 (si-USP34#1 or si-USP34#2) to knockdown USP34 in Molm-13 and Kasumi cell lines (Fig. 7C), which expressed higher level of USP34 (Fig. 3E). Compared with the control siRNA, USP34 silencing markedly decreased the colony formation capacity of Molm-13 and Kasumi cells, as determined by colony-formation assay (Fig. 7D). USP34 knockdown also significantly inhibited cell proliferation, as determined by MTS (Fig. 7E) and EdU incorporation (Fig. 7F and G) assays. These results suggest that USP34 knockdown inhibited the proliferation of AML cells in vitro, indicating that USP34 promotes the tumorigenic potentials of human myeloid leukemia cells by promoting cell proliferation.

Discussion

As crucial modulators of ubiquitination, DUBs play essential roles in tumorigenesis by removing ubiquitin from substrate proteins, thereby determining substrate fate and functioning as either tumor suppressors or promoters [6, 35, 36]. USP3 promotes 12-O-tetradecanoyl phorbol-13-acetate-mediated leukemia cell differentiation via reducing H2A-K119ub levels [37]. USP7 is highly expressed in relapsed AML patients, and inhibition of USP7 increases the sensitivity of AML to chemotherapy [10]. Inhibition of USP10 induces the degradation of oncogenic fms-related tyrosine kinase 3 (FLT3) and spleen tyrosine kinase (SYK) in AML [11, 38]. USP15 is overexpressed in human AML and drives AML cell function via suppressing oxidative stress sensor mechanism [39]. In contrast, USP22-deficient promotes myeloid cell differentiation blockade and AML transformation in mice [40]. USP34 was previously reported to be highly expressed in DLBCL [20]. However, to our knowledge, the expression and prognostic value of USP34 in AML are not investigated as yet. We systemically analyzed, for the first time, the expression and prognostic significance of USP34 in AML using informatics tools. USP34 was significantly higher expressed in AML as compared to the matched normal samples. More importantly, multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression analysis revealed that higher USP34 expression is independently associated with worse overall survival in AML patients. However, this aspect of our study has several limitations. Our findings regarding USP34 expression and its prognostic significance in AML are derived solely from bioinformatic analysis. Consequently, experimental validation is required to confirm the observed overexpression of USP34 in AML. Furthermore, the prognostic value of USP34 requires confirmation in larger AML patient cohorts. Finally, the mechanisms underlying USP34 overexpression in human AML remain unclear and warrant further investigation.

USP34 has been reported to be involved in regulating many aspects of cellular processes including immune-related signal [12], DNA double-strand breaks [13] and osteogenic differentiation [14] via regulating Toll and Imd signaling [12], nuclear factor (NF)-κB [15, 16] signaling and Wnt [17] signaling pathway. Enrichment analysis may provide some clues on the roles of USP34 and possible mechanisms in leukemogenesis. Interestingly, our analysis revealed significant enrichment for immune process, regulation of gene expression and metabolic process. Among them, immune process and immune response were the two most significantly enriched (Fig. 4B). The results of GSEA analysis also suggested that immunologic signatures were significantly associated with USP34 expression. Moreover, several important signaling pathways, like TOR and NOTCH1 signaling were enriched in the USP34 high expression group (Supplemental Table 3). The pronounced enrichment of immune regulatory pathways suggests that USP34 might be involved in AML development and progression and by regulating immune process.

AML is a clinically heterogeneous disease characterized by accumulation of malignant leukemic cells, which lead to the failure of normal hematopoiesis [1, 2]. Intrinsic oncogenic alterations in hematopoietic cells and extrinsic immune evasion both contribute to leukemogenesis [33, 34, 41]. AML immune evasion is caused by multiple mechanisms including both intrinsic and extrinsic immunosuppressive mechanisms. Intrinsic immunosuppressive effects, including defective antigen presentation and regulation of immunoregulatory checkpoints, can directly promote extrinsic microenvironmental changes and finally lead to accumulation of suppressive regulatory T cells (Tregs) and secretion of immunosuppressive cytokines [42]. Numerous studies have shown that the number of T cells was elevated in AML patients [43, 44], especially Tregs [45–47], and the altered immune microenvironment plays a crucial role in regulating AML progression [41–48]. However, there is no clear consensus studies regarding numbers and distribution for most of immune cells in the immune microenvironment of AML. The relationship between intrinsic oncogenic alterations and extrinsic immunological dysregulations also remain unclear [33, 34]. These findings collectively underscore the critical need to delineate relationships between oncogenic drivers and immune components within the AML microenvironment. In this study, we explored the correlation of USP34 expression with the proportion of immune cells. The results showed that USP34 expression was positively correlated with multiple immune cells, especially regulatory T cells and gamma delta T cells. Furthermore, USP34 expression showed significant positive correlations with key immune checkpoint genes, including TNFRSF4, TNFRSF25, CTLA4, CD44, CD40, CD80, TNFSF18 and CD40LG. It has been reported that the proportion of Treg, which is one of the most important immunosuppressive components, is increased in AML patients [45–47, 49]. In addition, studies have shown that immune checkpoint molecules promote tumor immune evasion [33, 34]. The positive correlations of USP34 with these immunosuppressive components and molecules suggest that high expression of USP34 may promote AML pathogenesis by enabling leukemia cells to subvert immune surveillance. The detailed mechanisms underlying immune regulation of USP34 in human AML is still fully unknown and need further research.

In summary, we disclosed, for the first time, that USP34 expression is increased in AML, and USP34 functions as a novel prognostic biomarker for human AML. Moreover, the high expression of USP34 was significantly correlated with excessive immune-suppressive cells in AML, and USP34 knockdown inhibits the proliferation of AML cells in vitro study. Further prospective and experimental studies should be carried out to verify the roles of USP34 on immunological regulation in AML.
