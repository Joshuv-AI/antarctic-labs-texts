Influence of prefabricated fissure angles on mechanical and infrared properties of red sandstone and failure prediction based on deep learning
Authors: Liwen He, Xin Xue, Xun Liu, Chengyu Xie, Dongping Shi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractUnderstanding the influence of structural planes on rock failure is crucial for engineering safety. In this study, uniaxial compression tests combined with infrared thermography monitoring were conducted on red sandstone with prefabricated fissure of different inclinations to explore its mechanical and infrared thermal response characteristics. The results indicate that the fracture inclination angle determines the failure mode,transitioning from tension-dominated to shear-dominated.Furthermore,it influences the trend of peak strength variation, which exhibits an increase with higher inclination angles . During failure, all specimens exhibit a sudden temperature rise caused by frictional Heating. Key indicators such as the standard deviation of infrared radiation temperature can effectively quantify the thermal field inhomogeneity associated with shear crack propagation. To predict failure using thermal precursors, a 1D-CNN-Bi-LSTM-Attention hybrid deep learning model was developed, which predicts stress-time curves by capturing the spatiotemporal evolution dynamics of infrared data. Through fourfold cross-validation, the model achieves a coefficient of determination (R2) greater than 0.99 and a root mean square error (RMSE) less than 1.0 for all fractured specimens, demonstrating excellent generalization ability and robustness. This study clarifies the link between the failure mechanism of fractured rocks and infrared energy release, providing a technical framework for the development of intelligent non-destructive early warning systems for rock mass stability.Keywords: Fractured rock mass, Infrared thermal imaging, Failure mechanism, Deep learning, Failure predictionSubject terms: Civil engineering, Coal

Introduction

Rock masses constitute the principal load-bearing medium and geological milieu for diverse civil engineering, water conservancy, and mining enterprises. Their stability directly governs the safety and sustainable operation of such infrastructure. Due to geological processes, natural rock masses develop structural discontinuities—including joints, fractures, faults, and microcracks. The presence of these discontinuities fundamentally compromises rock mass integrity and continuity, giving rise to highly complex, anisotropic, and heterogeneous mechanical responses1–11. Abundant theoretical investigations, numerical simulations, and engineering case studies have demonstrated that the deformation, damage, and subsequent macro-fracture instability of rock masses under external loading or environmental perturbations are predominantly governed by the activation, propagation, branching, and ultimate coalescence of pre-existing internal structural planes12–16. Thus, in-depth exploration of the mechanical response characteristics and progressive failure modes of fractured rock masses holds critical theoretical significance and bears broad engineering applicability. This endeavor is pivotal for enhancing disaster prediction and early-warning capabilities in rock engineering, optimizing engineering design and construction schemes, and ensuring the holistic safety and cost-effectiveness of engineering projects.

The mechanical behavior of fractured rock masses has been extensively investigated, with a primary focus on the influence of pre-existing fracture geometry on rock strength and failure modes. Researchers have systematically characterized how parameters like fracture inclination, length, and spatial arrangement dictate mechanical responses, using both experimental tests and numerical simulations to document the resulting failure patterns17–21. Furthermore, the effects of external loading conditions, such as varying loading paths and rates, on fracture activation, propagation, and overall failure characteristics have been well-documented, providing a foundational understanding of micro-mechanistic failure processes in fractured rock22–24.

The deformation and failure of rock is an energy-driven process accompanied by multiphysical phenomena, including thermal emissions25–32. Consequently, infrared thermography (IRT) has emerged as a powerful non-destructive technique for monitoring rock damage. By capturing surface temperature changes arising from thermoelastic effects, frictional heating, and energy release during crack propagation, IRT provides real-time insights into damage accumulation and impending failure33,34. Foundational studies established a robust correlation between applied stress and key metrics like the average infrared radiation temperature (AIRT)35–42. Subsequent research has refined this approach by introducing quantitative metrics, such as thermal field variance, to characterize damage evolution and has systematically investigated the influence of factors like moisture content and loading rate on these infrared signatures in intact rocks43,44.

Despite these advancements, applications of infrared thermography in rock mechanics have primarily focused on global temperature fields, leaving a critical gap in our understanding25,45. The thermal signature evolution within local stress concentration zones, such as at the tips of pre-existing cracks, and its direct correlation with fracture propagation remain poorly characterized. Specifically, the influence of fundamental geometric attributes, like fracture inclination, on the spatiotemporal evolution of infrared radiation has not been systematically investigated. This leaves a key question unanswered: how does fracture inclination govern stress distribution and, in turn, dictate the resulting thermal patterns predictive of failure?

To address this gap, this study employs a high-precision system combining uniaxial compression with synchronous infrared thermography to monitor red sandstone specimens with prefabricated fissures at varying inclinations (0°, 30°, 45°, 60°). Using a custom Python-based method, key infrared metrics, including the maximum infrared radiation temperature (MAXIRT), the average infrared radiation temperature (AIRT), the standard deviation of infrared radiation temperature (SDIRT) and the coefficient of variation of infrared radiation temperature (CVIRT), are quantitatively analyzed. These metrics are correlated with stress–strain data and failure modes to systematically elucidate the link between fracture inclination, mechanical behavior, and thermal energy release.

At present, artificial intelligence methods have been widely applied in the field of rock engineering46,47. To translate these physical insights into a practical predictive tool, we introduce a novel 1D-CNN-Bi-LSTM-Attention hybrid deep learning model. This model is designed to leverage the complex spatiotemporal evolution of the infrared data for high-precision, dynamic prediction of the rock’s loading stress state. By validating its performance against traditional models, we demonstrate its superior accuracy and robustness. Ultimately, this work provides a new theoretical and technological framework for developing intelligent, non-destructive early-warning systems for engineering rock mass stability based on infrared thermography.

Test equipment and samples

Test equipment

To systematically investigate the influence of prefabricated fissure angles on the mechanical properties, failure modes, and infrared radiation response characteristics of red sandstone, a deep understanding of the stress field, fissure evolution, and infrared radiation temperature field during rock damage is essential. To address this, we performed laboratory uniaxial compression experiments on the rock specimens, following the experimental protocol outlined below (Fig. 1):

Experimental setup.

(1) The experiment employed a custom-developed infrared thermal imaging monitoring system to capture the damage evolution of red sandstone specimens. The loading apparatus was a microcomputer-controlled pressure testing Machine. During uniaxial compression testing, this machine applied axial load to the specimen by Lifting the base upward; during unloading, the base descended at a controlled low speed. The Maximum loading capacity of the testing Machine was 300 kN.

(2) The infrared thermal imaging instrument (model Orion SC7000, manufactured by FLIR Systems, USA) exhibited a temperature measurement range of − 40 °C to 1200 °C, a spatial resolution of 640 × 512 pixels, a temperature sensitivity of 0.02°C, and noise correction capability for radiation up to 17 μm. It operated at a Maximum frame rate of 30 frames per second (fps) within a spectral range of 7.5–14μm. As previously suggested by researchers, high-sensitivity, high-performance infrared thermal imaging instruments were placed 1 m away from the front of the sample to continuously detect temperature changes48–50.

(3) The infrared data processing system is Altair (Version 5.91.010, released on Mar 20, 2012, www.flir.com) by FLIR Systems, which can view and store videos in real time at a speed of 200 frames per second and process the acquired image data.

Test program

Prior to the experiment, precise time synchronization calibration was conducted between the microcomputer-controlled pressure testing Machine and the infrared thermal imager. During testing, the specimen was positioned on the lower platen of the testing machine, with its center strictly coaxial with the platen center. The infrared thermal imager lens was adjusted to ensure the specimen center was vertically aligned at the same Height, Maintaining a horizontal distance of 1 m. The pressure testing Machine was set to displacement control mode, applying uniaxial loading to the specimen at a constant displacement rate of 0.4 mm/min. Concurrently, the infrared thermal imager was configured to acquire images at a sampling frequency of 30 Hz.

To mitigate environmental influences on infrared thermal radiation variations, all instruments and specimens were equilibrated at ambient temperature (approximately 17 °C) for 24 h prior to experimentation. Experiments were conducted at fixed times during nighttime to minimize external perturbations. Laboratory doors and windows remained closed throughout testing, with Light-tight black cloth barriers erected to create an isolated dark environment. Personnel movement was strictly prohibited, and each specimen test was completed within 10 min to maintain thermal stability.

Results and analysis

Effect of prefabricated fissure angle on mechanical properties and damage patterns of sandstone

To investigate the influence of prefabricated fissure inclination on rock mechanical properties, we plotted the stress–strain curves of the prefabricated fissured rocks. In this experiment, the compression process was controlled at a constant displacement rate of 0.4 mm/min, so the stress-time curves of the rock could be used in place of stress–strain curves. Based on the uniaxial compression damage processes of individual specimens (Fig. 4), the rock damage progression during compression can be categorized into four stages: compaction, elastic deformation, destabilization, and failure stages53.

Destruction process of red sandstone with different prefabricated fracture angles: (a) presents the time-stress curve of red sandstone with a prefabricated fissure angle of 60°, (b) presents the time-stress curves of red sandstone with prefabricated fissure angles of 0°, 30°, 45°, and 60°, as well as the curve of peak stress versus fissure inclination angle.

In the compaction stage, due to the intrinsic pores and microcracks within the rock, the initial voids and microfractures of the specimen gradually close, with impeded stress transmission leading to a slow stress increase and an upward concave trend in the curve.

During the elastic deformation stage, the sample undergoes linear elastic deformation, where stresses exhibit a nearly linear growth pattern as internal interstices have been compacted, minimal new cracks initiate, and the rock deforms predominantly elastically.

In the destabilization stage, stress reaches the crack initiation threshold, triggering the nucleation of internal microcracks. Under continuous loading, these cracks and preexisting microfractures propagate, coalesce, and form interconnected networks, causing structural destabilization and damage, with the stress-time curve displaying nonlinear characteristics.

During the failure stage, extensive crack coalescence occurs, and post-peak, through-going fractures develop, leading to rock failure accompanied by a cliff-like drop in stress. The post-peak failure phase is transient due to the brittle nature of the specimen.

Figure 5 further shows, in order to investigate the effect of prefabricated fracture angle on the damage pattern of red sandstone, our examination of the final damage pattern of each prefabricated fracture sample based on their respective final damage pictures. Cracks in the prefabricated fissured red sandstone specimens initiated from the tips of the prefabricated fissures. With an increase in the inclination angle of the prefabricated fissure, the number of cracks in the specimens exhibited a monotonic increase, and the damage mode gradually transitioned from tensile-dominated to shear-dominated. The 0° specimen developed tensile wing cracks nearly perpendicular to the prefabricated fissure, retaining basic structural integrity post-damage. The 30°, 45°, and 60° specimens all displayed a tensile-shear composite damage pattern. Analysis of surface crack density and propagation scale revealed that the 30° specimen was dominated by tensile wing cracks, exhibiting tensile-dominated composite damage. The fracture systems of the 45° and 60° specimens comprised three characteristic crack types: coplanar secondary shear cracks, tensile wing cracks, and reverse secondary shear cracks, with shear cracks predominating to form shear-dominated composite damage. Notably, as the prefabricated crack angle increased, the angle between tip-initiated cracks and the prefabricated fissure decreased, with the 60° specimen’s crack propagation direction becoming essentially parallel to the prefabricated fissure. Post-damage, the 30°, 45°, and 60° prefabricated fissured sandstones exhibited full crack propagation and significant structural degradation, displaying typical strain-type rockburst characteristics.

We analyzed the underlying mechanisms of the above results. According to the single discontinuity theory, the influence of prefabricated fissure inclination on specimen mechanical properties is governed by the horizontal projection area and the angle relative to the principal compressive stress direction. Fissures at 0° and 30° exhibit larger horizontal projections and orientations nearly orthogonal to the principal compressive stress, exacerbating stress concentration and fissure propagation. This amplifies the detrimental effect of discontinuities on specimen integrity, significantly weakening load-bearing capacity. In contrast, 45° and 60° fissures have smaller horizontal projections, with an increased normal stress component that facilitates load transfer through more intact rock-bridge structures, thereby enhancing load-bearing capacity. Simultaneously, when the prefabricated fissure angle is less than 45°, its larger horizontal projection area induces tensile stress concentration at the fissure tip under axial loading. This promotes the formation of tensile cracks that propagate perpendicularly to the fissure plane, eventually developing into tensile wing cracks. Conversely, for fissure angles of 45° and 60°, the reduced horizontal projection area shifts the dominant stress at the tip to shear forces. This results in the generation of shear cracks that extend parallel to the fissure orientation, culminating in the formation of shear wing cracks.

IRT response characterization of destruction processes

Through comparative analysis of the AIRT curves and stress-time curves for different samples, we observed that the stages of infrared radiation temperature (IRT) response in prefabricated fissured rocks are highly coincident with stress stage divisions, a pattern common to IRT curves of rocks with varying fissure angles. Moreover, the IRT trends of samples with different prefabricated fissure inclinations exhibit stage-specific correlations at corresponding stress phases54. The IRT curve can be categorized into four distinct stages: compaction, elastic deformation, destabilization, and failure (Fig. 6). Taking AIRT variations as an example, the specific characteristics of each stage are as follows:

AIRT curves for specimens with different prefabricated fissure angles:(a–d) present red sandstone specimens with prefabricated fissure angles of 0°, 30°, 45°, and 60°, respectively, where within each figure, the labels I–IV correspond to the four stages: compaction, elastic deformation, destabilization, and failure. Data in the figure are overall infrared radiation measurements on the surface of rock samples.

Stage I: Compaction Stage. During this phase, axial compression induces closure of pre-existing cracks and pores within the sandstone, leading to the expulsion of gases that carry away heat. No macroscopic damage initiates, and negligible heat is generated. Consequently, the average infrared radiation temperature (AIRT) of each specimen generally exhibits a downward temperature trend.

Stage II: Elastic Deformation Stage. During this phase, the rock’s intrinsic pores are fully closed. According to thermoelasticity theory, volume contraction caused by axial compressive stress induces a thermoelastic temperature rise. The specimen’s compressive deformation at this stage leads to a gradual increase in average infrared radiation temperature (AIRT). Meanwhile, the formation of various microcracks under stress introduces fluctuations in the temperature rise. Shear microcracks generate frictional heating, elevating temperatures, whereas tensile microcracks cause specimen dilation, absorbing heat and triggering localized infrared temperature decreases. Under the combined effect of these mechanisms, the overall AIRT curve exhibits an unstable fluctuating upward trend.

Stage III: Destabilization Stage. As the applied load increases, pre-existing microcracks within the rock accelerate in propagation and coalescence, with previously formed microcracks acting as structural weak planes that dominate the material’s mechanical behavior. Shear cracks become the primary mode of fracture, and frictional interactions along their surfaces intensify with increasing stress, generating a significant frictional thermal effect and releasing substantial heat. Consequently, the rock’s macroscopic infrared radiation temperature (IRT) response exhibits a notable rise in average infrared radiation temperature (AIRT).

Stage IV: Failure Stage. Upon reaching the specimen’s ultimate load-bearing capacity, microfractures propagate and coalesce into macrofractures, triggering rapid rock failure. Significant frictional sliding along fracture surfaces—particularly across prefabricated fissure sections—generates substantial heat emission, culminating in a sharp increase in the average infrared radiation temperature (AIRT) curve.

From these observations, it is evident that the existence of pre—existing cracks exerts a notable influence on the infrared radiation temperature (IRT) characteristics of the samples. For sandstone with varying pre—existing crack angles, the trends of the IRT curves are different, and the changes in IRT at the moment of sample failure also vary. When making comparisons among different samples, the standard deviation of infrared radiation temperature (SDIRT) curve reflects the dispersion characteristics of infrared radiation, and its overall increment rises as the crack inclination angle increases. This is due to the fact that as the projected area of the crack angle grows, the cracks emanating from the crack tip gradually become mainly shear cracks, which enhances the local thermal effects and, as a result, the overall temperature dispersion.

AIRT denotes the average infrared radiation value, with its overall increment diminishing as fracture dip angle increases—a decline is evident in 60° rock samples. This phenomenon arises from the progressive expansion of pre-existing cracks, which enhances the rock’s surface area exposed to external gases. Additionally, under the control of weak-plane angles and shear failure mechanisms, the density of microcracks nucleating at pre-existing crack tips increases with inclination, inducing rock dilatation and subsequent infiltration of low-temperature gases. This process attenuates the thermal effects generated by shear friction. Notably, the magnitude of abrupt changes in both indicators at failure correlates positively with pre-existing fracture angle, mirroring the trend observed in peak stress. The evolution of IRT curves and load curves therefore provides a precise characterization of failure behavior in pre-fractured sandstone.

These observations indicate that for sandstones with identical prefabricated fissure angles, the local MIRT and AIRT curves exhibit largely consistent trends, though the AIRT trends are more pronounced. This is because MIRT primarily records local transient hotspots, which are susceptible to random thermal perturbations during loading, causing the curve to undergo frequent fluctuations that culminate in a smoother overall trend.

The presence of pre-existing cracks exerts a profound influence on the local IRT characteristics of the specimen. Through comparative analysis of the evolution of local and global AIRT during uniaxial loading, it is revealed that modifications in the rock’s overall IRT are predominantly driven by crack nucleation in the vicinity of pre-existing fractures. During compression, cracks first initiate at the tips of pre-existing cracks, where thermal effects arising from crack propagation induce sustained localized heating around pre-existing fractures. This localized high temperature is transferred to the entire rock specimen via heat conduction, triggering temperature changes. Concurrently, however, the rock matrix undergoes concurrent energy dissipation processes, resulting in global AIRT values that are lower than local measurements, with comparatively subdued fluctuations.

With respect to sensitivity, the limited number of sampling points in local measurement regions renders them immune to regional anomalies. Analysis of locally sampled infrared index curves facilitates the identification of precursory signs of rock failure, particularly in samples with large pre-existing fracture inclinations. Their curves exhibit distinct failure precursors prior to approaching the common time point of damage-induced temperature surges, namely the onset of the destabilization phase.

Comprehensive analysis of infrared indicators for prefabricated fissured red sandstone

To comprehensively summarize the infrared radiation evolution law during the damage process of prefabricated fissured rock, this section analyzes the sensitivity of different infrared radiation temperature (IRT) indices across various stages of rock damage. Here, CVIRT (coefficient of variation of infrared radiation temperature) is introduced as a supplementary metric. CVIRT quantifies the relative dispersion of IRT distribution—i.e., the homogeneity of temperature distribution—and serves as a dimensionless indicator to eliminate the influence of absolute temperature values. The core advantage of CVIRT (Coefficient of Variation of Infrared Radiation Temperature) lies in its normalization property, which enables it to effectively complement the analytical dimensions of SDIRT and AIRT. Specifically, SDIRT reflects the absolute degree of dispersion of infrared radiation temperature, and AIRT characterizes the average temperature level. By calculating the ratio of SDIRT to AIRT, CVIRT can eliminate interference caused by differences in the base temperatures of different specimens, thereby more accurately quantifying the relative fluctuation of the temperature field. Figure 9 shows the evolutionary curves of each IRT metric for different prefabricated fissure inclinations, plotted by integrating the overall CVIRT with the previously used overall average infrared radiation temperature (AIRT), standard deviation of infrared radiation temperature (SDIRT), and local maximum infrared radiation temperature (MAXIRT).

Change curves of different IRT indexes of specimens: (a–d) present red sandstone specimens with prefabricated fissure angles of 0°, 30°, 45°, and 60°, respectively, where within each figure, the labels I–IV correspond to the four stages: compaction, elastic deformation, destabilization, and failure. Data in the figure are overall infrared radiation measurements on the surface of rock samples.

For 0° rock specimens, the coefficient of variation of infrared radiation temperature (CVIRT), standard deviation of infrared radiation temperature (SDIRT), and maximum infrared radiation temperature (MAXIRT) exhibited minimal variation during the compaction stage, elastic deformation stage, and destabilization stage. In contrast, the average infrared radiation temperature (AIRT) showed a continuous increasing trend with rising pressure, increasing by 0.005°C, 0.012°C, and 0.025°C during the compaction, elastic deformation, and destabilization stages, respectively, indicating superior sensitivity. However, in the failure stage, CVIRT and SDIRT increased abruptly coinciding with rock failure, whereas AIRT and maximum infrared radiation temperature (MIRT) showed no such abrupt increase.

For 30° rock specimens, all four indices displayed significant sensitivity, with distinct abrupt increases occurring at rock failure. During the first three stages (compaction, elastic deformation, and destabilization), CVIRT and SDIRT increased continuously with increasing load, rising by 0.0075°C and 0.003°C, respectively, by the end of the destabilization stage, while MaXIRT showed no obvious change. AIRT did not exhibit a steady increase after entering the elastic deformation stage, with minimal variation during the destabilization stage, but a slight temperature rise of 0.008°C prior to failure—potentially serving as a precursor to damage.

For 45° and 60° rock specimens, all four indices increased abruptly during rock failure. During the first three stages, CVIRT and SDIRT followed consistent trends, both increasing continuously with load, with respective increments by the end of the destabilization stage. In contrast, AIRT fluctuated, with a more pronounced downward trend as the prefabricated fissure inclination increased. In both 45° and 60° specimens, MaXIRT increased significantly overall prior to the destabilization stage, by 0.046°C and 0.040°C, respectively, which could serve as a precursor to the rock entering a destabilized state. A brief exploration into the underlying mechanism reveals that, for low-inclination fissures under loading, the tips are dominated by tensile stress, with crack propagation primarily characterized by tensile splitting. Frictional heating is minimal, and thermal precursors exhibit slow temperature rises. In contrast, for high-inclination fissures, the proportion of shear stress at the tips increases significantly; cracks propagate via shear slip, and friction at the fissure surfaces intensifies, leading to thermal precursors with rapid fluctuations.

These observations indicate that rock infrared radiation temperature (IRT) indices exhibit distinct responses to varying fissure inclinations, with each index showing stage-dependent sensitivity during rock damage. This highlights the limitations of relying solely on individual indices to characterize the relationship between IRT and stress evolution. Thus, synthesizing multiple IRT indicators is critical for accurately deciphering the damage and failure processes of loaded rocks.

Prediction of rock damage time

Rock damage time prediction based on 1D-CNN-Bi-LSTM-attention modeling

As revealed in Sect. 3.4 ("Comprehensive Analysis of Infrared Indicators for Prefabricated Fissured Red Sandstone"), distinct quantitative analysis metrics exhibit specific temporal evolutions during rock damage. Consequently, by extracting spatiotemporal information from different metrics—including SDIRT, MAXIRT, and AIRT—it is feasible to predict the rock’s stress curve and, in turn, forecast the timing of rock failure.

Bi-LSTM.

Long short-term memory (LSTM) networks are renowned for their efficiency in capturing long-range dependencies within sequential data. Data pre-processing and model parameters

Considering the discrepancies in rock failure timing under varying conditions and the non-uniformity of data sample specifications, we performed temporal alignment and downsampling on the infrared index data: First, within the full time-series dataset, we identified the rock damage time t and its two adjacent preceding/following moments. Based on the 5:1 temporal frame ratio between infrared indices and rock stress, we extracted the maximum value from every five consecutive infrared index points to align with the corresponding stress value in chronological order. This approach retains the original characteristics of the infrared index data while achieving unification of data specifications between infrared indices and stress, thereby enabling the infrared index data to serve as the input component of the sample dataset. The input data comprise three infrared indices (SDIRT, MAXIRT, and AIRT) and stress measurements from rock specimens with four distinct prefabricated fissure angles, with each angular group containing approximately 10,000 data points.

Discussion

On the selection of infrared radiation temperature indicators

The core rationale for selecting infrared radiation indicators in this study is their physical correlation with rock damage evolution and capacity to characterize the failure process. As the average surface temperature, AIRT (average infrared radiation temperature) is directly linked to the internal energy dissipation in rocks, offering an intuitive reflection of stress stage division and overall energy states, thus serving as a foundational indicator for macro-scale damage timeline analysis. SDIRT (standard deviation of infrared temperature) quantifies temperature distribution dispersion and is sensitive to local thermal effects from crack propagation, with its fluctuation amplitude positively correlated with crack density and propagation scale, effectively characterizing the non-uniformity of damage evolution—though less suitable for local temperature distribution analysis. CVIRT (coefficient of variation of infrared temperature), a dimensionless indicator, eliminates the influence of absolute temperature values, exhibits distinct multi-stage characteristics, and leverages full IRT field information to capture failure-related IRT anomalies; as this indicator has been thoroughly investigated in prior studies, it is only used for comparative analysis herein52. MAXIRT (local maximum infrared temperature) precisely captures high-temperature localization from crack propagation but is generally susceptible to unavoidable outlier points. In this study, it is applied to local region analysis to provide supplementary validation for AIRT-based interpretations.

1D-CNN-Bi-LSTM-Attention model features introduction

The 1D-CNN-Bi-LSTM-Attention model proposed in this study combines the advantages of convolutional neural networks (CNN), bidirectional long short-term memory networks (Bi-LSTM), and attention mechanisms. CNN is tasked with extracting local spatial features from input data, Bi-LSTM effectively captures temporal dependencies, and the attention mechanism enhances the model’s focus on critical information by dynamically weighting features at key time steps. The synergistic interplay of these three components significantly elevates the accuracy of rock stress time-series prediction, offering a more effective pathway for forecasting rock failure timing.

In the model architecture, a ReLu activation function is introduced after the convolutional layer to enhance nonlinear modeling capabilities. This function mitigates the gradient vanishing problem and accelerates network convergence, thereby improving training efficiency56. To further enhance generalization and suppress overfitting, dropout layers with a rate of 0.3 are incorporated after both the Conv1D and Bi-LSTM layers. This mechanism randomly“drops”activation values of selected neurons during training, compelling the model to learn more robust feature representations. Additionally, an early stopping mechanism is employed during training, terminating the process when validation loss ceases to improve, thus optimizing efficiency and preventing overfitting.

Novelty of the integrated deep learning approach for fractured rock monitoring

Deep learning has been applied in rock mechanics research for infrared radiation (IRT) analysis, such as using artificial neural networks (ANN), random forest regression (RFR), or k-nearest neighbor (KNN) for specific predictions, and convolutional neural networks (CNN) for damage classification55,57–59. However, among the various challenges in rock engineering disaster early warning, there remains a gap in stress damage prediction for fractured rocks, and limited research on how prefabricated fissure inclinations govern changes in IRT indicators and thereby influence thermal predictions over time. Our approach addresses the dual nature of IRT signals in pre-cracked rocks—both instantaneous mutations and long-term trends—by integrating 1D-CNN, bidirectional long short-term memory (Bi-LSTM), and an attention mechanism. This combination extracts local abrupt features, captures long-range spatiotemporal dependencies, and emphasizes critical periods for early warning, all grounded in the physical Link between IRT characteristics and damage evolution. For instance, 1D-CNN targets thermal anomalies induced by crack propagation, Bi-LSTM tracks stress-related thermal trends, and the attention mechanism highlights transitions between tensile and shear failure. Unlike prior studies that merely quantify IRT indicators or establish statistical angle-mechanics relationships, we establish a cross-scale correlation among"mechanical behavior–IRT characteristics–fissure angle."By innovatively focusing on thermal evolution at crack tips, we quantify the relationship between fissure inclination and tip thermal peaks—a rarely explored connection. Merging data-driven modeling with rock mechanics, this work transcends mere data fitting, advancing the application of deep learning in fractured rock monitoring.

Engineering significance of research on the influence of fissure inclination on rock failure modes

The transition from tension-dominated to shear-dominated failure with increasing fissure angles holds direct implications for practical rock engineering. In tunnel excavation, rock masses with low-inclination fissures (0°–30°) are prone to tensile roof collapses, as tensile failures propagate along fissure planes, demanding enhanced roof support measures. For high-inclination fissures (45°–60°), shear-induced sidewall slips become prevalent due to significant lateral displacement accompanying shear failure, necessitating reinforced sidewall anchoring. In mining operations, predicting failure modes based on fissure angles allows for adjusted mining sequences—such as stepwise pressure relief in high-inclination fissure zones—to mitigate collapse risks. This transition also guides the placement of monitoring systems: focusing on roof thermal anomalies for low angles and sidewall shear-induced thermal fluctuations for high angles, enabling targeted stability assessments and timely intervention in engineering sites.

Future research directions

Rock type, mineral composition, and defect geometry are likely to influence fracturing and thermal behavior. As a brittle rock, red sandstone exhibits pronounced infrared anomalies during failure, which aids in deciphering rock failure timing. Further investigations into diverse rock types, mineral compositions, and defect geometries are essential to validate the findings of this study. Additionally, developing environmental compensation algorithms to support in-situ IRT monitoring would enhance engineering applicability. Finally, under complex real-world conditions, rock surface fractures rarely exist in isolation; thus, expanding the hybrid model to characterize rock masses with intersecting fractures under multi-axial stress is imperative.

Conclusions

This study systematically investigates the damage evolution mechanism and infrared radiation (IRT) response characteristics of red sandstone with different pre-existing fissure angles through uniaxial compression tests and infrared thermography technology. Based on the 1D-CNN-Bi-LSTM-Attention neural network model, it realizes the prediction of stress changes during the failure process of pre-fissured red sandstone, thereby enabling the prediction of the timing of rock failure. The research results provide theoretical and technical support for real-time damage monitoring and failure early warning of rock mass with weak planes, and promote the application of infrared thermography and deep learning in geological engineering. For example, in tunnel monitoring, infrared thermal imagers and model algorithms can be integrated into existing monitoring systems to collect IRT data on the rock mass surface in real-time, and the model can output damage warning time to provide a basis for support decisions. In mining engineering, multiple sets of infrared sensors can be arranged for the surrounding rock of the stope, and combined with the model, focused early warning can be carried out for high-risk areas (such as areas with well-developed high-inclination fissures) to reduce the risk of collapse. This study also provides an effective reference for rock mass stability evaluation. The specific conclusions are as follows:

(1) This study demonstrates that the inclination angle of pre-existing cracks governs the failure mode, peak stress, and surface infrared radiation temperature evolution during the failure of pre-fractured sandstone. As the inclination increases, the failure mode transitions gradually from tension-dominated to shear-dominated, with the peak strength of the specimens increasing, which correlates with the horizontal projection area of cracks and stress component distribution.

(2) All infrared radiation temperature indicators exhibit a abrupt temperature surge due to frictional heating during the failure stage, with the amplitude increasing with pre-crack inclination. Global and local measurements of average infrared radiation temperature (AIRT) diverge: global AIRT remains lower than local values with smaller fluctuations. For high-inclination specimens, global AIRT decreases prior to failure due to gas intrusion. The standard deviation of infrared temperature (SDIRT) increases significantly with the proportion of shear cracks, reflecting enhanced thermal heterogeneity. The local maximum infrared temperature (MAXIRT) captures high-temperature localization at crack tips, demonstrating pronounced sensitivity to failure precursors.

(3) The proposed 1D-CNN-Bi-LSTM-Attention hybrid model integrates the spatiotemporal features of infrared indicators to enable high-precision prediction of stress-time curves for loaded rocks. Validated via K-fold cross-validation (K = 4), the model outperforms traditional two-layer LSTM models, with R2 > 0.99 and RMSE < 1.0 across all samples. This achievement ensures effective fitting of stress-time curves and demonstrates robust generalization capability and superior performance for specimens with varying prefabricated fissure inclination angles.
