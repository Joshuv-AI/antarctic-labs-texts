Associations of prenatal exposure to bisphenols and phthalates with the fetoplacental ratio in the New York University Children’s Health and Environment Study (NYU CHES)
Authors: Ayushi Bommireddipalli, Jacqueline A Erler, Duong Q Nguyen, Sara Hyman, Emma Spring, Eleanor A Medley, Kurunthachalam Kannan, Shilpi S Mehta-Lee, Leonardo Trasande, Whitney Cowell, Linda G Kahn
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFetoplacental ratio (FPR), the ratio of birthweight (BW) to placental weight (PW), indicates placental efficiency. Changes in FPR are linked to poor pregnancy outcomes and child health risks. Bisphenols and phthalates are endocrine disruptors found in plastics and personal care products that can cross the placenta and have been linked to pregnancy complications and adverse child health outcomes. We examined prenatal exposure to these chemicals in relation to FPR as a possible explanation for these risks. Our analysis included 393 participants in the New York University Children’s Health and Environment Study with data on prenatal chemical exposure, BW, and PW from singleton live births. We calculated molar sums of bisphenols and of metabolites of low and high molecular weight (LMW, HMW) phthalates, diethylhexyl phthalate (DEHP), and antiandrogenic phthalates. Linear regression models were adjusted for maternal age, prepregnancy BMI, parity, gestational age at delivery, and fetal sex. Analyses were stratified by fetal sex. HMW were positively associated with FPR in the combined fetal sex sample (beta=0.26, [0.01, 0.50]) with a similar trend for DEHP and antiandrogenic phthalates (betas=0.21 [−0.04, 0.45] and 0.21 [−0.04, 0.45], respectively). Stratified analyses revealed that these results were driven by females, among whom LMW were also associated with higher FPR (beta=0.23 [0.003, 0.45]). No associations were observed between chemicals and BW in either combined or sex-stratified models. In contrast, HMW, LMW, DEHP, di-n-octylphthalate and bisphenols had negative associations with PW, suggesting placental growth as a target for phthalate-mediated endocrine disruption.Keywords: Bisphenols, Phthalates, Endocrine-Disrupting Chemicals, Fetoplacental Ratio, Birthweight, Placenta

Introduction

Endocrine-disrupting chemicals, such as bisphenols and phthalates, are known to affect human pregnancy and fetal development, but associations with placental development and function that might explain these effects are underexplored (Rolfo et al., 2020). Fetoplacental ratio (FPR), the ratio of birthweight (BW) to placental weight (PW), is a clinical proxy for placental efficiency, as it reflects how effectively the placenta supports fetal growth relative to its size (Hayward et al., 2016; Zhang, 2023). Perturbation of the FPR may reflect alterations in nutrient exchange and placental development. Lower FPR has been associated with higher offspring risk of hypertension, high blood pressure, cardiovascular disease, and stroke in adulthood (Hayward et al., 2016; Barker et al., 1993). Higher FPR has been associated with upregulated nutrient transfer but can also signify issues such as insufficient oxygen supply to the fetus (Takemoto et al., 2018). While FPR has been studied in the context of maternal and child health, less is known about the relation between maternal exposure to environmental chemicals and FPR.

Bisphenols and phthalates are synthetic chemicals used as plasticizers and fragrance stabilizers in a wide range of daily-use products. High molecular weight (HMW) phthalates are commonly found in food and personal/household product packaging, polyvinyl chloride (PVC) flooring, and medical equipment, whereas low molecular weight (LMW) phthalates are found in dyes, pharmaceutical coatings, and scented products such as perfumes and lotions (Metcalfe et al., 2022). Phthalates have short half-lives in humans, but chronic exposure can adversely affect the endocrine system, resulting in long-term negative health outcomes in adults and children (Wang and Qian, 2021). Due to growing concerns over the health effects of the HMW phthalate, di-(2-ethylhexyl) phthalate (DEHP), which has been increasingly regulated, replacement phthalates such as di-n-octyl phthalate (DNOP) and di-isononyl phthalate (DINP) have been introduced (Nagorka et al., 2022). Bisphenol A (BPA) is a commonly manufactured industrial compound found in polycarbonate plastics, thermal receipt papers, dental sealants, and epoxy resins used in beverage and food containers (Nagorka et al., 2022). BPA can migrate from these materials and contaminate food, beverages, and the surrounding environment, resulting in ubiquitous population exposure (Cowell et al., 2023). Concerns regarding negative health effects have led to the gradual substitution of BPA with structurally similar compounds, such as bisphenol S (BPS), but the safety of these replacements remains understudied (Pelch et al., 2019).

Bisphenols and phthalates have been classified as endocrine-disrupting chemicals because of their interference with hormonal actions in biological systems, resulting in adverse reproductive, neurodevelopmental, growth, and metabolic outcomes (Gore et al., 2015). Bisphenols can bind to both nuclear and membrane estrogen receptors, affecting body weight and tumor formation, among other biological processes. Recent evidence suggests that BPA also has antiandrogenic properties that may affect male reproductive function by competing with 5α-dihydrotestosterone (DHT) for binding to androgen receptors (Cimmino et al., 2020). Phthalates have been shown to have both androgenic and antiandrogenic effects in humans and animals, and have been associated with altered thyroid, estrogen, and progesterone activity, as well as increased oxidative stress in adults, including pregnant women and children (Seymore et al., 2022). Both bisphenols and phthalates can cross the placenta and be detected in amniotic fluid, making them important chemicals to study in the context of fetal growth and development (Li et al., 2025).

Increasing evidence suggests that prenatal exposure to bisphenols and phthalates is associated with placental development and function. However, few studies have examined associations with placental measures since they are often not recorded in low-risk, uncomplicated deliveries. Using the FPR as an outcome measure can provide insight on how the fetus is growing relative to the placenta. A limited number of studies have reported associations between phthalates and placental outcomes, including reduced placental weight, altered microvasculature, and increased calcification. However, among these studies, none have included replacement chemicals such as BPS, DNOP, and DINP (Jovanovic et al., 2024; Rosen et al., 2024). To address this gap and better understand these associations, we examined prenatal exposure to a wide range of bisphenols and phthalates in relation to birthweight, placental disc weight, and FPR within a diverse New York City birth cohort.

Materials and methods

Study population

Participants included pregnant women from the New York University Children’s Health and Environment Study (NYU CHES), an ongoing, clinically enrolled, prospective cohort study with 4439 participants as of October 2024. The cohort has been previously described in detail elsewhere (the NYU Children’s Health and Environment Study Team et al., 2020). Study participants were recruited from three NYU-affiliated hospitals (NYU Langone-Manhattan, NYU Langone-Brooklyn, and Bellevue Hospital). Eligibility criteria included > 18 years of age, < 18 weeks gestational age, and fluency in English, Spanish, or Chinese.

From the NYU CHES cohort, we excluded pregnancies that resulted in miscarriage, termination, or stillbirth (N = 421), as well as participants without prenatal urinary bisphenol and phthalate concentration measurements available (N = 2398). High missingness in exposure data reflects the study design, as chemicals were assayed in only a subset of the cohort, beginning with the earliest enrollees, due to cost constraints. Exposure assessment was prioritized among those who had three urine samples, then two or one sample, respectively. We further excluded multifetal gestations (N = 78), as well as participants missing placental weight measurements (N = 1149). Routine placenta collection did not begin until partway into the study, resulting in limited overlap between exposure and outcome data. Some placental weights were also missing due to limited staff availability and refusal by the participants. The final sample size was N = 393 (Supplemental Figure 1). NYU CHES has been approved by the Institutional Review Board of the NYU Grossman School of Medicine. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Bisphenol and phthalate analysis

Prenatal bisphenol and phthalate metabolite concentrations were measured in urine samples collected up to three times during pregnancy: < 18 weeks, 18–25 weeks, and > 25 weeks gestation. Upon absorption, phthalates are metabolized into monoesters that have short half-lives and are excreted primarily in urine (Wang et al., 2019). Similarly, bisphenols are excreted in urine and have half-lives of < 24 h. Urinary levels of these chemicals are considered reliable short-term markers of internal exposure, especially when repeated measures are averaged. Samples were collected in polyethylene containers and aliquoted into bisphenol- and phthalate-free tubes that were stored at – 80°C until analysis. Twenty-two phthalate metabolites were measured using enzymatic deconjugation of glucuronidated and sulfated monoesters, followed by solid phase extraction (SPE) coupled with high-performance liquid chromatography electrospray ionization tandem mass spectrometry (HPLC-ESI-MS/MS), as previously described in detail (Cowell et al., 2023; Gaylord et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2022). Eight bisphenols (BPA, BPAF, BPAP, BPB, BPF, BPP, BPS, and BPZ) were analyzed using HPLC-ESI-MS/MS. With the use of isotopically labeled internal standards, the detection limits in 500 μL of urine were < 0.1 ng/mL, sufficient for measuring urinary bisphenols in non-occupationally exposed subjects (Calafat et al., 2008). Quality assurance and quality control procedures included the use of method blanks, spiked blanks, and matrix-spiked samples with duplicates during chemical analyses (Kannan et al., 2021). Phthalate metabolites were grouped based on their molecular weight, parent compounds, and endocrine-disrupting properties: ∑LMW, ∑HMW, ∑DEHP, ∑DINP, ∑DNOP, and ∑antiandrogenic phthalates (Howdeshell et al., 2017). Molar concentrations of the grouped chemicals were calculated as: [(concentration in ng/mL) × (1/molecular weight) × (1/10−3)] + [(concentration in ng/mL) × (1/molecular weight) × (1/10−3)] (Philips et al., 2018).

To account for urine dilution, chemical concentrations were standardized for creatinine concentrations at each prenatal timepoint using the Boeniger method (Boeniger et al., 1993). The median creatinine concentration for each batch was calculated, then each sample’s chemical concentration was multiplied by the batch-specific median creatinine concentration and divided by the creatinine concentration of the sample. Because bisphenols and phthalates are non-persistent and a single spot urine sample may not accurately reflect overall exposure, we then averaged creatinine-adjusted chemical concentrations across the available prenatal samples within each subject to represent exposure across gestation. Finally, pregnancy-averaged exposures were natural log-transformed to reduce the influence of outliers on linear regression models.

Outcome variables

Birthweight (BW):

Values were collected from participants’ electronic medical records.

Placental disc weight (PW):

Placentas that were not immediately sent to pathology were weighed by trained study staff within four hours of delivery. Following scale calibration, the unprocessed placenta with the umbilical cord and membranes still attached was first weighed in a container, and the total weight was recorded in grams (g). The placenta was then removed from the scale, and the container (with any remaining blood) was weighed, and the container weight was recorded. The pre-processed placental weight was calculated as placenta-plus-container weight minus the container weight. Following removal of the membranes and umbilical cord, the processed placenta was then weighed in the container (tared), and the PW was recorded. This disc weight measurement was used in our analysis.

Staff-measured PW data were cleaned according to the following procedure. We verified any outlier values (<160 g or >640 g) against pathology reports or measurements found in the electronic medical record (Epic), if available. If our measured value matched the patient’s pathology report and/or medical chart, the value was retained; otherwise, the value in the pathology report/medical record was used.

For placentas missing staff-measured PW data, we applied the following strategy. If the participant had a pathology report that contained PW, the pathology report value was used without adjustment. Some placentas had neither a pathology report nor a staff-measured PW but had a staff-measured pre-processed weight. In these cases, the PW value was imputed by discounting the pre-processed weight by the average difference between the pre-processed weight and PW in our sample (24 %). Placentas that lacked a pathology report, a staff-measured pre-processed weight, and a staff-measured PW were missing placental weight data.

Fetoplacental ratio (FPR):

The fetoplacental ratio was calculated by dividing BW (g) by PW (g).

Covariates

Covariates were selected a priori based on prior literature and causal considerations. The following variables were evaluated for their role as potential confounders, effect modifiers, or precision variables: race/ethnicity (Hispanic, non-Hispanic White, non-Hispanic Black, Asian, other, and multiple race), maternal age (years), insurance status (public vs. private), education status (high school or less, some college but no degree, associate degree, bachelor’s degree, post-graduate degree), gestational age at delivery (GA, weeks), pre-pregnancy body mass index (BMI, kg/m2), parity (nulliparous vs. parous), and fetal sex (male vs. female).

Consistent with prior literature, maternal age, and race/ethnicity were identified as sociodemographic factors associated with phthalate exposure (Liu et al., 2022). These variables, among others presented in Table 2, were evaluated for associations with the outcome measures to inform covariate inclusion. Race/ethnicity was not associated with the outcome measures and excluded to avoid redundant adjustment in the context of a modest sample size. Maternal age, prepregnancy BMI, parity, GA, and fetal sex were retained in the final model as precision variables due to their statistically significant associations with at least one outcome measure.

Demographic variables were collected via self-reported questionnaires, whereas pre-pregnancy BMI, parity, and fetal sex were obtained from participants’ electronic medical records. When pre-pregnancy BMI was unavailable, BMI at the initial prenatal visit was used.

Statistical analysis

We calculated descriptive statistics to evaluate distributions of participant characteristics and covariates. We conducted bivariate analyses (Spearman correlations and t-tests, as appropriate) to assess associations of covariates with outcomes. Covariates that were sufficiently associated with any of our outcomes (p-value <0.1) were included in our adjusted models as covariates or precision variables. These included maternal age, prepregnancy BMI, parity, GA, and fetal sex. We fit covariate-adjusted linear regression models to estimate associations of the bisphenol and phthalate exposure groupings with BW, PW, and FPR. To explore potential effect modification by fetal sex, we conducted sex-stratified analyses; however, we did not formally test for statistical interaction due to our modest sample size. We also conducted exploratory analyses of associations between the individual chemical concentrations and the three outcome variables as shown in Supplementary Tables 1–3. The threshold of statistical significance was set at p-value < 0.05. Sensitivity analyses were performed excluding preterm births (<37 weeks gestation) as well as using an alternate method for modeling the relation between BW and PW. Previous studies have highlighted that spurious associations between FPR and biological measures of placental efficiency, such as rates of nutrient transfer per gram placenta, may result if the association between BW and PW is non-linear. To address this problem, we used the non-linear FPR (BW0.75/PW) as part of our sensitivity analysis (Christians et al., 2018; Salafia et al., 2009). To assess any potential non-linear relationships between chemical exposure groups and FPR, we also categorized the exposure into quartiles and re-ran linear regression models. All statistical analyses were conducted in SAS Studio & RStudio version 4.4.1.

Results

Univariate analysis

Of the analytic sample (N = 393), 192 (48.8 %) were parous, 175 (44.5 %) identified as Hispanic, 159 (40.5 %) identified as non-Hispanic White, and 170 (43.7 %) were publicly insured. Approximately 111 (28.8 %) had a high school degree or less, 87 (22.5 %) had a bachelor’s degree, and 128 (33.2 %) had a postgraduate degree. Slightly more than half of the fetuses (53.4 %) were male. Average maternal age was 32.2 years (standard deviation [SD] = 5.4), average pre-pregnancy BMI was 26.3 kg/m2 (SD = 6.0), and GA was 39.1 weeks (SD = 1.5). Complete sample characteristics are displayed in Table 2. BW PW, and FPR were normally distributed. The median BW was 3320 g, median PW was 431.2 g, and median FPR was 7.7, which are all comparable to values reported among births in large US population-based cohorts, including the National Collaborative Perinatal Project (Baptiste-Roberts et al., 2009).

Bivariate analysis

Maternal age, pre-pregnancy BMI, parity, and GA were each associated with at least one outcome at p-value < 0.10 and were included in subsequent regression analyses. Variables related to socioeconomic status such as insurance status, race/ethnicity, and education were highly collinear and not associated with any of the outcome variables, hence were not included in adjusted models.

Associations with the fetoplacental ratio (FPR)

∑HMW phthalate metabolites were associated with significantly higher FPR in the total sample (beta = 0.26; 95 % confidence interval [CI]: 0.01, 0.50). A positive trend of similar magnitude was observed for ∑DEHP metabolites (beta = 0.21; 95 % CI: −0.03, 0.45) and ∑antiandrogenic phthalate metabolites (beta = 0.21; 95 % CI: −0.03, 0.45), although the 95 % CIs included the null. As observed in Fig. 1 and Supplemental Table 1, all other chemical groupings except ∑DNOP also showed positive, although non-statistically significant, associations with FPR.

Analyses stratified by fetal sex showed that positive associations observed in the total sample were primarily driven by females. Among females, ∑LMW phthalate metabolites (beta = 0.23; 95 % CI: 0.00, 0.45) and ∑DEHP metabolites (beta = 0.31; 95 % CI: 0.01, 0.61) were positively associated with FPR. Associations of ∑bisphenols, ∑HMW phthalate metabolites, and ∑antiandrogenic phthalate metabolites had similar effect sizes, but were not statistically significant. Among males, directionality and magnitude of associations were inconsistent across chemical groupings, and no associations were statistically significant.

As seen in Fig. 2 and Supplemental Table 2, we observed no statistically significant associations between any chemical exposure and BW, either among the total sample or when stratified by fetal sex.

Associations with placental disc weight (PW)

In the total sample, associations of chemicals with PW were null or in the negative direction (Fig. 3 and Supplemental Table 3). Stratified analyses showed sex-specific associations. Although the precision was limited due to the smaller sample size, the associations of bisphenols and all phthalate groupings except ∑DINP were consistently negative among females. Mono-(2-ethyl-5-oxohexyl) phthalate (mEOHP), a HMW metabolite included in both the DEHP and antiandrogenic groupings, was significantly associated with lower PW (beta = −18.89; 95% CI: −36.11, −1.68) in females. In contrast, among males, exposure to ∑DNOP, represented by mono(3-carboxypropyl) phthalate (mCPP), was positively associated with PW (beta = 14.42; 95 % CI: 0.71, 28.14) (Supplemental Table 3).

Sensitivity analyses

To ensure that the observed negative associations with PW and positive associations with FPR were not driven by preterm deliveries, we conducted a sensitivity analysis excluding participants with GA < 37 weeks (N = 23; 5.87 %). The magnitude and direction of associations remained consistent with the main findings, as seen in Supplemental Tables 4–6. Another sensitivity analysis was conducted using a non-linear FPR (BW0.75/PW) (Supplemental Table 7). Compared with the linear formulation of FPR, the non-linear FPR showed consistent results, although the magnitude of effect estimates was smaller, indicating that the non-linear FPR is more conservative. In our quartile-based exposure model, results were consistent with the main analysis (Supplementary Table 8). In comparison with the continuous exposure models, some of the quartile models showed a slight non-linear trend; however, no evidence of nonmonotonicity was observed. Overall, these sensitivity analyses supported the robustness of the association between the exposures and outcomes across different analytical approaches.

Discussion

In this study, we examined relations of prenatal exposure to bisphenols and phthalates with measures of placental efficiency and fetal growth. Overall, both bisphenols and most phthalate groupings were positively associated with FPR, driven by associations among female fetuses. Specifically, in the total sample, ∑HMW phthalate metabolites were significantly associated with higher FPR, and among females there were significant positive associations observed for ∑LMW phthalate metabolites and ∑DEHP metabolites, with similar trends for ∑HMW phthalate metabolites, ∑antiandrogenic metabolites, and ∑bisphenols. We observed no associations of chemicals with BW, either in the total sample or in fetal sex-stratified samples. However, we did observe generally negative associations with PW that appeared to be similarly driven by associations among females, although precision was limited in the stratified analyses.

A higher FPR, as we observed in relation to bisphenol and phthalate exposure, reflects greater fetal mass relative to the placenta. From a clinical and biological perspective, this pattern may represent placental compensation, where nutrient transfer is maintained through changes to vascularity, blood flow, diffusion, and nutrient transport despite placental growth constraints. A similar pattern has been observed in relation to other environmental conditions, including environmental chemicals and maternal disorders (Dimasuay et al., 2016; Sferruzzi-Perri et al., 2023).

While this observed difference is likely not clinically relevant on an individual level, it represents a subtle change in placental function and serves as an important datapoint on how environmental exposures shape the intrauterine environment. This analysis aims to contribute to a growing body of knowledge on placental adaptations to endocrine disruptors that may have implications across the life course, warranting longitudinal studies and further investigation in large cohorts.

Previous studies have highlighted associations between prenatal exposure to endocrine-disrupting chemicals, particularly phthalates, with adverse birth and placental outcomes. A meta-analysis of 36 studies reported a negative relation between the LMW phthalate metabolite monobutyl phthalate (mBP) and BW, with results driven by females (Jin et al., 2023). Another study showed that the individual metabolite mono-isononyl phthalate (mINP) and ∑DEHP metabolites were associated with impaired vasculature development of the placenta, an indicator of suboptimal efficiency. In addition, the LMW phthalate metabolite monoethyl phthalate (mEP) was associated with greater placenta stiffness and monobenzyl phthalate (mBzP) was associated with higher calcification, both of which could increase the likelihood of placental complications (Rosen et al., 2024). Similar to the present study, an analysis in the Environment and Reproductive Health (EARTH) cohort of couples seeking infertility treatment also observed a significant negative association between ∑DEHP and PW (Mustieles et al., 2019). In the French mother-child cohort SEPAGES, mBzP was associated with lower PW and placental-to-fetal weight ratio (PFR) (Jovanovic et al., 2024). This is consistent with our findings of associations of chemicals with higher FPR (inverse of PFR) and lower PW.

Multiple biological mechanisms could underlie our findings. Phthalates target peroxisome proliferator-activated receptors that are involved in key placental development processes such as trophoblast proliferation, migration, and invasion (Warner et al., 2021), which might explain our observation of an association of phthalates with lower PW. A recent prospective cohort study from the Ma’anshan Birth Cohort in China found sex-specific associations between prenatal phthalate exposure and placental oxidative stress: mBP and monomethyl phthalate (mMP) were associated with higher HIF1α expression in male fetuses, and mEOHP was associated with lower expression of specific mRNAs such as HO-1, HIF1α, and GRP78 in female fetuses (Wang et al., 2024). Other studies have reported that prenatal phthalate exposure is associated with higher levels of human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG) in placentas of female fetuses and lower levels in male fetuses, indicating the sexually dimorphic effects of phthalates on placental function (Adibi et al., 2015). Mono-(2-ethylhexyl) phthalate (mEHP) has been shown to differentially affect placental gene expression based on concentration, sex of the fetus, and trophoblast cell type (Lapehn et al., 2023). Prenatal phthalate exposure can also be associated with molecular changes in feto-placental tissue during pregnancy, specifically with the gene CGA, coding for hCG, levels of which are associated with fetal sex (Adibi et al., 2017).

A notable strength of this study is the collection of placentas and measurement of PW at birth from uncomplicated deliveries, which is not routinely done as part of clinical care. Another strength of this study lies in the measurement of bisphenols and phthalates at multiple timepoints during pregnancy. Evidence suggests that bisphenol and phthalate metabolite concentrations may vary considerably across repeated sampling, thus averaging concentrations in specimens collected at multiple timepoints provides a better proxy for overall bisphenol and phthalate exposure compared to a single spot measurement (Casas et al., 2018). Our sensitivity analysis excluding preterm births was consistent with our main findings, increasing the robustness of this analysis and reducing the chances that these associations were driven by GA-related effects. The similarities between our main findings and our sensitivity analyses using a non-linear FPR and using quartiles of exposure also suggest that the observed associations are consistent across various functional forms.

Despite its strengths, this study was also subject to certain limitations. Most notably, the sample size was relatively small due to limited overlap between participants with bisphenol and phthalate data, which was measured among the initial participants in the cohort, and those with PW measurements, which commenced later. This limited the statistical power of our analysis, resulting in wide confidence intervals, especially in the sex-stratified results. To assess potential selection bias, we compared maternal and birth characteristics between NYU CHES live deliveries included in the analytical sample and those excluded. As shown in Supplemental Table 9, included and excluded participants were largely similar, suggesting that substantial selection bias is unlikely. Parity differed modestly between groups and was therefore included as a covariate in the models. Despite the limited sample size, consistency in associations observed across the related chemical groupings supports the plausibility of our findings and highlights the need for replication of this analysis in other large cohorts. Furthermore, although we controlled for precision variables and potential confounders, there could still be unmeasured confounding. In particular, information on maternal nutrition during pregnancy was unavailable for this analysis, and dietary factors may affect placental function and growth. Additionally, unmeasured biological factors such as genetic variability in chemical metabolism may also have contributed to residual confounding.

Conclusion

We investigated associations between prenatal exposure to bisphenols and phthalates and FPR, BW, and PW in a New York City-based birth cohort. Consistent trends were observed for phthalate metabolites with respect to FPR in the positive direction and PW in the negative direction. We observed sex-specific differences in relations between these chemicals, especially phthalates, and placenta size, with associations driven primarily by female fetuses. Our results suggest that exposure to these endocrine-disrupting chemicals may lead to restricted placental growth, but not necessarily compromised efficiency, as no associations were observed with BW. Additional studies are needed to replicate these findings in other general population cohorts and to identify potential compensatory mechanisms by which fetal growth is protected even when placental growth has been restricted.
