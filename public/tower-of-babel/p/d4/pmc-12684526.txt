Enhancing Societal Participation for Stroke Survivors With Cognitive Impairments: A Randomized Controlled Trial
Authors: Shih‐Pin Hsu, Te‐Hsun Hung, Yen‐Nung Lin, Jiunn‐Horng Kang, Der‐Sheng Han, Valeria Chiu, Tsan‐Hon Liou, Yi‐Hsuan Wu, Peng‐Sheng Ni, Elizabeth R. Skidmore, Feng‐Hang Chang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundReturning to societal participation is an important goal for stroke survivors, but evidence for effective rehabilitation remains inconclusive. This study evaluated the efficacy of participation‐focused strategy training in improving poststroke participation among community‐dwelling survivors with cognitive impairments.MethodsThis multicenter, randomized trial enrolled stroke survivors with executive function impairments between January 2019 and March 2023. Participants received either strategy training (n=96) or an attention‐control intervention (n=99) in 12 to 15 sessions over 8 weeks. The primary outcome was the postintervention change in participation across productivity, social, and community domains from the baseline, measured by the Participation Measure‐3 Domains, 4 Dimensions. Secondary outcomes included changes in activity performance, self‐efficacy, and global cognition from baseline.ResultsEligible 195 participants were included in the intention‐to‐treat analysis (64 women, 32.8%; median [interquartile range] age: 62 [52–68] years). After adjusting for baseline primary outcome, stroke severity, and dose of regular rehabilitation, the strategy training group showed greater improvements in productivity and social participation compared with the control group (adjusted mean difference between groups [95% CI]: 7.89 [2.61–13.16] (P=0.004) and 0.29 [0.08–0.51] (P=0.009), respectively) from baseline to postintervention. These effects remained without adjustment, but did not persist at 3‐month follow‐up. No significant between‐group differences were observed for any other outcomes.ConclusionsParticipation‐focused strategy training significantly improved productivity and social participation among enrolled stroke survivors compared with the control intervention postintervention. Further research is warranted to confirm these findings in the oldest survivors and to explore approaches for enhancing the long‐term benefits of strategy training.RegistrationURL: https://www.clinicaltrials.gov; Unique identifier: NCT03792061.

Participation in society is considered an ultimate goal throughout stroke rehabilitation. 1 As a complex outcome, participation can be defined as active involvement in life situations across social, community, and productivity contexts. 2 Like most functional outcomes, participation typically improves in the first year poststroke. 3 , 4 However, >60% of stroke survivors attain lower participation levels compared with their prestroke status for years after their stroke. 5 Stroke survivors with participation restrictions may experience difficulty engaging in real‐life activities (eg, returning to work) or reduced participation frequency (eg, less time for social activities), even among those who have recovered significant functional outcomes. 6 This persistent consequence is closely associated with physical and neuropsychological functioning, as well as brain health, 5 , 7 , 8 , 9 negatively impacting quality of life. 10

Despite the importance of participation, there are limited rehabilitation approaches that robustly improve participation outcomes in stroke rehabilitation. 1 The most commonly adopted rehabilitation approaches are bottom‐up, impairment‐oriented interventions that focus on remediating neurological functions such as physical or cognitive functions. 11 However, evidence suggests that such physical or cognitive training has little to no effect on enhancing participation. 11 , 12 , 13 Targeting impairments alone may not address environmental barriers that hinder reengagement in valued activities. 14

Alternative approaches are top‐down interventions that utilize problem‐solving strategies to achieve individualized goals identified by the patient and family. 11 , 15 Recently, a randomized controlled trial (RCT) demonstrated the potential of this approach in improving social participation for stroke survivors transitioning from rehabilitation wards to their homes. 15 However, this study excluded survivors with cognitive impairments, a common sequelae of stroke that could significantly impact participation outcomes. 7 Efficacy testing of top‐down interventions for stroke survivors with cognitive impairments is needed.

One promising top‐down intervention is strategy training. This approach guides cognitively impaired individuals to generate personalized solutions to their life challenges using meta‐cognitive strategies. 16 Pilot studies have supported the feasibility and potential efficacy of strategy training in improving activity and participation outcomes after stroke. 17 , 18 , 19 However, to our knowledge, no robust studies have examined the efficacy of strategy training in enhancing participation among community‐dwelling stroke survivors with cognitive impairments. Therefore, we conducted a RCT to investigate whether a participation‐focused program, the Optimizing Participation after Stroke through Strategy training (OPASS) program, 20 which uses strategy training principles, had a beneficial effect on participation among stroke survivors with cognitive impairments. We hypothesized that the OPASS program would facilitate greater improvements in participation outcomes among stroke survivors compared with a dose‐matched control intervention.

METHODS

The data that support the findings of this study, as well as training materials for the intervention, are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request with permission of all contributing authors.

Study Design

We conducted a multi‐site, assessor‐blinded, prospective RCT of the OPASS program for community‐dwelling stroke survivors with cognitive impairment after stroke. The protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the collaborating hospitals, registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT03792061), and published. 20 Trial conduct and data monitoring were overseen by the Research Ethics Center at each participating site, with annual reviews. Notably, no formal Trial Steering Committee was established for this study. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before randomization. Longitudinal outcome assessments were performed at baseline (T1), postintervention (T2), and 3 (T3), 6, and 12 months after intervention. 20 Blinded assessors conducted face‐to‐face data collection at each hospital or at participants' homes. The present study focuses on the effects of OPASS from T1 to T3 and is reported according to CONSORT guidelines (Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials).

Participants and Recruitment

Community‐dwelling participants were recruited from the rehabilitation outpatient wards of 6 hospitals in northern Taiwan between January 2019 and March 2023. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age 20 years or older, (2) a diagnosis of stroke, (3) the ability to understand Mandarin, and (4) cognitive impairment as indicated by a score of ≥3 on the Mandarin version Quick Executive Interview. 18 , 21 , 22 Individuals with severe aphasia, or neurological or psychiatric diseases (eg, dementia, major depression, substance abuse) that interfered with their participation were excluded (Figure 1). Trained research staff screened for eligibility through medical chart reviews and interviews, in coordination with clinicians at the collaborating sites.

Randomization and Masking

Random allocation numbers were generated using SPSS 19 (IBM), with an equal allocation ratio. The statistician (P.S.N.) and a research assistant, who were not involved in screening, assessment, or intervention, performed the randomization and informed participants of their assignment. Pseudonymous codes were used to denote the treatment groups, masking the allocation results for assessors, the biostatistician, and the principal and co‐principal investigators.

Intervention

Both groups participated in 12 to 15 sessions held twice per week in the participant's preferred setting (eg, outpatient rehabilitation facility or their home), with each session lasting 45 minutes. 20 Routine outpatient rehabilitation was allowed during the study period. In Taiwan, stroke survivors discharged from hospitals, and who are deemed by physiatrists to have potential for recovery are eligible to receive outpatient occupational, physical, and/or speech therapy under the National Health Insurance, regardless of the time since stroke onset. These therapies, delivered by licensed occupational, physical, and speech therapists, target residual impairments and functional goals such as mobility, cognition, and communication, and are typically provided 1 to 5 times per week depending on institutional capacity. 23

OPASS Program

The participation‐focused OPASS intervention program expanded upon the activity‐focused strategy training described by Skidmore et al 16 , 17 , 18 and a feasibility study. 19 During the first 1 to 2 sessions, trained therapists engaged participants in discussions about their typical weekly routines before the stroke. Using this information, therapists applied the Canadian Occupational Performance Measure 24 and the Activity Card Sort 25 to assist participants in identifying and prioritizing 3 to 5 important participation‐focused goals. 2 Sessions 2 to 15 focused on using self‐monitoring and self‐evaluation of activity performance, a global metacognitive strategy, and guided discovery to facilitate the achievement of participation goals. First, therapists asked participants to perform the first activity (ie, the highest priority activity) or to describe how they would perform the activity if the activity could not be completed during the session (eg, buying foreign souvenirs for friends). Participants were then trained to monitor and evaluate their performance when executing the activity and to identify potential challenges that impeded performance. The therapists then taught participants a global strategy and guided them (1) to form a Goal to address the challenges or to improve performance, (2) to develop a working Plan to address the Goal, (3) to Do the plan in a real‐life context, and (4) to Check the actual performance and effectiveness of the plan. Using guided discovery, the therapists helped participants identify personalized strategies to achieve their goals. At the end of each session, the therapists prompted participants to summarize what they had learned and encouraged them to apply the acquired skills and principles to similar challenges with related activities. This process was repeated iteratively until the goal was met and participants moved on to the next activity, or until the end of the 12 to 15 sessions were completed. Dose‐Matched Control Intervention

This control intervention (CI) was designed to match the dose and control for the nonactive components of the OPASS program (ie, the attention of a skilled therapist) using a reflective listening approach. Within the first 2 sessions, participants in the control group engaged in goal‐setting procedures identical to those used in the OPASS group. After goal setting, trained therapists used scripted questions to prompt participants' reflections on their rehabilitation experience. 18 , 20

To ensure adherence to the intervention protocol, all research therapists received standardized training before delivering the intervention (Figure S1). Specifically, we recruited therapists holding bachelor's or master's degrees in rehabilitation or related fields. These therapists were required to review comprehensive in‐house training materials (eg, videos, manuals, published journal articles) and to participate in at least two 1‐hour individual training sessions with senior therapists and the principal investigator. These training sessions verified therapists' knowledge and skills related to the intervention protocol. Additionally, ongoing supervision through weekly team meetings and continuous mentoring from senior therapists was provided to each therapist.

Outcomes

The primary outcomes were the score changes on the Participation Measure‐3 Domains, 4 Dimensions 26 (PM‐3D4D) from T1 to T2. The PM‐3D4D is a multidimensional measure comprising 24 items across 3 domains: productivity (6 items), social (6 items), and community participation (12 items). It evaluates participation performance within each domain in terms of diversity, frequency, desire for change, and perceived difficulty. 26 Participation frequency and difficulty were selected for further analyses due to their higher responsiveness to change over time. 27 The frequency score for each domain was calculated by summing the ratings and dividing by the total number of items within each domain. The difficulty score for each domain was transformed to a 0 to 100 scale using calibrated item parameters. Higher scores in the frequency dimension represent more frequent participation in activities, while higher scores in the difficulty dimension indicate less perceived difficulty during performance. The validity, reliability, and responsiveness of the PM‐3D4D were validated in rehabilitation populations in Taiwan. 26 , 27 , 28 Individuals who achieved an improvement in PM‐3D4D scores that met or exceeded the minimal clinically important difference were defined as intervention responders. 27 The thresholds for clinically important improvement were at least 0.29, 0.23, and 0.39 for productivity, social, and community frequency scores, respectively, and at least 8.72, 6.79, and 8.08 for productivity, social, and community difficulty scores, respectively.

We assessed the feasibility of the intervention using 3 indicators, with higher scores indicating better outcomes: (1) Engagement: Participants' engagement during each session was evaluated by research therapists using the 6‐point Pittsburgh Rehabilitation Participation Scale 32 (range: 1–6, higher indicating greater engagement). (2) Comprehension: Participants' comprehension of intervention content was rated at each session by therapists using a 3‐point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (poor understanding) to 3 (good understanding). 33 (3) Satisfaction: Participant satisfaction with the intervention was measured using the 8‐item Client Satisfaction Questionnaire (range: 8–32), assessed postintervention by blinded assessors. 34

Statistical Analysis

Sample size estimation was based on data from our previous studies 19 , 27 where effect sizes (Cohens' d) for the total frequency and difficulty score ranged from 0.8 to 3.6. Calculations were based on the total frequency and difficulty scores, which accounted for all 3 domains simultaneously. Given a medium effect size of 0.5 and a power of 80%, we estimated that 170 participants would be required to detect a significance of 0.017 for each dimension, adjusting for 3 domains. Assuming an attrition rate of 20%, we estimated that 105 participants would be needed per group.

Following the modified intention‐to‐treat principle, 35 the full analysis set included all participants who attended at least 1 intervention session. Baseline demographic and stroke‐related characteristics were compared using 2‐sample t tests, Mann–Whitney U tests, or χ 2 tests. To determine between‐group effects, mean changes from baseline in primary and secondary outcomes were compared using ANCOVA, with baseline scores of each outcome measure and imbalanced pre‐intervention factors as covariates. To adjust for multiple comparisons of primary outcomes, a P value of <0.017 was considered significant for each participation dimension. Partial eta squared (ηp 2) was reported to estimate treatment effect: small=0.01, medium=0.059, and large=0.138. Additionally, Fisher exact test was used to compare the proportion of intervention responders between groups. Missing values were imputed 20 times using the automatic mode in SPSS 24 (IBM, Armonk, NY), with predictors listed in Table S1.

To examine the robustness of the findings on the primary outcomes, post‐hoc exploratory sensitivity analyses were performed in 2 ways: (1) using different inferential statistics with analysis of variance and Mann–Whitney U test; (2) conducting identical ANCOVA procedures on the subgroup of chronic stroke survivors. To explore the role of sex on participations outcomes, the sex‐by‐group interaction effects as well as sex effects on changed scores from baseline were separately investigated by use of the ANCOVAs. Finally, correlation analysis was used to explore the association between primary outcomes and baseline variables, including age, weekly rehabilitation hours, stroke severity (as measured by the National Institute of Health Stroke Scale), and outcome scores.

RESULTS

A total of 210 participants were enrolled but 14 withdrew consent and 1 passed away before initiation of intervention. Thus, 96 participants in the OPASS group and 99 participants in the CI group were included in the intention‐to‐treat analyses (Figure 1). Among them, 7 participants (OPASS=5) only completed 3 to 5 intervention sessions (Table S2). Notably, >80% of participants were chronic stroke survivors, and 70% received regular rehabilitation for 4 hours or more per week. Most demographic and stroke‐related factors were comparable between groups, except that the OPASS group had greater baseline severity, more regular rehabilitation hours, and lower social participation frequency (Table 1). Severity and weekly hours of rehabilitation were added in ANCOVA.

Participation Frequency

The change in social frequency scores from baseline to postintervention assessment was significantly higher in the OPASS group compared with the CI group (adjusted mean difference between groups 0.29 [95% CI, 0.08–0.51], P=0.009, ηp 2=0.035; Table 2). Participation Difficulty

The change in productivity difficulty from baseline to postintervention assessment was significantly greater in the OPASS group than in the CI group (7.89 [2.61–13.16], P=0.004, ηp 2=0.044; Table 2). No significant between‐group effect was found for the social (P=0.56, ηp 2=0.002) or community domain (P=0.39, ηp 2=0.004). No significant differences were observed between the groups in terms of intervention responders (P=0.06–1; Figure 3B).

Post‐Hoc Exploratory Sensitivity Analysis

Most sensitivity analysis results were consistent with ANCOVA findings (Table S3). The only difference was observed in the community frequency domain, where the OPASS group showed greater improvement than the CI group (P ≤0.009, η2 ≧0.035). However, when accounting for baseline community frequency alone, the significance level was downgraded to 0.039 (ηp 2 =0.022), which is larger than the prespecified threshold. To explore the effects specific to 160 chronic stroke survivors (OPASS=76, CI=84), the results were consistent with those based on the full data set (Table S4). Briefly, the OPASS group showed greater improvement in productivity difficulty score (P=0.011, ηp 2=0.041) and social frequency (P=0.008, ηp 2=0.061).

Secondary Outcomes

Follow‐Up of Participation

Both groups showed within‐group improvements (change scores) across all PM‐3D4D domains and dimensions. However, no significant between‐group effects were observed at this time point (all P >0.57, ηp 2≤0.002; Table 2). The absence of significant between‐group differences at follow‐up, compared with postintervention, may reflect later improvements in participation within the CI group. The results were consistent across analyses based on the imputed missing data or on the chronic survivors (Table S4).

Activity, Self‐Efficacy, and Cognition

From baseline to postintervention, no significant between‐group differences were found for the Activity Measure for Post‐Acute Care (all P >0.17, ηp 2 ≤0.01), the General Self‐Efficacy Scale (P=0.17, ηp 2=0.01), or the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (P=0.29, ηp 2=0.006; Table 3). From baseline to the 3‐month follow‐up, no significant between‐group effects were observed for any of the aforementioned outcomes (all P >0.16, ηp 2 ≤0.011; Table 3). The results remained stable across analyses based on the imputed missing data.

Exploring the Role of Sex on Participations Following Intervention

Sex did not moderate between‐group PM‐3D4D differences across time points (T1 to T2, T1 to T3), as indicated by nonsignificant sex‐by‐group interaction effects (Figure 4). These effects exhibited negligible effect sizes across all participation domains: productivity (frequency: P=0.85–0.89, ηp 2 <0.01; difficulty: P=0.51–0.99, ηp 2 <0.01), social (frequency: P=0.55–0.89, ηp 2 <0.01; difficulty: P=0.15–0.95, ηp 2 ≦0.01), and community participation (frequency: P=0.83–0.99, ηp 2<0.01; difficulty: P=0.65–0.83, ηp 2<0.01). Similarly, sex‐related changes in PM‐3D4D scores from baseline were not significant, demonstrating effect sizes ranging from negligible to small (all P=0.12–0.87, ηp 2=0.001–0.015).

Association Analysis

Tables S5 through S10 present the correlation matrices exploring the association between baseline variables and primary outcomes. For all participants, baseline scores of each primary outcome showed the strongest correlation coefficients among all significant variables (r=−0.36 to −0.44). There is a moderate relationship between baseline social frequency scores and their change scores in both groups (r=−0.3 for OPASS; r=−0.44 for CI); meanwhile, there is a moderate relationship between baseline productivity difficulty scores and their change scores in both groups (r=−0.43 for both groups, Figure 5). Age, months since stroke, and pre‐intervention functions (cognitive, motor, activity, and self‐efficacy) showed either nonsignificant or weak associations with PM‐3D4D changes after intervention, regardless of the treatment group. This suggests that the OPASS program could be implemented for stroke survivors with varying impairments and demographics to achieve similar outcomes.

Feasibility

Most participants showed moderate‐to‐high engagement throughout the intervention period, as indicated by median scores of 5 or above (the lower quartile was 4) on the Pittsburgh Rehabilitation Participation Scale for nearly all sessions, except the initial session (median=4; see Figure S2). Participants' comprehension of the OPASS intervention improved progressively: although initially, up to 10% demonstrated poor understanding, this number decreased to between 0% and 3% in subsequent sessions. Correspondingly, the proportion of participants rated as having good comprehension increased steadily, from ≈40% at the beginning to >80% by session 11 (Figure S3). Regarding satisfaction, both groups reported moderate‐to‐high satisfaction. The OPASS group demonstrated a mean±SD Client Satisfaction Questionnaire score of 30.4 (3.7) with a median (interquartile range) of 32 (32). Similarly, the control intervention group had a mean score of 29.4 (4.3) and median score of 32 (27–32).

DISCUSSION

This study is the first RCT with adequate power to investigate the efficacy of the OPASS program in improving participation among community‐dwelling stroke survivors with cognitive impairments. Our findings indicate that, regardless of age, stroke chronicity, and cognitive impairment level, the repeated use of meta‐cognitive strategies significantly enhanced poststroke participation immediately after the intervention, as evidenced by improvements in social frequency and productivity difficulty. However, no sustained effects were observed in the longer term, suggesting that while the OPASS program may accelerate participation improvements compared with the control intervention, these benefits may not persist over time.

People with poststroke cognitive impairments often experience unsatisfactory activity and participation outcomes, yet they are frequently excluded from rehabilitation trials. 7 Strategy training has been used to optimize the recovery of daily activity functions in acute stroke inpatients with cognitive impairments. 17 , 18 Our study demonstrated that strategy training can be successfully adapted for use during the subacute and chronic stages of stroke, showing clinically and statistically significant benefits in selected participation domains. Although the effect sizes were small to medium (comparable to those observed with exercise interventions 12 ), these findings are encouraging given the limited evidence supporting specific rehabilitation methods for improving poststroke participation. 1 , 36 Various personal (such as age, education, and chronic health conditions), stroke‐related (including severity, mobility, and cognitive functioning), and environmental factors have been reported to influence participation recovery in the years following stroke. 4 , 9 , 37 , 38 , 39 Most of these factors were well‐controlled in our study. Our post‐hoc analyses suggested that the baseline level of participation might be negatively associated with changes in participation, indicating that stroke survivors with lower participation frequency or higher perceived participation difficulty may be more responsive to additional interventions.

The effects of the OPASS program varied across different dimensions and domains of participation. One possible explanation is the varying responsiveness of each PM‐3D4D subscale. 27 Subscales with small‐to‐moderate responsiveness (standardized response measure=0.3–0.6) showed significant differences between the groups. Subscales with lower responsiveness may not adequately capture the intervention's effects, while those with higher responsiveness might be too sensitive, making them more likely to detect changes due to nonspecific effects from the control intervention. Another explanation relates to the complex nature of participation, particularly in the chronic phase of stroke. Although we accounted for a wide range of confounders, other significant factors, such as differences in social networks, mood, fatigue, balance efficacy, and walking capacity, may have influenced our findings. 38 , 39 , 40 For instance, balance and walking capacity are crucial for community participation. 9 Additionally, the significant reduction in productivity difficulty observed in the OPASS group, without a corresponding increase in productivity frequency, suggests that while participants developed strategies for returning to productive activities, such as paid work, these strategies may not have been sufficient to influence employers, possibly due to financial constraints. 41 Further research is needed to explore these and other factors in more detail.

Contrary to our expectations, this study did not find significant differences in participation between the groups during the 3‐month follow‐up. Additionally, the lack of differences in activity function between the groups was unexpected and inconsistent with findings from previous pilot RCTs on strategy training. 17 , 18 The stage of stroke recovery may be a key factor contributing to this discrepancy. 4 , 37 Skidmore et al. 17 , 18 conducted initial strategy training trials with acute stroke inpatients. A pilot study involving subacute stroke survivors <3 months poststroke showed a small‐to‐medium effect on participation outcomes at the 3‐month follow‐up. 42 However, the majority of participants in our study (80%) had experienced their stroke at least 6 months prior. For all participation outcomes, the magnitude of changes at follow‐up tended to decrease after removing subacute participants (those who had their stroke <6 months prior). Another possible factor is accessibility to rehabilitation services. Taiwan's National Health Insurance provides chronic stroke survivors with ongoing outpatient rehabilitation, including physical, occupational, and speech therapy. 43 It is also noteworthy that participants in the CI group exhibited less stroke severity compared with the OPASS group. The cumulative effects of rehabilitation might play a significant role in long‐term participation recovery, especially for those milder stroke survivors. 9 , 37

Aligned with Lin et al.'s 19 previous feasibility study conducted in Taiwan, our findings demonstrated high participant engagement, adequate comprehension, and high satisfaction with the OPASS program, confirming the feasibility of delivering OPASS to stroke survivors in this context. Furthermore, qualitative findings from Chiu et al. 44 highlighted participants' perceived benefits from OPASS, including enhanced social participation, which closely aligns with our current results. Collectively, these studies consistently indicate that strategy training is acceptable, beneficial, and feasible within Taiwanese stroke rehabilitation settings. Future studies might further explore specific factors that optimize implementation and overcome potential barriers, such as environmental challenges or unforeseen events (eg, the COVID‐19 pandemic).

Several study limitations need to be addressed. First, individuals with severe aphasia were excluded from the study due to the requirement of language comprehension in the OPASS program. The role of preserved language functions in the efficacy of the OPASS program remains an important area for further investigation. 45 Second, participation is highly context‐dependent; but the PM‐3D4D is a patient‐reported outcome measure, which may introduce recall bias. Supplementing this measure with ecological momentary assessment could offer additional insights into home‐ or community‐based behaviors. 46 Third, the diversity of our participants could be improved. Our findings are specific to chronic stroke survivors and may not be generalizable to individuals with acute or subacute stroke. Moreover, our participants resided in urban areas, and those with major medical conditions, including dementia, were excluded, resulting in a slightly younger cohort than the average stroke population in Taiwan (mean age of onset: 66.6 years). These factors may limit the generalizability of our findings to the oldest stroke survivors or those living in rural regions. Finally, the absence of a formal Trial Steering Committee may have limited opportunity for external oversight and broader expertise during the study's design and implementation.

CONCLUSIONS

This RCT evaluated the efficacy of the OPASS program for community‐dwelling stroke survivors with cognitive impairments, demonstrating significant immediate postintervention improvements in social participation frequency and productivity difficulty. However, these benefits did not persist at the 3‐month follow‐up. The OPASS program was efficacious across a diverse range of stroke survivors, regardless of baseline demographic or functional characteristics. While the study underscores the potential of strategy training in enhancing participation, it also highlights the need for further research to explore long‐term effects and the development of additional support mechanisms.
