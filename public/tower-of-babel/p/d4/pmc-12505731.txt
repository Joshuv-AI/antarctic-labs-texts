Aerosolization of Mycobacterium chelonae from heater-cooler-devices: a continuing risk of exposure during cardiac surgery

Background

A global outbreak of invasive Mycobacterium chimaera infections associated with aerosolization from contaminated heater-cooler-devices (HCDs) manufactured by Sorin/LivaNova was identified in 2013 [1–4]. Further analyses showed close genetic relationship between clinical and HCD isolates collected worldwide, proving a point source at the manufacturing site [5, 6]. Although disinfection of HCDs is recommended, it has proven insufficient to prevent persistent mycobacterial colonization [7, 8]. Differences in construction have been postulated as a possible explanation for the presence or absence of aerosolization during the use of HCDs from different manufacturers [9].

Based on international guidelines, regulatory authorities in Switzerland mandated microbiological monitoring of HCDs used in the operating room [10, 11]. Following replacement of HCDs contaminated with M. chimaera, microbiological monitoring of water tanks and air sampling was thus continued at the University Hospital Basel. Here, we report detection of M. chelonae aerosolization by routine HCD surveillance performed between January 2016 and August 2022.

Methods

Heater-cooler device maintenance

Since January 2016, four HCDs of the type HCU40 Maquet (Getinge) have been in operation at the University Hospital Basel, Switzerland. Weekly disinfection of the water circuits using 2% chloramine-T was performed following the manufacturer’s recommendations. Upon detection of non-tuberculous mycobacterial growth, intensified disinfection using 5% Chloramine-T was implemented in addition to weekly water changes. In accordance with national guidelines published by the Swiss regulatory authority (Swissmedic), the water tanks were filled with filtered water (pore size ≤ 0.22 μm, micron polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) membrane). Water filters were exchanged monthly. To reduce the risk of airborne transmission, HCDs are located in a separate enclosed room outside the operating theatre, with the exception of emergency surgery.

Microbiologic sampling and culture method

Water from the four HCD water tanks was cultured monthly for mycobacteria and Gram-negative bacteria. Furthermore, two water pipe faucets used to fill the devices were sampled once a month. Water sample collection and processing was performed in accordance with the protocol published by Swissmedic (https://www.swissmedic.ch/dam/swissmedic/en/dokumente/medizinprodukte/infos/richtlinien_zum_betriebundzurueberwachungvonheater-coolerdevices.pdf.download.pdf/richtlinien_zum_betriebundzurueberwachungvonheater-coolerdevices.pdf) using Middlebrook 7H11 agar plates for mycobacterial cultures (BioMérieux, Marcy-l’Etoile, France). Plates were incubated for seven weeks at 37 °Celsius (C) and in an atmosphere containing 5% carbon dioxide. Intermittent monthly air sampling for mycobacteria was carried out to assess the risk of aerosolization after detection of M. chelonae in HCD water tanks. The MAS-100 NT air sampling device (MBV, Stäfa, Switzerland) was used to collect a total air volume of 250 L (collection rate 100 L per minute for 2.5 min). Air samples were performed with a microbiologic air sampler at 30 cm (at the height of the fan) and 120 cm height beside running HCDs. Additional weekly swabs from the faucets and the water filters (Figs. 1 and 2a–d)—were collected for four consecutive weeks in December 2022. Water or air samples showing growth of mycobacteria were further processed by DNA extraction with subsequent whole genome sequencing.

Sequencing and analysis

To assess relatedness of all M. chelonae isolates, Ilumina® short-read WGS was performed. In short, DNA was extracted with an EZ1 DNA Tissue Kit in combination with EZ1 Advanced Instrument (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). The DNA was used to create a library with Nextera DNA flex Library Prep followed by sequencing applying a NextSeq500. Ridom Seqsphere + (8.5.1) was used to assemble genomes (SKESA 2.4.0) and to perform Multilocus Sequence Typing (MLST), core genome multilocus sequence typing (cgMLST) and minimum spanning trees based on allele differences. Phylogenetic trees based on allelic differences (single nucleotide polymorphisms) were created by CLC Genomics Workbench Version (v23.0.5) in combination with Microbial Genomics Module Version (v23.0.3). Default values were used for trimming, mapping and maximum likelihood tree formation, using the reference genome ASM163280v1 [12]. In brief, after variant call of all isolates, variants (SNPs, small insertion–deletion mutations [INDELs]) were filtered for quality and coverage. To be considered for SNP-tree creation, each isolate had to have at least 10x coverage at a position, the coverage had to be within 10% of the average coverage of all isolates, 10 nucleotides from both ends of each read were pruned, and the position had to have a minimum z-score of 1.96.

Identification of clinical samples

In order to identify a potential exposure during cardiac surgery a retrospective chart review was performed for all individuals with positive culture results for M. chelonae between 08/2013 and 04/2022.

Results

Microbiological monitoring of water samples from water tanks of HCDs between January 2016 and May 2018 revealed growth of several non-fermenters, but no growth of non-tuberculous mycobacteria. Routine surveillance data showed growth of Pseudomonas species in water samples as early as two weeks after initial use of the HCU40 devices. In contrast, M. chelonae was not cultured until June 2018, approximately 30 months after the HCU40 devices were put into operation. Out of 257 water samples a total of 43 (16.7%) grew M. chelonae during the surveillance periode. Figure 3 summarizes the sampling strategy and the microbiological surveillance timeline. Four years later, in January 2022, M. chelonae was detected in water samples from all four HCD water tanks. In May 2018, air sampling was initiated to assess the risk of intermittent aerosolization. Two air samples collected at the end of 2019 showed growth of M. chelonae. Starting from January 2022, intensified disinfection measures were implemented, but failed to eradicate M. chelonae from the water tanks. Consequently, monthly air sampling was resumed in March 2022. Growth of M. chelonae was detected in July 2022, in samples taken at 30 cm and 120 cm height beside three of four running HCD. No growth of M. chelonae was detected in air samples performed next to HCD devices that were switched off. Air samples collected from the cardiac surgery operating room remained negative.

Timeline showing microbiological surveillance with the dates of initial detections of M. chelonae in water and air samples from the heater-cooler-devices

Overall, 92 air samples were taken with a culture positivity rate of 8.7% (n = 8); seven samples grew M. chelonae while one sample grew M. chelonae and M. gordonae. Overall, 158 tap water samples from the faucet used to fill the devices were collected, however detection of M. chelonae occurred only once. This result was interpreted as contamination of the faucet by back splashes during the filling process, as further samples taken from the same faucet remained negative. In addition, weekly swabs of faucet and filter over four weeks never grew mycobacteria.

A maximum likelihood phylogenetic tree based on variant calling of single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) of all isolates showed clustering of M. chelonae with a maximum difference of four single nucleotide polymorphisms (Fig. 4) which confirmed close genetic relatedness between strains recovered from water tanks, air samples and the one positive tap water sample collected from the faucet used to fill the devices.There were a total of 13 SNPs and one deletion where isolates had differing results, when compared to the reference. More details on the sequencing results including quality control indicators and information on positions, where isolates showed differing signals as compared to the reference can be found in the Supplementary Table.

A total of 14 clinical samples collected between 08/2013 and 04/2022 tested positive for M. chelonae. Respiratory samples accounted for 71.4% (n = 10) of the isolates, while soft tissue infections and hardware infections (ocular foreign body and breast implant) each constituted 14.3% (n = 2). None of these patients underwent cardiac surgery prior to the onset of infection.

Discussion

Our surveillance data suggests that aerosolization of M. chelonae may be caused by HCU40 devices, contrary to previous assumptions that the design of these devices prevents such airborne dissemination.

So far, aerosolization of non-tuberculous mycobacteria has only been described in relation to 3T HCD devices manufactured by Sorin/LivaNova [9, 13–17]. Keeping water temperature constant at 4 °C, automated reminders to perform disinfection and differences regarding the design of the HCU40 devices have been considered to prevent aerosolization [9].

Growth of M. chelonae from water tanks of HCDs manufactured by Getinge has been described before [8, 18]. We could not identify any previous descriptions of aerosolization of non-tuberculous mycobacteria from running HCU40 devices, contaminated with M. chelonae. Other airborne sources were ruled out, as air samples from the cardiac surgery operation theater did not show growth of M. chelonae in the absence of a running HCU40 device. Close genetic relatedness between air and water samples from all four devices is suggestive for a point source of contamination. Inhouse contamination from faucets is unlikely as water samples remained negative for M. chelonae. Backwards contamination of the ≤ 0.22 μm water filters subsequently resulting in contamination of other devices was not detected. Despite intensive investigations, no source could be identified at the hospital level.

Mycobacterium chelonae belongs to the group of rapid-growing NTM. Despite this, there was a significant delay between commissioning of new HCU40 devices and the first documented detection of the bacterium from a water sample. Several factors may have complicated detection of M. chelonae. The optimal growth temperature for M. chelonae is 28 °C [19], however, the water temperature in HCU40 devices is consistently set at 4 °C, which likely slows bacterial replication. If the initial bacterial load was low, the time required to establish a detectable bacterial burden is also likely to be prolonged. Furthermore, standard culture incubation at 37 °C might reduce the detection efficiency of M. chelonae. Potentially, higher bacterial loads may be required to detect bioaerosol formation from HCU40 devices compared to 3T Sorin/LivaNova devices, due to the lower aerosolization risk. We hypothesize that internal water leakage due to corrosion could be a potential mechanism leading to aerosolization. Detection of Pseudomonas spp. only a few weeks after starting operation of the HCU40 devices points to the challenges in targeting colonization with waterborne pathogens even when cleaning and disinfection protocols are adhered to.

Over a period of more than 8 years, only clinical samples of 14 patients showed growth of M. chelonae. Fortunately, all M. chelonae clinical isolates detected, were unrelated to cardiac surgery. However, M. chelonae has been found in surgical site infections of the sternum and mediastinum [20–22]. Prosthetic valve endocarditis with M. chelonae, including outbreaks related to contaminated prosthesis, have been reported [23–26]. To our knowledge, M. chelonae infection after exposure to contaminated HCD have not yet been reported. In analogy to the outbreak with M. chimaera, formation of aerosols from HCDs contaminated with M. chelonae may establish colonization of exposed hardware or the surgical field, eventually resulting in active infection by direct inoculation during surgery. It has been previously shown, that recommended disinfection protocols are not able to eradicate mycobacteria from HCD water tanks [7, 27]. Furthermore, efficacy of disinfectants, even those recommended by the manufacturer, such as chloramine-T is questionable [28]. Since contamination of water tanks seems inevitable, placement of HCDs outside of the operating room is essential to prevent patient exposure.

Conclusion

Our surveillance data confirms the persistent risk of aerosolization of NTM from HCDs during cardiac surgery, irrespective of HCD-design and manufacturer. These findings highlight the need to perform routine microbiological surveillance by water and air sampling if HCDs are placed in the operation room. The most important measure to prevent non-tuberculous mycobacterial infection is to prevent exposure of the operating field to the air exhausts of HCDs.
