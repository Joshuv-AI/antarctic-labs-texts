Virtual cutting-based morphological differences in osteoarthritic and healthy knees: Implications for total knee arthroplasty prosthesis design
Authors: Bin Yu, Yu Zhang, Dongdong Cao, Jinchang Han, Weiyong Wu, Chao Zhang, Aifeng Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeEnd-stage knee osteoarthritis (OA) patients are the primary candidates for total knee arthroplasty (TKA). However, most morphological refinements of TKA prosthesis are based on anatomical data from the knees of healthy individuals. This study aimed to determine whether differences exist in key bony morphological characteristics of the distal femur and proximal tibia between osteoarthritic knees and healthy knees.MethodsThis was a retrospective cross-sectional observational study with a case-control design. Patients who were aged ≥ 50 years, had no history of trauma, fracture, or surgery in the studied knee, and had no obvious knee flexion contracture were included in this study by CT scans. Patients who met the American College of Rheumatology clinical criteria for knee OA were included in the study group. Kellgren-Lawrence grade III or IV knees were studied (for bilateral cases, the more severely affected knee was chosen). Patients who presented with unilateral knee pain or trauma were included in the control group, with CT scans from the opposite (asymptomatic) knee used for analyzing. The studied knee had a Kellgren-Lawrence grade of 0 or I and showed no abnormalities upon physical examination. Archived knee CT scans from 160 patients were divided into 2 groups: the study group (80 moderate-to-severe OA knees) and the control group (80 healthy knees). After 3-dimensional reconstruction and virtual cutting using a CT workstation, 13 morphological parameters of the distal femur and proximal tibia were compared between the 2 groups using independent-samples t-tests.ResultsNo significant group differences in the femoral anteroposterior dimension (p = 0.797), height of the lateral femoral condyle (p = 0.268), posterior condylar angle (p = 0.240), tibial anteroposterior dimension (p = 0.536), or tibial lateral anteroposterior dimension (p = 0.702) were observed. However, the femoral mediolateral dimension (p = 0.002), distal femoral aspect ratio (femoral mediolateral dimension/femoral anteroposterior dimension) (p < 0.001), height of the femoral trochlear groove (p < 0.001), height of the medial femoral condyle (p < 0.001), tibial mediolateral dimension (p = 0.001), proximal tibial aspect ratio (tibial mediolateral dimension/tibial anteroposterior dimension) (p = 0.004), tibial medial anteroposterior dimension (p = 0.005), and tibial asymmetry ratio (tibial medial anteroposterior dimension/tibial lateral anteroposterior dimension) (p = 0.006) were all significantly greater in the study group.ConclusionKnees with moderate-to-severe OA are significantly wider than healthy knees, and OA is a risk factor for increased tibial platform asymmetry. When refining the morphological parameters of TKA prostheses, the specific bony morphological characteristics of OA knees should be taken into account to reduce the potential risk of femoral or tibial component underhang and facilitate optimal balance between tibial component fit and rotational alignment.Keywords: Osteoarthritis, Total knee arthroplasty, Prosthesis design, Bone morphology, Distal femur, Proximal tibia

Introduction

Knee osteoarthritis (OA) is a prevalent condition in aging societies, posing a significant economic burden on families and the healthcare system.1 Total knee arthroplasty (TKA) is an effective treatment for various end-stage knee joint pathologies.2 Despite ongoing improvements in TKA prosthesis design, achieving complete restoration of the knee's natural anatomy and function remains unattainable. During TKA, mismatches between prosthetic components and the morphology of the distal femur and proximal tibia, especially in aspect ratios, are frequent challenges to achieving complete restoration. Aspect ratio mismatches can lead to femoral/tibial component overhang or underhang, which may result in postoperative pain, increased blood loss, or other adverse outcomes. When component sizing does not match the native knee's aspect ratio, orthopedic surgeons often have to make intraoperative compromises, altering the natural anatomy to fit the implant system. However, these compromises, regardless of type, can significantly impact clinical outcomes and patient satisfaction.3,4

Researchers around the world have explored this issue extensively, and major prosthesis manufacturers have attempted to optimize the aspect ratio parameters of implants. However, current prosthesis sizing often fails to match patient anatomy. One potential reason for these unsatisfactory outcomes is that while TKA is primarily performed on elderly patients with knee OA, most morphological studies that have informed prosthesis design are based on the data from healthy individuals. Recent studies have shown that degenerative changes can significantly alter knee morphology.5, 6, 7, 8, 9 Concerningly, only 1 study has directly analyzed the effects of OA on TKA parameters after cutting the distal femur and proximal tibia, and the selected parameters failed to reflect the actual TKA procedures that were performed.6

Therefore, our study aimed to quantitatively compare bone morphological parameters directly related to TKA after virtually cutting the distal femur and proximal tibia between persons with moderate-to-severe knee OA and healthy individuals. We hypothesized that significant differences exist in aspect ratios or other TKA-relevant morphological parameters between healthy and OA knees.

Methods

Study participants

This was a retrospective cross-sectional observational study with a case-control design, comparing key bony morphological parameters of the knee between moderate-to-severe OA patients and healthy patients. Archived knee CT scans from 160 hospitalized patients were carefully selected for this study. Informed consent was obtained from all patients. All CT scans were completed within 1 month before hospitalization, before any medical intervention.

Patients aged ≥ 50 years, having no history of trauma, fracture, or surgery in the studied knee, and no obvious knee flexion contracture were included in this study by CT scans. Patients who met the American College of Rheumatology clinical criteria for knee OA10 were included in the study group. Kellgren-Lawrence grade III or IV11 knees were studied (for bilateral cases, the more severely affected knee was chosen). Patients who presented with unilateral knee pain or trauma were included in the control group, with CT scans from the opposite (asymptomatic) knee used for analysis. A Kellgren-Lawrence grade for the studied knee was 0 or I, and no abnormalities upon physical examination was shown.

Patients with incomplete demographic or clinical data, knee valgus or hyperextension, poor CT image quality, presence of hemophilia, Kashin-Beck disease, periknee bone tumors, autoimmune diseases, or other inflammatory conditions, patellar dislocation/subluxation or trochlear dysplasia, significant coronal or sagittal deformity of the femoral or tibial shaft, or obvious bone defects in the distal femur or proximal tibia were excluded from this study.

CT scanning and 3-dimensional (3D) reconstruction

CT scans were performed with a 256-slice Revolution Apex CT (GE Healthcare, USA) with the following parameters: detector width 80 mm, tube voltage 100 kVp, smart mA (50 – 100 mA), pitch 0.508:1, slice thickness and interval 0.625 mm. Patients lie in a supine position on the CT table with both lower limbs extended neutrally and patellae facing upward. The scanning range encompassed 15 cm above and below the center of each knee. Original digital imaging and communications in medicine data were imported into a post-processing workstation (AW 4.7, GE Healthcare, USA), and the studied knee was reconstructed using 3D volume rendering software.12

Virtual cutting and measurement parameters on the femoral side

Given that CT cannot visualize the thickness of articular cartilage, the distal femoral resection plane was defined as 8 mm above the lowest point on the medial femoral condyle and set at a 6° valgus angle relative to the anatomical axis of the distal femur (Fig. 1). An anterior femoral resection was performed on the anterior cortical plane, with the cutting surface parallel to the surgical transepicondylar axis (sTEA), simulating the anterior referencing technique (Fig. 2A). In TKA, the vertical distance from the most prominent point on the posterior medial condyle to the anterior femoral cortex is used to determine femoral component size (Fig. 2B). Accordingly, we defined the femoral anteroposterior dimension (FAP) as the vertical distance from the most prominent point on the posterior medial femoral condyle to the anterior resection plane. The femoral mediolateral dimension (FML) was defined as the width of the distal femoral resection plane measured parallel to the sTEA. The distal femoral aspect ratio (DFAR) was calculated as FML divided by FAP. The height of the lateral femoral condyle (HLFC), the height of the femoral trochlear groove (HFTG), and the height of the medial femoral condyle (HMFC) were defined as the vertical distances from the highest point on the lateral condyle, the lowest point on the trochlear groove, and the highest point on the medial condyle, respectively, to the anterior femoral resection plane. The posterior condylar angle (PCA) was defined as the angle between the posterior condylar line (PCL) and the sTEA (Fig. 2C).

Virtual cutting and measurement parameters on the tibial side

The proximal tibial cutting depth in TKA is typically 10 mm. Given the possible cartilage thickness of approximately 2 mm, the tibial resection was set 8 mm below the highest point of the lateral plateau, with a posterior slope of 7° (Fig. 3). The tibial mediolateral dimension (TML) was defined as the maximum distance between the medial and lateral cortical edges of the tibial osteotomy plane, measured parallel to the sTEA. The tibial anteroposterior dimension (TAP) was defined as the maximum distance between the anterior and posterior cortical edges of the tibial osteotomy plane, measured perpendicular to the sTEA and passing through the midpoint of the TML. The proximal tibial aspect ratio (PTAR) was calculated as TML divided by TAP. The tibial medial anteroposterior dimension (TMAP) and tibial lateral anteroposterior dimension (TLAP) were defined as the maximum distances between the anterior and posterior cortical edges of the medial and lateral plateaus, respectively, measured parallel to the TAP and passing through the most prominent points of the posterior condyles. The tibial asymmetry ratio (TAR) was calculated as TMAP divided by TLAP (Fig. 4). All measurements on the tibial and femoral sides excluded osteophytes. The final morphological data used for analysis were the average of values measured independently by 2 trained orthopedic surgeons. The interobserver intraclass correlation coefficient values for all variables were more than 0.85.

Statistical methods

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 22.0 (IBM, USA). The normality of continuous variables was tested. Data conforming to a normal distribution are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and comparisons between the groups were conducted using independent-samples t-tests. For data not conforming to a normal distribution, the data are expressed as the median (Q1, Q3), and non-parametric tests were performed to compare the groups. Categorical variables are expressed as the number of cases (n) and percentages (%), and analyzed using the Chi-squared test. A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline demographic data

A total of 160 patients were included in this study, with 80 patients in the study group and 80 patients in the control group (Table 1). In the study group, the average age of patients was (65.96 ± 6.70) years, with 40 males and 40 females. The mean body mass index (BMI) was (26.74 ± 2.59) kg/m2. Knees of Kellgren-Lawrence grades III and IV were observed in 22 patients (27.5%) and 58 patients (72.5%), respectively. In the control group, the average age of patients was (64.73 ± 4.71) years, with 40 males and 40 females. The mean BMI was (26.16 ± 1.25) kg/m2. Knees of Kellgren-Lawrence grades 0 and I were observed in 67 patients (83.8%) and 13 patients (16.3%), respectively. There were no statistically significant differences between the 2 groups in terms of age (t = 1.351, p = 0.179) or BMI (t = 1.814, p = 0.071).

Morphological parameters of the distal femur

Compared with the control group, the study group showed significantly greater FML (p = 0.002), DFAR (p < 0.001), HFTG (p < 0.001), and HMFC (p < 0.001). In contrast, the FAP (p = 0.797), HLFC (p = 0.268), and PCA (p = 0.240) were not significantly different between the groups (Table 1). FML values (80.6%) were distributed in the 62 – 77 mm range, and FAP values (83.8%) fell within the 52 – 63 mm range (Fig. 5, Fig. 6). Based on the original groupings, a further subdivision by sex (male or female) created 4 subgroups (n = 160 knees in total). Scatter plots of the distal femur morphological parameters revealed the following trends: when FAP is constant, FML is larger in knees with OA compared with that in healthy knees, in males compared with that in females, and in males with healthy knees compared with that in females with knee OA (Fig. 7).

Discussion

The most commonly used method in knee joint morphology research is 3D CT reconstruction. A crucial step in 3D CT reconstruction is the accurate extraction of skeletal contours. However, the presence of osteophytes around OA knees makes bone segmentation extremely time-consuming and significantly increases the overall workload of digital reconstruction. Therefore, prior research has often selected knees with relatively mild degeneration for acquiring data on knee morphology. In recent years, the effect of OA on knee morphology has received increased research attention. Studies by Isik et al.5 using radiographs and Dong et al.6 using 3D CT have shown that the distal femoral width,5 medial femoral condyle width, and TMAP6 are significantly greater in OA knees than in healthy knees. Haverkamp et al.7 conducted a principal component analysis using morphological data from knee radiographs and found that the transverse diameter of knees with mild-to-moderate OA was significantly wider than that of the knees of healthy individuals, with an increase of approximately 3.7%. A cohort magnetic resonance imaging study by Wang et al.8 reported annual increases of 2.2% and 1.5% in the medial and lateral tibial plateau surface areas, respectively, in mild-to-moderate OA knees. They also found that a higher BMI and a narrower medial tibiofemoral joint space were associated with larger annual increases in medial plateau surface area.

These OA-related morphological changes may be due to compensatory bone remodeling in response to long-term abnormal mechanical stress. A statistical shape model constructed from 3D CT data by Lynch et al.9 indicated that subchondral bone expansion in severe OA is most pronounced in the femoral condyles and posterior medial tibial plateau, with unidirectional expansion of up to 10 mm. In this study, we measured and compared key morphological parameters between moderate-to-severe OA knees and healthy knees. In OA knees, FML, TML, and TMAP were significantly greater compared with those in healthy knees matched for age, BMI, and sex. Although compensatory bone remodeling resulted in greater HFTG and HMFC in OA knees, TAP, TLAP, PCA, and post-resection FAP did not significantly differ from healthy controls. As a result, DFAR, PTAR, and TAR were significantly higher in the study (OA) group.

The DFAR and PTAR are important indicators of distal femoral and proximal tibial morphology. The transverse diameter of the knee is typically slightly larger than the anteroposterior diameter. A higher DFAR or PTAR indicates a wider and flatter femoral condyle or a more elliptical proximal tibia, respectively, while lower values suggest a narrower, taller condyle or a rounder tibial plateau.3,4,13,14 During TKA, a mismatch between the aspect ratios of the femoral/tibial components and the native DFAR/PTAR may lead to overhang or underhang on the resected surfaces. Component overhang may irritate soft tissues and compromise postoperative function.15, 16, 17 Underhang, where cancellous bone is excessively exposed, may increase early postoperative blood loss,18 increase mid-to long-term risk of loosening,19 and affect soft tissue balance.20 In orthopedic practice, component-bone mismatch is categorically defined as > 3 mm for absolute mismatch, > 1 mm to ≤ 3 mm for relative mismatch, and ≤ 1 mm for precise fit.21, 22, 23 Our study identified > 2 mm mediolateral dimensional differences between OA and healthy knees. Consequently, femoral and tibial components designed based on healthy knee anatomy may potentially increase the underhang incidence in TKA for OA patients. The TAR is another parameter used to describe proximal tibial morphology.4,24 In most knees, the medial tibial plateau is slightly larger than the lateral side (medial dominant plateau),25 making it challenging to avoid anteroposterior mismatch with symmetrical tibial components.17 Striving for maximum coverage may result in excessive internal rotation of the tibial component.24 The OA-related increase in TAR is a key risk factor for tibial component overhang and makes it even harder to achieve both precise component fit and proper rotational alignment simultaneously. Therefore, supplementing conventional symmetrical tibial components with appropriately designed “anatomic” subtypes may better accommodate OA knees with pronounced medial predominance.

Hitt et al.13 originally defined PTAR as the ratio of TML to the mean of TMAP and TLAP (TML/TMAP + TLAP). Later studies, to facilitate comparison with symmetrical tibial components, preferred a simplified definition: TML was divided by central TAP (TML/TAP), with TAR used to quantify medial-lateral asymmetry.4,24 For DFAR, Hitt et al.13 defined it as FML divided by FAP measured after both anterior and posterior femoral resections. However, this choice of anteroposterior (AP) diameter (Fig. 11) is limited because it reflects compromises among various implant systems. Moreover, most femoral components have a 3° – 7° anterior flange angle,26 making the anterior cut surface imperfectly perpendicular to the distal cut, which reduces reproducibility in AP measurements post-resection. Like our study, Dong et al.6 also measured morphometric parameters after virtual cutting to compare OA and healthy knees. However, their chosen AP diameter included the uncut anterior condyle (Fig. 11), which limits its utility for guiding prosthesis design modifications. Compared with the parameters used in the studies of Hitt et al.,13 Dong et al.,6 and others,3 our selected FAP is more rational as it reflects both the cutting characteristics of TKA and natural knee morphology. However, this also makes direct comparisons with previous data or commercial implant parameters difficult.

Current morphology-related research for TKA prosthesis design primarily focuses on the effects of sex and race. Generally, males have higher DFAR than females with similar AP dimensions, and Caucasians have higher DFAR than East Asians.3,27 In contrast, the relationship between sex or ethnicity and PTAR remains controversial.4,27 Hence, more research attention has been directed toward modifying femoral component morphology. Some manufacturers have launched female-friendly or race-specific femoral components, but the prosthesis sizing often fails to match patient anatomy. For instance, Triathlon (Stryker, USA) and Gender Solutions (Zimmer-Biomet, USA) are designed for Caucasian females with narrower femoral components than conventional designs. However, these systems fail to match the morphology of Asian females,28,29 and even the fit with Caucasian females has been disappointing.30 TC-Dynamic (Plus, China), designed based on Chinese anatomy, is poorly fitted with Chinese knees.12 New-generation systems such as Attune (DePuy, USA) and Persona (Zimmer-Biomet, USA) offer smaller increments between sizes and subtypes with different aspect ratios (standard and narrow), improving the fit compared with conventional designs; however, this fit is only relative.31 Moreover, although narrower femoral components reduce overhang rates, they significantly increase under-coverage,28, 29, 30, 31 leading to even lower overall match rates than conventional systems.32 Currently, most femoral components, including redesigned ones, only provide ideal coronal morphological matching in 10% – 63% of patients.28,29 This may partly be the result of individual variability in knee morphology, but inappropriate sample selection in prior morphological studies is also an important contributing factor.

Our comprehensive analysis demonstrates the effects of OA on key morphometric parameters of the distal femur and proximal tibia after virtual cutting. Our findings may help to optimize future TKA prosthesis designs. However, this study has several limitations that should be noted. First, the small sample size limited our analyses of the potential influencing factors and the conduct of subgroup analyses. Second, we excluded valgus knees, which are relatively rare clinically. Future studies should explore how OA affects bone morphology in various types of deformity. Third, 3D CT cannot be used to visualize articular cartilage, which may introduce bias in resection and measurement. We used the built-in volume rendering tool on the CT workstation for 3D reconstruction. While sufficient for our study, it cannot fully simulate the complexity of actual TKA procedures, thus preventing more detailed data acquisition. Although Lee et al.33 found that CT-based rapid imaging measurements closely matched intraoperative measurements, whether their accuracy rivals professional image processing software requires further validation. Future studies should focus on identifying better research methods that balance cartilage visualization with accurate spatial imaging. They should also account for various intraoperative cutting errors in TKA, making it possible to assess the relationship between OA and knee morphology from more dimensions.

In conclusion, our findings confirmed the initial hypothesis that significant morphological differences exist between knees with moderate-to-severe OA and healthy knees in terms of the distal femur and proximal tibia. The OA-specific morphological characteristics, especially in DFAR, PTAR, and TAR, deserve greater attention to improve future TKA prosthesis designs.
