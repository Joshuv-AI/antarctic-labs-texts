Predicting capsicum leaf water stress using mid-infrared ATR-FTIR spectroscopy
Authors: Seham Al-Mahrouqi, Talal Al-Shukaili, Yaseen Al-Mulla
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLeaf water status is a key indicator for irrigation scheduling and early stress detection, but many spectroscopic prediction studies have mainly relied on near-infrared features. Here, practical prediction models were developed using mid-infrared (MIR) ATR-FTIR spectra of capsicum (Capsicum annuum L.) leaves collected under glasshouse conditions during a 10-day gradual dehydration period, alongside an irrigated control. Spectra (4000–450 cm⁻1) were measured with minimal sample preparation, and leaf water traits were quantified using fuel moisture content (FMC), equivalent water thickness (EWT), and specific leaf weight (SLW). Water-related MIR bands at 3370 and 1641 cm⁻1 showed the most consistent response to dehydration, and simple band ratios generally provided stronger predictions than single bands. The best ratios were A1641/A2159 for FMC (R2 = 0.81; RMSE = 12.80) and A3370/A2849 for EWT (R2 = 0.72; RMSE = 0.0034) and SLW (R2 = 0.62; RMSE = 6.95 × 10⁻4), while predicted-versus-measured performance yielded R2 values of 0.72 for FMC, 0.68 for EWT, and 0.52 for SLW. These results indicate that MIR ATR-FTIR spectroscopy, when coupled with selected band ratios, can provide a rapid, low-preparation laboratory-based approach for estimating capsicum leaf water traits under controlled dehydration, supporting plant-based water stress assessment under controlled conditions and providing a basis for further irrigation-related sensing studies. However, the models are preliminary and require validation with larger independent datasets and tightly standardised measurement conditions before operational use in irrigation management.Graphical Abstract Keywords: Attenuated Total Reflectance (ATR-FTIR), Mid-Infrared Spectroscopy, Leaf Water Status, Capsicum annuum, Spectral Band Ratios, Precision Irrigation

Introduction

Leaf water status is one of the most informative signals for scheduling irrigation and spotting water stress early. When plants begin to run short on water, their leaves lose hydration, stomata gradually close, and both transpiration and evaporative cooling decline. In greenhouse systems, these shifts can occur fast and they can quickly affect growth, yield, and overall product quality. This becomes even more critical for high-value crops like capsicum (Capsicum annuum L.), where irrigation decisions are a central part of day-to-day management. To describe leaf water condition and the accompanying structural responses, researchers and growers often rely on traits such as fuel moisture content (FMC), equivalent water thickness (EWT), and specific leaf weight (SLW). The problem is that the standard ways of measuring these traits are typically destructive, slow, and impractical for repeated measurements across many plants and time points. As a result, they are not easily adopted for routine greenhouse decision-making [1, 2].

Much of the earlier work has focused on the visible/near-infrared (VIS/NIR) and short-wave infrared (SWIR) regions, using spectral indices or specific wavelengths to estimate leaf water content or related traits [4, 5]. These regions are valuable for reflectance-based and canopy-scale measurements, but their response can be influenced by leaf structure, scattering, surface properties, and measurement geometry. In comparison, MIR spectroscopy is more closely linked to fundamental molecular vibrations of water and plant functional groups, including O–H, C-H, C = O, and C-O vibrations. Recent studies have also shown the potential of ATR-FTIR and related infrared approaches for detecting plant water deficit, abiotic stress responses, and physiological status [15–18]. Therefore, MIR-ATR spectroscopy can be useful for developing simple, interpretable, band-based predictors, although its shallow penetration depth and sensitivity to sample contact require careful measurement standardization.

Compared with the NIR region, the mid-infrared (MIR) range includes absorption features that are more directly tied to water molecules and key functional groups in plant materials. This means MIR spectroscopy could provide clearer sensitivity to dehydration and enable simpler, more interpretable predictors based on individual bands or basic band ratios. Despite this potential, MIR-based prediction of leaf water status is still relatively uncommon in routine monitoring, especially when the focus is on practical, band-driven models. Many FTIR studies in plant science have instead emphasized broader biochemical or structural characterization, tracking general leaf changes, or methodological issues such as sample preparation and measurement effects [6, 7]. Reviews of plant cuticle spectroscopy also indicate that infrared signals can reveal meaningful information about leaf surface properties and structure, yet turning those signals into simple, robust predictors for water-related traits remains underexplored [8]. In addition, factors like sampling conditions and effective measurement depth can influence ATR-FTIR outcomes in plant tissues, underscoring the need for model development that is both careful and practical [6, 9].

Capsicum provides a useful case for addressing this gap: it is widely cultivated in greenhouses and typically shows clear responses to water deficit. However, relatively few studies have systematically tracked MIR spectral changes during gradual dehydration and then evaluated simple regression models for multiple leaf water traits such as FMC, EWT, and SLW under controlled greenhouse conditions. In particular, it is still uncertain which MIR water-related bands remain most stable and responsive across dehydration stages, and whether simple band ratios consistently outperform single-band predictors for these traits.

The present study set out to develop practical, MIR band-based models for predicting capsicum leaf water traits under glasshouse dehydration conditions. Mid-infrared spectra were collected during a 10-day irrigation-withholding experiment, alongside an irrigated control group for comparison. Individual absorbance bands and straightforward band ratios were then screened and tested using linear regression to estimate FMC, EWT, and SLW. The key contribution of this work lies in its emphasis on simple, interpretable MIR predictors, its identification of the most responsive bands and ratios during progressive dehydration, and its evaluation of predictive performance against measured reference values under controlled conditions. In the long term, this approach may support plant-based water stress assessment, but wider testing is needed before use in irrigation management.

Methodology

Experimental design and plant material

The experiment was conducted in a glasshouse at the Agricultural Experimental Station, Sultan Qaboos University. Capsicum (Capsicum annuum L.) seeds were purchased from a local agricultural materials shop in Barka, Al Batinah South Governorate, Oman. The seeds were germinated in pots and the plants were grown under controlled conditions. Figure 1 shows a randomized arrangement of 25 capsicum pots under a completely randomized design (CRD) to minimize the effect of spatial variation of temperature, solar radiation, and humidity inside the glasshouse. The experiment included 20 dehydration-stress pots assigned to five dehydration durations: D1, D3, D5, D7, and D9, with four pots per dehydration duration, and five continuously irrigated control pots. Pot numbers 1–25 indicate randomized positions in the experimental area, not treatment numbers. The pot was considered the experimental unit, and three healthy leaves were collected from each sampled pot during the growth stage for FMC, SLW, EWT, and MIR spectral measurements. For D9, leaf samples were severely dehydrated and unsuitable for reliable laboratory measurement. Leaf position and age were not stratified, and this should be considered when interpreting the models.

Dehydration treatment and leaf sampling

The pots were irrigated daily with 300 ml of tap water. Inside the glasshouse, the plants were exposed to controlled conditions of air temperature, relative humidity, and homogeneous sunlight. After three months, when the plants were in the mature stage, irrigation was cut off on all pots except for the control pots, which were kept irrigated until the end of the experiment (10 days).

Leaf samples were collected at five different dehydration stages during the experiment. Those were: days 1, 3, 5, 7, and 9 after stopping the irrigation. On each sampling day, three leaves from each pot were collected and transported to the laboratory. A total of 15 samples/day were collected representing four dehydrated pots and one control pot. The collected leaf samples were kept in plastic bags and then transported to the laboratory in a dark, cold box filled with ice. This procedure minimizes the leaves' water transpiration and prevents the photosynthesis process [3, 5].

Environmental monitoring and laboratory measurements

Each sampling day, the soil moisture content (SMC), air temperature (Ta), leaf temperature (Tl), relative humidity (RH), and solar radiation (SR) were recorded. The SMC was measured using the Theta probe soil moisture sensor (ML2x, Eijkelkamp Agrisearch Equipment, United Kingdom), and the leaf temperature was measured using a thermal infrared thermometer (830-T1, Testo, Germany). A weather station (MiniMent Automatic Weather Station, Skye Instrument, Canada) was used to measure the air temperature, relative humidity, and solar radiation.

The irrigated control block and dehydration blocks were maintained inside the same glasshouse and were exposed to the same general environmental conditions. Environmental variables were recorded on the same sampling days to describe the experimental conditions and to support interpretation of the dehydration response.

This study used the same procedure of [10] to calculate the leaf water content (wet basis and dry basis) in each sample. First, each sample's fresh weight (FW) was measured using an analytical balance (Sartorius AZ214, Sartorius, Germany). After that, the leaf samples were dried at 80 ℃ for 24 h in a drying oven (Gallenkamp Hotbox Oven, England). Finally, the dry weight (DW) of the leaf samples was measured. Then, the leaf water content on a wet and dry basis was calculated. The leaf surface area (A) was measured using a portable leaf area meter (AM350 meter, ADC BioScientific Ltd, England). The AM350 meter comprises a scan board and a high-resolution scanner with integrated data analysis and image storage.

Spectral measurements of leaf samples

Spectral measurements were taken using an FTIR spectrometer (Agilent Cary 600 Series FTIR spectrometer, Agilent Technology, United States) attached to a Diamond Attenuation Total Reflection (ATR) cell (GladiATR, PIKE Technology, United States). The FTIR spectrometer has a spectral range between 4000 and 450 cm-1. For each leaf sample, four spectral measurements were collected from four selected locations on the leaf surface: top right (TR), top left (TL), bottom right (BR), and bottom left (BL). All four measurements were made on the adaxial surface only excluding major veins. Four ATR-FTIR spectra were recorded at various spots on each leaf and averaged to get one spectrum per leaf. This averaged spectrum was employed as the independent observation when developing and testing the model. The following samples were not used in the regression analysis: (i) a sample from which desiccation precluded adequate and uniform contact with the ATR diamond prism at the four measurement positions, (ii) or a sample for which fresh weight, dry weight or leaf area could not be measured without tearing. All exclusion decisions were taken before the results of the regression were known.

The samples' MIR spectra were collected using Resolutions Pro FTIR software (version 4.1), averaging 16 scans at a resolution of 4 cm-1. The pressure tip of the ATR cell of the spectrometer was used to apply pressure on the leaf sample against the diamond prism to ensure good contact between the leaf surface and the prism surface. A transparent plastic square-shaped sheet (1 × 1 cm) was put on the leaf surface before applying the tip pressure to eliminate the tip pressure concentration on one point of the leaf surface to prevent leaf rupture. To minimize the variability between the different contact measurements, all spectral measurements were conducted by the same operator for the duration of the experiment, and according to the manufacturer's advice, the pressure tip was tightened to the same tactile resistance. The actual force applied by the ATR was not recorded numerically, but the use of only one operator, a pressure-distribution sheet and a standard procedure to tighten was consistently used for all samples. Effective contact force residual inter-sample variation cannot be ruled out and further studies should be done using ATR accessories that include load-cell force monitoring. The location of each scan was added to the number of the plant leaf to produce a label for each spectrum.

Spectral analysis of the collected spectra

After each spectral measurement of the leaf samples, the spectra were saved in two formats, a comma-separated file (.csv) and Grams spectra (.spa), for further analysis. The average of four technical replicate measurements was calculated for each leaf sample using OMNIC 9 software (version 9.2.86). The spectra then underwent automatic baseline correction using the same software settings for all samples, and no manual baseline adjustment was applied to individual spectra. Calculating the simple ratio of absorption wavelengths (wavebands)

The candidate MIR bands were selected using a combined literature-based and data-screening approach. First, the bands at 3370, 2917, 2849, 2159, 1641, 1025, and 700 cm−1 were chosen because they represent prominent features commonly reported in ATR-FTIR spectra of plant leaves and plant surface materials [6, 8, 15, 16]. Second, these bands and their simple ratios were screened against the measured leaf water traits using correlation analysis. Thus, the final regression models were based on candidate bands that were both physically interpretable and empirically responsive in the present dataset. The simple ratio values of absorption were calculated from peak heights at these wavenumbers. The total number of simple absorption ratios was 42.

Statistical analysis

Three prediction models were developed using 2/3 of the data (training set) and evaluated using the remaining 1/3 (validation set). Because both sets came from the same experiment, this is considered internal validation, not independent validation. Pearson correlation coefficient (r) was used as a preliminary screening metric to identify responsive individual bands and simple band ratios for FMC, EWT, and SLW. The correlation coefficients were calculated and the relationships between each water-status trait and the selected spectral variables were plotted. Because 42 simple band ratios were screened, this step was treated as exploratory band selection rather than as a formal multiple-comparison test. No multiple-comparison correction was applied, so this step was used only to guide band selection. Linear regression analyses were then used to develop prediction equations from the selected spectral variables. Model performance was evaluated using the coefficient of determination (R2), root mean square error (RMSE), mean absolute error (MAE), and mean absolute percentage error (MAPE). Residual plots, line-fit plots, and normal probability plots were used as supporting diagnostics. The exploratory nature of the band-ratio screening is acknowledged as a limitation, and the selected ratios should be confirmed using larger independent datasets in future studies.

Results and discussion

Effect of dehydration in plant leaf water content

Some leaf samples were excluded from the analysis, particularly those collected at advanced dehydration stages, including all Day 9 samples. The excluded observations are indicated as missing data in Figs. 2, 3, 4. Missing values occurred mainly because some leaves became too dry or deformed to allow reliable leaf-area measurement and spectral analysis. Consequently, the missingness was likely associated with dehydration severity and should not be considered completely random. This may have biased the later-stage means toward leaves that remained physically measurable. Therefore, late-stage trends, especially those related to EWT and SLW, should be interpreted with caution.

Figure 2 illustrates the change in leaf fresh moisture content (FMC) during dehydration stress. The FMC remained almost unchanged during the early stage of dehydration, with mean values of 80.45% on Day 1 and 80.51% on Day 3. These values were very close to the mean FMC of the continuously irrigated control plants, which was 81.53%, indicating that the plants maintained high leaf water status during the first three days after irrigation withholding. However, a sharp decline was observed by Day 5, where the mean FMC decreased to 40.98%, followed by a similar value of 40.70% on Day 7. The larger SE values on Days 5 and 7 indicate greater variability among the leaf measurements at advanced dehydration stages, suggesting that the plants did not respond uniformly to prolonged water stress. Overall, the figure demonstrates that FMC was stable during the first three days of dehydration but declined markedly between Day 3 and Day 5, confirming the effectiveness of the dehydration treatment in inducing water stress in capsicum leaves.

The change in equivalent water thickness (EWT) during dehydration stress is shown in Fig. 3. EWT decreased progressively with increasing dehydration duration, from 0.024859 g cm⁻2 on Day 1 to 0.020884 g cm⁻2 on Day 3. A further decline was observed on Day 5, where mean EWT decreased to 0.016153 g cm⁻2, followed by the lowest mean value of 0.013756 g cm⁻2 on Day 7. The control leaves showed a higher mean EWT value of 0.030610 g cm⁻2, indicating greater water retention under continuous irrigation. The lower sample size at Day 5 (n = 6) and Day 7 (n = 3) should be considered when interpreting the later dehydration stages. Overall, the results show a gradual reduction in leaf water thickness as dehydration progressed, confirming that irrigation withholding reduced the water status of capsicum leaves.

The change in specific leaf weight (SLW) during dehydration stress is shown in Fig. 4. The SLW decreased progressively as dehydration duration increased, with the highest mean value observed on Day 1 (0.005869 gcm⁻2), followed by a decrease on Day 3 (0.004979 g cm⁻2). A further reduction was observed on Day 5 (0.004604 g cm⁻2) and Day 7 (0.004529 gcm⁻2), indicating that leaf structural or water-related mass per unit area declined under prolonged irrigation withholding. The continuously irrigated control plants showed a higher mean SLW value (0.006119 gcm⁻2) than the dehydration-stressed samples, supporting the effect of dehydration on reducing leaf water-related weight per unit area. The SE values were relatively small across dehydration days, suggesting limited variation within each sampling day; however, the number of available samples was lower on Days 5 and 7 (n = 3) compared with Days 1 and 3 (n = 12), which should be considered when interpreting the later-stage dehydration results. Overall, the figure indicates a gradual reduction in SLW with increasing dehydration duration, with most of the decline occurring between Day 1 and Day 3.

The dehydration treatment produced clear changes in the three leaf water-related indicators, but the magnitude of response differed among them. FMC showed the strongest and most abrupt response, remaining almost unchanged between Day 1 and Day 3, with mean values of 80.45% and 80.51%, respectively, before declining sharply to 40.98% on Day 5 and 40.70% on Day 7. In contrast, SLW decreased more gradually, from 0.005869 g cm⁻2 on Day 1 to 0.004979 g cm⁻2 on Day 3, then slightly further to 0.004604 g cm⁻2 on Day 5 and 0.004529 g cm⁻2 on Day 7. Similarly, EWT showed a progressive decline with dehydration duration, decreasing from 0.024859 g cm⁻2 on Day 1 to 0.020884 g cm⁻2 on Day 3, 0.016153 g cm⁻2 on Day 5, and 0.013756 g cm⁻2 on Day 7. Compared with the control means, which remained higher for FMC (81.53%), SLW (0.006119 g cm⁻2), and EWT (0.030610 g cm⁻2), all three parameters confirmed a reduction in leaf water status under irrigation withholding. Overall, FMC was the most sensitive indicator of the sharp dehydration effect between Day 3 and Day 5, while SLW and EWT provided more gradual indicators of changes in leaf mass and water thickness per unit area. Because the control pots were intentionally kept irrigated while the other pots were subjected to irrigation withdrawal, differences in soil moisture between treatments were part of the experimental design. However, possible local microclimate effects around the plants cannot be completely excluded. Therefore, the observed spectral and leaf-water responses should be interpreted as responses under the applied glasshouse dehydration conditions.

Two of the selected spectral bands were associated with the presence of water, particularly 3370 and 1641 cm−1, which agrees with previous ATR-FTIR studies of plant tissues [7, 14, 16]. Selection of the best band ratio for correlation

Two selected spectral band ratios predicted a high correlation with capsicum leaves' FMC as shown in Table 4. The first one was (A1641/A2159) and the second one was (A1641/A2917). The band ratio (A1641/A2159) had the highest coefficient of determination (R2 = 0.81) and lowest RMSE of 12.80, lowest MSE of 163.88, lowest MAE of 10.36 and the lowest MAPE of 30.17% compared to the band ratio (A1641/A2917). As a result, the band ratio (A1641/A2159) was selected to be the best spectral band ratio to predict FMC values of capsicum leaves.

The band ratios A3370/A2849 and A3370/A2159 were used to develop prediction models 1 and 2, respectively, for EWT (Table 5). A3370/A2849 had the highest R2 value (0.72) and the lowest RMSE, MSE, and MAE values. Therefore, A3370/A2849 was selected as the best simple band ratio for predicting EWT values of capsicum leaves.

Comparison of the models showed that the lower values of R2 in the predicted-versus-actual plots, especially for SLW, suggest that models have moderate generalization in the available data. This decrease in performance could be attributed to the small number of samples taken at the leaf level, missing data due to dehydration at later stages and biological variance between leaves, as well as the possibility of ATR contact condition variance [6, 19]. The prediction models proposed should thus be regarded as preliminary and crop and condition specific until validated with a larger independent data set. The accuracy of their predictions differed greatly, and so did the utility of the models, which are related to the physiological basis of the indices. The calibration R2 is 0.81 for FMC, indicating a strong linear relationship between the ratio of A1641/A2159 and the bulk leaf water mass, with the O–H and C = O vibrations of water being directly sensitive to water mass [13, 14]. Nevertheless, the validation MAPE of 30.17% is a significant practical constraint: at an FMC of 45%, the absolute error is around ± 13.5 percentage points, which allows for a misclassification of either a stressed leaf, or a moderate stressed leaf as a stressed leaf and vice versa. Hence, the FMC model should be considered as a proof-of-concept piece of evidence of sensitivity of MIR band ratios to molecular level dehydration of leaves, not as a tool ready for precision irrigation decision making [15, 16]. For EWT the ratio of the O–H absorption band to the C–H band of lipids (A3370/A2849) removes the broad O–H band, thus normalising the results against a lipid C–H band that is relatively insensitive to hydration of the sample, thereby giving an effective internal correction for baseline variation [5, 10]. The MAPE (14%) and validation R2 (0.68) show moderate to good predictability within the calibration range. In the case of SLW, the interaction with MIR water bands is not direct; SLW is related to dry mass per unit area and the spectral signal is mainly due to the residual structural components [20]. The lower R2 of validation (0.52) is probably due to the narrow range of the physiological responses of SLW in this experiment, the effect of leaf area reduction under severe stress [21, 22], and the relatively small numbers in the D5 and D7 treatments.

Limitations

Although this study shows that ATR-FTIR spectroscopy can detect leaf water stress in capsicum, several limitations affect how the results can be used and interpreted. First, the sample size was small. The experiment used only 4 pots for the drought treatment and 5 pots for the control. In addition, at later stages of drought (Days 5 and 7), some leaves were missing, leaving only 3 to 6 samples per day. This small number means the results may not represent the full range of leaf water stress in real situations. Future studies should use more plants and ensure complete sampling across all time points. Second, all measurements were done in a controlled greenhouse with stable temperature, humidity, and light. In a real field, these conditions change throughout the day and across seasons. Third, this study used only one pepper variety (Capsicum annuum). Different varieties may have different leaf structures and water-holding properties, so the results may not apply to other peppers or other crops such as tomato or eggplant. Fourth, the ATR measurement depends on how firmly the leaf is pressed against the crystal. We followed a consistent procedure, but the contact pressure was not measured. Small differences in pressure can change the spectral signal. Using equipment that controls pressure would reduce this source of error. Fifth, the study used internal validation (2/3 training, 1/3 testing) from the same experiment. Both the training and validation data came from the same plants under the same conditions. This is weaker than independent validation, where new plants from a different experiment are used for testing. As a result, the R2 values (0.81 for FMC, 0.72 for EWT, 0.52 for SLW) may overestimate how well the equations work on new data. Finally, four spots were measured on each leaf and the values were averaged. However, water does not distribute evenly across a leaf. The upper and lower leaf surfaces may dry at different rates, and the area between veins may lose water differently from the veins themselves. Averaging four spots may miss these spatial patterns of water stress. These limitations suggest that the developed equations should be considered preliminary and should not be used for real irrigation decisions. The equations need to be tested on new plants, in field conditions, and across different pepper varieties before they can be applied in practice. Future research should address these limitations by using larger sample sizes, field trials, multiple crop types, standardized ATR equipment, and truly independent validation datasets.

Conclusion

This study developed a simple and practical laboratory-based approach to relate capsicum (Capsicum annuum L.) leaf water status to mid-infrared (MIR) absorption features measured by ATR spectroscopy. Leaf moisture was described using three indices, namely fuel moisture content (FMC), equivalent water thickness (EWT), and specific leaf weight (SLW). The MIR bands at 3370 and 1641 cm-1 showed consistent sensitivity to leaf water variation, confirming their relevance as water-associated spectral indicators. In addition, band ratios provided stronger relationships than individual bands, likely because ratios can partially compensate for baseline shifts and measurement variability. Among the tested combinations, A1641/A2159 gave the best association with FMC (r = 0.898) and produced the strongest predictive performance (R2 = 0.81; RMSE = 12.801), while A3370/A2849 showed the best relationships for EWT (r = 0.847; R2 = 0.72; RMSE = 0.0034) and SLW (r = 0.789; R2 = 0.62; RMSE = 6.95 × 10–4). The predicted-versus-measured plots support the potential of ATR-MIR spectroscopy combined with selected band ratios for estimating leaf water-related traits under the studied controlled dehydration conditions. However, the approach remains laboratory-based and was not validated as a portable field-based monitoring system. Broader validation across additional crop types, larger independent datasets, and tightly standardized ATR handling procedures would be needed before direct operational use in irrigation scheduling. Because the FMC error remained relatively high, the equations should be treated as preliminary screening models, not irrigation rules.
