Passage of Time or Delay Signal at the Level of Milliseconds: A New Approach and a Selective Difficulty in Individuals With Schizophrenia
Authors: Ljubica Jovanovic, Laurence Lalanne, Florent Bernardin, Vincent Laprévote, Anne Giersch
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and HypothesisIndividuals with schizophrenia report that time can feel discontinuous. Difficulties in benefiting from the passage of time at the level of hundreds of milliseconds are associated with the sense of self. However, time continuity might require millisecond-level processing. Here we leverage sequential effects in a synchrony judgment task to test whether individuals with schizophrenia and controls benefit from increased delays in the order of tens of milliseconds.Study DesignWe re-analyzed 3 datasets (65 individuals with schizophrenia and 67 controls). Two visual stimuli appeared simultaneously or with a stimulus onset asynchrony (SOA, 8-96 ms). Rather than comparing sensitivity in asynchrony detection, we contrasted performance when the SOA increased, remained identical, or decreased from the previous to the current trial. In a control masking task, 19 individuals with schizophrenia and 19 controls located a masked target.Study ResultsPerformance improved for all participants when the SOA increased (and the task became easier), but more so for controls than for individuals with schizophrenia. These results were replicated across datasets and were specific to trials in which SOA increased on consecutive trials. This effect persisted even when SOAs were frequently identical and when we controlled for SOA magnitude. The group difference was specific to temporal judgments and was not observed in the masking task.ConclusionsThese findings reveal a difficulty for individuals with schizophrenia to benefit from delay signals at the millisecond level, which we suggest underlies their experience of time fragmentation.

Introduction

Individuals with schizophrenia report that their conscious experience is fragmented in time, as if for them time itself would be discontinuous rather than continuous.1–3 This disruption of temporal continuity is believed to contribute to the disorders of the sense of self1,4–6 as illustrated by the following citation of an individual with schizophrenia1: “Time splits up and doesn’t run forward anymore. There arise uncountable disparate now, now, now, all crazy and without rule or order. It is the same with myself. From moment to moment, various ‘selves’ arise and disappear entirely at random. There is no connection between my present ego and the one before.” Albeit those experiences have long been described in phenomenology, they are not integrated in the usual clinical descriptions. Our work aims at understanding the cognitive mechanisms involved in the strange experience of time described by the individuals with schizophrenia.

Consistent with the phenomenological descriptions, the passage of time is reported to be experienced as disturbed in individuals with schizophrenia. In previous studies, the passage of time has been explored at the level of seconds or minutes.3,7 However, disturbances have also been described at the level of milliseconds in individuals with schizophrenia.8–16 We suggested that these disturbances might be based on impairments in monitoring time at shorter time scales, specifically at the level of milliseconds.17,18 If this were true, then alterations of the passage of time should be observed for shorter durations than one second in individuals with schizophrenia. In the present study, we explore how the passage of time at the level of tens of milliseconds is used to temporally distinguish 2 successive visual events, in individuals with schizophrenia and in healthy controls. Of note, the present work does not directly address the phenomenological experience of the passage of time, which is elusive at the level of milliseconds. Rather, the existence of an experience of passage of time is inferred by comparing performance across tasks that require distinguishing events in time explicitly or implicitly (synchrony judgment and masking, respectively). To avoid any confusion with the passage of time described in the literature, we refer to it as a millisecond-level delay signal. The reasoning is the following. In any task in which stimuli are presented in rapid succession, it becomes easier to perceptually separate stimuli as the interval between them increases. If the change in delay from trial to trial is encoded, it should further impact tasks with a temporal judgment. More specifically, a signal of delay increase relative to a previous trial should help in tasks requiring an explicit decision about the relative onset time of 2 successive events. In contrast, the delay signal does not help when participants have to decide about a characteristic of the stimuli that is unrelated to the delay, such as the spatial position of one of the stimuli. Our work does not make any assumptions about the phenomenological nature of the millisecond-level delay signal, but aims to verify whether there is evidence for it, and whether it is preserved in individuals with schizophrenia.

In previous studies, we tested the hypothesis of time disruption experimentally, by evaluating the capacity to exploit the passage of time when anticipating events at the level of hundreds of milliseconds. To that end we used the variable foreperiod paradigm. Participants responded to a target presentation by pressing on a response key. The target occurred at variable delays (foreperiods) after a warning signal. Typically, control participants become better prepared as time passes and react faster to the target when foreperiods are long rather than short.19 We showed that individuals with schizophrenia did not benefit from the passage of time as much as control participants, and that these disturbances are correlated with disorders of the sense of self.20,21

However, it is difficult to explore our hypothesis of an impaired time monitoring at the millisecond timescale with the variable foreperiod paradigm, as it is difficult to distinguish and react to successive stimuli when the stimulus onset asynchrony (SOA) is less than 20-50 ms.22 To address the question of the millisecond-level delay signal, we developed a different approach. We used a task requiring participants to detect and report an asynchrony between the presentation of 2 successive visual stimuli. The presented SOAs varied from trial to trial, and we investigated sequential effects: how the SOA and response on the previous trial (t − 1) affect performance on the current trial (t). Specifically, we tested the possibility that participants take advantage of the increase in SOA from one trial to another (eg, for trial t − 1 SOA = 10 ms and for trial t SOA = 40 ms) to improve their performance (Figure 1). If the millisecond-level delay signal is monitored from trial to trial, and if this monitoring continues automatically beyond the delay of the previous trial, then the SOA increase from one trial to the next may be processed, and should further facilitate the detection of an asynchrony. If the millisecond-level delay signal is not monitored efficiently, only the incidental benefit of reduction in task difficulty would remain.

Methods

Participants

We re-analyzed datasets from 2 published studies testing visual asynchrony discrimination (Study 1 and 3 in the manuscript), and 1 yet unpublished dataset (Study 2). In Study 1, 20 controls and 20 individuals with schizophrenia participated.13 In Study 2, 29 controls and 26 individuals with schizophrenia were included. In Study 3, 18 participants were tested per group.12 In addition, to test the specificity of the observed effects, we re-analyzed data from a masking experiment23 (Study 4), in which 19 controls and 19 individuals with schizophrenia were tested. Stimuli and Apparatus

Stimuli presentation was controlled by a custom-build software using Psychtoolbox24 (Study 1) and Cambridge Research System (Rochester, Kend, United Kingdom; Study 2, 3, and 4) running with Matlab 7.0.1 (Mathworks, 1984-2004).

Procedures

The simultaneity and asynchrony discrimination task (Studies 1-3) was the same in the 3 studies. On each trial, 2 stimuli were displayed either simultaneously or with an asynchrony below 100 ms, and participants decided whether or not the stimuli were asynchronous. They gave their response by pressing the “f” key for simultaneity, and the “j” key for asynchrony. The location of the stimuli and the precise SOAs differed between experiments, and are detailed in Table 2.

In Study 1, all participants first completed a temporal order judgment task (not shown here), before completing the asynchrony discrimination task that is presented in this manuscript. In Study 2, participants completed a typical asynchrony discrimination task, but there was no simultaneous condition. Furthermore, the SOA and stimuli direction (left–right or right–left) remained the same across 2 consecutive trials in 48 of the trials. This allowed us to verify whether these repetitions improved performance more so than a SOA increase from one trial to the next. In both Studies 1 and 2, there were 2 lateralized stimuli, 1 to the left and 1 to the right from the central fixation point. In Studies 3 and 4, 4 empty squares were drawn in the 4 corners of a virtual square (5.5° × 5.5°) around a central fixation point. In a quarter of trials, connectors linked squares horizontally, and in a quarter of trials, they linked squares vertically. In the remaining trials there was no connector. On each trial only 2 squares were filled in, at the top, bottom, or left or right sides of the virtual square. In the asynchrony discrimination task in Study 3, the 2 squares were displayed with various SOAs like in Studies 1 and 2 (Table 2). Study 423 was designed to test forward and backward masking on the detection of visual stimuli. On each trial, 2 targets were presented in the same locations as in Study 3, but always simultaneously. The targets were preceded (forward masking) or followed (backward masking) by a mask, consisting of 4 squares presented at each of the 4 tested conditions. In Study 4, participants were asked to locate the target.

Analyses

To test the hypothesis of implicit monitoring of the passage of time at the millisecond level, we tested the effects of previous trials (t − 1) on performance in the current trial (t), for the 2 groups of participants. More precisely, we tested whether performance improved when there was an increase in SOA from one trial to the next one, as a function of their response on the previous trial (correct or incorrect). Responses on trials t − 1 were considered separately, assuming that incorrect responses reflected a failure to detect the asynchrony. Furthermore, we considered trials with simultaneous squares as a special case, as simultaneity may elicit special processing, eg, by the detection of coincidence.25–27 We thus reasoned that the transition from a trial with simultaneous squares to asynchronous squares may differ from a transition from 1 given asynchrony to another. For this reason, we excluded all trials with simultaneous squares (on the current or the previous trial). In all the analyses, we compared the SOA on trial t − 1 and on trial t. We then grouped performance across trials as a function of the response on trial t − 1 (asynchrony correctly detected, or not), and of the SOA change from trial t − 1 to trial t (decrease, no change, increase in SOA). Note that this procedure grouped SOAs of different magnitudes, as trials preceded by trials with smaller SOAs necessarily include larger SOAs than trials preceded by trials with larger SOAs. Given the manner in which SOA direction is defined, the 3 conditions (SOA increase, decrease, or no change) were different with respect to SOA on trials t for the 3 conditions. Specifically, in the “increasing” condition there were no trials with the smallest, and in the “decreasing” condition no trials with the largest SOA (see Figure S1). Here we present the analysis of the data where the 2 extreme SOAs for each experiment were excluded, in order to match the difficulty across the categories. We have also repeated the analysis without the exclusion of the extreme SOAs. The detailed results of this analysis are shown in the Supplementary material. We performed the same analysis with inclusion of SOA on the current trial (t) and for each experiment separately, as shown in the Supplementary material. Importantly, the key results were replicated.

Given the difficulty of individuals with schizophrenia to benefit from the passage of time,20,21 and our hypothesis that their difficulty arises from impairments at the level of milliseconds, in this group we expect only a benefit from the reduction in task difficulty. In contrast, controls may display greater improvement when SOA increases from one trial to the next. Moreover, a benefit from the passage of time should be observed specifically in the case of a SOA increase (Figure 1). When the SOA remains the same, SOA expectation can be based on the previous trial, but the lack of change is not informative about a decision between the asynchrony and simultaneity of the stimuli. Similarly, when the SOA decreases there can hardly be a benefit: even if the decrease is encoded, it only indicates that the previous trial was asynchronous. The current trial with a shorter delay can correspond to either a simultaneity or an asynchrony. If individuals with schizophrenia have a specific impairment in monitoring the passage of time at the level of milliseconds, an impairment relative to controls should be observed only in the case of a SOA increase.

Statistical Analyses

In order to test our hypothesis simultaneously with all 3 datasets, we used a generalized linear mixed-effect model. Log-odds of correct response were predicted by the fixed factor structure consisting of the group (controls or individuals with schizophrenia), the study (Studies 1-3), the difference in SOA between the 2 successive trials (decreasing, same, or increasing), response on the previous trial (correct or incorrect), and their interactions. A random intercept was specified at the level of participant, nested within the study. However, given the complexity of the model, the full model ran into convergence issues. In order to automate the identification of the maximal generalized linear model that converges given the data, we used the buildmer function.28,29 The buildmer function was used to iterate through the models of different complexity and structure (with fixed and random effects) and to build the model with maximal complexity that was supported by the data. The criterion for inclusion of terms was set to Akaike Information Criterion (AIC). First, the terms were included in order of their contribution to the model to obtain the maximal model that converges, and then backward elimination was performed to find the maximal model with terms that explain the data best. The retained model included the 4 fixed factors and their first order interactions. Odds ratios (OR) are shown as measures of effect size.30

Results

Studies 1-3: Sequential Effects in Asynchrony Judgments

Performance on trial t is shown in Figure 2 as a function of the 3 categories of SOA difference between t − 1 and t, separately for correct and incorrect decisions on the previous trial (columns). Each row shows performance in a different study (Studies 1-3), and color codes the 2 groups (schizophrenia, left and control group, right). As shown in Figure 2, performance was better when SOA increased from trial t − 1 to trial t, compared to conditions when it remained the same or decreased. This was true regardless of the response on the previous trial, and in the 3 studies. Of note, trials on which SOA increases were easier than previous trials, which in part explained the increase in the performance. However, if individuals with schizophrenia have difficulties in benefiting from the passage of time at these short timescales, we expect their improvement to be smaller than that of individuals in the control group.

We tested a generalized linear mixed-effect model, and log-odds of correct response were predicted by the fixed factor structure consisting of the group (controls or individuals with schizophrenia), study (Studies 1-3), the difference in SOA between the 2 successive trials (decreasing, same, or increasing), response on the previous trial (correct or incorrect), and their interactions. A random intercept was specified at the level of participant, nested within study.

There was a significant effect of the SOA direction (χ2(2) = 555.321, P < .01), the response on the previous trial (χ2(1) = 304.433, P < .01), and the study (χ2(2) = 48.289, P < .01). Relative to the condition where the SOA decreased from trial t − 1 to trial t, performance was better when the SOA increased (βSOA Increasing = 1.205, SE = 0.058, z = 20.635, P < .01, OR = 3.336; βSOA Same = 0.170, SE = 0.058, z = 2.199, P < .05, OR = 1.19). Furthermore, when the response on the previous trial was correct, log-odds of correct response on trial t were greater (βResponse_t-1 = Correct = 1.403, SE = 0.089, z = 17.448, P < .01, OR = 4.067). Finally, there was an effect of the study, and relative to Study 1, overall performance was worse in Study 2 and Study 3 (βStudy2 = −0.867, SE = 0.169, z = −5.111, P < .01, OR = 0.420; βStudy3 = −1.120, SE = 0.182, z = −6.553, P < .01, OR = 0.326).

There was no evidence for a significant main effect of the group (χ2(1) = 2.2, P = .137). Importantly, the group interacted with the SOA direction (χ2(2) = 60.484, P < .01), such that performance of participants in the control group increased more when the SOA increased from the previous to the current trial, relative to individuals with schizophrenia (βSOA Increasing × Controls = 0.487, SE = 0.066, z = 7.411, P < .01, OR = 1.627; βSOA Same × Controls = 0.060, SE = 0.080, z = 0.727, P = .467, OR = 1.06).

The SOA direction also interacted with the previous response (χ2(2) = 108.887, P < .01). The interaction with the previous response indicated that an improvement in the performance for trials with a relative SOA increasing was smaller when the response on the previous trial was correct (βSOA Increasing × Response_t-1 = Correct = −0.185, SE = 0.069, z = −2.671, P < .01, OR = 0.831). When relative SOA was the same, the effect was greater if the previous response was correct; βSOA Same × Response_t-1 = Correct = 0.676, SE = 0.086, z = 7.862, P < .01, OR = 1.966).

There was also an interaction between response on the previous trial and the study (χ2(2) = 128.2476, P < .01), suggesting that the improvement in performance when response was correct on the previous trial was smaller in Studies 2 and 3 (βResponse_t-1 = Correct × Study3 = −0.570, SE = 0.070, z = −8.098, P < .01, OR = 0.565; βResponse_t-1 = Correct × Study3 = 0.09, SE = 0.082, z = 1.158, P = .247, OR = 1.09). Finally, there was evidence for an interaction between the response on the previous trial and the group (χ2(1) = 8.535, P < .01), such that the effect when the previous response was correct was greater in the control group (βResponse_t-1 = Correct × Controls = 0.181, SE = 0.07, z = 2.922, P < .01, OR = 1.198).

In support of our hypothesis, there was evidence for a greater improvement in performance for the increasing SOA condition in the control group of individuals compared to individuals with schizophrenia. The same pattern of results was obtained when sequential effects were analyzed in each study separately (see Supplementary material, Section 7).

Study 4: Masking Experiment

Results presented so far indicate that individuals with schizophrenia do not improve their performance as much as the participants in the control group, when the SOA is longer on the current trial than on the previous trial. These results are in line with the hypothesis that individuals with schizophrenia have difficulties in implicitly using the passage of time at the level of milliseconds. However, it is possible that they simply benefit less from the task becoming easier, regardless of the nature of the task. They may also have difficulties in segregating the successive stimuli, thus making it more difficult to benefit from the SOA increase. This segregation does not necessarily entail the coding of the delay change between the successive stimuli, as asynchrony and duration are 2 orthogonal concepts.17 To test the possibility of a temporal segregation impairment, we analyzed sequential effects in a masking paradigm, in which participants located a target presented at various delays before or after a mask. As emphasized in introduction, if the coding of the delay was impaired in individuals with schizophrenia, then a group effect should be observed selectively in the asynchrony detection task, whereas if it is the segregation of the stimuli in time that is impaired, their performance should be impaired in the same way in both tasks.

To test these alternatives, we re-analyzed a dataset from a study with a different task, with a design closely matching the visual display of Study 3 presented in this manuscript. Briefly, this was a novel masking paradigm, with 2 targets instead of only one typically presented in masking paradigms.23 We chose this task because its spatial complexity required a large number of trials, thus allowing for a calculation of performance as a function of the SOA change between trials.

We considered consecutive trials without order change, both with forward or both with backward masking, and consecutive trials with order change (one trial with forward masking and one trial with backward masking). Notably, the mechanisms underlying the 2 types of masking are suggested to differ,31 which could have in turn precluded any benefit from SOA increase. Figure 3 shows probability distributions of correct responses in the masking experiment, separately for the trials when masking type was different (top row) or the same for the two consecutive trials (bottom row), and as a function of the response on the previous trial (columns). Within each panel, distributions are shown for three difference categories: Decreasing, Same, and Increasing SOA, with each category corresponding to the relative difference between SOA on the current and previous trial. Blue violin plots represent performance of individuals with schizophrenia (left), and orange violin plots that of individuals in the control group (right). Performance was better when performance on the previous trial was correct, as well as when the difference in SOA increased from one trial to another, equally in the two groups of participants.

To test the specificity of the sequential effects obtained in the simultaneity and asynchrony discrimination tasks, we fitted data to a generalized linear mixed-effects model, with response on trial t as dependent variable, and the 4 fixed effects (group, response on trial t − 1, the difference in SOA, the difference in type of masking between the 2 consecutive trials) and their interactions as predictors. Participants were included as random intercept. We used the same analyses as before, with automatized model selection. As before, we included only intermediate SOAs, shared between the conditions. For analysis on the full dataset, see Supplementary material. The final model consisted of the fixed effects for the 4 predictors, 2-way interactions between the previous response and the difference in SOA, type of masking and group and a 3-way interaction between the response on trial t − 1, the masking type and group. We found an effect of the response on the previous trial (χ2(1) = 9.927, P < .01), and responses were more likely to be correct if the response on the previous trial was correct as well (βPrevious Correct = 0.357, SE = 0.113, z = 3.145, P < .01, OR = 1.429). There was also an effect of the difference in SOA between the 2 consecutive trials (χ2(2) = 12 756, P < .01). Performance was better when the SOA on the current trial t was greater than that of the previous trial t − 1 (βSOA Increasing = 0.229, SE = 0.064, z = 3.567, P < .01, OR = 1.257; βSOA Same = 0.1101, SE = 0.083, z = 1.207, P = .227, OR = 1.116), but there was no evidence for an interaction with the group. There was also evidence for a 3-way interaction between the group, the response on the previous trial and the relative masking type (χ2(1) = 10.604, P < .01). These effects suggest that the effects of the previous response in the control group depended on the difference in the masking type from 1 trial to the next (βPrevious Correct × Control × Masking Same = 0.759, SE = 0.233, z = 3.254, P < .01, OR = 2.136). Similar results were obtained when we considered only the trials in which the masking type did not change from 1 trial to the next (see Supplementary material for more details and visual representation of results). In summary, the pattern of results observed here is in agreement with the hypothesis that individuals with schizophrenia have reduced sensitivity to passage of time at the millisecond level rather than a difficulty to benefit from the task becoming easier, or a non-specific difficulty to segregate stimuli in time.

Correlations

We correlated the change in performance across conditions with clinical parameters, including the dosage of antipsychotics, but did not find any correlation.

Discussion

Not surprisingly, the results show that all participants benefit from the task becoming easier from 1 trial to the next. This was observed in 2 types of tasks. First, it was observed when participants were asked to detect an asynchrony between 2 successive stimuli (Studies 1-3). Second, an improvement was observed in the masking experiment, when participants detected a spatial location of a stimulus (Study 4). Most importantly, the improvement observed with the SOA increase was larger in the control group than in individuals with schizophrenia, and this improvement was task specific. It occurred only in the simultaneity/asynchrony discrimination task, not in the masking task. The results are consistent with our hypothesis that individuals in the control group benefit from the millisecond-level delays when asynchrony detection is task-relevant, beyond the advantage of reduced task difficulty. Nevertheless, some alternatives need to be discussed before settling on that conclusion.

The fact that in the masking task all participants benefited equally from the task becoming easier is important. This result indicates that we can exclude an explanation of the results on the temporal task in terms of a non-specific insensitivity to the task becoming easier, and that the improvement was related to the nature of the task. Most importantly, the masking results also confirm that individuals with schizophrenia are sensitive to the SOA increase from trial to trial. This may appear as surprising, given their difficulty in explicitly detect asynchronies documented in the literature.8–16 However, despite the difficulty of individuals with schizophrenia have in explicitly reporting asynchronies, several studies indicate that they are implicitly affected by short asynchronies, and sometimes more so than controls.11,12 Here we show that in spite of their sensitivity to short asynchronies, individuals with schizophrenia do not benefit from this increase as much as controls when the task is to detect an asynchrony. Is there another explanation for this group difference than a difficulty benefiting from the delay increase?

First, it should be noted that the results obtained in the present study in the simultaneity/asynchrony tasks cannot be easily dismissed. They are replicated in 3 datasets, including individuals with schizophrenia from different centers (in Strasbourg or Nancy). The group difference was selectively observed when the SOA increased from trial t − 1 to trial t, both with equalized SOAs across conditions and in the complete datasets (see the Supplementary material for a more in-depth discussion of the relationship between the present results and the specificities previously observed in patients when processing millisecond-level asynchronies). The results are consistent with the prior observations that individuals with schizophrenia are disturbed when the delay on trial t is not identical to the delay in trial t − 1, at least when those delays are small.32

Previous work showed that when participants made pointing actions on a virtual surface, and the haptic feedback (ie, the moment of contact with the surface) was postponed, the feeling of control of individuals with schizophrenia decreased even when the delay was only 15 ms, whereas the feeling of control remained stable in the control group for short delays. In the context of this study, we might expect individuals with schizophrenia to be perturbed when the SOA increased because they are oversensitive to the SOA changes from trial to trial. This might explain a smaller benefit from the SOA increase in individuals with schizophrenia relative to controls. Two results are not consistent with this hypothesis. First, a reduction in SOA should perturb their performance in the same manner, which was not the case. Additionally, if individuals with schizophrenia were implicitly perturbed by a SOA increase, then we would expect the same pattern of results in the masking experiment (Study 4), which is not what we observed.

In fact, the contrast between the results found in the masking and the asynchrony detection task is consistent with our hypothesis of a decreased benefit of the millisecond-level delay signal in individuals with schizophrenia. As already emphasized in the introduction, one important difference between the masking and simultaneity/asynchrony discrimination tasks concerns the incidental impact of the SOA in the masking task, whereas in the temporal judgment the detection of a delay increase from trial t − 1 to trial t is bound to help participants to decide that stimuli were asynchronous. Individuals with schizophrenia do benefit from the task becoming easier as much as controls in the masking experiment. This suggests that the group difference in the temporal judgment reveals that controls benefit from the increase in the delay from trial t − 1 to trial t when detecting asynchronies, whereas individuals with schizophrenia show impaired ability to benefiting from this increase. This may fit with their inability to explicitly detect short asynchronies,11,12,32 and with the studies reporting noisy processing at the millisecond level.

In individuals with schizophrenia, asynchronies and delays would be detected at the subthreshold level but would not be experienced consciously. Such a difficulty may or may not be related to a general difficulty in conscious realization.33 However, whatever the precise mechanisms of the group difference found in the present study, the reduced benefit of SOA increase in individuals with schizophrenia may bear a special clinical significance, if it reveals a failure to process increased delays from one trial to the next. This may correspond to the experience of the individuals with schizophrenia, who report that time stops and disappears.34 It may represent an elementary difficulty paving the way for a distorted experience of the passage of time, as it may affect processing at the level of seconds as well.20–21 It may thus contribute to disorders of the sense of self, given the established link between time and self.1–6

Recent evidence suggests increased variability in responses to external events in individuals with schizophrenia, reflected in greater phase variability of electroencephalographic responses.35–37 Difficulties in temporal processing in individuals with schizophrenia could be related to this imprecision in responses, particularly in the millisecond range.35 The sequential effects reported here could be a consequence of unreliable detection of event onsets in time.

This study has some limitations. Individuals with schizophrenia were evaluated clinically, but no phenomenological scale was used, limiting our conclusions regarding the clinical meaning of the results. Furthermore, our suggestion that the results are related to a general difficulty in experiencing the passage of time is indirect. Future studies should focus on the relationship between the benefit of the passage of time at several time scales, to verify the hypothesized link between difficulties in processing delays at the level of milliseconds and the passage of time at the level of seconds.
