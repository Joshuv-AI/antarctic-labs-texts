How does LAMB2 contribute to kidney disease? Insights from a pediatric case
Authors: I. Trutin, L. Oletić, D. Galešić Ljubanović, D. Krgović, Tamara Nikuševa Martić
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWe present the case of an 11-year-old girl who has been followed for the past four years after the incidental discovery of asymptomatic microhematuria during school enrollment.Case presentationOver the course of follow-up, glomerular-origin microhematuria persisted, and mild proteinuria developed and persisted for over six months. This prompted the need for a kidney biopsy. Light microscopy revealed normal kidney tissue, and immunofluorescence findings were negative. However, electron microscopy demonstrated significant variation in glomerular basement membrane (GBM) thickness, with focal splitting. A diagnosis of Alport syndrome was initially suspected, but genetic testing for collagen IV mutations (COL4A3, COL4A4, COL4A5) did not support this diagnosis. Instead, a variant in the LAMB2 gene (c.5039 C > T, p.Ala1680Val) was identified. The variant has not been previously described in the literature but is listed in ClinVar as a variant is of uncertain significance (VUS) and may be associated with the GBM abnormalities observed, leading to the persistent hematuria and proteinuria. Over a one-year follow-up period, the patient has maintained normal renal function without significant proteinuria or hypertension, with only persistent microhematuria.ConclusionsGiven the uncertain long-term outcome and the potential impact of the LAMB2 variant on renal function, regular nephrological monitoring remains essential to detect and manage any progression to end-stage renal disease.

Background

Microhematuria may result from a wide spectrum of benign to serious conditions, including hereditary glomerulopathies [1]. Alport syndrome (AS) is among the most recognised hereditary causes of GBM abnormalities, characterised by progressive nephropathy, sensorineural hearing loss, and ocular findings. Accurate diagnosis of hereditary nephropathies remains challenging, particularly when clinical, histopathological, and genetic features do not align [2]. The GBM contains an atypical assortment of basement membrane protein isoforms, including collagen α3, α4, and α5 (IV) and laminin-521 (LM521; α5, β2, γ1) [3]. Mutations in collagen α3, α4, and α5 (IV) cause AS, a kidney disease characterised by variable ear and eye defects [2]. Mutations in the laminin β2 gene (LAMB2) cause Pierson syndrome (PS), which is characterized by congenital nephrotic syndrome with severe ocular and neuromuscular defects, and occasionally milder nephropathy without extrarenal abnormalities [4]. Genetic testing is essential in cases with overlapping phenotypes or inconclusive histology to refine diagnosis and guide management.

Case presentation

An 11-year-old girl, previously healthy except for a history of phlegmonous appendicitis, was followed for four years due to an incidental finding of asymptomatic microhematuria (6–10 red blood cells per high-power field) during school enrollment. Initial evaluation showed normal urea (2.2 mmol/L), creatinine (41 µmol/L), and proteinuria within normal limits (< 4 mg/m²/h). There was no hypercalciuria (1.4 mmol/24 h), and glomerular filtration rate (GFR) calculated using the Schwartz formula was normal (129 mL/min/1.73 m²). Autoimmune screening revealed a positive ANA (1:160) with negative ANCA and normal complement levels (C3 1.25 g/L, C4 0.35 g/L). Renal ultrasound was unremarkable. Family urinalyses were normal. Microhematuria persisted with onset of mild proteinuria (7.5 mg/m²/h) over six months, prompting a kidney biopsy. Light microscopy was normal, and immunofluorescence was negative. Electron microscopy (EM) revealed focal splitting and variable GBM thickness (160–470 nm; mean 274 nm, SD 62 nm), suggestive of a hereditary GBM disorder in the spectrum of AS (Fig. 1). However, no ocular, auditory, or neurological abnormalities were present. A next-generation sequencing (NGS) panel for progressive kidney disease revealed no pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants in COL4A3, COL4A4, or COL4A5. Seven VUS were identified. The identified heterozygous LAMB2 variant (c.5039 C > T, p.Ala1680Val) lies within the C-terminal LG5 domain, a region important for laminin-β2 function in the GBM. This variant is listed in ClinVar as a variant of uncertain significance (VCV000964308.1). Predictive tools such as SIFT (tolerated), PolyPhen-2 (benign), and AlphaMissense (benign) do not strongly support pathogenicity. However, in silico predictions are not definitive, particularly when ultrastructural changes are present. Its population frequency in gnomAD v4.1.0 is approximately 3.26 × 10^-4.

Discussion

LAMB2 mutations are classically linked to PS, a severe autosomal recessive disorder characterised by congenital nephrotic syndrome, ocular malformations, and early renal failure. Loss-of-function variants often result in the absence of laminin β2 expression. Several studies have described LAMB2 variants associated with mild phenotypes. For example, p.Arg469Gln and p.Arg1078Cys have been found in patients with isolated proteinuria or hematuria without ocular or neuromuscular symptoms. Kikkawa et al. observed that missense variants associated with non-syndromic nephropathy tend to cluster in the β₂ short-arm region (exons 10–24; LEa–LF–LEb), a domain not typically implicated in PS, further supporting a genotype–phenotype correlation wherein mutations in this region are associated with isolated renal involvement and the absence of ocular or neuromuscular symptoms [5]. Hypomorphic missense variants (like p.Arg469Gln, p.Arg1078Cys) in LEa–LF–LEb maintain secretion of laminin β₂ and show milder effects, supporting framework for our hypothesis [5]. Classic PS is linked to truncating LAMB2 mutations scattered throughout the gene, most notably in the LN domain, causing absent or severely dysfunctional laminin β₂ [6]. Compared to these, our patient’s p.Ala1680Val variant is located in a region not previously associated with severe truncating mutations, possibly supporting a hypomorphic effect. The presence of GBM lamellation on EM, clinical persistence of glomerular hematuria and proteinuria, and absence of mutations in collagen IV genes raises suspicion for a LAMB2-associated nephropathy. This expands the phenotypic spectrum associated with LAMB2 and highlights the complexity of genotype-phenotype correlations in glomerular diseases.

Variants in HOGA1 and SDHB are unlikely to be related to the observed renal findings. HOGA1 is associated with autosomal recessive primary hyperoxaluria type III [7], which was excluded by normal metabolic screening. SDHB mutations, though linked to pheochromocytoma and paraganglioma syndromes, typically cause catecholamine excess and tumour-related hematuria, not glomerular microhematuria [8, 9].

Our patient’s phenotype-persistent microhematuria, mild proteinuria, and absence of systemic symptoms is consistent with this emerging spectrum of mild LAMB2-related nephropathies.

Although the p.Ala1680Val variant in LAMB2 is rare in the general population, its precise contribution to the patient’s phenotype remains uncertain. Given the absence of truncating changes and the localization to a region not typically associated with severe disease, it is unlikely to be causal. Nevertheless, within a polygenic background, it may modestly contribute to the observed clinical features. The pathogenicity of this variant, however, remains unclear without functional validation. Segregation analysis was not performed, limiting conclusions regarding de novo occurrence or familial inheritance. Although whole-exome sequencing (WES) excluded pathogenic variants in COL4A3, COL4A4, and COL4A5, whole- genome sequencing or transcriptomic approaches would be necessary to exclude deep intronic or regulatory mutations. These represent important limitations of our report.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this case supports the possibility that heterozygous variants in LAMB2 may contribute to isolated nephropathy in the absence of classical Pierson syndrome features. Although a definitive pathogenic role of the LAMB2:c.5039 C >T variant remains unconfirmed, its correlation with clinical and EM findings suggests potential relevance. Further studies, including family testing and functional assays, are necessary to clarify its clinical significance. Genetic analysis was instrumental in avoiding misdiagnosis and unnecessary treatment, highlighting its importance in cases of ambiguous nephropathy.
