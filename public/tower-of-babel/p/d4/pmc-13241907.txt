Dental Care Utilization Among Sexual and Gender Minority Individuals
Authors: Ishita Singh, Oladipo Afolayan, Sarah S Jackson, Irene Tamí‐Maury
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesSexual and gender minority (SGM) individuals can experience notable health disparities, including higher HIV prevalence, social isolation, substance abuse, lack of culturally competent providers, and poorer health outcomes compared to heterosexuals due to stigma and discrimination. This study aims to identify predictors of dental care utilization across sexual minorities using the 2023 TEXAS PRIDE Survey.MethodsWe analyzed data from 517 SGM individuals, assessing sociodemographic characteristics, health behaviors, and recent medical and dental visits. Multivariable logistic regression identified predictors of dental care utilization (i.e., a dental visit in the past year).ResultsAmong sexual orientation groups, reported dental care utilization was highest among gay (23.7%) and lesbian (21%) individuals, followed by bisexual (18.3%), pansexual (11.1%), queer (9.9%), and other sexual orientations (16.2%). Those with a college degree were significantly more likely to report dental utilization compared to those with less education. Individuals who did not have a medical visit in the past year were 0.27 times as likely to utilize dental care than those who did. No significant disparities in dental care utilization were found based on sexual orientation in the regression analysis.ConclusionsHigher education and recent medical visits are significant predictors of dental care utilization among SGM individuals. Efforts to reduce oral health disparities should focus on affordable dental care coverage, improving health literacy, fostering inclusive dental environments, and training providers in SGM cultural sensitivity. Future research should explore integrated healthcare approaches and provider cultural competence training to address these gaps.Keywords: dental care, dental visit, health disparities, LGBTQ+, sexual and gender minorities, sexual and gender minority, United States

Introduction

In the United States, 7.6% of US adults identified as lesbian, gay, bisexual, transgender, queer, and other sexual and gender minority (SGM) identities, in 2023 [1]. SGM individuals can experience notable health disparities, including an increased prevalence of HIV, social isolation, substance abuse, lack of culturally competent providers, and poorer health outcomes compared to their cisgender heterosexual counterparts due to stigma, discrimination, and marginalization [2]. However, existing literature on healthcare utilization in SGM communities is sparse, and research on access to and utilization of dental care within this population is particularly lacking [3]. Previous research has revealed lower rates of routine healthcare utilization [4], insurance coverage [5], and an unmet need for care [6, 7] in SGM individuals compared to non‐SGM individuals.

Previous research has shown that sexual minorities experience limited access to dental care and worse oral health outcomes, including higher rates of caries, tooth loss, chronic periodontitis, and oral HPV infection compared to heterosexual individuals [3]. Moreover, gender minorities, such as transgender adults, have shown poor oral health and significant oral mucosal disorders, including oral nicotine stomatitis, leukoplakia, and ulcers, exacerbated by heightened tobacco and alcohol use [8]. Despite these challenges, dental utilization among SGM individuals, particularly the differences between subgroups, remains underexplored.

Rationale for the Study

Dental utilization is frequently used in public health research as a proxy for preventive care, since routine visits allow for early detection and management of disease. While our data did not differentiate reasons for the visit, framing utilization in this way remains important for SGM populations, who face heightened risk factors and barriers to care. By examining overall utilization, this study provides insight into whether SGM adults are equitably accessing dental services. Additionally, we seek to investigate dental care utilization across different sexual minorities, using data from the 2023 TEXAS PRIDE Survey conducted by the University of Texas Health Science Center at Houston.

Methods

Study Design

We conducted a cross‐sectional study to determine dental care utilization among SGM individuals in Texas, United States. Participants were recruited using a non‐probability convenience sampling method during Pride Month (June 2023).

Study Population—Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

During Pride Month, a total of 547 SGM individuals were surveyed. Inclusion criteria were age ≥ 18 years, residence in Texas at the time of survey, and self‐identification as SGM. We excluded respondents who did not provide consent and those with missing data on the primary outcome (time since last dental visit). The analytic sample was 517 SGM respondents with non‐missing outcome data; cisgender heterosexual respondents were not included. Multivariable models used complete‐case estimation, so sample sizes vary across analyses.

Data Collection and Management

A bilingual (English/Spanish) survey, consisting of 46 items, was administered to SGM individuals. Adapted from previous TEXAS PRIDE studies, the survey was distributed in two formats: paper surveys at the 2023 Houston Pride Parade and Festival, and online via anonymous links and QR codes in email blasts to SGM and ally organizations across Texas. The survey took approximately 10 min to complete.

Data from paper surveys was manually entered into the Qualtrics electronic platform, which also received data from the online responses. Qualtrics was chosen for its robust, user‐friendly features, ensuring efficient and secure data collection. Detailed data collection procedures are available in previous publications [9, 10].

Measures

Outcome (Dependent) Variable

The main outcome of interest in this study was dental care utilization, which was assessed using the survey question, “About how long has it been since you last visited a dentist, orthodontist, oral surgeon, and/or dental hygienist?” Participants who answered, “6 months or less,” or “more than 6 months, but not more than 1 year” were categorized as having “dental utilization” (coded as 1), while those who answered, “more than 1 year,” “never been,” or “I do not know/am not sure” were categorized as having “no dental utilization” (coded as 0). This dichotomous outcome variable was used for multivariable analyses, controlling for the independent variables listed below. The 30 individuals with missing responses to the question, “About how long has it been since you last visited a dentist, orthodontist, oral surgeon, and/or dental hygienist?” were excluded.

Main Explanatory (Independent) Variables

Sociodemographic characteristics included a diverse range of factors among participants. Age was modeled as a continuous variable (in years). Gender identity was classified as man, woman, and transgender/nonbinary/genderfluid/genderqueer. Sex assigned at birth was recorded as male, female, and intersex. Sexual orientation was categorized into lesbian, gay, bisexual, pansexual, queer, and others (including asexual, straight/heterosexual, unsure, among gender‐minority respondents). Race and ethnicity were categorized as Hispanic/Latino, non‐Hispanic White, non‐Hispanic Black, and non‐Hispanic other. Relationship status was classified as married/living with significant other/partnered and single/never married/divorced/separated. Participants were also queried about living with children (yes/no), level of education (High school or less, some college/associate degree, and college degree or more), work status (working full‐time/part‐time and not working/volunteering/retired), healthcare coverage (insured vs. uninsured), and being out as an LGTQ+ person (to family, friends, coworkers, doctors and other healthcare providers, and no).

Participants' medical history and disease conditions included body mass index (BMI, kg/m2; modeled as continuous), the length of time since their last doctor visit, categorized as within the last year or more than 1 year; and COVID‐19 vaccination status categorized as fully vaccinated, few doses or no. Participants also reported on the medical conditions they were diagnosed with, categorized as obesity/overweight, depression/anxiety/PTSD, hypertension, sleep disorder or trouble sleeping, and others.

The following health behaviors were included in this study: current smoking status (yes/no), and levels of physical activity categorized as less than 3 days per week and 3 days or more per week [11].

Statistical Analysis

We used STATA 18.0/SE statistical software for all analyses. Data analysis consisted of descriptive statistics with frequency distributions and bivariate regression for participants' sociodemographic, medical history, and behavioral characteristics by dental care utilization. Pearson's Chi‐square test (or Fisher's exact test, when appropriate) was performed to assess associations between categories. The mean (±SD) was calculated for continuous variables. Continuous variables were compared using an independent samples t‐test.

We used multivariable logistic regression to examine the predictors of dental care utilization, using the purposive variable selection technique [12]. Covariates were selected a priori and included education level, medical health coverage, time since last doctor visit, BMI, age, transgender status, gender identity, sex at birth, sexual orientation, race/ethnicity, work status, relationship status, living with children status, current smoking status, physical activity status, being “out” as an SGM person, COVID‐19 vaccination status, and diagnosed medical conditions (mutually exclusive categories as defined in Table 1). Models used complete‐case estimation. Results are reported as adjusted odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals. We emphasized effect sizes and confidence intervals; p values are two‐sided and correspond to the model‐adjusted estimates, with a significance threshold of 0.05.

Ethics Approval and Consent to Participate

Participation in the study was voluntary, and written informed consent was granted from all subjects. The Institutional Review Board (IRB) at the University of Texas Health Science Center at Houston (UTHealth Houston) approved the study under protocol number HSC‐SPH‐20‐0200. Individuals did not receive any form of compensation for their participation.

Results

Sociodemographic and Health‐Related Characteristics

Our study included 517 participants, with a mean age of 32.7 years (SD ±13.5 years). Of these, 19.9% identified as lesbian, 22.2% as gay, 19.2% as bisexual, 13.5% as pansexual, 9.3% as queer, and the remaining 15.9% identified with other sexual orientations, including asexual, unsure, and so forth. The sample was predominantly assigned female at birth (64.0%) and non‐Hispanic White (56.5%). Almost a quarter self‐identified as transgender (23.3%). More than half of the participants reported being single/never married/divorced or separated (62.9%), and less than a quarter were currently living with children (20.1%). A significant portion of the sample lacked medical health coverage (13.4%), were retired/not working or volunteering (17.4%), and were current smokers (12.9%). 84.8% of the sample was overweight or obese, and nearly one‐third of the respondents reported being diagnosed with psychological distress such as depression, anxiety or posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) (35.0%) (Table 1).

Prevalence and Predictors of Dental Utilization

Among sexual orientation groups, reported dental care utilization (i.e., a dental visit in the past year) was highest among gay (23.7%) and lesbian (21%) individuals, followed by bisexual (18.3%), pansexual (11.1%), queer (9.9%) and other sexual orientations (16.2%). After adjusting for potential confounders, the associations between sexual orientation and dental care utilization were not significant (p > 0.05). Those with some college or associate degree were slightly over half as likely to have dental utilization compared to those with at least a college degree or higher (aOR = 0.60, 95% CI = 0.36–0.94; p = 0.048). Individuals who did not visit a doctor or other health professional in the past year were 0.27 times as likely to have dental utilization than those who did have a doctor visit during the same period (aOR = 0.27, 95% CI = 0.15–0.50; p < 0.001) (Table 2). Results were similar in sensitivity analyses that additionally adjusted for mode of data collection (paper vs. online) and area of residence (Houston Metropolitan Area vs. other) (Supporting Information Appendix A).

Discussion

Our study is the first to explore the predictors of dental utilization among the SGM community in Texas, and one of the few in the United States. Our findings indicate that recent medical visits and higher educational attainment are significant predictors of increased dental utilization in this group. Interestingly, disparities in dental care utilization were not found based on sexual orientation—there were no statistically significant differences in the likelihood of having had a dental visit in the past year among lesbian, gay or bisexual women; gay or bisexual men; and individuals of other sexual orientations. This pattern suggests that unmeasured barriers may be influencing utilization, extending beyond commonly assessed factors such as insurance, employment, or outness, and instead reflecting structural issues (e.g., provider availability, cost barriers, clinic inclusivity) and cultural factors (e.g., stigma, prior negative experiences, community norms). These findings underscore the need for more nuanced research into the social contexts shaping SGM oral health care access.

The minority stress model [14] explains health disparities among SGM individuals by highlighting the chronic stress they face due to their sexual orientation and gender identity. It identifies three key mechanisms: internalized homophobia, anticipated rejection, and actual experiences of prejudice, which together lead to poorer health outcomes. Consequently, these individuals are at an elevated risk for substance abuse, tobacco, and alcohol use, which significantly increases their vulnerability to oral diseases such as dental caries, periodontal disease, tooth loss, and oral cancer, including precancerous lesions [15, 16]. Barriers such as stigma, marginalization, homophobia, perceived discrimination, negative attitudes, accessibility issues, and the high cost of dental services further exacerbate these disparities [3, 15, 17]. Additionally, SGM patients often lack trust in their relationship with physicians, fearing poor quality care and having their sexual orientation or gender identity compromised [17], making them less likely to seek health care services than cisgender heterosexual people.

Our findings reveal a significant association between medical visits in the past year and dental care utilization among respondents. Almost 80% of those who sought dental care during this period also had a medical visit. Conversely, SGM individuals who did not seek dental care were less likely to utilize medical services. This finding reflects a pattern where individuals who prioritize their overall health through medical visits also tend to address their dental health needs. Literature shows that healthcare‐seeking behaviors are influenced by past negative experiences in healthcare settings related to gender identity such as verbal and physical abuse, refusal of treatment, and incorrect use of pronouns [14]. Studies indicate that 33% of transgender individuals and between 20% and 40% of SGM individuals report such negative encounters and medical mistrust while accessing healthcare [18]. While these experiences are recognized barriers, our findings showed that respondents with recent medical visits were more likely to also report a dental visit, and most were out to their providers. This suggests that, in this sample, engagement with health care may facilitate rather than hinder dental utilization, although stigma and past experiences remain important considerations for future research.

High education level was significantly associated with dental utilization, consistent with previous research [19, 20, 21]. Education may enhance health literacy, enabling individuals to navigate healthcare systems, advocate for their needs, and engage in preventive care and timely treatment [22]. However, socioeconomic disparities and discrimination often limit educational opportunities for SGM individuals, particularly among people of color, worsening health disparities and reducing dental utilization [23]. Targeted educational interventions may be necessary to improve health literacy within SGM communities and encourage proactive healthcare‐seeking behaviors.

Access to insurance coverage is crucial for facilitating dental utilization among SGM individuals, as it helps reduce financial barriers and promotes preventive and routine care [24, 25, 26]. Although prior studies have shown insurance to be an important determinant of dental care, our study did not find medical insurance status (used as a proxy for dental coverage) to be a significant predictor of utilization. This may reflect differences in how medical versus dental benefits are structured, or unmeasured factors that more directly affect dental coverage. Nonetheless, there is a critical need to expand dental coverage, especially for adults, given the limited dental benefits in many public insurance plans and the high costs of private dental insurance [27]. Additionally, SGM individuals often face insurance discrimination. A systematic review indicates that only 75% of gender minority (people whose gender does not align with the sex assigned at birth) people are insured, 15% lower than the general population's prevalence at 91.2% [28]. This disparity can be tied to higher rates of employment discrimination [29] and unemployment rates in this community [30]. Furthermore, SGM individuals may face challenges qualifying for government programs like Medicaid or the Affordable Care Act (ACA) subsidies due to varying income levels, marital status, and employment statuses. Expanding healthcare coverage to include comprehensive dental benefits and addressing insurance discrimination is crucial for improving dental utilization rates and, consequently, dental health outcomes within the SGM community.

One of the primary strengths of this study is its focus on the dental utilization patterns within the SGM community, an underrepresented and marginalized population in healthcare research. Our study's focus on SGM individuals, without including a heterosexual control group, is a key strength. Given the heterogeneity within the SGM community, it is crucial to explore disparities among the subgroups to capture their unique experiences and healthcare needs. By examining disparities within this diverse population, our study provides a more granular and nuanced understanding of dental care utilization, which is often overlooked in broader analysis. Unfortunately, we had small numbers for some SGM categories, particularly bisexual individuals, and transgender and nonbinary people, which precluded us from analyzing these groups separately. Being a cross‐sectional survey, our data cannot be used to infer causality and is subject to recall and social desirability biases. Our measure of dental utilization did not distinguish between preventive visits and visits for treatment, which limits interpretation of utilization strictly as preventive care. The TEXAS PRIDE Survey did not include specific questions on dental insurance, necessitating assumptions based on responses about medical insurance coverage. The unique sociocultural dynamics of the study population (all data were collected from individuals living in the State of Texas by the time of survey completion) may limit the generalizability of our findings to the broader SGM population across the United States. Several variables that might capture systemic barriers, such as insurance status, employment, race, and outness to providers, were not significantly associated with dental utilization in our analyses, which limits the extent to which broader systemic conclusions can be drawn.

Conclusion

Results suggest that predictors of dental care utilization among SGM individuals include having recent medical visits, and attaining higher education (i.e., a college degree). These findings suggest that integrated approaches linking medical and dental care, as well as targeted outreach to those with lower educational attainment, may help reduce disparities. While broader factors such as affordability, provider inclusivity, and medical mistrust are well‐documented in the literature, our study highlights the need for future research to directly examine how these factors shape oral health care access among SGM populations.
