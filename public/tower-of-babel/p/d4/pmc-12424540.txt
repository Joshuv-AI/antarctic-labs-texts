Efficacy and safety of the academic anti‐CD19 chimeric antigen receptor T‐cell product varnimcabtagene autoleucel for the treatment of relapsed/refractory follicular lymphoma
Authors: Núria Martínez‐Cibrián, Valentín Ortiz‐Maldonado, Marta Español‐Rego, Leticia Alserawan, Sergio Navarro, Nil Albiol, Miquel Lozano, Paola Charry, Laura Magnano, Andrea Rivero, Juan G Correa, Pablo Mozas, Albert Cortés‐Bullich, Carlos Jiménez‐Vicente, Eva Giné, Mercedes Montoro‐Lorite, Carla Ramos, Pilar Ayora, Hugo Calderón, María Sánchez‐Castañón, Daniel Benítez‐Ribas, Juan J Mata‐Molanes, Kelly Rojas, Xavier Setoaín, Sonia Rodríguez, Adrià Murias, Pau Alcubilla, Sara Varea, Eulalia Olesti, Mireia Bachiller, María Calvo‐Orteu, Carla Sans‐Pola, Joaquín Sáez‐Peñataro, Carlos Fernández de Larrea, Armando López‐Guillermo, E Azucena González‐Navarro, Manel Juan, Julio Delgado
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe report the outcome of patients with relapsed/refractory (R/R) follicular lymphoma (FL) treated with varnimcabtagene autoleucel (var‐cel), an academic anti‐CD19 chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T‐cell product. Patients were included in the CART19‐BE‐01 clinical trial and a compassionate use program. Twenty‐seven patients with FL were treated. Cytokine release syndrome (any grade) occurred in 55% of patients (4% Grade ≥3). Only 1 case (4%) of Grade 1 neurotoxicity was documented. The objective response rate was 100% at Day +100 (93% complete response rate), and the 3‐year duration of response was 78%. The 3‐year progression‐free survival and overall survival were 78% and 81%, respectively. All patients developed B‐cell aplasia, and the 3‐year incidence of B‐cell recovery was 17%. In conclusion, patients with R/R FL treated with var‐cel obtained excellent disease control, with prolonged CAR T‐cell survival and manageable toxicity. This trial was registered as NCT03144583.

INTRODUCTION

Chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T‐cells targeting CD19, such as tisagenlecleucel (tisa‐cel) 1 , 2 and axicabtagene ciloleucel (axi‐cel), 3 , 4 have shown outstanding results in patients with relapsed/refractory (R/R) follicular lymphoma (FL), leading to their approval in the EU and other jurisdictions. A similar product, lisocabtagene maraleucel (liso‐cel), has obtained comparable results in the same patient population 5 and was recently approved for this indication in the United States, but not yet in the EU. More specifically, these products achieved outstanding efficacy in high‐risk patients, including those with high follicular lymphoma international prognostic index (FLIPI), those experiencing progression of disease within 24 months of frontline treatment (POD24), or patients who were refractory to rituximab and alkylating agents (double refractory). Unfortunately, however, access to these products is hampered by a variety of reasons, including high cost, lack of reimbursement, limited manufacturing capacity, complex referral schemes, and controversial health‐technology assessment, all of which have paved the way to the development of academic CAR T‐cell products. 6

In Spain, the results from the CART19‐BE‐01 clinical trial evaluating the safety and efficacy of the academic anti‐CD19 CAR T‐cell product varnimcabtagene autoleucel (var‐cel, ARI‐0001 cells), manufactured by Hospital Clínic de Barcelona (HCB), 7 , 8 , 9 led to its approval, as Hospital Exemption, for patients older than 25 years of age with R/R B‐cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia (B‐ALL). 10 The product's price was subsequently agreed with the Spanish Ministry of Health at €89,270. Furthermore, var‐cel has been granted PRIority MEdicines designation by the European Medicines Agency for the same indication.

Var‐cel is not approved for the treatment of R/R FL, but inclusion into a name‐based compassionate use program (CUP) may be requested in the absence of alternative therapy. These requests are individually approved by the Spanish Medicines Agency (AEMPS) and the Spanish National CART‐cell therapy Experts Committee. Patients with R/R FL treated under the CUP are those not covered by the approved indications for commercial CAR T‐cell products.

Here, we report the updated outcomes of all consecutive patients with R/R FL treated with var‐cel within the CART19‐BE‐01 clinical trial 7 and subsequent CUP.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Patient population

This study includes all patients with R/R FL treated with var‐cel at HCB from July 2017 to December 2024, including those treated in the CART19‐BE‐01 trial (registered as NCT03144583) and the consecutive CUP. 7 The CART19‐BE‐01 study was a single‐arm, multicenter, open‐label pilot study evaluating the safety and efficacy of var‐cel in patients with R/R B‐cell malignancies. Eligible patients with FL had to have all the following: (i) CD19‐positive second or further relapse in patients who were either ineligible for, or had relapsed after, autologous or allogeneic hematopoietic cell transplantation (HCT); (ii) age from 2 to 80 years; (iii) no macroscopic involvement of the central nervous system (CNS) at infusion; (iv) ECOG performance status 0–2; (v) estimated life expectancy from 3 months; and (vi) adequate venous access. Key exclusion criteria included history of other malignancy unless it had been in remission for more than 3 years; significant renal, hepatic, pulmonary, or cardiac impairment; active immunosuppressive therapy; human immunodeficiency virus infection; active hepatitis B virus or hepatitis C virus infection; and active infection requiring systemic therapy. Patients with R/R FL treated under the CUP fulfilled the same inclusion/exclusion criteria as those included in the CART19‐BE‐01 trial.

The AEMPS and the Institutional Review Board (IRB)/Ethics Committee (EC) of HCB approved the original CART19‐BE‐01 trial, which was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki (last updated version, Fortaleza, Brazil, 2013). The retrospective analysis was also approved by the IRB/EC. All patients receiving var‐cel under the CUP were individually authorized by the AEMPS and the National CART‐cell Experts Committee.

Study design and endpoints

The primary endpoint of the CART19‐BE‐01 trial was safety as determined by incidence of adverse events (AEs) and non‐relapse mortality (NRM) at Day +100. 7 AEs of special interest were cytokine release syndrome (CRS) and immune‐effector cell‐associated neurotoxicity syndrome (ICANS). For this analysis, CRS and ICANS were graded according to the ASTCT 2019 consensus grading guidelines. 11 Late immune‐effector cell‐associated hematotoxicity (ICAHT) was graded as previously described. 12 Other AEs were graded according to common terminology criteria, version 4.0. Efficacy was analyzed using the Lugano criteria, 13 including bone marrow aspirate/biopsy and/or cerebrospinal fluid analysis in case of bone marrow and/or CNS involvement, as appropriate. Secondary endpoints included objective and complete response rates (ORR/CRR), duration of response (DOR), progression‐free survival (PFS), overall survival (OS), and cumulative incidence of B‐cell recovery. The ORR/CRR was assessed at Day +28, Day +100, Month +6, Month +12, and Month +18, but the prespecified primary efficacy evaluation was performed at Day +100 from var‐cel infusion.

Var‐cel production, treatment, and laboratory analysis

Following screening and confirmation of eligibility, patients were enrolled, and var‐cel manufactured as previously described. 14 , 15 Before var‐cel infusion, patients received lymphodepletion chemotherapy with fludarabine 30 mg/m2/day plus cyclophosphamide 300 mg/m2/day on Days −6, −5, and −4; followed by a target dose of 5 × 106 CAR+ cells/kg. Bendamustine lymphodepletion (100 mg/m2/day on Days −5 and −4) was allowed in case of renal failure or intolerance to either fludarabine or cyclophosphamide. All patients received the first fraction (10%) of var‐cel on Day 0, followed by the second (30%) and third (60%) fractions separated at least by 24 h if there were no signs or symptoms of Grade 2 or higher immune‐related toxicity (i.e., CRS or ICANS). Var‐cel expansion and persistence were measured weekly for 6 weeks, every 2 weeks until Month 3, monthly up to the first year, and then quarterly. This was accomplished not only by estimating the time to B‐cell recovery (a minimum of two consecutive determinations showing the presence of B‐cells in peripheral blood) but also by quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) and flow cytometry, as previously described. 7 The presence of human anti‐mouse antibodies (HAMA) was evaluated on Day +14, Day +28, and then every 3 months, as previously described. 7

Statistical analysis

Response rates are presented with 95% exact Clopper–Pearson confidence intervals (CIs). NRM was calculated as a cumulative incidence from infusion, considering disease relapse as a competing event. DOR was calculated, for responding patients only, from Day +100 until disease progression, death, or last follow‐up. B‐cell recovery was computed as a cumulative incidence, considering death or disease relapse without B‐cell recovery as a competing event. PFS, OS, and incidence of B‐cell recovery were calculated from the day of infusion. DOR, PFS, and OS curves were plotted using the Kaplan–Meier method. Statistical analyses were performed using R, version 4.2.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). The CART19‐BE‐01 trial (EUDRA no 2016‐2972‐29) was registered at clinicaltrials.gov (NCT03144583).

RESULTS

Baseline characteristics

Two patients with R/R FL were enrolled in the CART19‐BE‐01 study, and 25 additional patients were enrolled in the CUP. All eventually received at least one dose of var‐cel. Preliminary results of 11 of these patients have already been published. 16 Table 1 displays the main baseline characteristics of this patient population. Median age was 61 years (range, 33–73), with 26% of patients being older than 65 years. Twelve (44%) patients were female. The median number of prior lines of therapy was 3 (range, 2–9), and 56% of patients progressed within 24 months of initiating frontline therapy (POD24). The cutoff date for this analysis was December 2024, when the median follow‐up for survivors was 17.9 months (range, 3.3–60.4) from var‐cel administration and 19.1 months (range, 4.7–61.7) from signature of the informed consent.

Cell manufacturing, bridging, and lymphodepleting chemotherapy

Patients received var‐cel therapy a median of 42 days (range, 28–69) from inclusion and 30 days (range, 21–51) from apheresis. Median cell production time was 8 days (range, 6–10); the target cell dose of 5 × 106 CAR+ cells/kg was accomplished in 24 (89%) patients. The remaining three patients had a maximum yield of 2.5, 3.5, and 4 × 106 CAR+ cells/kg, respectively. In 1 (4%) patient, a second manufacturing procedure was required.

Sixteen (59%) patients required bridging therapy while var‐cel was being manufactured. Drugs and drug combinations most used for bridging therapy were cyclophosphamide (33%) and gemcitabine + oxaliplatin (11%). Conventional fludarabine/cyclophosphamide lymphodepletion was completed in all patients except one, who received bendamustine, as per protocol, due to reduced glomerular filtration rate. Lymphodepletion was administered in the outpatient setting in 25 (93%) patients and during hospital admission in 2 (7%) patients.

Fifteen patients received bendamustine before apheresis, but this happened at least 9 months before apheresis in all patients and had no impact on cell manufacturing.

Var‐cel expansion and persistence: Immunogenicity

Var‐cel in vivo expansion was confirmed by qPCR in all patients and by flow cytometry (a less sensitive technique) in 22 (81%) patients. At the point of maximum expansion (median of 14 days from the first fraction), the median number of transgene copies per cell was 1.74 (range, 0.09–39.1) and the median percentage of CAR+ cells was 4% (range, 0%–33%) of the total number of T‐cells in peripheral blood. In terms of functional persistence of var‐cel, the median cumulative incidence of B‐cell recovery had not been reached and the time of this report, and was estimated as 17% at 3 years.

Before therapy, HAMA were already present in 5 (18.5%) patients, whereas 9 additional patients developed HAMA after var‐cel administration (33%). Of note, there was no correlation between the preexistence or occurrence of HAMA and the duration of B‐cell aplasia and/or patients' outcome.

Toxicity

The NRM in this patient population was 0% at Day +100 and 14% at 3 years, accounting for three patients in total. The first patient deceased of pneumonia after a diagnosis of lung adenocarcinoma 13 months after var‐cel infusion. The second patient developed toxic epidermal necrolysis (TEN), which was diagnosed 2 weeks after var‐cel infusion and was only partially responsive to corticosteroids. Repeated skin biopsies ruled out the presence of CAR T‐cells on the skin or any other causes. The patient eventually deceased of uncontrolled bacteraemia, in the context of active TEN, 5 months after therapy. The third patient deceased of John Cunningham virus‐positive progressive multifocal leukoencephalopathy 8 months after var‐cel infusion. All patients deceased while in response to their FL and with ongoing B‐cell aplasia.

Regarding AEs of special interest, CRS (any grade) occurred in 15/27 patients (55%, 95% CI: 35%–75%), with a median onset time of 1 day (range, 0–1) from infusion, and a median duration of 2 days (range 1–8). There was one case of Grade 3 CRS (4%, 95% CI: 0%–19%), and one case (4%, 95% CI: 0%–19%) of Grade 1 ICANS. As a result of CRS, tocilizumab was administered to seven patients (26%) with Grade 1–3 CRS, three of whom (11%) also received therapy with corticosteroids. Moreover, 3 (11%) patients received only one (1 patient) or two (2 patients) fractions of var‐cel, as per protocol, because of significant CRS (Grade 2 in two patients, Grade 3 in one patient). No cases of macrophage activation syndrome were documented. The occurrence of CRS (all grades) was associated with maximum var‐cel expansion by qPCR, but not with the area under the curve (Figure 1).

Grade ≥3 neutropenia was observed in 25 patients (93%, 95% CI: 76%–99%), with a median duration of 14 (range, 1–730) days. There were 11 cases (41%, 95% CI: 22%–61%) of Grade ≥3 anemia, and there were 12 cases (44%, 95% CI: 25%–65%) of Grade ≥3 thrombocytopenia. Granulocyte‐colony stimulating factor (G‐CSF) and eltrombopag support was needed for the treatment of cytopenia in 63% and 15% of patients, respectively. Grade ≥3 late ICAHT was documented in 9 (33%) of patients.

Efficacy

The prespecified ORR (at Day +100) was 100% (95% CI: 87%–100%), with a CRR of 93% (95% CI: 76%–99%). The median DOR from response assessment had not been reached and was 78% (95% CI: 62%–98%) at 3 years. At 3 years, PFS and OS were estimated as 78% (95% CI: 62%–98%) and 81% (95% CI: 65%–100%), respectively (Figure 2). Two patients experienced disease relapse, and its cumulative incidence was 8% at 3 years. Both relapses were CD19‐positive, one 7 months after B‐cell recovery and one while B‐cell aplasia was still ongoing. DISCUSSION

In this paper, we present the results obtained with var‐cel in patients with R/R FL. All patients were treated, either before commercial CAR T‐cell products became available in Spain or because they were ineligible for them. This was a population of heavily pretreated patients, with a median of three prior lines of therapy, prior autologous HCT in 44%, and prior allogeneic HCT in 11% of them. Moreover, a high proportion (59%) of patients required bridging therapy during cell manufacturing to control rapidly proliferating disease, which is quite remarkable for an indolent histology such as FL. These baseline characteristics are comparable with other trials evaluating the efficacy and safety of CAR T‐cells for the treatment of R/R FL. 1 , 3 , 17 , 18 , 19 , 20

One of the advantages of this program was a relatively short median time from enrollment to infusion (42 days) and time from apheresis to infusion (vein‐to‐vein time, 30 days). Another advantage of our product was its manageable safety profile, with Grade ≥3 CRS and ICANS documented in 4% and 0% of patients, respectively, and a very low admission rate to the intensive care unit during the acute period. These rates are comparable to those obtained with liso‐cel, 5 tisa‐cel, 1 , 18 and axi‐cel 3 in similar patient populations (Table 2), which is notable considering that the median dose administered to our patients was significantly higher (5 × 106 CAR+ cells/kg) in most patients. We believe this is the result of a liberal use of tocilizumab (26% of patients) as currently recommended in most guidelines, 11 , 21 but more importantly, to the fractionated administration of our product, which significantly reduced the incidence of Grade ≥3 CRS from 27% to 4% in patients with R/R ALL. 7 Of note, our anti‐BCMA product (ARI0002h or cesnicabtagene autoleucel) is also administered in fractions, and the incidence of Grade ≥3 CRS/ICANS is equally low. 22 Moreover, a completely unrelated product (obe‐cel) has also demonstrated a significant reduction of CRS/ICANS rates when administered in fractions. 23 , 24

In this trial, and contrast to similar products, we have confirmed the very rare occurrence of ICANS, of any grade, in patients receiving var‐cel. This reduced risk of ICANS was most striking in patients with more aggressive B‐cell malignancies, such as B‐ALL, 25 , 26 , 27 , 28 but it was also remarkable in patients with R/R FL. We have no explanation for this finding, which has been repeatedly confirmed, and appears to be a genuine feature of our construct across all disease types. Due to the reduced incidence of severe CRS/ICANS, persistent cytopenia became one of the most relevant AEs in this study. 12 However, a judicious use of drugs such as G‐CSF and eltrombopag and the existence of a well‐established at‐home hospitalization program at our institution facilitated the management of this complication in the outpatient setting. Var‐cel in vivo persistence was also remarkably long in patients with R/R FL, with a very low cumulative incidence of B‐cell recovery over time (17% at 3 years from infusion). This explains the low relapse rate observed in this trial, but at the same time, it could cooperate with long‐term neutropenia in increasing the infection risk. Indeed, all three non‐relapse deaths observed in the study were caused by infections, even though there were other very relevant intervening events (lung adenocarcinoma and TEN) in two of these patients. As such, a strict protocol of immunoglobulin replacement appears crucial in this clinical context.

The efficacy of var‐cel was remarkably high in this patient population, including a 100% response rate and a 3‐year incidence of relapse of 8%. We are convinced that a prolonged var‐cel persistence was responsible for this remarkably low risk of recurrence. In contrast to other more immature diseases (e.g., B‐ALL or aggressive B‐cell lymphoma), where CD19‐negative relapses are relatively common, this is an exceedingly rare phenomenon in patients with R/R FL. Therefore, a prolonged CAR T‐cell survival may be particularly valuable in this patient population, but this view is not shared by all investigators. Indeed, ZUMA‐5 investigators observed that half of their patients with ongoing response after axi‐cel administration had normal B‐cells in peripheral blood, concluding that functional CAR T‐cell is not required for long‐term response. 3 A similar conclusion was reached by ELARA investigators, who found no clear relationship between prolonged tisa‐cel persistence and long‐term clinical efficacy. 1 , 2 We also investigated the potential deleterious effect of prior bendamustine therapy on var‐cel manufacturing or patient outcome, which has been identified for other products in the same clinical context. 4 , 29 In total, 15 (55%) patients received prior bendamustine, but in all cases, this happened at least 9 months before apheresis, which possibly explains why we did not find any impact on manufacturing, response rate, or DOR.

A further limitation of this study, besides the relatively low number of patients, is the compassionate use administration of var‐cel, outside a formal clinical trial, in most patients. However, the inclusion/exclusion criteria, the follow‐up, the institution, and the professionals looking after the patients were the same, and their inclusion into the program was collectively discussed at multidisciplinary meetings and ratified by the national competent authority and expert committees, thus reducing heterogeneity and subjectivity and reflecting a more pragmatic scenario than some clinical trials. On the other hand, we firmly believe that these alternative regulatory pathways (e.g., compassionate use, hospital exemption) are increasingly important in providing these complex, expensive, and life‐saving products to patients without approved options, both in the EU and elsewhere, 30 , 31 , 32 and should not be overlooked.

In conclusion, we have presented the results obtained with var‐cel in patients with R/R FL, an indication where commercial CAR T‐cell products were not reimbursed in Spain until very recently. Despite the limitations referenced above, we believe that var‐cel holds promise for the treatment of adult patients with R/R FL. Moreover, the toxicity of the product was manageable, both manufacturing and hospital admission times could be shortened, and the product was 3–4 times cheaper than axi‐cel or tisa‐cel, which reduced the burden for patients, families, healthcare providers, and payers.
