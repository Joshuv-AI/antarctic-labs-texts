Investigating the role of transposable elements in shaping abdominal fat and egg production phenotypic traits in geese
Authors: Silu Hu, Yi Luo, Ying Chen, Haiwei Wang, Lihui Wang, Zhuping Chen, Lin Ma, Xianzhi Zhao, Keshan Zhang, Jiajia Xue, Wang Chao, Xianwen Wu, Youhui Xie, Shaoxiang Kuang, Qigui Wang, Guangliang Gao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
While transposable elements (TE) are critical drivers of genomic diversity, their influence on phenotypic traits in geese remain largely unexplored, primarily because most research has focused on single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNP). In this study, we identified 157,044 TE absence polymorphisms (TAP) in the genome of 566 Sichuan White geese through whole-genome resequencing (with an average coverage depth of 12.44 ×) to evaluate their influence across different populations, and we extended our investigation to include a TE genome-wide association study (TE-GWAS) encompassing 48 traits, with a particular focus on abdominal fat weight. Notably, a TE within an intron of the CDCC171 gene was identified and significantly affects abdominal fat deposition, exhibiting minimal Linkage with SNPs within a 100 kb region. Additionally, co-expression analysis of ovarian transcriptome data from four geese populations revealed significant correlations between long terminal repeat (LTR) and terminal inverted repeat (TIR) elements and genes related to egg production. Collectively, these findings highlight the potential of TE as key drivers of phenotypic variation in geese, presenting new opportunities for targeted breeding strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-025-11976-1.

Introduction

Geese are economically important waterfowl widely raised for meat, eggs, feathers, and fatty liver, serving both as a food source and as raw material for down products [1, 2]. Domestication has given rise to two major lineages: Chinese geese, derived from the Swan goose (Anser cygnoides), and European geese, derived from the Greylag goose (Anser anser) [3]. The Greylag and Swan geese share a common ancestor dating back approximately 3.4 million years and retain the ability to interbreed [4]. Evidence of gene flow between domestic and wild populations, as well as between the two lineages, has also been reported [5, 6]. Continued artificial selection has resulted in diverse domestic breeds with distinct phenotypic traits. For example, the Landes goose is widely used for foie gras production due to its strong capacity for hepatic fat deposition [7]. Specifically, the Zi goose lays approximately 90 eggs per year and has a small body size with white feathers, the Lion-headed goose weighs around 13 kg and features brownish feathers, while the Sichuan White goose is distinguished by its pure white plumage [8, 9]. Recent genomic studies have identified several candidate genes associated with important phenotypic traits in geese, including IGF1 (body size) [10], TYR (plumage coloration) [11], GDF9 and TMEM161A (egg production) [12, 13], and SCD (lipid metabolism) [14], which collectively contribute to breed-specific diversity. In parallel, growing evidence indicates that TE play regulatory roles in livestock by modulating gene expression and chromatin accessibility. TE have been implicated in various complex traits, such as litter size in pigs (PDIA, FSHb) [15, 16], skeletal development [17], vertebral number [18]. In sheep, a LINE insertion upstream of BMP2 is associated with fat tail development [19]. In cattle, TE insertions located near APOB, TP53, and IFNAR2 have been linked to cholesterol metabolism, milk persistency, and immune response, respectively [20]. These findings underscore the potential influence of TE on the regulation of economically important traits and highlight their relevance in understanding phenotypic variation in geese.

Advancements in genomics and bioinformatics have continually refined our capacity to domesticate and enhance traits in plants and animals. The substantial phenotypic diversity observed in geese provides fertile ground for more comprehensive studies. Nevertheless, most current population genomics and genome-wide association studies (GWAS) on geese focus predominantly on single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) and insertions or deletions (Indels). For instance, GWAS pinpointed SNPs linked to reproductive traits, egg quality [21], and meat quality [22] in geese. In contrast, research on structural variation (SV)—despite its prevalence through transposable element (TE) mobilization—remains limited [23]. In avian species, TE, although present at lower frequencies than in mammals, are still influential in shaping the genome structure. Specifically, TE insertions are linked to SVs such as large indels, which have been identified in the golden pheasant genome [24]. TE in birds, primarily in the form of long terminal repeats (LTRs), contribute significantly to genomic diversity [24, 25]. TE activity is often seen in regions related to immune response, particularly dot chromosomes, where immune-related genes are amplified [26, 27], TE insertions may play a role in immune gene duplications and other functional adaptations. Despite the lower frequency of TE activity in birds compared to mammals, recent studies have found a notable impact of TE on gene expression regulation and genome evolution.

As fundamental genomic components, TE significantly influence genetic variability through their insertion, excision, and replication activities. Their propensity for replication not only elevates recombination rates but also makes them a potent source of genomic diversity [28]. Some TE insert into genes or promoter regions, potentially affecting gene expression and reorganizing gene expression networks through epigenetic regulation and alterations in transcription factor (TF) binding sites [29, 30]. In birds, TE exhibit distinct characteristics compared to those in mammals. Avian genomes are typically compact, displaying relatively low TE content dominated by ancient, inactive elements. The major classes of TE identified in birds include LINEs—particularly the CR1 family, which constitutes the largest fraction—as well as SINEs and DNA transposons [31, 32]. Unlike mammalian genomes that contain lineage-specific, recently active TE such as primate-specific Alu repeats and hominid-specific SVAs, avian genomes generally lack recent TE expansions. Instead, TE activities in birds predominantly reflect ancient insertion events associated with early avian lineage diversification. Nonetheless, despite their lower abundance and older origin, avian TE have significantly contributed to genome organization and lineage-specific gene regulatory innovations [31]. Therefore, understanding the TE composition and evolutionary dynamics within bird genomes provides valuable insights into avian evolutionary history and species-specific adaptive processes. Recently, research into TE-associated diversity has intensified within crop genomics, highlighting correlations between TE insertions and numerous domestication or agronomic traits. Consequently, TE-focused genome-wide association studies (TE-GWAS) have gained popularity for analyzing plant phenotypes. For example, Vendrell-Mir et al. devised methodologies to analyze TE insertion polymorphisms (TIPs) in resequencing data, applying LTR-TIP-GWAS to investigate long terminal repeat (LTR) retrotransposon TIPs in rice [33]. Similarly, Domínguez et al. conducted comprehensive SNP and TIP GWAS analyses on 17 agronomic traits and 1,012 metabolic traits in tomatoes, alongside studies on the influence of TE on the structural evolution of rice genomes [34]. These results underscore the crucial role of TE in genetic variation. However, analogous GWAS investigations into the role of TE within the domestic goose genome remain scarce.

In the present study, we employed resequencing methods of 566 geese to elucidate population structure and investigate the influence of TE polymorphisms on the domestication pathways of distinct goose breeds from the previous study [35]. Our results revealed pronounced divergences in TE population structures between Chinese and European geese, highlighting the genetic differentiation driven by domestication. A GWAS of 48 phenotypic traits identified a TE within the intron of the CCDC171 gene, which was significantly associated with abdominal fat weight. Concurrently, a co-expression analysis of 13 ovarian transcriptome datasets uncovered extensive interactions between TE and egg production-related genes, implicating regulatory networks involving calcium signaling, cortisol secretion, gland development, and systemic physiological regulation. Taken together, these findings of our research enhance knowledge of the molecular mechanisms underlying genetic diversity and phenotypic variation in geese, laying the groundwork for future advancements in genetic breeding.

Material and method

Genomic sequencing and phenotypic datasets

We collected 566 whole-genome sequencing (WGS) data from NCBI for eight distinct goose breeds, including 423 Sichuan white geese, 20 Magang geese, 24 Zi geese, 15 Xupu geese, 20 Landes geese, 20 Lion head geese, 24 Huoyan geese, and 20 Taihu geese [35] (Table S1). To complement this genomic data, we assessed abdominal fat phenotypes in a cohort of 191 Sichuan white geese [22] (Table S2). Furthermore, ovarian transcriptome datasets were procured for co-expression analyses between TE and egg production-related genes across several populations, including Huoyan, Xupu, Zi, Landes, and Sichuan white geese (Table S3).

Analysis of TE in goose genome

We identified TE in the Tianfu goose genome [36] using the Extential de novo TE Annotator (EDTA, v1.9.6) package [37]. To assess the mappability of short reads across the 39 chromosomes of the reference goose genome, we utilized the GEM-library [38]. Custom scripts quantified the distribution of genes and different TE categories on each chromosome, and we visualized these distributions through Circos plots generated using Tbtools [39]. The genomic locations of eight TE categories were analyzed with Bedtools, categorizing TE by their presence in intergenic regions, intragenic regions, or within 1,000 bp upstream or downstream of genes [40]. We then identified genes with TE insertions either within intragenic regions or within the 1,000-bp flanking regions upstream, which were subsequently subjected to enrichment analysis via the R package clusterProfiler. Enrichment significance was defined at p < 0.05 [41].

Analysis of TE absence polymorphisms (TAP)

TAP were identified using 566 high-depth sequencing datasets [42]. Raw whole-genome sequencing data were first converted from SRA to FASTQ format using the fastq-dump tool from the SRA-Toolkit, followed by quality control with Fastp (v.0.20.1) with default parameters [43]. The quality-controlled reads were then aligned to the Tianfu goose reference genome using BWA software (https://github.com/lh3/bwa), with subsequent sorting and indexing of the resulting BAM files performed by Samtools (https://github.com/samtools/samtools) software [44]. TE genotyping was executed with detettore, applying the parameters -m tips taps –require_split –include_invariant. After merging the TAP data across all samples, TAP were filtered using vcftools (https://github.com/samtools/bcftools), with a missing rate of below 0.3 [45].

TE-based population structure and differential TE analysis

Principal component analysis (PCA) of the filtered TE dataset was conducted using GCTA (v1.91.4beta3) [46]. A neighbor-joining tree, constructed with 100 bootstraps in PHYLIP (v3.696) and visualized via the iTOL platform (https://itol.embl.de/) [47]. Population structure was examined using ADMIXTURE (v1.3.0), with cluster numbers (K) ranging from 2 to 9, each replicated 10 times, and the results were visualized using ggplot2 [48]. TE gene flow among populations was analyzed with Treemix, applying default parameters. Fixation index (FST) values were calculated using VCFtools (v0.1.13) [45, 48]. Given the substantial differences in egg-laying capacity across the eight populations, samples were categorized into high fertility (Huoyan and Zi geese), moderate fertility (Sichuan White geese), and low fertility (Xupu geese) groups. TE with genotypes “0/0” and “0/1” were classified as present, while those with genotypes “1/1” and “./.” were classified as absent. TE frequency was calculated for each group, and Fisher’s exact test, adjusted by the Benjamini–Hochberg method, was used to assess the significance of differences between groups. TE with an FDR < 0.001 and a |log2(fold change)|> 1 were considered significantly different between groups.

TAP-GWAS analysis

We excluded outlier phenotypic data that deviated from a normal distribution and performed an association analysis between the TAP and phenotypes using the linear mixed model (LMM) in EMMAX (version beta-07Mar2010) [49], incorporating the kinship matrix (emmax-kin -v -d 10) as a random effect to account for population structure and minimize false positives, with the Q matrix from ADMIXTURE included as a covariate. The significance threshold for TAP-GWAS was adjusted using an independent Benjamini–Hochberg correction. The results were visualized using the R package CMplot, and the r2 of SNPs located within 50 kb upstream and downstream of the TAP was calculated using PLINK [50].

TE related genes co-expression analysis

The raw ovarian data, following rigorous quality control as previously described, was subjected to transposable element (TE) quantification using TEspeX (https://github.com/fansalon/TEspeX), where read counts were normalized to fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (FPKM). Gene expression was assessed by aligning the reads to the Tianfu goose reference genome via STAR [51], followed by gene quantification through featureCounts [52], and subsequent normalization of TE read counts with FPKM. The Pearson correlation coefficient (PCC) was calculated to examine the relationship between the expression levels of genes associated with egg production and TE, with a particular emphasis on the top 20% variance across all samples. Co-expression was identified for genes and TE with a PCC exceeding 0.9 and a Q-value below 0.005, and the resulting co-expression network was visualized using Cytoscape [53]. Gene functional enrichment analysis was conducted using the Metascape platform to explore the biological characteristics [54].

Result

Genome-wide distribution and function of TE

In this study, a total of 223,398 TE were annotated, accounting for 8% of the goose genome sequence length. Those TE were classified into three major types: DNA transposons, long terminal repeats (LTR) and miniature inverted-repeat transposable elements (MITE). Of which, DNA transposons were more prevalent than LTRs and MITE across all chromosomes, comprising 56.79% of the total TE (Fig. 1A). Notably, the eight subtypes of transposons with the highest abundance in the genome included Gypsy (LTR) and retrotransposon (LTRs) as well as CACTA, hAT, Mutator, PIF, helitron and Tc1 Mariner of DNA type. A majority of these TE (68.96%) were located in intergenic regions, which does not preclude their transcription; they are likely situated in gene regulatory regions. The potential function of a TE is determined by its proximity and relationship to genes (e.g., acting as cis or trans-regulatory elements or forming new exon structures). Our analysis also revealed that different types of TE exhibit a preference for functional regions in the goose genome. For instance, hAT transposons are less frequent within 1000 bp upstream of genes but are more commonly found within genes themselves (Fig. 1B). TAP were conservative across varies goose breeds

Through comprehensive analysis of whole-genome sequencing data from 593 geese across eight breeds, we identified 163,028 TAP. After filtering to exclude TAP with a missing rate exceeding 0.7, a refined dataset of 157,044 TAP was retained, representing approximately 96.3% of the original TAP. Phylogenetic analysis based on these TAP revealed distinct genetic clustering, with European geese forming a separate clade, while the seven Chinese breeds exhibited closer genetic affinities, as illustrated by both the neighbor-joining tree and PCA results (Fig. 2A, B). Population structure analysis at K ≥ 4 clearly distinguished European from Chinese geese, consistent with the phylogenetic findings. At K = 6, we observed shared population structures among specific Chinese breeds, such as Magang, Lion-head, and Taihu geese, as well as between Zi and Xupu geese (Fig. 2C). Notably, the Sichuan White goose genome displayed a significantly higher TAP content than other breeds, whereas Magang geese exhibited the lowest TAP abundance (Fig. 2D), suggesting that TAP may be influenced by selective breeding pressures. Gene flow analysis further indicated an absence of genetic exchange between Chinese and European geese (Fig. 2E). However, gene flow was detected within Chinese breeds, with notable exchanges observed between Huoyan and Taihu geese and among Sichuan White, Magang, and Lion-head geese (Fig. 2F). TAP within these populations exhibits considerable diversity, with distinct functional characteristics among TAP families. To further investigate the population-level dynamics, we analyzed the frequency distribution of TAP across various TAP families, finding a concentration in the 0.75 to 1 frequency range. Relationship between TAP and trait variation in geese

As regulatory elements, TAP can provide insights into more trait-related variations in geese through phenotypic association analysis. Based on the filtered 157,044 TAP, a GWAS analysis was conducted using EMMAX software across 48 traits, including the abdominal fat weight. We identified three TAP associated with three traits (weight of abdominal fat, Carcass Diving Length, and Carcass Neck Length). The TAP associated with Carcass Diving Length and Carcass Neck Length were DTC transposons located in intergenic regions. The TAP associated with the weight of abdominal fat was a DNA transposon, CCDC171_helitron (chr12:8,631,690), inserted within an intron of the CCDC171 gene (coiled-coil domain-containing protein 171), with a length of 85b p. (Fig. 3A, B). Additionally, to determine if the insertion or deletion of TAP is a driving variant for phenotypic variation, we performed linkage disequilibrium (LD) analysis between significant TAP and SNPs within 100 kb (50 kb upstream and downstream). A total of 1,178 SNPs were found within 100 kb of CCDC171_helitron, with an average LD value of 0.003, and there were no SNPs showed high linkage with the TAP (r2 > 0.2, Fig. 3D). This indicates that TAP exhibit lower observed LD compared to SNPs, consistent with previous findings in Arabidopsis [55], maize, and humans [56], suggesting that TAP may have additional heritability compared to SNPs.

Impact of CCDC171 on weight of abdominal fat

To further elucidate the effect of the CCDC171_helitron genotype on fat accumulation in geese, we used Candihap software to study the genotype–phenotype relationship of CCDC171_helitron. Among the 180 re-sequenced samples, 95 samples had a homozygous TAP genotype (0/0), 80 samples had a heterozygous genotype (0/1 or 1/0), and 5 samples had a TE deletion (1/1) (Fig. 4). The association results between genotype and phenotype indicated that fat content was significantly higher when CCDC171_helitron was completely absent compared to the other two groups. No significant difference in fat content was observed between the heterozygous and homozygous TAP groups, although the heterozygous group had slightly higher fat content. Co-expression of TE and goose egg production-related genes

The egg production is a key economic trait in geese, which is a trait with low heritability. The transcriptomic analysis elucidates gene expression patterns, structural characteristics, and related regulatory networks by analyzing the expression profiles of organs such as the ovary, thereby identifying candidate genes associated with goose egg production traits. This study collected 136 reported genes related to goose egg production traits (Table S4) were collected as well as ovarian transcriptomic data from NCBI for Huoyan goose (high fertility), Xupu goose (low fertility), Landes goose, Zi goose (high fertility), and Sichuan white goose (moderate fertility). Our analysis revealed significant co-expression of genes in ovarian tissue enriched in key biological pathways, including the Calcium signaling pathway, regulation of system processes, Cortisol synthesis and secretion, gland development, cellular response to organic cyclic compounds, and regulation of secretion (Table S5). These pathways are particularly relevant to reproductive processes, hormone secretion, and ovarian function.

A total of 118,202 TE was identified in the transcriptomes. Consistent with gene expression pattern, the expression of some TE is also specific (Fig. 5A, B), suggesting that the expression of TE, like protein-coding genes (PCGs), is involved in the regulation of reproductive ability. Restricting to the top 20% most variable TEs (n = 23,640) and using PCC > 0.9 with Q < 0.005, 25 PCGs were found to co-express with 468 TE (Table S6). There may be differences in TE frequencies among goose populations with different reproductive abilities (Xupu goose vs Huoyan goose, Sichuan white goose vs Huoyan goose, Zi goose vs Xupu goose, Zi goose vs Sichuan white goose, Xupu goose vs Sichuan white goose). Statistical analysis revealed 45 high-frequency TE and 338 low-frequency TE among these five comparisons. Integrating the above co-expression networks, we found five genes co-expressing with these TE: ZP3, THBS2S, PER2, GRIK3, and INHA (Fig. 5C). Discussion

TE are sequences in the genome with capable of movement, inducing gene sequence mutations or altering gene regulation by direct insertion or generating duplicate sequences through RNA reverse transcription [57–59]. Therefore, Identifying insertion and deletion polymorphisms in TE across populations is essential to understanding their distribution and regulatory roles. In Our study, a population of 593 samples across 8 breeds, 157,044 TAP were identified, providing abundant genomic markers for this research purpose. Population analysis based on these TAP revealed significant structural differences between European and Chinese geese populations, consistent with previous findings. This is primarily due to European geese being domesticated from the Greylag goose, while Chinese geese were commonly domesticated from the Swan goose [35]. Additionally, we observed TE exchanges among different Chinese goose breeds (Sichuan white, Magang, Zi, Xupu, Lion head, Huoyan and geese), suggesting extensive hybridization during domestication, which aligns with other studies [6, 35]. These results further increase the complexity of TE in population genomes, impacting gene expression and phenotypic regulation. Such complexity underscores the importance of considering TE in the study of genetic diversity and functional genomics in geese.

TE are widely distributed in the genome, and their frequent insertions and deletions create rich genomic structural variations (SVs) [60–62]. SVs also have genetic effects on phenotypic variation. Unlike other waterfowl, geese have a remarkable ability to store fat. In this study, we conducted TE-GWAS analysis on several fat-related phenotypes in geese. The significant TAP identified generally had low LD with surrounding SNPs, suggesting that they might contribute to phenotypic variation differently from SNPs. This has also been confirmed in TE studies of plants such as tomatoes [60] and rice [63, 64]. Our study identified a TE that might affect the weight of abdominal fat. This TE is located within an intron of the CCDC171. Proteins containing the coiled-coil domain (CCDC) are highly conserved and are primarily secreted by the vascular system and adipose tissue. They have been found to be associated with several crucial functions in humans, including gametogenesis, embryonic development, hematopoiesis, angiogenesis, and cilia development [65]. CCDC3, a member of the CCDC family, has been shown to be positively correlated with obesity in 3T3-L1 cells [66]. Mice lacking CCDC proteins exhibit leaner phenotypes and may experience either alleviation or exacerbation of hepatic steatosis in older mice, potentially affecting the expression of adipogenesis genes [66, 67]. We hypothesize that CCDC171, belonging to the same family as CCDC3, may play a similar role in geese, influencing fat formation. We found that the complete absence of CCDC171_helitron significantly increased the weight of abdominal fat. Although the increase was not significant with the presence of heterozygous TE, there was still a trend towards increased abdominal fat weight. In quantitative genetics, the widespread lack of heritability in phenotypes requires researchers to have a deeper understanding of different types of genomic variations and their potential impacts on phenotypes. The study of TE insertions and deletions undoubtedly facilitates the discovery of more functional polymorphic sites in geese.

Egg production, another vital trait in geese, was conducted by co-expression analysis between TE and egg production-related genes using ovarian transcriptome data from 13 different goose breeds. As we know, LTR retrotransposons are directly Linked to the 5'ends of genes, providing enhancer and promoter sequences to regulate tissue- and developmental stage-specific mRNA synthesis of the cis-linked genes [68]. The extensive co-expression of LTRs and TIRs with egg production-related genes offers valuable resources. Additionally, these TE exhibit frequency differences between high and low-fertility breeds at the population level, offering clues for identifying key TAP markers. In total, 157,044 TAP were identified, most of which are LTR retrotransposon sequences and DNA transposons. However, we did not consider shorter TE sequences such as MITE and short interspersed nuclear elements (SINEs), which may also contribute to phenotypic variation [69]. These shorter elements are typically hard to identify with short-read sequencing due to their short length and high copy number. Future studies using long-read sequencing technologies will provide a better assessment of TE insertion polymorphisms.

Our analysis underscores the significant regulatory roles TE play in modulating gene expression variation, aligning closely with recent findings from comprehensive human genomic studies. In humans, systematic investigations have demonstrated that TE substantially impact gene expression and alternative splicing patterns through modifications to regulatory regions, reshaping transcription factor binding profiles, and altering chromatin states [70–72]. Notably, these studies have identified diverse TE families, such as Alu and L1 elements, as active contributors to transcriptomic diversity and complexity of regulatory networks. In contrast, avian genomes typically exhibit lower TE abundance and fewer recent lineage-specific expansions. Nevertheless, our findings in domestic geese similarly indicate that TE act as potent cis-regulatory elements influencing gene expression. Comparative analyses between human and avian genomes therefore provide valuable insights into both evolutionary conservation and divergence in TE-mediated regulatory mechanisms across vertebrates, further highlighting their evolutionary significance in shaping complex phenotypic traits. Although TE insertions are well-established contributors to various cancers and neurological disorders in humans, mice, and dogs—recently exemplified by associations with Parkinson’s disease [73]—analogous evidence linking TE directly to disease pathogenesis in birds remains limited. The comparatively reduced abundance and lower recent activity of TE in bird genomes may partially explain the scarcity of observed TE-driven oncogenesis or other pathologies in avian species. However, given the broadly conserved regulatory capacities of TE among vertebrates, future research should investigate whether specific TE insertions in birds might influence health outcomes or disease susceptibility, analogous to established mammalian paradigms.

Previous studies have shown that CCDC171 may interact with cholesterol intake in humans [74], while another secreted protein from the same family, CCDC3, plays a significant role in lipid metabolism [67]. Interestingly, our findings suggest that the genotype at the TAP locus may influence abdominal fat weight in geese, with samples lacking the CCDC171_helitron exhibiting lower fat weight compared to those possessing the transposable element (TE). The insertion or deletion of the CCDC171 helitron may regulate lipid metabolism in geese, and further investigation of this locus could provide valuable insights into the molecular mechanisms that govern lipid metabolism, particularly in relation to the action of TE. While our study highlights the potential role of the TAP locus and CCDC171 in modulating abdominal fat weight in geese, several limitations should be acknowledged, including a moderate sample size and the lack of validation across diverse goose populations. Moreover, biological interpretation should take into account species-specific physiological differences: in avian species, adipose tissue primarily functions as an energy reservoir, whereas in mammals it also plays broader roles in systemic metabolic regulation. Future research should focus on improving genomic data resolution through advanced sequencing technologies and incorporating a wider range of genetic backgrounds from different goose breeds, thereby strengthening the generalizability of the findings and advancing our understanding of lipid metabolism and related traits.

Among the key regulatory pathways, AKT has been shown to regulate the expression of metabolic enzymes through downstream transcription factors, including Forkhead box protein O1 (FOXO1) and Sterol regulatory element binding protein 1c (SREBP1c). SREBP1c, a major insulin-responsive isoform in the liver, induces lipogenic gene expression and promotes fatty acid synthesis [75]. Additionally, mTOR, a critical nutrient-sensing pathway in various cell types, including hepatocytes, has been identified as an important regulator of hepatic lipid homeostasis, with mTORC1 playing a central role in this process [76]. Moreover, KEGG pathways such as"Oocyte meiosis"and"Phosphatidylinositol signaling system"offer further insights into the regulatory effects of TE on signaling pathways. For instance, high-fat levels may influence oocyte meiosis [77]. In summary, our study highlights the potential role of TE, particularly the CCDC171 helitron, in regulating lipid metabolism in geese. By exploring the molecular pathways and associated genes, we can gain a deeper understanding of how TE contribute to phenotypic variation and metabolic traits in geese.

TE are mobile genetic elements that can mobile genetic units within the genome, potentially causing significant changes in genomic architecture while TE contribute to genetic diversity and can play a positive role in phenotypic variation, they also have potential negative effects that are crucial to consider [78, 79]. One major concern is genomic instability. TE can induce chromosomal rearrangements, including insertions, deletions, duplications, and inversions, and the rearrangements can disrupt normal genomic structure and function, potentially leading to chromosomal instability, such instability may result in loss of heterozygosity, aneuploidy, or structural variations that affect cellular function [80]. Further researches are needed to elucidate the functional consequences of these genetic variations and to explore their application in breeding strategies for improving lipid metabolism and other key traits in poultry.

In our study, we observed significant co-expression of genes in ovarian tissue enriched in the Calcium signaling pathway, which plays a critical role in cellular processes such as muscle contraction, neurotransmitter release, and gene expression, and is essential in ovarian cells for processes like oocyte maturation and follicular development, with the genes within this pathway likely coordinating cellular responses to hormonal signals to ensure optimal conditions for reproduction [81, 82]. Additionally, the Cortisol synthesis and secretion pathway is crucial for regulating stress responses and metabolic processes, and through its regulation of the HPA axis [83], cortisol influences reproductive hormones and ovarian steroidogenesis, thereby highlighting the interconnection between stress and fertility, with the co-expression of genes involved in cortisol synthesis suggesting their role in fine-tuning ovarian function under varying physiological states such as stress or reproductive cycles [84]. Overall, the co-expression of genes in these enriched pathways in ovarian tissue provides valuable insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying reproduction, hormone secretion, and reproduction metabolism.

Conclusion

In summary, this study employed population-level TAP to reveal TE insertion patterns across distinct goose populations. Notably, there are significant differences in TE population structures were revealed between European and Chinese geese, with extensive gene flow observed among Chinese goose breeds. GWAS identified a TE variation within an intron of the CDCC171 gene as a potential key factor influencing the weight of abdominal fat in geese. These findings enhance our understanding of the genomic characteristics within goose populations and provide valuable genomic polymorphism markers for breeding improvement.
