Introduction

Cholecystectomy is among the most common abdominal surgical procedures performed worldwide, with laparoscopic technique being the standard of care [1]. Despite its relatively safe nature, postoperative complications can occur, ranging from immediate surgical issues to delayed functional disorders such as diarrhea [2].

Bile acid diarrhea (BAD) is a recognized complication following cholecystectomy, most often seen in adults but rarely observed in pediatric patients [3]. It is defined as chronic watery diarrhea resulting from excessive bile acids entering the colon due to defective ileal reabsorption or impaired hepatic feedback regulation [3]. BAD affects up to 25–50 % of adults with chronic diarrhea and may account for a subset of patients misclassified under irritable bowel syndrome with diarrhea (IBS-D) [4].

Three types are recognized: Type 1 (secondary to ileal disease or resection), Type 2 (idiopathic), and Type 3 (secondary to other conditions such as post-cholecystectomy, celiac disease, or pancreatitis) [4,5]. Post cholecystectomy diarrhea being a common finding is classified as a Type 3 bile acid malabsorption [5]. The incidence of diarrhea post-cholecystectomy is high, with studies revealing an alarming incidence of 57.2 % [3]. However, many of these cases remain underdiagnosed due to delayed investigation or misattribution to other causes [6].

The pathophysiology of BAD involves the disruption of the normal enterohepatic circulation of bile acids. The absence of the gallbladder alters the kinetics of bile acid delivery to the intestines, whereby continuous trickling of bile into the intestine overwhelms ileal absorptive capacity, leading to spill over into the colon and secretory diarrhea [3,7]. The FXR-FGF19/FGFR4-CYP7A1 axis, responsible for feedback inhibition of hepatic bile acid synthesis [8,9], may also be disrupted postoperatively, compounding the bile acid load.

Despite advances in understanding, diagnosis remains difficult due to limited access to definitive testing like SeHCAT (75selenium-homotaurocholic acid test) [9,10], especially in resource-limited settings. Alternative markers such as serum C4 or FGF19 are not widely validated in children [11]. Presently, therapeutic trials with bile acid sequestrants are most commonly used for diagnosis and management [4,7,12].

Cholelithiasis is one of the common complications in sickle cell disease patients, as these patients have a higher incidence of gallstones due to increased hemolysis and bilirubin production [13], making cholecystectomy one of the most common abdominal surgical interventions in this population. Patients with sickle cell anemia also represent a particularly vulnerable population for post operative complications such as increased risk for infections, poor wound healing, and complications related to underlying organ dysfunction. These patients have increased postoperative morbidity rates reported between 12 and 34 % [14].

This case report presents a rare occurrence of bile acid diarrhea following laparoscopic cholecystectomy in an adolescent patient with sickle cell disease. The condition was effectively managed with bile acid sequestrants, highlighting the importance of clinical suspicion for this underdiagnosed entity in the post-cholecystectomy pediatric population, particularly in those with underlying hematological disorders. This paper has been reported in line with the revised SCARE 2025 criteria [15].

Case presentation

A 14-year-old female with known sickle cell disease (HbSS genotype), maintained on hydroxyurea and daily folic acid, had a history of recurrent admissions for vaso-occlusive crises and episodic right upper quadrant pain. She was diagnosed with symptomatic cholelithiasis and subsequently underwent an elective laparoscopic cholecystectomy. The procedure was uneventful, and she was discharged on postoperative day 3 in stable condition.

Her one-week postoperative follow-up was unremarkable, and she had resumed a regular oral diet. However, on postoperative day 11, she developed the sudden onset of profuse, watery diarrhea, averaging 10 bowel movements per day. This was accompanied by malaise and mild, diffuse abdominal discomfort. She denied fever, hematochezia, bone pain, or any dietary triggers. There was no similar history among family members or community contacts.

She was readmitted with a working diagnosis of acute gastroenteritis. Laboratory workouts which were performed including complete blood counts, inflammatory markers, renal and liver function tests were all within normal limits. Stool microscopy and culture revealed no evidence of infection (table of results). Given the onset of symptoms in the second postoperative week and the absence of infectious or inflammatory findings, a presumptive diagnosis of post-cholecystectomy bile acid diarrhea (PCBAD) was made.

In the absence of SeHCAT testing or serum C4 levels, a therapeutic trial was initiated with cholestyramine (a bile acid sequestrant), oral loperamide, and fluid therapy with oral rehydration salts and IV maintenance fluids. Within 48 h, there was marked clinical improvement with resolution of diarrhea. She was discharged after three days, symptom-free. Upon one week follow up the patient showed great clinical improvement of symptoms, and family was counselled further with more emphasis of early recognition of episodes and adequate hydration.

At three-month follow-up, the patient remained asymptomatic with no recurrence of diarrhea. Growth parameters were appropriate for age, and she had resumed full school attendance. Cholestyramine was discontinued after four weeks without relapse.

Discussion

This case demonstrates the diagnostic challenge of post-cholecystectomy bile acid diarrhea (BAD) in a pediatric patient, a condition that is well established in adults but remains underreported in children. Our patient, a 14-year-old with sickle cell disease, developed persistent watery diarrhea on postoperative day 11 after laparoscopic cholecystectomy. Infection, inflammation, and metabolic causes were excluded by normal laboratory and stool investigations. The subsequent rapid resolution with cholestyramine strongly supported a diagnosis of BAD. While some diarrheal illnesses improve with supportive care alone, the temporal relationship to surgery, absence of alternative explanations, and sustained response following discontinuation of therapy argue against coincidence and reinforce the clinical diagnosis [16].

The differential diagnosis of postoperative diarrhea is broad and includes acute gastroenteritis, antibiotic-associated colitis, inflammatory bowel disease, and functional disorders such as irritable bowel syndrome. In our patient, normal inflammatory markers and negative stool studies excluded infectious or inflammatory etiologies. The clinical timing and stool characteristics, combined with therapeutic response, made BAD the most likely explanation. This approach is consistent with international guidelines, which endorse an empirical trial of bile acid sequestrants in patients with high clinical suspicion when diagnostic modalities are not available.

Although BAD is increasingly recognized in adults, pediatric data remain scarce. A multicenter audit reported chronic diarrhea in 9 % of adults within two years of cholecystectomy, with two-thirds demonstrating bile acid malabsorption [6]. A systematic review pooling 25 studies estimated the prevalence of confirmed post-cholecystectomy BAD at approximately 6 % [7]. However, only 1–2 % of patients are formally diagnosed, reflecting significant under-recognition [6]. The apparent rarity in children may reflect the lower frequency of cholecystectomy in this group rather than true physiological differences. Current evidence suggests that bile acid synthesis and feedback mechanisms are similar in adolescents and adults, implying that the condition is likely underdiagnosed rather than absent in younger populations [13].

Ideally, confirmation would be obtained with the ^75SeHCAT retention scan or biomarkers such as serum 7-α-hydroxy-4-cholesten-3-one, fibroblast growth factor 19 (FGF19), or fecal bile acid quantification [17]. In our setting, these tests were unavailable, which is the reality for many low- and middle-income countries. In such contexts, a pragmatic diagnostic pathway systematic exclusion of other causes followed by an empirical cholestyramine trial remains the only feasible approach [12]. This strategy simultaneously diagnoses and treats, avoiding unnecessary delays and invasive investigations.

The rapid improvement in our patient mirrors adult data, where 70–80 % respond to bile acid sequestrants [18]. Colesevelam is often preferred for its palatability in high-income countries, but cost limits its use in resource-constrained environments [19]. Cholestyramine remains a safe, effective, and affordable alternative. In this case, symptoms resolved within 48 h and therapy was discontinued after four weeks without relapse, suggesting re-equilibration of bile acid synthesis. Follow-up at three months confirmed ongoing symptom resolution, normal growth, and full school attendance. These findings are reassuring but highlight the importance of monitoring pediatric patients for potential long-term nutritional or developmental effects of chronic BAD, an area that remains poorly studied.

Sickle cell disease itself is not directly associated with BAD. Its relevance lies in the high prevalence of pigment gallstones due to chronic hemolysis, which necessitates early cholecystectomy [13]. This earlier surgical exposure may increase the likelihood of functional complications such as BAD in this population. Our case therefore broadens the limited pediatric literature and emphasizes the need for heightened awareness in children with hemolytic disorders undergoing cholecystectomy.

Finally, while continuous bile secretion after gallbladder removal does not invariably cause diarrhea, only a subset of patients develop clinically significant BAD. This is explained by the combination of ileal transport saturation and disruption of the FXR–FGF19 feedback pathway, which expands the bile acid pool and drives colonic secretion [8,9]. These mechanisms account for the variability in clinical expression and the rapid therapeutic response observed with sequestrants in our case.

In summary, this report highlights several practice lessons. First, BAD should be considered in children who develop persistent watery diarrhea after cholecystectomy, particularly in those with hemolytic disorders at risk for early surgery. Second, in resource-limited settings, an empirical trial with bile acid sequestrants is a practical diagnostic and therapeutic strategy when advanced testing is unavailable. Third, timely recognition and treatment prevent dehydration, growth faltering, and unnecessary investigations. Broader reporting and prospective studies are needed to clarify the prevalence, natural history, and long-term impact of BAD in pediatric populations.

Conclusion

Post-cholecystectomy bile acid diarrhea, though uncommon in children, should be considered when persistent watery diarrhea occurs after surgery. In this case, clinical suspicion and rapid response to cholestyramine allowed diagnosis and successful management without extensive investigations. Wider awareness of this underrecognized entity is essential, and pragmatic diagnostic strategies are especially important in resource-limited settings.
