Real-World Onset of Atopic Comorbidities Relative to Atopic Dermatitis in Pediatric Patients
Authors: Yael A Leshem, Clara Weil, William W Busse, Lisa A Beck, Gabriel Chodick, Sonya L Cyr, Kwinten Bosman, Robert Lubwama
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPatients with atopic dermatitis (AD) have a high atopic comorbidity burden. Although traditionally seen as beginning with AD and progressing to other atopic comorbidities, the “atopic march” model does not fit all patients. This manuscript describes the onset of atopic comorbidities among children newly diagnosed with AD.MethodsThis retrospective, observational, cohort study used data from the Israeli Maccabi Healthcare Services. Patients (< 18 years) first diagnosed with AD during 2000–2019 with ≥ 12-month enrollment before and after AD diagnosis were included. Outcomes included cumulative asthma, allergic rhinitis (AR), and food allergy (FA) prevalence. Introduction

Atopic dermatitis (AD) is a common pruritic skin condition, resulting from a complex interaction of genetic, environmental, and immunologic factors [1, 2]. Patients with AD are at an increased risk of other type 2–mediated diseases, including asthma [3], allergic rhinitis (AR) [4], and food allergy (FA) [5]. Classically, the “atopic march” is thought to begin with AD and progresses to immunoglobulin E (IgE)–mediated FA, asthma, and AR [6–8]. However, this model of sequential progression from AD to other atopic diseases may be oversimplified; there may be multiple atopic trajectories [9, 10] or simply a clustering of these disorders [11, 12]. Current real-world evidence regarding comorbidity burden among children with AD is limited, particularly regarding trends over time in the onset of type 2 conditions following AD diagnosis.

The prevalence of AD in Israel in 2017 was 11.0% among children aged 0.5–11 years and 5.8% among those aged 12–17 years [13]. Most pediatric patients had mild disease, assessed by dispensed AD-related treatments. Specifically, mild, moderate, and severe disease accounted for 65.9, 29.6, and 4.4% of children aged 6 months to < 12 years and for 59.7, 32.7, and 3.1% of adolescents 12–17 years, respectively.

Here, we describe the onset of type 2–associated conditions included in the classical “atopic march” among pediatric patients newly diagnosed with AD in a large healthcare provider database in Israel.

Methods

Study Design and Data Source

This retrospective, observational, cohort study used data from the Israeli Maccabi Healthcare Services (MHS) database, which captures all information on patient interactions within the healthcare system, including demographics, inpatient/outpatient visits, diagnoses, procedures, prescriptions, and dispensed medications. It contains data of > 2.6 million members and represents approximately 25% of the Israeli population.

The MHS institutional review board approved the study, which was conducted in accordance with the Good Clinical Practice guidelines of the International Council for Harmonization of Technical Requirements for Registration of Pharmaceuticals for Human Use and with the Declaration of Helsinki, Ethical Principles for Medical Research Involving Human Subjects. The MHS institutional review board granted a consent waiver owing to the study’s retrospective nature.

Inclusion Criteria

Patients aged < 18 years at index (i.e., the earliest AD diagnosis during 2000–2019) with an AD diagnosis during the study period (1998–2020) defined as any of the following were included: ≥ 2 records of an AD International Classification of Diseases, 9th revision (ICD-9) diagnosis code (691.8) from a general physician or pediatrician; or ≥ 1 record of an AD ICD-9 diagnosis code from a dermatologist or allergist; or ≥ 1 AD diagnosis from an MHS medication approval center or hospital. To exclude prevalent AD cases and to allow for baseline data collection, patients must have had enrollment for ≥ 12 months before and after index date.

Baseline Demographics

Baseline demographics were assessed on the index date and included age, sex, region of residence (north/central/south regions of Israel), and socioeconomic status (Supplementary Text 1).

Outcomes

Diagnosis data were obtained during 1998–2020 to describe cumulative asthma, AR, and FA prevalence, on the basis of ≥ 1 ICD-9 code during the study period (Table S1). These conditions were assessed before, at, and during the years after index, through 31 December 2020.

Statistical Analyses

Descriptive statistics are provided, with categorical variables summarized using patient counts and percentages and continuous variables using medians and quartiles 1 and 3. All AD- and type 2-associated diagnoses were taken into consideration—including same day diagnosis and those occurring before the index AD diagnosis. Missing data were reported, without imputation.

Time-to-event analyses were performed using Kaplan–Meier analysis to estimate cumulative incidence (i.e., onset of a given atopic condition) over time starting from 12 months preindex date, censoring patients at the first date of leaving MHS, death, or end of follow-up (31 December 2020). Cumulative events (%) are reported at different time points of interest. These estimates were combined with the observed prevalence rates at index date to estimate cumulative prevalence over time. All analyses were stratified by age at index (< 3, 3–5, 6–11, and 12–17 years).

At AD diagnosis (time = 0; Fig. 1), asthma prevalence was much lower among children aged < 3 versus ≥ 3 years (10.6% versus similar rates of 26.3–28.5%). AR prevalence at AD diagnosis increased steadily with age (from 2.2% to 23.2%), while FA prevalence decreased with age (from 4.9% to 2.1%) (Fig. 2). Cumulative asthma and FA prevalence increased sharply in the year after AD diagnosis among children aged < 3 years (asthma: from 10.6% at AD diagnosis to 19.1% at 1 year; FA: from 4.9% at AD diagnosis to 8.9% at 1 year), while only minimal increases were observed in older age groups (Fig. 1a, c). AR prevalence increased steadily and similarly in all age groups (Fig. 1b).

Approximately 30% of children aged < 3 years at AD diagnosis and approximately 40% of older children had ≥ 1 atopic comorbidity within 1 year of their AD diagnosis (Fig. 1d). By 10 years after AD diagnosis, it was estimated that approximately 50% of children had been diagnosed with ≥ 1 atopic comorbidity (mainly asthma and/or AR), regardless of age at AD diagnosis (Fig. 1d), aligning with earlier findings [14].

Most children had only AD or AD plus one atopic comorbidity in the years before and after AD diagnosis, with the highest increase in children aged < 3 years (Fig. 3). However, the proportion of children with two or three atopic comorbidities at AD diagnosis increased over time and with increasing age (Fig. 3).

Discussion

The validity of a strict “atopic march” (i.e., AD followed by FA, asthma, and AR [6–8]) has been much debated [9–12]. Atopic march was likely suggested to start with AD as this is often the earliest type 2–associated condition diagnosed [10]. We noted that patients aged < 3 years at AD diagnosis versus those aged 3–17 years were less likely to have a prior or concurrent diagnosis of asthma or AR. This is consistent with AD being the first clinical manifestation of the atopic march in the infant/toddler age group. However, as AD is not always diagnosed first and children with AD do not necessarily develop other type 2 conditions and vice versa [12], many prefer the term atopic multimorbidity to atopic march [9].

The observed increase in cumulative atopic comorbidities prevalence from 1 year before AD diagnosis may be a true depiction of age-related patterns in occurrence of atopic conditions [15], but it may also result from the lag period between AD onset and AD diagnosis, obscuring the observed sequence of disease development. Moreover, patients diagnosed with an atopic condition before AD may have had skin barrier dysfunction owing to subclinical AD, which could have contributed to their atopic multimorbidity [16]. It is also possible that AD is underdiagnosed in infancy owing to subtler presentations, or alternatively, overdiagnosed in toddlers with nonspecific dermatitis, both of which could affect the apparent temporal relationship between AD and comorbidities. Nevertheless, this study and multiple previous studies [3–5, 12] support an elevated risk of other type 2–associated conditions among patients with AD. Furthermore, AD has been identified as a major risk factor for asthma development in young children with recurrent wheezing [17].

While the results of this study do not suggest a strict adherence to the traditional view of the atopic march, in which pediatric patients, irrespective of age at diagnosis, are first diagnosed with AD and subsequently acquire one or more type 2 comorbidities, children < 3 years of age at AD diagnosis rapidly acquired an additional burden of type 2 comorbidities. Children in older age groups diagnosed with AD had often already developed other type 2-mediated diseases. These observations highlight the importance of early recognition and management of AD. Although evidence remains preliminary, early interventions—such as proactive topical treatment—may not only help control skin inflammation but could also reduce the risk of subsequent atopic conditions.

It may be possible to inhibit atopic march with targeted therapies [18]. Preliminary data indicate that certain biologics may reduce the risk of new/worsening allergies in patients with AD, with greater benefit in patients aged < 18 years, those with early AD onset (< 2 years of age), those with more severe AD, and those with baseline asthma [19]. Some biologics may also reduce FA sensitization in adults [20, 21]. In a recent study, pediatric patients with AD (< 18 years) who were prescribed a biologic had reduced risk of atopic march progression compared with conventional therapies [22]; however, data in younger children are currently lacking. The potential impact of targeted therapies on atopic march is an exciting area for future investigation.

Strengths and Limitations

The key strength of this study is that the MHS database contains > 2.6 million members (> 25% of the population) and is representative of the Israeli population. MHS has an annual retention rate > 99%; its database includes routine healthcare data from multiple sources, including inpatient/outpatient diagnoses, providing robust longitudinal follow-up.

Some limitations include the restricted geographic location of the study population, potential for misdiagnoses (e.g., AD versus contact dermatitis), especially within different age groups (e.g., underdiagnosis in infant versus overdiagnosis in toddlers), and lack of established criteria for IgE-mediated FA diagnosis. Over the long study period, diagnostic practices including criteria could have changed, which may affect the comorbidity incidence. The Israeli healthcare system may influence the generalizability of our findings. Universal health coverage, direct access to dermatologists without referral, and wide availability of subsidized treatments likely facilitate earlier diagnosis and more consistent management of AD. These factors may impact both the observed prevalence and the progression of disease compared with countries with more limited access. Diagnoses before MHS enrollment were not captured. This could explain the lower FA prevalence among older children, who often outgrow FAs [23], and earlier FA diagnoses may not have been captured. Finally, the study design did not distinguish between trends over time post-AD versus trends over time related to age, independent of AD. Future studies including patients without AD could provide additional insights into the relationship between AD and other atopic comorbidities, as well as the sequence of atopic march and other disease trajectories.

Conclusions

Results from this study are consistent with previous evidence that AD is primarily an early childhood-onset disease. Children diagnosed with AD early (< 3 years at AD diagnosis) often develop other type 2 conditions after AD diagnosis, and very rapidly (within 1 year) in the case of asthma and FA. Children diagnosed with AD later, often already carry a high atopic comorbidity burden at AD diagnosis. Regardless of age, approximately 50% of pediatric patients with AD were estimated to have ≥ 1 diagnosis of asthma, AR, or FA within 10 years of their AD diagnosis, highlighting the comorbidity burden in this patient population.
