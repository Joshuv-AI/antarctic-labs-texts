Supervised machine learning algorithms for classifications of gender-based violence in Somalia: a comparison of oversampling techniques
Authors: Seyifemickael Amare Yilema, Denekew Bitew Belay, Mahad Ibrahim Ali, Nigussie Adam Birhan, Najmeh Nakhaei Rad, Ding-Geng Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Gender-based violence can include sexual, physical, mental, and economic harm inflicted in public or in private. This violence also has a direct psychological effect, physical and financial consequences, and it has multiple underlying reasons, such as social, economic, cultural, political, and religious aspects. By applying multiple resampling techniques, this study aims to improve the precision and accuracy of supervised machine learning classifications of gender-based violence (GBV) using the SDHS dataset. The class imbalance between GBV-positive and GBV-negative instances makes it very challenging to produce reliable classification machine learning models. To address this issue, oversampling machine learning approaches, including synthetic minority over-sampling technique (SMOTE), adaptive synthetic (ADASYN), and random over-sampling (ROS), were employed to classify the GBV data in Somalia. The logistic regression (LR), decision tree (CART), random forest (RF), naïve Bayes (NB), k-nearest Neighbors (KNN), and support vector machine (SVM) methods were trained and evaluated. In addition, oversampling techniques were employed for improving the imbalanced datasets. Receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC) and the area under the curve (AUC) were used to assess each machine learning classifier and to compare performance on the original GBV dataset. Among the resampling techniques, SMOTE (RF = 0.992, CART = 0.969, and KNN = 0.957) outperformed ADASYN (RF = 0.912, CART = 0.910, and KNN = 0.876) and ROS (RF = 0.920, CART = 0.919, and KNN = 0.880) across almost all evaluation metrics. The classifiers that performed the best were random forest (RF) and classification and regression trees (CART), then k-nearest Neighbors. After resampling the imbalanced dataset, we may therefore conclude that the random forest (AUC = 0.972), CART (AUC = 0.969) and KNN (AUC = 0.957) machine learning classifiers are better at accurately classifying the k-nearest Neighbors dataset. In addition, compared to the other oversampling techniques, SMOTE was used to the machine learning classifiers to balance the imbalanced class distributions in favour of the minority class. In addition, SMOTE with the Mathews correlation coefficient (MCC) outperformed ADASYN and ROS resampling techniques. The MCC values for SMOTE reached their highest values (RF = 0.86, CART = 0.85, and KNN = 0.80), indicating strong overall predictive reliability of the machine learning models. Therefore, the findings of this analysis will assist government and non-government organizations in making policy decisions to GBV risks.

Introduction

Although Gender-based violence (GBV) has only lately started to be acknowledged as a public health concern, it is a significant contributor to female morbidity and death1,2. A more a comprehensive description of GBV encompasses a wide range of harmful actions, including physical, sexual, psychological harm, economic abuse, harassment, sexual exploitation, reproductive coercion, arbitrary deprivation of freedom, and controlling actions that isolate individuals or limit their access to financial resources, employment, education, or medical care3. An increasing number of studies indicates that there is pervasive violence against women; according to reports, one in three women worldwide have been abused at some point in their childhood, teenage years, or adult life4–6. This violence also directly affects the psychological effects, physical violence and financial consequences. GBV has multiple underlying reasons, such as social, economic, cultural, political, and religious aspects7,8.

The World Health Organization conducted a study across multiple countries on violence against women. The findings showed that the occurrence of physical or sexual assault by an intimate partner varied between 15% and 71% across a woman’s lifetime, and between 4% and 54% within the last 12 months while between 20% and 75% of women reported experiencing emotional violence. The prevalence of all forms of violence was continuously lowest in Japan city, while the provinces of Bangladesh, Ethiopia, Peru, and the United Republic of Tanzania recorded the highest percentages6. A comprehensive study of 161 countries, covering 90% of women and girls aged 15 and older worldwide, indicates that 27% of women aged 15–49 have experienced physical or sexual intimate partner violence in their lifetime, with 13% facing such violence in the past year. This issue begins early, affecting 24% of girls aged 15–19 and 26% of women aged 19–24. As a result, many experiences unwanted pregnancies, some of which end in induced abortions9. Furthermore, low-income countries report higher rates of both lifetime and past-year intimate partner violence compared to high-income countries10. There is substantial evidence indicating a significant prevalence of GBV within educational institutions in Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA). The Global Based School Survey (GBSS) reveals that physical assault affects between 27% and 50% of students, while sexual assault impacts between 9% and 33% across five African countries11. GBV presents a substantial barrier to worldwide development. Within the regions of Eastern and Southern Africa, a significant proportion of women, about 42%, experience physical or sexual assault at some stage during their lifetimes12. Somalia is considered one of the worst places for women13, mothers14, and children15 GBV due to numerous factors. These include high female illiteracy, widespread Female Genital Mutilation (FGM), early and forced marriages, high maternal and infant mortality rates, inadequate healthcare, high rates of rape and Sexual and Gender-based Violence (SGBV), poverty, malnutrition, displacement, armed conflict, and significant social, economic, and political gender inequality9,16–22. The Somali Health and Demographic Survey (SHDS) conducted in 2020, showed physical violence experienced by women with reproductive age; about 14% of women have experienced physical violence since the age of 12, and 8% experienced it in the 12 months before the survey23. Younger women, mainly those aged 15–19, are more likely to experience physical violence, with 16% reporting violence since age 12 and 10% within the past year. In contrast, 11% of women aged 45–49 reported experiencing physical violence since age 12, and 4% reported it in the past year23. At the same time, only 17% of ever-married women aged 15–49 who experienced emotional, physical, or sexual violence sought help, while 83% did not seek any help. Urban women are more likely to seek help, about 23%, compared to rural which is 12% and nomadic dweller women is about 10%23. There is no clear pattern in help-seeking behavior based on the woman’s age, education, or wealth status, underscoring the urgent need for further investigation and support for affected women23.

The study conducted on internal displaced people (IDP) found that 18.5% of women and girls in IDP camps in Somalia had experienced gender-based violence in the past year. The types of violence included physical assaults (44.0%), psychological abuse (24.8%), forced marriage (18.8%), attempted rape (7.2%), rape (4.0%), and denial of resources (1.6%). Additionally, the study revealed that intimate partners or their relatives were responsible for 57.7% of the violence, with 60.6% of these incidents occurring during the daytime. Over half (57.7%) of the victims were over 20 years old, and 43.7% experienced multiple assaults. Most of these incidents (over 70%) took place in the victims’ homes, often (83.1%) when they were alone24.

Similarly, the study conducted on Mogadishu IDP camp highlights several causes of SGBV against internally displaced. Women traveling to and from the camps face the risk of rape by thieves and rapists. Within the camps, women are vulnerable to sexual and physical violence due to the flimsy plastic shelters they live in, often without protection from male relatives. Domestic violence is also common, exacerbated by chat abuse among men. Additionally, women working outside the camps are at risk of SGBV from their employers25.

The existing literatures have examined factors associated with GBV among women in Somalia using traditional analytical approaches24–26. However, these studies were limited in scope, as they were conducted on small samples and in specific regions, which prevented them from capturing the full extent of GBV among women aged 15–49 nationwide. To the best of our knowledge and experience, there is a significant literature gaps for machine learning-based GBV data at Somalia and this area has not yet been adequately discovered. It is the first in its kind machine learning applications for GBV nationally representative survey data in Somalia, fragile and data scare settings where empirical evidence is extremely limited. Addressing the class imbalance for critical GBV risk predictions in low prevalence with clear implication for future machine learning-based and oversampling techniques, including Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique (SMOTE), Adaptive synthetic sampling (ADASYN) and Random Over Sampler (ROS) for artificially generating samples, is crucial for GBV predictions27–29. Therefore, the present study aims to apply supervised machine learning models to improve GBV classification through the use of various oversampling resampling techniques. Using SHDS data, this study integrates a broad set of socioeconomic characteristics to provide a comprehensive analysis of GBV.

Methods and materials

Data sources

The target variable with its input variables for this study was selected from the 2020 SHDS data. The SHDS, which was carried out from 2018 to 2019 and published in 2020, represents the first survey of such data in Somalia’s history, offering essential information for policymakers to develop evidence-based policies that deliver the necessary services to the Somali population. The survey employed a three-stage stratified cluster sample design for urban and rural areas and a two-stage design for nomadic areas, using probability proportional to size for selecting Primary Sampling Units (PSUs) and systematic sampling for households. The target variable in this study is categorized into two categories; categorizing violence against women with reproductive age. It separates respondents into two groups: those who experienced any types of violence (14.2%); which is considered as the minority group in our analysis and those who did not experience any violence (85.8%, majority).

The data is split in to 70:30 train-test commonly used for machine learning algorithms. 70% of the data is used for model training, which provides sufficient examples for the model to learn underlying patterns, while the other 30% is used for testing data that offers a robust and independent assessment of model performance. This split balances the need for adequate training data with reliable evaluation, In this study, stratification was applied to preserve the original class distribution, ensuring that both the training and test sets adequately represent minority (GBV) and majority classes, thereby supporting reliable model training and evaluation. The input variables, referred to as features in machine learning models, were chosen based on the previous studies and available features in the survey30,31.

Feature selection

Many studies in machine learning algorithms, the target variables are referred to as features, and feature selection is the process of identifying variables that are helpful in predicting and categorizing the target variable. Identifying significant features is regarded as a best approach in the construction of appropriate predictive models. This approach seeks to eliminate insignificant variables, diminish the high-dimensional dataset, and enhance computational performance while preserving the original dataset. We employed mean decrease accuracy (MDA) approach for feature selection, utilizing the random forest algorithm. The MDA uses the random forest model to assess the significance of features in revealing the variation in the outcome variable (violence status: violence or nonviolence) utilizing the training dataset. Upon assessing the significance of each attribute, it systematically eliminates them sequentially in each iteration32.

Imbalance data for the target variable in machine learning

When data issues become more complex, the classification of imbalanced data sets has emerged as a crucial challenge. However, resolving this challenge could have a significant impact on the field of data analysis for better prediction. Imbalanced data set occurs when the quantity of one type of class/category significantly exceeds that of other types within the sample. For instance, when the data type is divided into two categories, the category with a limited number of samples is classified as the positive type (minority type), while the quantity of samples is of a negative kind: class (predominance) category33. Imbalanced datasets, especially in health-related data, are essential for tackling increasing data challenges. In Somali health and demographic survey data, a significant class imbalance exists in GBV, characterized by a majority class with a higher number of negative cases and a minority class with fewer positive cases34. Numerous literatures categorize the severity of class imbalance according to the minority representation into three distinct categories: mild (20–40%), moderate (1–20%), and severe (less than 1%)35,36. The current analysis shows that 14.16% of the data relates to women experiencing violence (minority class), while 85.84% pertains to non-violence respondents (majority class). This demonstrates the class imbalance in our dataset, which results in a biased model and misleading assessment measures37. For such cases, a common set of techniques for mitigating data imbalance is resampling38,39.

These methods entail altering the dataset by either under-sampling (fewer samples from the majority class) or over-sampling (more samples from the minority class)40. Under-sampling strategies entail the random removal of cases from the majority training dataset to achieve a more balanced distribution. This creates an issue of limited sample size, wherein the minority class does not possess a sufficiently big training set for classifiers to effectively discern its characteristics. This can significantly affect the training process, leading to inadequate generalization capabilities and classification bias, particularly when coupled with a substantial imbalance ratio. Furthermore, a limited number of samples renders it infeasible to guarantee that the training set adequately reflects the distribution of the real-world event, a situation referred to as data shift41. Over-sampling is a data augmentation technique employed to mitigate class imbalance and it seeks to equalize the distribution of training data by averaging the instances from the minority class42. The resampling techniques SMOTE, ADASYN and ROS are widely utilized approach for artificially generating samples from the minority class in over-sampling procedures27–29. SMOTE establishes connections between minority class samples and adjacent data points, generating new minority class samples along these connections27,38,43.

Machine learning algorithms

This methodology allows researchers to independently evaluate the efficacy of different algorithms, typically employed in the absence of a clear ideal choice44. The outcomes are subsequently compared among the various algorithms. This technique assists researchers in selecting the algorithm that is likely to be most effective for their classifications and forecasts. The selection of algorithm is crucial for imbalanced datasets, such as those utilized for predicting infrequent occurrences like GBV. Meticulous algorithm selection techniques enhance the robustness of models for predicting and classifying GBV. The prospective machine learning classifier algorithms, along with brief descriptions, are as follows:

Decision Trees: A decision tree (DT) is a supervised learning method utilized for classification and regression tasks, while it is predominantly applied for classification45. Based on the features that maximize the similarity of the generated groups, the given dataset is repeatedly divided into progressively similar groups. The topmost or starting node among the several tiers of nodes that comprise the DT is called the root node. To create forecasts and classifications, the new individual is assessed using the predefined criteria. Binary trees were created utilizing the regression and classification trees, and DT classifiers were constructed46,47. DT classifiers were built, and binary trees were produced using the classification and regression tree (CART).

Random Forest: Random forest (RF) is an ensemble machine-learning algorithm. The fundamental principle of ensemble learning is the combination of numerous underperforming models to provide predictions that are more accurate. Because it performs so well in a variety of classification and regression predictive modeling applications, RF is the most widely used machine learning method34. This technique builds decision trees based on a random subset of data samples, then extracts predictions from each tree. After that, the most likely choice is chosen by voting or averaging. The two crucial phases of RF are bootstrapping and aggregation, sometimes referred to as bagging. RF chooses a subset of features at each decision split in addition to bagging to create trees independent of each other48. A primary advantage of utilizing RF is its ability to mitigate the over fitting issue by averaging or voting on all predictions, hence removing biases49. Furthermore, RF has developed into a highly effective and resilient algorithm capable of addressing feature selection issues, even in the presence of several variables50. The present study has selected RF for its capacity to address over fitting resulting from imbalanced data and numerous predictors.

Logistic Regression: logistic regression (LR) is a most commonly used machine learning classification technique that forecasts binary outcomes51, usually used as a baseline models. It forecasts the probability (p) of an event and distinguishes positive entities from negative ones by establishing a threshold limit. It is classified as one if p > = 0.5; else, it is ranked as zero. The logistic function maps continuous probability values to discrete classes (0 and 1). Its probabilistic characteristics render it suitable for the contemporary prediction of GBV risks52.

Naive Bayes: Naïve Bayes (NB) is a prominent classification technique in machine learning that categorizes data based on conditional probability values. It applies Bayes’ theorem for probability calculation and employs class levels depicted as feature values or predictor vectors for classification. The NB is a good choice for classification problems requiring probability estimates53,54.

Support Vector Machine: Support Vector Machine (SVM) is a supervised machine learning technique employed for regression and classification, especially in classification tasks. The basic objective is to identify an ideal hyperplane in an N-dimensional space that segregates data points into distinct feature space classes, striving for the maximum possible buffer between the nearest points. SVM is a predictive model that optimizes accuracy while minimizing the risk of over fitting to the dataset. It is characterized as systems employing linear or nonlinear functions within a high-dimensional feature space, taught utilizing a learning algorithm derived from optimization theory55–57.

K-Nearest Neighbours: The k-nearest neighbors (KNN) algorithm is a non-parametric, supervised learning classifier that utilizes proximity to classify or predict the categorization of a specific data point. It is supervised as it acquires knowledge from training data and is frequently employed for classification purposes. KNN designates a class label according to the predominant class among its nearest neighbors and formulates predictions based on the proximity of data points inside the feature space. KNN is easy to comprehend and apply, but it can be computationally expensive for large datasets because it needs to calculate the distances between every pair of data points, which necessitates proper data pre-processing. In spite of this, KNN is adaptable and appropriate for a range of classification and regression tasks, particularly in situations when the decision boundary is ambiguous or complex40,58,59.

Calibration probability metrics

The probabilistic accuracy of a predictive model is determined by choosing a suitable performance indicator, which indicates the model’s ability to predict outcomes34. Nevertheless, the learning techniques used to create machine learning models frequently result in calibration problems. Because tree-based ensembles, such as random forests, are inherently variable in their predictions, they frequently overestimate or underestimate probabilities. Accuracy is given priority above probabilistic calibration in other models, such as support vector machines. Furthermore, a lot of models are assessed and optimized using binary measures like accuracy, which only take accuracy into account rather than certainty.

The Brier score and log-loss metrics are employed to evaluate the efficacy of calibrating operations. The Brier score quantifies the disparity between expected probability and actual results, whereas the log-loss metric assesses the precision of probabilistic forecasts. A diminished Brier score and log-loss signify superior calibration, with the optimal score approaching zero, indicating precise probability calibration in predictions.

Statistical models are evaluated and contrasted with a selected fitted model for prediction, using the criteria for model assessment. K-fold cross-validation is a robust statistical method used in machine learning to estimate the generalization performance of a model and prevent overfitting. Splitting the dataset into K equal-sized folds (subsets) for common choices for K are 5 or 10, as they offer a good balance between bias and variance. The k-fold cross-validation technique is implemented by randomly splitting the available data into k-folds. Models are trained on k-1 folds and tested on the remaining kth fold. By iterating over all k-folds, the practitioner can obtain out-of-fold predictions and performance for the entire dataset. This is referred to as cross-validation (CV) performance. k-fold cross-validation is widely used for model selection choosing both between model classes and tuning hyperparameters13.

In the current study, additional evaluation metrics are used for assessing classification performance for imbalanced datasets. These evaluation metrics are Mathew correlation coefficient (MCC), geometric mean (G-mean), Cohen’s kappa (kappa), and geometric discrimination rate (GDR)62–65.

MCC is a robust measure that provides a balanced measure by providing all elements of the confusion matrix. The MCC values are ranging from − 1 to 1, with 1 indicating a perfect prediction, 0 indicating a random prediction, and − 1 indicating a total disagreement63. Kappa is a statistical measure of agreement between prediction and actual classification, adjusted by chance, and is particularly important for skewed class distributions. Its value is in between − 1 and 1, with the highest value indicating better agreement65. G-mean assesses the balance between sensitivity and specificity, confirming that the model achieves well for both majority and minority classes, and GDR indicates the ability of the machine learning model to correctly classify between the minority and majority classes, with higher values indicating effective classification performance.

In this study we used Python 3.12.4 for generating and disseminating computational findings66. One of the main Python packages handling imbalanced datasets is imbalanced-learn, (imblearn), which is a widely used library to offer different resampling techniques such as SMOTE, ROS and ADASYN. Different Python libraries were used in this analysis to enhance reproducibility with the respective machine learning classifiers. The most commonly used libraries are scikit-learn (sklearn), pandas, and numpy, while matplotlib Python libraries was used to produce maps.

Descriptive statistics

In this study, the class distribution in a dataset, comprising a total of 33,183 observations is revealing a substantial imbalance between the non-violence and violence classes. There are 28,485 cases of non-violence, constituting the bulk of the sample, compared to only 4,698 cases of violence, indicating a significant bias towards non-violence. The GBV data are imbalanced, with non-violence accounting for 85.84% of the observations and violence comprising only 14.16%. This class imbalance can lead to a moderate risk of misclassification if not properly addressed. This mismatch might result in biased predictions in machine learning algorithms, favoring the majority class (non-violence group). To mitigate such problem this issue, the SMOTE, ROS, and ADASYN techniques were proposed to generate synthetic samples of the minority class (violence), hence enhancing the model’s predictive capability for both classes. Following the use of these synthetic sampling techniques, the formerly imbalanced dataset is now equilibrated, with both the non-violence and violence classes. This guarantees that the machine learning model trained on this data will not exhibit bias towards the majority class and will enhance its predictive performance for both classes.

Model performance evaluation metrics

Figure 3 presents the area under the curve (AUC) evaluation metrics for each machine-learning algorithm applied to the original GBV data. The AUC is a scalar metric that evaluates the classifier’s ability to differentiate between minority and majority classes across all possible classification thresholds. A predictive model is deemed accurate for classification if its kernel lines approach the upper left corner of the receiving operating characteristics (ROC) curve or if its AUC values are close to one.

In this analysis, different over sampling machine learning techniques are employed for balancing the imbalanced GVB datasets. Figure 3 illustrates the AUC scores under the ROC curves for each machine learning classifier after before and after resampling the imbalanced GBV data using the different over sampling techniques such as SMOTE, ROS and ADSYN. This Figure illustrates the AUC scores for various machine learning classifiers, including random forest67, LR, CART, NB, SVM and KNN. The predictive model classifiers exhibit substantial enhancement following the oversampling of the dataset. Implementing SMOTE oversampling techniques enhanced the performance of the classifiers in predictive machine-learning models to others. Although all model classifiers were enhanced, the RF classifier yielded the highest performance across all evaluation metrics making it the most effective for GBV data classifications following the application of oversampling. Overall, the application of SMOTE for oversampling markedly enhanced the accuracy of machine learning models across all classifiers, particularly the RF and CART algorithms.

Calibration metrics for evaluating probability predictions

Calibration is a crucial model evaluation indicator that aligns the actual likelihood of an event occurring with the projected probabilities. Several metrics are employed in the literature to assess the quality of projected probabilities, including the Brier score, log loss, and calibration plots (reliability plots). An optimally calibrated probability would ideally yield reduced log loss and Brier score values, in addition to exhibiting a straight diagonal line.

Figure 5 (left) illustrates the calibration probability plot of machine learning classification methods for an imbalanced dataset, revealing that the calibration curves for each algorithm significantly deviated from the real values. The classifier’s significant divergence from the diagonal indicates substantial calibration issues for this imbalanced dataset, as illustrated in the graph on the left. This indicates that the classifiers exhibit suboptimal performance on imbalanced GBV samples.

Figure 5 (right) displays the calibration plots of model classifiers for the balanced dataset. Each model classifier approaches the ideal lines; however, the RF and CART classifiers are virtually consistent with the perfect lines, indicating that the actual likelihood probabilities correspond closely with the anticipated probabilities. Consequently, RF and CART models generally excel in generating well-calibrated probabilities in comparison to alternative models.

Hyperparameter tuning with stratified cross-validation for classification models

Hyperparameter tuning was applied for all classification models using a systematic grid-search strategy within a stratified cross-validation framework. Specifically, for each classifier CART, RF LR, NB, SVM, and KNN, we defined model-specific hyperparameter grids and optimized them using GridSearchCV under 5-fold stratified cross-validation, ensuring preservation of class imbalance structure. For the CART model, the maximum tree depth, minimum number of samples required to split an internal node, and minimum number of samples required at a leaf node were tuned. The RF model was optimized by tuning the number of trees (n_estimator), maximum tree depth, and the minimum number of samples for node splitting and leaf nodes. For LR, the regularization strength parameter was tuned under an L2 penalty. The NB classifier was optimized by tuning the variance smoothing parameter. For the SVB, the regularization parameter, kernel type, and kernel coefficient were tuned. Finally, for the KNN classifier, the number of neighbors, distance weighting scheme, and distance metric were optimized.

The results of each machine learning classifier associated with each oversampling techniques for hyperparameter tuning using a 5 fold stratified cross-validation is presented in Table 3. LR, used as the baseline model, achieved moderate discriminatory performance, with AUC values of 0.608 under SMOTE and ROS and 0.589 under ADASYN. CART, RF, SVM, and KNN classifiers substantially outperform the baseline LR across all oversampling techniques. Under the SMOTE oversampling technique, CART, RF, SVM, and KNN achieved AUC values of 0.948, 0.958, 0.877, and 0.943, corresponding to improvements of approximately + 0.34, + 0.35, + 0.27, and + 0.34 AUC units, respectively, compared with the LR baseline model. Similar and consistent performance gains were observed under the ROS and ADASYN oversampling methods, confirming the robustness of these non-linear models to different class-imbalance correction strategies.

Discussions

GBV is a critical issue impacting millions of women globally. In recent years, machine learning methodologies have been progressively employed to discern and forecast aspects leading to GBV. Our research represents a significant category of studies utilizing machine learning to forecast domestic violence in Somalia. Prior research conducted by69,70 has utilized machine learning algorithms to forecast domestic violence67,71. SMOTE, ADSYN, and ROS resampling techniques were utilized due to the significant class imbalance in the original dataset. SMOTE provides synthetic samples for the GBV positive class to enhance the performance of various machine learning methods27.

In this study, among the socio demographic features wealth index, age at first marriage, family size, residence, and mothers’ age are significantly contributing for the machine learning classifications3,7,10. Literatures support the current study that mother’s age difference is a substantive for GBV risks3,7,10. The prior studies indicating that age is a significant factor in negotiating power and autonomy within households. In pastoralist communities, younger women often have limited social and economic independence, increasing their risk of both domestic and community-based violence. In addition, family sizes also another significant factor for the GBV in Somalia; most of the societies are living in pastoralist and rural settings; limited to social support and protective services in dispersed pastoralist settlement and rural areas residence. Societies or communities living in the rural or pastoralist areas usually experience geographic isolation, poor access to justice and health services, and high exposure to displacement exposes for high GBV risk. In line with the current findings, mothers’ age-related vulnerability, pastoralist or rural living conditions, family sizes and economic status of the communities are associated with a higher risk of GBV in Somalia24–26.

The machine learning algorithms (i.e., RF, LR, CART, SVM, NB, and KNN), and calibrating the predicted probabilities were applied in the analysis for balanced and imbalanced dataset. The basic learners employed distinct assessment metrics for balanced and imbalanced GBV, even utilizing identical dataset sources. Comparing the original (imbalanced) dataset with the SMOTE (balanced) dataset based on AUC-ROC scores across several machine learning methods, the SMOTE results markedly enhanced the performance of the respective classifiers compared with the original dataset, supporting findings from prior studies39,72,73.

Under the SMOTE data configuration, the AUC-ROC values of the classifiers under imbalanced and balanced conditions, reported as imbalanced, balanced, were as follows: RF (66.2, 97.2), CART (66.1, 96.8), SVM (56.8, 68.5), NB (52.4, 59.5), KNN (61.4, 87.6), and LR (52.3, 67.2). SMOTE is applied to balance the originally imbalanced GBV dataset and showed substantial improvements in classification performance, as reflected by the increase in AUC-ROC scores from the imbalanced to the balanced setting. These results indicate that SMOTE was particularly effective for tree-based and distance-based classifiers (RF, CART, and KNN), while more modest improvements were observed for probabilistic and margin-based methods (NB and SVM). Overall, the findings demonstrate that addressing class imbalance through SMOTE significantly enhances model discrimination ability, as measured by AUC-ROC.

For ROS oversampling techniques setups, the AUC values for learners when they were imbalanced versus balanced using ROS were RF (66.2, 92), CART (66.1, 91.9), SVM (56.8, 68), NB (52.4, 58.1), and KNN (61.4, 88), LR (52.3, 58.6), Applying ROS methods to balance the data over imbalanced dataset benefited to the improvements of the classifiers as RF (25.8%), CART (25.8%), KNN (26.6%), and LR (6.3%) SVM (11.2%) and NB (5.7%). In ADSYN data setups, the AUC values for learners under imbalanced versus balanced conditions were as follows: RF (66.2, 91.2), CART (66.1, 91), SVM (56.8, 68.5), NB (52.4, 59.5), KNN (61.4, 87.6), and LR (52.3, 67.2). Utilizing ADSYN techniques to equilibrate the data inside an imbalanced dataset resulted in enhancements for the classifiers: RF (25%), CART (24%), KNN (26.2%), LR (14.9%), SVM (11.7%), and NB (7.1%). Over all the predictive model classifiers RF, CART, KNN, and SVM exhibited a significant enhancement in AUC when the dataset was resampled using SOMTE, ROF, and ADSYN, whereas NB showed only modest improvement and it remained less effective than tree-based models. Among these methods, SMOTE particularly demonstrated its ability to enhance the performance metrics for most classifiers. This is because balancing the dataset mitigates over fitting, bias, and noise effects38. The superior performance of SMOTE compared with other resampling methods is due to its ability to generate synthetic minority-class samples that better represent the minority class27,38,43. This approach reduces overfitting and improves classifier performance, particularly for tree-based models, by enabling them to learn complex, non-linear relationships in highly imbalanced GBV datasets27–29. In addition, RF algorithm has been gain benefits from ensemble learning that reduce variances and improves generalizations, particularly for imbalanced datasets like GBV. While, CART also benefits because of effectively handles non-linearity and mixed data types.

The significant and strong performances of RF and CART algorithms shows their ability to model complex, non-linear relationships and higher-order interactions among socio-demographic, household, and contextual features that are inherent in GBV data. Tree-based methods are also robust to multicollinearity and mixed data types, which are prominent characteristics of the SHDS variables. In addition, RF benefits from ensemble learning, which reduces variance and improves generalization, particularly in imbalanced datasets for GBV outcomes. Naïve Bayes shows poor performance in GBV classification because it assumes that all predictors are independent given the outcome variable67,71. However, this independence assumption is unlikely to hold for GBV data. Socioeconomic and other explanatory variables are often correlated with GBV outcomes; as a result, the Naïve Bayes assumption is violated, leading to biased probability estimates and reduced classification accuracy69,70.

This study operated a calibration probability analysis, measuring performance by Brier loss, log loss, and probability plots. In calibration probabilities, the RF exhibits the lowest Brier and log loss scores and is nearly completely aligned with the ideal diagonal lines in the calibration predicted probability plots (Fig. 5). Consequently, the performance and calibration metrics from the machine learning analyses indicate that the RF classifier performs well in both classification and calibration34.

In the current study other evaluation metrics are employed for further confirming the improvements of the imbalanced data. The study demonstrates that the resampling techniques significantly improve classification performance for the imbalanced datasets. For the original imbalanced data, all machine learning classifiers showed a weak predictive ability with low values of evaluation metrics including MCC, kappa and G-mean, signifying poor agreement and strong bias towards the majority class. This finding is in line with other research finding36, which reported the imbalanced data showed a bias towards a majority class.

After applying oversampling techniques, particularly SMOTE, ADASYN, and ROS, all evaluation metrics showed substantial improvement. SMOTE provides the best performance for overall evaluation metrics, particularly for RF classifiers achieving approximately optimal values (MCC = 0.86, kappa = 0.85, G-mean = 0.94, Dice = 0.92, QDR = 0.97), aligning with the first proposed SMOTE finding43. SMOTE methods significantly enhance the performance classifications of class imbalance, which demonstrates its effectiveness in balancing the minority (violence) and majority (non-violence) classes under supervised machine learning methods. SMOTE methods of oversampling with an RF classifier outperform the other techniques by significantly improving the original imbalanced data, showing the most reliable performance34,73,74.

Limitations and future research directions

Although the proposed machine learning classifiers along with the over sampling methods achieved strong performances for the SDHS dataset, some limitations of the study should be acknowledged. First, the study mainly focused on the conventional machine learning classifiers, which demonstrated satisfactory performance, they might not fully capture highly complex nonlinear relationships for big datasets or high-dimensional datasets. Deep learning neural network approaches were not explored in the present study. Second, while these oversampling methods are effectively addressing the class imbalance, other imbalanced techniques including under sampling methods were not addressed because of the sample sizes.

Future research may focus on extending to the application of deep learning architectures, including multilayer perceptron that may further improve the model performance, particularly when modeling complex feature interactions and handling large-scale datasets. Furthermore, since this dataset are survey-based and that may have spatial components across provinces and districts, further studies will explore spatial machine learning and deep learning approaches. This may includes spatial autocorrelation structures and geographic information systems (GIS) based features or spatially explicit neural networks might improve the model performance accuracy.

Conclusions

By applying multiple resampling techniques, this study aims to improve the precision and accuracy of supervised machine learning classifications of GBV using the SDHS dataset. The class imbalance between GBV-positive and GBV-nonviolence instances makes it very challenging to produce reliable classification machine learning models. Therefore, oversampling techniques such as SMOTE, ADASYN and ROS are employed. Among these, SMOTE demonstrated a notable enhancement in minority class detection for GBV, thereby improving the overall accuracy and robustness of the model.

The assessment of evaluation metrics for SMOTE, such as ROC-AUC, accuracy, precision, and F1-score indicated significant improvements in the detection of GBV-positive instances. Therefore, RF exhibited the highest accuracy, and AUC followed by CART for overall oversampling techniques across each classifier. In addition, the calibration probabilities for RF under SMOTE is the smallest overall other corresponding metrics. We conclude that the both the performance metrics and calibration probability metrics show that SMOTE oversampling techniques with RF algorithms better enhance the machine learning classification of GBV data in Somali. Furthermore, hyperparameter tuning was optimized using GridSearchCV within a 5-fold stratified cross-validation framework. This systematic tuning further improved the performance of all machine learning algorithms. Notably, RF, CART and KNN showed improved performance, demonstrating that careful hyperparameter optimization methods can substantially enhance accuracy and reliability when classifying imbalanced GBV datasets. In addition, other evaluation metrics such as MCC, G-Mean, Dice score and QDR are employed for all oversampling techniques across each machine learning classifier. Among the classifiers under SMOTE, RF consistently performs better than other oversampling model classifiers. RF achieved the highest values across all metrics, confirming strong predictive reliability and good discriminative capability, and demonstrating significant improvement over the original imbalanced dataset.

The improved machine learning models, particularly those based on SMOTE, will help policymakers facilitate evidence-based decisions on prevention, intervention, and resource allocation for high-risk GBV populations in Somalia. Furthermore, relevant authorities should train local agencies and non-governmental organizations (NGOs) to utilize the predictive model findings for monitoring, decision-making, and early detections of GBV risk.
