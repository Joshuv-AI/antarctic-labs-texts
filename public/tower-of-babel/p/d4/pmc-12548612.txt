Improving viral load testing coverage among orphan and vulnerable children in Jinka Town, South Ethiopia: a quality improvement project implemented by the USAID FFHPCT activity team
Authors: Abdu Dawud, Mihiret Tesfaw, Temesgen Gebrasilase, Addisalem Asefa, Asrat Abota, Fikru Ligaba, Abiyu Million, Yeshihareg Yosef, Kalkidan Tsegaye, Mebratu Markos
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOrphan and vulnerable children (OVC) are at a heightened risk of poor health outcomes, particularly in areas with a high prevalence of HIV/AIDS. Viral load (VL) testing is a vital component of HIV care that enables early detection of treatment failure and improves health outcomes. During the first half of the US Agency for International Development Family Focused HIV Prevention, Care and Treatment activity implementation (October 2023–March 2024), OVC VL testing coverage in Jinka town was 89%. This shortfall affects the timely diagnosis and treatment adjustments for OVC, posing a challenge in achieving the third 95% HIV target. This quality improvement (QI) project aims to improve VL testing coverage among OVC in Jinka town.MethodA multidisciplinary QI team used the fishbone diagram to identify the root causes of low VL testing coverage. The nationally adopted model for improvement was employed using the plan–do–study–act (PDSA) cycles. Baseline data were taken from January to March 2024. The QI project was implemented from 1 April to 30 September 2024, for 6 months. A run chart was used to track the progress of the QI project and enforce evidence-based decision-making.InterventionsThe QI team tailored five well-worked interventions tested in three PDSA cycles, including appointment date reminders for OVC caregivers, on-the-job capacity building and targeted supportive supervision for staff, conducting caregiver awareness sessions, weekly VL result tracking and feedback mechanism and enhancing community–facility linkages.ResultsThe run chart demonstrated a 7% increment in OVC VL testing coverage, evidenced by six consecutive data points above the median line, indicating a statistically significant association between the identified QI gap and the implemented interventions.ConclusionThe QI project effectively improved the OVC VL testing coverage in Jinka town. This collaboration not only improves the VL testing coverage but also provides a comprehensive supportive environment for caregivers and families affected by HIV in the area.

Introduction

Problem description

Ethiopia continues to face a significant burden of HIV, as hundreds of thousands of people in the country are living with the virus, with the vast majority concentrated in high-prevalence regions and cities. According to the Ethiopian Public Health Institute’s annual HIV-related report of 2024, an estimated 605 238 people in the country were living with HIV in 2023, of which 27 051 were children under the age of 15 years. Of the total people living with HIV (PLHIV) in Ethiopia, 90% (547 723) know their status, 94% (513 990) of those who know their status were on treatment and 96% (495 519) of PLHIVs on treatment have achieved viral suppression. Orphan and vulnerable children (OVC) in Ethiopia are at a heightened risk of acquiring HIV through mother-to-child transmission or indirectly affected by HIV as their families are living with or have died from HIV. In 2023, an estimated 27 051 children were living with HIV in Ethiopia.1 More disproportionately, OVC in Ethiopia are vulnerable to malnutrition, poor hygiene, child sexual abuse, drug use and child labour exploitation.2

Jinka town is among the high HIV burden towns in Ethiopia, included in the Family Focused HIV Prevention, Care and Treatment (FFHPCT) activity implementation. A total of 2032 people are living with HIV in Jinka town, of which 81 are under the age of 18 years, with 55 of them who enrolled in the OVC programme of the FFHPCT activity were eligible for viral load (VL) testing. Among the total OVC eligible for VL testing in the town, only 89% obtained a VL testing service as of March 2024.3

Ethiopia has made progress in VL testing coverage among the OVC, yet significant gaps remain in this programme.4 The Ethiopian government, in collaboration with different stakeholders, is striving to increase access to antiretroviral therapy (ART), implementing different national programmes aiming at enhancing healthcare access for OVC and targeted community engagement activities enhancing HIV treatment adherence that contribute to VL suppression among these population groups. However, national OVC VL testing often encounters suboptimal VL monitoring, with a rate of approximately 85%, falling short of the 95% target due to various barriers.5 A list of several contributing factors, including lack of awareness, socioeconomic factors, limited accessibility to healthcare facilities, widespread myths and misconceptions, facility-based data management and tracking problems and provider-related gaps, contributes to the low VL testing coverage.6

VL testing is a critical aspect of HIV care, enabling healthcare professionals to provide evidence-based decision-making on treatment adjustments for HIV-positive clients on ART.4 7 To support the country’s move towards accomplishing the 95-95-95 global HIV targets,7 the US Agency for International Development in Ethiopia (USAID Ethiopia) awarded a 5-year Cooperative Agreement to Mary Joy Ethiopia (MJE) as the prime implementing partner for the project entitled FFHPCT Activity. The Ethiopian Kalehiwot Church-Development Commission (EKHC-DC), one of the local implementing partners of MJE, has been implementing the FFHPCT Activity in Jinka town since 2023.

To enhance the VL testing coverage in Jinka town, the project team identified the need for a quality improvement (QI) project. QI is a systematic process of assessing health system performance and its services, identifying root causes and introducing appropriate measures to improve quality and monitor progress over time.8 Aligning with USAID’s minimum service quality standards and PEPFAR’s HIV indicators for the programme,9 MJE, in collaboration with EKHC-DC, has recruited a multidisciplinary QI team in Jinka town. The established QI team developed Terms of Reference that clearly define the role, membership, meeting frequency and QI focus areas. The team comprises 13 members from the project staff, government stakeholders, local associations, health facility ART focal and health extension workers, and assigned a team leader and secretary. As the team is responsible for regularly monitoring the quality of the FFHPCT activity implementation in the area, the issue of VL testing coverage in Jinka town came to the QI team’s attention. The team designed a QI project to increase the VL testing coverage from 89% in March 2024 to 95% by the end of September 2024, in 6 months. The QI team critically identifies the existing barriers for testing, introduces and implements interventions and fosters community engagement to promote the awareness creation and assurance of sustainable HIV care and treatment approaches. During this QI project implementation, the FFHPCT activity team seeks to systematically act on the main issues affecting VL testing to improve the health outcomes of OVC that could, in turn, support the broader goal of HIV-related mortality and morbidity.7

Rationale of the QI project

Addressing the existing gap in VL testing coverage among OVC enrolled in the USAID FFHPCT Activity in Jinka town (89%) with evidence-based interventions to improve the health outcomes of OVC and the community.

Population unit

All HIV-positive children under the age of 18 years enrolled in the OVC programme at Jinka town make up the QI project’s target group.

Project context/setting

The QI project in Jinka town was implemented by a QI team of 13 members with different backgrounds and expertise. Jinka is the capital city of Ari Zone, located 755 km south of Addis Ababa, the capital city of Ethiopia. It is a tourist destination town and is one of the USAID’s FFHPCT activity implementation towns with an estimated 2032 PLHIV. The QI project involved 55 children who enrolled in the OVC programme of the FFHPCT activity and were eligible for VL testing.3 In collaboration with the existing local government structures and relevant stakeholders in Jinka town, the multidisciplinary QI team cascaded the project implementation for 6 months (1 April–30 September 2024).

Problem statement

The QI team analysed the findings from field-level OVC service quality assessment and system data review from 1 January to 30 March 2024 and discovered that the OVC VL testing coverage was at 89%. This low performance of VL coverage affects the timely diagnosis and treatment adjustments for OVC in the area, posing a challenge in achieving the third 95% HIV target.

Aim statement

The QI team aims to increase the OVC VL testing coverage from 89% in March 2024 to 95% by the end of September 2024, implementing the project for 6 months.

Problem identification, assessment and cause analysis

After identifying a bundle of QI gaps on the OVC programme using the standard assessment checklist, the team used a problem prioritisation matrix to identify a focus area. The set of criteria that the QI team uses to prioritise the problem is its magnitude, risk severity and whether it is a beneficiary concern or not (online supplemental material table 1).

Analysis of priority quality gaps

Once the priority problems are identified, the QI team further analyses the quality gap to identify the root cause of the problem that might reasonably cause the identified QI problem or prevent the likelihood of the problem recurring. In the current project, the fishbone diagram and ‘Five Whys’ are used to identify the root cause of the QI problem. The fishbone diagram conducted in this QI project represents the categories of causes leading to the QI problem, which helps to identify multiple causes of a single QI problem. The problem statement is written on the head of the fish, by which the QI team was able to brainstorm the common cause categories (figure 1).

Fishbone diagram illustrating the identified QI gap and its root causes. QI, quality improvement; VL, viral load.

For each identified cause, the QI team used the iterative question asking technique (‘Five Whys’) to explore the root cause and effect relationships to uncover the layers of problems. After the QI team has drawn the fishbone diagram and Five Whys, the team listed all the identified issues and considered them in terms of frequency, severity, ease of application and cost-effectiveness.

The root cause analysis identified that the suboptimal VL testing coverage among OVC in Jinka town was mainly attributable to a combination of systemic and individual-level factors. Facility-level factors (shortage of supplies, weak sample transportation and weak community collaboration), project staff (capacity gap and competing responsibilities that decrease VL follow-up), beneficiary level (low awareness, stigma and transportation problems) and monitoring and evaluation (absence of strong tracking and data utilisation) all these interlinked factors reduce timely VL testing among OVCs. As a result, the team urges finding intervention ideas through using the driver’s diagram (online supplemental material figure 2).

Several intervention ideas identified through the drivers diagram were merged to form more consolidated strategies by thematic areas. These intervention ideas were prioritised according to the change idea’s feasibility, potential impact on the problem and workability by the QI team’s scope using the intervention prioritisation matrix (online supplemental material table 2). This tool identifies five priority intervention ideas, scoring the maximum points in sum.

Interventions/change ideas

The drivers diagram (theory of change) is used to explore possible intervention ideas for the QI problem. The drivers diagram is a visualisation tool that supports the QI team by outlining a pathway to achieve the project’s aim, showing the relationship among the project’s aim, primary drivers (key factors that directly impact the aim) and secondary drivers (detailed factors supporting the likelihood of primary drivers to happen). The secondary drivers then enable the QI team to brainstorm possible intervention ideas. Reviewing literature, referring to national working documents and guidelines, and adopting previous experiences from other parties or in placing novel ideas were considered to generate change ideas. A mix of different intervention ideas was drawn from the drivers diagram4 7 9 (online supplemental material figure 2).

Interventions implemented in the QI project

Intervention 1: capacity building and targeted supportive supervision (TSS) to SSWs and CWs

As a part of plan–do–study–act (PDSA) cycle 1, the lack of adequate programme knowledge and skills among SSWs and CWs was identified as a prevalent barrier contributing to low VL coverage, which drew the attention of the QI team to its priority implementation. In early April 2024, a dedicated QI team member (Project QI Officer) was assigned to conduct regular capacity-building sessions (on-the-job coaching and SS) with the SSWs and CWs (n=4). These include undergoing regular on-the-job coaching, mentoring and supportive supervision programmes for SSWs and CWs regarding OVC VL result tracking and techniques of engaging their caregivers. The FFHPCT activity SS and on-job coaching checklists were used as a guiding tool and kept filled at the service delivery unit. A total of 12 TSS and on-job coaching sessions were conducted with the SSWs and CWs in 6 months. After a consecutive 2 months of implementation, the on-job coaching and TSS efforts improved the technical capacity of frontline teams evaluated through pre- and postcoaching and SS results in meeting the OVC checklist requirements and increased the VL testing coverage to 93% (Online supplemental figure 1). The intervention supports ensuring data quality and consistency of services, as capacitating the frontline workforce improves competency that might increase effective OVC programme implementation.10

Intervention 2: appointment date reminder system

Several studies explore the reason behind the low VL coverage among OVC. The leading cause is forgetting the appointment date/recalling problem.6 7 As a part of the PDSA cycle 2, the QI team in Jinka addressed this issue by developing an action plan and assigning one SSW and one RLO to send SMS messages to OVC caregivers 1–2 days before the VL testing appointment date. Initiated in the first week of June 2024 and continued throughout the project implementation period, the dedicated QI team members were able to reach out to 51 OVC caregivers with SMS reminders, and remarkable results were gained within 2 weeks of the start of the intervention. Through the MS Excel sheet, the monitoring and evaluation officer was tasked with weekly data collection and plotting the run chart for this intervention (online supplemental figure 2). After a month, we conducted an assessment, showing that the VL testing coverage had risen to 95%.

Intervention 3: conduct community caregiver awareness creation sessions

During the third PDSA cycle, the CWs were tasked to undergo biweekly caregiver sessions in the community to foster tackling of the community-rooted myths and behaviours that result in poor adherence among OVC. The CWs tailored the sessions using various job aids. This task was implemented based on the consideration of the total number of caregivers in the programme. The assigned CWs aggressively conducted the community awareness sessions starting from the first week of August 2024 to the third week of September 2024, resulting in reaching 52 OVC caregivers in 26 home-to-home caregiver literacy sessions in Jinka town. Data were collected biweekly from the health education logbook and the weekly activity report of CWs and plotted in process measure run chart (online supplemental figure 3). This intervention idea is proven effective, raising the OVC VL testing coverage to 96% in August 2024. The result is also supported by many scientific studies and recommended by the WHO.9 11

Concurrently implemented interventions

Intervention 4: weekly VL result tracking and feedback mechanism

Evidences show that inconsistent receiving of VL results from health facilities could significantly affect the health outcomes of OVC due to a lack of timely treatment adjustments as required.12 13 During the root cause analysis phase of this QI project, the QI team identified a delayed VL result collection from the health facility to the community (FFHPCT activity). The QI team in Jinka then developed a weekly plan to timely collect VL results from the health facility and ensure communication with caregivers of OVC. The referral linkage officer (FFHPCT project staff) was designated to receive weekly VL results from the health facility and provide feedback to the caregivers. This enables the QI team to address the long-standing problem of delayed result receipt from the facility, as well as the timely updating of the VL follow-up logbook and the CommCare system, delivering VL results to clients and ultimately improving the health outcomes of OVC in the town.

Intervention 5: community–facility linkages integration

This intervention involves harmonising the project staff, including the Social Service Workers (SSWs), Case Workers (CWs) and Comunity Engagment facilitators (CEFs), with the Health Facility (HF) to enhance the strengthening of the client referral system and timely tracking of VL results from registers, Smart Care (HF database) and the CommCare system. It is well researched that the integration of community-based HIV initiatives and healthcare facilities in the context of fragmented healthcare access results in better HIV-related health outcomes. Integrating the community health workers into the health system is vital to link the integrated models and ensure the efficiency of HIV service delivery.11 12 In the current QI project, the team established a clear referral pathway between the frontline community health workers and the health facilities. At the same time, continuous capacity building for the community health workers was implemented through regular coaching to enable them to navigate the healthcare system, especially the OVC programme. This intervention facilitates the timely exchange of relevant information on the health of OVC, which further contributes to the increase in VL coverage in Jinka town.

Intervention design

This QI project used the nationally adopted framework known as the model for improvement. The QI team considers the three basic questions of this model to enhance the process and outcomes of the project. These include: (1) what are we going to accomplish? (2) how will that change be an improvement? and (3) what changes can we make that will result in improvement?.8 This model used the PDSA cycles to develop an actionable QI plan, implement it within the given time, monitor the progress with run charts and, finally, enables the team to decide one of the three A’s (Adopt, Adapt and Abandon) options of the three ‘A’s’ of the PDSA approach (online supplemental material figure 3).14

Data collection method and quality

Baseline (BL) data for the QI project were collected from January to March 2024, for 3 months before the actual project implementation period. These BL data serve as an initial reference point to assess the effect of changes implemented during project implementation. The required data for the QI project were collected by each assigned QI team member weekly, biweekly and monthly using Microsoft Excel, and finally, the outcome measure was visualised using a run chart. The detailed data collection methods, frequency and sources are found in the online supplemental material tables 3 and 4. To ensure the accuracy of data, monthly data verification and triangulation activity were implemented by the QI team. Continuous progress assessment, regular supervision and follow-up and leadership engagement activities were found to be useful for the successful completion of this QI project. Data integrity was maintained by keeping data secure, anonymous and ready for audits throughout the project. The details of data collection methods, sources and frequency are found in online supplemental tables 2 and 3.

Methods of data analysis

The analysis for the weekly, biweekly and monthly collected raw data for each process measure was conducted independently through Microsoft Excel, and separate run charts were plotted (online supplemental material graphs 1–3). The outcome measure (VL testing coverage) was then plotted in the run chart every month for 6 months. We analysed the data using run chart rules to identify non-random variation signalling improvement. The four standard run chart interpretation rules: ‘shift’, ‘trend’, ‘runs’ and ‘astronomical data points’ were considered. The x-axis in the run chart displays the time series, while the y-axis represents the outcome measure (VL testing coverage). Each point in the chart shows a measurement that the collected data in the spreadsheet have been kept in three columns: the BL, the median and the goal.

A 3-month BL data collection was conducted before applying our intervention and included in the analysis. The median value represents the central tendency of the data, calculated by ordering the collected values in ascending order and taking the middle number in the dataset. If there is an even number of observations (dataset), the average of the two middle values is taken as the median value. The goal value (95% of VL testing coverage) is the desired performance-level set based on the project’s target, aligning with the national and global HIV targets.4 15 Finally, the run chart analysis is conducted and presented in a graph.

The manuscript is prepared using the BMJ’s Standards for Quality Improvement Reporting Excellence guideline for QI reports.16

Lessons learnt

In this QI project, it is evident that through applying different intervention ideas, it is possible to improve the VL testing coverage efficiently within a short period, which in turn improves the health and well-being of OVC. Integrated involvement of all stakeholders from different backgrounds and a multidisciplinary QI team enables addressing the identified gap shortly. Regular capacity building and supportive supervision of frontline workers lead to an increased knowledge and confidence that substantially contributes to better service provision. Engaging community members increased trust and willingness, reduced myths and misconceptions attached to HIV services and fostered ownership for the sustainable implementation of health initiatives.

Therefore, the current QI project provides many transferable strategies that can be adapted across programmes. These include strengthening the capacity of frontline workers through continuous on-job coaching and SS, meaningful community engagement and caregiver awareness creation, customising the caregiver appointment reminder system, strengthening community–facility linkage and data-driven decision-making. However, barriers, including high staff turnover, resource limitations, sociocultural barriers and competing priorities among stakeholders, pose ongoing difficulties for consistent implementation.

Limitations of the study

Though the QI project demonstrated a scientific approach to improve the VL testing coverage in Jinka town, the number of OVC and their caregivers considered in this study was small, which may not represent the general population. This project also did not assess the sociodemographic characteristics of OVC and their caregivers that might influence adherence rate; for this reason, it is unable to control for confounding variables and generalise the findings. Additionally, the QI project was implemented over a short period (6 months of implementation), which may not capture the sustainability of interventions. While the project improved VL testing coverage, our sustainability plan was not rigorously evaluated, and long-term viability depends on external funding and health system integration. Educational interventions were linked to outcomes indirectly; further work should include pre–post knowledge assessment for staff and caregivers.

Future researchers could improve this by performing such QI initiatives on a larger scale to address this important public health problem.

Conclusion and recommendation

Conclusion

The QI project aimed at improving VL testing coverage in Jinka town provided significant insights. The collaboration of all the relevant parties effectively enhanced OVC VL testing coverage by applying the five well-worked intervention ideas in three PDSA cycles. The QI project focused on interventions, including appointment date reminders, consistent VL result receiving, enhancing community–facility linkages, conducting caregiver community sessions, undergoing capacity building and TSS sessions. This collaboration not only improves the VL testing coverage among OVC but also provides a comprehensive supportive environment for caregivers and families affected by HIV in the study area.

Recommendations

To sustain the enhancements gained on VL testing coverage among OVC, it is important to engage communities meaningfully. The role of working against stigma through regular and targeted community awareness creation campaigns is crucial to ensure inclusive community services. Researchers and QI experts working to improve VL testing coverage among OVCs need to explore their community dynamics, evaluate the effectiveness of training and analyse logistical barriers affecting VL testing, which might enable them to develop an evolving QI innovation.
