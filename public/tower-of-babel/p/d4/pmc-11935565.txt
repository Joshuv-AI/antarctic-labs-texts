Personalized Heart Failure Management: Bridging Technology and Care
Authors: Ting‐Tse Lin, Jyh‐Ming Jimmy Juang
Published: 2024

Despite advances in neurohormonal and device‐based therapies for heart failure (HF) with reduced ejection fraction, hospitalized patients still face significant challenges. After discharge from the hospital, they have an ≈50% chance of readmission within 6 months and continue to experience symptoms that impair their quality of life and sense of control. This issue also affects those with HF with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF), which now represent most HF hospitalizations but receive limited benefit from treatments as effective as patients with HF with reduced ejection fraction. 1 Remote monitoring technologies are revolutionizing HF management by enabling care beyond traditional clinical settings. These systems, which use implantable monitors and wearable sensors to track critical metrics like cardiac filling pressures and lung water content, are particularly effective in reducing hospitalizations through early intervention. 2 , 3 Accurate and actionable signals are crucial, especially for high‐risk patients, where timely reassessment can make a significant difference. The COVID‐19 pandemic has accelerated the shift toward home‐based HF management, highlighting the importance of telehealth. 4 Multiparameter scores and algorithms, such as SELENE‐HF (Selection of Potential Predictors of Worsening Heart Failure), HeartLogic, and Triage‐HF, derived from cardiac implantable electronic devices (CIEDs), are useful in identifying patients at elevated risk. 5 , 6 , 7 However, most patients with HF do not meet the criteria for CIED implantation. On the other hand, by pooling data from the CardioMEMS Heart Sensor allows Monitoring of Pressure to improve outcomes in New York Heart Association class III Heart Failure patients (CHAMPION), GUIDE‐HF (Hemodynamic‐Guided Management of Heart Failure), and MONITOR‐HF (Remote Hemodynamic Monitoring of Pulmonary Artery Pressures in Patients With Chronic Heart Failure) trials, which included 1898 patients with HF, the meta‐analysis found that pulmonary artery pressure‐guided management significantly reduced HF hospitalizations by 30% but without a significant reduction in overall mortality rate. 3 , 4 Moreover, device‐related complications were still observed in 1% to 3% of cases, and the implantation technique remained particularly challenging. 3 In addition, pulmonary artery pressure monitoring devices, such as CardioMEMS (Abbott), were contraindicated in patients with recurrent pulmonary embolism/deep vein thrombosis, inability to tolerate right heart catherization, inability to tolerate dual antiplatelet therapy for 1 month, or full anticoagulation and body mass index >35 with chest circumference >165 cm. 8 Given these limitations, there is still a need for a simpler monitoring system that can be easily installed and is suitable for a broader range of patients with HF. Developing such a device would help ensure that more patients can benefit from advanced monitoring without the risks and complexities associated with current technologies.

Zile et al used a minimally invasive subcutaneous insertable cardiac monitor (ICM) to develop a novel HF diagnostic risk score. 9 By combining various physiological metrics obtained from the ICM, including subcutaneous impedance, respiratory rate, heart rate and variability, atrial fibrillation burden, and activity duration, the developed HF risk score demonstrated the ability to identify patients with New York Heart Association class II/III HF at high risk of impending worsening HF events, with a sensitivity of 73.7% and 68.4% in the development and validation data sets, respectively. The ICM–HF risk score offers a new tool for early identification of worsening heart failure, particularly in patients with mildly reduced or preserved ejection fractions who are ineligible for traditional cardiac devices. The current article by Kahwash et al 10 in this issue of the Journal of the American Heart Association (JAHA) assesses the safety of a predefined protocolized intervention pathway activated by the high‐risk alerts of ICM–HF risk score (Algorithm using LINQ Sensors for Evaluation and Treatment of Heart Failure, [ALLEVIATE‐HF] phase 1 study). It used a randomized interventional design, enrolling 59 patients with New York Heart Association class II to III and recent HF events. The study's primary focus was to assess the safety of a nurse‐executed diuretic intensification strategy triggered by high‐risk alerts from ICMs. Over a follow‐up period averaging 11.8 months, 146 high‐risk scores were recorded, leading to 118 interventions. Notably, the study reported no serious adverse events, underscoring the safety of this approach. Symptom resolution was achieved in 80% of symptomatic patients following intervention, while new symptoms developed in only 7% of asymptomatic patients receiving proactive care. These results highlight the potential of ICMs to identify and mitigate worsening HF before it becomes clinically apparent.

Kahwash et al demonstrate that a personalized, ICM‐based intervention strategy can be safely implemented, even in the absence of worsening symptoms. This approach addresses a critical gap in current HF management: connecting remote monitoring data with actionable interventions. They structured an intervention pathway, executed by trained HF nursing staff. The centralized implementation ensured consistency and minimized variability in care, which is critical when deploying new technologies in clinical practice. 2 , 11 , 12 The use of ICMs provides several advantages over traditional monitoring methods. By offering continuous, passive data collection, ICMs eliminate the need for patient‐driven data acquisition, reducing the burden on patients and enhancing the reliability of monitoring. Additionally, the integration of multiple diagnostic parameters into a single risk score allows for a more comprehensive assessment of HF risk, facilitating timely interventions that could prevent hospitalizations and improve patient outcomes.

There is growing importance of remote multiparametric monitoring for patients with HF using CIEDs. The IN‐TIME (Implant‐Based Multiparameter Telemonitoring of Patients With Heart Failure) trial, which used daily multiparameter telemonitoring in patients with an implantable cardioverter‐defibrillator and showed significant benefits. The primary end point, a composite clinical score including all‐cause death, overnight HF admissions, changes in New York Heart Association class, and in‐patient global self‐assessment, was significantly improved by telemonitoring (odds ratio, 0.63 [95% CI, 0.43–0.90]). Additionally, the trial found a reduction in secondary end points, including all‐cause death (hazard ratio [HR], 0.36 [95% CI, 0.17–0.74]; P=0.004) and cardiovascular death (HR, 0.37 [95% CI, 0.16–0.83]; P=0.012), driven largely by the prevention of worsening HF events. These findings suggest that patients with more severe HF may derive greater clinical benefit from remote monitoring. 13 These devices have evolved to monitor various physiological parameters remotely. These parameters can include heart rate, intrathoracic impedance, and respiratory rate, among others. The combination of these metrics into multiparametric scores has shown promise in predicting the risk of HF worsening, potentially allowing for early interventions that could prevent hospitalizations. 14 However, while the sensitivity of these monitoring systems is high, their specificity remains a challenge, leading to false positives that could result in unnecessary hospital visits. 15 Therefore, there is a need for clear clinical pathways and decision‐making processes when handling alerts generated by these devices. In addition, the integration of remote monitoring into clinical practice, underscoring the role of multidisciplinary teams in managing patients with HF effectively through CIEDs. 2 , 8 , 15

The management of congestion in HFpEF is crucial, as volume overload is commonly associated with functional intolerance and an increased risk of hospitalization and death. Studies show that intracardiac filling pressures rise weeks before hospitalization, indicating an opportunity to prevent decompensation with effective congestion management. 16 Loop diuretics remain a cornerstone of treatment, despite concerns about their potential adverse effects at higher doses. Data from the CHAMPION and GUIDE‐HF trials suggest that hemodynamic‐guided management using pulmonary artery pressure sensors can effectively reduce hospitalizations in patients with HFpEF. 4 Device‐based therapy for HFpEF focuses on early detection and intervention to manage worsening congestion, as HF events in these patients are often preceded by a gradual rise in intracardiac filling pressures. Ambulatory hemodynamic monitoring, such as with an implantable pulmonary artery pressure sensor or CIED, has shown promise in addressing this gap. 17

The prevalence of CIEDs in the HF population varies according to the severity of left ventricular dysfunction. In patients with a left ventricular ejection fraction <40% (HF with reduced ejection fraction), 46.4% had any device, compared with 28.6% of the patients with HF with mildly reduced ejection fraction. 18 On the other hand, the study pooled data from 3 major HFpEF trials: CHARM‐Preserved (Candesartan in Heart Failure: Assessment of Reduction of Mortality and Morbidity), I‐PRESERVE (Irbesartan in Heart Failure With Preserved Systolic Function), and TOPCAT (Treatment of Preserved Cardiac Function Heart Failure With an Aldosterone Antagonist), analyzing 8466 patients, of whom 682 (8%) had a pacemaker at baseline. 19 Due to the lower prevalence of CIEDs in patients with HFpEF, these individuals are less likely to benefit directly from CIED‐based HF monitoring. To address this gap, the use of implantable loop recorders provides a valuable alternative for continuous monitoring. Kahwash et al further demonstrates how the ICM–HF risk score, generated by Reveal LINQ, can be effectively used to implement personalized intervention strategies aimed at reducing rehospitalization rates in this patient population. On the other hand, based on the atrial fibrillation burden and heart rate recorded by the LINQ device, physicians can prescribe antiarrhythmic drugs and anticoagulants as needed. Additionally, procedures such as atrial fibrillation ablation and cardioversion can be undertaken to restore sinus rhythm. Atrial fibrillation is frequently associated with worsening left atrial and right ventricular dysfunction, which contributes to exercise intolerance and reduced functional capacity. This underscores the importance of pursuing rhythm control to maintain sinus rhythm. 17 , 20

In conclusion, while ALLEVIATE‐HF phase 1 provides compelling evidence supporting the safety and potential efficacy of an ICM‐based intervention strategy, several questions remain. The study's relatively small sample size and the absence of a powered control arm limit the generalizability of the findings. Future research in phase 2 of the study will be crucial in confirming these results and exploring the impact on clinical outcomes such as hospitalization rates and death. Moreover, the study highlights the need for further refinement in the technology and protocols used in remote HF monitoring. As the field progresses, it will be essential to address the integration of these systems into existing health care infrastructures and ensure that they are accessible to a diverse patient population. The success of this model in phase 1 of the study sets a strong foundation for the ongoing larger, blinded, randomized phase 2 trial, which will further assess the efficacy of this intervention strategy on a broader scale.
