Practical Considerations for Managing Transitions from Parenteral Prostacyclins to Oral Selexipag in Pulmonary Arterial Hypertension
Authors: Jean M. Elwing, Christina Benninger, Paul Strachan, Korawin Triyasakorn, Marla Choren, B. Kody Timms, Lori Reed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The prostacyclin pathway represents a foundational pathway in the treatment of patients with pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH). Parenteral prostacyclin pathway agents (PPAs) are often used for treatment of severe PAH but their broader use is limited by administration difficulties, including a need for continuous infusion, administration side effects, and complexities of a chronic indwelling catheter. Administration of the prostacyclin receptor agonist selexipag, an oral therapy with more than 10 years’ clinical experience, may be more favorable for some patients. However, treatment protocols for transitioning from parenteral PPAs to oral selexipag are lacking. Here, we describe practical considerations for managing such transitions. The transition process requires an individualized and shared decision-making approach between the patient and multidisciplinary team to optimize treatment outcomes. Key considerations include patient selection criteria, setting expectations, transition setting (inpatient vs outpatient), transition speed, dose adjustment, and clinical follow-up. Appropriate patient candidates for transition to oral selexipag should be clinically stable and selected based on a comprehensive evaluation using validated risk scores and hemodynamic parameters. Expectations and realistic treatment goals should be discussed collaboratively and must include the possibility of returning to parenteral PPAs if clinically indicated. Transition protocols should be flexible and tailored to each patient. Outpatient transition may be considered depending on transition speed, the patient’s ability to follow dosing instructions, and access to emergency care if needed. Speed of the transition to oral selexipag varies based on individual patient needs. Close monitoring and long-term follow-up are essential following transitions to maximize success and patient safety. In summary, transitioning from parenteral PPAs to oral selexipag can be challenging and complex. However, success is achievable for suitable patients through careful planning, setting transparent expectations, and an individualized approach with close monitoring and long-term follow-up.Graphical abstract available for this article.Graphical Abstract

Commentary

Pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH) is a rare, progressive, chronic disease; in the USA, it has an estimated incidence of 14 cases per million [1]. Characterized by elevated pressure in the pulmonary arteries due to obstructive changes and narrowing, PAH can result in serious complications, including progression to right heart failure [2]. The prostacyclin pathway plays a central role in the pathophysiology of PAH [3]. With more than 30 years of clinical use [4], prostacyclin pathway agents (PPAs), including parenteral, inhaled, and oral options [5], have a key part to play in the management of PAH.

Based on long-term outcomes data in PAH, guidelines support parenteral PPAs for high-risk patients, or for those who remain intermediate-high risk despite initial combination therapy [5]. Although some parenteral PPAs may be more potent vasodilators than non-parenteral options [6], parenteral PPA use is limited by various factors and not all patients are appropriate candidates. Treatment is predominantly administered at home via ambulatory infusion pumps, with dosing adjusted incrementally according to clinical response, side effects, and symptoms. Patients and/or their care partners must have the cognition, dexterity, and vision that allow them to safely maintain access and manage parenteral PPA administration, including preparing medications, programming and priming infusion pumps, and changing dressings and intravenous (IV) tubing. Close monitoring and management are required, and adequate support (both care partner and nursing) must be in place. Among patients suitable for and offered parenteral PPAs, many refuse or delay treatment [7]. Patients’ concerns may center around the impact of parenteral PPAs on their quality of life. These concerns include the logistical and psychological burdens of continuous infusion on daily living activities, worry about managing administration-related side effects (e.g., injection-site pain and local infections associated with subcutaneous [SC] PPAs), fear of delivery system complications (e.g., pump malfunction and problems with central venous catheters, tubing, and infusion site), apprehension about the complexities of living with a chronic indwelling catheter for those on IV PPAs (e.g., catheter obstruction, catheter-associated bloodstream infections, thrombosis, and risk of abrupt loss of access), and anxiety about how to obtain specialized help in the event of problems [5, 7–9]. Even within the inpatient setting, parenteral PPA infusion can be challenging; errors are not uncommon and are associated with considerable safety risk, including hypotension or even death [10]. Together, these factors may warrant transitioning from an IV or SC PPA to an alternative option, such as an inhaled or oral PPA. Transition between PAH medications is a complex process that requires individualization and shared decision-making with patients and healthcare providers [11]. The 7th World Symposium on Pulmonary Hypertension highlighted transition strategies in PAH as an area for ongoing exploration [11]. Although transition to an inhaled PPA is an option [12–15], this commentary focuses on transitioning from parenteral PPAs to the oral prostacyclin receptor agonist selexipag.

Selexipag, approved by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) in 2015 [16], is indicated for the treatment of PAH (World Health Organization [WHO] Group 1) to delay disease progression and reduce the risk of PAH-related hospitalization [16]. Oral selexipag provides an alternative option for targeting the prostacyclin pathway without the need for continuous infusion and central venous access, which may simplify care and improve convenience for some patients. GRIPHON—the largest PAH clinical trial to date—has provided insights into the clinical effectiveness and use of oral selexipag [17], but the study population excluded patients receiving prostacyclin analogues. Therefore, the study findings do not provide guidance on transitioning from parenteral PPAs to oral selexipag.

Patients transition from parenteral PPAs to oral selexipag for several reasons, including patient preference, intolerable side effects, line complications (e.g., infection) or vein stenoses for those on IV PPAs, and injection-site pain for those on SC PPAs. Some transition to improve adherence through a simpler administration route. Multiple case studies and small studies indicate that carefully selected, clinically stable patients can be transitioned from parenteral PPAs to oral selexipag with acceptable tolerability and maintenance of functional status [18]; however, treatment protocols are still lacking.

A recent systematic literature review examined the published evidence on transitioning from parenteral PPAs to oral selexipag in adults with PAH [18]. It revealed a high degree of heterogeneity in approaches to transitioning to oral selexipag from IV or SC treprostinil or IV epoprostenol. In total, 44 publications were included, of which 31 were case reports/series and 13 were observational/interventional studies.

Across studies, patients were aged 19 to 78 years, and differed in risk status and scores, functional classes as classified by the WHO or the New York Heart Association, and comorbidities, highlighting the clinical complexity of managing PAH [18]. Reasons for transitioning were highly specific to each patient’s unique circumstances, including patient preference, administration route complications, and side effects. Transitions were performed in both inpatient and outpatient settings, often combining approaches for safety and convenience, and flexibility to allow adjustment to evolving clinical scenarios. Transition protocols varied widely and were tailored to each practice setting, individual patient needs, patient risk status and risk score, clinical status, and center experience. Oral selexipag dose titration generally followed the approach undertaken in the GRIPHON trial and FDA label recommendations [16, 17], with a 200-µg twice-daily starting dose followed by uptitration in 200-µg increments, though there was some variation according to individual patient response and tolerability of side effects.

While oral selexipag has not been studied as an alternative to parenteral PPAs in clinical trials, the evidence from a recent systematic review of the literature illustrates that transition to oral selexipag is feasible for some patients [2]. Indeed, 75% of transitions were considered successful. Additionally, outcomes were more likely to be improved when clinicians and patients collaboratively assessed safety, risks, and benefits.

Given the complexity of transitioning from parenteral PPAs to oral selexipag and the lack of transition protocols, there is a need for standardized, evidenced-based, practical recommendations that encompass guidance on shared decision-making, individualization of care based on clinical judgement and patient response, appropriate monitoring, and follow-up. Here, we propose practical steps and considerations for managing the transition of patients from parenteral PPAs to oral selexipag based on clinical experience and previous studies. This article is based on previously conducted studies and does not contain any new studies with human participants or animals performed by any of the authors.

Steps to Consider When Transitioning from Parenteral PPAs to Oral Selexipag

Managing transitions from parenteral PPAs to oral selexipag requires an individualized and collaborative approach with shared decision-making between the patient and a multidisciplinary team to optimize treatment outcomes. Frequent monitoring (in person and remotely) is needed, and transitions must remain flexible to enable adjustment as clinical scenarios evolve, including the possibility of returning to a parenteral PPA should clinical deterioration occur. Patient Selection

Candidates for transition from parenteral PPAs to oral selexipag must be clinically stable. Practically, selection is based on a comprehensive patient evaluation using validated risk scores (e.g., REVEAL 2.0/REVEAL Lite 2), key hemodynamic parameters (e.g., pulmonary vascular resistance and cardiac index obtained from right-heart catheterization [RHC]), and assessment of right ventricle (RV) size and function by echocardiogram (ECHO). Patients and clinicians should participate in shared decision-making to evaluate the safety and risks/benefits of the transition before proceeding, including possible transition failure and clinical worsening. There is a tendency for patients who transition to fall into one of two groups: (1) those who choose to transition (elective patients), and (2) those who must transition (non-elective patients).

Elective patients who choose to transition from parenteral PPAs to oral selexipag should be hemodynamically improved and stable, have comorbidities managed, have normal right ventricle function on ECHO, and be low-risk status. Patients with higher risk classification, more comorbidities, or more severe PAH may have potential for additional complications during transition [18]. Non-elective patients are those who may not have reached low-risk or intermediate-low-risk status and have been on current guideline-recommended parenteral PPAs. However, they may have experienced a clinical or social change that could necessitate the transition to oral therapy. Clinical factors that may cause concern for ongoing parenteral PPAs include the inability to maintain access, recurrent line infections, intolerable/unmanageable side effects (e.g., diarrhea, nausea, headache, hypotension, or worsening hypoxemia), or a change in cognitive or fine motor skills required to manage parenteral PPAs. Social factors may pertain to the patient’s support system with many patients receiving assistance from care partners to manage parenteral PPA mixing and/or administration. A change in a patient’s social support could limit their ability to safely proceed with therapy.

Expectation Setting

We have found that setting expectations is likely the most impactful step in achieving a successful transition from parenteral PPAs to oral selexipag. It is important to allow sufficient time for this stage and to provide adequate education to ensure patients have a clear understanding of their disease and risk status. The patient’s journey before reaching this point should be reviewed, including use of prior medications. This involves understanding a patient’s tolerance of and adherence to previous medications and any challenges related to access or continuity. This background is critical for selecting the most appropriate, individualized therapy for each patient. In addition to oral selexipag, all options for next steps should be discussed, including continuing or modifying parenteral PPAs, using combination therapy, and alternative SC or inhaled PPA formulations. Discussions should center around developing a transition plan that encompasses the logistics and practicalities of the transition setting (i.e., inpatient or outpatient), the transition timing and speed, all monitoring requirements (i.e., scheduled follow-ups and home monitoring), as well as potential adverse effects and planned strategies to manage them—including the possibility of returning to parenteral PPAs at any step of the process in the event of a clinical deterioration. Realistic expectations and treatment plans need to be discussed collaboratively with patients; it is important to consider patient preferences and to tailor each plan to individual needs while remaining flexible. Long-term expectations should also be covered. It is vital to review the roles and support provided by the PAH multidisciplinary team members so that patients understand who to contact for assistance with issues about prior authorization, questions around medication tolerance, and patient follow-up coordination.

Location (Inpatient vs Outpatient)

The transition from parenteral PPAs to oral selexipag can take place in the outpatient or inpatient setting. The choice of setting is multifactorial, including a hospital’s ability to admit patients for transition and patient safety considerations. Although the outpatient setting may potentially be more complicated with IV PPAs compared with SC PPAs, both IV and SC PPAs may be transitioned to oral selexipag in either location setting, with no notable differences in strategy based on catheter route. Instead, the transition is often driven by patient stability, dose, comorbidities, and patient or clinician preference.

The potential for outpatient transition depends on several factors: (1) speed of transition required; (2) ability to coordinate the uptitration of selexipag with simultaneous adjustment and downtitration of the parenteral PPA dose; (3) the level of at-home support to assist with monitoring; and (4) emergency care access, should it be needed. Remote monitoring infrastructure availability may also impact the transition setting. A clear plan needs to be in place to ensure availability of as-needed medications for adverse event management and rapid access to a 24-hour urgent care team and PAH specialist team. For patients with previous indicators of confusion around initial titration, or for those who decline hospital admission, it may be prudent to request increased specialty pharmacy visits and have additional touchpoints with the multidisciplinary team during outpatient transition.

The inpatient setting can be more appropriate than the outpatient setting for some patients who are transitioning. This may include those facing potential access barriers because of medical center distance, particularly the distance to urgent medical care. More rapid transitions are possible in the inpatient versus outpatient setting, therefore making it particularly suitable for patients with complex clinical needs. Transition plans can be clearly outlined for inpatient teams to follow, detailing close monitoring of symptoms and adverse events, safety protocols for clinical worsening assessments, and procedures for returning to previous dosing/medications as needed. The inpatient setting should also be considered for patients who lack social support at home (e.g., support with medication management, observation of symptoms or deterioration, transportation, and/or daily living tasks) and those who had difficulty or made errors during the initial titration, or if there is an expectation or previous experience of significant adverse events.

Transition Speed

Often in practice the speed of transition to oral selexipag varies based on individual patient needs. Typically, this transition is faster in the inpatient versus outpatient setting. Short-interval (sometimes daily) oral selexipag dose increases mean the maintenance dose is achieved quickly, with faster downtitration of parenteral PPA. In the outpatient setting, there is more flexibility in terms of speed when transitioning from a parenteral PPA to oral selexipag. Transitions can be performed quickly (i.e., over a few days) or take longer (i.e., several weeks), depending on what is appropriate for the individual patient. In both settings, it is helpful to remember that small changes in dosing are important for patient tolerance to minimize adverse events, and the transition speed chosen should reflect the patient’s motivation and any adverse events experienced.

Dose Adjustment

During transition from parenteral PPAs, patients should be titrated until an individualized maintenance dose of oral selexipag is achieved. In practical terms, this involves uptitration in 200-µg increments from a starting dose of 200 µg twice daily until the patient reaches 1600 µg twice daily or experiences dose-limiting side effects, at which point the dose should be decreased to the previous tolerated dose [16]. Oral selexipag uptitration simultaneously with parenteral PPA downtitration before its full discontinuation should be considered.

In the outpatient setting, regular follow-ups should be scheduled with healthcare support team members (e.g., nurse practitioner, advanced practice provider, or physician) to determine transition success and identify any adverse events. On the basis of this information, the titration speed can be adjusted as clinically indicated. Specialty pharmacy nurses can also be involved when patients undergo transition in the outpatient setting. From our perspective, they are central to the identification of dosing strengths associated with potential adverse effects and are well placed to offer adverse event management support.

In the inpatient setting, close monitoring of symptoms and potential adverse effects allows for a more rapid transition. Higher doses of oral selexipag can also be considered in this setting if patients have not reached their clinical goal by the end of the transition and tolerability is good; however, such use falls outside the FDA-approved dose range [16]. Some patients may start transition as an inpatient and continue their uptitration as an outpatient.

Clinical Follow-up

Clinicians recognize that close monitoring and long-term follow-up are essential after transition from parenteral PPAs to oral selexipag, to maximize success and patient safety. For patients transitioning therapies in the outpatient setting, symptoms, oxygen levels, vital signs, and weight should be monitored routinely and reported back to the PAH center. Patients should be encouraged to self-monitor vital signs and weight every day. During transition, ECHO, 6-min walk test (6MWT), laboratory assessments, and follow-up RHC can be scheduled as needed. In the inpatient setting, assessment of symptoms, oxygen levels, real-time vital signs, and laboratory assessments should be performed before discharge. A post-discharge ECHO should then be scheduled at a set time, followed by RHC if required. Continuous evaluation of treatment efficacy is imperative to assess disease control and identify any early indicators that therapy adjustment may be required. Transition failure can occur any time after transition. Ongoing routine follow-up and risk assessments may identify early warning signs of transition failure, including worsening right heart failure symptoms, worsening shortness of breath, elevation in B-type natriuretic peptide/N-terminal pro B-type natriuretic peptide (BNP/NT-proBNP), and worsening RV changes on ECHO. A key takeaway is to assess the risks and benefits of returning to parenteral PPAs as part of a shared decision-making approach between the patient and care team.

An effective strategy to ensure compliance and assess tolerability is to conduct an early transition follow-up review. This clinical reassessment can be conducted in person or as a “virtual” visit by phone/video, as appropriate. It would usually be scheduled 1–2 weeks after transition and be performed by the healthcare provider or support staff. This touchpoint can provide an opportunity to schedule removal of a tunneled central line for patients previously on IV therapy. However, adequate time should be allowed to ensure successful transition before venous access removal.

Once patients reach their individualized maintenance dose of oral selexipag and are no longer on a parenteral PPA, we propose that they should undergo an in-person risk status assessment, BNP/NT-proBNP test, 6MWT, and an ECHO—preferably within 2–3 months. RHC may be considered and offered to appropriate candidates at approximately 6 months based on clinical status and provider discretion. The target hemodynamic profile should be individualized for each patient based on the shared treatment goals of the patient and multidisciplinary care team.

Long-term follow-up should include continued monitoring, risk assessment, re-evaluation, and continued discussions with patients to identify educational opportunities to reinforce understanding of the disease state, treatment plans, and ongoing expectations.

Conclusion

Transitioning from parenteral PPAs to oral selexipag can be a challenging and complex process. However, based on our clinical experience, a patient’s opportunity for a successful transition is maximized through careful planning with a multidisciplinary team. Patient selection is key when considering stable patients for transition. When patients need to transition because of issues with parenteral PPAs, clinicians should discuss the associated risks with them and their care partners, including possible transition failure and clinical worsening. It is important to set transparent and realistic expectations—including the possibility of returning to parenteral PPAs if needed—through shared decision-making with patients and involvement of a multidisciplinary team to frame a flexible, individualized approach to transitioning. Close monitoring and long-term follow-up are essential for successful transition.
