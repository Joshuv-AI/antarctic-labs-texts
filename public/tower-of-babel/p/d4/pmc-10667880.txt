Combined antegrade-retrograde cystourethroscopic rendezvous procedure for the management of a severe urethral stricture during kidney transplantation: A case report
Authors: Hector Saucedo-Crespo, Anna Myrmoe, Kyler Hardie, Sena Uzunlar, Sujit Vijay Sakpal, Christopher Auvenshine

Introduction

The urinary tract rendezvous technique is a combined antegrade-retrograde endoscopic approach that has been utilized for complex ureteric and urethral strictures arising from several pathologies, ranging from trauma to radiotherapy-induced strictures [[1], [2], [3]]. However, the rendezvous technique has not been described in the setting of a complex urethral stricture encountered during kidney transplantation (KT). We describe a urinary tract rendezvous technique performed during a KT in a community-based hospital to manage an occult urethral stricture. This case report has been reported in line with the SCARE Criteria [4].

Presentation of case

A 69-year-old man with end-stage renal disease (ESRD) secondary to diabetic nephropathy was taken to the operating room for a living-related donor KT. Of note, he had a history of benign prostatic hypertrophy (BPH) and a high-riding bladder neck which were treated with transurethral incision of the prostate 5 years prior which helped resolve his urinary obstructive symptoms until he became anuric secondary to ESRD. After the living donor nephrectomy had been performed and immediately prior to KT, multiple attempts at Foley catheter insertion including the use of a Coudé catheter were unsuccessful past the mid-urethra. Our consulting urology colleague was unsuccessful at blindly passing a guide wire initially. Following, a retrograde flexible cystoscopy was performed which revealed a blind-ending of the mid-bulbar urethra through which a guidewire could not be passed despite multiple attempts. Retrograde urethrogram revealed one mid-bulbar stricture about 5 mm in length without passage of contrast into the bladder. Given our common goal, we chose to perform a midline suprapubic incision instead of a Gibson or Morrison incision which enabled intraperitoneal exposure of the urinary bladder dome, creation of a cystotomy measuring ∼1 cm for antegrade cystourethroscopy, and eventual renal allograft implantation with end-to-side vascular anastomoses to the right external iliac vessels and ureteroneocystostomy (using already created cystotomy without difficulty). Cold ischemia time was 4.5 h. Once the renal allograft was reperfused, an antegrade, flexible cystourethroscope (Fig. 1A) was advanced through a cystotomy on the dome of the bladder to the posterior urethra proximal to the stricture which was 1 cm distal to the verumontanum. Simultaneously, a rigid, retrograde cystourethroscope was advanced just distal to the brightly lit antegrade cystourethroscope. With the two scopes aligned vertically adjacent (Fig. 2A) and the light sources from each scope visualized to ensure the true route, a stiff 0.035 in. guidewire passed from the antegrade scope “pierced” the short segment of the stricture with minimal force and was grasped by the retrograde scope (Figs. 1B & 2B) which was then retrieved through the urethral meatus. With the guidewire in good position, the strictured urethra was dilated up to a 26-French Goodwin Sound (Fig. 2C) which enabled subsequent placement of a 20-French Council-tip catheter (Fig. 2D). As mentioned earlier, the ureteroneocystostomy was created using the same cystotomy. The patient experienced an uneventful post-operative course, voided without difficulty following Foley catheter removed at four weeks, and had excellent allograft function at last follow-up at one year.

(A) Antegrade flexible cystourethroscope seen introducing a guidewire through a cystotomy into bulbar urethra just proximal to the stricture while a retrograde rigid cystourethroscope is positioned to grasp it distally; (B) Grasper secures guidewire past the stricture and retrieves it out through urethral meatus; (C) Goodwin Sound dilator is passed over the guidewire through the stricture; (D) Foley catheter inserted over the guidewire past the dilated urethral strictured segment.

Discussion

Almost 100 years ago, a surgeon named Frederic Foley devised a flexible balloon-tip catheter that could be inserted through the urethra and into the bladder to drain urine for the treatment of urethral obstructions [5]. Today, approximately 30 million Foley catheters are used in the United States annually [6]. Urethral strictures are a common problem encountered at the time of surgery when attempting to insert a Foley catheter. In particular, older men over the age of 55 years are at an increased risk for urethral stricture disease, with an estimated prevalence of 0.6 % arising from etiologies that include infection, post trauma, lichen sclerosis, BPH and post-prostate cancer treatment [7]. In KT, external bladder drainage with an indwelling transurethral catheter is standard during surgery and is used to monitor urinary output after KT. When faced with a difficult Foley catheter placement, initial management options include the use of Coudé catheters. If standard placement technique fails, a 16 or 18 French Coude catheter may be attempted, or a rigid smaller caliber silicone Foley may be trialed. If these techniques fail, flexible cystoscopy is the next recommended approach. Passage may be aided by guide wire and sequential dilation using Heyman or Goodwin sounds or with a high-pressure balloon dilator. In the case of our patient, each of these techniques represented only retrograde methods of catheterization and each had been trialed with unsuccessful outcomes. Additionally, standard direct visual urethrotomy is an alternative technique for serial retrograde dilations but requires a wire crossing the stricture and could cause an increased risk for bleeding, urethral damage and recurrent stricture. A suprapubic catheter was the last resort option if the rendezvous procedure was unsuccessful. The authors felt the rendezvous technique ultimately offered a less invasive form of catheterization post-operatively compared to a suprapubic catheter. Nevertheless, risks associated with the rendezvous procedure may include bleeding, urethral or bladder injury, recurrent stricture, urine leak and infection.

A comprehensive urological assessment before KT inclusive of post-void residual measurement and cystourethroscopy is essential in patients with obstructive symptoms and/or history of urinary tract interventions. However, intervention for etiologies such as BPH and urethral stricture may be deferred until after KT in the oliguric or anuric patient due to high rates of stricture recurrence [8]. The combined antegrade-retrograde approach was first described by Bagley et al. to open an obliterated ureteropelvic junction with nephroscopy and flexible ureteropyeloscopy while Watson et al. presented the ‘rendezvous procedure’ to cross complex ureteric strictures [2,9]. To date, the combined antegrade-retrograde rendezvous technique has largely been described in the treatment of complex ureteric strictures more so than urethral ones [[10], [11], [12], [13]]. There are reports of suprapubic antegrade urinary tract access for urethral realignment or recanalization as an indication following traumatic injuries and radiation-induced urethral strictures in addition to its use for KT as described here in this case report [14,15]. Nirmalarajan et al. successfully demonstrated the use of the rendezvous technique using a radio-frequency guidewire to achieve retrograde urethral recanalization with simultaneous suprapubic cystoscopy in a 5-year-old boy with a posterior urethra disruption. Similarly, Lorber et al. presented a case of successful realignment of a complete posterior urethra disruption through the rendezvous technique by manipulating the light sources of the cystoscopes to achieve direct visualization and transversal of the urethra disruption. Furthermore, Drye et al. obtained antegrade urethral access applying the rendezvous technique via a percutaneous nephrostomy to manage a radiation-induced urethral stricture [3].

To date, successful application of the combined antegrade-retrograde rendezvous technique to overcome an occult urethral stricture at KT has not been described. In our illustrated case, we have demonstrated the feasibility and utility of a cystotomy for antegrade cystourethroscopy in combination with retrograde access to overcome a resistive urethral stricture in addition to its creation for an ureteroneocystostomy in KT.

Conclusion

The combined antegrade-retrograde urinary tract rendezvous technique is a feasible and safe technique that can help manage occult urethral strictures found at the time of KT. This technique is particularly useful given exposure of the bladder dome occurs during KT and can be simultaneous utilized with cystotomy for antegrade cystourethroscopy.
