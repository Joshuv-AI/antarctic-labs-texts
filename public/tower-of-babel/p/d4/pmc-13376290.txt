Nyap1 Redirects JNK Signaling to Couple Neuroprotection with Circuit Regeneration After Spinal Cord Injury
Authors: Wenyuan Li, Yi Li, Yong Liu, Xiaoya Wang, Feng Ji, Jinying Hu, Yingying Shen, Zhihan Guo, Xiumei Fu, Ying Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Spinal cord injury (SCI) causes permanent motor deficits primarily due to neuronal apoptosis and inadequate axonal regeneration, with the c-Jun N-terminal kinase (JNK)/c-Jun pathway playing a dual role in neurodegeneration and regeneration. Here, we identified Nyap1 as a spatiotemporally limited modulator that rearranges JNK signaling to shift its function from degeneration to regeneration. Time-resolved transcriptomic profiling showed Nyap1 was dynamically upregulated as a hub gene during the acute-to-subacute phase of SCI. Nyap1 was associated with altered subcellular distribution of p-JNK, with increased cytoplasmic retention, with overexpression enriching p-JNK in the cytosol and limiting nuclear translocation-sufficient to drive regenerative transcription without inducing apoptosis. This interaction was associated with modulation of apoptosis-related and regeneration-related signaling, including changes in the Bcl-2/Bax ratio, caspase-3 activation, c-Jun phosphorylation, and GAP43 expression, with these effects abrogated by JNK inhibition. In vivo, Nyap1 overexpression preserved thoracic spinal motor neurons, promoted descending propriospinal tract (DPST) axonal regeneration, enhanced lumbar motor neuron dendritic arborization and synaptic input, and reduced lesion cavity volume. These structural improvements were accompanied by better gait coordination, neuromuscular reinnervation, and translesional motor conduction. Our findings support a model in which Nyap1 is associated with spatial redistribution of p-JNK-related signaling, linking neuronal survival with circuit-level plasticity after SCI, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic candidate for CNS trauma.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10571-026-01749-z.

Introduction

SCI provokes a biphasic pathological sequence: an initial mechanical injury is immediately succeeded by secondary degenerative processes driven by ischemia, excitotoxicity, and persistent neuroinflammation, ultimately causing irreversible loss of motor neurons (MNs) and disconnection of descending motor circuits (Anjum et al. 2020). This secondary phase accounts for half of total neuronal loss after SCI, as pro-inflammatory cytokines and damage-associated molecular patterns release to enlarge the original tissue injury (Fan et al. 2020; Jiang et al. 2025). Despite decades of research aiming at extrinsic repair barriers including glial scar formation and myelin-derived inhibitory factors, clinical functional recovery still stays at suboptimal level, highlighting that the intrinsic inability of adult CNS neurons to reactivate endogenous regenerative programs is a major bottleneck in SCI repair.

The descending propriospinal tract (DPST) is an intraspinal descending pathway that links thoracic propriospinal neurons to lumbar motor circuitry. Among the remaining intraspinal pathways, the DPST-motor neuron (MN) circuit has obtained notable attention because of its extraordinary ability for adaptive rewiring. DPST neurons form intraspinal relay connections through collateral sprouting below the lesion site, which partially restores supraspinal control on lumbar MNs and shows a strong positive relation with locomotor recovery in rodent models (Han and Xu 2020; Deng et al. 2022). As the final output units of the motor system, MNs face a double challenge: they must survive the unfriendly post-injury microenvironment and dynamically integrate new synaptic inputs via dendritic and synaptic remodeling (Huang et al. 2021). Nevertheless, most central neurons cannot satisfy this double demand, which severely blocks functional recovery.

The JNK/c-Jun signaling axis serves as a key molecular node that governs the balance between neuronal survival and regeneration (Kawasaki et al. 2018; Schellino et al. 2019; Zheng et al. 2018). Sustained nuclear localization of activated JNK promotes mitochondrial apoptosis through upregulation of Bim and Bax (Putcha et al. 2003; Nijboer et al. 2013), whereas transient JNK activity characterized by predominant cytoplasmic retention with limited nuclear entry promotes cytoskeletal reorganization and axonal growth via induction of GAP43 and other regeneration-associated genes (Sun et al. 2013; Kawasaki et al. 2018; Coffey 2014). This functional duality hinges on the precise subcellular distribution of activated JNK—a regulatory layer that remains poorly understood in the injured CNS.

Here, we identify neuronal tyrosine-phosphorylated adaptor protein 1 (Nyap1) as a candidate endogenous scaffold capable of spatially biasing JNK signaling. While prior work established that Nyap1 modulates actin cytoskeleton dynamics via the WAVE complex during cortical development-supporting roles in neurite outgrowth and synaptic organization (Yokoyama et al. 2011; Wang et al. 2020), its function in the injured spinal cord, particularly in relation to stress kinase signaling, has not been explored.

Time-resolved transcriptomics identified Nyap1 as a dynamically upregulated hub gene during the subacute phase of SCI, a critical window when JNK signaling shifts from driving neuronal apoptosis toward enabling structural plasticity. Notably, co-expression network analysis placed Nyap1 within a module significantly enriched for JNK pathway components and regeneration-associated genes, we hypothesized that Nyap1 functions as a spatiotemporally restricted scaffold that biases JNK signaling away from mitochondrial apoptosis and toward pro-regenerative transcription in the DPST-MN circuit. Through an integrated strategy-encompassing transcriptomics, in vitro oxygen–glucose deprivation (OGD) models, and in vivo SCI models-we show that Nyap1 acts as a scaffold for JNK, diverging its downstream signaling outputs. Our results uncover a previously unrecognized role for Nyap1, it coordinates neuroprotection and circuit regeneration via spatially targeted kinase regulation, defining a unified mechanism for integrated neural repair and a promising therapeutic target for SCI.

Materials and Methods

Laboratory Animals and Spinal Cord Injury Models

Adult Sprague–Dawley rats of both sexes were used for the in vivo experiments. Animals were randomly allocated to groups, with sex distribution balanced using randomization stratified by sex. Adequate anesthesia was confirmed by loss of pedal withdrawal reflex. Following laminectomy at the T9-T10 level, moderate spinal cord contusion was induced using a standardized electromagnetic impactor (impactor tip diameter: 2.0 mm; velocity: 1.1 m/s; dwell time: 100 ms; displacement depth: 1.5 mm)(Wang et al. 2025). Injury consistency was ensured by standardized impact parameters and predefined postoperative exclusion criteria based on BBB scores. Sham controls underwent laminectomy only. Postoperative care included analgesia (tramadol, 4 mg/kg, intravenous, 72 h) and manual bladder expression until autonomous voiding resumed. The animals were randomly allocated to the subsequent experimental cohorts. Transcriptomic Profiling and Functional Enrichment Analysis

Before conducting downstream analysis, the raw sequencing data underwent strict quality control via Sangerbox to guarantee data integrity. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were pinpointed using the subsequent criteria: an absolute value of the log₂ fold change (FC) greater than 1.0 and FDR P ≤ 0.05. Visualization of transcriptional landscapes was achieved through volcano plots and hierarchical clustering heatmaps. Functional enrichment analyses were performed using standard GO and KEGG databases implemented in R/Bioconductor packages. The sequencing data have been archived in the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) under the accession number GSE304492.

Protein–Protein Interaction (PPI)

To pinpoint the key regulators in the transcriptional response, the up-regulated DEGs were fed into the STRING database (version 12.0). This was done to build a PPI network, where the minimum required confidence score for an interaction was set at 0.7. The resulting network data were visualized and analyzed using Cytoscape (v3.10.0). Hub proteins were defined as the top 10% of proteins ranked by degree centrality.

qRT-PCR

TRIzol was employed to extract the total RNA from spinal cord tissues. Subsequently, qRT—PCR was carried out on a 7500 Real-Time PCR System using SYBR Green chemistry, with primers specific to the genes (see Table 1). The relative expression was computed by means of the 2⁻ΔΔCT method. Cultures of Primary Spinal Cord Neurons (SCNs) and an In Vitro Ischemia Model

Primary SCNs were obtained from neonatal Sprague–Dawley rats as previously described (Wang et al. 2018). In short, the dissociated tissues underwent a digestion process. They were treated with 0.25% trypsin at a temperature of 37 °C for a duration of 15 min. Subsequently, the digestion was halted by the addition of DMEM enriched with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS). After centrifugation (1,000 × g, 5 min), cells were filtered (200-mesh) and seeded onto poly-L-lysine—coated dishes. The seeding density was set at 2 × 10⁶ cells per milliliter. Cultures were treated with cytarabine (3 μM, 3 days) to suppress glial proliferation, followed by maintenance in Neurobasal medium supplemented with B27 (37 °C, 5% CO₂).

Primary SCNs were transduced with AAV-Nyap1 or AAV-NC 24 h before OGD induction in vitro, and oxygen–glucose deprivation/reperfusion (OGD/R) was performed after allowing sufficient time for transgene expression under optimized culture conditions. At 9 days in vitro (DIV9), SCNs were subjected to oxygen–glucose deprivation in glucose-free DMEM under hypoxic conditions for 4 h, followed by 20 h reperfusion under normoxic conditions in complete Neurobasal medium. This timing scheme was selected with reference to prior spinal cord neuron OGD/R studies and was intended to produce a stable, measurable injury phenotype while preserving a window for assessing neuroprotective and neurite-regenerative effects (Freeman et al. 2015).

Experimental Groups and Viral Transduction

SCNs were divided into six experimental groups: (1) Control (untreated); (2) OGD/R + AAV-NC (transduced with empty vector, 6.5 × 10⁹ vg/mL, 24 h prior to OGD); (3) OGD/R + AAV-Nyap1 (transduced with Nyap1-overexpressing virus, same titer and timeline as AAV-NC); (4) OGD/R + AAV-Nyap1 + SP600125 (pretreated with 20 μM JNK inhibitor SP600125 for 24 h prior to OGD, followed by viral transduction); (5) OGD/R + AAV-shNC, transduced with a negative-control knockdown vector;(6) OGD/R + AAV-shNyap1, transduced with an AAV vector expressing shRNA targeting Nyap1.Both vectors were applied at the same titer and using the same transduction timeline as the AAV-NC overexpression experiments. (Li et al. 2022). Transduction efficiency was quantified as the percentage of GFP-positive cells among total DAPI-positive cells.

Assessment of Cellular Viability

Cell viability was assessed using CCK-8 and MTT assays. Absorbance values were detected at 450 nm and 570 nm for CCK-8 and MTT tests respectively. To check cell membrane integrity, Calcein AM/Propidium iodide staining was implemented, and the obtained results were observed and analyzed by fluorescence microscopy. All experiment steps followed standard operation rules, with appropriate incubation durations and control groups being set up in advance.

In Vitro Dynamic Neurite Outgrowth

To carry out assessment of dynamic neurite outgrowth, SCNs were made to receive transduction via viral vectors and then seeded inside 48-well plates at the density of 2 × 105 cells/well. At three days after transduction, real-time phase-contrast imaging was conducted through utilization of the IncuCyte ZOOM system from Essen BioScience. Three fields of view for each well were recorded, neurite metrics were extracted from individual neurons within each field, and neuron-level data were averaged across fields to generate one value per well. Each well was treated as the experimental unit for statistical analysis, and neurite outgrowth was quantified into the indicator of total neurite length per single cell by means of IncuCyte NeuroTrack software, with a number of ≥ 50 neurons being analyzed for every experimental condition.

Molecular Docking

Protein structures were prepared using the Schrödinger Suite (2019.01). Protein–protein docking with p-JNK (PDB: 3OY7) was performed using HADDOCK 2.4, incorporating known phospho-binding motifs as ambiguous interaction restraints. Top models were selected based on HADDOCK score and cluster analysis. The docking algorithm generated 100 conformational models, which were scored using the knowledge-based ITScore-PP function. Lower scores indicated higher binding affinity. Representative docking poses were visualized and analyzed using PyMOL (v2.1).

Anterograde and Retrograde Tracing

To trace the descending propriospinal tract (DPST), anesthetized animals received a stereotaxic injection of biotinylated dextran amine (BDA; 0.2 µL, 0.05–0.1 µL/min) into the T7-T8 dorsal segments (Deng et al. 2013). The needle was retained for 5–10 min post-injection to minimize backflow. Following tracer transport, BDA-labeled fiber density in anatomically matched sections spanning the lesion and adjacent caudal regions, including the ventral horn, was quantified and averaged per animal.

To label spinal lumbar MNs, CTB (1%) was injected into the sciatic nerve (Wang et al. 2018). Animals were anesthetized and placed in a prone position. The sciatic nerve was exposed via a lateral gluteal incision. CTB (1%, 2 µL total) was injected intraneurally at 3–4 sites along the sciatic nerve (0.1 µL/min), 5–10 mm proximal to its trifurcation. The needle was retained for 2 min at each site to ensure diffusion. For lumbar motor neuron morphometric analyses, multiple CTB-labeled neurons were sampled from each animal. Neuron-level measurements were first averaged within each animal, and the animal was used as the experimental unit for group comparisons.

For immunoprecipitation (IP), cell lysates were incubated with 20 µg of p-JNK1/2 antibody or normal rabbit IgG (isotype control) and 25 µL of Protein A/G magnetic beads overnight at 4 °C. Beads were washed extensively with IP lysis buffer. Immunocomplexes were eluted and subjected to Western blot analysis.

Cytoplasmic and nuclear parts were made from thoracic spinal cord tissue ± 2 mm relative to the lesion epicenter at 35 dpi, using the Active Motif Nuclear Extraction Kit from Carlsbad, CA, with small changes for solid neural tissue. The fractionation effectiveness was usually checked through detecting GAPDH, a cytosolic marker, and Lamin B1, a nuclear envelope protein. Both compartments were then examined by immunoblotting method for total JNK and its phosphorylated form p-JNK.

Immunofluorescence

At 35 days post-injury, the segments of the spinal cord underwent post-fixation in a 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) solution and cryoprotected in a 20–30% sucrose gradient. Transverse cryosections (14 µm) were cut and processed for immunofluorescence. After blocking, the sections were placed in a humidified chamber and incubated with primary antibodies (as detailed in Table 2) throughout the night at a temperature of 4 °C. After thorough washing, the sections were then incubated with secondary antibodies. This incubation period lasted for 2 h, was conducted at room temperature (RT), and was carried out in the dark. All antibody dilutions were empirically optimized for specificity and signal-to-noise ratio under our experimental conditions. Finally, fluorescence images were obtained using a confocal laser scanning microscope. For histological and immunofluorescence analyses, anatomically matched spinal cord sections were collected at defined rostrocaudal levels relative to the lesion epicenter. Predefined ROIs and consistent ImageJ threshold settings were used across groups unless otherwise specified. Analyses were performed blinded to group allocation, and measurements from serial sections were averaged per animal as one biological replicate.

Transmission Electron Microscope

Spinal cord tissues were placed to be fixed inside 2.5% glutaraldehyde contained in 0.1 M cacodylate buffer at 4 °C during one whole night, then underwent post-fixation treatment in 1% osmium tetroxide for 1 h, went through dehydration via a graded ethanol series, and were embedded into Epon resin. Ultrathin sections with 70 nm thickness were cut, mounted on copper grids, and stained using uranyl acetate and lead citrate before they were imaged on a Talos L120C TEM. G-ratios were calculated from myelinated axons located in the ventrolateral white matter which is adjacent to the lesion epicenter.

HE Staining

Frozen tissue slices were subjected to fixation in a 4% paraformaldehyde solution for 20 min at room temperature. Subsequently, a standard HE staining protocol was implemented. After staining, the sections underwent a dehydration process using a series of graded ethanol solutions. Following dehydration, the sections were cleared in xylene and then mounted with a neutral resin. To capture images of the stained sections, a panoramic tissue quantitative system was employed.

LFB Staining

Sections were incubated in preheated LFB staining solution A (65 °C, 4 h), followed by rapid differentiated in 0.05% lithium carbonate (Solution B) and 70% ethanol (Solution C). Myelin-preserved area was quantified within the white matter ROI using standardized thresholding criteria in anatomically matched sections by a blinded investigator.

For histological and immunofluorescence analyses, anatomically matched spinal cord sections were selected at defined rostrocaudal distances relative to the lesion epicenter. Regions of interest (ROIs) were predefined according to anatomical or lesion boundaries and applied consistently across groups. Quantification was performed in ImageJ using identical thresholding settings within each assay unless otherwise specified. Cell counting and area measurements were conducted by investigators blinded to group allocation. For each animal, measurements from serial sections were averaged to generate one biological replicate.

Nissl Staining

Frozen sections were dyed using toluidine blue solution at 56 °C for 1 h, and differentiation was conducted so as to reach optimal contrast. After dehydration and clearing procedures, Nissl-positive neurons were quantified in predefined regions of interest from multiple sections per animal, and the mean value per animal was used for statistical analysis.

Acetylcholinesterase Staining

Cryosections were placed to incubate inside pre-warmed acetylcholinesterase (AChE) substrate liquid until light brown sediment came into being. Sections were given counterstaining with hematoxylin, and mounted by means of neutral balsam so that microscopic inspection can be carried out.

BBB Scale

Hindlimb motor recovery degree was measured by means of the BBB locomotor rating scale. The testing activity was implemented inside a soundproof room at pre-set time nodes. Each experimental rat was under observation for a duration of 3 min per testing process. Scoring work was carried out in an independent way by two investigators with blinded status; final scores for each individual animal are the average value from both raters, hence inter-rater reliability was verified through intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC > 0.85).

Inclined Plane Test

To assess anti-gravity muscular power and postural steadiness, the inclined plane test was implemented. Rats were placed in a head-up posture upon a plane whose angle was increased step by step. The maximum angle that could be maintained for more than 5 s without hind limb sliding over 5 cm or severe trunk deviation over 30 degrees was recorded. For each time point, the results of three trials were calculated to get an average value for that animal.

Rotarod Test

Motor coordination and balance were evaluated by means of an accelerating rotarod (3–30 rpm over 300 s, acceleration rate: 0.13 rpm/s). The test process stopped at the time when the animal fell down or when 300 s were reached. The length of time before falling was written down as recorded data. Each animal was required to finish three trials in one test session, The average value of the latency to fall for each animal was computed in the end.

Footprint Analysis

Prior to injury, rats underwent treadmill acclimation (5–10 min/day). For analysis, forelimbs and hindlimbs were stained with distinct colors. Animals traversed a white corridor, and footprints were recorded within each animal. Only sequences containing ≥ 3 consecutive, uninterrupted steps were analyzed for stride length and coordination.

Transcranial Magnetic Motor Evoked Potentials (tcMMEP)

Corticospinal tract integrity was evaluated via tcMMEP. Magnetic stimulation was applied to the scalp to elicit action potentials, which were recorded from the bilateral tibialis anterior muscles, reflecting the functional status of the descending motor pathways. The animal was treated as the experimental unit.

Statistical Analysis

The data are shown as the mean together with the standard deviation SD. For in vivo histological and imaging-based analyses, the animal was treated as the experimental unit. When multiple sections, fields, or neurons were sampled from the same animal, values were averaged within each animal before statistical testing. For in vitro imaging assays, the well was treated as the experimental unit, with measurements from multiple fields/cells averaged within each well. Statistical assessments were implemented by using GraphPad Prism 9.5.0. The Student's t-test was applied when we make comparison between two groups. When we handle multiple groups, we either use one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post hoc test after it or two-way ANOVA for repeated measurements. A level of statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Using strict standards (|log₂ fold change|> 1, FDR-adjusted P < 0.05), we found 2,584 differentially expressed genes at 7 dpi, this number is higher than that at 3 dpi and 14 dpi, thus highlighting a transcription activity peak during early tissue reorganization (Fig. 1A-C). Unsupervised hierarchical clustering of top 100 DEGs with most variability clearly separated sham samples from all SCI time points and sorted out transcriptomic landscape’s progressive evolution; an acute inflammation and stress-response profile at 3–7 dpi changed to a more adaptive, repair-related signature by 14 dpi (Fig. 1D-F). It is notable that Nyap1 shows continuous up-regulation across all three post-injury time points, and its expression levels are higher compared with sham controls. Under strict limit values, 114 DEGs were continuously upregulated across all time points, defining a core set of transcriptional responses (Fig. 1G, H). For the purpose of prioritizing key candidate genes, we built a protein–protein interaction network by use of the STRING database (Fig. 1I), which displays a tightly connected module that is rich in immune signaling and synaptic regulation. Nyap1 Promotes Neurite Outgrowth and Reduces Apoptosis Through the JNK/c-Jun Signaling Axis

To characterize the dynamics of Nyap1-mediated neurite regeneration, we performed time-lapse imaging of SCNs using the IncuCyte ZOOM system over 4 days post-OGD/R (Fig. 3A). Control neurons displayed progressive neurite outgrowth, while OGD/R significantly suppressed both branching complexity and total neurite length. AAV-Nyap1 overexpression rescued these deficits, enhancing branch points (branch points: F(12,140) = 17, P < 0.001; Fig. 3B) and neurite length (F(12,140) = 23.39, P < 0.001; Fig. 3C). These effects were fully abrogated upon SP600125 (P < 0.001), indicating a JNK-dependent mechanism.

Western blot analysis confirmed robust Nyap1 overexpression (F(3,8) = 29.20, P < 0.01; Fig. 3D, E), accompanied by engagement of the JNK–c-Jun signaling axis. Phosphorylation of JNK1/2 (F(3,8) = 78.49, P < 0.05) and its downstream target c-Jun (F(3,8) = 59.70, P < 0.001; Fig. 3F,G). SP600125 pretreatment suppressed this phosphorylation, confirming pathway specificity. Nyap1 overexpression also upregulated growth-associated protein 43 (GAP43) (F(3,8) = 58.22, P < 0.05; Fig. 3H), a well-established transcriptional target of c-Jun and a key effector of axonal regrowth in CNS injury models (Sanna et al. 2020; Wang et al. 2025). This prediction was verified through experiments: following OGD/R, co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assays have shown that endogenous Nyap1 and p-JNK1/2 have a physical association in SCNs (Fig. 3J). These findings suggest that Nyap1-mediated neurite outgrowth is associated with p-JNK interaction and downstream c-Jun/GAP43 signaling.

Discussion

The JNK pathway exerts a context-dependent duality in SCI, sustained nuclear accumulation of activated JNK drives mitochondrial apoptosis and neuronal loss (Nijboer et al. 2013; Huang et al. 2022), whereas transient signaling with limited nuclear entry supports axonal sprouting and synaptic plasticity (Zheng et al. 2018; Morel et al. 2018). This condition-dependent dual character has obstructed therapeutic targeting of JNK. A critical unresolved question is whether endogenous regulators exist that can spatially or temporally bias JNK output toward repair. Here, our data support a model in which Nyap1 functions as a spatial regulator of JNK signaling in injured spinal neurons, potentially biasing stress-kinase output toward a more neuroprotective and plasticity-permissive state. By coupling neuroprotection with circuit-level plasticity through spatially resolved kinase control, Nyap1 may represent a candidate mechanism linking neuronal survival with circuit-level remodeling after SCI.

Time-resolved transcriptomic analysis positions Nyap1 within the transition from acute injury signaling to subacute reparative plasticity, supporting its candidacy as a context-dependent regulator of post-SCI remodeling. Moreover, weighted gene co-expression network analysis put Nyap1 into the top hub genes in a recovery-related module that is rich in axon guidance, synaptic assembly, and cytoskeleton reconstruction—processes necessary for circuit rewiring. Although NYAP family proteins are traditionally related to developmental neuritogenesis via actin cytoskeleton regulation (Wang et al. 2020; Yokoyama et al. 2011), our data indicate that Nyap1 is reused in the injured adult CNS to connect with stress-responsive kinase cascades. These results place Nyap1 not only as a passive marker of injury reaction, but also as a time-adjusted regulator whose expression changes match the functional switch of JNK output.

To our knowledge, Nyap1 has not previously been linked to JNK signaling in injured neural tissue. Our work now reveals that Nyap1 redirects the canonical JNK/c-Jun stress pathway-not by altering its intrinsic activity, but by biasing its subcellular distribution-thereby shifting the balance from a pro-apoptotic response toward a pro-regenerative state. Pharmacological inhibition of JNK by SP600125 removed both the neuroprotective and axon-promoting effects of Nyap1 overexpression. These findings support the involvement of JNK/c-Jun-related signaling in the effects of Nyap1. Co-IP assays demonstrated a direct physical interaction between endogenous Nyap1 and p-JNK in SCNs after injury, supporting Nyap1’s role as a putative scaffold. Given that compartmentalized JNK signaling decides neuronal life-or-death result and axodendritic architecture after injury (Coffey 2014), the results of subcellular fractionation of thoracic spinal cord tissue revealed that Nyap1 overexpression drives a pronounced accumulation of p-JNK in the cytosol—far exceeding its modest nuclear increase-without altering total JNK levels. This demonstrates that Nyap1 spatially redistributes activated JNK rather than amplifying its overall activity. This compartmentalization was accompanied by an increased Bcl-2/Bax ratio and reduced caspase-3 activation, consistent with attenuation of apoptosis-related signaling, thereby attenuating intrinsic apoptosis. At the same time, a different part of p-JNK moves to the nucleus in Nyap1-expressing neurons, where it drives continuous c-Jun phosphorylation and raises the expression level of regeneration-associated genes such as Gap43. Although we did not formally assess whether GAP43 is necessary for Nyap1’s effects, the concurrent upregulation of phospho-c-Jun and its well-documented target GAP43-both central to CNS regenerative responses (Wang et al. 2019; Sanna et al. 2020), which suggests that Nyap1 co-opts a canonical JNK/c-Jun–GAP43 axis. GAP43 likely acts as a critical downstream effector, translating Nyap1 signaling into structural changes in injured axons. This regulatory logic is in line with prior reports about FOXO3- and HO-1-mediated neuroprotection via JNK/c-Jun (Hassanein et al. 2023). This dual regulation—anti-apoptotic in cytoplasm and pro-regenerative in nucleus—resembles emerging principles that scaffold proteins decide the functional output of stress-activated kinases. While classical scaffolds like JIP1 promote JNK nuclear accumulation and neuronal death (Centeno et al. 2007), Nyap1 represents a previously unrecognized predominantly neuron-associated modulator that functionally uncouples pro-death and pro-repair arms of the same JNK cascade through compartmentalization. Our data place Nyap1 not as a binary on/off switch, but as a contextual integrator that reshapes JNK signaling dynamics to at the same time enhance neuronal survival and enable structural plasticity.

Nyap1-caused redirecting of JNK signaling pushes coordinated structural plasticity across a key intraspinal relay circuit, offering a material base for functional recovery after thoracic SCI. At the circuit level, these findings suggest that Nyap1-associated neuronal protection may be coupled to remote lumbar circuit remodeling after thoracic SCI, thereby linking local neuroprotection with distributed motor network plasticity (Du et al. 2024). The higher density of BDA⁺ axons in ventral horn matches the already known function of GAP43 in supporting strong regenerative ability of spinal interneurons(Holahan 2017). Meanwhile, retrograde labeling plus Sholl analysis proved that Nyap1 boosts dendritic branching and total neurite length in surviving lumbar MNs. This came with a clear rise in SYP⁺ presynaptic terminals near MN somata, implying increased synaptic connection. These results repeat earlier research that neurotrophic support can recover MN dendritic structure (Kabdesh et al. 2025), but importantly extend this idea by finding an intrinsic neuron-associated scaffold protein—Nyap1—that organizes both pre- and postsynaptic remodeling without outside growth factors. Critically, Nyap1 overexpression increased co-expression of c-Jun and GAP43 in regenerating neurons, copying a transcriptional mark closely linked to regenerative ability (Carulli et al. 2002; Kawasaki et al. 2018). These data show that Nyap1 links cell-autonomous survival with two-way structural plasticity, promoting DPST axon extension toward caudal direction and MN dendritic growth toward rostral direction. The resulting growth of possible synaptic contacts across lesion site suggests reconstruction of an intraspinal propriospinal relay circuit, a mechanism different from corticospinal tract regeneration or cell transplantation methods.

Our colocalization analysis indicates that Nyap1-associated signal is enriched in NeuN-positive cells, with relatively limited but detectable overlap in non-neuronal populations under the current AAV9-CMV delivery conditions. Consistent with this distribution pattern, AAV-mediated Nyap1 overexpression produced predominantly neuron-associated transgene expression and was accompanied by neuroprotective and pro-regenerative effects after SCI. However, these findings should not be interpreted as evidence of strictly neuron-specific transduction or as proof that the observed functional benefits are entirely independent of non-neuronal contributions (Pang et al. 2021). Although Nyap1 expression was predominantly associated with neurons, its overexpression was nevertheless linked to reduced phagocytic cell accumulation and decreased neuronal apoptosis in the injured thoracic spinal cord, suggesting that neuron-associated protection may secondarily influence the local injury microenvironment. Because secondary neuroinflammation after SCI is driven in part by neuronal debris and damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) released from dying neurons (Fan et al. 2020), the reduction in ED-1-positive inflammatory signal most likely reflects an indirect consequence of improved neuronal survival and reduced release of cellular debris and DAMPs, rather than a direct immunoregulatory action of Nyap1. Thus, while our data support a predominantly neuron-associated protective effect of Nyap1, they do not establish a cell-autonomous anti-inflammatory mechanism. They fully match an indirect mechanism: Nyap1 limits the release of damage-associated molecular patterns by inhibiting mitochondrial apoptosis, which is proved by recovered Bcl-2/Bax ratio and decreased caspase-3 cleavage, breaking the feed-forward cycle of neuroinflammation and degeneration. This decrease of pro-inflammatory mediators creates a microenvironment more helpful for axonal plasticity. These results are in line with rising evidence that neuronal integrity controls glial reactivity (Burda and Sofroniew 2014), and meanwhile, they extend this model because they find Nyap1 is a neuron-intrinsic regulator whose upregulation non-cell-autonomously shapes the injury microenvironment via maintaining neuronal viability.

The present structural and functional findings support the view that Nyap1 is associated with coordinated tissue preservation and circuit-related remodeling after SCI. In addition to limiting damage at the lesion core, Nyap1 overexpression was accompanied by improved preservation of myelin-related architecture and ventral horn motor neurons, suggesting a shift toward a more survival-permissive post-injury state. These histological changes were paralleled by sustained improvements in locomotor performance and electrophysiological readouts, indicating that the effects of Nyap1 are not limited to local neuroprotection and are consistent with recovery of functionally relevant neural connectivity. In this regard, our findings are broadly consistent with previous studies (Liu et al. 2010; Xu et al. 2021) showing that enhancement of intrinsic neuronal resilience can facilitate axonal repair and functional recovery after CNS injury. The present data suggest that Nyap1 may differ from interventions directed at single downstream effectors, as its actions are consistent with coordinated regulation of anti-apoptotic and growth-associated programs within a shared signaling context, thereby linking neuronal preservation with circuit-level plasticity.

An important limitation of the present in vivo approach is that the CMV promoter does not confer cell type-specific expression, particularly in the injured spinal cord, where both viral tropism and promoter activity may extend beyond neurons (Ageeva et al. 2026). Consistent with this, our colocalization analysis supports a predominantly neuron-associated distribution of the transgene-associated signal under the current AAV9-CMV conditions, but does not establish neuron-restricted expression. The beneficial effects observed in vivo therefore remain compatible with a major neuron-intrinsic contribution, while also leaving open the possibility that non-neuronal transduction or secondary alterations in the lesion microenvironment contributed to the overall phenotype. More selective expression systems and cell type-resolved quantitative approaches will be required to define these contributions more rigorously.

Several additional limitations also warrant consideration. First, although Co-IP supports an association between Nyap1 and p-JNK, the present study does not define the molecular determinants of this interaction or establish whether specific post-translational modifications of Nyap1 modulate binding affinity or signaling output. Thus, the proposed model in which Nyap1 shapes the spatiotemporal distribution of JNK signaling remains mechanistically incomplete. Second, JNK activity was assessed at the level of phosphorylated JNK1/2 rather than individual isoforms. Accordingly, although our data support the involvement of JNK/c-Jun-related signaling in Nyap1-associated neuroprotection and regenerative responses, they do not establish isoform-specific regulation, including the potential contribution of neuron-enriched JNK3 (Kim et al. 2007). In addition, because downstream analyses were limited to representative apoptosis- and regeneration-related markers, including Bcl-2/Bax, caspase-3, and GAP43, the involvement of other relevant JNK-dependent effectors cannot be excluded. Third, the present work focused primarily on the acute-to-subacute phase after injury. Because chronic SCI is accompanied by progressive scar maturation, circuit refinement, and long-term tissue remodeling, it remains unclear whether Nyap1 can modify established pathological states at later stages. Addressing these questions will require cell type-restricted loss-of-function strategies, more refined analysis of Nyap1-JNK signaling mechanisms, and longitudinal evaluation across chronic stages of injury.

Conclusion

In summary, our study identifies Nyap1 as a predominantly neuron-associated regulator linked to modulation of JNK/c-Jun-related signaling after SCI. The data support an association between Nyap1 overexpression, reduced apoptosis-related signaling, and a more plasticity-permissive structural response in both thoracic and lumbar spinal circuits. These findings suggest that spatial regulation of stress-kinase signaling may represent a useful framework for integrating neuroprotection with circuit remodeling after SCI. Although further work will be required to define cell-type specificity, downstream effectors, and long-term efficacy, Nyap1 emerges as a mechanistically grounded candidate for therapeutic strategies aimed at preserving neuronal viability while promoting functional circuit repair.
