The role of physiotherapists in acute post-stroke neurorehabilitation: qualitative perspectives from clinicians and stroke unit managers
Authors: Sagrario Pérez-De La Cruz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThe aim of this study was to explore and compare the perspectives of both physiotherapists and medical managers regarding the professional role and clinical contributions of physiotherapy within stroke units.MethodA qualitative study was conducted involving ten physiotherapists and five medical managers from stroke units. Participants shared their professional experiences concerning work performance and the perceived impact of physiotherapy on patient care.ResultsThematic analysis identified four key areas for physiotherapists: specific training, professional functions, treatment modalities, and the relevance of professionalism and empathy. For medical managers, the analysis focused on their perception of physiotherapy work, their understanding of factors that enhance treatment, and assigned functions. The findings reveal a lack of standardized treatment protocols (non-heterogeneity) and emphasize the need for strong interpersonal professional relationships and high standards of professionalism.ConclusionsThe observed heterogeneity in practices leads to inconsistencies in patient care. Furthermore, there is a notable gap in medical managers' understanding of the specific clinical scope and technical interventions provided by physiotherapists. These findings highlight the need for coordinated rehabilitation programs and a clearer definition of the physiotherapist's role in acute stroke units to ensure equitable and comprehensive care.Keywords: Healthcare; physiotherapy; medical manager; stroke, qualitative study

Introduction

In Spain, strokes are the second leading cause of mortality and their incidence in Spain is 187.4/100,000 inhabitants/year (European Stroke Organisation, 2021; Alonso de Leciñana et al., 2023; Díaz-Guzmán et al., 2012). This has a significant impact on the quality of life of the patients and their families, while representing a considerable health and social burden.

The Spanish National Health System’s Stroke Strategy and the regional plans of the autonomous communities have established organisational systems adapted to implement the advances in clinical practice based on the recommendations of societies and the available scientific evidence (Alonso de Leciñana-Cases et al., 2009; Alvarez-Sabín et al., 2017; Díaz-Guzmán et al., 2012; Kalra et al., 2005; Kotseva et al., 2019; Norrving et al., 2018; Powers et al., 2019; Alonso de Leciñana et al., 2014; Leys et al., 2007; Stroke Unit Trialists’ Collaboration, 2007, 2000; Thijs et al., 2009; Lindsay et al., 2008; MacKay Lyons et al., 2022). These plans include the creation of Stroke Units (SUs) as the most effective and efficient healthcare resource for treating stroke patients, as they benefit a greater number of patients while reducing mortality, dependency and the need for institutionalisation. The plans also include the formation of Stroke Teams (STT) in hospitals that have less geographical coverage (Hörer et al., 2011; Alonso de Leciñana et al., 2014; Intercollegiate Stroke Working Party, 2023). The effectiveness of these units has been supported by numerous clinical trials and meta-analyses, with the Stroke Unit Trialists' Collaboration (SUTC) being one of the most prominent; their latest update concluded that stroke units improve patient survival as well as their likelihood of recovery and level of Independence (Stroke Unit Trialists’ Collaboration, 2000, 2007).

However, there are notable differences between the various autonomous communities in the degree to which stroke care plans are implemented but it has been slow and uneven across the country.

Despite the increase in studies and systematic reviews that have analysed the effectiveness of rehabilitation (Alonso de Leciñana-Cases et al., 2009; Alvarez-Sabín et al., 2017; Díaz-Guzmán et al., 2012; Norrving et al., 2018; Langhorne & Ramachandra, 2020; Norrving et al., 2018; Stroke Unit Trialists’ Collaboration, 2007; Thijs et al., 2009), there is a scarcity of research covering the opinions and experiences of physiotherapists who carry out their care work in stroke units, There is also a lack of research on the knowledge that those responsible for these units have regarding their work or the functions that each one has, nor whether the rest of the professionals who comprise the stroke unit staff are aware of how applying physiotherapy treatment might affect the subsequent evolution of the patients admitted to the units.

Qualitative research of this type can provide valuable insights into the complexity of optimising therapeutic judgements and help inform the design of appropriate interventions. It is recommended that researchers collaborate with practitioners who have direct experience in these units in order to understand the key factors that influence appropriate and tailored therapy programming.

The Spanish National Health System’s Stroke Strategy and regional plans have established organisational systems to implement clinical advances based on scientific evidence (Alvarez-Sabín et al., 2017; Kotseva et al., 2019). These plans prioritise the creation of Stroke Units (SUs) as the most effective healthcare resource for reducing mortality and dependency (Norrving et al., 2018; Stroke Unit Trialists’ Collaboration, 2007). The effectiveness of these units has been extensively supported, confirming that specialised care improves patient survival and independence (Stroke Unit Trialists’ Collaboration, 2000; Intercollegiate Stroke Working Party, 2023). However, the implementation of these stroke care plans across various autonomous communities remains slow and uneven.

Despite the abundance of studies analysing the clinical effectiveness of rehabilitation (Langhorne & Ramachandra, 2020), there is a scarcity of research exploring the professional dynamics and perceptions within these units (Loft et al., 2019; Pindus et al., 2018). Specifically, more evidence is needed regarding the professional experiences of physiotherapists in SUs and the specific understanding that medical managers have concerning the scope and functions of physiotherapy practice.

Therefore, the objective of this study was to analyse the perceptions of both physiotherapists and medical managers regarding the professional role, assigned functions, and the clinical impact of physiotherapy within acute stroke units. By identifying these perspectives, this qualitative research aims to provide insights into the complexity of optimising therapeutic coordination and to inform the design of integrated rehabilitation programmes.

Materials and methods

Study design

The present study adopts a qualitative approach with an interpretive orientation to collect and analyse the data obtained (Ritchie et al., 2013). The data collection was conducted systematically using a combination of individual face-to-face and telephone interviews. In cases where a participant could not attend the interview in person, he/she was offered the option of having the interview over the telephone (Carter et al., 2014). In this way, in-depth and semi-structured interviews were conducted (Supplementary file 1); this fostered greater understanding and trust on the part of the participants, thus enriching the quality of their contributions and opinions.

The Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) checklist was used to design the present study (Ritchie et al., 2013).

Sample/participants

The participants in this study comprised physiotherapists working in the stroke units of several national public hospitals along with the managers of these units (medical staff). The inclusion criterion for participating in this study was that the physiotherapists were working in these units (full-time and/or part-time, together with on-site care and/or physiotherapy gymnasium work). Participants were recruited using a purposive and snowball sampling strategy. Initial contact was established through professional gatekeepers (key clinical staff) at participating stroke units who identified eligible physiotherapists and managers. Potential participants were then contacted via email or telephone to provide them with a detailed information sheet regarding the study’s objectives and the voluntary nature of their participation. Participants signed an informed consent form after being informed about the study. Out of eleven professionals invited, one declined to take part due to lack of time. No participants dropped out once the interviews had commenced.

The geographical location of the participants covered central and southern Spain. This study was approved by the Bioethics Committee of the University of Almería (Spain) under reference UALBIO2023/045.

Research team

The interviews were conducted by SPD, a physiotherapist and PhD, with experience in qualitative research. The researcher had no prior personal or professional relationship with the participants, which helped maintain neutrality. Participants were informed that the study was part of a research project.

Data collection

Data collection was carried out between September and early December 2024 in a participant-friendly environment, encompassing various locations across the country, specifically in central and southern Spain.

During the process, the interviewer maintained a neutral relationship with the participants to minimise bias or prejudice.

The interview guides were developed following a deductive-inductive approach. The initial structure was derived from the study’s specific objectives and a review of existing literature on interprofessional collaboration in stroke care (deductive). Subsequently, the guide was refined through a “brainstorming” session among the research team and a pilot test with two clinical experts (inductive) to ensure the questions were open-ended and culturally sensitive to the Spanish hospital context. The underlying principle was to move from general professional experiences to specific perceptions of interdisciplinary impact.

The average duration of the interviews was approximately ranging from ten to twenty five minutes, depending on the depth of the participants' accounts.

Face-to-face interviews were conducted in private offices at the participants' respective hospitals to ensure confidentiality and a non-disruptive environment. No non-participants were present during the sessions. The sample consisted of ten physiotherapists and five managers.

Results analysis

Audio recordings of the interviews were transcribed verbatim, replacing personal data with ordinal numbering. The analysis of the recordings was conducted through the framework approach (also known as qualitative content analysis), allowing descriptive or explanatory conclusions to be drawn and grouped according to specific themes. Before establishing a thematic structure, the reviewer familiarised himself/herself with the interviews by listening to the recordings and reviewing notes taken during the sessions.

Data analysis was performed by two independent coders. Data saturation was reached when no new themes emerged from the interviews.

Framework analysis consists of two major components: creating an analytic framework and applying this analytic framework. These two major components occur through five steps: (1) data familiarisation; (2) identifying a thematic framework; (3) indexing all study data against the framework; (4) charting to summarise the indexed data; and (5) mapping and interpretation of patterns found within the charts (Ritchie & Spencer, 1994). While the primary themes were aligned with the interview guide (deductive), the sub-themes, such as the “economic burden of training” or the “hierarchical dependence”, emerged inductively from the familiarisation with the transcripts.

All the interviews were coded using ATLAS-TI 7.1 software and the codes were organised by theme.

Results

Ten physiotherapists finally participated in the study along with five managers from four different hospitals that have stroke units. The physiotherapists were part of these stroke units and each met the required inclusion criterion.

Regarding demographic characteristics, the ten physiotherapists (four female gender and six male gender with an average age of 39 years- standard desviation 2, 01 years-) had an average of five years of clinical experience in acute neurorehabilitation, with a distribution of 80% full-time and 20% part-time contracts. The five medical managers consisted of three neurologists (two male gender and one female gender) and two rehabilitation physicians (one male gender and one female gender), with a mean age of 47.6 years and a standard deviation of 3.1 years, all with over eight years of experience managing Stroke Units in the central and southern regions of Spain. Physiotherapist´s opinion

Professional barriers to specialised neurorehabilitation training

A predominant finding was the perceived gap between institutional requirements and available specialised training. Participants highlighted that while Stroke Units demand high-level expertise, the economic and personal burden of acquiring specific neurorehabilitation skills (e.g., Bobath or Perfetti concepts) falls primarily on the individual clinician rather than the healthcare system.

Three of the participants are trained in therapies or techniques specific to treating adult neurological patients, such as training based on the Bobath or Perfetti Concept.

“I have extensive training in adult neurological pathology” (he does not specify the specific training, but this professional has dedicated part of his professional work to training therapists) (P 2).

“I have done Bobath training for adults, with several courses and updates. I have also done Perfetti” (P 3).

Others, on the other hand, are trained in different therapeutic options that are not indicated as neurological therapy although the participants recognise that they apply them to their patients.

“I don't know if it is considered a specific training in neurology, but as I am also trained in myofascial therapy, I apply it with some of my patients and I see positive results” (P 3-P 6).

Role perceptions: direct care and limitations

The team of physiotherapists is responsible for implementing personalised exercise programmes and specific activities that improve mobility, gait, balance, coordination and muscle strength.

These objectives must be worked on and incorporated into the professionals’ daily regimen. They must divide their working hours between direct patient care (the initial assessment, recovery process and provision of services), indirect care, management issues and family care and training.

All the study participants were clear about their care work but differed in how they organised their working hours. While two of them devoted their entire working day to direct patient care (P 1 and P 5), the other five professionals admitted taking some time out of their working day to cover the other functions attributed to their duties as health professionals.

“They hold us in high regard. The main role in these units is played by the doctors and nurses, who are monitoring the patient every 4 hours. The patient’s stay in the unit is short, and we start working with them after 24 hours. We are in direct contact with nursing and neurology, for any incident or query that may arise in relation to the patients”. (P 7).

“Care role. We may also make recommendations to relatives (occasionally) and even accompany neurologists during check-ups”. (P 8-P 10).

What they all admitted was that the management function was focused on completing the daily protocol they carried out (to inform the rest of the team) and none of them reported having direct contact with the family, which would involve health education work, such a necessary aspect when the patients are discharged and return to their home and/or centre.

The hierarchical nexus: professional dependency on medical supervision

Practically all of the participants indicated that, within the hospital centre, the physiotherapist’s role depends on the rehabilitation doctor, for the most part, and/or neurologist.

“Hierarchically, we depend on the rehabilitation doctor” (P 1, P 2, P 3, P 5, P 9 and P 10).

“The head of the stroke unit is a neurologist” (P 4, P 7 and P 8).

“Similarly, if a neurologist is needed, a cross-consultation with physiotherapy might take place, as it can with the nursing staff, but the patient must be assessed by the rehabilitation physician” (P 1 and P 9).

“The rehabilitation doctor will decide whether to refer the patients to a different health centre (medium-stay hospitals) or to their homes, thus considering them as outpatients, not allowing much time to pass from when they get home and when they continue physiotherapy” (P 2).

Therapeutic interventions: constraints in time and clinical scope

The average time that these professionals dedicate to each patient as part of their direct care portfolio is 30 minutes: “we have half an hour assigned to each patient” (P4); “the time we have is between 20 and 30 minutes, depending on the needs of the patient that day” (P8). Although there are several who distribute their treatment time in a variable way, with the centre giving them the freedom to do so, as long as we treat all the patients during our working day’ (P 1).

In no case did the treatment time extend to sixty minutes even though these are patients who require a high level of specialisation, many of whom have multiple pathologies or require different therapeutic approaches.

The techniques carried out by the physiotherapists in stroke units consist of: “mobilisations, motor activity focused on the hemiplegic patient, proprioception, balance and standing (if needed and if the patient is able). Respiratory (if needed, but normally this is not the case)” (P 5), but always adapted to the needs and capabilities of the patient at that moment: ’the treatment applied is work on rotations, trunk control in the seated position, while for the less affected patients, we propose activities in the standing position.

Patients who are more affected receive treatment that is more passive.

The respiratory issue is handled by another physiotherapist (we only focus on motor treatment). We leave time between both physiotherapists because of the effort involved in each therapy’ (P 9).

It is important to point out that the physiotherapists do exhibit differences in the techniques or therapies they employ, with mobilisation being the aspect they dedicate most of the session to, relegating the respiratory, circulatory, sensory and cognitive issues to a different level, or leaving the work to other professionals (either due to lack of knowledge or lack of time).

The most important aspect of their work: professionalism or empathy

The quality that the physiotherapists working in the stroke units unanimously consider to be the most important when carrying out their duties is professionalism: “both aspects are important, but if I had to choose, then it would be professionalism” (P 1).

Medical managers´opinion

Opinion of the physiotherapy work carried out in the stroke unit

Both the rehabilitation doctors (M 1 and M 2) and the neurologists responsible for the stroke units (M 3 and M 4) expressed support for the physiotherapists who form part of the teams, considering their work as “essential” (M1 and M4) and “very beneficial” (M 2 and M 3) for the patients being treated.

Knowledge about the role/benefits/actions of physiotherapy with patients that have neurological damage.

In contrast, there was no unanimity on this issue. The rehabilitation doctors were aware of the specific work that these professional physiotherapists carried out (although in a general way and without knowledge of the speciality involved in neurological therapy) because they commented that their work was limited to “mobilisation and preparing the patient for transfer to the ward” (M 2), the participating neurologists stated “we neurologists know little about the work that physiotherapists carry out. We know that it comes in handy, but little more. We trust that the work is done by professionals who dedicate their day to the recovery of physical functions lost after the stroke” (M3).

Functions assigned to physiotherapists as part of their care work

Managers indicated that physiotherapists are responsible for positioning, mobilisation, and trunk control. This aligns with international guidelines that emphasise early mobilisation as a core component of acute stroke care to prevent secondary complications and promote neuroplasticity. (Alt Murphy et al., 2025). In relation to the previous question, those responsible for stroke units (neurologists and rehabilitators) show their knowledge of the physiotherapists’ functions by stating “they are in charge of positioning, mobilisations, trunk control, etc. They are free to decide what is required” (M 3).

However, as mentioned above, these patients often have multiple pathologies, which means that their function is not limited to the motor area, but also to the respiratory area (necessary to improve their quality of life and possibility of survival by avoiding respiratory infections). Despite the clinical importance of respiratory care in multi-pathological stroke patients, participants acknowledged a lack of specific protocols. This absence of standardised procedures is noteworthy, as integrated respiratory physiotherapy is recognised as a key factor in improving survival rates and reducing hospital-acquired infections in acute units (Spruit et al., 2013). It is at this point they all recognise that “there is no protocol in this respect, and they are free to decide what to do, there is no restriction” (M 4).

Discussion

It is essential that the rehabilitation of acquired brain injury (ABI) is carried out in a multidisciplinary team environment. This means that specialist doctors, nurses, physiotherapists, occupational therapists, speech therapists, neuropsychologists and social workers, among others, should be involved (National Institute for Health and Care Excellence, 2013). It is crucial that all members of the team assess the patient to establish a common treatment plan, requiring intensive coordination between all team members, the patient, and his or her family (Díaz-Guzmán et al., 2012; Ministry of Health and Consumer Affairs, 2025; Teasell et al., 2009). Regarding the scope of treatment, our findings reveal a predominant focus on motor rehabilitation, often overshadowing cognitive and emotional domains. While physiotherapists in the analysed units prioritise early mobilisation, the integration of neuropsychological or speech therapy assessments appears less systematic. This discrepancy suggests that the multidisciplinary ideal (National Institute for Health and Care Excellence, 2013) is partially implemented in practice, focused more on physical stability than on a holistic recovery of the ABI patient.

The effectiveness of this holistic approach often hinges on the professional background of the unit’s leadership.The rehabilitation physician is aware of the therapies indicated in the rehabilitation process, but the neurologist is not (Stroke Unit Trialists’ Collaboration, 2000; Lindsay et al., 2008). This lack of knowledge may be due to the fact that neurologists, until quite recently, had no contact with physiotherapists. Our results reflect this gap, showing that direct treatment referrals often depend on which specialist leads the unit, which directly impacts the scope of the intervention. Specifically, our findings suggest a treatment bias toward motor domains, often leaving cognitive or speech-language needs as secondary priorities—a common issue when the multidisciplinary coordination is not fully horizontal.

This professional gap also extends to the timing of interventions. A relevant aspect in the relationship between physiotherapists and doctors is deciding the optimal moment to start clinical intervention. To date, most of the scientific studies available indicate that neurorehabilitation treatment efficacy increases significantly when started early (Bernhardt et al., 2016, 2017; Coleman et al., 2017). While the medical managers interviewed in this study expressed theoretical agreement with these guidelines, our data reveals a disconnect in practice: the commencement of physiotherapy is frequently delayed by administrative hurdles and a lack of fluid communication between neurologists and therapists. There is a consensus in the scientific community that rehabilitation should begin as soon as possible, the recommendation being that it should be started within the first 24-48 hours, and that it should be adapted to the particular clinical situation of each patient. Early initiation is defined as beginning treatment between 3 and 30 days post-stroke, as it has been observed that this approach is associated with a more favourable recovery prognosis (Bernhardt et al., 2017; Wang et al., 2022). In addition, it has been documented that an early start is correlated with a reduction in the length of hospital stay, which, in turn, helps to lower the economic costs; this is applicable to both moderate and severe cases (Tay-Teo et al., 2008; Teasell et al., 2009). Direct action includes simple interventions such as early mobilisation, adaptation of the environment and orientation to reality, as well as treating specific deficits such as neglect or aphasia in the cognitive field (Kwakkel et al., 2023; MacKay-Lyons et al., 2010; Rahayu et al., 2020). In the case of the professionals interviewed in this study, the medical managers agreed with the statements made by the scientific community regarding the commencement of physiotherapy; however, for administrative or other reasons, the first contact that the professionals have with the patient who has suffered brain damage does not always occur within the recommended time frame. In the dialogue resulting from the questions raised, the physiotherapists indicated the reality they have to deal with, where the lack of coordination between medical professionals affects the protocol and speed of action. This study highlights that different stroke units have different managers, with the rehabilitation doctor having a somewhat better understanding than the neurologist of the work performed by the physiotherapist - sometimes leading to functions being duplicated or excessive referrals, with the patient leaving the unit without having received adequate and necessary treatment for their recovery. Consequently, our findings are of significant interest to hospital managers and policy-makers worldwide who aim to transition from fragmented care to truly integrated stroke units. Given the accumulated evidence, high-quality specific training in neurological rehabilitation is required to ensure effective action. In the centres focused on in this study, the physiotherapists presented a disparity regarding their training, with few being specifically trained in therapies focused on adult neurological patients.

The relevance of these findings extends beyond the specific stroke units analysed. The tensions identified between neurologists and rehabilitation physicians, as well as the administrative delays in treatment commencement, reflect systemic issues common to multidisciplinary teams in various healthcare contexts. While this study focuses on the Spanish health system, these “siloed” professional dynamics and the gap between clinical evidence and administrative execution are challenges likely present in other national and international stroke care networks. Therefore, the need for integrated protocols and specific neurological training is a universal requirement for improving post-stroke outcomes, regardless of the specific geographical location.

Limitations

First, the data were obtained exclusively from professionals working in the central and southern regions of Spain, potentially restricting the study’s geographical representativeness. Second, all the participants practice their professional work in urban environments, where the quality of healthcare resources and care tend to be higher and the time in accessing such services is generally shorter compared to rural areas. However, the core findings regarding inter-professional friction and the prioritisation of motor over non-motor domains are likely applicable to any multidisciplinary ABI team, regardless of location.

The findings presented in this study underline the critical need for a coordinated approach to improve access to rehabilitation and care in the short-to-medium term. It is essential to increase accessibility to medical resources, improve interaction between professionals who care for patients in the stroke unit, and propose a specific training programme that can provide the best therapeutic response to the patient’s needs, specifically to a person who has suffered a stroke. Governments, healthcare providers, and communities must work together to create environments that favour this interdisciplinary work methodology, fully addressing the needs of both the patients and the professionals who care for them.
