Anti-FAP CAR-NK cells as a novel targeted therapy against cervical cancer and cancer-associated fibroblasts
Authors: Robert Polten, Ivana Kutle, Jan Lennart Stalp, Jens Hachenberg, Ann-Kathrin Seyda, Lavinia Neubert, Jan C. Kamp, Constantin von Kaisenberg, Dirk Schaudien, Peter Hillemanns, Rüdiger Klapdor, Michael Morgan, Axel Schambach
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe tumor microenvironment (TME) has a central role in many cancers, particularly by fostering an immunosuppressive milieu. Chimeric antigen receptor (CAR)-based immunotherapy displays a promising strategy to re-direct immune cells toward specific antigens, thereby inducing targeted cytotoxicity. The fibroblast activation protein (FAP) is overexpressed in various cancer types and has shown promise in CAR-based therapies. However, its application in gynecological cancers remains unexplored. This study evaluates the efficacy of anti-FAP CAR-NK cells as a targeted immunotherapy for cervical cancer and cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs). FAP expression was quantified on cervical cancer cell lines, primary cervical cancer tissues, and cells isolated from these tissues. Alpharetroviral SIN vectors were used to transduce NK-92 cells and primary cord blood-derived NK cells with 3rd-generation anti-FAP CARs. Immunohistochemistry and flow cytometry revealed high FAP expression on CaSki cells, cervical cancer tissues, and primary cervical CAFs. In 2D co-cultures with FAP-positive target cells, anti-FAP CAR-NK cells exhibited significantly enhanced cytotoxicity and elevated degranulation compared to control NK cells, with no observed effects against FAP-negative target cells. Primary NK cells revealed high cytotoxicity against cervical cancer cells with a high release of cytolytic enzymes. Anti-FAP CAR-NK cells also showed efficient elimination of cervical cancer cells and CAFs in 3D tumor spheroid models. These findings underscore the potential of anti-FAP CAR-NK cells as a potent therapeutic approach for cervical cancer and suggest broader applicability in diseases characterized by high FAP expression.

Introduction

Cervical cancer remains a major global health concern, with an estimated 661,021 new cases and 348,189 deaths reported in 2022, ranking it the fourth-most common cancer in women worldwide. Notably, cervical cancer was reported to be the most frequently diagnosed cancer in 25 countries and the leading cause of cancer-related death in 37 countries, highlighting the burden of this disease.1

Invasive, locally advanced cervical cancers are typically treated with chemoradiotherapy with or without immunotherapy. Here, checkpoint inhibitors like Pembrolizumab have become a therapy option for patients with recurrent platinum-resistant cervical cancers.2 Despite these treatment options, late-stage cervical cancers continue to present with poor prognosis and high mortality rates, with less than 20% five-year survival probability for patients with distant metastasis,3 emphasizing the urgent need for novel therapies to improve patient outcomes. Targeted therapies offer promising strategies with the potential to impact tumor cells while selectively sparing healthy tissues.4 Here, immune cells can be equipped with chimeric antigen receptors (CAR) to direct them toward cancer cells, facilitating their targeted elimination. Particularly, CAR-modified T cells have yielded promising results in treating hematological malignancies, including anti-CD19 CAR-T cell therapy against B cell lymphoblastic leukemia.5 Besides T cells, natural killer (NK) cells are an emerging alternative to CAR-based therapies due to their intrinsic anti-cancer effects. In addition, NK cells provide benefits such as a lower risk of cytokine release syndrome (CRS), MHC independence, and their potential for off-the-shelf cell therapies, making them an attractive option for CAR-based approaches.6

Compared to liquid malignancies, the development of CAR-T cell therapies has progressed more slowly for solid cancers, as these diseases present additional challenges. Among these is the tumor microenvironment (TME), which is usually composed of extracellular matrix, blood vessels, immune cells, and cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs). These components can secrete immunosuppressive cytokines such as TGF-β and IL-10, which hinder immune cell infiltration and dampen immune responses.7 Hence, one approach to overcome these barriers is to target cellular elements within the TME, such as CAFs, through CAR therapy. Here, a key target is the fibroblast activation protein (FAP), a cell surface protein highly expressed on fibroblasts of the tumor stroma that surrounds over 90% of human epithelial cancers, but with low or undetectable expression on normal fibroblasts and healthy adult tissues.8 Due to FAP overexpression in many cancers, several studies have assessed the feasibility of anti-FAP CAR-T cells.9,10 To date, only two clinical trials (NCT03932565, NCT01722149) have investigated CAR-T cells targeting FAP in tumors, but the official reports remain to be published. Notably, neither anti-FAP CAR-T nor CAR-NK cells have been explored for cervical cancer or other gynecologic malignancies.

In this study, we successfully generated and characterized 3rd-generation anti-FAP CAR-NK cells that were modified using alpharetroviral SIN vectors. NK-92 cells and primary cord blood-derived NK cells were engineered with anti-FAP CARs and subsequently assessed for their cytotoxic activity using 2D and 3D spheroid models. The results demonstrate that anti-FAP CAR-NK cells effectively eliminate FAP-positive cervical cancer cells and CAFs while sparing FAP-negative cells. These findings underscore the therapeutic potential of anti-FAP CAR-NK cells as a promising strategy against gynecologic malignancies and other diseases associated with elevated FAP expression.

Materials and methods

Cell culture

Cervical cancer cell lines (SiHa (RRID:CVCL_0032), HeLa (RRID:CVCL_0030), CaSki (RRID:CVCL_1100)), primary cervical cancer cells, and primary CAFs were cultivated in ‘complete’ RPMI-1640 medium (PAN-Biotech, Aidenbach, Germany) containing 10% fetal bovine serum FBS Standard/Good (PAN-Biotech), 1% penicillin/streptomycin (P/S) (PAN-Biotech), and 1 mM sodium pyruvate (PAN-Biotech). HEK-293T (RRID:CVCL_0063) cells (DSMZ, Brunswick, Germany) were cultivated in ‘complete’ DMEM medium, supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS Standard/Good, PAN-Biotech) and 1% P/S (PAN-Biotec). HEK-293T, SiHa and HeLa cells were passaged every 2–3 days using Trypsin/EDTA (#P10–024100, PAN Biotech). Primary cervical cancer cells, and primary CAFs were passaged using Accutase (#P10–21500, PAN-Biotech). NK-92 (RRID:CVCL_2142) cells were cultivated in complete RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 400 IU/mL human IL-2 (PeproTech, Winterhude, Germany). Primary NK cells were cultivated in ‘complete’ NK MACS medium (Miltenyi Biotec, Bergisch Gladbach, Germany) supplemented with 5% human serum (c.c.pro, Oberdorla, Germany), 1% P/S (PAN-Biotech), 500 IU/mL of hIL-2 (Proleukin®, Novartis), 70 ng/mL hIL-15 (PeproTech, Hamburg, Germany), and 1% NK MACS supplement (Miltenyi Biotec). All cells were cultured in a humidified incubator at 37°C and 5% CO2. Cells were routinely analyzed for mycoplasma contamination.

Primary cell isolation

Primary NK cells (pNK) were isolated from cord blood-derived mononuclear cells (CBMCs) derived from three different donors. Isolation was conducted using the human NK Cell Isolation Kit (Miltenyi Biotec) as described in the manufacturer’s protocol. In detail, CBMCs were labeled with a biotin antibody cocktail and, after a short incubation step, incubated with a microbead cocktail. Afterward, CBMCs were added onto columns of a MACS separator, and the flow-through containing unlabeled pNK cells was collected and then cultivated as described above. After isolation, pNK cells were screened for the expression of CD3 and CD56 using flow cytometry. Primary cervical cancer cells and cancer-associated fibroblasts were isolated from cervical cancer tissues that were surgically removed from the patients. Tissue samples were placed on ice and transferred to the laboratory, where they were minced with a scalpel into small tissue pieces. Afterward, the tissue pieces were incubated in Collagenase IV (1 mg/mL) (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) or Dispase II (1 mg/mL) (Roche Diagnostics, Mannheim, Germany) for 45–90 minutes at 37°C on a mechanical shaker. Dissociated cells were filtered through a 70 µm cell strainer (Sarstedt, Germany). Subsequently, the cells were seeded onto 6-well adherent plates (Sarstedt, Germany) and cultivated as described above. This investigation was approved by the Hannover Medical School ethics committee (Nr. 10948_BO_K_2023; Nr. 6090_BO_K_2018). Healthy female donor and cervical cancer patient samples were collected upon written informed consent based on the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Inclusion criteria were confirmed clinical diagnosis with cervical cancer, age >18 years, and sample availability.

Immunohistochemistry stainings

Paraffin sections were incubated twice with xylene for 10 minutes and then incubated in descending concentrations of ethanol (100%, 90%, 70%, 50%, H2O) for two minutes. Sections were incubated for 30 minutes with Tris (pH = 9, T-EDTA buffer #ZUC029, Zytomed Systems, Berlin, Germany) or citrate (pH = 6, citrate buffer #ZUC028, Zytomed Systems) at 98°C. Next, sections were incubated for 10 minutes in H2O, followed by 5 minutes of H2O2. After washing, sections were incubated for one hour with the FAP polyclonal antibody (ab53066, Abcam, Cambridge, United Kingdom) diluted in antibody diluent (#ZUC025, Zytomed Systems), washed afterward for 5 minutes, and then incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP) polymer (ZUC050/ZUC032, Zytomed Systems) for 30 minutes. After another washing step, a 3,3’-diaminobenzidine (DAB) solution (DAP substrate kit #DAB057, Zytomed Systems) was added for 15 minutes. Sections were then incubated for 5 minutes in H2O, and then hematoxylin was counterstained. The NanoZoomer S210 (Hamamatsu Photonics, Shizuoka, Japan) was used to digitalize the stained sections, which were analyzed with the Visiopharm software (Visiopharm, Hørsholm, Denmark) using the nuclei detection AI (brightfield) module to count DAB-positive and DAB-negative cells.

Vector cloning and production of vector supernatants

The ESC11 anti-FAP scFv11 was codon-optimized and the sequence was synthesized by GeneArt (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The anti-FAP scFv was cloned into a 3rd-generation (CD28, 4-1BB, CD3ζ) CAR in an alpharetroviral self-inactivating (SIN) vector plasmid.12 3rd-generation lentiviral SIN vectors (LV.SFFV.mCherry.pre; LV.MPSV.eGFP.pre) were used to label cells with mCherry or eGFP as fluorescent markers. To generate retroviral vector supernatants, 5 × 106 HEK-293T cells were seeded onto 10 cm adherent plates (Sarstedt). HEK-293T cells were transfected using calcium phosphate, 25 µM chloroquine and a split-packaging design.13 To produce alpharetroviral vector supernatants, 5 µg of alpharetroviral vector construct, 2.5 µg pcDNA3-α-gag/pol.co, and 2 µg phCMV-RD114TR were used. To generate lentiviral vector supernatants, 5 µg lentiviral vector construct, 12 µg pcDNA3.GP.4xCTE, 6 µg pRSV-Rev, and 2 µg pMD.G-VSVg were used. After calcium phosphate transfection, supernatants were harvested after 24 and 48 hours and filtered using 0.22 µm Millex-GP filters (Millipore, Schwalbach, Germany). RD114/TR pseudotyped particles were concentrated by ultracentrifugation for 16 hours at 12,259 xg. VSVg pseudotyped particles were concentrated by ultracentrifugation for 2 hours at 76,615 xg. Retroviral vector supernatant was stored at −80°C until further usage.

Retroviral vector transduction

To stably label cervical cancer cell lines and primary cervical cancer-derived cells with mCherry or eGFP, 5 × 104 cells were seeded on a 24-well adherent plate (Sarstedt). The next day, the medium was removed and fresh complete RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 4 µg/mL protamine sulfate was added and spinoculated with lentiviral vector particles for one hour at 37°C. Approximately 8 hours later, the medium was replaced with complete RPMI-1640 medium (without lentiviral vector supernatant and protamine sulfate). After 5–7 days, the transduction efficacy, indicated by eGFP-positive or mCherry-positive cells, was assessed using the CytoFLEX S flow cytometer. Cervical cancer cell lines and primary cervical cancer cells were sorted at the MHH cell sorting core facility for eGFP-positive or mCherry-positive cells. Primary cervical CAFs were cultured with ≥95% mCherry-positivity, but not sorted to retain viability. To transduce NK-92 cells, 48-well suspension plates were first coated with 24 µg/mL retronectin overnight at 4°C. The next day, retronectin was removed, and the cells were blocked with 2% BSA in PBS for 30 minutes at room temperature. Then, the wells were washed with HBSS/HEPES, the viral vector supernatant was added, and the plates were centrifuged for 30 minutes at 863 xg (4°C). Subsequently, the supernatant was removed, and 1 × 105 NK-92 cells were seeded onto the pre-coated wells and incubated overnight at 37°C and 5% CO2. The next day, NK-92 cells were transferred onto a new (non-coated) well and expanded. Unmodified NK-92 cell and CAR-NK cell expansions were 17.0-fold and 13.8-fold, respectively, over 13 days. 1.5 × 105 primary NK cells were transduced twice on consecutive days at MOI30 (titrated on NK-92 cells), supplemented with 1 mg/mL Synperonic F-108 (Sigma-Aldrich, Missouri, USA) to enhance the transduction efficacy. Mean unmodified pNK cell and CAR-pNK cell expansions were 27.2-fold and 34.2-fold, respectively, over 13 days.

Vector copy number determination

The QIAamp DNA Blood Mini Kit (Qiagen N.V., Venlo, Netherlands) was used to isolate genomic DNA (gDNA) from NK-92 cells as described in the manufacturer’s protocol. Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) was run on a StepOnePlus device (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA) using the TaqMan Fast Advanced Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Primers were specific for the woodchuck hepatitis virus posttranslational regulatory element (WPRE) and the polypyrimidine tract binding protein 2 (PTPB2). WPRE forward primer: GAGGAGTTGTGGCCCGTTGT; WPRE reverse primer: TGACAGGTGGTGGCAATGCC; WPRE probe: CTGTGTTTGCTGACGCAAC; PTPB2 forward primer: TCTCCATTCCCTATGTTCATGC; PTPB2 reverse primer: GTTCCCGCAGAATGGTGAGGTG; PTPB2 probe: ATGTTCCTCGGACCAACTTG.

Immunoblotting

NK-92 cells were harvested, centrifuged for 5 minutes at 300 xg, and then washed with PBS. After removal of the supernatant, cell pellets were incubated for 20 minutes in lysis buffer (pH = 7.5, 50 mM Tris-HCl, 150 mM NaCl, 100 mM NaF, 1% Triton X-100), supplemented with 1 mM Na3VO4, 1 mM DTT, and protease inhibitor (1x cOmpleteTM, Mini Protease Inhibitor Cocktail, Roche Diagnostics). Afterward, cell lysates were centrifuged for 15 minutes at 13,000 rpm (4°C), and the supernatant was collected for further analysis. A Bradford assay (Cat. #5000006, Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA) was performed to determine total protein concentrations. 25 µg of total protein was used per sample for separation via SDS-PAGE with the prestained protein standard (#P7719S, New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA) and blotted to nitrocellulose membranes. 5% milk in TBST was used to block the membranes for one hour at room temperature and membranes were then incubated with the HRP-coupled CD3ζ antibody (1:1,000 in 5% milk, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, CA, USA) overnight at 4°C. After washing, the SuperSignal West Pico PLUS substrate was added and incubated for 5 minutes at room temperature. The FusionFX (PeqLab Biotechnologie GmbH, Erlangen, Germany) imaging system was used to visualize the signals.

Flow cytometric antigen detection

The expression of cell surface antigens and fluorescent reporters was investigated using flow cytometry. Harvested cells were centrifuged for 5 minutes at 300 xg, and the supernatant was removed. Afterward, cells were resuspended in PBS containing FcR blocking reagent (1:50) (130–059–901, Miltenyi Biotec) and incubated for 10 minutes at 4°C. After another centrifugation step, cells were resuspended in the respected antibody diluted 1:100 in PBS and incubated for 30 minutes at 4°C. Subsequently, the samples were centrifuged, washed with PBS, and resuspended in FACS buffer (PBS containing 2% FBS and 0.4% EDTA). Samples were measured with a CytoFLEX S (Beckman Coulter Brea, CA, USA) or a BD FACSCanto (Becton Dickinson, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA) flow cytometer. The following antibodies were used for antigen stainings: anti-CD3-APC (OKT3, BioLegend, San Diego, CA, USA), anti-CD56-FITC (REA196, Miltenyi Biotec), anti-FAP-FITC (clone 427,819, R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA), anti-FLAG (L5, BioLegend), anti-CD107a-APC (H4A4, Miltenyi Biotec).

Degranulation assay

NK-92 cells were starved of human IL-2 for 24 hours before coculturing with target cells. Then, 30,000 target cells were co-cultured with NK-92 cells in a 1:1 effector-to-target (E:T) ratio in complete RPMI-1640 medium, supplemented with an anti-CD107a antibody for 1 hour at 37°C on a shaker. Then, Monensin (1:1,000, BioLegend) and Brefeldin A (1:1,000, BioLegend) were added for an additional 4 hours on a shaker. Moreover, cells were stained with an anti-CD56 antibody, fixed with fixation buffer (BioLegend), and measured using the CytoFLEX S flow cytometer.

Flow cytometry-based cytotoxicity assay

11,000 target cells (CaSki cells or primary CAFs) were seeded per well onto 48-well adherent cell culture plates (Sarstedt) in a total volume of 400 µL complete RPMI-1640. To discriminate effector from target cells, CaSki cells were labeled with mCherry, while CAFs were labeled with a red-fluorescent cell tracker (1:1,000, #C34564, Thermo Fisher Scientific) as described in the manufacturer’s protocol. The next day, target cells were co-cultured in different effector-to-target (E:T) ratios with effector NK-92 cells that were added in 400 µL complete RPMI-1640 medium, supplemented with 800 IU/mL human IL-2 (400 IU/mL final concentration). Cells were harvested after 0, 24, and 48 hours, washed with PBS, and resuspended in FACS buffer for measurement using flow cytometry. As a readout for cytotoxicity, the percentage of remaining mCherry-positive target cells was analyzed using the FlowJo software (RRID:SCR_008520), and the cell number was normalized to the 0-hour time point to calculate the fold change.

Live-cell imaging-based cytotoxicity assay

For 2D live-cell imaging-based cytotoxicity assays of cancer target cells, 5,000 mCherry-positive target cells (CaSki cells, primary cancer cells) were seeded per well in triplicates onto 96-well adherent cell culture plates (Sarstedt) in a total volume of 100 µL complete RPMI-1640 medium. The next day, targets were co-cultured in different E:T ratios with effector NK cells, similarly as described earlier.14 For 2D live-cell imaging-based cytotoxicity assays of CAF target cells, CAFs were labeled with a red-fluorescent cell tracker (1:1,000, #C34564, Thermo Fisher Scientific) as described in the manufacturer’s protocol. After 2–3 hours of incubation to allow for cell settling, CAF target cells were co-cultured with effector NK cells in a 1:1 E:T ratio. NK-92 cells were added in 100 µL complete RPMI-1640 medium, supplemented with 800 IU/mL human IL-2 (400 IU/mL final concentration). Primary NK cells were added in 100 µL complete NK MACS medium (final hIL-2 concentration = 400 IU/mL; final hIL-15 concentration = 35 ng/mL). Then, the plates were placed in the CELLCYTE XTM (Discover Echo, San Diego, CA, USA) or the Incucyte® S3 (Sartorius, Göttingen, Germany) live-cell imaging device, and images were acquired in two-hour intervals for 72 hours. For 3D live-cell imaging-based cytotoxicity assays, 1000 mCherry-positive target cells were seeded in 96-well round-bottom ultra-low attachment (ULA) plates (S-Bio, Hudson, NH, USA) and cultivated for 3 days at 37°C and 5% CO2 to allow for spheroid formation. To form mixed spheroids containing mCherry-positive CAFs and eGFP-positive SiHa/HeLa cells, 500 cells of each cell type were mixed to achieve a final cell number of 1000 cells/well and then cultivated for 3 days to form spheroids. Then, different E:T ratios of NK-92 cells were added in 100 µL complete RPMI-1640 medium, supplemented with human IL-2 to achieve a final concentration of 400 IU/mL. Finally, the plates were placed in the CELLCYTE XTM or the Incucyte® S3 live-cell imaging device, and images were acquired every four hours for 72 hours.

LEGENDplex assay

Supernatants of primary NK cells that were mono-cultured or co-cultured with mCherry-positive CaSki cells in a 1:1 E:T ratio were harvested after 0 and 72 hours. Supernatants were centrifuged at 300 xg for 5 minutes, and the supernatant was stored at −20°C. Cytokine concentrations were measured using the LEGENDplexTM Human CD8/NK Panel (13-plex) kit (Cat. #740267, BioLegend), as described in the manufacturer’s protocol, and data was analyzed with the LEGENDplexTM data analysis software (BioLegend).

Statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism 5 and 9 software tools (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA) were used for statistical analyses and two-way ANOVA with the Dunnett multiple comparison test. Error bars in all figures indicate the standard deviation (SD) of the mean. Statistical significance is denoted by *p≤0.05, **p≤0.01, ***p≤0.001. In the figures, ns = not significant and nt = not tested due to n<3.

Results

FAP expression on cervical cancer and CAFs

FAP expression was investigated on sections derived from a healthy tissue and tissues derived from three different cervical cancer samples. Sections of Tumor #1 and Tumor #3 are derived from invasive cervical adenocarcinoma (both FIGO stage IIIC) of a 48- and 37-year-old patient, respectively (Figure 1A, Supplementary Figure S1). The section of Tumor #2 is derived from a squamous cell carcinoma from a 55-year-old patient (FIGO stage IIIb). Immunohistochemical analysis revealed a high percentage of FAP-positive cells in sections of all tumor tissues (Tumor #1 = 79.9%; Tumor #2 = 70.9%; Tumor #3 = 85.8%), while the healthy tissue showed less FAP-positive cells (28.3%) (Figure 1A, Supplementary Figure S2). Cervical cancer cell lines (CaSki, HeLa, SiHa) showed a heterogeneous FAP expression by flow cytometry, as 74.1% of CaSki cells were identified as FAP-positive, but only 8.8% of HeLa cells and 2.9% of SiHa cells (Figure 1B, Supplementary Figure S3). Moreover, CaSki cells showed a 2.5-fold increase in the geometric mean fluorescence intensity (gMFI) after FAP staining, which was not observed for HeLa and SiHa cells (Figure 1C). Besides cervical cancer cell lines, we investigated primary cells isolated from tissues of 18 cervical cancer patients (CP1–18). Most of these isolated cells had a characteristic fibroblast spindle or stellate morphology (Supplementary Figure S4, S5A) and could be cultured for several days up to a few months, with variations between the samples. In contrast, cells derived from 2 cervical cancer tissues, CP5 and CP17, had typical cancer cell morphology (Supplementary Figure S5B) and showed continuous cell division over multiple months. CP1 cells had a cancer cell morphology initially, but were overgrown with fibroblasts during cultivation. Primary cells with a cancer morphology (CP5 & CP17) did not show FAP expression, while FAP was detected on up to 98.8% of cancer-derived cells with a fibroblast morphology, which showed a 3.2 to 11.5-fold increase in gMFI following FAP staining (Figure 1B,C). Therefore, based on their morphology, proliferation, and origin, they are considered as CAFs, while CP5 and CP17 cells are considered as cancer cells. Generation and characterization of anti-FAP CAR-NK-92 cells

Alpharetroviral SIN vectors were used to generate anti-FAP CAR-NK cells (Figure 2A). RSV was used as an external promoter in combination with an SV-40 enhancer, and MPSV was used as an internal promoter to drive CAR expression. The scFv was derived from the high-affinity ESC11 anti-FAP antibody equipped with a FLAG-tag to allow for cell surface detection. The scFv was linked via an IgG1-based CH3 hinge to a CD28 transmembrane domain. CD28 and 4-1BB were used as co-stimulatory domains and linked to the CD3ζ signaling domain. The vector includes an IRES-eGFP sequence that was used as a fluorescent marker. Another vector was used to express CARs that lack the extracellular domain (scFv and hinge regions) “ΔCAR,” which served as a control for potential tonic CAR signaling that is independent of binding to FAP (Figure 2A,B). Cultures of modified NK cells were assessed by flow cytometry to ensure high percentages of CAR and eGFP transgene expression in NK cells (≥90%) (Figure 2C). Three independently generated batches of anti-FAP CAR-NK and ΔCAR-NK cells were tested to exclude potential batch-dependent effects. As another readout to assess the CAR protein expression in sorted NK-92 cells, Western blotting was performed to investigate CD3ζ expression in all groups. Here, CD3ζ signals were detected at expected molecular weights that were in relation to the construct size, with anti-FAP CAR-NK cells showing bands at approximately 67 kDa and ΔCAR at approximately 38 kDa (Figure 2D, Supplementary Figure S6). In contrast, unmodified cells did not show such signals corresponding to either of these molecular weights. Endogenous CD3ζ (17 kDa) was detected in all groups and was used as a loading control. The average number of vector insertion sites in CAR-modified NK-92 cells was estimated using qPCR to determine the mean vector copy numbers (mVCN). An iPSC-derived clone with a known vector copy number of 1 was used as a positive control, while unmodified NK-92 cells served as a negative control. ΔCAR and anti-FAP CAR-NK cells were found to have mVCNs below 1.5. In detail, the three batches of ΔCAR-NK-92 cells had a combined mVCN of 1.03 (±0.27), while the three batches of anti-FAP CAR-NK-92 cells had a combined mVCN of 0.82 (±0.12) (Figure 2E).

Activity of anti-FAP CAR-NK-92 cells against cervical cancer cells

To evaluate the functionality of the CAR-modified NK-92 cells against cervical cancer cells, cytotoxicity experiments were conducted against CaSki cells based on the comparatively high FAP-positivity that was determined by flow cytometry (Figure 1B,C). Here, mCherry-positive CaSki cells were co-cultured with (CAR-)NK-92 cells in different effector-to-target ratios (E:T) (1:1, 3:1, 10:1), and changes in the E:T ratio were analyzed by flow cytometry at 0, 24, and 48 hours after the addition of effector cells. After 48 hours of co-culture, anti-FAP CAR-NK-92 cells significantly reduced the percentage of remaining target cells compared to unmodified NK-92 cells at all tested E:T ratios (Figure 3A). Contrarily, ΔCAR-NK-92 cells did not significantly reduce target cells compared to unmodified NK-92 cells at any timepoint or E:T ratio. In contrast to unmodified- and ΔCAR-NK-92 cell controls, anti-FAP CAR-NK-92 cells led to nearly complete elimination of target cells after 48 hours of co-culture at the 10:1 E:T ratio (Figure 3A). At all E:T ratios (0.5:1, 1:1, 5:1), anti-FAP CAR-NK-92 cells significantly reduced the red fluorescent target cell area (indicative of mCherry-positive CaSki cells) compared to unmodified NK-92 cells (Figure 3B). Live-cell imaging of mono-cultured primary CAFs derived from all five different patients showed a two-fold increase in the red fluorescence area within 24–48 hours of cell culture (Figure 4B). Upon co-culture, ΔCAR-NK-92 cells showed similarly low cytotoxicity to unmodified NK-92 cells. In addition to cytotoxicity assays as direct measures of immune cell-mediated killing, degranulation assays were performed to assess the surface expression of lysosomal-associated membrane protein 1(LAMP-1), also known as CD107a, on effector NK-92 cells following co-culture with target cells, including CAFs (CP2, CP3, CP6) and CaSki cells. Discussion

Challenges of the tumor microenvironment for immune cell therapies

Immune cell therapies against solid cancers can be associated with several obstacles that can limit clinical success. Most prominently, the infiltration and activity of immune cells can be decreased by an immunosuppressive TME, which can be driven by HPV oncoproteins in cervical cancer.15 The TME is frequently composed of tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), regulatory T cells (Tregs), and CAFs that can induce hypoxia and secrete immunosuppressive cytokines (e.g., TGF-β, IL-10), thereby limiting therapeutic efficacies.16 In detail, TGF-β was described to directly suppress effector functions of T cells, e.g., by repression of perforin, granzymes, or IFN-γ that are typically released to induce cytotoxicity against cancer cells and were also detected in this study (Supplementary Figures S14-S16).17 Moreover, IL-10 was reported to enhance PD-L1 expression on dendritic cells (DCs), contributing to immune cell exhaustion.18 Also, CAFs have been shown to suppress proliferation19 and affect migration,20 differentiation, and exhaustion21,22 of T cells. Similar effects have been described for NK cells,23 but CAFs can also downregulate NK cell function by influencing activating and inhibitory receptors.24 Beyond the tumor-suppressive effects of the TME, it can also directly support cancer progression. In cervical cancer, CAFs were described to exhibit tumor-promoting functions, e.g., cancer proliferation, migration, and invasion by secretion of proteins, lncRNAs, and miRNAs.25,26 Also, cervical cancer cells and Tregs were described to reduce IL-2 levels in the TME,16 thereby suppressing the proliferation and activation of NK and T cells.27–29 However, this inhibitory effect may be mitigated by equipping these immune cells with CARs that can stimulate their activation upon antigen recognition.

Potential modification of CARs to target immuno suppressive TMEs

In this study, we used alpharetroviral SIN vectors that encode for 3rd-generation CARs (Figures 2A, 7A). Compared to gammaretroviral- and lentiviral vectors that tend to integrate into transcriptional start sites30 and active transcription units,31 respectively, alpharetroviral vectors are associated with a more neutral integration profile, thus displaying a potentially safer approach for cell and gene therapies.13,32 While the 3rd-generation CAR-NK cells efficiently eliminated cancer cells and CAFs in this study (Figures 3–7), other generations of CARs might also be considered for future approaches. While most studies explore 2nd-generation (e.g., including CD28 or 4-1BB domains) CARs,33 3rd-generation CAR T cells have been associated with enhanced expansion, persistence, and cytokine secretion.34,35 However, increased cytokine production might be related to side effects such as a higher risk of cytokine release syndrome (CRS).36 This concern might be decreased for CAR-NK cells due to the lower risk of developing CRS based on their different cytokine release profile.37,38 Moreover, the profile of released cytokines can be actively complemented using 4th-generation CARs, which incorporate inducible gene expression cassettes that allow for the targeted release of cytokines upon target antigen binding.39 This approach is especially interesting when targeting cellular components of the TME, as it might enable the reprogramming of an immunosuppressive TME toward an immune-permissive TME. These versatile options to further improve CAR therapies offer promising strategies that need further investigation, particularly in the treatment of underexplored malignancies like cervical cancer.

FAP as a target antigen for CAR therapies

The expression of FAP in adult tissues was described to be mainly restricted to the tumor stroma, cells that undergo epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition, and wound healing processes, but is low or absent in healthy adult tissues.40,41 Our findings support this FAP expression profile, as all examined cervical cancer tissues revealed an increased number of FAP-positive cells compared to healthy tissues (Figure 1A, Supplementary Figure S2). Moreover, high levels of FAP were detected on the cell surface of all primary CAFs and CaSki cells, while other tested cervical cancer cell lines and primary cells had mainly low FAP levels (Figure 2B,C). This aligns with the characterization of FAP as a robust marker for CAFs, which is expressed in only a subset of cancer cells.8,40 The restricted expression of FAP in healthy adult tissues, but high expression in malignant tissues, combined with the observed specificity of the anti-FAP CAR-NK cells against FAP-positive cells (Figure 4B,C), indicates that this therapeutic approach may have a favorable safety profile. In a phase I clinical trial (NCT01722149) that investigated the feasibility and toxicity of anti-FAP CAR-T cells in patients with malignant pleural mesothelioma (MPM), no CAR-T-related toxicities were detected.42 Besides anti-FAP CAR therapies, several preclinical and clinical studies investigated anti-FAP targeted radioligand therapy (TRT) in end-stage cancer patients, with most studies reporting promising responses and only limited adverse events.43,44 However, as not all cervical cancer cells express FAP (Figure 1B,C), dual CAR therapy that combines anti-FAP CAR-NK cells with other CAR-modified immune cells that are directed against cancer-associated antigens might be an encouraging strategy to eliminate remaining target cells (Figure 6A) while reducing antigen escape.45 Here, a sequential treatment approach could be investigated, as an initial treatment using anti-FAP CAR-NK cells could be used to eliminate CAFs and thereby decrease immunosuppressive barriers for subsequent immune cell therapies. Interestingly, our mixed co-culture models revealed that the primary cervical CAFs assembled in the spheroid core with cervical cancer cells surrounding them (Figure 6A). While CAFs are frequently found both in the core and outer periphery of the tumor stroma,46,47 the phenomenon of CAF assembly in a spheroid core has been previously observed in mixed spheroids using colorectal and breast cancer cell lines.48 The presence of CAFs in the cancer core that can be eliminated by anti-FAP CAR-NK cells (Figure 6A,B) may facilitate the disruption of the tumor architecture. This process could improve the infiltration of additional immune cells, providing support for the potential of combinatorial or sequential CAR therapies to enhance therapeutic efficacy.

In addition to cancer, FAP was also detected on fibroblasts at high levels for a variety of fibrotic diseases, including cardiac fibrosis,49 liver fibrosis,50 and lung fibrosis.51 Therefore, the anti-FAP CAR-NK cell therapy described in this study might be a suitable approach that could be transferred to treat other diseases. While anti-FAP CAR-NK cell therapies against non-cancerous fibrotic diseases are currently lacking, few preclinical studies evaluated anti-FAP CAR-T cells against these diseases. Here, two studies assessed the use of anti-FAP CAR-T cells against cardiac fibrosis in mice models, and showed an effective decrease in cardiac fibrosis with restoration of cardiac functions.52,53 These results indicate a promising use of CAR therapies against fibrotic diseases that require further investigation.

NK cell sources for CAR therapies

While most studies investigate CAR-modified T cells, an increasing number of studies explore alternative cell types.54 Among these, NK cells are a promising cell type due to their innate functions in immunosurveillance and their intrinsic anti-cancer effects.55 Also, virus-infected and cancer cells can downregulate the cell surface expression of MHC-I molecules.56,57 This process can be controlled by HPV oncoproteins in cervical cancers and contributes to reduced recognition by T cells. Here, NK cells can detect insufficient expression of MHC-I molecules and consequently eliminate these transformed cells.58 Additionally, NK cells are associated with decreased safety concerns and do not require strict HLA-matching, enabling off-the-shelf therapeutic approaches.59 Here, optimized protocols, cytokine support, and early cryopreservation of CAR-modified NK cells are crucial to retain their viability and cytotoxicity post-thawing, with first studies successfully highlighting the clinical feasibility of such off-the-shelf CAR-NK cell therapy strategies.60,61 In addition to the CAR-induced cytotoxicity and the intrinsic anti-cancer effects through NK cell activating and inhibitory receptors, CAR-NK cells can kill through antibody-dependent cell cytotoxicity, thereby offering versatile killing mechanisms to target the heterogeneous nature of solid malignancies and the potential for increased cancer cell killing even if the target antigen is down-regulated. Also, CAR-NK cells have shown first promising results in clinics, with allogenic anti-CD19 CAR-NK cells achieving 63% complete responses against lymphoid tumors without signs of GvHD or CRS.38 While the longer in vivo persistence of CAR-T cells may be considered an advantage over CAR-NK cells, cell therapies that exhibit a shorter in vivo persistence are more favorable in the case of potential adverse events. If desired, in vivo persistence of CAR-NK cells can be prolonged by incorporation of IL-15 expression cassettes into the CAR vector.38 This increased persistence is accompanied by improved metabolic fitness of the CAR-NK cells and better in vivo tumor control, indicative of prolonged cytotoxic activity.62 With more than 100 clinical trials investigating CAR-NK therapies, those focusing on solid cancers are still in early phases. While these studies show a promising safety profile, the clinical efficiency of CAR-NK cells against solid malignancies still needs to be explored.63 Here, two studies (NCT01722149, NCT03932565) are currently exploring FAP as a target antigen against solid malignancies, but results remain to be published.

Main sources for NK cell therapies include cord blood, peripheral blood, induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSC), and NK cell lines (e.g., NK-92 cells).64 While NK-92 cells are frequently used for research purposes, they have also been FDA-approved and frequently implemented in CAR NK cell-based clinical trials.65,66 In this study, we tested CAR-modified NK-92 cells (Figures 2–6) and primary cord blood-derived NK cells (Figure 7) for their potential as cell immunotherapy against cervical cancer. Here, we identified an increased cytotoxic activity of unmodified pNK cells against cervical cancer cells compared to unmodified NK-92 cells (Figures 3B, 7C). This aligns with a study by Alekseeva et al.67, who identified target-dependent differences and increased anti-cancer effects of peripheral blood-derived primary NK cells compared to NK-92 cells.67 In contrast, another study reported a higher release of granzymes by NK-92 cells than blood-derived NK cells.68,69 Aarsund et al.70 showed similar distributions of cytolytic proteins in extracellular vesicles (EV) of NK-92 and primary NK cells.70 Due to the limited number and heterogeneity of available studies, further research is necessary to directly compare these NK cell types and to determine the most relevant parameters for CAR therapies.

iPSC-derived NK cells represent another promising cell source for CAR-NK therapies, especially as they have a robust expansion potential that facilitates scalability for clinical use, combined with a highly consistent phenotype that is largely free from donor-dependent variations like blood-derived NK cells.71,72 Moreover, iPSC-derived CAR-NK cells were described to elicit similar or stronger cytotoxic effects against cancer cells compared to peripheral blood-derived CAR-NK cells.73,74 However, iPSC-derived (CAR-) NK cells can have an immature phenotype, which may limit their functionality. Hence, robust protocols are necessary to optimize the generation of iPSC-derived CAR-NK cells. Similarly, cord blood-derived NK cells were described to exhibit a more immature phenotype compared to peripheral blood-derived NK cells, with increased expression of inhibitory receptors that are associated with lower cytotoxicity against target cells.75,76 However, cord blood-derived NK cells were also reported to exhibit enhanced ex vivo proliferation, and cell numbers are typically more consistent between donors than peripheral blood-derived NK cells.75,77 The successful use of cord blood-derived CAR-NK cells was shown by Liu et al. (2020), who described the successful use of cord blood-derived anti-CD19 CAR-modified NK cells for the treatment of lymphoid tumors, achieving 73% response rates and 64% complete remissions.38 In our study, we observed efficient elimination of cervical cancer cells by cord blood-derived NK cells from each of the three donors, with some donor-dependent variabilities among unmodified pNK cells (Figure 7C). Here, anti-FAP CAR-pNK cells derived from Donor #2 that were co-cultured with target cells revealed higher levels of IFNγ and Granzyme B compared to co-cultures with unmodified pNK cells of this Donor (Figure 7C, Supplementary Figure S15). This increase of cytotoxic cytokines in the supernatant was not detected in co-cultures of cervical cancer cells with pNK cells from Donor #1 and Donor#3, likely due to their higher basal cytotoxic activity of unmodified pNK cells (Figure 7C, Supplementary Figure S14, S16).

In vivo engineering of immune cells is another rapidly developing strategy that may replace, or at least complement, ex vivo cell modification, thus offering the opportunity to streamline the delivery of CAR therapies.78 Here, different gene transfer technologies such as lipid nanoparticles, adeno-associated viral vectors, and lentiviral vectors are explored for in vivo immune cell engineering. While promising, some limitations to current in vivo engineering approaches include the need for high vector doses to successfully reach target cells, inflammation response through activation of the complement system, the presence of neutralizing antibodies, and the requirement for targeted delivery to prevent modification of cells other than the intended target cell population.

Overall, we generated novel anti-FAP CAR-NK cells to treat cervical cancer. IHC and flow cytometry experiments revealed high FAP expression in cervical cancer tissues and CAFs. Anti-FAP CAR-NK cells, generated from NK-92 cells and cord blood-derived NK cells, showed enhanced degranulation combined with efficient and specific cytotoxicity against FAP-positive cervical cancer cells and primary CAFs. Moreover, anti-FAP CAR-NK cells maintained high anti-cancer and anti-CAF cytotoxicity in advanced 3D models. These results indicate anti-FAP CAR-NK cells as a promising novel immune cell therapy for cervical cancer that might be extended to other FAP-positive diseases.
