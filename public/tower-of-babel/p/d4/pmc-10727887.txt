A Case of Generalized Xerotic Eczema in a Patient with Congenital Insensitivity to Pain with Anhidrosis
Authors: Eun Ji Hong, Hee Jung Yoon, Sul Hee Lee, Young Lip Park, SangHoon Lee
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractCongenital insensitivity to pain with anhidrosis (CIPA) is an extremely rare disease characterized by insensitivity to pain, anhidrosis, and intellectual disability. CIPA is caused by a genetic mutation in the neurotrophic tyrosine receptor kinase 1 (NTRK1) gene on chromosome 1. The anhidrosis leads to cutaneous changes such as skin dryness, lichenification, and impetiginization. Moreover, patients with CIPA may experience repeated trauma and recalcitrant eczema due to excessive scratching of wounds on their skin, because they do not feel any pain. Severe whole-body eczema in a patient with CIPA may be overlooked, leading these patients to be frequently diagnosed with atopic dermatitis and common eczema. Indeed, in patients with treatment-resistant or atypically distributed eczema and underlying anhidrosis, CIPA should be considered as a potential causative disease. Increased awareness of CIPA among dermatologists is necessary to ensure that patients receive an appropriate diagnosis. Herein, we report a rare case of generalized xerotic eczema in a patient with CIPA.Keywords: Eczema, Hereditary sensory and autonomic neuropathies

INTRODUCTION

Congenital insensitivity to pain with anhidrosis (CIPA), also known as hereditary sensory and autonomic neuropathy type IV (HSAN IV), is an extremely rare disease first described in 19631. Characterized by insensitivity to pain, anhidrosis, and intellectual disability1,2, CIPA is an autosomal recessive disorder caused by a genetic mutation in the neurotrophic tyrosine receptor kinase 1 (NTRK1) gene on chromosome 13. The anhidrosis may cause cutaneous changes such as skin dryness, lichenification, ulceration, and impetiginization. However, due to the low prevalence of CIPA, case reports from dermatologists are still lacking. Herein, we report a rare case of generalized xerotic eczema in a patient with CIPA.

CASE REPORT

A 10-year-old male presented with repeated severe eczema on his whole body. He had no history of atopic dermatitis or contact dermatitis. He had been unable to sweat, which resulted in extremely dry skin, and had long experienced discomfort associated with severe whole-body eczema with diffuse excoriation. None of his family members had a history of similar symptoms. On physical examination, he had multiple erythematous papules and some large patches with yellowish crusts. He wore a cast on his right foot due to repeated fractures. He also complained of severe skin dryness on his whole body (Fig. 1A~D). A laboratory test revealed no abnormalities except for increased eosinophils (16.2%) and total immunoglobulin E (>200 IU/ml). A skin biopsy was performed on a palm-sized eczematous patch from the right thigh (Fig. 1E, F). The histological examination showed spongiotic dermatitis with perivascular infiltrations of lymphocytes and eosinophils in the upper dermis (Fig. 2A, B). S100 staining revealed normal nerve innervation of the sweat glands (Fig. 2C). In addition to skin lesions, he had chronic osteomyelitis from repeated trauma because he could not feel any pain. We initially assumed that he had atopic dermatitis or eczema, but there was no improvement despite comprehensive treatment, and his lesions had a different distribution to typical atopic dermatitis. Therefore, we focused on his underlying conditions as the potential cause of the recalcitrant symptoms. The patient underwent genetic testing at another hospital 1 year previously due to repeated traumatic fractures and careless behavior. According to his medical records, he had previously been diagnosed with CIPA based on the identification of NTRK1 c.851-33T>A homozygote gene mutations. His family indicated that they were not aware of the association between CIPA and skin symptoms, and the patient was finally diagnosed with generalized xerotic eczema caused by CIPA. He improved significantly with supportive care, including use of emollients. The patient has consented to the photographing of all lesions and their use in the paper.

DISCUSSION

HSAN include a number of inherited disorders related to sensory dysfunction (depressed reflexes, altered pain and temperature perception) and autonomic dysfunction (gastroesophageal reflux, postural hypotension, abnormal sweating) 2. CIPA is the second most common type of HSAN, and begins in infancy like the other HSAN types. Mutations in the NTRK1 gene result in failed differentiation and migration of neural crest cells, leading to a complete absence of small myelinated and unmyelinated nerve fibers4. The consequences of these mutations are anhidrosis and the loss of pain and temperature sensation.

Although the exact prevalence of CIPA is unknown, several hundred cases have been reported worldwide, including in Korea4,5,6,7. About 20% of patients with CIPA die before the age 3 of years from heatstroke and other serious systemic complications, such as generalized infection8. Interestingly, although the data are still limited, the NTRK1 c.851-33T>A gene mutation in this case appears to be highly prevalent in Korea9, and several reports suggest that it is associated with a ‘mild’ manifestation of CIPA characterized by a healthy mental state, partially preserved sensory function and the ability to sweat10. Our case also indicates that there is an underlying phenotypegeno-type relationship between CIPA and NTRK1 gene mutations; the patient exhibited no mental retardation and had a relatively long healthy life compared to individuals with the severe form of CIPA.

Although a CIPA diagnosis is generally based on clinical features (anhidrosis, insensitivity to pain, and intellectual disability), genetic testing should be performed for a definitive diagnosis2. Among the 105 NTRK1 gene mutations reported in CIPA patients worldwide10, the NTRK1 c.851-33T>A homozygote gene mutation was identified in this case. Based on the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics guidelines and sequencing analysis, this mutation is classified as‘likely pathogenic’11 and has been observed in several cases in East Asia9,10,12. Therefore, we concluded that it was the cause of CIPA in our patient. According to the literature, CIPA patients exhibit cutaneous changes such as severe skin dryness, lichenification, and eczematous dermatitis due to anhidrosis4,6,7. Moreover, insensitivity to pain may lead to repeated trauma and excessive scratching of skin wounds, which can make patients susceptible to bacterial infections and even sepsis. CIPA patients also exhibit non-dermatologic symptoms such as self-mutilation, thermal injuries, joint dislocations and deformities, corneal ulcerations and aggressive behavior13.

Histopathologically, skin biopsies from CIPA patients reveal normal adnexal structures including sweat glands1,8,14. However, a confocal microscopy study revealed severe loss of cutaneous nerve fibers and non-innervated sweat glands in these patients15. In addition, electron microscopy of sural nerve biopsies revealed atrophy of unmyelinated and small myelinated nerve fibers16.

Unfortunately, there is no definitive cure for CIPA. Thus, supportive care for the accompanying cutaneous symptoms is the central focus of treatment. Medical management is focused on the control of body temperature, prevention of self-mutilation, and treatment of serious systemic complications, which can cause death. Skin moisturization is very important to prevent severe dry skin and xerotic eczema. It is also necessary that patients resist excessive scratching of skin wounds to avoid skin infection and recurrence. A multi-disciplinary approach including dermatology, orthopedics, and pediatrics is needed for optimal care.

Our patient presented with severe whole-body xerotic eczema that persisted for several years. His condition could have been overlooked and easily diagnosed as atopic dermatitis and common eczema. However, we consider patients who have treatment-resistant generalized eczema and an atypical presentation as potential cases of underlying anhidrosis. Although CIPA is not frequently suspected in these patients, we consider it to be a potential causative disease in cases with severe whole-body eczema have insensitivity to pain and anhidrosis. Since patients with CIPA require proper diagnosis and comprehensive management due to poor prognosis, it is necessary that dermatologists be aware of the characteristics and skin symptoms of this disease.
