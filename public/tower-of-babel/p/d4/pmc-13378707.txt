Food Service Professionals’ Experiences of Accommodating Dietary Requests and Texture Modification
Authors: Charlotte Hutchinson, Simone Howells, Alana Hutchison, Anna Rumbach, Laurelie Wishart, Bena Brown, Rebecca L. Packer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundPrevious research highlights that people with specific dietary needs, such as texture‐modified diets for dysphagia, often struggle to find suitable food in the community. However, few studies have investigated food service professionals’ role in remedying this barrier.AimThe current study aimed to understand food service professionals’ perspectives and experiences accommodating dietary requests, with a specific focus on texture modification for dysphagia.MethodsUsing a qualitative descriptive approach, food service professionals from a range of contexts (n = 13) engaged in semi‐structured interviews about their personal experiences and perspectives on accommodating dietary requirements.ResultsThrough reflexive thematic analysis, four themes were identified: (1) consumer demand for dietary modifications drives change in the food service industry; (2) factors that facilitate accommodating dietary modification requests; (3) a lack of knowledge and training about dysphagia exists in the food service industry; and (4) accommodating dietary needs requires a collaborative approach.ConclusionModification requests are becoming increasingly common in the food service industry and are resulting in menu and service changes. The lack of awareness and knowledge about dysphagia among staff highlights the need for speech‐language pathologists to collaborate with both the food service industry and people with dysphagia, to improve accessibility of suitable food.WHAT THIS PAPER ADDS What is already known on this subject Previous literature has identified the challenges faced by individuals with dysphagia in accessing appropriate food in the community. However, there is a distinct lack of research exploring the perspectives of the food service industry in accommodating this population. What this study adds to existing knowledge With an aim to address this gap in knowledge, the current study investigated the perspectives and experiences of food service professionals in providing texture modified foods for this consumer group. The results indicated that the overall lack of education and training for food service professionals about dysphagia is contributing to reduced knowledge and awareness about how to make appropriate dietary accommodations. What are the clinical implications of this study? As such, there is a strong need for speech‐language pathologists to collaborate with the food service industry to improve accessibility of suitable foods, and work with clients with dysphagia to educate and assist them in accessing suitable foods within commercial settings. It is hoped that the findings from this study will support the development of appropriate and relevant education and training initiatives about texture modification for the food service industry.

Introduction

Food is a universal language that brings people together, and encourages social, emotional, and physical well‐being (Glanz et al. 2021). Access to adequate food is a human right (United Nations General Assembly 1948), yet this basic need may be unattainable for up to 16% of the world living with dysphagia (Kertscher et al. 2015). Although dysphagia can arise at any age, it most frequently affects older populations; an estimated 15% of community‐dwelling older Australians have a diagnosis of dysphagia (Madhavan et al. 2016). This condition may be partly managed with texture modified foods, as prescribed by speech‐language pathologists (Ballesteros‐Pomar et al. 2020). In many countries, food and fluid textures are arranged from thin liquid to regular diet using the International Dysphagia Diet Standardisation Initiative (IDDSI) (Cichero et al. 2017).

Regardless of the aetiology, people with dysphagia who are prescribed texture‐modified diets can experience a number of functional impacts on daily life. In particular, difficulty accessing suitable foods in the community may lead to social isolation (Farri et al. 2007) and has the potential to negatively impact quality of life (Smith et al. 2022). The support and attitudes of family, friends, and food service professionals can be an important barrier or facilitator for people with dysphagia when accessing appropriate meals outside the home (Howells et al. 2021; Nund et al. 2014). Howells and colleagues (2021) interviewed 15 people with dysphagia living in the community. Qualitative thematic analysis identified that people with dysphagia frequently experience difficulties accessing food outside the home, particularly in relation to limited menu choices and places to eat, with safety concerns inhibiting their enjoyment and participation when dining out. Participants reported experiencing frustration when communicating with food service professionals across restaurants, cafes, and airlines, and highlighted the emotional and financial consequences of the food service industry's lack of awareness of dysphagia and food texture modification. People with dysphagia shared a desire for appropriate meals to be accessible in the community.

Whilst this study provided important insights into the challenges experienced by people with dysphagia, the perspective of food service professionals providing these accommodations is yet to be elucidated. There is currently limited literature exploring how the challenges experienced by people with dysphagia and their loved ones can be addressed by food service professionals. A small number of studies have focused on food service professionals’ knowledge and understanding of dysphagia; however, these studies have been conducted across variable settings and with heterogeneous participant groups, making comparison of results across studies difficult.

The first known study to explore food service professionals’ knowledge and perceptions of dysphagia was conducted by Broz (2012). In this study, 40 food preparers and deliverers working at a large healthcare facility in the United States were asked to respond to a 21‐item survey. Results of the survey indicated overall low levels of knowledge regarding dysphagia and its management, with only 12% of participants recognising the potential medical consequences of dysphagia, such as aspiration pneumonia. In addition, 76% of participants did not realise the danger of people on modified diets using thin liquids to wash down solid food. Thus, a clear need for education about dysphagia management in this sector was identified.

Broz and Hammond (2014) conducted a follow‐on study which sought to explore food service professionals’ knowledge and awareness of dysphagia, outside of the healthcare sector. Their exploratory study surveyed 139 hospitality, nutrition, and culinary students from three training facilities in the United States, using the same questionnaire developed for Broz's (2012) earlier paper. A total of 77% of participants were unfamiliar with the term ‘dysphagia’, and as such, demonstrated minimal knowledge regarding necessary accommodations for swallowing safety. Furthermore, many participants were unaware of the food service industry's role in providing dysphagia‐friendly food.

Similar findings were reported by Azizan et al. (2023) who used a 40‐item questionnaire to explore the knowledge, attitudes, and practices of 161 food handlers across three Malaysian public hospitals, regarding the preparation of a pureed diet. Despite all participants having a direct role in the preparation of pureed food, the study revealed limited knowledge of pureed food preparation techniques, with food handlers reporting they tended to adjust or ignore recipes for pureed food. The negative attitudes towards pureed diets were associated with the minimal knowledge of food service professionals in this area. Notably, the IDDSI framework is yet to be implemented in Malaysian hospitals (Azizan et al. 2023). However, despite the existence of a Hospital Diet Manual, participants were reportedly disengaged from this resource when preparing pureed meals, raising the possibility that kitchen staff may be insufficiently trained and equipped to deliver high‐quality dysphagia‐friendly meals.

Although these studies provide important insights into food service professionals’ perspectives, all have utilised survey methodology, which limits participants’ ability to fully express their experiences. The findings from these studies may therefore miss the depth and nuance of food services professionals’ knowledge, experiences and attitudes towards providing texture modifications and further exploration is warranted.

While the specific challenges of texture modification for dysphagia are unique, they share similarities with other dietary needs, such as food allergies and intolerances, which also require careful food modifications to prevent health risks. Allergy research has shown that despite having higher public awareness, food service professionals still struggle to meet these needs effectively (Javangwe et al. 2024; Jianu and Goleţ 2019; Lokman and Akoğlu 2022). This raises concerns about whether similar or greater gaps in awareness and practice exist for dysphagia, as it is far less recognised in the community and among the general public (McHutchion et al. 2021).

Collectively, the literature to date has specifically targeted food service professionals’ knowledge of dysphagia and allergies. There is a paucity of research exploring the perspectives and experiences of food service professionals in actually accommodating and making these food modifications. Elucidating the main issues from the perspectives of food service professionals may allow for the identification of key challenges so that potential solutions can be developed. Therefore, the current study aimed to explore Australian food service professionals' experiences in accommodating dietary requests, with a focus on texture modifications for individuals with dysphagia. However, by adopting a broader lens that included allergies and dietary preferences like vegetarianism, the study also aimed to identify similarities in challenges across different dietary needs.

Methods

Study Design

A qualitative descriptive study design was implemented to investigate food service professionals’ perspectives and experiences of accommodating for people with dietary modifications. This approach enables qualitative investigation of phenomena related to the research topic, without conforming to one specific theoretical stance (Caelli et al. 2003), thus allowing for a straightforward description of data. Qualitative descriptive designs are common in health research, as they provide in‐depth insight into participants’ experiences and perspectives (Doyle et al. 2020). Given that allergies and other dietary preferences are generally more readily recognised within the community, and that challenges and solutions to accommodate different dietary needs may share similarities with those of texture modification, this study was designed to initially explore the more common modifications such as allergies and vegetarianism, along with a specific focus on dysphagia.

Sampling and Participants

Purposeful sampling was utilised to recruit participants with a broad range of perspectives (Neergaard et al. 2009). Food service professionals with various roles (chef/nutrition assistant/food server), experience levels (trainee/qualified), and from different industry types (corporate/hospitality/healthcare/residential aged care centres) were sought. Participants were eligible if they were >18 years of age, worked in the food service industry in Australia with roles in food preparation or provision, and had sufficient English proficiency to engage in an interview.

Procedure

This study received ethical clearance through The University of Queensland [2019002751]. All participants provided written consent prior to the commencement of interviews. Participants engaged in a semi‐structured interview, in which they were encouraged to reflect on their experiences and perspectives of providing food modifications in their workplace. Participants were asked about their experiences with common dietary needs as well as requests related to dysphagia. Interviews were conducted by two members of the research team, who are mid‐career researchers and speech‐language pathologists with experience in qualitative research. A semi‐structured interview guide, consisting of open‐ended questions, was used (see supplementary material) to engage participants in a conversational style interview. Interview questions were modified, omitted, or expanded as necessary, to support participants in openly discussing their experiences and perspectives. Interviews ranged in duration from 22 to 44 min. All interviews were audio‐recorded and transcribed verbatim for analysis.

Data Analysis

Data were analysed using reflexive thematic analysis, following the six‐step structure described by Braun and Clarke (2006, 2022). This method is useful in analysing themes within the dataset to identify and report patterns (Sandelowski 2000). Thematic analysis is a reflexive process in which the researcher becomes deeply familiar with the data, whilst remaining cognisant of their own potential biases, assumptions and influence on the analysis of the data (Rohleder and Lyons 2015). Specifically, data were coded using an inductive approach, whereby codes were developed directly from the data, rather than through the implementation of pre‐existing templates or theoretical frameworks (Braun and Clarke 2022). Coding was facilitated by the NVivo12 software program (https://www.qsrinternational.com/).

Two members of the research team (CH, RP) commenced analysis through individual in‐depth engagement with the data, by reading interview transcripts and listening to audio recordings to familiarise themselves with the data and conducting preliminary analysis of patterns within the data. Initial code sets, consisting of related sections of data, were then created and subsequently arranged into groups. Analysis and interpretation of these groups was checked against the research question and the individual transcripts. The code sets were then developed into themes which were revised and refined to ensure they aligned with the interview data and research question. To ensure the trustworthiness and credibility of the data analysis, two members of the research team (CH, RP) met regularly to consult, reflect, and discuss the formulation of codes, sub‐themes, and themes (Denzin 1970).

Four main themes were developed from the interview data that described food service professionals’ experiences and perspectives in accommodating food modifications. These included: (1) consumer demand for dietary modifications drives change in the food service industry, (2) factors that facilitate accommodating dietary modification requests, (3) a lack of knowledge and training about dysphagia exists in the food service industry, and (4) accommodating dietary needs requires a collaborative approach.

In recent years, participants reported noticing an increase in the prevalence of general dietary needs: ‘Because eating habits have changed dramatically in the last five years, where we would not even think of vegan as being mainstream, or vegetarianism as being as mainstream, it's now mainstream’ (P007). This shift in consumer demand has resulted in long‐term changes to the availability of dietary‐friendly options on menus, with multiple participants stating they now consistently offer gluten‐free, vegan and vegetarian meals on their menus. Menus now also commonly outline dietary information to assist consumers in selecting appropriate meals. Furthermore, participants reported actively planning for potential dietary requests when preparing menus, which often involved designing meals that could easily be altered to suit a variety of dietary needs. As a result of these changes, participants observed that consumers regularly choose to order dietary‐friendly meals regardless of their own dietary needs: ‘Often people that aren't vegetarian will order that [vegetarian option] anyway’ (P010). This high frequency of choosing dietary‐specific meals was believed to be linked with the general public being more ‘aware of … conditions’ (P001) and an increase in dietary trends, thus resulting in modification requests being ‘the new normal’ (P008) in the food service industry.

Compared with common dietary requests, such as vegetarian and gluten‐free, only three participants mentioned experiencing requests due to swallowing disorders, unprompted by the interviewer. Notably, all three participants were working in roles affiliated with the healthcare industry. One participant further clarified that his role in healthcare has helped him retrospectively attribute texture modification requests from consumers in previous restaurant work as likely being due to swallowing difficulties.

One chef summarised the responsibility of the food service industry to adapt with changing consumer demand: ‘Obviously, with [dietary requests] being on the rise, intolerances growing thicker and thicker every day, we will have to meet that demand moving forward. That's just the way it's gonna be’ (P004). Not only is the changing dietary scene influencing food service professionals’ practices, but it is also affecting their attitudes towards accommodating modification requests:

‘Ten years, 15 years ago [people requesting modifications] was a pain in the butt, you know, it was just yeah, how dare you. But in the modern era, and people have changed, and science has changed, and we're more educated in the needs of customers’ (P008).

Most participants indicated that receiving advanced warning about incoming dietary needs allowed for increased ability to accommodate, with one participant concluding that this is ‘the best way of getting the best possible outcome for that person’ (P008). Prior notice was highlighted as particularly essential regarding accommodation for dysphagia, despite relatively few participants having experienced such requests. Participants mentioned a preference for consumers to provide this information by ringing in advance or detailing dietary requirements during the booking process. Conversely, receiving on the spot dietary requests can be ‘frustrating’ (P005) and ‘very difficult to produce’ (P006), as chefs may not have the required food items at their disposal without pre‐planning.

The need for appropriate resources to accommodate modification requests was prevalent in the data. Reduced access to appropriate equipment, such as blenders to produce texture‐modified food, and infrastructure, such as separate kitchen areas for production of allergen‐friendly meals, was commonly reported, with participants implying that availability of these would facilitate their ability to accommodate: ‘It depends if you've got that structure and things available for you to be able to produce [modifications]’ (P006). Many participants also commented on contextual considerations, such as kitchen size, as having implications for accommodation abilities, particularly regarding texture modification for individuals with swallowing disorders. In general, larger facilities, such as hospitals, were believed to be more able to service these demands, whereas smaller establishments were less equipped: ‘If you went to your local restaurant down at the corner shop or something like that, they're not really geared up to service that sort of a [dysphagia‐friendly] menu’ (P006).

Consideration of financial resources was raised as pivotal in determining the ability to accommodate modification requests, with one participant asserting ‘anything is possible, but it costs’ (P007). Similar to kitchen size, this factor was perceived to be either a facilitator or a barrier, depending on the service context. A chef from a high‐end restaurant perceived cost of meals to positively influence attitude and motivation towards accommodating requests, remarking that ‘because of the price to eat at our establishment, we would bend over backwards … to make something for the guests’ (P005). In contrast, other participants highlighted the at times excessive costs of producing and offering dietary‐friendly options, with chefs reporting they ‘[pay] top dollar for a really poor‐quality product’ (P002).

Participants spoke of receiving varying amounts of training related to dietary requests such as allergies, vegetarianism, and gluten‐free; however, they described a total absence of mandatory training about dysphagia and associated texture‐modification techniques when preparing to commence work in the food service industry. Despite five participants currently working or having previously worked in healthcare‐related roles, only three participants had heard of IDDSI, and only two participants reported receiving specific on‐the‐job training regarding dysphagia and texture modification. Furthermore, one of these participants works within a residential aged‐care setting and was instrumental in establishing IDDSI compliance at her workplace.

Similarly, outside of the healthcare sector, participants reported limited knowledge about dysphagia in general: ‘I don't have a working understanding of what would be needed… [for modifications for swallowing disorders]’ (P003). Due to the lack of formal training, two chefs with a personal interest in dysphagia reported educating themselves about the condition through reading literature and initiating conversations with speech‐language pathologists and dietitians. Five participants mentioned receiving training regarding textural quality and variation in dishes; however, they highlighted that this was purely from a fine dining point of view to ‘[teach] you that you need varied textures on a plate to make it interesting’ (P011). As one participant discussed, general society tends not to understand that texture modification is actually part of regular meals, particularly in fine dining, and a ‘fancy [entrée of salmon mousse] … would actually be referred to as a level 4 puree’ (P013).

One participant pointed out that food service industry training about dysphagia would make ‘life a lot easier for people with swallowing disorders because they would feel more comfortable being able to go out and eat’ (P003). The majority of participants expressed interest in receiving education and training about dysphagia, as it would enable them to provide more appropriate accommodations for these individuals. Of note, however, a perceived lack of demand for accommodation requests due to dysphagia prompted two participants to question whether instating mandatory training on such a topic would be necessary outside the healthcare sector. Limited training and experience with dysphagia‐related modification requests did not seem to translate to decreased willingness or confidence in making accommodations. Eight participants reported willingness and confidence in making such accommodations, despite reporting minimal levels of training and experience in this area.

Participants cited multiple factors that contribute to their role in accommodating dietary needs, including creating a positive consumer experience, retaining consumers to optimise financial outcomes, and simply meeting the demands of the job: ‘[It is] part of the job and part of your responsibility as the chef to have those [dietary] options available’ (P003).

Liaising with consumers was perceived by all food service professionals to be valuable in carrying out their role; however, participants working in healthcare‐related roles also identified a need to collaborate with health professionals. Participants pointed out the importance of clear communication from consumers regarding their dietary needs, as this assists chefs and cooks in making appropriate modifications. Some participants reflected that the absence of modification requests due to dysphagia is not necessarily due to a lack of demand, rather that these consumers employ strategies to replace having to talk about their condition: ‘I guess there could be a lot more going on than we're aware of, but they [people with swallowing disorders] tend to order a smoothie or things I would say’ (P011).

Regarding collaboration between food service professionals and health professionals in dysphagia management, some participants expressed that accommodating dysphagia would not be within the interests of their workplace due to a lack of demand and reported barriers, but recognised the need for IDDSI in healthcare settings. One participant working in the food manufacturing setting reported initiating conversations with speech‐language pathologists and dietitians to gain their professional perspective of the compliance of his texture‐modified food with the IDDSI standards. This participant acknowledged the different, yet equally valuable skills food service professionals and health professionals bring regarding texture‐modified foods and endorsed their collaboration: ‘I work in food, I know what I want to achieve with the food, the flavours, the appearance, the aromas. But I don't know what a speech pathologist or a dietitian professionally is necessarily looking for’ (P013).

Discussion

This study investigated food service professionals’ experiences of accommodating both general dietary requests and dysphagia‐specific modifications. Overall, analysis of the data identified four main themes that describe the current practices and attitudes of people working in the food service industry in Australia. These themes relate to the influence of consumer demand in driving change in the food service industry, factors that facilitate accommodation of dietary needs, the lack of knowledge and training about dysphagia amongst food service professionals, and the need for a collaborative approach in accommodating dietary requests.

Participants in the current study spoke about the significant surge in modification requests, attributing this to a rising prevalence of dietary requirements in general society. This anecdotal evidence aligns with the increases in coeliac disease (Singh et al. 2018), allergies (Mullins et al. 2022) and lifestyle diets such as vegetarianism and veganism (Morgan 2019) described in the literature. The food service industry has responded to the evolving dietary landscape by making deliberate changes to production methods and menu options, to ensure continuity of service to society's varied consumer base for some dietary needs (Oktadiana et al. 2020). However, few participants in the current study reported experiencing or accommodating requests due to dysphagia. When compared with the myriad of options now available for people with other general dietary needs, such as gluten‐free or vegetarian, people with dysphagia are at a distinct disadvantage in accessing appropriate food in the community (Howells et al. 2021). As has occurred with general dietary modifications, it is timely that accommodating for dysphagia becomes the next foci for food services professionals, to ensure the industry is well‐equipped and prepared to cope with this demand that will only continue to increase.

The disparity between consumer desire to access dysphagia‐friendly food in the community and actual availability of these meals prompts the need to consider how current practices in the food service industry, particularly in the context of increased accommodation of other general dietary requests, could be expanded upon to bridge this gap. The current study underlined the collective opinion that accommodating dietary requests is possible when food service professionals are given prior notice. Primary methods of sharing this information include phoning the food establishment in advance and detailing dietary requirements in online booking forms. These are simple steps consumers can take to facilitate food service professionals in being prepared to accommodate their needs. Speech‐language pathologists could potentially assist this process by providing people with dysphagia with adaptable scripts to help them explain their textural requirements to food service professionals. In addition, speech‐language pathologists could also encourage consumers who require such modifications to consider whether an establishment is likely to have the necessary equipment readily available to meet their needs. For example, a larger restaurant may be more likely to have access to food processors to create a puree, than a small café, meaning texture modification is more achievable in the larger restaurant. Furthermore, increasing awareness amongst the food service industry about typical menu items that may already be appropriate for a person with dysphagia, such as pumpkin soup for a puree diet, should be addressed. As such, meals do not require additional modification, supporting food service professionals to identify those that currently exist on their menu may enhance their confidence in advertising as a dysphagia‐friendly establishment. Despite the limited research regarding the efficacy of these approaches for accommodating people with dysphagia, similar strategies are prevalent in the context of allergies. Lee and Xu (2015) found that 81.8% of food service professionals believed it to be the consumer's responsibility to notify the restaurant of their allergy requirements. Participants in this study stated that it would be inappropriate to ask consumers about medical conditions, and therefore, the consumer must be the one to raise the topic. Lee and Xu (2015) concluded that restaurants must strive to create a safe environment to facilitate these conversations. Consumers themselves also benefit from these strategies, as the risk of eating unsuitable meals can be reduced (Kwon et al. 2020).

Importantly, having access to appropriate equipment and infrastructure was cited in the current study as a necessary factor contributing to food service professionals’ ability to accommodate modification requests. Whilst participants in the current study understood that special equipment, such as blenders, is essential in preparing texture‐modified food that is safe and IDDSI‐compliant (New South Wales Government Food Authority 2015), many described their current shortage of such necessary equipment as inhibiting their ability to offer suitable meals to individuals with dysphagia. Similarly, participants raised the financial considerations associated with dietary modifications as another factor that either facilitated or inhibited their ability to accommodate. Advocating for suitable resourcing for the food service industry, to enable them to provide suitable meals for people with dysphagia, is therefore warranted.

Overall, the current study highlighted the lack of knowledge and training about dysphagia and its management that exists in the food service industry. However, food service professionals expressed willingness to be educated on the topic and identified potential strategies and approaches that could increase their ability to make accommodations for this population. Participants primarily attributed their reduced knowledge and awareness of dysphagia to limited training and experience in providing these modifications. Ideally, food service professionals should have access to training programs to assist their understanding and skill development in preparing texture‐modified food, to align with the current training offered for more general dietary modifications, such as allergies (Soon 2020). Although some food service professionals in the healthcare sector receive training about dysphagia, a gap exists in the consistency of this training, as observed through both our study and the present evidence base (Garcia et al. 2018). Participants in the current study concluded that training regarding dysphagia and texture modification would be highly beneficial given the current lack of knowledge, echoing comparable views acknowledged in the literature (Azizan et al. 2023). In general, participants reported a preference for simple, visual‐based training that can be delivered on‐the‐job, and not interfere with the busyness of the kitchen environment (Soon 2020). Taken together with the literature, the current study accentuates both the clear need and desire for training about dysphagia in the food service industry. In addition to knowledge and skill gain for food service professionals regarding dysphagia management, it is also likely that such training could result in significant changes to community access and participation for individuals with dysphagia. However, future research must consider the most appropriate and sustainable method of delivering this education, to ensure it is user‐friendly and achievable.

Such large‐scale and systemic changes cannot be achieved by the food service industry alone, and enhancing the availability of dysphagia‐friendly food in the community is a shared responsibility that requires thoughtful collaboration. Considering the few requests for modifications due to dysphagia reported by food service professionals in the current study, the obvious influence of consumer priorities in changing the services provided by the food service industry underlines the need for speech‐language pathologists to act as mediators between consumers and the food service industry. Of note, the lack of uptake of IDDSI outside the healthcare industry identified in the current study is likely resultant of the lack of community awareness of dysphagia (McHutchion et al. 2021), coupled with the general food service industry's reduced awareness of dysphagia. In addition, there was a lack of representation of chefs and cooks in the development of the IDDSI framework (Cichero et al. 2017). Although these professionals were involved in the later validation of the guidelines, their inclusion in earlier iterations may have been beneficial. Whilst acknowledging speech‐language pathologists’ and food service professionals’ individual responsibility to engage in professional partnerships across the two sectors, an alliance between the food service industry and the health industry at the governing body level could be instrumental in driving this collaboration. Training and professional development events for food service professionals and speech‐language pathologists to learn from and with each other could potentially be delivered through professional associations. Such opportunities would support shared understanding of each professionals’ roles and provide a formal platform for communication.

Furthermore, collaboration between the food service industry and the health industry needs to extend to include individuals with dysphagia, as they arguably provide the most critical insights into what is required to meet their needs. Participants in the current study spoke about the impact of consumer‐staff communication on their ability to accommodate, surmising that individuals with dysphagia may be reluctant to disclose their condition to chefs to avoid being negatively perceived. Barnett et al. 2020 found similar attitudes amongst individuals with allergies who reported feelings of embarrassment and awkwardness when initiating conversations with food service professionals about their dietary needs. The current study further emphasises the importance of supporting both food services professionals and consumers to be confident in discussing dietary needs, to avoid the potentially fatal consequences that can result from poor management of these conditions.

The current study has provided new insights into the previously limited evidence regarding accommodations for dysphagia in the food service industry, by adding the perspectives and experiences of food service professionals. Author reflexivity enhanced the rigour of the research, as did the use of investigator triangulation. Through these approaches, potential bias was both acknowledged, and reduced, to increase the credibility of the study's findings. However, the limitations of the current study are acknowledged. Despite the adequate sample size (Hennink and Kaiser 2022), increasing the number of participants from each food service context could have allowed for greater diversity of perspectives across the food service industry. Similarly, it must be acknowledged that this study was purely conducted in Australia. Further research should investigate the perspectives of food service professionals working around the world, to understand the global perspective of accommodating dietary needs in the context of different food cultures and food service environments. Lastly, all participants reported >5 years’ experience working in the food service industry, meaning they may not be aware of topics covered in current formal education. Future research could explore the perspectives of early career food service professionals to gain a more contemporary understanding of the training opportunities available. Additionally, research regarding current formal and informal dysphagia‐related training opportunities for food service professionals may contribute to the development of robust education programs in the future.

Conclusion

The current study has provided insight into food service professionals’ perspectives and experiences accommodating both general dietary requirements and dysphagia specifically. The food service industry plays an essential role in society, and although accommodating dietary needs like allergies and gluten‐free, is becoming more widespread, the food service industry has been slower to respond to the demand for services for people with dysphagia. Limited training and education opportunities, alongside insufficient experience, have resulted in food service professionals being less prepared and able to provide accommodations for people with dysphagia. Finally, this study has highlighted that a collaborative approach between health professionals, food service professionals, and people with dysphagia may help to support the availability of dysphagia‐friendly services and meals in the community.
