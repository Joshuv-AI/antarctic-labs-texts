Epidemiological Trends and Clinical Features of Trichophyton rubrum Infections: A 10-Year Retrospective Review of 38,391 Cases (2014–2023)
Authors: Nam Gyoung Ha, Yong Jun Bang, Weon Ju Lee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTrichophyton rubrum is the most common dermatophyte responsible for superficial fungal infections worldwide. This study provided a comprehensive epidemiological analysis of T. rubrum infections from 2014 to 2023, focusing on incidence trends, demographic characteristics, seasonal variation, and anatomical distribution.MethodsWe retrospectively reviewed the medical records of 38,391 patients diagnosed with T. rubrum infections during a 10-year period. Data were analyzed regarding annual incidence patterns, demographics, seasonal trends, and clinical presentations.ResultsT. rubrum consistently accounted for over 90% of all dermatophyte infections, although there was a slight decline in annual incidence. Individuals in their 50s constituted the most commonly affected age group, with significantly higher incidence rates observed in males. A marked seasonal peak occurred during the summer months. Tinea pedis was the most common clinical manifestation, followed by tinea unguium and tinea cruris.ConclusionT. rubrum remains the leading cause of dermatophytosis, with stable demographic and seasonal infection patterns over the past decade. Preventive strategies focusing on older males, particularly during summer, may help reduce the prevalence of these infections.Keywords: Demography, Dermatophytes, Epidemiology, Incidence, Trichophyton rubrum

INTRODUCTION

Dermatophytosis is a common superficial fungal infection affecting the skin, nails, and hair, with Trichophyton rubrum as the predominant causative agent worldwide.1 These infections impose a significant health burden, with dermatophytes estimated to affect approximately 20–25% of the world’s population.2 The incidence of dermatophytosis may vary depending upon several factors, including gender, age, season, socioeconomic status, and geographic location.3 Studies have shown that males tend to be affected more frequently than females, and older adults exhibit the highest rates of infection.4,5 Environmental factors, particularly high temperature and humidity, also play important roles, creating ideal conditions for fungal growth and thus contributing to higher infection prevalence in tropical regions.2

Despite advances in antifungal therapy, the burden from T. rubrum infections remains significant, particularly among certain demographic groups, such as the elderly, immunocompromised individuals, and those living in resource-limited settings.6 Such populations are often more susceptible to severe and recurrent infections, resulting in prolonged treatment durations and increased healthcare costs.6 Furthermore, the increasing prevalence of antifungal resistance presents a developing challenge, with reports of therapeutic failures becoming increasingly common.7,8 Factors contributing to resistance include inappropriate antifungal use, inadequate treatment regimens, and the inherent adaptive properties of fungi.9

This study aimed to provide a comprehensive analysis of epidemiological trends and clinical features of T. rubrum infections over a 10-year period (2014–2023) by examining data from 38,391 patients. Through an assessment of these trends, the study seeks to identify populations at greatest risk and explore environmental factors contributing to the persistence of these infections.

METHODS

Study population and data collection

We conducted a retrospective analysis of 41,015 patients diagnosed with dermatophytosis who tested positive by means of a 15% potassium hydroxide solution and fungal culture at our institution from January 2014 to December 2023. Fungal cultures were grown on potato dextrose agar and corn meal Tween 80 media, incubated at 24–26°C, and monitored for growth over a period of 2 to 4 weeks. Among these patients, we conducted a detailed review of medical records for 38,391 patients specifically diagnosed with T. rubrum based on fungal culture results. For each patient, we collected demographic data from electronic medical records, including age, gender, date of infection onset, and geographic location. We additionally recorded clinical manifestations associated with the infection, such as the affected body region. Seasonal data were categorized by quarter to evaluate potential patterns related to seasonal variation.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used for demographic data analysis. To examine gender differences, we applied the chi-square test to determine whether the distribution of infections significantly varied between males and females. Seasonal trends in infection rates were assessed using one-way analysis of variance to identify statistically significant differences in infection incidence across the four seasons. A P value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all tests.

Ethics statement

The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Kyungpook National University Hospital (IRB No. 2024-10-007). The requirement for informed consent was waived by the IRB because this research involved the analysis of existing medical records. Nevertheless, all patient information was anonymized and managed with strict confidentiality to ensure individual privacy.

RESULTS

Annual incidence and stability of proportion

During the 10-year study period, T. rubrum infections accounted for 38,391 cases, representing 93.6% of the total 41,015 dermatophyte infections. The remaining 6.4% consisted mainly of T. mentagrophytes (5.0%), followed, in descending order, by Microsporum canis, T. tonsurans, M. gypseum, T. verrucosum, and Epidermophyton floccosum. This proportional distribution remained stable, with T. rubrum constituting over 90% of all dermatophyte cases annually (Fig. 1). The absolute incidence of T. rubrum infections showed a declining trend, decreasing from 4,426 cases in 2014 to 2,648 cases in 2023 (Fig. 2). Although the annual incidence decreased, T. rubrum remained consistently the predominant species among dermatophyte infections throughout the study period.

Annual proportion of Trichophyton rubrum infections among total dermatophytosis cases. The proportion of T. rubrum infection remained stable, accounting for over 90% of all dermatophyte cases.

Absolute annual incidence of Trichophyton rubrum infections throughout the study period. The annual incidence of T. rubrum exhibited a slightly decreasing trend.

Age, gender, and geographic distribution

The age distribution of T. rubrum infections over the 10-year study period is illustrated in Fig. 3. The most frequently affected age group consisted of patients in their 50s, who accounted for 28.4% of all T. rubrum cases. Among those infected, male patients represented 26,277 cases (68.45%), resulting in a male-to-female ratio of 2.3:1 (P < 0.01), indicating that males were significantly more affected than females. An analysis of geographic distribution showed that 33,502 participants (87.3% of the total population) resided in urban areas, while 4,889 participants (12.7%) were from rural areas.

Age distribution of Trichophyton rubrum infections during the study period. Among all age groups, patients in their 50s had the highest proportion of T. rubrum infections.

Seasonal peak

The monthly frequency of T. rubrum infections is summarized in Fig. 4. A pronounced seasonal variation was observed, with the highest number of infections occurring during the summer months (June to August), accounting for 42.7% of all cases (P < 0.05). This finding correlates with increased humidity and sweating during summer months, which create favorable conditions for fungal growth.

Seasonal incidence. The highest number of infections occurred during the summer months (June to August), accounting for 42.7% of all cases.

Topographical manifestations

The distribution of T. rubrum infection sites is illustrated in Fig. 5. Tinea pedis, affecting the toe web and sole, was the most frequently observed clinical manifestation, comprising 54.4% of cases. This was followed by tinea unguium, involving the toenails and accounting for 19.8%, and tinea cruris, affecting the groin and observed in 10.7% of cases. The preference of T. rubrum for warm, moist environments, such as the feet and nails, aligns with the observed distribution across these anatomical sites.

DISCUSSION

This study provided a comprehensive analysis of T. rubrum infections over a decade, highlighting trends in incidence, patient demographics, and associated clinical manifestations. Despite a slight decrease in the overall incidence of T. rubrum infection, T. rubrum remains the leading cause of dermatophyte infections, accounting for more than 90% of all cases. This high prevalence reaffirms the pathogen’s persistent role as the primary cause of superficial mycoses, notably tinea pedis and tinea unguium. In 2015, Lee et al.10 reported that among 131,122 patients with dermatophytosis, 115,846 (88.35%) were infected with T. rubrum between 1979 and 2013. These findings align with existing literature, which consistently identifies T. rubrum as the predominant dermatophyte across various populations and geographic regions.3,11 Compared with the previous cohort (1979–2013), the current data demonstrate a gradual decline in T. rubrum infections. This trend may be attributed to multiple factors, including improvements in public hygiene, enhanced accessibility to antifungal therapies, lifestyle modifications such as increased adoption of breathable footwear, and heightened public awareness, resulting in earlier diagnosis and treatment.

One key observation in our study is the high incidence of T. rubrum infections seen in individuals in their 50s, with a notable male predominance. This pattern may be influenced by several factors, including occupational exposure, which may place workers in moist, warm environments conducive to fungal growth.5 Over time, cumulative exposure to environmental factors, along with age-related changes in skin integrity, could contribute to the higher prevalence of T. rubrum infection observed in this age group. Additionally, age-related factors such as comorbidities and decreased immunity may play significant roles. As individuals age, immune function tends to decline, making older individuals more vulnerable to infections.12 Moreover, conditions such as diabetes or cardiovascular diseases, which are more prevalent in older populations, may further compromise the skin’s ability to resist fungal colonization.13 Aging skin may also exhibit diminished barrier functionality, increasing susceptibility to fungal invasion.14,15 Gender differences in T. rubrum infections also warrant further investigation, as infections among males are consistently more common.16 This disparity has been largely attributed to occupational and lifestyle factors that increase males’ exposure risk. Urbanization has resulted in more males working in industries such as manufacturing, construction, and transportation, in which prolonged use of closed footwear in warm, moist environments promotes fungal growth. Moreover, higher levels of physical activity and greater use of communal facilities such as gyms may also contribute to the increased incidence of T. rubrum infections among males. As urban environments intensify exposure to these risk factors, men are more likely to develop such infections. While environmental and lifestyle factors related to urbanization appear to be major contributors to this gender disparity, further research is required to better elucidate the interplay between occupational hazards and urban living conditions.

Another significant finding from our study is the seasonal variation in the prevalence of T. rubrum infection, with a noticeable peak during the summer months. Warm, humid conditions have long been recognized as favorable for fungal proliferation, especially in areas such as the feet and nails, where moisture tends to accumulate.17 This seasonal trend aligns with findings from previous studies, reinforcing the hypothesis that temperature and humidity are key factors driving the transmission and growth of dermatophytes. Public health initiatives, including raising awareness about proper foot care, the importance of breathable footwear, and timely treatment, could help mitigate the impact of this seasonal rise in infections.

The predominance of tinea pedis and tinea unguium in our cohort underscores the need for interventions targeted specifically at these clinical forms of dermatophyte infections. Such interventions may include educational programs promoting proper foot hygiene, such as keeping feet clean and thoroughly dry, as well as screening high-risk populations, including individuals with diabetes or immunocompromised conditions. Both conditions, particularly tinea unguium (onychomycosis), are often chronic and challenging to treat, usually requiring prolonged courses of antifungal therapy and carrying a high risk of recurrence.18 Additionally, the aesthetic and functional consequences of nail infections can significantly impair quality of life.19 Thus, developing more effective antifungal agents, coupled with improving patient compliance in completing treatment courses, remains essential to enhance patient outcomes.

Despite these valuable insights, limitations of our study should be acknowledged. As a retrospective, single-center study, the ability to generalize these findings to broader populations is limited. Regional variations in environmental conditions, healthcare access, and patient demographics could influence patterns of T. rubrum infection. Moreover, relying exclusively on culture-based diagnostics may limit both the accuracy and sensitivity of detection. Although culture methods remain standard practice, incorporating molecular techniques such as polymerase chain reaction and sequencing could enhance diagnostic precision and enable more accurate identification of T. rubrum infections. Future research involving multiple centers and more diverse populations will be necessary to validate these observed trends and further clarify risk factors associated with these infections.

In conclusion, despite a slight decline in overall incidence, this 10-year analysis confirms the continuing significance of T. rubrum as the leading cause of dermatophytosis, demonstrating consistent epidemiological and clinical trends. Public health efforts aimed at reducing infection incidence should prioritize high-risk populations, particularly older males, and emphasize prevention strategies during the summer months.
