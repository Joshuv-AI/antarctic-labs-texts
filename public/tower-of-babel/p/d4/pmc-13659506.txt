Microbiome research in liver transplantation
Authors: Ulrich Wirth, Josefine Schardey, Christian Schulz, Joachim Andrassy
Published: 2026

Liver transplantation remains the only curative treatment for end-stage liver disease (1). Thus, advanced liver disease is characterized not only by organ failure but also by profound disturbances in host-microbiome homeostasis (1). Small intestinal bacterial overgrowth, increased intestinal permeability, and translocation of microbial products contribute to a heightened susceptibility to severe infections, including spontaneous bacterial peritonitis (1).

Over the past decade, microbiome research has substantially expanded our understanding of chronic liver diseases (2). Numerous studies have demonstrated that gut microbial composition and diversity are altered in conditions such as cirrhosis, non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, and alcoholic liver disease (3). Microbial dysbiosis has been linked to systemic inflammation, metabolic dysregulation, and disease progression (2). However, despite this growing body of evidence, microbiome data in the specific context of liver transplantation—and particularly their integration into clinical decision-making—remain limited (1,3-5).

Emerging data suggest that the gut microbiota may influence post-transplant outcomes (1,3-5). The perioperative course of liver transplantation is characterized by factors that profoundly disrupt the intestinal microbiome, including broad-spectrum antibiotic therapy, critical illness, and frequently reduced or delayed enteral nutrition (3). Reconstitution of a “healthy” gut microbiome after transplantation appears to require considerable time and may parallel the patient’s overall physiological recovery (3,5).

The liver occupies a central role not only in metabolic and synthetic processes but also in immune regulation (6). Through the gut-liver axis, the liver is continuously exposed to microbial products, metabolites, and antigens derived from the intestine via the portal circulation (1,2). While extensive research has focused on gut microbiota–host interactions across the intestinal barrier, increasing evidence indicates that microbial signatures are also detectable within liver tissue and the biliary tract. These findings challenge the traditional concept of the liver as a sterile organ and suggest the presence of a distinct intrahepatic and biliary microbial niche (1,7).

Nevertheless, the relationship between the gut microbiome, the liver microbiome, and the biliary microbiome remains poorly defined. It is unclear to what extent these microbial communities overlap and whether they represent transient translocation events or stable ecological niches. In the intestine, host-microbiome interactions are tightly regulated by epithelial barriers and mucosal immune mechanisms (8). In contrast, within liver tissue, potential host-microbe interactions may occur in a different immunological context, possibly involving direct exposure of hepatic immune cells to microbial components (9).

Because the gut is easily accessible for biosampling, most current knowledge about host-microbiome interactions derives from fecal analyses (1,3,5). In contrast, the role of tissue-resident microbiota and local host-microbe crosstalk—particularly within the liver—has not been sufficiently investigated (10,11). Especially in the setting of liver transplantation, data regarding both gut and intrahepatic or biliary microbiomes have not been available in the past.

The study by Lucas-Ruiz and colleagues, published in Gut (2025), provides the first systematic investigation of the intrahepatic donor microbial DNA signature as a determinant of early outcomes after liver transplantation (7). The authors analyzed microbial DNA detected in the organ preservation solution (OPS) using 16S rRNA sequencing and characterized the metataxonomic composition of OPS samples from a discovery cohort of 110 liver transplantations and an independent validation cohort of 29 cases (7). The OPS-derived microbial profile clearly differed from the gut microbiome, further supporting the emerging paradigm that the liver harbors a distinct microbial signature (7).

In their work, specific microbial taxa were associated with clinically meaningful endpoints, including 1-year survival, acute rejection, hepatic artery thrombosis, and biliary complications (7). Notably, the prognostic signal was driven by compositional shifts in specific genera rather than microbial diversity, highlighting the importance of taxonomic structure over diversity metrics in this context (7). Furthermore, the authors implemented an integrative, multi-omics approach and subsequent RNA sequencing of donor liver tissue demonstrated that grafts from non-survivors exhibited a distinct transcriptomic profile characterized by upregulation of inflammatory chemokines and downregulation of key metabolic and DNA repair genes (7). By application of a machine learning approach to predict transplant outcomes based solely on OPS microbial features, the authors achieved excellent predictive performance in the discovery and validation cohorts, supporting the generalizability of the findings (7). These results indicate that donor-derived microbial DNA patterns may serve as pre-implantation biomarkers for risk stratification (7).

Taken together, this study expands the concept of the gut-liver axis by introducing the graft microbiome as an intrinsic immunological factor in transplantation (7). Previous work has demonstrated that gut microbial composition can modulate alloimmune responses (12); the present study extends this framework to suggest that the organ-resident microbial DNA signature itself may shape the inflammatory environment of the graft (7). Second, it contributes to the growing body of literature challenging the traditional dogma of sterile internal organs, positioning microbial DNA as a potential regulator of tissue immunity and metabolism even in the absence of overt infection (1,7).

The study by Lucas-Ruiz and colleagues represents a notable shift in the conceptual framework of solid organ transplantation. The graft is no longer viewed as a passive substrate awaiting implantation, but as a preconditioned biological system that may carry an intrinsic inflammatory and metabolic imprint. Therefore, the variability in transplant outcomes may not be determined solely by recipient factors or perioperative management but may also be encoded within the donor organ itself—potentially including microbial signals that reflect prior injury, exposure, and vulnerability.

The classical gut-liver axis, while still central, is insufficient to capture the complexity of the whole microbial ecosystem, but multiple interacting microbial compartments are involved (2). The intestinal microbiome remains the primary upstream source of microbial products, metabolites, and translocation pressure, particularly in the setting of cirrhosis, where barrier dysfunction is pronounced (2,4). The biliary compartment represents a distinct ecological niche shaped by bile composition, cholangiocyte integrity, and surgical reconstruction, and is particularly relevant for biliary complications, whereas in the intrahepatic compartment, microbial signals intersect directly with hepatic immune and metabolic pathways (2). These compartments are not independent but dynamically connected and profoundly perturbed by the transplantation process.

The results are limited by substantial inter-individual heterogeneity in microbial niche composition. Although graft-associated microbial DNA signatures were detectable and associated with clinical outcomes, their interpretation requires caution. A central limitation relates to the low-biomass nature of OPS, in which not only contamination by viable microbiota but also by microbial detritus might play a relevant role (13). The observed associations with outcomes support biological relevance, but do not conclusively establish the intrahepatic origin of the signal.

Sequencing of quite stable microbial DNA fragments demonstrates the presence of genetic material but does not allow differentiation between viable microorganisms, non-viable bacterial remnants, or translocated microbial products, but these scenarios have fundamentally different implications (14,15). The current data do not resolve this distinction, and as a result, the concept of an “intrahepatic microbiome” must be interpreted with caution. In this context, RNA-based approaches have been proposed as a complementary strategy, as they may preferentially capture transcriptionally active and potentially viable microbial communities (14). Current evidence does not support a clear methodological advance over DNA-based techniques, but in our experience, the RNA-based approach may support the reliability of such analysis (14,15).

The predictive modelling presented in the study is another aspect that warrants cautious interpretation. The reported performance metrics highlight the potential of microbiome-derived features as predictive biomarkers. However, high-dimensional datasets combined with relatively small sample sizes are inherently susceptible to overfitting (16). Although the inclusion of an independent validation cohort strengthens the robustness of the findings, broader validation across centers, donor populations, preservation protocols, and analytical pipelines will be essential to establish generalizability. Without such validation, the clinical applicability of these models remains uncertain.

Perhaps the most important limitation of the work by Lucas-Ruiz et al., however, is the lack of mechanistic insight. The association between microbial signatures and donor liver transcriptomic profiles is biologically plausible, but it remains correlative. It is unclear whether microbial signals actively contribute to graft inflammation and injury, whether they amplify pre-existing pathological processes, or whether they simply reflect underlying organ quality. Resolving this question will require functional studies that directly link microbial components to host pathways and clinical outcomes.

These limitations are particularly relevant when considering the translational implications of the study. Before clinical application, standardization of sampling procedures, sequencing methodologies, and analytical pipelines will be necessary to ensure reproducibility. Furthermore, a clear framework for clinical decision-making is currently lacking. It remains unclear how a high-risk microbial signature should influence organ acceptance, perioperative management, antimicrobial strategies, or immunosuppressive regimens. Lacking this clinical impact, microbiome profiling remains an informative but non-decisive layer of data.

Despite these challenges, the study provides relevant insights into transplantation research by demonstrating that intrahepatic microbial signals are associated with graft function and recovery (7). If they actively influence immune and metabolic pathways, they could represent novel targets for intervention.

Future research will need to address several key challenges. These include clarifying the biological nature of intrahepatic and biliary microbial signals and establishing mechanistic links between microbial patterns and host responses. Longitudinal and interventional studies will be essential to determine whether microbial signatures can be modified and whether such modifications translate into improved outcomes. Until then, the intrahepatic microbial signature should be viewed as a promising but still exploratory dimension of transplantation biology.
