Arthroscopic All-Suture 8-Shaped Fixation Technique for Patellar Fracture
Authors: Qiang zhuang Shi, Tong Pan, Xue fei Mei, Wen bo Cu, Zhen long Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Compared with other forms of fractures, patellar fractures are relatively rare. For the treatment of patellar fractures, a wide variety of surgical fixation methods have been developed. Nevertheless, most of them apply to transverse and comminuted fractures, and there is no surgical fixation method specifically for longitudinal patellar fractures. This article describes a technique for treating longitudinal patellar fractures with slight comminution by using an OD suture 8-shaped winding fixation method under arthroscopy. This technique is carried out under arthroscopic guidance throughout, taking full advantage of the feature of less tension at the fracture site during longitudinal fracture. No metal fixation materials are used, effectively limiting the pain caused by hard object stimulation, which is the most common postoperative complication. At the same time, it avoids the need for a second operation, which is helpful to alleviate the patient’s discomfort, thereby improving the therapeutic effect of longitudinal patellar fracture.

The patella is the largest sesamoid bone in the human body, located in front of the lower end of the femur, within the quadriceps tendon, with an overall wide upper and narrow lower shape, smooth at the back and rough at the front. The patella plays an important mechanical leverage role, moving the tendon of the quadriceps femoris forward, increasing the torque of the quadriceps femoris muscle, and improving the efficiency of knee extension.1,2 Furthermore, the patella reduces friction injuries between the quadriceps tendon and the cartilage of the femur. As an integral part of the knee joint, it also protects the joint and assists in maintaining its stability.

Patellar fractures account for about 1% of all fractures,3 but the number of patients has gradually increased in recent years with the development of construction, transportation, and other industries.4 The mechanisms of patellar fracture mainly include direct forces and indirect forces. Direct forces are thought to be a common cause of patellar fracture, namely, direct impact on the anterior aspect of the patella, which may be caused by falls or traffic accidents, and such injuries are prone to comminuted fractures accompanied by osteochondral damage.5 Indirect injuries, on the other hand, are caused by centrifugal contraction of the quadriceps femoris due to rapid flexion of the knee joint and usually cause transverse fractures with less joint damage.1

According to the AO classification proposed by Speck and Regazzoni,6 patellar fractures are categorized as A (longitudinal fracture), B (transverse fracture), and C (multifragmentary fracture). Among them, transverse fractures are the most common, and the incidence of longitudinal fractures is relatively low. For this reason, most current surgical treatments are developed for transverse or comminuted fractures,7,8 and there is a lack of surgical fixation methods specifically targeted at longitudinal fractures of the patella.

Currently, the treatment for patellar fractures mainly involves open reduction and internal fixation, with conservative treatment serving as a supplementary option.4 The modified anterior tension band (MATB) technique is a reliable technique for open reduction and internal fixation of patellar fractures, and this surgical technique is relatively mature.9 However, the direction of stress in longitudinal patellar fractures differs from that of transverse fractures, with lower tension and mainly shear forces parallel to the fracture line, making the MATB fixation, which is centered on converting tension into pressure, no longer applicable. In addition, the postoperative pain due to metal stimulation also affects the therapeutic efficacy of the MATB technique in treating patellar fractures.

The technique described in this article is the arthroscopic all-suture 8-shaped fixation technique for patellar fractures. This technique improves the fixation method of longitudinal patellar fractures by operating on the whole procedure arthroscopically, using OD sutures instead of hard internal fixation materials, eliminating the need for a second operation to remove the fixation device, and reducing soft tissue irritation and the risk of postoperative pain. On top of that, arthroscopic surgery has better aesthetics. In this article, we describe the technique of arthroscopic all-suture fixation and show it in Video 1.

Surgical Technique

Preoperative Evaluation

Physical examination, computed tomography, and magnetic resonance imaging are performed before surgery to evaluate the patellar fracture.

Preparation

The patient is placed in the supine position. Sterile preparation and draping are performed, and the anatomic landmarks on the knee joint are outlined.

Surgery Procedure

An anterolateral portal for arthroscopic imaging and examination is created. Viewing from the anterolateral portal, a puncture needle (Smith & Nephew) is used to locate the anteromedial portal.

A probe hook (Smith & Nephew) is introduced into the joint from the anteromedial portal to probe the articular surface. Then, the probe hook is removed from the anteromedial portal, and a shaver (Smith & Nephew) is inserted from this portal to remove soft tissue, accumulated blood, and broken bone fragments from the patellar surface (Fig 1). The upper surface of the patella is explored using a puncture needle to locate the fracture line at the site of the patellar fracture (Fig 2). The condition of the cartilage is examined from the inferior surface of the patella using a probe hook to inspect cartilage stability and whether there is any cartilage peeling. The exploration results show cracks in the cartilage surface and no peeling, and it can be overlapped by pressing. A suitable entry point is selected at the medial margin of the inferior pole of the patella, and then a guider (Smith & Nephew) is positioned at the selected entry point. With the aid of a guider, a channel, which serves as the inferior bone tunnel, is drilled using a 2.0-mm Kirschner wire (Smith & Nephew) (Fig 3). The fracture surface is explored again after drilling to check the alignment of the fracture line. The Kirschner wire is temporarily left in the inferior bone tunnel to help maintain patellar stability.Fig 3Arthroscopic image viewing from the anterolateral portal (the patient in the supine position and the patient side is right side) shows that the Kirschner wire (blue arrow) is being drilled with the assistance of the guider (black arrow).

Arthroscopic image viewing from the anterolateral portal (the patient in the supine position and the patient side is right side) shows that the Kirschner wire (blue arrow) is being drilled with the assistance of the guider (black arrow).

A puncture needle is passed through the surface of the patellar superior pole and used to locate the patellar superior pole (Fig 4). Then, in the same manner, a 2.0-mm Kirschner wire, which is parallel to the upper surface of the patella, is used to drill a hole in the middle of patellar medial margin as the superior bone tunnel. Using a cannulated suture passer (Smith & Nephew), the polydioxanone sulfate (PDS) coil is threaded through the superior bone tunnel at the medial margin of the patella (Fig 5) and out from the lateral margin. The double-strand OD sutures (Johnson & Johnson) are passed through the PDS coil, which in turn introduces the sutures into the bone tunnel. The Kirschner wire in the inferior bone tunnel is removed, and a straight suture passer (Smith & Nephew) is inserted to introduce the PDS coil from the lateral margin of the patella through the bone tunnel to the medial margin (Fig 6). The double-strand OD sutures are passed through the PDS coil by using a wire grasper (Smith & Nephew) to grasp the lateral head of sutures and the PDS coil out of the body. The soft tissue of the patellar surface is adequately cleaned with a shaver to reduce the patient’s postoperative reaction and to improve the fixation effect. The double-strand OD sutures are then passed through the inferior bone tunnel and out the lateral opening with the help of the PDS coil. The lateral head of the sutures is pulled to the medial opening of the superior bone tunnel by using a wire grasper. The sutures are then crossed from the lateral opening of the superior bone tunnel to the medial opening of the inferior bone tunnel, forming a figure 8 on the patellar surface (Fig 7), and then the ends of the sutures are grasped and tightened.Fig 6Arthroscopic image viewing from the anterolateral portal (the patient in the supine position and the patient side is right side) shows that the polydioxanone sulfate suture coil (arrow) is introduced into the medial margin of the patella through the bone tract.Fig 7Arthroscopic image viewing from the anterolateral portal (the patient in the supine position and the patient side is right side) showing the figure 8 shape (arrow) formed by the crossing of OD sutures on the surface of the patella.

The upper surface of the patella is explored by using a probe hook, separating soft tissue in the suture area. A shaver is inserted to remove the soft tissue on the patellar surface to improve the fixation result. The 2 strands of OD sutures are knotted several times and placed on the medial side of the patella, and then the sutures are cut. The stability of the sutures and fracture blocks is checked again by using a probe hook. Thus, the arthroscopic all-suture 8-shaped fixation of the patellar fracture is completed.

Discussion

This all-suture 8-shaped fixation technique makes use of the characteristic that the tension at the fracture site is relatively low when longitudinal patellar fractures occur. In addition, the use of OD sutures instead of metal fixators avoids the need to perform a second operation while fixing the fracture. The use of sutures also allows for micro-movements, promotes the formation of bone callus, and thereby facilitates natural healing. In the MATB technique, patients are prone to develop postoperative pain and other reactions related to metal foreign bodies,9,10 while the fixation technique described in this article reduces irritation and improves the repair effect of longitudinal patellar fractures. In addition, this technique is performed completely under arthroscopy, which is less traumatic for the patient. Therefore, it results in significantly less postoperative pain compared to open surgery and reduces the risk of complications. The minimally invasive nature of the procedure also means that rehabilitation training can begin sooner, and recovery is better.
