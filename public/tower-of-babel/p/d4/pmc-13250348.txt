Prothrombin complex concentrate for oral factor Xa inhibitor reversal prior to urgent invasive procedures
Authors: Mohammad Shamiea, Alex Osnis, Awan Kashoua, Orli Avnery, Gilad Itchaki, Shuli Isman, Tali Tohami, Osnat Jarchowsky‐Dolberg, Martin Ellis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOral factor Xa (FXa) inhibitors are anticoagulants with no approved reversal agent for use before urgent invasive procedures. Four‐factor prothrombin complex concentrate (4F‐PCC) is used off‐label for this despite limited evidence regarding its effectiveness and safety.AimsTo determine the efficacy of 4F‐PCC prior to urgent invasive procedures in FXa inhibitor‐treated patients. Secondary aims were hemoglobin levels and transfusion requirements within 48 h following the procedure, to determine the effect of treatment on anti‐FXa levels and to document thromboembolic events and mortality at 30 days.MethodsWe conducted a prospective, single‐center observational study of consecutive adults receiving 4F‐PCC for reversal of oral FXa inhibitors before urgent invasive procedures with moderate‐to‐high bleeding risk.ResultsFifty‐nine were included in the analysis (median age 78 years). Apixaban was the most frequently used anticoagulant (83.1%). 74.6% underwent urgent surgical procedures and 25.4% underwent other invasive procedures. The median 4F‐PCC dose was 2000 units. The primary endpoint of normal or mildly abnormal hemostasis was achieved in 89.8% of patients (84.7% normal, 5.1% mildly abnormal). About 10.2% had moderately abnormal hemostasis; no patients had severe hemostatic failure. Median hemoglobin decline was 1.5 g/dL, with 8.5% of patients requiring red blood cell transfusion. In 17 patients with paired measurements, median decrease in anti‐FXa activity was 42%. Thromboembolic events occurred in 5.1%, and 30‐day mortality was 10.2%.ConclusionsThromboembolic events were infrequent. These data support the use of 4F‐PCC for reversal of FXa inhibitors prior to urgent invasive procedures with a moderate or high bleeding risk.Keywords: factor Xa inhibitor reversal, hemostatic efficacy, prothrombin complex concentrate

INTRODUCTION

Direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) have largely replaced vitamin K antagonists in clinical practice because of their predictable pharmacokinetics, fewer drug interactions, and improved safety profile. Factor Xa (FXa) inhibitors, rivaroxaban, apixaban, and edoxaban are widely used for stroke prevention in atrial fibrillation and venous thromboembolism treatment, with efficacy and safety established in large, randomized trials. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4

A major challenge arises when rapid anticoagulation reversal is required before urgent invasive procedures. While the 2024 American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association guidelines recommend time‐based DOAC interruption for elective procedures with bleeding risk, urgent procedures—affecting 0.5%–1% of DOAC‐treated patients—may require immediate reversal. 5 While idarucizumab is a specific dabigatran antidote, 6 FXa inhibitor reversal is more challenging. Andexanet alfa has demonstrated efficacy in major bleeding trials 7 but in the setting of intracranial hemorrhage is associated with a high incidence of thromboembolism. 8 , 9 , 10 Notably, andexanet alfa is no longer commercially available following the manufacturer's decision not to pursue transition from accelerated to full Federal Drug Agency (FDA) approval.

Four‐factor prothrombin complex concentrate (4F‐PCC) is used off‐label for FXa inhibitor reversal and despite limited prospective data, current guidelines from the European Society of Anesthesiology and Intensive Care (ESAIC) and the International Society on Thrombosis and Hemostasis (ISTH) guidelines conditionally recommend its use at doses of 25–50 U/kg. 11 , 12 , 13

We conducted this prospective study to evaluate the efficacy and safety of 4F‐PCC for FXa inhibitor reversal in patients undergoing urgent invasive procedures with a moderate‐to‐high risk of bleeding.

METHODS

Study design

This was a prospective, single‐center, observational study conducted at Meir Medical Center between July 2024 and July 2025. Consecutive adult patients who received 4F‐PCC for reversal of oral FXa inhibitors prior to urgent invasive procedures with a moderate‐to‐high risk of bleeding were enrolled.

Inclusion criteria were: (1) age ≥18 years; (2) current treatment with an oral FXa inhibitor (apixaban, rivaroxaban, or edoxaban); and (3) administration of 4F‐PCC prior to an urgent invasive procedure considered to carry a moderate‐to‐high procedural bleeding risk, as determined in accordance with ISTH peri‐procedural anticoagulation guidance and following multidisciplinary assessment by the treating proceduralist and hematologist.

Exclusion criteria were

(1) concomitant antiplatelet therapy; (2) venous thromboembolism (deep vein thrombosis or pulmonary embolism) within the preceding 14 days; (3) postponement of the procedure to more than 18 h after 4F‐PCC administration; (4) procedures considered to carry a low procedural bleeding risk according to ISTH peri‐procedural guidance, for which anticoagulant reversal was not deemed clinically indicated; (5) use of 4F‐PCC for active bleeding rather than for pre‐procedural anticoagulant reversal; or (6) incomplete documentation of procedure‐related hemostasis.

Treatment protocol

Andexanet alfa was not available at our institution during the study period, and all patients were managed according to a standardized institutional protocol requiring hematologist approval before 4F‐PCC administration. The reversal agent used was Octaplex® (Octapharma), prepared and dispensed by the hospital pharmacy. 4F‐PCC was administered intravenously at a dose of 25–50 units/kg over 15–20 min in 100 mL of 0.9% NaCl, consistent with manufacturer recommendations and institutional guidelines. The exact dose was determined at the discretion of the treating hematologist, considering comorbidities, renal function, and time from last FXa inhibitor intake. Anti‐FXa activity was measured immediately prior to 4F‐PCC infusion and again 30–60 min post‐infusion. The results of these tests were not available at the time of 4F‐PCC administration nor at the time of performance of the invasive procedure and were not intended for use in clinical decision making.

DOAC measurement

Apixaban and rivaroxaban levels were measured using a commercially available kit, HemosIL Liquid anti‐ Xa® (Werfen, UK) on the ACL TOP550 hemostasis testing system according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Data collection

All clinical and laboratory data were prospectively collected within 48 h of 4F‐PCC administration by a single investigator (M.S.) using a standardized electronic case report form (Excel®, Microsoft). Variables included patient demographics, comorbidities, indication for anticoagulation, FXa inhibitor used, time of last anticoagulant dose, procedure type, timing, and dose of 4F‐PCC relative to the procedure. Peri‐procedural hemoglobin values, type and volume of blood product transfusions from admission through 48 h post‐procedure, documentation of procedure‐related bleeding and thrombotic complications up to 30 days post‐procedure were documented.

Outcomes

The primary efficacy endpoint was hemostatic efficacy, defined as the proportion of patients achieving normal or mildly abnormal hemostasis, assessed using methodology adapted from A Study of the RE‐VERSal Effects of Idarucizumab on Active Dabigatran (RE‐VERSE AD) trial of idarucizumab for dabigatran reversal. 6 Hemostatic outcomes were adjudicated from operative or procedural reports documented by the treating proceduralist (surgeon, interventional radiologist, or endoscopist) and classified as: (1) Normal hemostasis, defined as bleeding within the expected range for the procedure; (2) Mildly abnormal hemostasis, defined as greater‐than‐expected bleeding that did not require hemostatic intervention or blood transfusion. When intraoperative blood loss was not explicitly documented, hemostasis was classified as normal; (3) Moderately abnormal hemostasis, defined as bleeding requiring focal hemostatic intervention or meeting ISTH criteria for major bleeding, including a hemoglobin decrease of ≥2 g/dL, transfusion of packed red blood cells within 48 h, or bleeding resulting in end‐organ damage; and (4) Severely abnormal hemostasis, defined as uncontrolled bleeding requiring major intervention or leading to premature termination of the procedure. Secondary endpoints included absolute and relative hemoglobin change within 48 h of the procedure, utilization of blood products (packed red blood cells, plasma, and platelets) intraoperatively and within 48 h, and the effect on FXa inhibitor activity assessed by comparing pre‐ and post‐infusion anti‐FXa levels. Safety endpoints included objectively confirmed arterial or venous thromboembolic events within 30 days (deep vein thrombosis, pulmonary embolism, ischemic stroke/transient ischemic attack, myocardial infarction, or acute limb ischemia) and 30‐day mortality.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were recorded as numbers and percentages and continuous variables as medians with interquartile ranges (IQR). Outcome events were calculated as proportions. Paired pre‐ and post‐administration laboratory values (hemoglobin and anti‐FXa activity) were compared using nonparametric paired tests (Wilcoxon signed‐rank test). Missing data were not imputed.

Ethics approval

The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee (Helsinki Committee) at Meir Medical Center prior to initiation of data collection. Patient consent was waived for this study.

RESULTS

During the study period, 70 patients were screened for eligibility. Of these, 59 patients met inclusion criteria and were enrolled, and 10 were excluded: 3 patients received 4F‐PCC but underwent procedures >18 h after administration, 3 received 4F‐PCC for procedures not classified as moderate‐to‐high bleeding risk, 3 received 4F‐PCC >24 h after last anticoagulant dose, and 1 patient was excluded because of concurrent aspirin use.

The median age of enrolled patients was 78 years (73–84), with 31 (52.5%) men and 28 (47.5%) women. Median body weight was 78 kg (67–87), and the median estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was 54 mL/min/1.73 m2 (31–77) (Table 1).

The indication for anticoagulant reversal was surgery in 44 patients (74.6%) and other invasive procedures with moderate‐to‐high bleeding risk in 15 patients (25.4%) (Table S1).

Primary outcome

Normal or mildly abnormal hemostasis was achieved in 53 of 59 patients (89.8%). Specifically, 50 patients (84.7%) had normal hemostasis, three (5.1%) had mildly abnormal hemostasis, and six (10.2%) had moderately abnormal hemostasis. These patients are described in detail in Table S2. No patient had severely abnormal hemostasis.

Secondary outcomes

The median hemoglobin at admission was 12.7 g/dL (10.5–14). The median nadir hemoglobin within 48 h post‐procedure was 10.9 g/dL (9.1–12.5), for a median reduction in hemoglobin of 1.5 g/dL (0.5–2.0). Five patients (8.5%) required transfusion of packed red blood cells and a total of 12 units were administered. No plasma nor platelet transfusions were required.

Anti‐FXa activity was measured in 38 patients. Paired pre‐ and post‐4F‐PCC anti‐FXa levels were available in 17 patients. In this subgroup, the median pre‐4F‐PCC anti‐FXa level was 155 ng/mL (85.5–187.5), compared with 106 ng/mL (IQR 25–131) post‐4F‐PCC, corresponding to a median reduction of 42% (13–67.3) (Table 3).

Safety outcomes

Three patients (5.1%) had thromboembolic complications within 30 days: two had ischemic strokes and one had an ST‐elevation myocardial infarction (Table S3), six patients (10.2%) died within 30 days of treatment (Table S4).

DISCUSSION

In this prospective single‐center study, 4F‐PCC was effective in achieving reversal of the anticoagulant effect of oral FXa inhibitors in patients requiring urgent invasive procedures. Nearly 90% of patients underwent these procedures with normal or only mildly abnormal hemostasis, and there were no cases of severe hemostatic failure, underscoring the reliability of 4F‐PCC as a reversal strategy in the urgent pre‐procedure setting. The 30‐day thromboembolic event rate was 5.1%, which is lower than the approximately 10% observed with andexanet alfa in the ANNEXA‐4 trial of patients with major bleeding. 7 and considerably lower than that reported for the pre‐procedure use of andexanet alfa in a retrospective analysis of 44 patients undergoing urgent procedures among whom thromboembolic events occurred in 27.3% and mortality in 34.1%. 14 Furthermore, in the United States andexanet alfa is more expensive than 4F‐PCC associated with mean hospitalization costs in United states of approximately $22,000 per patient versus $5500–$6700, respectively, when used for similar indications. 15 , 16

Our findings are consistent with previous studies. Schulman's smaller Prothrombin complex concentrate for direct factor Xa inhibitor‐associated bleeding or before urgent surgery (PROXY) study of 20 FXa inhibitor‐treated patients receiving 4F‐PCC prior to urgent invasive procedures which demonstrated adequate hemostasis in 80% of patients, with only one asymptomatic thrombotic event. 17 Larger retrospective cohorts have reported comparable efficacy rates and low thromboembolic event rates ranging from 2% to 7%. 18 Collectively, these results, together with our prospective data, support the safety and effectiveness of 4F‐PCC for pre‐procedure FXa inhibitor reversal.

A notable laboratory finding in our study was a median 42% reduction in anti‐FXa activity among 17 patients with paired measurements (median 155 ng/mL pre‐4F‐PCC vs. 106 ng/mL post‐4F‐PCC, within 30–60 min). This observation was unexpected, as 4F‐PCC does not bind or neutralize FXa inhibitors and therefore should not alter anti‐FXa levels. 19 , 20 4F‐PCC appears to be effective in reversing the anticoagulant effect of FXa inhibitors by replenishing vitamin K‐dependent clotting factors, allowing for the restoration of thrombin generation while the inhibitor remains present and quantifiable in plasma. 21 The consistent reductions observed raise important mechanistic questions: do these findings represent assay interference, alterations in plasma factor composition, or an unrecognized pharmacologic effect of 4F‐PCC? This hypothesis‐generating observation warrants confirmation and further study.

Importantly, all 38 patients with available pre‐procedural anti‐FXa measurements had levels within the therapeutic range, with values exceeding 50 ng/mL, indicating clinically relevant anticoagulant activity at the time of presentation. According to ISTH guidance, drug‐specific anti‐FXa levels >50 ng/mL are consistent with therapeutic anticoagulation and may warrant reversal in urgent procedural settings. Notably, anti‐FXa activity did not influence the decision to administer 4F‐PCC in this study; reversal decisions were based exclusively on clinical criteria, namely the urgency and bleeding risk of the procedure and anamnesis confirming that the last FXa inhibitor dose had been taken within the preceding 24 h, as detailed in the Methods section. In this clinical context, the finding that all measured anti‐FXa levels exceeded 50 ng/mL is expected and further supports the appropriateness of anticoagulant reversal in our cohort.

Six patients in our cohort had moderately abnormal hemostasis resulting from the invasive procedure that was generally attributable to complex clinical circumstances or critical illness rather than 4F‐PCC failure. Of these, two were patients with sepsis who required blood transfusions and subsequently died of multiorgan failure.

Regarding safety, two patients developed ischemic stroke within days of 4F‐PCC administration; one was fatal and may plausibly represent a 4F‐PCC‐related thrombotic complication. In contrast, an ST‐elevation myocardial infarction that occurred 29 days after 4F‐PCC administration, despite resumption of anticoagulation, was unlikely to be causally related.

Concerns regarding the safety of Andexanet alfa, particularly thromboembolic complications, have been well described, with thromboembolic event rates of approximately 10% reported in pivotal trials and subsequent analyses, including ANNEXA‐I and ANNEXA‐4. In parallel, studies evaluating 4F‐PCC for FXa inhibitor reversal have generally reported lower thromboembolic event rates, typically in the range of approximately 5%, including cohorts analyzed alongside andexanet alfa. 22 Although andexanet alfa demonstrated efficacy in controlled clinical trials, its real‐world use has been constrained by safety concerns, substantial acquisition costs, and limited feasibility of widespread implementation. Recently, andexanet alfa was withdrawn from the United States market following the manufacturer's decision not to pursue conversion from accelerated to full FDA approval. As a result, 4F‐PCC currently represents the only widely available pharmacologic option for FXa inhibitor reversal in US clinical practice, underscoring the importance of real‐world data evaluating its effectiveness and safety in urgent clinical settings.

Other deaths occurred in patients with advanced malignancy, severe sepsis, or postoperative complications despite adequate hemostasis, indicating that most deaths were related to the patients' underlying disease or related complications rather than to 4F‐PCC.

Limitations of our study include its single‐center nature, which may restrict generalizability; absence of neurosurgical and cardiovascular procedures in our cohort, where reversal strategies may present distinct challenges; reliance on a proceduralist to estimate and quantify bleeding; and the lack of a control group of patients undergoing urgent invasive procedures without anticoagulant reversal, which was not feasible for ethical and practical reasons in this clinical setting.

In contrast, the prospective design, systematic adjudication of hemostatic outcomes using validated RE‐VERSE AD criteria, 6 and comprehensive 30‐day follow‐up represent notable strengths of our study. We also collected detailed peri‐procedural data, including hemoglobin levels, transfusion requirements, proceduralist‐estimated blood loss, and need for intervention to treat bleeding complications, providing a multidimensional assessment of hemostatic efficacy.

In conclusion, our data suggest that 4F‐PCC appears to be safe and effective for the reversal of oral FXa inhibitors prior to urgent invasive procedures supporting current guideline recommendations for using 4F‐PCC for this indication.
