Improving sensitivity and drug tolerance of assays for neutralizing anti-drug antibodies to semaglutide and native GLP-1
Authors: Nicoline Videbæk, Louise Jørgensen, Carina de Lemos Rieper, Lone Hummelshøj, Steffan Svejgaard Petersen, Dorthe Bianca Corlin Wøldike, Lars Ole Andresen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimThe purpose of this work was to optimize the sensitivity and the drug tolerance of two cell-based assays for the detection of neutralizing antibodies (nAbs) to semaglutide (a GLP-1 analogue) and to endogenous GLP-1.MethodologyThe two assays were developed and validated in three distinct iterations. Enhancements in sensitivity and drug tolerance were achieved through platform optimization, sample pre-treatment and the alteration of control antibodies.ResultsThe sensitivity and drug tolerance improved gradually with the different versions of the assays. For detection of nAbs to semaglutide, sensitivity was improved from 3,400 ng to 98 ng/ml antibody, and drug tolerance was improved from 2.5 nM semaglutide when detecting 3,400 ng/ml antibodies to 4.8–5.6 nM semaglutide when detecting 1,000 ng/ml antibody. For the endogenous GLP-1 assay, sensitivity was improved from 6,900 ng/ml to 46 ng/ml antibody, and drug tolerance improved from 1.0 nM semaglutide when detecting antibody concentration of 8,800 ng/ml to 2.5 nM semaglutide when detecting 1,000 ng/ml antibody.ConclusionKey factors for enhancing the sensitivity and drug tolerance of the assays included the concentration of the drug standard for receptor activation, pre-treatment of the samples, and better understanding of the binding properties of the control antibody.KEYWORDS: Anti-drug-antibodies, optimization, neutralizing antibody assay, semaglutide, glucagon-like peptide-1

Introduction

Assessment of the immunogenicity of drugs is an integral part of the development and clinical evaluation of biopharmaceuticals [1,2]. The formation of anti-drug antibodies (ADAs) may have various effects ranging from negligible impact to undesirable interference with the activity of the drug or serious immune-mediated reactions such as hypersensitivity or induction of autoimmune responses.

The laboratory methods used for detection and characterization of ADAs follow a tiered approach. This approach consists of 1) screening of blood samples for ADA-positive reactors, followed by 2) confirmation of the positive reactions, and successively 3) characterizing the response by determination of titer, cross-reactivity to any endogenous counterpart, and identification of samples with in vitro neutralizing ADAs [1,2]. In cases where the drug is an analogue of a naturally occurring substance with a unique function, nAbs may also inhibit the activity of the native protein, thereby potentially leading to clinical deficiency type syndrome. Therefore, testing for nAbs is not limited to evaluating the effect of the ADAs interference with the function of the drug, but also interactions with the endogenous counterpart including neutralizing effect. Often, modestly varied protocols for the same assay can be used for testing for nAbs to drug and to endogenous counterpart [1].

Investigating ADA for neutralizing effect is considered important by authorities [1,2] and activity-based assays (most often cell based) representing the drug mode of action are generally recommended as format. However, developing fit-for-purpose cell-based nAb assays present multiple challenges, especially when excessive drug level in patient samples interferes with nAb detection and decreases assay sensitivity [3].

Semaglutide, a glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonist (GLP-1RA) class drug, is applied in treatment of Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2D) (Ozempic, s.c. and Rybelsus, oral) [4], and overweight/obesity in adults (Wegovy, s.c.) [5]. Semaglutide is a long-acting analogue of human glucagon-like peptide-1 (hGLP-1) with 94% amino acid identity to human GLP-1. The long-acting effect of semaglutide is attributable to a covalently linked fatty acid moiety that facilitates binding to albumin. This results in an extended half-life of the compound and favorable pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic characteristics [6].

Cell-based assays for assessment of in vitro neutralizing activity of anti-semaglutide antibodies and their cross-neutralizing effect on native human GLP-1 were validated for use in clinical trials. Semaglutide administered once-weekly by subcutaneous injection was approved on the basis of these trials [7]. However, with the FDA approval of the first semaglutide-based medical product Novo Nordisk received a post marketing commitment (PMC) requesting Novo Nordisk to develop and validate new nAb assays with improved sensitivity and drug tolerance. These regulatory requirements, together with the need to robustly assess potential immune responses against both semaglutide and the endogenous GLP-1, were the major drivers of the work described here.

This paper presents the development and validation of three different versions of two cell-based assays for detection of in vitro nAbs to semaglutide and to the endogenous counterpart GLP-1. Results of the development and optimization efforts leading to assays with improved sensitivity and drug tolerance are presented, with a discussion of key parameters influencing the sensitivity and drug tolerance.

Materials and methods

Assay type and platform

Briefly, the cell-based assay involves the use of baby hamster kidney (BHK) cells transfected with genes encoding the hGLP-1 receptor. The assay detects GLP-1 receptor activation through measuring cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP) formation, activation of luciferase, and quantification of light emission in Relative Luminescence Units (RLU). The neutralizing effect of ADA is evaluated by measuring the inhibition of the drug-induced receptor activation through quantification of the inhibition of light emission.

In vitro cell-based neutralizing antibody detection assay

One day prior to performing the neutralizing assay, BHK cells FCW467-12A (Novo Nordisk A/S) transfected with a human GLP-1 receptor plasmid (pbw302) and a luciferase reporter gene plasmid (pKZ10, CRE luciferase/DHFR) were thawed from −80°C and directly suspended in maintenance medium (Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM, Gibco, 31,053, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Carlsbad, CA USA) with 10% fetal calf serum (FCS) (Heat inactivated FCS, Gibco, 16140, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Carlsbad, CA USA), 1 mM Na-pyruvate (Sigma-Aldrich, S8636, Merck, Schnelldorf, Germany), 220 nM methotrexate (Pfizer 016396, New York, NY, USA), 1 mg/ml Geneticin G418 (Gibco,10131027, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Carlsbad, CA USA) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin 5000 U/mL (Gibco 15070063, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Carlsbad, CA USA). Subsequently, 100 μl/well of 7 × 104 cells/ml in maintenance medium were seeded into 96-well plates (Nunc 260251, Roskilde, Denmark) and allowed to rest for 40 min at approx. 18–22°C (room temperature) in a flow cabinet before being incubated overnight at approx. 37°C with approx. 5% CO2.

On the day of the analysis, antibody positive serum samples were heat-inactivated at 56°C. The inactivated samples, negative control (QC neg), the maximal stimulation control (MAX), and positive QC samples were then incubated with final concentrations (as shown in Table 1) of either semaglutide (NNC0113-000–0217, Novo Nordisk, Denmark) or GLP-1 (G8147, Sigma-Aldrich, Merck, Schnelldorf, Germany) + 30 µM Val-pyr (Novo Nordisk A/S) for 30 min at room temperature on a plate shaker. Appropriate concentrations of GLP-1 receptor agonists (semaglutide or GLP-1/Val-pyr) were prepared in assay medium consisting of DMEM without phenol red (Gibco,11880028, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Carlsbad, CA USA), 10 mM HEPES (Gibco,15630, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Carlsbad, CA USA) 1% GlutaMAX (Gibco, 35050061, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Carlsbad, CA USA) and 20% FCS.

The maintenance medium was discarded from the BHK cells in the 96-well plates, and 100 μl of the pre-incubated GLP-1 receptor agonist/sample mix was added to the wells of the plate according to the plate layout. Plates were incubated for 3 hours at 37°C and 5% CO2.

Following incubation, the supernatant was discarded, and 200 μl lysis buffer was added to each well. Lysis buffer was a 1:1 mixture of SteadyLite Plus (SteadyLite Plus Reporter Gene Assay System, Perkin Elmer, 6066759, Shelton, CT, USA) and assay medium. The cells were lysed for approx. 15 minutes in the dark at room temperature. Subsequently, luminescence was measured in relative light units (RLU) using a Tecan plate reader (GeniosPRO and Infinite F500, Tecan, Mannedorf, Switzerland). Samples were tested either in triplicate (version 1) or in duplicate determinations (version 2 and 3).

Quality controls

A set of negative and positive controls, prepared in serum pools sourced from healthy individuals, were utilized in the various versions of the assays (Table 1). The negative controls represented nonspecific assay background (NSB), maximal cellular stimulation in presence of a standard drug concentration (MAX), and a QC neg representing the absence of nAbs. NSB and MAX were employed for calculation of % neutralizing effect (% N) and the stimulation index (SI).

The SI denotes the ratio (as a whole number) between the MAX and the NSB obtained in the individual assay plates. Acceptance criteria for SI was SI > 3.

The positive controls represented a low concentration of nAbs (QC low) and a high concentration of nAbs (QC high), respectively. In the two latest assay versions, QC low was divided in QC low1 and QC low2, where QC low1 was determined as [Mean sensitivity] + [(one-sided t0.01,df x SD] and QC low2 was calculated as 1.5 ×QC low1 and 2 ×QC low1, in version 2 and version 3, respectively.

All QC samples, including MAX and NSB, were prepared in commercially obtained pools of heat-inactivated healthy human serum.

A range of different monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) (Novo Nordisk) served as positive controls in the different versions of the neutralizing antibody assays and are listed in (Table 1).

In the anti-semaglutide nAb assay, an anti-GLP-1 monoclonal antibody (mAb); GLIP-C1 F27 (Novo Nordisk, Denmark) was used as control antibody in the first and second version. In the final version, two human IgG mAbs, NNC1212-7141 and NNC1212-7148 (Novo Nordisk, Beijing, China) identified and maturated using a yeast display system (Adimab, Lebanon, New Hampshire, USA) were tested as positive controls.

In the anti-GLP-1 nAb assay, an equimolar mix of three different anti-GLP-1 mAbs; GLIP-C1 F27, Mab26.1 and GLP F5A4 (Novo Nordisk, Denmark) were used as positive controls in the first and second version. In the final version of the assay, the human IgG mAbs; NNC1212-7141 and NNC1212-7148 (Novo Nordisk, Beijing, China) were tested as positive controls.

Statistical calculations

The Magellan software, version 7.1 for the Tecan plate reader (Magellan Tracker v. 7.1, Tecan, Mannedorf, Switzerland) was employed for calculation of mean RLU, %CV of the determinations of samples and QC samples, the percent neutralization relative to the maximal RLU response, %N, and the SI. Microsoft Excel, version 2016 (Microsoft Corporation/USA) and SAS.JMP, version 12, (JMP/USA) were utilized for all other statistical calculations. Outlier tests were conducted using the inter-quartile range method during data distribution analysis of the data in SAS.JMP to identify and exclude any outliers from the calculations.

Validation

Validation of the ADA assays for nAb detection toward semaglutide and endogenous GLP-1 was performed according to guidelines effective at the time of validation and white papers [1–3,8–11]. The validation parameters sensitivity, selectivity, drug interference, drug tolerance, assay precision, and robustness were each meticulously addressed, approved, and evaluated for each version of the assay. The details of the methods employed during the validation of the three versions of the assays are provided in the supplementary methods and supplementary results.

Method optimization

Influence of serum and minimal required dilution (MRD) on assay sensitivity

The impact of FCS concentration in the assay medium on the assay sensitivity of the semaglutide nAb assay was investigated by adding 0.1%, 2.5%, 5% and 10% FCS, respectively, to the assay medium. Dose responses to dilutions of semaglutide ranging from 6 pg/ml to 3.0 ng/ml were performed in the different assay media. The standard FCS concentration in the assay medium was 20%, as mentioned previously.

The influence of serum sample volume on the assay sensitivity in the absence or presence of different concentrations of semaglutide (1.25, 5.0, and 10.0 nM) was tested at a low (1%) and at a high (20%, standard) concentration of FCS in the assay medium. The assay sensitivity was determined by testing serum sample volumes corresponding to 5%, 10%, 20%, or 30% (corresponding to MRDs of 20, 10, 5, and 3.33) in assay medium with either 1% or 20% FCS. Additionally, the average standard deviations of individual serum samples from drug-naïve individuals, measured in the semaglutide nAb assay with 1% and 20% FCS in the assay medium, were compared.

Sample pre-treatment

Heat treatment

Before analysis, all samples underwent heat-inactivation at 56°C for 60 minutes in an incubator or for 30 minutes in a water bath. As all QC samples were prepared in heat-inactivated serum, they were not subjected to further heat pre-treatment.

Sample pre-incubation with drug

All samples and all controls, except the NSB, were incubated with the GLP-1RA (semaglutide or GLP-1) toward which the nAb-activity was measured, prior to addition to the wells in the nAb assay. The concentrations of semaglutide and GLP-1 used for pre-incubation stimulation of the BHK-cells before measuring the light emission was optimized by measuring the cellular response to 10-fold dilutions of either semaglutide (1.0 µg/ml − 1 pg/ml) or GLP-1 (1.5 µg/ml − 1.5 × 10−4 pg/ml). Dose response curves were generated using GraphPad Prism version 9 to calculate the effective concentration (EC) at various EC-values for activation of the cells (see Supplementary Tables, Table S4).

Polyethylene glycol (PEG) precipitation

The optimal concentration of PEG6000 for precipitating anti-semaglutide antibodies while minimizing precipitation of semaglutide and human serum albumin (HSA) was determined using a Gamma Counter Wizard 3470 (Perkin Elmer, Waltham, MA, USA) with the WorkOut Plus software. The method used was similar to a radioimmunoassay (RIA). Briefly, 66.6 nCi/ml of either 125I-labeled mAb to semaglutide; GLIP-C-1F27, 125I-labeled HSA and 125I-labeled semaglutide, respectively, in samples of human serum pool (total sample volume was 85 µl) was precipitated by adding a stock solution of (Merck 807,491, Darmstadt, Germany) in TBS-buffer consisting of 0.01. M Trizma® base and 0.1% sodium chloride (Merck 106,404, Darmstadt, Germany), pH 8.6, to reach a final concentration of either 20%, 15%, 11.3%, or 8.4% PEG6000.

Tubes were vortexed for approx. 10 seconds and centrifuged for approx. 15 minutes at 2620 relative centrifugal force (RCF) and 4°C. After centrifugation the supernatant was removed, and tubes inverted to allow remaining liquid to be absorbed by a single use cellulose sheet, while ensuring that the precipitate remained in the tube.

A set-up of an analysis always had the following order of controls and samples: 5 totals, the samples in the set-up and finally 2 totals. All samples were analyzed in duplicates.

All tubes including the totals were placed in a Gamma Counter Wizard 3470 (Perkin Elmer, Waltham, MA, USA) in the above-mentioned sequence and counted – all on the same day – for at least 120 seconds each.

The gamma counter software WorkOut Plus calculated the mean counts per minute (cpm) of the totals, which was set to 100%, the mean of the duplicates of each sample % precipitated protein as the mean of the sample relative to the mean value of the first 5 totals, %B/T, with one decimal.

The mean %B/T values were used for calculation of the ratio between precipitated semaglutide and precipitated anti-semaglutide antibody, and the ratio between precipitated HSA and anti-semaglutide antibody.

Other control antibodies

Several polyclonal anti-semaglutide antibodies were developed by immunizing rabbits and goats using either Imject Mariculture Keyhole Limpet hemocyanin (mcKLH) coupled or cationized Bovine Serum Albumin (cBSA) coupled semaglutide emulsified in incomplete Freunds adjuvant.

Additionally, two anti-semaglutide mAbs (NNC1212-7141 and NNC1212-7148) were selected for test from a panel of anti-semaglutide mAbs generated after two rounds of maturation using an antibody discovery platform from Adimab, Lebanon NH, USA.

Surface plasmon resonance (SPR) measurements

Experiments were performed on a Biacore T200 (Cytiva, Uppsala, Sweden) using a Protein A sensor chip. A capture assay was set up, capturing each of the mAbs NNC1212-7148 and NNC1212-7141 (0.5 µg/mL) on the sensor chip surface for 160 sec at a flow rate of 10 µL/min. Following the capture step, semaglutide in 0.1 M HEPES, 1.5 M NaCl, 0.03 M EDTA and 0.5% v/v Surfactant P20, pH 7.4, was injected at concentrations of 1, 2.5, 5, 10 and 25 nM at 25°C and a flowrate of 50 µL/min. The complexes were allowed to associate for 400 sec and to dissociate for 800 sec. The surfaces were regenerated with five 30-sec pulses of Glycine-HCl pH 1.5. Data were collected at a rate of 10 Hz. The resultant sensor-grams were analyzed using the Biacore T200 Evaluation Software (version 3.0) where data were globally fitted to a 1:1 interaction model. Similar experiments were performed with the mAb GLIP-C1 F27 at 2 µg/ml and 20 µg/ml loads, respectively, using an Octet HTX instrument (ForteBio, Fremont, California, USA) and semaglutide concentrations of 12.5, 25, 50 and 100 nM. For GLIP-C1 F27 the association and dissociation time was 300 sec each. Data analysis was done using Octet Data analysis HTsoftware (ForteBio, Fremont, California, USA).

The sensitivities and drug tolerances of each assay were determined using the control antibodies also employed as QC samples in each assay. The sensitivities of the two assays in absence of drug were improved from 3400 ng/ml (anti-semaglutide nAb) and 6900 ng/ml (anti-GLP-1 nab) in the initial versions of the assays to 98 ng/ml and 46 ng/ml, respectively, in the final versions of the assays (Figure 1, Table 2 and Table 3). Assay neutralization cut point (NCP) and normalization factors (NF)

Determination of the assay NCP is necessary to reliably determine the sensitivity of an assay. Assay NCP and NF were calculated as described in the supplementary methods and supplemental results. The median NCP was 30 for all matrices tested. Healthy matrix had slightly lower NCPs than other matrices tested (Table 4). However, even when NCPs were based on non-healthy trial populations, the QC low samples prepared in healthy matrix were still above NCP (data not shown). Hence, healthy matrix could be used for preparation of positive controls.

Optimization of assay platform

Optimization of the platform itself included among other things, reevaluation of seeding cell densities, incubation periods, as well as evaluation of %FCS in assay medium, minimum required dilutions (MRD) of samples and drug concentrations. The initially chosen seeding cell density was confirmed to be acceptable and thus remained unchanged. Incubation times were tested at 1, 3 and 5 hours. While one hour was found to be insufficient for achieving the best SI, 5 hours incubation was deemed optimal. However, for practical reasons and as the original 3-hours incubation resulted in acceptable SIs, this duration of incubation was retained. The MRD was evaluated in conjunction with the assessment of the concentration of FCS in the assay medium.

Assay medium and FCS concentration

The mode of protraction for semaglutide involves binding to serum albumin through the fatty acid linker. Since semaglutide binds 99% more strongly to albumin than to the GLP-1 receptor, the level of FCS in assay medium may impact the amount of drug required to stimulate the cells. Therefore, semaglutide dose response curves were tested with varying FCS concentration in the assay medium (Figure 2). As predicted, an increase in FCS concentration resulted in a right-shift of the curves, indicating that a lower FCS concentration may lead to better sensitivity due to the reduced drug concentration needed for stimulation of the cells.

Dose response curves obtained with different concentrations of fetal calf serum (FCS) in the assay medium. A right-shift of the curves is seen with increasing FCS concentration. The same right-shift in dose response curves was not observed in the nAb assay for anti-drug antibodies (ADAs) to human glucagon-like protein-1 (GLP-1) as GLP-1 is not acylated and therefore does not bind to albumin (results not shown).

A similar right-shift in dose-response curves was not observed when stimulating the cells with hGLP-1, as the GLP-1 does not bind to albumin (results not shown).

The nAb assay sensitivity and drug tolerance was assessed using assay medium with a low (1%) and a high (20%) concentration of FCS, along with different sample volumes corresponding to MRD from 3.33 to 20. Results indicated that 30% of sample (MRD = 3.33) in combination with a high (20%) concentration FCS in the assay medium, in contrast to 1% FCS, yielded the best sensitivity (Table 5). This was confirmed in both the absence and presence of semaglutide. The lower FCS concentration in the assay medium yielded a greater variation in the individual serum samples. Thus, the standard deviation (SD) between determinations of human drug naïve individual samples was much higher with lower amounts of FCS (1% FCS) compared with 20% FCS in the assay medium. Due to the high SD the NCP becomes higher and yields a poorer sensitivity (Figure 3).

Determination of drug standard concentration

Various concentrations of drug used for pre-incubation of samples and controls in the different versions of the assays corresponding to EC values between EC60 and EC89 were tested (Supplemental methods and Supplementary results and Supplementary Tables, Table S4). Lowering the standard concentration of drug naturally leads to better sensitivity, but not better drug tolerance.

Sample pre-treatment

Various pre-treatment regimes, such as PEG precipitation, Acid and PEG treatment, SPEAD, heat-inactivation of drug, HSA saturation of samples, and others, were evaluated to improve the drug tolerance of the assay. Among the numerous tested approaches, optimization of the PEG concentration was the only sample pre-treatment that improved drug tolerance of the assay.

Results from the PEG precipitation experiments on the anti-semaglutide antibody GLIP-C-1F27, semaglutide and HSA, respectively, using PEG6000 are presented in Figure 4. The results demonstrate that the ratio between precipitated semaglutide and antibody, and the ratio between precipitated HSA and antibody decreased with decreasing PEG6000 concentration. Hence, the optimal PEG6000 concentration for precipitation of anti-semaglutide antibodies while minimizing precipitation of semaglutide and HSA was a final concentration of approximately 10% PEG6000. Using PEG1000 and PEG600 in similar experiments did not result in better precipitation of anti-semaglutide antibodies compared to the use of PEG6000 (results not shown).

Change of control antibodies

Both polyclonal rabbit anti-semaglutide antibodies and polyclonal goat anti-semaglutide antibodies were tested in the in vitro neutralizing assay for anti-semaglutide antibodies but did not demonstrate improved performance compared to the original GLIP-C1 F27 (results not shown).

Two human IgG mAbs identified using the Adimab discovery platform (NNC1212-7148 and NNC1212-7141) and a mouse mAb GLIP-C1-F27 were tested for binding properties. NNC1212-7148 and NNC1212-7141 were utilized in the validation of the final assays, with the latter being employed for the positive QC samples.

The equilibrium constant, KD, for all the tested mAbs was in the nanomolar to picomolar range, with GLIP-C1 F27 having the highest affinity, KD < 10−12 M and the two human IgG mAbs (NNC1212-7148 and NNC1212-7141) having slightly lower affinities of 1.1x10−9 and 9.9x10−10 M. This was mainly driven by the dissociation rate constant, kd (s−1) which is < 10−7 for GLIP C1 F27 and 3.2x10−4 and 3.7x10−4 for the two mAbs. The average association rate constant, ka (M−1 s−1) for GLIP-C1 F27 was 8.4x104 (8.7x104 M−1 s−1 at a concentration of 2 µg/ml GLIP-C1 F27 and 8.1x104 M−1 s−1 at a concentration of 20 µg/ml) whereas the association rate constant for NNC1212-7148 and NNC1212-7141 was 2.8x105 M−1 s−1 and 3.7x105 M−1 s−1, respectively, as illustrated in Figure 5.

Discussion

The combined development and validation process for the assays took place from 2006 to 2020, with internal validations reported in 2015, 2018, and 2020. Figure 1 provides an overview of the changes of important assay-parameters made in the three versions of the assays and the validations conducted. From version 1 to 2, the sample pre-treatment was optimized, and a change in the pre-drug stimulation concentration in the assays was introduced. Version 2 and 3 of the assays differed as the QC antibodies were replaced by the human mAb NNC1212-0000–7141. The assay optimization focused on improving the key assay characteristics: sensitivity and drug tolerance.

As the assays for detection of nAbs to semaglutide and to GLP-1 were developed to be used in clinical trials involving patients with obesity, T1D and/or T2D, serum samples from these patient-groups were included in the validations of the first versions of the two assays. Additionally, patients with T2D are frequently being treated with GLP-1 analogues, and commercially available sera from these patients are thus often unsuitable due to drug interference in the assay. Therefore, in the validation of the third version of the assays, only serum from healthy individuals were included.

During development and validation, serum matrices from the different populations performed equally well. The NFs and NCPs for each of the populations were in a similar range (Table 4). However, the NFs and the NCPs varied between the different serum matrices in the different validations. This illustrates why it is appropriate to evaluate NFs and NCPs in untreated individuals (baseline samples) from the trial populations.

Selectivity experiments were performed to assess the potential for individual matrices to interfere with the detection of nAbs (Tables 2 and 3). The results indicate that the level of interference of antibody in the matrixes investigated was acceptable when compared with the normalized NCP. However, just as in the case of calculation of NFs and study specific NCPs, baseline samples from a specific study population should be evaluated with respect to selectivity of the assay to correctly assess any possible assay-interference from antibody positive study samples.

The sensitivity of version 1 of the assays was the primary concern in the PMC issued by FDA. To improve sensitivity and drug tolerance, three different approaches were taken: 1) optimization of the platform itself, 2) pre-treatment of samples, and 3) change of control antibodies. The sensitivity and drug tolerance gradually improved with the different versions of the assays (Figure 1, Tables 2, and 3).

The fatty acid acylated to the GLP-1-derivative semaglutide mediate binding to albumin with an affinity higher than the affinity of semaglutide to the GLP-1 receptor in vitro [12]. Testing varying concentrations of FCS in the assay medium revealed a right-shift in the semaglutide dose response curves with increasing FCS concentration, consequently resulting in a drop in the EC50 with decreasing concentration of FBS in the assay medium (Figure 2), which should improve assay sensitivity. However, data from testing different human serum sample concentrations with 1% FCS or 20% FCS in the assay medium (Table 5) indicated that better sensitivity was obtained with the higher concentrations of FCS and higher concentrations of serum sample. Importantly, the average standard deviation between samples was higher when using 1% FCS compared to using 20% FSC in the assay medium (Figure 3). This could be due to variation in the HSA concentration in the patient samples, which seems to be less prominent at 20% FCS than at 1% FCS, as the high amount of bovine albumin in the 20% FCS-assay medium would “even out” the variance and magnitude of the average SD. It was decided to use 20% FCS in the assay medium and 30% sample serum in the assays as this combination resulted in the best sensitivity and a low level of inter-sample variability (%CV) both in absence and presence of drug.

The concentration of drug used for stimulating the cells was also reevaluated. Drug at a specific concentration and sample was pre-incubated before incubation with the cells to activate the cAMP formation and luciferase in the BHK-cells before measuring the light emission. In version 1 of the assays, the pre-incubation concentrations of semaglutide (3 ng/ml) and GLP-1 (1.5 ng/ml), respectively, were relatively high compared to the concentration of EC60 that was aimed for during optimization of this parameter of the assay. These high concentrations were part of the reasons for the unsatisfactory sensitivity. While decreasing the drug concentration means a better sensitivity, the drug tolerance is usually adversely impacted by the same. Hence to obtain an optimal sensitivity and drug tolerance, the standard drug concentration to stimulate the cells should be carefully selected. The dose-response experiments performed for optimization of the pre-incubation of the cells with drug in between version 1 and version 2 of the assays showed that the drug concentration for both semaglutide and GLP-1 could be reduced 7.5-fold. The EC60-values of drug used for pre-incubation in the cell-assay was 400 pg/ml semaglutide and 20 pg/ml GLP-1, respectively. The combination of an optimized PEG concentration for pre-treatment of samples and the reduction in the standard concentration of the drugs resulted in a considerable improvement of the sensitivity of the assays as shown in Figure 1. The pre-incubation concentrations of 400 pg/ml semaglutide and 20 pg/ml GLP-1 were also used in version 3 of the assay.

Several approaches were tested to improve the sample pre-treatment and remove the excess of semaglutide in the samples. Prior to the validation of version 1 of the assay, the inter-individual variability in %N of antibody negative samples was very high and a pre-treatment with 15% PEG6000 was introduced to remove the high matrix interference (results not shown). For optimization of the PEG-precipitation procedure from version 1 to version 2 of the assays, different final concentrations of PEG6000 were tested for their ability to precipitate anti-semaglutide antibody (GLIP-C-1F27), HSA and semaglutide, respectively, as described in materials and methods. The results of these experiments showed that a final concentration of 10% PEG6000 equaled an optimal precipitation of anti-semaglutide antibodies relative to a minimal precipitation of semaglutide and HSA in serum. It was therefore decided to use 10% PEG6000 (final concentration) in the sample pre-treatment step in versions 2 and 3 of the assays. The use of this concentration of PEG6000 for sample precipitation should result in lowering the presence of semaglutide, both free semaglutide and HSA-bound semaglutide, during sample preparation, and consequently reducing the effect of drug interference in the assays, i.e., improving sensitivity.

Testing the dissociation of bound drug from the ADA in samples by solid-phase extraction with acid treatment was done using the SPEAD method [13], see supplementary methods and supplementary results. The results obtained in the SPEAD-experiments indicated that dissociating ADA from bound drug in the samples by acid pre-treatment would result in decreased sensitivity of the nAb assay. This may have been due to a combination of a high affinity between the drug and the control antibody and lack of stability toward acid treatment leading to loss of functional control antibody. Therefore, it was decided not to use the SPEAD method.

The sensitivity of the assay in the presence of drug is important, as samples often are analyzed while drug still is present in the samples. To minimize drug interference in the assays, samples for measuring ADAs should be collected after a wash out period. Depending on the dose, the steady state concentration of semaglutide in the clinical trials varied between 30 nM − 75 nM [14,15]. At these levels of circulating semaglutide, the measurement of the neutralizing effect of antibodies is not feasible. In clinical trials, a 5 to 7 weeks drug wash out period should therefore be included to ensure sufficiently low levels of remaining semaglutide between 1–2 nM. The sensitivities of the nAb assays were evaluated at these concentrations of semaglutide. The results of the drug interference experiments presented here show that it was only version 3 of the assays that had acceptable sensitivities below 1000 ng/ml with drug interference at the level of 1–2 nM excess drug, which was mainly due to the change of control antibody.

The sensitivity and drug tolerance of an assay is influenced by the quality of the control antibodies used. A high affinity antibody would typically be expected to yield a better sensitivity than a low affinity antibody. However, it is not just the overall affinity of an antibody that may lead to poorer or better sensitivity and drug tolerance. Thus, despite having a higher affinity to semaglutide, GLIP-C1 F27 displays poorer sensitivities both in the absence and in the presence of semaglutide than the two human mAbs (NNC1212-7148 and NNC1212-7141). The association rate is important for the sensitivity of the assay, and the faster the on-rate, the better sensitivity. GLIP-C1 F27 has a 3–4-fold slower on-rate than the two mAbs, which both gives rise to better sensitivities. Indeed, the on-rate appears to correlate well with the detected sensitivities of the optimized assays. Thus, the mAb 1212–7148 which has the fastest on rate also has the best sensitivity (62 ng/ml versus 98 ng/ml for 1212–7141 and 342 ng/ml for GLIP-C1 F27).

Whereas the association rate is important for the sensitivity of the assay, the dissociation rate is important for the drug tolerance. The slower the dissociation rate is, the poorer the drug tolerance. The GLIP-C1 F27 has a very slow dissociation rate around 10−7(1/s) which is also reflected in the poor sensitivity in presence of drug whereas the dissociation rates for the 1212–7141 and 1212–7148 are 3–4-fold faster, leading to much better sensitivities in presence of drug.

Conclusion

Drug tolerance in anti-drug antibody assays has been addressed in several publications [9,16–18], and guidelines [1,2] and several studies have suggested different methods for improving drug tolerance [13,19–22]. However, only few papers have focused specifically on the drug tolerance of assays for neutralizing antibodies [23,24]. In this paper, we have demonstrated that the sensitivity and drug tolerance of a cell-based neutralizing antibody assay critically depend on three factors: The right choice of drug standard concentration for receptor activation, appropriate sample pre-treatment, and a detailed understanding of the binding properties of the control antibody.

When developing neutralizing antibody assays, one should consider a thorough understanding of the characteristics of the control antibody as a key driver of assay performance with regard to optimal sensitivity in absence and presence of drug.
