PTEN Loss Promotes PI3Kβ Phosphorylation and EPHA2/SRC/p-PI3KβY962 Complex Assembly to Drive Tumorigenesis
Authors: Shuang Tang, Qian Zhou, Johann S. Bergholz, Tao Jiang, Weihua Wang, Renlei Ji, Wendi Huang, Jiachen Wu, Yutong Li, Xiaochun Wan, Xiadi He, Qianchen Jing, Menghong Sun, Jared L. Johnson, Tomer M. Yaron-Barir, Da-Qiang Li, Bo Dai, Zefeng Wang, Xiaoling Li, Lewis C. Cantley, Thomas M. Roberts, Jean J. Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PTEN loss promotes PI3Kβ phosphorylation and EPHA2–SRC–PI3Kβ complex signaling to drive tumorigenesis, revealing PI3Kβ phosphorylation as a druggable vulnerability and biomarker for targeted therapy in PTEN-deficient cancers.

Introduction

The PI3K pathway is hyperactivated across human cancers through genetic PI3K mutations or loss of the PTEN tumor-suppressor (1–3), making the PTEN–PI3K axis a critical therapeutic target. Significantly, the p110α catalytic isoform of PI3K (PI3Kα), encoded by the PIK3CA gene, is frequently activated through hotspot mutations in diverse cancer types. Recently, the FDA granted approval for alpelisib (BYL719), the first selective PI3Kα inhibitor, in combination with endocrine therapy for treating advanced or metastatic breast cancers harboring PIK3CA mutations (4). This milestone underscores the potential for targeting oncogenic PI3K signaling with isoform-specific precision therapies in genetically defined patient populations.

PTEN loss drives tumor progression and multi-therapy resistance but remains therapeutically untargeted. Interestingly, PTEN loss in cancer creates a differential reliance on distinct PI3K catalytic isoforms, with many contexts exhibiting dependence on the p110β catalytic isoform of PI3K (PI3Kβ) over other isoforms (5, 6). Notably, whereas PI3Kα inhibitors are being evaluated in the clinic, PTEN-null cancers are usually clinically resistant to PI3Kα inhibition due to their PI3Kβ reliance (1, 7–9). Using genetically engineered mouse models (GEMM), we demonstrated critical functions of PI3Kβ in regulating a range of PTEN-deficient cancer events, including tumor growth, progression, therapeutic resistance, and, recently, immune evasion (1, 6, 9–13). However, the basis for this unique PI3Kβ dependency remains poorly understood.

The finding has led to the development of several PI3Kβ kinase inhibitors as potential therapies for PTEN-null cancers (1, 7, 9, 14). However, whereas genetic ablation of PI3Kβ has suppressed the growth of PTEN-null tumors in several independent studies (9, 12, 14, 15), pharmacologic inhibition of PI3Kβ kinase activity has resulted in variable efficacy in preclinical PTEN-null cancer models and in patients with PTEN-deficient advanced solid tumors (1, 16, 17). Although there may well be pharmacokinetic reasons for the limitations of these PI3Kβ kinase inhibitors, we hypothesized that a mechanism beyond PI3Kβ kinase activity might be important in PTEN-null tumors. Of note, a considerable body of literature suggests kinase-independent functions of PI3Ks (18). For instance, Pik3cb knockout (KO) mice die early at day E3.5 (19), whereas kinase-dead Pik3cb knock-in mice develop to maturity with defects in size and significant defects in male fertility (20). These developmental observations support an important alternative role of PI3Kβ (21). However, knowledge beyond traditional kinase function(s) of PI3Kβ in cancer is limited.

Through BioID protein interactor screening, we identified a selective interaction between PI3Kβ-EPHA2 in PTEN-null cancer cells. This interaction was driven by elevated phosphorylation at tyrosine on PI3Kβ (p-PI3KβY962)—a modification that became significantly enhanced upon loss of PTEN. These findings led to the identification of PTEN as a tyrosine phosphatase that directly regulates PI3Kβ, specifically through dephosphorylation at Y962.

Further mechanistic studies revealed that p-PI3KβY962 forms a complex with EPHA2 and SRC, in which both tyrosine kinases contribute to PI3Kβ phosphorylation. This tripartite complex, which assembles specifically in the absence of PTEN, triggers the activation of key oncogenic signaling cascades, including pERK/c-MYC and pAKT pathways, thereby driving tumor progression in PTEN-deficient contexts. Importantly, p-PI3KβY962 has emerged as both a potential pharmacodynamic and prognostic biomarker in PTEN-null tumors. We demonstrated that both genetic and pharmacologic inhibition of tyrosine kinases, SRC and EPHA2, responsible for PI3Kβ phosphorylation resulted in effective tumor suppression in in vivo models, including PTEN-deficient human cancer cell lines, patient-derived xenografts (PDX), as well as GEMMs. Notably, dasatinib, an FDA-approved SRC family inhibitor for treating certain leukemias, although its efficacy in solid tumors remains modest (22–24), can inhibit p-PI3KβY962 and be highly effective in PTEN-null tumors. These findings suggest future clinical trials repurposing dasatinib for PTEN-deficient solid tumor treatment guided by elevated p-PI3KβY962 expression. Collectively, p-PI3KβY962 is both a therapeutic target and a predictive biomarker for a wide spectrum of PTEN-deficient cancers.

Our findings not only offer critical insight into how PTEN loss reprograms oncogenic dependency toward PI3Kβ but also reveal a promising therapeutic strategy: targeting PI3Kβ phosphorylation. This approach may deliver effective antitumor activity in PTEN-null cancers while sparing normal PTEN wild-type (WT) tissues, minimizing toxicity and increasing treatment specificity. Given that no approved therapies currently exist that specifically target PTEN-deficient tumors, this strategy addresses a major unmet clinical need.

Results

BioID Protein Interaction Profiling Reveals a PI3Kβ–EPHA2 Interaction Induced by PTEN Loss in Cancer Cells

To investigate whether PI3Kα and/or PI3Kβ form novel interactions in the context of PTEN deficiency, we performed BioID proximity labeling analyses for both isoforms in PTEN-deficient breast cancer cell lines (25, 26). Specifically, BirA-PI3Kα or BirA-PI3Kβ constructs were introduced into BT549 and MDA-MB-468 cells (Fig. 1A; Supplementary Fig. S1A). Interestingly, PI3Kα and PI3Kβ showed distinctive interactomes in these PTEN-null triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) cells, with only approximately 30% overlap between the two isoforms (Fig. 1B and C). Among the four interactors that were selectively associated with PI3Kβ in both BT549 and MDA-MB-468 cells (Fig. 1B and C), EPHA2 emerged as the most abundant and the only that colocalized with PI3Kβ at the plasma membrane (Fig. 1C and D; Supplementary Fig. S1B). Co-immunoprecipitation (IP) analyses further validated that endogenous EPHA2 interacted with PI3Kβ, but not PI3Kα, in PTEN-null BT549 and HCC70 cells (Fig. 1E).

Whereas receptor tyrosine kinases (RTK) like EPHA2 are known to bind to both PI3Kα and PI3Kβ upon growth factor stimulation, we found that in PTEN-WT HEK293T cells, EPHA2 interacted comparably with both isoforms (Fig. 1F). However, PTEN KO markedly increased the EPHA2–PI3Kβ interaction while decreasing the EPHA2–PI3Kα interaction (Fig. 1F; Supplementary Fig. S1C). Conversely, restoring PTEN expression in PTEN-null BT549 cells significantly blunted the interaction of both exogenous and endogenous EPHA2 and PI3Kβ (Fig. 1G and H). Collectively, these results indicate that PTEN loss specifically promotes a stronger interaction between EPHA2 and PI3Kβ.

PTEN Acts as a Protein Tyrosine Phosphatase Regulating PI3Kβ–EPHA2 Interaction through Dephosphorylation of Tyrosine-962 (Y962) on PI3Kβ

We next investigated the mechanism by which PTEN loss enhances the PI3Kβ–EPHA2 interaction. In addition to its well-known lipid phosphatase activity that converts PIP3 to PIP2, PTEN also possesses a protein tyrosine phosphatase activity (27). We found that the phospho-tyrosine (p-Tyr) level of immunoprecipitated PI3Kβ, but not PI3Kα, was markedly increased in the PTEN-KO cells compared with PTEN-WT cells (Fig. 2A). To further dissect this, we used PTEN-null cancer cell lines (BT549 and HCC70) stably expressing either WT PTEN or mutants defective in lipid phosphatase activity (G129E), protein phosphatase activity (Y138L), or both (C124S). In both BT549 and HCC70 cell lines, reexpression of PTEN-WT or PTEN-G129E markedly decreased p-Tyr levels on PI3Kβ, whereas the PTEN-C124S or PTEN-Y138L mutants had little or no effect (Fig. 2B and C). These results suggest that PTEN regulates tyrosine phosphorylation of PI3Kβ via its protein phosphatase activity. Moreover, co-IP showed that PTEN interacts with PI3Kβ, and the substrate-trapping mutant PTEN-C124S displayed markedly stronger binding to PI3Kβ compared with PTEN-WT in both HEK293T PTEN-KO cells and PTEN-null BT549 cells (Supplementary Fig. S1D and S1E). These findings supported that PI3Kβ is a bona fide physiologic substrate of PTEN. Consistently, expression of PTEN-WT or PTEN-G129E significantly decreased the PI3Kβ–EPHA2 interaction in PTEN-null BT549 cells, whereas either PTEN-Y138L or PTEN-C124S did not (Fig. 2D). These results suggest that PTEN functions as a protein phosphatase that modulates PI3Kβ phosphorylation and its interaction with EPHA2.

To identify which phosphorylation site(s) on PI3Kβ was regulated by PTEN, flag-tagged PI3Kβ was expressed and purified from PTEN-KO HEK293T cells for analysis by LC-MS/MS (Fig. 2E). This analysis detected phosphorylation at tyrosine 962 (Y962) and threonine 930 (T930) in PI3Kβ (Fig. 2F), both of which are highly conserved across species (Fig. 2G; Supplementary Fig. S2A). In a cell-free dephosphorylation assay, PTEN-WT and PTEN-G129E mutant (lacks lipid phosphatase activity but retains protein phosphatase function) effectively dephosphorylated a synthetic PI3Kβ peptide containing phospho-Y962. In contrast, the protein phosphatase–deficient mutants PTEN-Y138L and PTEN-C124S failed to do so (Fig. 2H). Notably, none of the PTEN variants could dephosphorylate a peptide containing pT930 (Fig. 2H), indicating that PTEN acts as a tyrosine–phosphatase targeting Y962 in PI3Kβ.

To investigate whether phosphorylation at these sites of PI3Kβ affects the PI3Kβ–EPHA2 interaction, we generated PI3Kβ mutants mimicking either nonphosphorylated (T930A, Y962F, and T930A/Y962F) or phosphorylated (T930E, Y962E, and T930E/Y962E) states. These constructs were introduced into BT549 cells in which endogenous PI3Kβ had been silenced using a short hairpin RNA (shRNA) targeting a noncoding sequence (BT549 shPIK3CB) that was not present in the addback constructs. Compared with PI3Kβ-WT, the Y962F and T930A/Y962F mutants markedly blunted the interaction with EPHA2, whereas the T930A mutation alone had no effect in PTEN-null BT549 cells (Fig. 2I) or PTEN-KO HEK293T cells (Supplementary Fig. S2B). These results indicate that dephosphorylation of PI3Kβ-Y962 weakens the PI3Kβ–EPHA2 interaction in PTEN-null cells. Conversely, in PTEN-WT MDA-MB-231 cells, expression of the phosphomimetic T930E or T930E/Y962E mutants enhanced the PI3Kβ–EPHA2 interaction, whereas the T930E mutation alone had no significant effect (Fig. 2J). Immunofluorescence (IF) staining of flag-tagged PI3Kβ and EPHA2 revealed that the phosphomimetic PI3Kβ-Y962E mutant localized predominantly to the plasma membrane and co-localized with EPHA2, whereas the nonphosphorylated PI3Kβ-Y962F mutant was mainly cytosolic (Supplementary Fig. S2C and S2D). WT PI3Kβ exhibited an intermediate membrane localization between Y962E and Y962F (Supplementary Fig. S2C and S2D). These results supported that PI3Kβ-Y962 phosphorylation increases its interaction with EPHA2, promoting membrane localization of PI3Kβ in PTEN-null cells. Collectively, these results suggest that PTEN controls the PI3Kβ–EPHA2 interaction by regulating PI3Kβ phosphorylation at Y962 and that loss of PTEN promotes this interaction by allowing persistent PI3Kβ-Y962 phosphorylation.

Sustained PI3Kβ Phosphorylation Supports the Growth of PTEN-Null Tumors

To assess the functional importance of PTEN loss–induced PI3Kβ tyrosine phosphorylation in tumorigenesis, we examined whether a phosphorylation-defective PI3Kβ mutant could phenocopy the genetic ablation of PI3Kβ and inhibit PTEN-null growth in vivo. We utilized primary PPB tumor cells derived from a GEMM of breast cancer harboring concurrent loss of Pten and Trp53, along with deletion of Pik3cb (Supplementary Fig. S2E; ref. 13). Whereas PPB cells grow readily in vitro, they are deficient to form tumors in vivo, providing a tractable isogenic platform to assess the oncogenic function of various PI3Kβ mutants.

We reconstituted PPB cells with murine PI3Kβ-WT or phosphorylation-deficient variants of PI3Kβ (Supplementary Fig. S2F). In line with the observations in human PTEN-null cancer cells, adding back the Y956F mutant (orthologous to human Y962F), or the T924A/Y956F double mutant, reduced PI3Kβ binding to EPHA2, whereas the T924A mutation alone had no appreciable effect (Supplementary Fig. S2G). These results demonstrate that dephosphorylation of mouse PI3Kβ-Y956 impairs the PI3Kβ–EPHA2 interaction in mouse PTEN-null tumors.

To assess the functional consequence of this interaction in vivo, we orthotopically transplanted these engineered PPB tumor cells into mammary fat pads of FVB mice (Supplementary Fig. S2H). Whereas PI3Kβ-WT restored the tumorigenic potential of PPB cells, the Y956F and T924A/Y956F mutants exhibit a significantly diminished ability to rescue tumor growth, accompanied by reduced Ki67 staining, indicating impaired proliferation (Fig. 2K–N; Supplementary Fig. S2I). These results suggest that phosphorylation of PI3Kβ is critical for PTEN-null breast tumor growth.

We extended this analysis to a PTEN-null prostate cancer model using PC3 cells. After depleting endogenous PI3Kβ, we overexpressed either WT or nonphosphorylatable human PI3Kβ mutants (T930A, Y962F, or T930A/Y962F) and transplanted these engineered PC3 cells into Balb/c nude mice (Supplementary Fig. S2J and S2K). Whereas PI3Kβ-WT promoted robust tumor growth, the Y962F and T930A/Y962F mutants substantially reduced this effect (Fig. 2O–Q). Consistently, tumors expressing Y962F or T930A/Y962F double mutants showed significantly decreased Ki67 levels (Fig. 2R; Supplementary Fig. S2L). Taken together, these data show that tyrosine phosphorylation of PI3Kβ is critical for driving tumorigenesis in multiple PTEN-deficient cancer models in vivo.

Elevated PI3Kβ-Y962 Phosphorylation is Tightly Associated with PTEN Deficiency in Human Patients with Breast and Prostate Cancers

To further assess PTEN-mediated phosphorylation of PI3KβY962, we developed a polyclonal antibody that specifically recognizes human PI3Kβ phosphorylated at Y962, which also cross-reacts with the corresponding mouse site (pY956; Supplementary Fig. S3A). Validation by Western blotting and IF assays confirmed that the antibody selectively detects both overexpressed and endogenous p-PI3KβY962 across multiple cell lines (Supplementary Fig. S3B and S3C).

We next assessed the relationship between PTEN expression and p-PI3KβY962 levels. Endogenous PI3Kβ was silenced using 5′-untranslated region–targeting shRNA, followed by restoring either PI3Kβ-WT or -Y962F to generate isogenic pairs of PTEN-null BT549 and PC3 cells. IF analysis revealed robust p-PI3KβY962 staining in both BT549/PI3Kβ-WT and PC3/PI3Kβ-WT cells, whereas the signal was absent in their respective Y962F counterparts (Fig. 3A; Supplementary Fig. S3C). Western blotting confirmed that PI3Kβ knockdown reduced p-PI3KβY962 levels, which were restored upon reexpression of PI3Kβ-WT but not the Y962F mutant (Supplementary Fig. S3D and S3E). Furthermore, ectopic expression of PTEN in BT549 cells markedly reduced the endogenous p-PI3KβY962 level, whereas CRISPR-mediated PTEN-KO in PTEN-WT MDA-MB-157 cells led to marked increased in p-PI3KβY962 signal (Fig. 3B–D). Collectively, these data suggest that p-PI3KβY962 is tightly controlled by PTEN.

Using this validated and specific anti–p-PI3KβY962 antibody, we analyzed the relationship between PTEN loss and p-PI3KβY962 levels in two independent cohorts of human patients with cancer. First, we performed multiplexed IHC on a matched cohort of 10 TNBC cases carrying PTEN mutations and 10 control cases with WT PTEN, matched for ages and disease stages (Fig. 3E). Strikingly, levels of p-PI3KβY962 were significantly higher in patients with PTEN-mutated tumors compared with WT PTEN controls (Fig. 3F and G). Linear regression analysis further revealed a strong inverse correlation between PTEN expression and p-PI3KβY962 levels within this TNBC cohort (Fig. 3H). Furthermore, within the PTEN-null tumor tissues, PTEN-intact stroma cells adjacent to tumor nests displayed markedly lower p-PI3KβY962 levels than the PTEN-mutated tumor cells (Fig. 3F, I, and J), reinforcing the link between PTEN loss and enhanced p-PI3KβY962 in clinical samples.

To extend these findings, we analyzed a larger cohort of 106 patients with prostate cancer with comparable clinical profiles (Fig. 3K). Consistent with the TNBC data, prostate tumors carrying PTEN mutations showed significantly higher p-PI3KβY962 levels than those with WT PTEN (Fig. 3L), and phospho-PI3KβY962 levels inversely correlated with PTEN levels across the cohort (Fig. 3M and N). Pathology-based Gleason scoring was used to stratify tumor aggressiveness, with a score of seven indicating intermediate stage and scores of 8 to 10 reflecting high-grade, poorer prognosis tumors. PTEN levels were significantly lower in patients with higher Gleason scores (Fig. 3O), whereas p-PI3KβY962 levels were significantly elevated in these same high-grade tumors (Fig. 3P).

Together, these data demonstrate that p-PI3KβY962 levels are significantly associated with PTEN loss and correlate with poor clinical prognosis in both TNBC and prostate cancer, highlighting its potential as a prognostic biomarker. These results suggest that assessing p-PI3KβY962 levels could aid in identifying high-risk patients with PTEN-deficient tumors and may inform future therapeutic strategies targeting this signaling axis.

PI3Kβ Phosphorylation Promotes EPHA2 Binding to Activate pERK/c-MYC and AKT Signaling in PTEN-Deficient Tumor Cells

We next investigated how PI3Kβ-Y962 phosphorylation influences downstream signaling through its interaction with EPHA2 in PTEN-deficient tumor cells. Given that EPHA2, an RTK, can be regulated by a reciprocal feedback loop with the AKT pathway and promote PI3K/AKT and pERK/c-MYC signaling in various cancers (28–33), we hypothesized that PTEN loss enables PI3Kβ-Y962 phosphorylation to enhance EPHA2 binding and activate oncogenic pathways.

Supporting this hypothesis, PI3Kβ depletion selectively reduced pERK and c-MYC levels in PTEN-null breast cancer models, including BT549 cells and PP cells (derived from a GEMM of TNBC with concurrent Pten and Trp53 deletion; Fig. 4A; Supplementary Fig. S4A; ref. 13). Our findings suggest that PTEN-deficient tumors rely on PI3Kβ to sustain pERK/c-MYC signaling, a pathway critical for solid tumor progression (34).

To assess the role of PTEN’s phosphatase activity in regulating this pathway, we reintroduced WT and mutant PTEN constructs into PTEN-null BT549 cells. PTEN-WT and PTEN-G129E (lipid phosphatase–deficient) significantly reduced pERK and c-MYC levels, whereas PTEN-Y138L (protein phosphatase–deficient) and PTEN-C124S (dual phosphatase–deficient) failed to do so (Fig. 4B). These results indicate that PTEN’s protein phosphatase activity, rather than its lipid phosphatase function, is critical for suppressing the ERK/c-MYC pathway in PTEN-null cancers.

We next investigated the role of PI3Kβ phosphorylation in regulating pERK/c-MYC signaling in PTEN-deficient tumor cells. In BT549 cells with PI3Kβ knockdown, reexpression of PI3Kβ-WT, the T930A mutant, or the kinase-dead K805R mutant restored pERK and c-MYC expression, whereas the Y962F and T930A/Y962F mutants failed to rescue this signaling (Fig. 4C). Similar results were seen in PPB cells restored with WT or mutant PI3Kβ (Supplementary Fig. S4B). In PTEN-proficient MDA-MB-231 cells, overexpression of phosphomimetic mutants PI3Kβ-Y962E and T930E/Y962E significantly increased pERK and c-MYC levels compared with PI3Kβ-WT or T930E alone (Supplementary Fig. S4C), underscoring the specific contribution of PI3Kβ-Y962 phosphorylation to ERK/c-MYC activation.

To determine whether the PI3Kβ–EPHA2 interaction contributes to this pathway, we disrupted EPHA2 function in PTEN-null BT549 cells. Both genetic deletion and pharmacologic inhibition of EPHA2 significantly reduced pERK and c-MYC protein levels (Fig. 4D; Supplementary Fig. S4D), supporting a role for PI3Kβ–EPHA2 signaling in driving pERK/c-MYC activation. Furthermore, in PI3Kβ-depleted BT549 cells, reintroduction of PI3Kβ-WT restored EPHA2 activity, whereas the Y962F and T930A/Y962F mutants did not (Fig. 4E).

To further clarify the role of the ERK–c-MYC axis as a downstream effector of p-PI3KβY962–EPHA2 signaling in PTEN-null cells, we examined its impact in BT549 cells with endogenous PI3Kβ knockdown. Reexpression of PI3Kβ led to increased levels of p-ERK1/2, p-cMYCS62, and total c-MYC protein (Fig. 4F). Notably, silencing ERK via shRNA or pharmacologic ERK inhibition markedly suppressed c-MYC protein levels, particularly p-cMYCS62, without affecting c-MYC mRNA expression (Fig. 4F and G; Supplementary Fig. S4E), indicating that ERK functions upstream of c-MYC by promoting its stabilization rather than transcription, consistent with prior studies (35). Moreover, blocking either ERK or c-MYC activity impaired overexpression of PI3Kβ-induced proliferative effect in BT549 cells lacking endogenous PI3Kβ (Fig. 4H and I). These findings indicate that PI3Kβ-Y962 phosphorylation enhances EPHA2-mediated ERK activation, which stabilizes c-MYC and drives proliferation in PTEN-deficient tumor cells.

Because reexpression of PI3Kβ Y962F or T930A/Y962F mutants in BT549-shPIK3CB cells resulted in reduced p-AKT levels, comparable with those observed with the kinase-dead mutant PI3Kβ K805R (Fig. 4C), we evaluated whether these phosphorylation-site mutations impaired lipid kinase activity. Using the ADP-Glo lipid kinase assay (Promega), we confirmed that whereas K805R lacked kinase activity, the T930A, Y962F, and T930A/Y962F mutants retained proficient lipid kinase function (Fig. 4J).

Interestingly, despite its loss of catalytic activity and impaired p-AKT signaling, the K805R mutant maintained its ability to bind EPHA2 and facilitate activation of the ERK/c-MYC pathway (Fig. 4C and K). These findings suggest that PI3Kβ phosphorylation, independent of its enzymatic activity, is essential for engaging EPHA2 and triggering downstream ERK/c-MYC signaling. Moreover, the observation that Y962 phosphorylation is also important for p-AKT activation indicated that EPHA2 interaction enhanced membrane accessibility of PI3Kβ on the cell membrane, facilitating PIP2 phosphorylation and p-AKT signaling in the PTEN-null context (Fig. 4C and K; Supplementary Fig. S2C and S2D). To evaluate whether PTEN’s protein phosphatase function influences PIP3 levels, we measured the relative PIP3/PIP2 ratio using Echelon’s ELISA kits in PTEN-null cancer cell lines BT549 stably expressing either WT PTEN or mutants defective in lipid (G129E), protein (Y138L), or both (C124S) phosphatase activities (Supplementary Fig. S4F). PTEN-WT markedly decreased PIP3/PIP2 ratio. As expected, the lipid phosphatase–deficient G129E mutant largely lost this effect, whereas the protein phosphatase–deficient Y138L mutant showed only a mild impairment. PTEN-G129E/C124S showed no effect on the PIP3/PIP2 ratio. These results suggest that PTEN’s lipid phosphatase activity is the primary regulator of PIP3, with a modest contribution from its protein phosphatase function.

Collectively, these findings support a model in which PTEN loss leads to hyperphosphorylation of PI3Kβ at Y962, strengthening its interaction with EPHA2. This interaction facilitates EPHA2 activation, which in turn drives pERK/c-MYC signaling and enhances PI3Kβ-mediated AKT activation, ultimately promoting tumor cell proliferation in PTEN-deficient cancers (Fig. 4L).

The SRC–EPHA2–PI3Kβ Tripartite Complex Drives Oncogenic Signaling in PTEN-Null Tumors

Given the critical role of p-PI3KβY962 in PTEN-null cancer, we sought to identify tyrosine kinases responsible for its phosphorylation. We first applied a motif-based prediction algorithm developed by the Cantley laboratory (36, 37) to score all tyrosine kinases for their potential to phosphorylate PI3Kβ at Y962. This analysis identified top candidates primarily from the EPHA (EPHA1, EPHA2, and EPHA3) and SRC families (FES, PYK2, ACK, LYN, and SRMS; Supplementary Fig. S5A).

To complement this computational approach, we performed a targeted compound screen using 60 tyrosine kinase inhibitors against the top-ranked kinases. The screen was conducted in PTEN-null BT549 and PC3 cells in which endogenous PI3Kβ was replaced with either PI3Kβ-WT or PI3Kβ-Y962F mutant (Fig. 5A). The screening was designed to identify compounds that effectively inhibit cancer cells expressing PI3Kβ-WT, but not those expressing the PI3Kβ-Y962F mutant, thereby highlighting inhibitors that may specifically target p-PI3KβY962. Notably, two SRC inhibitors, dasatinib and KX2-391, emerged among the top hits, significantly reducing growth in PI3Kβ-WT cells but showing diminished effects in cells expressing the Y962F mutant in both BT549 and PC3 cell line systems (Fig. 5B and C). These results suggest that SRC inhibition may effectively target p-PI3KβY962 in PTEN-null cancers. Indeed, dasatinib not only reduced phosphorylation of SRC and suppressed cell growth but also markedly decreased p-PI3KβY962 and pERK/c-Myc protein levels across multiple PTEN-null cancer cells (Fig. 5D and E; Supplementary Fig. S5B–S5D). This effect was confirmed with KX2-391, which similarly reduced p-PI3KβY962 and c-Myc in BT549 cells (Fig. 5F). Moreover, both inhibitors significantly impaired the growth of PPB cells expressing PI3Kβ-WT but were less effective in PPB cells expressing the PI3Kβ-Y956F mutant (Fig. 5G). These results indicate p-PI3KβY962 as a druggable target that can be effectively inhibited by kinase inhibitors.

Our targeted compound screening data implicated SRC and EPHA2 as potential kinases involved in PI3Kβ-Y962 phosphorylation. Supporting this, knockdown of either SRC or EPHA2 decreased PI3KβY962 phosphorylation and c-Myc in PTEN-null BT549 and PC3 cells (Fig. 5H; Supplementary Fig. S5E). Interestingly, in PTEN-KO cells, EPHA2 was required for SRC to interact with PI3Kβ (Fig. 5I). SRC overexpression increased interaction between PI3Kβ and EPHA2, an effect particularly pronounced in PTEN-KO cells (Fig. 5H), suggesting the formation of a PI3Kβ–EPHA2–SRC complex. Moreover, cell-free kinase assays using both a PI3Kβ-Y962–containing peptide and full-length PI3Kβ demonstrated that GST-tagged SRC and EPHA2 can directly phosphorylate PI3Kβ at Y962, whereas this phosphorylation was abolished in the Y962F mutant (Fig. 5J and K). These results suggest that both SRC and EPHA2 can function as tyrosine kinases for PI3Kβ at Y962. Consistently, shRNA-mediated knockdown of SRC or EPHA2 reduced p-PI3KβY962 levels in PTEN-null BT549 cells (Fig. 5L and M), confirming their involvement in this modification. Notably, whereas EPHA2 overexpression partially restored PI3Kβ-Y962 phosphorylation and downstream pERK and c-MYC signaling in SRC knockdown BT549 cells (Fig. 5L), SRC overexpression failed to do so in EPHA2-silenced BT549 cells (Fig. 5M), suggesting that SRC acts upstream of EPHA2 and may depend on EPHA2 as a scaffold to phosphorylate PI3Kβ (Fig. 5L and M). This was further supported by the observation that SRC overexpression enhanced p-ERK and c-Myc levels in control BT549 cells but not in EPHA2 KO (Fig. 5N).

Together, these findings indicate that SRC and EPHA2 cooperate to phosphorylate PI3Kβ at Y962 by forming a functional signaling complex. This tripartite complex drives oncogenic signaling in PTEN-null cancer cells, identifying p-PI3KβY962 as a druggable vulnerability that can be effectively targeted by SRC/EPHA2 inhibitors (Fig. 5O). Dasatinib emerged as a top hit in our kinase inhibitor screen and is known to target both SRC family kinases and EPHA2, making it well-suited as a proof-of-concept therapeutic in this context. Given our findings that both SRC and EPHA2 are key regulators of PI3Kβ in PTEN-deficient settings, dasatinib offers a rational and effective strategy for targeting this signaling axis—at least within the scope of our preclinical models.

Dasatinib Exhibits Potent and Selective Antitumor Activity in PTEN-Deficient Cancers

To evaluate the therapeutic potential of dasatinib in targeting PTEN-null tumors in vivo, we tested its efficacy across multiple PTEN-deficient models (Supplementary Fig. S6A). Intraperitoneal administration of dasatinib significantly suppressed tumor growth in the PP syngeneic model, leading to a marked reduction in both tumor volume and weight (Fig. 6A–C). Mechanistically, dasatinib treatment diminished the interaction between PI3Kβ and EPHA2, as well as reduced levels of p-PI3KβY962 and c-MYC in PTEN-null tumors (Fig. 6D and E). IHC analyses further confirmed that dasatinib decreased expression of p-PI3KβY962, c-MYC, and the proliferation marker Ki67 in PP tumors (Fig. 6F–I). These findings indicate that dasatinib effectively inhibits the SRC/EPHA2/PI3Kβ–c-MYC axis by reducing PI3Kβ-Y962 phosphorylation, thereby suppressing PTEN-deficient tumor growth in vivo. This supports the potential use of p-PI3KβY962 as a predictive biomarker to guide dasatinib-based therapy through biomarker-driven patient stratification.

To determine whether the antitumor efficacy of targeting the SRC/EPHA2/pPI3KβY962–c-MYC axis is dependent on PTEN status, we established human xenograft models using PTEN-WT (HCC1954) and PTEN-null (HCC70) TNBC cells. Upon dasatinib treatment, only the PTEN-null HCC70 tumors showed significant growth inhibition and reduced c-MYC expression (Fig. 6J; Supplementary Fig. S6B and S6C), whereas HCC1954 tumors exhibited no meaningful changes in tumor volume, weight, or c-MYC levels compared with controls (Fig. 6K; Supplementary Fig. S6D and S6E). These results underscore the importance of PTEN loss in sensitizing tumors to dasatinib, likely through dependency on the SRC/EPHA2/pPI3KβY962 complex–driven signaling axis.

We further extended our investigation to two PDX models of TNBC: DFBC-1732, which harbors a biallelic PTEN deletion, and DFBC-1613, which has a monoallelic PTEN deletion along with low PTEN expression. Remarkably, dasatinib treatment significantly suppressed tumor growth in both models, reducing tumor burden and c-MYC expression (Fig. 6L–P; Supplementary Fig. S6F–S6J). These findings confirm that dasatinib effectively targets SRC/EPHA2/pPI3KβY962-driven signaling in diverse PTEN-deficient tumor contexts.

Discussion

PTEN loss is a well-established driver of tumor progression and resistant to a wide range of therapies, including inhibitors of PI3Kα, CDK4/6 inhibitors, and immune checkpoint inhibitors (7, 38–40). Despite this clinical importance, no approved drugs exist specifically designed for PTEN-null cancers. Our findings, in conjunction with previous results, demonstrate that PTEN-null tumors are dependent on PI3Kβ for survival (9, 10, 13), highlighting the potential for developing PI3Kβ-targeted therapeutic strategies to fight against a broad range of PTEN-deficient cancers. However, the efficacy of PI3Kβ kinase inhibitors in PTEN-null tumors has been inconsistent across models (1, 17), underscoring the need to move beyond enzymatic inhibition and investigate alternative mechanisms underpinning PI3Kβ dependency.

In this study, we identify a phosphorylation-based mechanism of PI3Kβ activation that plays a critical role in PTEN-null tumorigenesis (Fig. 6Q). Specifically, we characterize phosphorylation at tyrosine 962 (p-PI3KβY962) as a key regulatory event, controlled by PTEN, that enhances PI3Kβ oncogenic function. Through BioID-based interactome profiling, we uncovered a PTEN loss–specific interaction between PI3Kβ and EPHA2, which leads to c-MYC upregulation via activation of the ERK pathway. Mechanistically, PTEN loss induces PI3Kβ-Y962 phosphorylation and facilitates formation of a tripartite PI3Kβ–EPHA2–SRC signaling complex. This complex activates EPHA2, promotes ERK phosphorylation, and stabilizes c-MYC, thereby driving tumor progression. Interestingly, although this phosphorylation does not significantly alter PI3Kβ’s kinase activity, it enhances membrane localization and accessibility of PI3Kβ to its substrate PIP2, contributing to increased p-AKT signaling in PTEN-deficient contexts.

Through a targeted screen of tyrosine kinase inhibitors, we identified dasatinib as a potent inhibitor of p-PI3KβY962. Dasatinib treatment suppressed tumor growth in diverse PTEN-deficient models, including cell line–derived xenografts and PDXs. However, its clinical performance in solid tumors has historically been limited, in part due to the absence of effective biomarker-guided patient selection (22–24). Our data suggest that PTEN loss or elevated p-PI3KβY962 expression could serve as biomarkers to identify patients most likely to benefit from SRC-targeted therapies such as dasatinib.

Moreover, we demonstrate that SRC is required for assembling the SRC/EPHA2/p-PI3KβY962 complex and for the phosphorylation of both EPHA2 and PI3Kβ. Dasatinib’s ability to inhibit both SRC and EPHA2 makes it a powerful proof-of-concept agent for disrupting this oncogenic complex. In this study, we also developed a novel antibody against p-PI3KβY962, which could enable future clinical trials by allowing precise patient stratification based on biomarker expression.

The central insight from our study is the identification of p-PI3KβY962 as a selective and druggable vulnerability in PTEN-deficient cancers. Targeting this phosphorylation event offers a distinct therapeutic advantage: it spares PTEN-proficient cells, thereby providing a treatment window that reduces off-target toxicity while maintaining strong tumor-specific efficacy. This strategy represents a compelling alternative to conventional PI3K kinase inhibition and opens new possibilities for developing therapies aimed at noncatalytic mechanisms of oncogenic signaling.

Although dasatinib is already FDA-approved for treating certain leukemias, its efficacy in solid tumors has been modest—likely due to the lack of biomarker-based patient stratification (22–24). Our findings offer robust preclinical evidence that SRC inhibitors such as dasatinib can be highly effective in PTEN-null tumors when guided by molecular markers. Specifically, stratifying patients by PTEN status or elevated p-PI3KβY962 expression may substantially enhance clinical responses, positioning p-PI3KβY962 as both a therapeutic target and a predictive biomarker for a wide spectrum of PTEN-deficient malignancies.

In PTEN-deficient cancers, our data demonstrate that phosphorylation of PI3Kβ-Y962 drives the formation of the SRC–EPHA2–PI3Kβ complex, which not only activates pERK/c-MYC signaling but also enhances PI3Kβ membrane localization, facilitating PIP2 phosphorylation and downstream activation of p-AKT signaling (Figs. 4L and 5O). Notably, capivasertib, a pan-AKT inhibitor, was recently FDA-approved in combination with fulvestrant for hormone receptor–positive, HER2-negative, harboring PIK3CA, AKT1, or PTEN alterations (41, 42). Given the distinct yet complementary mechanisms, optimal suppression of PTEN-deficient tumors may require dual blockade of the SRC/EPHA2/p-PI3KβY962 complex and AKT signaling. Combination therapies involving capivasertib and p-PI3KβY962 inhibitors may offer synergistic therapeutic benefit in this context.

In conclusion, this work uncovers a previously uncharacterized PTEN–SRC–EPHA2–PI3Kβ signaling axis and establishes SRC/EPHA2/p-PI3KβY962 as a critical driver of tumorigenesis in PTEN-null cancers (Fig. 6Q). These insights lay a strong foundation for the design of biomarker-driven clinical trials and the development of targeted therapies aimed at PI3Kβ phosphorylation, with the potential to significantly improve outcomes for patients with PTEN-deficient tumors (Fig. 6Q).

Methods

Animal Studies

All animal experiments were conducted following the animal use guidelines established by the NIH. The experimental protocols were approved by either the Dana-Farber Cancer Institute Animal Care and Use Committee or the Fudan University Shanghai Cancer Center Animal Care and Use Committee. We used female mice for breast cancer research and male mouse for prostate cancer research.

FVB (RRID: MGI:3609372) or BALB/c nude (RRID: IMSR_RJ:BALB-C-NUDE) or NOD SCID (RRID: IMSR_RJ:NOD-SCID) mice (5–6 weeks old) were housed in specific pathogen-free facilities at either the Laboratory Animal Center of Fudan University Shanghai Cancer Center or the Dana-Farber Cancer Institute. They followed a 12-hour light–dark cycle and had free access to standard rodent chow and water, in accordance with institutional guidelines.

Generation of Transgenic Mice Model

Our laboratory has established a GEMM of invasive TNBC driven by codepletion of PTEN and P53 in FVB background (PP). We further crossed the mice with additional PI3Kβ deletion (PPB). To circumvent deleterious effects elicited by these deletions, we transplanted mammary epithelial stem cells from PP and PPB GEMM into the cleared mammary fat pad of nude mice and then used the resulting tumor to transplant recipient mice to get PP and PPB TNBC preclinical models (13).

PDXs

Tumor tissue obtained from patients named DFBC-1613 and DFBC-1732 was transplanted into NOD SCID mice (RRID: IMSR_RJ:NOD-SCID). The mice were treated with dasatinib [MedChemExpress (MCE), cat. #HY-10181, CAS No: 302962-49-8] 15 mg/kg or vehicle control at day 12, and the tumor growth and response to treatments were monitored.

Tumor Models and Treatments

For the xenograft tumor model in Fig. 2K–N, 1 × 106 PPB cells stably expressing vector, mPI3KβWT, mPI3KβT924A, mPI3KβY956F, mPI3KβT924A/Y956F double mutants, and mPI3KβK799R were injected into the inguinal mammary fat pad region of FVB (RRID: MGI:3609372) mice. For the xenograft tumor model in Fig. 2O–R, 1 × 106 PC3 shPIK3CB stably expressing WT PIK3CB or three mutants were injected into the subcutaneous region of BALB/c nude mice (RRID: IMSR_RJ:BALB-C-NUDE). Tumor size was measured every 3 days using calipers. Tumor volume (mm3) was calculated using the formula tumor volume = 0.5 × length × width2. Mice were euthanized, and the tumor weights were recorded at the endpoint of experiments.

Dasatinib (15 mg/kg) treatment was subcutaneous injected every day.

Clinical Cohorts

Cohorts of patients with breast cancer and prostate cancer from Fudan University Shanghai Cancer Center were obtained with approval from the FUSCC (Fudan University Shanghai Cancer Center) Ethics Committee. Each patient provided written informed consent for data and tissue use.

BioID Protein Profiling

The BioID proximity-based biotin labeling technique was used to discover interaction companions of PI3Kα and PI3Kβ in PTEN-null TNBC cancer cells. We constructed human PI3Kα-BioID and PI3Kβ-BioID fusion proteins by cloning PI3Kα and PI3Kβ into BioID2-pBABE-puro vector (RRID: Addgene_186002). We further generated stable cell lines stably expressing PI3Kα-BioID or PI3Kβ-BioID and control cells expressing BioID vector. Fifty mmol/L biotin (Bio Basic, cat. #A429451, CAS No: 58-85-5) was added to BT549 (RRID: CVCL_1092) and MDA-MB-468 (RRID: CVCL_0419) cells for 24 hours before harvest. A published protocol was followed to perform BioID protein pull-down to investigate binding partners of PI3Kα and PI3Kβ (25). The pulled down Biotin interacting protein were sent to the TMSF core of Harvard Medical School for MS analysis and protein identification.

Co-IP

Cells were lysed with a lysis buffer containing 0.1% NP-40, 50 mmol/L Tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 150 mmol/L NaCl, and protease inhibitors. The lysates were then incubated with anti-Flag M2 agarose (Sigma, cat. #M8823, RRID: SCR_024337), anti-HA M2 agarose (Sigma, cat. #SAE019), or anti-EPHA2 (Cell Signaling Technology, cat. #6997, RRID: AB_10827743) antibody together with protein A agarose (MCE, cat. #HY-K0203) for 3 hours at 4°C. The beads were washed three times with the lysis buffer and then subjected to Western blotting analysis.

Cell Lines and Cell Treatments

Primary mouse breast cancer cells (PP and PPB) were isolated and grown by modifying a previously described protocol for primary tissue culture (13). Human embryonic kidney cells (HEK293T, RRID: CVCL_0063) and human breast cancer cell lines MDA-MB-231 (RRID: CVCL_0062), MDA-MB-468 (RRID: CVCL_0419), HCC1954 (RRID: CVCL_1259), and MDA-MB-157 (RRID: CVCL_0618) were cultured in DMEM, and human breast cancer cell lines BT549 (RRID: CVCL_1092) and HCC70 (RRID: CVCL_1264) and human prostate cancer cell line PC3 (RRID: CVCL_1092) were cultured in RPMI 1640 media (Sigma-Aldrich, R8758). MS/MS Identification of Phospho-PI3KβT930 and Phospho-PI3KβY962

PTEN-KO HEK293T cells were overexpressed with PI3Kβ-Flag for 48 hours, and the cells were then lysed in 0.5% NP-40 lysis buffer with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. The lysate was incubated with anti-FLAG M2 magnetic beads (cat. #M8823, Sigma-Aldrich) for 3 hours at 4°C. Afterward, the beads were washed twice with 0.5% NP-40 buffer, twice with ddH2O, and thrice with 50 mmol/L NH4HCO3. Subsequently, an on-bead trypsin digestion was performed at 37°C overnight. The supernatants were dried in a speed vacuum (Eppendorf), and the collected peptides were subjected to LC-MS/MS analysis.

The LC-MS/MS raw data were processed with UniProt Human protein database (70,727 entries, download in 20,161,202) and using Protein Discoverer (version 2.4, Thermo Fisher Scientific) with Mascot (version 2.7.0, Matrix Science). The mass tolerances were 5 ppm for precursor and fragment mass tolerance of 0.05 Da. Up to two missed cleavages were allowed. The acetylation on the protein N-terminal, oxidation on methionine, and carbamidomethylation on cysteine were set as variable modifications. FDR thresholds for peptide were at 0.01.

Polychromatic IF Staining of Patient Tumor Sections

The use of human TNBC and prostate cancer samples were approved by Fudan University Shanghai Cancer Center. Polychromatic IF staining was performed by using the four-color multiple fluorescent IHC staining kit (abs50012, Absin) based on the tyramide signal amplification (TSA) technique following the manufacturer’s manual. Briefly, the formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded 5-μm tumor tissue sections from patient samples were dewaxed using xylene and rehydrated through ethanol gradients. Antigen retrieval was performed using EDTA buffer (pH 8.0), followed by blocking endogenous peroxidase activity using 0.3% hydrogen peroxide. The sections were incubated with primary antibodies against specific targets (phospho-PI3KβY96 and PTEN) for 1 hour at room temperature and horseradish peroxidase–labeled goat anti-rabbit/mouse secondary antibodies conjugated with fluorescent dyes (TSA 520 and TSA 620). The process was repeated for additional primary antibodies to detect different antigens. The sequence of antibodies was as follows: PTEN–TSA 520 and phospho-PI3KβY962–TSA 620. Lastly, sections were stained with DAPI (4′,6-Diamidino-2-phenylindole) and mounted in glycerol and gelatin mounting medium. To further validate the results, negative controls were included in each experimental run. Imaging of the tissue sections was performed using an Olympus scanning confocal microscope.

IF of Cells

The overlap coefficient was determined using ImageJ software to assess the colocalization between the PI3Kβ mutants and EPHA2 signals.

IHC Staining and Scoring

IHC was performed following a published protocol (43). In brief, formalin-fixed and paraffin-embedded samples from mice bearing PP or PC3 tumors were stained with antibodies against c-Myc or ki67. Cells exhibiting positive reactions were detected with the aid of DAB+ (3,3′-Diaminobenzidine) chromogen. The assessment of staining was carried out using ImageJ software, considering both the proportion of cells stained positively and the intensity of staining in high-power fields from representative tissue sections for each experimental group. Subsequently, the tissue samples were scored quantitatively based on the criteria of cell positivity percentage and staining intensity, as previously detailed (44).

EPHA2 and PI3Kβ Kinase Activity Assay

The kinase assays for EPHA2 and PI3Kβ were conducted using ADP-Glo Kit (Promega), designed to quantify ADP production in kinase reactions. The presence of ADP triggers a rise in the luminescence signal when ADP-Glo is added. These assays adhered strictly to the manufacturer’s guidelines. Luminescence from the 96-well plate was measured using the ADP-Glo Luminescence Protocol (Promega; cat. #E7031) using a GloMax plate reader.

In Vitro PTEN Phosphatase Assay

The PTEN phosphatase assay was executed in a 30 μL solution containing 20 mmol/L HEPES at pH 7.5, 50 mmol/L NaCl, 5 mmol/L MgCl2, 1 mmol/L DTT, 0.1% NP-40, 1 mmol/L PMSF, and 0.05 mg/mL synthetic phosphorylated peptide. After reaction for 3 hours at 30°C, the peptides underwent desalting via a C18 ZipTip from Millipore and were analyzed using a MALDI-TOF/TOF mass spectrometer (Brucker), specifically the SCIEeX-5800 model.

In Vitro SRC and EPHA2 Kinase Assay

In vitro SRC and EPHA2 kinase assay was conducted using either Flag-tagged PI3Kβ or synthetic peptides as substrates. For immunoblotting detection, Flag-tagged PI3Kβ was overexpressed in HEK293T cells and immunoprecipitated from the cell lysate using anti-Flag M2 magnetic beads (Sigma). The beads were washed twice in the kinase reaction buffer (20 mmol/L HEPES, pH 7.5, 10 mmol/L MgCl2, 5 mmol/L β-glycerophosphate, and 0.1 mmol/L Na3VO4) and then resuspended in 20 μL kinase reaction buffer supplemented with 100 μmol/L ATP and 1 mmol/L DTT, with or without 250 ng of purified GST–SRC or GST-EPHA2 (MCE). The samples were incubated for 30 minutes at 30°C with shaking before being resuspended in 1 × SDS loading buffer for immunoblotting.

For peptide reactions, a 30 μL reaction system containing kinase reaction buffer added with 100 μmol/L ATP, 1 mmol/L DTT 250 ng of purified GST–SRC or GST-EPHA2 (MCE), and 0.05 mg/mL synthetic nonphosphorylated peptide were prepared. After reacting at 30°C for 3 hours, the peptides were desalted via a C18 ZipTip (Millipore) and subjected to analysis using a MALDI-TOF/TOF mass spectrometer (SCIEeX-5800).

Clonogenic Assay Using Crystal Violet Staining

Cells were plated in either a six-well plate at a density of 1 × 105 cells per well or a 12-well plate at 5 × 104 cells per well. These cells underwent the indicated treatment for a duration of 3 to 6 days before harvesting. The cells were fixed with a 4% paraformaldehyde solution for 10 minutes and stained with a 0.05% crystal violet solution for 30 minutes at room temperature.

Cell Proliferation Assay

BT549 and PC3 cells were seeded at a density of 5,000 cells per well in 96-well plates. The cells were cultured for 24 hours in culture medium before dasatinib or control (DMSO) treatment for 6 days. Subsequently, cell viability was assessed using the CCK-8 (Cell Counting Kit-8) Assay (MCE), in adherence to the protocol provided by the manufacturer.

PIP3 and PI(4,5)P2 Quantification

PIP3 and PI(4,5)P2 levels were quantified by ELISA (Echelon, K-2500s and K-4500). In brief, BT549 stable cell lines were fixed with ice-cold 0.5 mol/L tricarboxylic acid (TCA), scraped, and centrifuged. The pellet was washed twice with 5% TCA/1 mmol/L EDTA, followed by two rounds of neutral lipid extraction using MeOH:CHCl3 (2:1). Acidic lipids were then extracted with MeOH:CHCl3:12 mol/L HCl (80:40:1), and the organic phase was partitioned by adding CHCl3 and 0.1 mol/L HCl. Aliquots of the organic phase were vacuum-dried, resuspended in kit-specific buffers, sonicated, and analyzed by ELISA.

Motif-Based Prediction Method to Search for Tyrosine Kinases that Phosphorylate PI3KβY962

Kinase Substrate Specificity Assays

The Cantley laboratory developed a motif-based prediction approach for kinase substrate identification. This method involved conducting peptide library assays using a kinase substrate library (anaspec) and streptavidin-conjugated membranes (Promega; refs. 36, 37, 45). To determine the substrate motifs, in vitro phosphorylation assays were performed with recombinant kinases on the oriented peptide array library in the presence of ATP (γ-32P; Perkin-Elmer). The peptides were spotted onto streptavidin-coated filter sheets (Promega SAM2 biotin capture membrane) and visualized by phosphorimaging on Typhoon FLA 7000.

Matrix Processing and Substrate Scoring

For matrix processing and substrate scoring, normalization was performed against the sum of the 18 randomized amino acids, excluding tyrosine and cysteine, resulting in a position-specific scoring matrix. The cysteine column was scaled by their median to be 1/18. The tyrosine column was replaced with the phenylalanine column to score adjacent tyrosines. For scoring substrates, the values of the corresponding amino acids in the corresponding positions were multiplied and scaled by the probability of a random peptide:Scorekin X = ΠPos Pkin x (AA, Position)/(1/#Random AA)length(positions)

To search for tyrosine kinases that contribute to the phosphorylation of PI3KβY962, we computed the a priori score distribution of all tyrosine kinases by scoring the Y962 phosphorylation site of PI3Kβ from a human reference phosphoproteome (46) by the method discussed above. A list of the top candidates that might phosphorylate PI3KβY962 was generated using the motif-based prediction method (45).

Kinase Inhibitor Library Screening

The kinase inhibitor library containing 60 compounds was purchased from MCE. BT549 or PC3 shPIK3CB cells adding back with either PI3Kβ-WT or PI3Kβ-Y962F were seeded in 96-well plates. Cells were subsequently treated with kinase inhibitors at 1 or 0.1 μmol/L or with DMSO as control for 48 hours. The cell viabilities were determined by CCK-8 assay. Cell viability (%) was calculated using the following formula: ODtreat/ODcontrol × 100%, in which ODcontrol and ODtreat are the optical density of the same cell line after DMSO control or inhibitor treatment, respectively. Whereas cells expressing the nonphosphorylatable mutant exhibit significantly reduced growth, the extent of cell viability was normalized to DMSO-treated PIK3CB knockdown cells expressing either the PI3Kβ-WT or PI3Kβ-Y962F, respectively.

Quantification and Statistical Analysis

Values are expressed as the mean ± SEM for bar figures and median with IQR for violin plots from at least three independent experiments or biological replicates. Statistical analysis was performed using Prism 9.0 (GraphPad Software, Inc., RRID: SCR_002798). Significant differences between two groups were analyzed by the unpaired two-tailed t test or nonparametric Mann–Whitney test. One-way or two-way ANOVA was used to compare multiple groups. One-way ANOVA with Tukey multiple comparisons test was applied to compare the means of multiple groups. When testing differences in a cohort with multiple groups, one-way ANOVA with Kruskal–Wallis test was used to compare multiple groups, and one-way ANOVA with Dunnett correction multiple-comparison test was used to compare multiple groups with the same control. The statistical significance of tumor growth curves was analyzed using two-way ANOVA. Survival analyses were presented as Kaplan–Meier curves using log-rank test to compare the differences. Differences were considered statistically significant if the P value was less than 0.05. Significance was indicated as follows: *, P < 0.05; **, P < 0.01; ***, P < 0.001; and ****, P < 0.0001.
