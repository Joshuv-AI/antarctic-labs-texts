TON1 and FASS are required to recruit myosin VIII, a cell division guidance factor
Authors: Shu-Zon Wu, Darren R. Mallett, Samantha E. Ryken, Magdalena Bezanilla
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SignificanceAmong plants, the filamentous tissues of moss, known as protonemata, are at a unique position in the evolution of tissue complexity. Far simpler than most plant tissues, protonemata comprise a two-dimensional network with cells at the filament tips that divide in half without much input from neighboring cells. In contrast, divisions that give rise to branching filaments do not occur in isolation from neighboring cells. By studying cell division plane establishment in protonemal branching cells, it has been possible to uncover core molecular machinery that marks the future site of cell division and recruits cell division guidance factors, ultimately defining the tissue organization at filament branch sites.

In plants, cell division occurs by construction of a new cell plate from within the mother cytoplasm. Construction of this plate occurs via the phragmoplast, a complex structure composed of microtubules and Golgi-derived transport vesicles that fuse together to form a membrane-bound compartment containing the new cell plate. During division, the phragmoplast expands from a region previously occupied by the spindle midzone toward the mother cell wall where the nascent cell plate ultimately fuses dividing the cell into two daughters (1, 2). Many plant cells build a ring of microtubules at the cell cortex before the onset of mitosis known as the preprophase band (PPB) (3, 4). The PPB serves to fine-tune cell division, helping to align the spindle and reduce variation in cell division angle and size between the resulting daughter cells (5, 6).

Some plant cells, such as endosperm (7), meiocytes (8), moss protonemata (9), and moss gametophore initials (10) lack a PPB. In moss, the highly elongated protonemal apical cell divides in the middle, requiring very little adjustment because the nucleus always resides roughly in the center of the cell. However, subapical protonemal cells also divide, but they do so after they have initiated a protrusion near an old cross wall that ultimately results in a branch in the filament. The branching cell is L-shaped and prior to division, the nucleus migrates from the middle of the long stem of the L, approximately 50 µm, to the base of the short stem of the L. There mitosis ensues and a new cell plate is formed near the base of the branching cell (11, 12). While it is known that microtubules are essential for nuclear migration (13, 14) and microtubule–actin interactions underlie phragmoplast expansion (15), the positional cues that promote these rearrangements are largely unknown.

A complex comprised of TONNEAU1 (TON1), TONNEAU2/FASS (TON2/FASS), PROTEIN PHOSPHATASE TYPE 2A (PP2A) and the TON1 RECRUITING MOTIF (TRM) proteins, known as the TTP complex, is essential for microtubule organization in plant cells (16–18). Plants lacking TON1 or FASS are severely stunted due to defects in both cell expansion and division arising from disorganized cortical microtubules and the lack of a PPB (17, 19). TON1 shares sequence similarity with the human centrosomal protein, FGFR1 oncogene partner (FOP) (19), and some TRM proteins share domains found in CAP350 a centrosomal protein required to recruit FOP to the centrosome (20). FASS is a type B” regulatory subunit of PP2A that binds to TON1 and TRM proteins (17). In Arabidopsis, there are 34 TRM proteins characterized by a motif that binds TON1 and five other short, conserved motifs (20). Some TRM proteins bind to cortical microtubules (20) and TRM7/8 associate with PPB microtubules, with TRM6/7/8 being required for PPB formation (21). Thus, TRM proteins are thought to regulate TON1/PP2A localization and the TTP complex is thought to function as an organizational center similar to the centrosome in animals (18).

In contrast to the strong loss of function phenotypes in Arabidopsis, in moss ∆ton1 was reported to have normal protonemata (22), possibly because protonemata do not perform diffuse cell expansion mediated by organized cortical microtubules. However, protonemata do divide without the benefit of a PPB composed of microtubules, presenting an ideal and unique system to disentangle TTP’s roles in division versus expansion. Motivated by TON1’s central role in PPB formation together with the isolation of artichoke, a novel TON1 mutant allele, we took advantage of the ability to endogenously tag TON1 to analyze TON1 localization and function in P. patens. We found that efficient dimerization is required for TON1 localization to the plasma membrane and localization is necessary for TON1 function. We also show that TON1 and FASS unexpectedly function in phragmoplast guidance in branching protonemal cells by recruiting myosin VIII to the plasma membrane along the wall adjacent to the emerging branch.

Results

Efficient Dimerization Is Required for TON1 Plasma Membrane Localization and Function.

P. patens is propagated vegetatively by blending tissue. Wherever the tissue is damaged, the cells dedifferentiate and initiate protonemal growth. As a result, it is possible to occasionally isolate spontaneous mutants. We identified a spontaneous mutation that arose in a wild-type line that expresses a fluorescent protein used to image actin in living cells (Lifeact-mRuby2) (23). After regenerating plants from single Lifeact-mRuby2 protoplasts, we found that a small fraction of plants had dramatically stunted gametophores as compared to the original Lifeact-mRuby2 line (Fig. 1A). We isolated these plants and named them artichoke. The mutant gametophores greatly resembled gametophores in the previously published ∆ton1 mutant (22) as well as the ∆ton1 mutants we generated (Fig. 1A and SI Appendix, Fig. S1 and Table S1).

To identify candidate mutations that might confer the stunted gametophore phenotype, we sequenced the genome of the parental line and artichoke. Based on our protoplast regeneration, we were aware that the parental line was a mixed population of plants that had a small fraction of artichoke mutants as well as wild-type plants. Thus, we analyzed the whole-genome data as if the plants were diploids: The Lifeact-mRuby2 genome should contain a mixture of wild-type and artichoke alleles at any given locus and as such was considered “heterozygous” for the artichoke allele. Isogenic artichoke mutants, however, should have the artichoke allele fixed in 100% of plants to ensure a stable phenotype and thus were considered “homozygous” for the artichoke allele. By finding all sequence variants that changed the protein coding region of a gene and were homozygous in artichoke but heterozygous in the parental line, we dramatically reduced the number of candidate sequence variants to two, one of which was in the TON1 (Pp3c14_5580) gene. In artichoke, we found a single nucleotide change in TON1 at position 398 in the coding sequence from T to C, resulting in a change from Leucine to Proline at position 133 (Fig. 1B).

To determine whether this nucleotide change affects TON1 function, we used CRISPR-Cas9 genome editing coupled with homology directed repair (24, 25) to revert the mutation in artichoke back to the wild-type nucleotide. We found a guide RNA in artichoke in which the protospacer adjacent motif (PAM) was generated by the mutation. Thus, this guide RNA would be prevented from targeting wild-type TON1 after homology-directed repair. We performed two transformations into artichoke: one with the plasmid encoding the Cas9/guide RNA (pMKCas9-TON1SNP) only and another with pMKCas9-TON1SNP and the homology template (pGEMTON1-SNPHDR). If the artichoke allele were repaired, we would expect to observe a fraction of transformants that performed the repair to have normal gametophores only in the transformation containing the homology template, which is what we observed (SI Appendix, Fig. S1). We genotyped a plant that formed normal gametophores and sequencing validated that the plant contained the wild-type sequence (SI Appendix, Fig. S1), demonstrating that simply reverting C398 back to T rescued the artichoke mutant phenotype. We also identified plants that had both wild-type and artichoke gametophores, which likely resulted from chimeric plants where editing occurred after the first cell division.

The L133P mutation is located within the PLL motif converting PLL to PLP. This motif was suggested to influence dimerization (19). To determine if the artichoke mutation affects dimerization, we generated expression constructs of the coding sequence of wild-type TON1 and artichoke with different epitope tags. After transiently expressing TON1-3XMyc with TON1-3XFlag and artichoke-3XMyc with artichoke-3XFlag in protoplasts, we performed an immunoprecipitation of the Myc tagged protein (Fig. 1C). We found that wild-type TON1 efficiently pulled down the 3XFlag tagged protein, while the artichoke protein did not (Fig. 1D), suggesting that the L133P mutation affects dimerization.

To investigate the localization of TON1, we tagged TON1 with sequences encoding for three tandem mRuby2 proteins (3XmRuby2) on the 5’ end of the gene at its endogenous locus in a line expressing mEGFP-tubulin (15), resulting in a translational N-terminal fusion of 3XmRuby2 with TON1 (SI Appendix, Fig. S2). Importantly, N-terminally tagging the protein did not alter TON1 function, as the gametophores were fully expanded in 3XmRuby2-TON1 plants (Fig. 2A). We found that TON1 localized to the plasma membrane in gametophores (Fig. 2B and Movie S1). When we introduced the T398C mutation into the 3XmRuby2 tagged TON1 line generating 3XmRuby2-artichoke (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 and Table S1), we observed stunted gametophores as in the original artichoke mutant (Fig. 2A), further validating that T398C confers the artichoke mutant phenotype. Notably, the 3XmRuby2-artichoke fluorescence was now diffuse in the cytoplasm (Fig. 2B and Movie S2), suggesting that efficient self-dimerization is required for localization to the plasma membrane and furthermore if TON1 is not recruited to the membrane it does not function.

Previous studies have demonstrated that TON1 binds FASS, a B” regulatory subunit of PP2A (17). When we knocked out FASS in the 3XmRuby2-TON1 line, we observed stunted gametophores similar to gametophores in the artichoke and ∆ton1 mutants (Fig. 2A). However, unlike in artichoke gametophores, TON1 fluorescence was observed at the plasma membrane in the ∆fass mutants (Fig. 2B and Movie S3), suggesting that TON1 localization to the plasma membrane is required but not sufficient for its function and does not depend on binding to FASS.

TON1 Functions in Cell Division during Protonemal Branch Cell Divisions.

In contrast to the severely stunted growth of gametophores in artichoke and ∆ton1 mutants, protonemal growth, as described previously (22), was similar to wild type under normal conditions and showed an agravitropic defect in the dark (SI Appendix, Fig. S3). In A. thaliana, GFP-TON1 localized to PPB in premitotic cells and aligned with cortical microtubule arrays in interphase cells. These localizations strongly correlated with the defects in cell division and cell expansion observed in TON1 knock-out mutants (19). Given the lack of a phenotype, we did not expect to detect 3XmRuby2-TON1 fluorescence in light-grown protonemata. In general, we found weak cytoplasmic signal of 3XmRuby2-TON1 in protonemal apical cells. There was no obvious enrichment of TON1 at the cell cortex but a weak enrichment at the phragmoplast midzone in mitotic cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S4A). When imaged with variable angle epifluorescence microscopy (VAEM), TON1 appeared as discrete puncta at the cell cortex. However, most of the TON1 puncta did not align with microtubules in protonemal apical cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S4B). Consistent with the lack of enrichment of TON1 in mitotic structures or interphase microtubules, we did not find measurable defects in cell division orientation as cell plate angles were similar to wild type in both chloronemal and caulonemal cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S4 C and D). We did observe a mild defect in cell lengths that is not easily explained by altered cell expansion. While chloronemal tip cells were not different between wild type and ∆ton1, caulonemal tip cells were slightly shorter in ∆ton1 (SI Appendix, Fig. S4 C and E). In contrast, both caulonemal and chloronemal subapical cells were slightly longer in ∆ton1 (SI Appendix, Fig. S4 C and F). Measuring the length of the first two cells resulted in no significant difference between ∆ton1 and wild type (SI Appendix, Fig. S4 C and F). These data suggest that the observed cell length differences likely result from division occurring closer to the tip of the apical cell, which might result from altered nuclear migration. Given that TON1 is so weakly expressed in apical cells, it is not clear how TON1 contributes to this minor phenotype.

Surprisingly, we found that 3XmRuby2-TON1 accumulated on the plasma membrane at the branch-adjacent wall (hereafter this plasma membrane localization is referred to as the branch-adjacent wall) before the branch cell divides (Fig. 3A, magenta arrowhead). 3XmRuby2-TON1 also appeared as discrete puncta on the cell cortex, forming a loose ring around the branch neck (Fig. 3A, green arrowhead). Live cell imaging revealed that the TON1 signal from both these sites gradually attenuated during mitosis and cytokinesis (Fig. 3A and Movie S4).

To investigate whether cell division placement in branching cells was affected in ∆ton1 mutants, we stained protonemata with aniline blue to observe the position of the cell plates. In wild-type cells, the newly formed cell plate in the branch cell is frequently connected to the branch-adjacent wall (Fig. 3 B and C). Interestingly, this wall is where TON1 accumulates prior to mitosis and cell division. A division with this geometry results in a junction between three cells, the daughter cells from the divided branch cell and the cell apical to the branch. In contrast, in ∆ton1, the branching cell division more often generated a cell plate across the branch neck and rarely intersected with the branch-adjacent wall. This geometry results in a junction between only two cells—the daughter cells resulting from the branch cell division (Fig. 3 B and C). These data suggest that TON1 plays a role in cell division placement in branching cells in moss protonemal tissue.

The difference in cell plate placement could result from defects in the position of the nucleus, the spindle, or the phragmoplast. To distinguish these possibilities, we used live cell imaging of lines containing fluorescent microtubules to observe the position of the nucleus, spindle, and phragmoplast in wild-type and ∆ton1 branching cells. Representative branching cell divisions are shown in Fig. 3D and Movie S5. In wild type, the nucleus migrated to a position close to the emerging branch. The spindle and the early phragmoplast formed in a very similar position and the late phragmoplast did not deviate from this position, resulting in the division plane intersecting the branch-adjacent wall (Fig. 3D and Movie S5). In ∆ton1, the nucleus migrated to a similar position as in wild type. The spindle and the early phragmoplast similarly formed where the nucleus had been, but the late phragmoplast deviated significantly from the position of the early phragmoplast, resulting in a division plane across the branch neck (Fig. 3D and Movie S5).

To quantify the differences between wild type and ∆ton1, we characterized the position of the nucleus just before nuclear envelope breakdown with a scoring system described in Fig. 3E. Briefly, when the average position of the nucleus was near the cross wall, the cell received a score of two. A score greater than two indicated that the nucleus moved further into the branch, while a score less than two indicated that the nucleus remained in the main filament. Wild-type cells on average scored 1.7 (n = 16), and ∆ton1 cells scored 1.2 (n = 14) (Fig. 3E). These data reveal a small difference in nuclear positioning between wild type and ∆ton1. If incorrect nuclear positioning were the driver of the defect in cell plate placement in ∆ton1 cells, then in most cells the nucleus would have moved closer into the branch. This would have resulted in cell division across the branch neck and in an average score greater than two for ∆ton1. Instead, the ∆ton1 score was less than two, indicating that most of the nuclei in ∆ton1 cells were located centrally similar to wild type. For cells where the phragmoplast formed at the branch neck, the ensuing cell division resulted in a 2-cell junction (wild type, 4/16; ∆ton1, 6/14). For 12/16 wild-type cells where the phragmoplast formed near the branch-adjacent wall (Fig. 3E), cell division resulted in a 3-cell junction, while only 2/14 ∆ton1 cells divided similarly. For the majority of ∆ton1 cells (6/8) that formed the phragmoplast near the branch-adjacent wall, we observed instead that the phragmoplast shifted toward the branch neck, resulting in a 2-cell junction (Fig. 3E). These data suggest that the phragmoplast is actively guided toward a TON1-dependent signal on the branch-adjacent wall, reminiscent to TON1’s role in the cortical division site in cells that have a PPB.

TON1 and a Functional PP2A Complex Recruit Myosin VIII to the Branch-Adjacent Wall.

It was previously proposed that branching cells had a cortical division site at the branch neck based on a few observations: P. patens myosin VIII, when expressed in BY2 cells, localized to the PPB and the cortical division site; in P. patens, myosin VIII localized to a loose cortical ring at the branch neck; and plants lacking all myosin VIII had aberrant cell divisions at the branch (15). Myosin VIII enrichment on the branch-adjacent wall was observed but not reported because it appeared unconnected to a role in cell division for a few reasons. The localization appeared hours before mitotic entry and was extremely stable compared to myosin VIII in other regions of the cell. Additionally, cell divisions still occurred in ∆myo8abcde cells even when the branches were very far away from the branch-adjacent wall. However, our current findings that TON1 localizes to the branch-adjacent wall and that the majority of branch divisions lead to 3-cell junctions, led us to reexamine myosin VIII’s role on the branch-adjacent wall.

We investigated the relationship between myosin VIII and TON1 by tagging TON1 with 3xmRuby2 in its endogenous locus in a line where all five class VIII myosins were knocked out and replaced by a single myosin VIII tagged with three tandem mEGFPs, hereafter referred to as Myo8A-GFP (15). We found that TON1 and Myo8A colocalize on the branch-adjacent wall before the branch cell divides (Fig. 4A). Interestingly TON1 localization to the branch-adjacent wall is not affected in a line lacking all five myosin VIII genes, ∆myo8abcde (26), suggesting that TON1 does not depend on myosin VIII to localize to the branch-adjacent wall (Fig. 4B). TON1 localization is also not affected in the gametophores of ∆myo8abcde (SI Appendix, Fig. S5). In contrast, when TON1 was knocked out in the Myo8A-GFP line, Myo8A no longer accumulated on this wall, suggesting that Myo8A depends on TON1 to localize to the branch-adjacent wall (Fig. 4C). Furthermore, when FASS was knocked out, TON1 localized normally to the branch-adjacent wall (Fig. 4D), but Myo8A-GFP no longer localized to this wall (Fig. 4E). These data suggest that a functional PP2A complex, including TON1 and FASS, recruits Myo8A to the branch-adjacent wall before branch cell division.

Myosin VIII Is a Positive Phragmoplast Guidance Signal at the Cell Cortex.

Using time-lapse imaging we analyzed TON1 and Myo8A during branch cell division. We found that before the branching cell divides, both TON1 (Fig. 5A and Movie S6) and Myo8A (Fig. 5B and Movie S7) signals are at their highest levels on the branch adjacent cross wall. However, the rate of accumulation of TON1 and Myo8A differ. Two hours before the cell entered mitosis, TON1 already reached its maximum intensity on the branch-adjacent wall (Fig. 5A, time 0:00). In contrast, the Myo8A signal only started to increase at this time point (Fig. 5B, time 0:30), and its intensity continued to increase in the next 2 h (Fig. 5D). Upon mitotic entry, both TON1 and Myo8A intensity decreased rapidly at the branch-adjacent wall (Fig. 5 C and D). Interestingly, the kinetics of wall-associated TON1 is not affected by the absence of FASS (SI Appendix, Fig. S6B) or actin (SI Appendix, Fig. S6C). Furthermore, acute depolymerization of microtubules did not affect TON1 accumulation at the branch-adjacent wall (SI Appendix, Fig. S6D). These data suggest that TON1 arrives to the branch-adjacent wall before other PP2A complex components, and TON1’s localization at this site does not require a functional TTP complex or microtubules.

While TON1 appeared to uniformly disappear from the branch-adjacent wall after mitotic entry, Myo8A-GFP displayed an interesting pattern. We found that Myo8A redistributed along the branch-adjacent wall focusing into a narrow spot during phragmoplast expansion (Fig. 6A, yellow arrowheads). The focal point correlated with the site where the phragmoplast attached to the wall (Fig. 6A and Movie S8). In ∆ton1 cells, Myo8A was absent from the branch-adjacent wall but remained on the cell cortex at the branch neck (Fig. 6B, white arrowheads, Movie S9). We observed that the cortical Myo8A foci at the branch neck correlated with the site of phragmoplast attachment to the parental wall in ∆ton1 cells. These data suggest that Myo8A, at the branch-adjacent wall in wild type and on the cell cortex at the branch neck in ∆ton1, serves as a guidance cue for the expanding phragmoplast.

Myo8A, on the plus ends of microtubules in the phragmoplast midzone, has been shown to require actin to connect the leading edge of the phragmoplast to Myo8A at the cell cortex (15). When either myosin VIII or actin was disrupted, phragmoplast guidance was altered (15). While actin is not required to recruit myosin VIII to the branch-adjacent wall (SI Appendix, Fig. S7), we investigated whether actin is needed for phragmoplast guidance at this site. We imaged Myo8A-GFP in cells entering mitosis. In untreated cells, we observed focusing of Myo8A-GFP along the branch-adjacent wall. Furthermore, the phragmoplast expanded to the site of maximal Myo8A-GFP accumulation (Fig. 7A and Movie S10). In contrast, in cells treated with Latrunculin B, we found that Myo8A-GFP remained broadly distributed along the branch-adjacent wall and the signal did not decrease even at the end of cytokinesis (Fig. 7B and Movie S10). These data suggest that myosin VIII depends on actin to act as a positive signal to guide the phragmoplast to a cortical site for insertion into the parental wall.

Discussion

Here, we took advantage of the relatively simple geometry in protonemal branching cells, as well as the fact that organized cortical microtubules are not required for cell expansion in protonemata, to specifically investigate the role of TON1 in cell division. In protonemata, branching cells divide asymmetrically, requiring nuclear migration to the base of the branch and phragmoplast expansion near that site. In the absence of a PPB, it has been unclear how the orientation of this division is regulated. TON1, which in Arabidopsis is required for PPB formation, had previously been studied in P. patens and found to play a critical role in cell expansion in the gametophores with some evidence that PPBs no longer formed in gametophores (22). Since protonemata do not form PPBs and the ∆ton1 mutants were mostly normal in light grown tissue, protonemata were not examined further (22). Here, we were surprised to find that TON1 expresses well in protonemata and localizes to the plasma membrane on the cross wall adjacent to an emerging branch prior to and during cell division. This motivated us to examine the placement of cell plates in branching cells revealing that TON1 is required for proper positioning of the cell plate during branch division.

By analyzing nuclear position, mitotic spindle formation, and phragmoplast expansion using live cell imaging, we showed that TTP complex function is required for the later stages of cytokinesis in branching cells. TTP function ensures that the phragmoplast inserts into the branch-adjacent wall where TON1 localizes, resulting in a three-cell junction. In ∆ton1 mutants both nuclear migration as well as mitotic spindle assembly and positioning are largely unaffected. In fact, in ∆ton1 cells the phragmoplast often expanded along the branch neck and did not insert into the branch-adjacent wall, reorienting to this position even when the nucleus and spindle had set up such that the phragmoplast could intersect the branch-adjacent wall. Given that TON1 localizes to the branch-adjacent wall, our data suggest that in the absence of TON1 at this site, a signal present at the base of the emerging branch guides the phragmoplast to the branch neck.

We found that myosin VIII may be a component of this positive signal. Myosin VIII requires TON1 to localize to the branch-adjacent wall but not vice versa, placing TON1 upstream of myosin VIII. In fact, TON1 localizes to this site before myosin VIII. Furthermore, TON1 alone is not sufficient to recruit myosin VIII, since myosin VIII no longer localizes in ∆fass. These data suggest that the TTP complex must be active and contain FASS to recruit myosin VIII to the branch-adjacent wall prior to mitosis. Myosin VIII remains distributed along the length of the branch-adjacent wall until later in cytokinesis when it accumulates in an actin-dependent manner to a small area coincident with the phragmoplast insertion site. In the absence of TTP, the phragmoplast may continue to use myosin VIII, which now only localizes to branch neck. If myosin VIII is the only component of the positive phragmoplast guidance machinery, then we would expect that loss of myosin VIII function should lack phragmoplast guidance altogether. However, this has been difficult to determine because myosin VIII plays multiple roles in protonemal patterning. For example, myosin VIII helps to coordinate microtubules and actin at the growing tip directing polarized growth (27) and cells lacking myosin VIII function often have improperly positioned branches that are very short before they divide (15, 26). For cells that do have relatively normal side branches, cell plate positioning was normal (26), suggesting that multiple signals recruited by TTP may be mediating phragmoplast guidance (5). We posit that some of these signals could include the homologs of phragmoplast orienting kinesins POK1 (28–30), myosin XI (31), and microtubule binding protein TANGLED1 (32–34), etc. Future studies analyzing the dependence of the localization and function of additional phragmoplast guidance factors on TTP may help to identify pathways redundant with myosin VIII in protonemata.

By analyzing TTP function in protonemata, we have gained insights into TTP’s role specifically in phragmoplast guidance. In angiosperms, investigating the specific role of TTP in cell division has been complicated by the severe phenotypes of ton1 and fass mutants that result from a global loss of microtubule organization impacting both cell division and expansion. Efforts to separate the two have been made by examining mutants in a subset of TRM proteins. TRM4 was found to associate with CESA3 to help guide cellulose deposition in seeds (35), overexpression of TRM2 led to elongation of several organs including petioles, leaf blades, floral organs, and siliques (36), and a null mutant in TRM21 regulated flavonoid metabolism and resulted in stunted plants with shorter root hairs (37). In rice, TRM proteins have also been implicated in grain shape (38). In maize, the TRM protein, ZmLNG1, was shown to link the TTP complex to the ovate family of proteins, a family of proteins known to regulate organ shape (39). With respect to cell division, TRM6/7/8 are required for PPB formation and for fine-tuning the orientation of cell division, but not for microtubule orientation in expanding cells (21). Based on these findings, it has been hypothesized that specific TRM proteins function to recruit TON1 and FASS to distinct sites where TTP complex activity is required to modulate cell expansion and cell division (18, 20).

Interestingly, whether TON1 and other components of the TTP complex still localize in mutants lacking TRM has not been investigated. However, in the trm6/7/8 mutant, phragmoplast guidance factors such as myosin XI and POK1 are still recruited to the cortical division zone (21, 31). In contrast, in a fass mutant, myosin XI and the cortical division site protein TANGLED are unable to enrich at the future site of cell division (31, 32). We have found that TON1 localization to the future site of cell division occurs many hours before mitosis and is upstream and independent of FASS, suggesting that TON1 may be one of the earliest factors recruited to the cortical division zone. Thus, we propose that TON1 defines the cortical division site, recruiting other components of the TTP complex, and ultimately recruiting cortical division proteins that are required for phragmoplast guidance. In cells that use a PPB, TTP would then recruit microtubule-binding proteins to facilitate the formation of the microtubule-based PPB, while in cells lacking a PPB, TTP would recruit guidance machinery independent of microtubules.

Materials and Methods

Tissue Propagation.

Propagation of P. patens was performed by moderately homogenizing tissue in water and pipetting onto cellophane covered solid media (1.03 mM MgSO4, 1.86 mM KH2PO4, 3.3 mM Ca(NO3)2, 2.72 mM (NH4)2-tartrate, 45 μM FeSO4, 9.93 μM H3BO3, 220 nM CuSO4, 1.966 μM MnCl2, 231 nM CoCl2, 191 nM ZnSO4, 169 nM KI, and 103 nM Na2MoO4, 0.7% agar). Tissue was grown under 85 µmolphotons/m2s light with long-day conditions at room temperature in Percival growth chambers.

Whole Genome Sequencing.

Genome sequencing was performed by Novogene. We sent eight plates of 1-wk-old ground tissue of artichoke and Lifeact-mRuby2 (the parental line which was a mixture of artichoke and wild type). Novogene extracted DNA, prepared libraries, and performed sequencing with the Illumina Platform PE150. Novogene also performed the standard analysis, which included data quality control (filtering reads containing adapter sequences or that are of low quality), alignment with the reference genome, statistics of sequencing depth and coverage, as well as variant calling. Using variant calling and keeping in mind that Lifeact-mRuby is a mixture of artichoke and wild type, we searched for variants in coding regions that were homozygous in artichoke and heterozygous in Lifeact-mRuby2. With this approach, we found two genes (SERINE/THREONINE-PROTEIN KINASE QKGA-RELATED: Pp3c5_15380; and FGFR1 oncogene partner (FGFR10P)//TONNEAU 1: Pp3c14_5580) with nonsynonymous changes.

Transient Expression in Protoplasts.

To clone the TON1 and artichoke cDNAs, RNA was isolated from 7-d-old ground wild type and artichoke protonemal tissue using the RNeasy plant mini kit (QIAGEN), according to the manufacturer’s recommendations. We generated total cDNA using oligo(dT) and SuperScript III Reverse transcription (Invitrogen) following the manufacturer’s protocol. The full-length TON1 and artichoke cDNAs were amplified from total cDNA using primers listed in SI Appendix, Table S2 that incorporated attB1 and attB5. The PCR amplicons were then cloned into a vector using a BP Clonase reaction generating the pENTR-L1R5-TON1 and pENTR-L1R5-artichoke plasmids. These entry clones were used in a two-fragment LR Clonase recombination with pTH-UBI-gate (40) and pENTR-L5L2-3XFLAG (41) reaction to generate pTH-UBI-TON1-3XFlag and pTH-UTI-artichoke-3XFlag. To generate the 3XMyc tagged version, we amplified full-length TON1 and artichoke cDNAs from total cDNA using primers listed in SI Appendix, Table S2 that incorporated attB1 and attB2 sites. We cloned these amplicons using a BP clonase reaction to generate pENTR-L1L2-TON1 and pENTR-L1L2-artichoke plasmids. We then modified pENTR-L1L2-TON1 and pENTR-L1L2-artichoke and incorporated in-frame Myc tags using three consecutive NEB Hi-Fi reactions with the Myc oligos listed in SI Appendix, Table S2. The final expression constructs were generated using an LR clonase reaction with pTH-UBI-gate to generate pTH-UBI-TON1-3XMyc and pTH-UTI-artichoke-3XMyc.

We cotransformed 20 to 30 µg of the 3XMyc expression plasmid and 5 to 7.5 µg of the 3XFlag expression plasmid using PEG mediated transformation of protoplasts as previously described (15). Protoplasts were incubated for 3 d in Hoagland’s medium supplemented with 8.5% mannitol and 10 mM CaCl2. Protoplasts were collected by centrifugation at 600× g and then resuspended in 100 µL of lysis buffer (5 mM HEPES pH 7, 2 mM EDTA, 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM DTT, 1 mM PMSF, 0.1% (v/v) Triton X-100, 0.1 mg/mL Leupeptin). We lysed the protoplasts by three consecutive freeze (liquid N2)/thaw (37 °C)/vortex for 30 s cycles; spun in a microcentrifuge at maximum speed at 4 °C for 10 min. Myc-Trap magnetic agarose beads (Proteintech) were pre-equilibrated in wash buffer (lysis buffer with no Triton X-100) and then 10 µL of the beads were added to the lysate and incubated at 4 °C for 2 h with constant rotation. The beads were washed three times with wash buffer and the bound proteins were eluted by resuspending the beads in 2× SDS sample buffer and boiling for 10 min. All samples were separated by electrophoresis on a 4 to 15% gradient Mini-PROTEAN® TGX™ Precast Gel (BIO-RAD) and subsequently transferred to nitrocellulose using a Trans-Blot Turbo Transfer System (BIO-RAD). Immunoblotting was performed with a standard protocol (41) using a 1:5,000 dilution of anti-Flag (Sigma) or anti-Myc (Santa Cruz Biotechnology) primary antibodies followed by a 1:25,000 dilution of horse radish peroxidase conjugated anti-mouse secondary antibodies (Santa Cruz Biotechnology). Chemiluminescence was performed with ECL Select Western Blotting Detection Reagent (Amersham) and detected using an ImageQuant 500 gel doc (Amersham).

Generation of Knockout and Tagged Lines.

Except for previously published moss lines, moss lines were created using CRISPR-Cas9 genome editing using the procedures described in refs. 24 and 25 and are listed in SI Appendix, Table S1. To tag TON1, we generated two constructs: a plasmid containing modules that expresses the guide RNA and Cas9, and a homology plasmid that contains the fluorescent protein sequence in between sequences upstream and downstream of Cas9 target site. The plasmids were constructed using the methods and modular vectors described in ref. 24 and primers listed in SI Appendix, Table S2. Genotyping was performed according to ref. 25 and the primers are listed in SI Appendix, Table S2. We isolated the initial ∆ton1 in the mEGFP-tubulin line (15) as a result of transforming both a tagging construct/protospacer mix as well as plasmids to incorporate the artichoke mutation. To cure the artichoke mutation, we used a homology plasmid containing ~1,000 bp of homology that had been amplified with primers listed in SI Appendix, Table S2 from wild-type genomic DNA and cloned into pGEM-Teasy (Promega) using T4 DNA ligase. The CRISPR plasmids were generated using the primers listed in SI Appendix, Table S2 and the methods and vectors described in ref. 24 for curing the artichoke mutation and for tagging the TON1 locus with 3XmRuby2 and mEGFP. To introduce the artichoke mutation in the various backgrounds listed in SI Appendix, Table S1, we generated a CRISPR plasmid using the methods and vectors described in ref. 25 and we used a double-stranded oligo as the homology template (SI Appendix, Table S2).

Genotyping.

To genotype mutants generated by CRISPR-Cas9 mutagenesis, we extracted DNA from plants that were 3 to 4 wk old (0.5 to 1 cm in diameter) using the protocol described in ref. 25. For editing experiments, we used one of two methods: Amplification primers surrounding the expected Cas9 cleavage site (~300 to 400 bp on each side) followed by a T7 endonuclease assay were used to screen candidate mutants as described (24) or competition PCR as described in ref. 25. All genotyping primers are listed in SI Appendix, Table S2. For homology-directed repair tagging and homologous recombination tagging, we used primers (SI Appendix, Table S2) outside of the homology region to avoid amplification of residual DNA donor template.

Brightfield Microscopy.

Bright field images were acquired with a 1× objective using a stereomicroscope (Nikon SMZ25) equipped with a CCD camera (Nikon digital sight DS-Fi2).

Laser Scanning Confocal Microscopy.

To image cell plates in protonemal filaments, we stained 5 to 7 d old ground tissue with aniline blue, a dye that stains callose. Briefly, moss tissue was placed on an agar pad in Hoagland’s buffer (4 mM KNO3, 2 mM KH2PO4, 1 mM Ca(NO3)2, 89 µM Fe citrate, 300 µM MgSO4, 9.93 µM H3BO3, 220 nM CuSO4, 1.966 µM MnCl2, 231 nM CoCl2, 191 nM ZnSO4, 169 nM KI, 103 nM Na2MoO4, and 1% sucrose) containing 20 µg/mL aniline blue, covered by a glass coverslip and sealed with VALAP (1:1:1 parts of Vaseline, lanoline, and paraffin). Images were acquired on a Nikon A1R confocal microscope system with a 0.75 NA 20× objective (Nikon) at room temperature. Image acquisition was controlled by NIS-Element AR 4.1 software (Nikon). 1% laser illumination at 405 nm was used to excite aniline blue, and the emission filter used was 525/50 nm aniline blue. GaAsP PMT detector collected the aniline blue emission.

To image TON1 in gametophores, we used microfluidic imaging chambers (42). Ground protonemal tissue was gently pipetted into the central part of the device followed by an infusion of Hoagland’s medium. Then the chamber was submerged in Hoagland’s medium and placed under constant 85 µmolphotons/m2s light for at least 2 wk prior to imaging. Images were acquired on a Nikon A1R confocal microscope system with a 0.75 NA 20× objective (Nikon) at room temperature. Image acquisition was controlled by NIS-Element AR 4.1 software (Nikon). Laser illumination at 561 nm was used to excite 3XmRuby2 (laser power, 0.5 to 1%; Gain, 40 to 60; PMT offset, 28 to 33). The emission filter was 595/50 nm and collected with a GaAsP PMT.

To image protonemata containing 3XmRuby2 and mEGFP fluorophores, we mounted cells on agar pads as described (43). Cells grew rapidly for >2 h as long as cells received white light illumination between each image acquisition. Images were acquired on a Nikon A1R confocal microscope system with a 1.49 NA 60× TIRF oil immersion objective (Nikon) at room temperature. Image acquisition was controlled by NIS-Element AR 4.1 software (Nikon). Laser illumination at 488 nm was used for mEGFP (laser power, 0.5 to 1%; Gain, 40 to 60; PMT offset, 28 to 33) and 561 nm was used for 3xmRuby2 (laser power, 0.5 to 1%; Gain, 40 to 60; PMT offset, 28 to 33). Emission filters were 525/50 nm for mEGFP and 595/50 nm for mRuby2. Both channels were equipped with GaAsP PMTs.

Statistical Analyses.

Kruskal–Wallis tests were performed with KaleidaGraph software (Synergy software).
