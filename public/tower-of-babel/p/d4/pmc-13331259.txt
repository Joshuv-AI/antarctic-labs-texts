The Multiple Dimensions of Self-Management in Active Inflammatory Bowel Disease: A Qualitative Analysis of Patients’ Daily Lived Experiences
Authors: Shirley Cohen-Mekelburg, Gal Hodish, Drew Ebner, Ken Resnicow, Akbar K. Waljee, Sameer Saini, Sarah Hawley, Melissa Dejonckheere, Megan Riehl, Jessica Naftaly, Christina Jagielski, Nicholas Henry, Kathleen Swalwell, Peter D. R. Higgins, Sarah L. Krein

ABSTRACT
PurposeSelf-management is critical to improving inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) health but challenging for patients. To inform the development of IBD self-management interventions and improve IBD health, our objective was to develop an in-depth understanding of factors that affect quality of life and health outcomes for patients with IBD.MethodsWe conducted semi-structured interviews of adult IBD patients with a recent flare drawn from two health system cohorts. We analyzed interview data using an inductive-deductive rapid qualitative analysis process to identify key domains by age (18–44 years, 45–64 years, ≥ 65 years) and cohort.ResultsWe conducted 55 total interviews. Participants in both cohorts and across all ages focused on five key domains that are critical to living well with IBD: 1) key skills required for living and dealing with IBD; 2) perceptions about living with and confidence in dealing with IBD; 3) impact of social support; 4) effects of stress and emotions; and 5) self-management tools and resources.ConclusionIBD self-management requires complex behavioral changes, including building several skills in recognizing and managing symptoms, managing diet and treatment, navigating care, and coping with unpredictability. Patients must work to build their confidence, avoid maladaptive thinking, and remain motivated despite setbacks. Addressing stress and mental health issues as well as eliciting social support are also critical to helping patients cope with IBD.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10620-026-09858-x.

Background

Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) treatment is driven by gastroenterologists, who prescribe medications that target active inflammation to control symptoms and prevent bowel damage. However, medication treatment alone is not sufficient for preventing IBD-related flares and hospitalizations nor improving health-related quality of life. In fact, 30% of patients with IBD experience inflammatory symptoms such as abdominal pain or rectal bleeding each year (i.e., active IBD), 30% of patients report impaired health-related quality of life, and IBD-related healthcare utilization continues to rise [1–4].

While gastroenterologists follow their patients with IBD closely, day-to-day IBD management occurs between clinic visits, demanding patients’ active participation in their care [5]. Therefore, patients need to master self-management (i.e., the daily behaviors they can perform to manage their illness, to minimize its impact on their physical health, and to help cope with comorbid psychological symptoms) to improve their IBD health [6]. IBD self-management includes behavioral skills, such as recognizing and coping with symptoms, managing medications, collaborating with clinicians on treatment choices, and navigating the health system and insurance coverage. IBD self-management also involves psychosocial capacities, such as understanding the course of IBD and having the confidence to manage it [7–9]. However, self-management is challenging for patients with IBD, especially those with active disease, and gastroenterology clinics have insufficient resources to fully support self-management. These difficulties with IBD self-management are associated with increased physical and emotional symptoms and impaired health-related quality of life [10–12].

Self-management interventions, incorporating disease-specific health education, goal setting, cognitive behavioral techniques, self-regulation strategies, relaxation exercises, and trackers, have successfully improved health outcomes for patients with various chronic conditions [13–16] The field of psychogastroenterology has emerged to address the complex interaction between the gut and brain in gastrointestinal disorders, including IBD. Gastrointestinal psychologists integrate evidence-based behavioral medicine strategies with disease-specific knowledge, providing interventions that target stress, maladaptive thought patterns, and health behaviors that can influence disease course and symptom burden. [17] For patients with IBD, such interventions can strengthen self-management skills, improve coping, and enhance adherence to treatment plans, thereby supporting both physical and psychological health [17]. However, access to gastrointestinal psychologists is limited, and therefore, a critical need exists for more widely available interventions to support patients with IBD in self-management. Integrating principles from psychogastroenterology into standalone, tailored IBD self-management interventions may address both behavioral and psychological barriers to care, creating a more comprehensive approach to improving health outcomes [18].

Several standalone self-management interventions have been developed for patients with IBD but have overall shown inconsistent effects. In one study, systematic review data from fifteen IBD-focused interventions designed to support psychological functioning and build behavioral skills showed improvement on health-related quality of life [9]. In contrast, in several other systematic review studies, IBD-focused self-management interventions failed to show a positive intervention effect [8, 19]. Existing data has shown that in-person interventions are less effective than digital interventions [9], and that both education and symptom monitoring are critical to improving health-related quality of life [8, 20, 21]. However, these interventions are not necessarily tailored to the needs of patients with active inflammation nor comprehensively address critical behavioral and psychosocial factors. Therefore, our study objective was to develop an in-depth understanding of factors that affect quality of life and health outcomes for patients with active IBD to inform the tailoring of IBD self-management interventions. We also focused specifically on whether there are age-related differences in patient experiences as a potentially important aspect of developing a tailored self-management approach.

Methods

Following a qualitative descriptive study design [22], we conducted qualitative interviews of adult IBD patients (18 years or older) with a recent flare, drawn from two health systems. We defined recent flare as an increase in IBD symptoms sufficient to warrant a dose, medication, or treatment change in the six months prior to study enrollment, as this timing should be sufficient to allow patients to speak meaningfully about their experiences managing active IBD. We recruited patients from the University of Michigan Health System, a tertiary referral academic center with several community clinics in Southeast Michigan, as well as the Veterans Health Administration (VHA), a national integrated healthcare system, to maximize transferability of our study findings and gain insight into the needs and experiences of a diverse group of patients. We purposefully recruited patients from three age subgroups (ages 18–44 years, 45–64 years, 65 years or older) so that study findings are inclusive of different perspectives and experiences. We excluded patients who had uncontrolled medical or psychiatric comorbidities due to possible confounding variables and patients who were unable to speak English given limitations to completing the interview process.

We used methods tailored to each individual population to maximize recruitment. For the University of Michigan cohort, study eligibility was confirmed by review of the electronic health record and eligible patients were invited to participate in the study by email correspondence, followed by two additional email reminders or by in-person invitation during a clinical IBD-related visit. For the national VHA cohort, potentially eligible patients were identified using VA Corporate Warehouse data to select patients with a diagnosis of IBD. These patients were mailed a recruitment letter with a brief description of the study, contact information for the study team, and information on how to opt-out of further contact. Veterans who did not opt out received a follow-up phone call, with up to three additional calls, to discuss study participation. Participants received a $20 gift card for participation in the interview. We anticipated that we would need an estimated 30 participant interviews from each population to achieve data saturation (i.e., informational redundancy) [23].

Semi-structured interviews were conducted via phone, video (i.e., Zoom or Microsoft Teams), or in-person by one of five study team members (GH, SCM, DE, KW, NH). Interviews were audio-recorded and transcribed and took 45–60 min to complete. Interviews were assisted by an interview guide, which was developed by the study team, and pre-tested using five pilot interviews (Supplementary File). Interview questions were informed by a conceptual model (Fig. 1) developed from Lorig’s self-management theory, social cognitive theory, and self-determination theory, and focused on patients’ confidence and motivation in performing self-management behaviors and related psychosocial factors, including but not limited to recognizing and coping with symptoms, managing medications, communicating with medical teams, coping with stress and anxiety, and social support. Interviews explored participants’ experiences, perspectives, facilitators, barriers, and unmet needs within each category of interest [6, 7]. We also elicited patients’ current strategies for overcoming barriers or strategies that facilitated IBD self-management, and their opinions about various intervention components. Finally, we asked participants about their preferences regarding delivery of a self-management intervention. During the interview, we administered a brief survey to collect demographic information in addition to self-efficacy and motivation scores using the validated IBD Self-Efficacy Scale (IBD-SES)[24] and Treatment Self-Regulation Questionnaire (TSRQ)[25], respectively. The IBD-SES is a 29-item questionnaire that quantifies an individual’s confidence in managing their IBD and symptoms, managing medical care, managing stress and emotions, and maintaining remission [24]. Total IBD-SES scores range from 29 to 290 with higher scores representing higher self-efficacy. The TSRQ quantifies an individual’s autonomous and controlled motivation to perform a specific behavior and scores range from 1 to 7, with higher scores representing higher levels of motivation [25]. Data Analysis

Quantitative cohort data including demographic and IBD-specific self-efficacy and motivation measures were described as means (standard deviation) and frequency (percentages), as appropriate. We analyzed qualitative interview data using an inductive-deductive rapid qualitative analysis process [26, 27]. We anticipated that several key factors were relevant to patients with IBD and important to managing active IBD, as described in our conceptual model (Fig. 1). First, we developed a summary template, informed by the interview guide core concepts and our conceptual model. Then, using the summary template, one team member (GH, SCM, DE, KW, NH) created an initial interview summary using detailed notes taken during each interview. This initial summary was subsequently reviewed by a second study team member who compared the initial summary to the interview notes and the transcript for any further additions or corrections. We then leveraged a consensus-based process with a third reviewer to discuss analytic discrepancies and challenges to ensure consistency in data summarization. Finally, the focused summaries were combined into a summary matrix organized by domain to identify patterns and to compare responses within these key domains by age group (18–44 years, 45–64 years, and 65 years and older) and by site (university vs VHA).

Results

A total of 55 participants completed interviews and quantitative questionnaires, leading to data saturation (i.e., informational redundancy) and comprising the analytic cohort. The university cohort (n = 25 participants) had a mean age of 50.16 (standard deviation [sd] 18.25) years, were predominantly White (22, 88.0%), and 12 (48.0%) were female. The VHA cohort (n = 30 participants) had a mean age of 55.77 (sd 15.41) years, were predominantly White (24, 80%), and 6 (20%) were female (Table 1). Domain 1: Key Skills Required for Living and Dealing with IBD (Table 2)

Recognizing and Managing Symptoms Is a Daily Effort

Although predominating symptoms such as diarrhea, abdominal pain, fatigue, fecal urgency, and fecal incontinence varied over time, patients described spending a lot of day-to-day effort (i.e., emotional energy) managing and coping with their symptoms. Patients across age groups described using multiple modalities including rest, hydration, prescribed medications, alternative therapies, dietary changes, and activities such as running, yoga, and meditation to manage their symptoms. Some patients indicated that they routinely monitored their symptoms to recognize improvements, deteriorations, and correlations with triggers such as changes in diet, sleep, physical activity, medication, or stress. Many patients reported checking in with their bodies daily to understand their health status, while some patients also leveraged diaries and mobile applications to document and track symptom patterns. Symptom tracking was especially helpful for older adults with memory issues. A few patients disliked daily symptom monitoring as it forced them to think more about their disease but were motivated to do so to assist their care team. Patients also described how the perceived benefit of symptom monitoring changed depending on their remission or flare state.

Selecting and Adhering to a Medical Regimen Requires an Understanding of Benefits and Adverse Effects and Expectations for Treatment Response

Patients identified prescription medications as essential to treating their IBD and related symptoms. While patients across age groups were accepting and satisfied with their prescription IBD medications, they also endorsed hesitancy with initiating advanced therapies (e.g., biologics), including concerns about dependence, self-injection, and side effects. These concerns led patients to seek additional information on the internet and reluctance to initiate treatment. Patients also described corticosteroid side effects and preference for future corticosteroid avoidance, including delaying communication of symptom changes with their care team to avoid further corticosteroid prescribing. After initiation of advanced therapies, the key factors that promoted medication adherence included clinicians who set expectations for time to treatment response, and patients’ desire to avoid worsening symptoms or complications. However, patients across age groups were frustrated by inadequate treatment responses and frequent medication changes. In context, patients perceived a need for more holistic strategies to handle their symptoms. For example, one patient found chamomile and licorice tea to help with pain. Others pursued Kegel exercises to manage fecal urgency. Some of these strategies were developed through trial and error, while others were advised by peers.

Diet Plays a Major Role in Triggering Symptoms and Patients’ Sense of Control but Few Patients Feel that They Receive Clear Dietary Guidance

Patients are interested in the role of diet in reducing or preventing IBD symptoms. Efforts to identify trigger foods were often initiated by patients seeking non-medication treatment. Patients of all age groups described using trial and error or an elimination process to develop an understanding of their optimal diet. Some patients found systematic diet tracking helpful in supporting this process along with daily meal planning to promote consistent eating habits. Patients reported the most benefit from closely tracking and restricting potential food triggers during times of flare. Patients of all ages described inadequate dietary guidance from clinicians and identified a need for proactive dietary guidance. Patients sought to learn from others’ experiences with diet through reading, internet searches, and discussion with peers to inform their dietary habits. A few patients sought out diet as an alternative to medication, though often this was a misunderstanding of the role of diet for symptom relief rather than inflammation treatment.

Navigating Healthcare Is Frustrating and Filled with Barriers to Recovery

Patients experienced many challenges with navigating healthcare. First, patients of all ages were frustrated by quick visits (e.g., 30-min appointments), medication-focused office visits, and limited communication with their care team, which made them feel alone handling day-to-day care. When they experienced new symptoms, some patients found it difficult to receive timely medical information, and therefore, patients sought out new clinicians who were not dismissive and met their individual needs. Second, patients of all age groups, and in particular in the university cohort, felt that insurance coverage drove care decisions (e.g., positioning of treatments), and experienced coverage lapses and delays in treatment due to prior authorization processes. These challenges put patients at risk for worsening symptoms, anxiety, complications, and hospitalizations. For example, uninsured patients described having to stop their medication and living in fear of a flare as they were navigating insurance applications and others made career decisions based on insurance needs. Over time, patients gained expertise in navigating healthcare, communication with medical staff, and handling insurance coverage issues.

The Unpredictability of Symptoms and Flares Is Anxiety-Provoking and a Disruptor to Patients’ Daily Lives

Patients of all ages identified the unpredictability of flare symptoms (especially abdominal pain and fecal urgency) as the most difficult aspect of living with IBD. The inability to anticipate or control symptom recurrence resulted in chronic stress and anxiety for patients of all ages. Patients structured trips outside the house around accessible bathroom locations so that they could plan for anticipated and unanticipated symptoms. They learned to prepare for accidents by carrying a change of clothes. Some patients found symptom monitoring helpful in anticipating symptoms. Many patients identified a gap in resources to support them in navigating the unpredictable nature of the disease. One participant suggested the development of a patient guide with information on identifying accessible bathroom locations and a recommended list of items to carry in a “go-bag” (such as extra clothing).

Domain 2: Perceptions About Living with and Confidence in Dealing with IBD (Table 3)

Negative Thinking Can Magnify Negative Experiences and Feelings of Futility

Patients had varied perceptions about living with IBD. Patients in all age groups described maladaptive coping strategies including denial, thoughts of self-blame, and thoughts of futility that led them to avoid self-care. Self-blame and embarrassment led to thought distortions. One patient described being bothered by the disease “dictating my life”. Over time and with experience, these perceptions often transitioned into acceptance of their IBD. Other patients compared their circumstances to others and felt that they were “lucky” to not have it worse. These patients perceived their IBD as an opportunity to be healthy, exercise, and eat a balanced diet. Some patients sought cognitive therapy to alter their way of thinking to better cope and minimize thoughts of futility or used humor as a way of handling their circumstances.

Through Experience and Learning Some Patients Gained Confidence in their Ability to Deal with Their IBD but Self-Doubt Often Persists

Confidence was critical to managing IBD for patients in all age groups. However, both patients who self-identified as confident and not confident in managing their IBD during their interview, also identified difficulties with disease self-management. Confident patients reported that they were unsure if they could do more or have a better self-management strategy, due to the lack of routine feedback, the unpredictability of IBD, and the perception that they were managing this disease on their own. For patients who described a lack of confidence in managing their IBD, access to information, resources such as medication for pain (e.g., anti-spasmodic), or a plan for communicating with their physician, strengthened their confidence. Other patients who reported a lack of confidence in managing their IBD described symptom monitoring and communication with their care team as a means of increasing their confidence. Ultimately, even confident patients wanted regular reassurance, especially during flares when they questioned their daily habits and ability to cope with their IBD.

Internal and External Factors Affect Patient’s Motivation to Actively Manage Their IBD

Motivation promoted self-management for patients of all ages. Patients identified their families, hobbies, ability to work, avoidance of complications, and role modeling as drivers of self-management. Some patients prioritized their health and did not want IBD to limit them. Some patients were more motivated to actively participate in their care during flares as compared to remission. In contrast, patients noted that the unpredictability of flares negatively impacted motivation to actively manage their IBD.

Domain 3: Impact of Social Support (Table 4)

Support from Family and Friends Is Key to Handling Symptoms, Controlling Diet, and Managing Stress

Social support provided by family members or friends, including direct aid, empathy, shared experience, and advice was valuable to patients of all ages. Patients valued receiving direct support for preparing foods that met their dietary needs, caring for them during illness, joining them for clinic visits, identifying bathrooms, or guiding them through the hurdles of navigating healthcare. Some patients were dependent on their social relationships for managing their IBD. Some patients endorsed abandoning relationships with people who did not understand their disease. Others hid their IBD from their social circle all together, which led to social isolation.

Few Patients Have Access to Peers for Support and Experiences with Support Groups Are Variable

Patients of all ages described few social connections with other IBD patients, and specifically, peers with a shared experience. They described a desire for advice from other patients like them of similar ages or with similar experiences and expressed preference for matched peer support. Indeed, some patients had negative experiences connecting with peers who thought differently than them (e.g., lack of motivation, maladaptive thoughts). Some patients also had positive experiences with support groups, while others had negative experiences. Most expressed interest but had not engaged with support groups.

Domain 4: Effects of Stress and Emotions (Table 5)

Stress and Anxiety Are Viewed as a Trigger

Patients of all age groups correlated stress with their IBD symptoms. Patients experienced anxiety around healthcare settings and increased stress related to the inability to anticipate flares, complications, and need for surgery. Patients of all ages developed effective strategies for handling their stress and IBD, including engaging in journaling, video games as a distraction, relaxation tapes, meditation, physical activity, social support, avoidance of conflict, and acupuncture. The medical team was often absent from these stress management efforts.

Depression Confounds Patients’ IBD Experience

Flare symptoms, lack of control, and healthcare challenges contributed to depressed mood. Depression was worse during flares as compared to remission. Patients sought out cognitive behavioral therapy to improve their mood, as well as to reduce stress and anxiety. Some stress management strategies, such as physical activity, were also used to improve depressed mood.

Domain 5: Self-Management Tools and Resources (Table 6)

Patients Are Interested in Self-Management Programs and Are Open to Virtual Program Options

Most patients of all ages had never engaged with self-management tools, such as educational programs, trackers, cognitive behavioral treatments, but expressed an interest in self-management and a need for self-management support. Existing self-management resources included mobile applications for symptom tracking, journaling, or breathing exercises. Patients identified limitations of existing tools, including limited support in correlating symptoms with diet, medication, or activity changes. Patients who had previously learned about other patients’ experiences by viewing patient testimonials (e.g., during online searches) felt that viewing these testimonials improved their confidence in managing their symptoms and provided them with helpful advice on coping with their illness. Convenience and privacy were identified as advantages of digital and asynchronous tools (e.g., mobile or computer) by patients in all age groups. Some patients preferred a synchronous virtual option (e.g., Zoom) for building accountability and community. Others preferred phone calls or in-person but were open to learning more about virtual options.

Patients Highlight a Need for Tailoring Self-Management Support to Patients’ Individual Needs

Patients showed interest in tracking tools, dietary and exercise guides, testimonials, stress management tools, and evidence-based information resources. For patients of all ages, there were mixed opinions and preferences for virtual versus in-person delivery of self-management tools, and synchronous versus asynchronous options. Patients often discussed the need for tailoring of content, intensity, and modality by individual and circumstance.

Discussion

Developing effective interventions to support self-management of patients with IBD and active disease requires an in-depth understanding of factors that affect health-related quality of life and health outcomes. Our findings suggest that coping with IBD and active disease requires skills for managing symptoms, disease unpredictability, medications, diet, and healthcare challenges. Clinicians can support patients by offering skill building and peer support resources and addressing mental health concerns. Addressing negative thinking may also be beneficial. Finally, IBD self-management support needs to be tailored to individual patients’ needs, but separate interventions for different age groups may not be necessary.

IBD self-management requires developing behavioral skills such as recognizing and coping with symptoms, managing medications and diet, collaborating with clinicians on treatment choices, and navigating the health system. A Swedish study leveraged qualitative interview data to classify IBD self-management behaviors into four categories: (1) recognition of physical and psychological symptoms; (2) managing symptoms through dietary changes, medical treatment, stress management, and use of complementary remedies; (3) planning activities; and (4) seeking new opportunities through information gathering and social engagement [28]. Another study of focus group participants with IBD in the United Kingdom found that patients effectively controlled their symptoms of fatigue, urgency, and pain through stress management practices, dietary changes, and other home remedies [29]. While these studies were not specific to patients with IBD and active inflammation, our study findings confirm the value of these skills in an American population with active IBD.

We found that navigating healthcare, including communicating with care teams and managing health insurance obstacles, is an additional skill that requires mastery in context of active IBD. A recent patient survey by the Crohn’s Colitis Foundation identified several barriers to accessing IBD care including timely access to medications, coverage for diagnostic tests, and financial constraints [30]. The challenge of navigating healthcare extends beyond the U.S., as demonstrated by a Canadian study describing the complexity of accessing IBD care [31]. While several publicly available resources exist to support patients in navigating healthcare [30], support for building skills to navigate health system challenges, such as insurance and access barriers, have not been systematically incorporated into effective IBD self-management programs.

We also found that unpredictability of symptoms further complicates patients’ lives with active IBD. Prior qualitative studies with IBD patients also describe the importance of adapting to uncertainty [29, 32]. To support patients in effectively building these skills for self-management requires multicomponent IBD-tailored interventions grounded in established behavioral theory. Practical strategies to cope with symptoms include cognitive behavioral strategies, dietary guidance, and preparing for accidents [33].

A lack of control is often described by patients with IBD, followed by eventual gain of control and increasing confidence levels once they accomplish tasks such as learning to self-inject medications [32]. In our prior work, we showed that higher self-efficacy or confidence in performing specific IBD self-management tasks was associated with lower perceived daily IBD burden [34]. Similarly, this study highlights the role of maladaptive and balanced thoughts, confidence, and motivation on self-management behaviors. GI psychologists can help patients navigate the emotional and behavioral challenges of living with IBD [35]. More specifically, thought distortions and low self-efficacy can be addressed effectively with GI-specific cognitive behavioral therapy focused on identifying negative thought patterns and replacing them with more balanced thoughts [17]. However, existing IBD self-management interventions tend to focus solely on cognitive behavioral therapy in isolation or on a specific behavioral skill (e.g., medication adherence), limiting their broader impact [19, 21, 36, 37]. Therefore, GI-specific cognitive behavioral theory principles including goal setting, action planning, and feedback on performance could be incorporated into future optimized self-management programs.

Social support improves IBD health through direct aid, encouragement, empathy, and advice [38]. However, not all social support is equally effective. Beyond the need for several support roles, social support should be well-aligned with patients’ perspectives, experiences, and thought processes. A peer-mentoring program alone was not found to be effective in improving self-management behaviors among adolescents with IBD, but improved patients sense of belonging [39]. Patients with IBD identify a need for eliciting social support, but the optimal way to deliver this support is not fully understood. Social support is likely most beneficial in the context of additional self-management resources rather than a stand-alone intervention.

The bidirectional relationship between mental health and IBD is well known [40]. A prior study evaluating IBD adolescent patients’ needs found that emotional distress related to illness was common among participants [41]. A majority of our study participants identified stress, anxiety, and/or depressed mood as major contributors to symptom exacerbations as well as their ability to cope with their disease. Therefore, understanding the role of mental health in IBD and addressing GI-specific stressors, anxiety, and depression is essential to supporting patients in self-management. Many study participants learned to incorporate various strategies for stress management into their daily lives including psychological therapy, mindfulness practices, and breathing exercises. These strategies have been described previously in self-management studies of patients with IBD in remission for residual symptoms [33], but based on our study data, can be extended to a self-management program for patients with active IBD.

In our study, participants of all ages and in both cohorts were open to virtual self-management tools, regardless of their individual preferences, including patients 65 years and older. This is similar to other studies in IBD that have demonstrated IBD patients’ willingness to engage with eHealth technology [12]. Systematic review data has confirmed IBD patients’ acceptance of virtually-delivered care [11]. In fact, many patients with IBD already use virtual technologies to support their health [42]. However, younger patients (≤ 30 years) have reported higher confidence in using eHealth technology [12], suggesting a need for addressing differences in baseline skills and confidence using virtual care modalities in the deployment of IBD self-management tools.

The study’s strengths include enrollment of participants from multiple centers including a national health system to maximize transferability of study findings, purposeful sampling to include perspectives from different age groups, and a behavioral theory-driven qualitative analysis process to inform intervention development. However, these findings may not be representative of all patient experiences, including patients with inactive IBD, given the study inclusion criteria, as well as minority populations or patients with IBD not receiving IBD specific care who are less well represented in this cohort.

In conclusion, self-management for patients with active IBD requires complex behavioral changes, including building several skills in recognizing and managing symptoms, managing diet and treatment, navigating care, and handling uncertainty. Patients can also work to build their confidence, limit maladaptive thinking, and enhance motivation to assist with disease self-management. Further, eliciting social support and addressing stress and mental health concerns are critical to building self-management skills and assisting patients with coping with their disease. Overall IBD self-management programs have shown mixed results and an optimized self-management treatment for IBD has not yet been identified [8, 9, 19]. To support self-management and improve IBD health, we must better understand patients’ needs and preferences and develop patient-centered IBD tailored interventions.
