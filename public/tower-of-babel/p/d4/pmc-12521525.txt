Evaluating the association between TSH and FT4 levels and the incidence of metabolic syndrome: insights from the Tehran thyroid study using joint modeling
Authors: Alireza Amirabadizadeh, Ladan Mehran, Atieh Amouzegar, Samaneh Asgari, Fereidoun Azizi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study aims to investigate the longitudinal association between thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH), free thyroxine (FT4), and the incidence of metabolic syndrome (MetS) using joint modeling techniques over a ten-year follow-up in the Tehran Thyroid Study (TTS). Data from 1436 participants aged 20 years or older in the TTS cohort were analyzed. The study spanned four years, beginning in 1999 and concluding in 2012, with follow-up assessments conducted every three years. Longitudinal changes in logarithm-transformed TSH and FT4 levels were examined as time-varying exposures to evaluate their association with the incidence of metabolic syndrome (MetS). Over a median follow-up of 8.92 years, 574 new MetS cases were identified. Higher TSH levels were positively associated with MetS risk (HR: 1.17, 95%CI: 1.03–1.33), while higher FT4 levels were inversely associated with MetS risk (HR: 0.54, 95%CI: 0.29–0.97). Higher TSH levels also correlated with an increased risk of hypertension, while higher FT4 levels were linked to a reduced risk of abdominal obesity and hypertriglyceridemia. Our study highlights the association between thyroid hormone changes and the development of MetS, utilizing joint modeling of longitudinal and time-to-event data over a ten-year follow-up. The observed shift toward hypothyroidism, characterized by increasing TSH and declining FT4 levels, emphasizes the importance of regular thyroid function monitoring as part of MetS prevention and management strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-19725-w.

Introduction

MetS is a combination of several metabolic abnormalities that collectively increase the risk of developing atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, diabetes mellitus, insulin resistance, vascular and neurological complications, and all-cause mortality1,2. The syndrome significantly affects an individual’s health and healthcare expenses2,3. It is estimated that the incidence of MetS affects 20–30% of the adult population globally, leading to significant health, social, and economic burdens4. In NHANES, the incidence of metabolic syndrome increased from 27.6 to 32.3 in 2013–2014 compared to 1999–20005. The overall incidence of metabolic syndrome in adults aged 20 years and above was reported at 550.9 per 10,000; in men, it was 794.2 per 10,000, and in women, it was reported at 443.5 per 10,0006. The overall incidence rate of MetS in Iran was 97.96 per 10007.

MetS and thyroid dysfunction (TD) share common abnormalities, including hyperglycemia, hypertension, abdominal obesity, reduced high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), and elevated triglycerides (TG). Both conditions are well-established independent risk factors for atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease8,9. Numerous studies have examined the association between TD and metabolic syndrome (MetS) as well as its components. However, the results remain inconsistent and controversial9–12. Moreover, most investigations into the relationship between TD and the components of MetS have predominantly focused on hypothyroidism and subclinical hypothyroidism (SCH). Recent studies have shown that individuals with hypothyroidism or subclinical hypothyroidism are at a higher risk of MetS13,14. There is increasing evidence that thyroid problems impact the body’s lipid and glucose processing, weight, and blood pressure. These effects are closely linked to various metabolic parameters and may contribute to the development or exacerbation of components of metabolic syndrome15.

Although many studies in this field have examined the associations between thyroid disorders and MetS or its components, there is still a lack of longitudinal evidence. The analytical approach in most previous studies has predominantly relied on survival and logistic regression models, which only consider baseline thyroid hormone levels in their association with metabolic syndrome (MetS) and fail to capture the dynamic nature of the relationship between thyroid hormones and MetS over time. This limitation may overlook important temporal changes that contribute to the development or progression of MetS. In contrast, a longitudinal approach that measures thyroid hormone levels alongside the time-to-event occurrence of MetS allows for a more robust and accurate understanding of this association. Moreover, joint modeling (JM) combines longitudinal and survival data to analyze repeated measurements and event occurrence simultaneously, improving statistical efficiency and understanding the temporal association between thyroid dysfunction and MetS.

Studying TD in populations with MetS can provide valuable insights into the extent of overlap between these two conditions. It may also underscore the importance of routinely assessing thyroid function in patients with MetS. Such investigations could facilitate the development of targeted management strategies, ultimately reducing cardiovascular morbidity and mortality associated with the coexistence of MetS and TD. Therefore, the main goal of this study was to examine how changes in serum TSH and FT4 levels over ten years are associated with the incidence of MetS. The examination was performed using JM of longitudinal and survival data.

Method

Study population

The current TTS longitudinal cohort study is being carried out as part of the Tehran Lipid and Glucose Study (TLGS). The first phase was a cross-sectional prevalence study of non-communicable diseases and their risk factors (1999–2001), which involved 5783 individuals aged 20 years and older, and ongoing prospective follow-up examinations conducted every 3 years. Details of the rationale, sampling, and data collection of the TTS have been reported elsewhere16,17. This study used data from the second (2000–2005), third (2005–2009), and fourth (2009–2012) phase of the TTS for a longitudinal analysis.

771 individuals on thyroid medications, 327 individuals taking corticosteroids, 30 individuals with a history of radioiodine treatment, patients with MetS (n = 1767), pregnant women (n = 155), thyroid surgery (n = 140), individuals with cancer history (n = 58), and eGFR < 30 mL/min/1.73m2 (n = 3) were excluded. After further excluding those with incomplete data on baseline body mass index (BMI), triglycerides (TG), systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), fasting plasma glucose (FPG), TG/HDL, HOMA-IR data (n = 547), as well as missing data on four measurements of serum FT4 and TSH levels (n = 549),1436 individuals (61.6% women) were included in the study (Fig. 1).

Clinical and laboratory measurements

Once participants in the TTS provided their informed consent, they completed a questionnaire administered by trained interviewers. BMI is determined by dividing weight(kg) by height (m2). The waist circumference was assessed in centimeters at the navel level18. Blood pressure was recorded using a typical mercury sphygmomanometer on the right arm of seated individuals following 5 min of relaxation. SBP and DBP were determined by taking the average of two readings 15 min apart.A blood sample was collected after an overnight fasting period of 12 to 14 h. All blood tests were conducted on the same day at the TTS research laboratory. Serum lipid and Plasma glucose levels were measured using previously described methods17.TSH and FT4 levels were evaluated in serum samples frozen at − 70 °C using Roche Diagnostics kits and a Roche/Hitachi Cobas e-411 analyzer (Mannheim, Germany) with the electrochemiluminescence immunoassay technique. Lyphochek Immunoassay Plus Control, a lyophilized quality control material from Bio-Rad Laboratories in Hercules, CA, was used to confirm the accuracy of the assay. The CVs for FT4 were 1.3% intra-assay and 3.7% inter-assay, while for TSH they were 1.5% and 4.5%, respectively. Thyroid peroxidase antibodies (TPOAb) were assessed with an immunoenzymometric assay kit (IEMA; Monobind, Costa Mesa, CA) and examined with the Sunrise ELISA reader (Tecan Co., Salzburg, Austria). The intra-assay coefficient of variation for TPOAb was 3.9%, while the inter-assay coefficient of variation was 4.7%. All measurements were taken simultaneously in the research laboratory of RIES.

Definitions

Educational levels were divided into three categories: under 6 years (no education/elementary school), 6–12 years (diploma/less than a diploma), and over 12 years of education (higher than a diploma). A current smoker is someone who smokes cigarettes either daily or sometimes. Euthyroidism was defined as having a TSH level between 0.32 and 5.06 mU/L and fT4 between 0.91 and 1.55 ng/dL. HOMA-IR was determined by multiplying FPG (mg/dl) by fasting serum insulin (µIU/mL) and dividing by 405.The Modifiable Activity Questionnaire19 was used to measure the physical activity level (PAL), with a score of ≥ 600 metabolic equivalent of task (MET) minutes per week indicating a high PAL20. MetS was determined based on the Joint Interim Statement (JIS) criteria adjusted for the Iranian population21. Diagnosis necessitated at least 3 of the following 5 criteria: (i) Waist circumference (WC) > = 95 cm in both genders; (ii) Serum TG ≥ 150 mg/dL or receiving specific treatment; (iii) Low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) < 40 mg/dL in males and < 50 mg/dL in females, or receiving specific treatment; (iv) SBP ≥ 130 mm Hg and/or DBP ≥ 85 mm Hg, or receiving treatment for previously diagnosed hypertension; (v) FPG ≥ 100 mg/dL or receiving diabetes treatment.The MetS incidence rate was determined by dividing the quantity of new MetS cases by the person-years at risk for the entire study population. Survival time was determined as the duration from the start of the study to the first instance of MetS. Euthyroidism was defined as a FT4 level between 0.91 and 1.55 ng/dL and a TSH within the normal range of 0.32 to 5.06 mIU/L. Subclinical hypothyroidism is characterized by TSH levels exceeding 5.06, while FT4 levels range from 0.91 to 1.55 ng/dl. Overt hypothyroidism was defined by TSH levels exceeding 5.06 and FT4 levels falling below 0.91. Subclinical hyperthyroidism was recognized with TSH levels under 0.32 and FT4 levels ranging from 0.91 to 1.55, while overt hyperthyroidism was characterized by TSH levels below 0.32 and FT4 levels exceeding 1.5522,23.

Statistics analysis

The study population’s basic characteristics were described by providing the mean (SD) for continuous variables and the number (%) for categorical variables. The median (IQR: interquartile range) was presented for covariates with a non-symmetric distribution.

We used a joint modeling approach to assess the relationship between FT4 and TSH levels over time and the development of MetS. Three distinct mixed-effects models were applied for the longitudinal analysis during model selection. The optimal model for our analysis was selected based on the model with the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) value closest to zero, as shown in Supplementary Table 1. Cubic spline-based plots were generated to illustrate the longitudinal trajectories of FT4 and logTSH over time, demonstrating the overall trend while accounting for individual variability (Fig. S1).

We used a linear mixed-effects model to evaluate the mean trends of FT4 and TSH levels after logarithmic transformation. Adjustments were made for gender, TPOAb (IU/ml), and current smoking status in the longitudinal part of the joint model.

In the survival component of the model, Cox regression was utilized. Two models were examined: Model 1 included baseline measurements of age, gender, education, and PAL. The occurrence date of the MetS incident was determined as the middle point between the date of the follow-up appointment where MetS was first identified and the date of the most recent prior follow-up appointment before the diagnosis.

The parameter α quantifies the association between pre-selected features of the longitudinal process (TSH or FT4) and the hazard for incident MetS.

To determine the best joint model for our data, we used the deviance information criterion (DIC) to select the model with the lowest DIC value from Supplementary Table 2.

In this research, we calculated all parameters of the joint model using MCMC methods with Gibbs sampling in a Bayesian framework. The JMbayes2 package in R version 4.3.0 software was utilized for the analysis.

Multiple imputation was conducted to handle missing, unrecorded, or incomplete data across study phases. The mice and Jomo packages in R 4.3.0 were utilized to generate ten imputed datasets, ensuring greater consistency and reducing potential bias associated with missingness. A sensitivity analysis was performed using the imputed datasets to assess the robustness of the results.

Results

In this study, 1436 individuals (61.6% women) with a mean age of 39.08 ± 12.98 years were enrolled. A total of 574 new cases of MetS were identified, with 307 cases occurring in women. The median follow-up period was 8.92 years (IQR: 4.87–9.80 years). The overall incidence rate of MetS was 536 cases per 10,000 person-years (95% CI: 494–582). When stratified by gender, the incidence rate of MetS was 691 cases per 10,000 person-years in men (95% CI: 613–780) and 448 cases per 10,000 person-years in women (95% CI: 401–502). Table 1 presents the baseline characteristics of the study participants. Among the participants, 11.9% had abdominal obesity, 25.8% had hypertriglyceridemia, 14.5% had hypertension, 7.7% had hyperglycemia, and 60.2% had low HDL levels. The mean FT4 level was 1.22 ± 0.25 ng/dL, and the median TSH level was 1.52 (0.95–2.46) mU/L.

Figure 2 illustrates the longitudinal trajectories of mean logTSH and FT4 levels over time, stratified by MetS event status. Individuals who developed MetS had consistently higher logTSH levels, especially in later follow-up years. In contrast, FT4 levels were generally higher in those without MetS.Table 2 demonstrates that a higher serum logarithmic TSH level is strongly associated with a significant increase in the incidence of MetS. The joint model shows that each unit increase in serum logarithmic TSH levels corresponds to a 28% higher risk of incident MetS (HR: 1.28, 95% CI: 1.09–1.58, p = 0.008) in Model 1 (adjusted for gender, age, education, and physical activity), and a 17% higher risk in Model 3 (HR: 1.17, 95% CI: 1.01–1.28, p = 0.01) after further adjustment for BMI and HOMA-IR. In Model 4, which includes further adjustment of Model 2 for FPG, SBP, DBP, TG/HDL, and the use of anti-hyperglycemic, lipid-lowering, and antihypertensive medications, a one-unit increase in the log-transformed serum TSH level was associated with an 18% higher risk of incident MetS (HR: 1.18; 95% CI: 1.05–1.38; p = 0.03).

Figure 3 further examines the association between serum TSH levels and individual components of metabolic syndrome. In Model 2, which includes additional adjustment for BMI beyond Model 1, the joint model results indicate that each one-unit increase in the log-transformed TSH level is associated with an 8% higher risk of developing hypertension (HR: 1.08; 95% CI: 1.02–1.22; p = 0.03). This association remains significant in Model 4, where further adjustment is made for FPG, SBP, DBP, TG/HDL ratio, and the use of anti-hyperglycemic, lipid-lowering, and antihypertensive medications, with each unit increase in log-TSH associated with a 10% increased risk of hypertension (HR: 1.10; 95% CI: 1.03–1.25; p = 0.04).

Discussion

In the population-based prospective TTS, we employed joint modeling to evaluate the association between longitudinal changes in serum TSH and FT4 levels with the risk of developing MetS and its components. No study has utilized joint modeling to investigate this association, making our approach novel. Our findings revealed that each unit increase in serum logarithmic TSH level is associated with a 17% increase in the incidence of MetS. Conversely, each unit increase in FT4 level reduced the risk of MetS incidence by 46%. Additionally, an increase in serum TSH level was associated with a 9% reduction in the risk of hyperglycemia and an 8% increase in the risk of hypertension. Conversely, an increase in serum FT4 level decreased the risk of hypertriglyceridemia by 14% and the risk of abdominal obesity by 42%.

Many research projects have looked into how thyroid hormones are related to MetS, but most of them are either cross-sectional or case-control studies and the results are Contradictory10,11,24,25. In various studies, the association between thyroid function and MetS has been mixed results. Shin et al.11 reported no association between TSH and FT4 levels and the onset of metabolic syndrome in either sex. Conversely, in a study by Huang et al. involving euthyroid individuals, it was found that men with elevated FT4 levels had a 26% lower risk of MetS, while women with higher FT4 levels had a 39% lower risk compared to those with lower FT4 levels. However, no association was observed between TSH levels and MetS. In another study by Gu et al.26, higher serum TSH and FT4 levels were associated with a 12% increased risk and a 1% decreased risk of MetS in euthyroid individuals, respectively. On the other hand, Kim et al.10 found no such association between TSH levels and the occurrence of MetS.

The variation in results between our study and the previous research could be attributed to several factors. Most earlier studies utilized linear or logistic regression models, which may not fully capture the dynamic relationship between TSH, FT4, and the development of metabolic syndrome over time. Additionally, many of these studies focused on populations with predominantly euthyroid individuals, which may limit the generalizability of their findings to broader or more diverse populations. Applying a joint modeling approach allows for the simultaneous assessment of longitudinal changes in thyroid hormone levels and their association with the incidence of MetS. This methodology provides a more comprehensive understanding of the evolving association between thyroid function and MetS.

In the current study, higher logarithmic TSH levels were associated with an 8% increase in the incidence of hypertension and a 9% reduction in hyperglycemia. Additionally, elevated FT4 levels were linked to a 14% decrease in hypertriglyceridemia and a 42% reduction in abdominal obesity. These findings align partially with Shin et al.‘s11 study, which reported that higher TSH levels significantly increased the risk of hypertension (OR = 1.28, 95% CI: 1.11–1.47) and hypertriglyceridemia (OR = 1.25, 95% CI: 1.12–1.40). In contrast, higher FT4 levels were associated with increased abdominal obesity (OR = 1.24, 95% CI: 1.04–1.47) but reduced hypertriglyceridemia risk (OR = 0.69, 95% CI: 0.62–0.77). Similarly, Huang et al.25 observed that in Taiwanese euthyroid subjects, higher FT4 levels reduced the risk of abdominal obesity (OR = 0.73, 95% CI: 0.64–0.84). However, Jang et al.24 reported no significant association between TSH levels and metabolic syndrome components, while higher FT4 levels were positively correlated with fasting glucose and inversely associated with triglycerides.

The diverse physiological effects of thyroid hormones on glucose and lipid metabolism, body fat distribution, and blood pressure regulation provide insights into their potential role in influencing the development of MetS and its components12,27. In our study, we observed that an increase in serum TSH levels was associated with an elevated risk of hypertension and a reduced risk of hyperglycemia, while higher FT4 levels were linked to a decreased risk of hypertriglyceridemia and abdominal obesity. These findings are consistent with the multifaceted roles of thyroid hormones in metabolic regulation.

Thyroid hormones play a critical role in glucose homeostasis. Hyperglycemia associated with hypothyroidism may be attributed to reduced glucose absorption, whereas in hyperthyroidism, increased hepatic glucose production predominates28,29. Our finding of a 9% reduction in hyperglycemia risk with higher TSH levels could reflect adaptive mechanisms in glucose metabolism, potentially mediated by altered insulin secretion and degradation regulated by thyroid hormones. Similarly, the observed reduction in abdominal obesity and hypertriglyceridemia with increased FT4 levels aligns with the role of thyroid hormones in regulating metabolic rate, appetite, and sympathetic nervous system activity30,31. FT4’s influence on lipid metabolism, through both synthesis and degradation, could explain the 14% reduction in hypertriglyceridemia risk in our study32. Blood pressure regulation by thyroid hormones, particularly through genomic pathways affecting vascular tone and non-genomic effects on cardiac function, may underlie the 8% increased risk of hypertension associated with higher TSH levels in our findings33. This highlights the complex interplay between thyroid function and cardiovascular regulation.

Our sex-stratified analyses revealed important differences in the association between thyroid hormone levels and the risk of metabolic syndrome. In women, higher TSH levels were consistently associated with an increased risk of metabolic syndrome across all models, whereas this association did not reach statistical significance in men. Conversely, higher FT4 levels were significantly associated with increased risk of metabolic syndrome in both sexes, although the magnitude of the effect was more pronounced in women. These findings suggest potential sex-specific biological pathways linking thyroid function to metabolic syndrome, underscoring the importance of considering sex as an effect modifier in endocrine-metabolic research. Our findings align with those of Zhou et al.,34 who reported a significant association between elevated TSH levels and increased risk of metabolic syndrome in women, with a 2.7-fold (HR: 2.70, 95%CI: 1.34–5.43, p = 0.005) higher risk. In contrast, while a 12% (HR: 1.12, 95%CI: 0.93–1.36, p = 0.23) increased risk was observed in men, the association was not statistically significant. Consistent with our findings, a population-based study from the Netherlands (LifeLines Cohort) demonstrated a sex-specific association between FT4 levels and metabolic syndrome. In that study, lower FT4 levels were significantly associated with a higher prevalence of metabolic syndrome and its components in both men and women35. The observed sex-specific associations between thyroid hormones and metabolic syndrome may be explained by biological and hormonal differences between men and women. Estrogen plays a critical role in modulating thyroid function, partly through its effect on increasing levels of thyroxine-binding globulin (TBG), which alters the bioavailability of thyroid hormones in circulation. This modulation may lead to differential feedback responses in the hypothalamic-pituitary-thyroid (HPT) axis, particularly in women36.

Moreover, sex differences in HPT axis regulation and tissue-specific thyroid hormone sensitivity may contribute to these disparities. Studies have shown that women with metabolic syndrome often exhibit higher TSH levels than men with the same condition, indicating possible sex-related differences in endocrine-metabolic feedback mechanisms37.It is also important to note that the lack of statistically significant associations observed in men in our study may be partially due to a smaller sample size in the male subgroup, which likely reduced statistical power and the ability to detect meaningful effects. This limitation should be considered when interpreting sex-stratified results.

The present research highlights multiple advantages, including a population-based cohort design with a long follow-up duration, suitable exclusion criteria, and the consideration of multiple confounding variables in the analysis. We focused on studying how thyroid hormone level changes over time would affect the occurrence of metabolic syndrome and its components. Our collaborative model reduced standard errors, showcasing improved efficiency versus conventional Cox models. By using joint models, we can achieve more precise and impartial estimates of the association between longitudinal variables and events as time passes, reducing any bias in the forecast. The study was limited by the absence of data on levels of FT3 or T3, as well as the FT4/FT3 ratio. Another limitation of our study is the lack of data on certain potentially important confounders, such as dietary patterns, family history of metabolic disorders, and inflammatory markers (e.g., CRP), which were not available in our dataset.

Conclusion

Our study is the first to investigate the dynamic association between serum thyroid hormone levels and the development of MetS and its components utilizing JM that incorporates longitudinal and time-to-event data. Through examining the complete longitudinal patterns of thyroid hormones during a ten-year follow-up, we observed a tendency towards hypothyroidism, marked by upward TSH levels and decreasing FT4 levels, which may play a role in the progression of MetS. This underscores the importance of thyroid function monitoring in MetS prevention and management strategies. Regular assessment of serum thyroid hormone trajectories and their potential long-term impacts could provide a valuable strategy in clinical practice, enabling healthcare providers to adopt a more personalized approach to treatment and optimize thyroid hormone levels based on individual patient needs.
