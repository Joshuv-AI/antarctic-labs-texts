Comments on “Effectiveness of Binaural Beats in Reducing Anxiety in Alcohol Withdrawal Patients: A Single-group Pre-post Interventional Study”
Authors: Omar Afroz, Raja Babu Ramawat
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

We read with interest the study “Effectiveness of Binaural Beats in Reducing Anxiety in Alcohol Withdrawal Patients: A Single-group Pre-post Interventional Study” by Prithiviraj et al. 1 The study found that binaural beats lead to a significant reduction in anxiety among patients with severe alcohol withdrawal. This is a novel use of binaural beats, as most previous studies have used this intervention in the context of procedural anxiety. It is particularly relevant in the Indian context, which has a significant burden of alcohol use disorders, with 5.7 crore problem users and 2.9 crore dependent users. 2 However, certain points need discussion.

Discussion

The choice of alcohol withdrawal patients as the study sample is interesting, since alcohol withdrawal is a dynamic state marked by frequent fluctuations in symptoms, including anxiety. 3 Guidelines suggest monitoring of moderate-to-severe alcohol withdrawal patients every 1–4 hours. 4 The symptom severity and fluctuations vary based on factors such as time since last alcohol intake, and the response to interventions is generally measured over short time intervals. As studies on the natural course of alcohol withdrawal are relatively few, possibly due to ethical concerns, there is limited data on short-term (minutes to hours) changes in symptoms. This has implications for assessing response to intervention, as some changes may be attributed to the dynamic nature of withdrawal symptoms.

In the current study, the mean change in Hamilton Anxiety Rating Scale (HAM-A) was 1.31 ± 1.32 in patients with severe withdrawal. It is a point of discussion as to how much of this change may be attributable to the natural course of alcohol withdrawal. Furthermore, anxiety reduction was found only in patients with severe alcohol withdrawal; that is, patients with higher baseline anxiety improved more. This may reflect regression to the mean. Moreover, regarding the use of HAM-A in the current study, there is a paucity of validation studies on this instrument in alcohol withdrawal, as most studies have used it to assess comorbid anxiety in alcohol dependence. 5 The retest reliability of HAM-A has been tested over longer durations, such as 1 week. 6 This needs to be considered while using it to assess changes in anxiety over minutes, as in the current study. The application of HAM-A usually takes around 12–15 minutes, 6 which might affect the measurement of changes in anxiety over a 10-minute interval. Most previous studies used measures such as the State-Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI) and the Visual Analog Scale (VAS), as well as physiological indicators of anxiety, such as heart rate, blood pressure, and electroencephalography (EEG). 7 We understand that EEG may not be feasible in a large sample, particularly among patients with alcohol withdrawal. Measurement of indicators such as blood pressure and heart rate could have substantiated the findings from a physiological perspective.

It is also important to consider that factors such as the amount of alcohol use, duration of use, and duration since the last alcohol use affect the severity and timeline of alcohol withdrawal symptoms. For instance, two patients at different time durations since last alcohol intake: one on the third day since last use, and one on the fifth day since last use, might have different trajectories and dynamic changes of alcohol withdrawal symptoms. This might have implications for confounding. Another point of discussion is the lack of laboratory data to rule out the use of other substances, particularly benzodiazepines. It has been highlighted previously that patients underreport use of substances such as pheniramine and benzodiazepines, and urine drug screens may be utilized in such cases. 8 This could help in controlling the confounding effect of benzodiazepines.

The authors mention the use of simple random sampling. However, all patients who sought treatment for alcohol withdrawal during a set duration, both outpatient and inpatient, were recruited, which, in our opinion, appears closer to convenience/purposive sampling. This may impact the generalizability of the findings. Further, the sample size calculation was based on the prevalence of alcohol use in Puducherry. We believe that to better align it with the study aim, it could have been based on the expected effect of the intervention (binaural beats) on anxiety. 7 There might be a potential mismatch between the aim and objectives, as the first objective, “factors impacting the change in anxiety levels,” goes beyond the aim.

Conclusions

We appreciate the authors for generating preliminary evidence on a novel non-pharmacological intervention in a resource-limited setting. The use of tools such as the Clinical Institute Withdrawal Assessment for Alcohol-Revised (CIWA-Ar) and environmental control ensures standardization. The protocol for using binaural beats is detailed and replicable. The study may pave the way for future controlled trials from India.
