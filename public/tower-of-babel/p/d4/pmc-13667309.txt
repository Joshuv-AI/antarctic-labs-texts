Physiology‐Guided Drug‐Coated Balloon Angioplasty: What We Know and What is Yet to be Understood
Authors: Marco Frazzetto, Filippo Luca Gurgoglione, Bharat Khialani, Sandeep Basavarajaiah, Bernardo Cortese
Published: 2026

Physiology‐Guided Angioplasty in Contemporary Era

The advent of coronary physiology has significantly reshaped the approach to percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI), establishing its role as an essential tool for step‐by‐step guidance during revascularizations with drug‐eluting stents (DES). Coronary physiology was introduced as a pre‐PCI decision‐making tool to guide revascularization of intermediate coronary artery stenoses, demonstrating better clinical outcomes compared to angiography‐guided PCI. In recent years, postprocedural coronary physiology has proven to be a valuable tool for optimizing DES‐PCI results and for reducing the risk of future cardiovascular events.

Recently, the latest European practical guidelines on chronic coronary syndromes have endorsed the use of coronary physiology tools (i.e., fractional flow reserve [FFR], instantaneous wave‐free ratio [iFR], and quantitative flow ratio [QFR]) at the end of the procedure to identify patients at high risk of persistent angina and subsequent clinical events based on pioneering studies.

The rapid spread of drug‐coated balloons (DCB) as a strategy for the treatment of coronary artery disease (CAD) is evolving either with technological advancements or procedural strategies [1]. To date, quantitative coronary angiography (QCA) is commonly used to assess the quality of lesion preparation and to determine the success of DCB angioplasty, which is indicated by residual stenosis ≤ 40%, Thrombolysis in Myocardial Infarction (TIMI) flow grade 3, and the absence of flow‐limiting dissections. However, QCA may be inaccurate in evaluating the hemodynamic significance of residual stenosis, particularly in the presence of vessel dissection, and in assessing the risk of abrupt vessel closure associated with the latter. Hence, the concept of a “physiology‐guided DCB angioplasty” to overcome the limitation of QCA and improve the outcomes of DCB angioplasty seems attractive.

It is noteworthy to underscore that the implementation of coronary physiology after balloon angioplasty in the attempt to predict short‐ and long‐term events is not a novel framework. Indeed, as early as 1987, Redd and colleagues [2] suggested that a rising trend pattern identified by an increasing pressure gradient after deflation of the balloon was associated with an increased rate of acute vessel closure and myocardial infarction immediately after the procedure.

Several studies have investigated the potential application of different coronary physiology tools for guiding decision‐making in PCI with DCB and the final optimization of DCB angioplasty. It is worth taking a closer look at how these results can be integrated into current recommendations and what implications they might have for future treatment decisions.

FFR and DCB

Back in 1999, Bech et al. [3] suggested that an FFR > 0.90 after simple angioplasty was a good indicator of immediate functional improvement and reduced restenosis at 2 years follow‐up.

In the DCB era, various FFR cut‐off values measured after adequate lesion preparation, especially in larger vessels, have been reported to guide the final therapeutic decision, as well as to predict angiographic and clinical outcomes [4, 5, 6, 7] (Table 1). An FFR value ≥ 0.75 emerged as a cut‐off for identifying lesions suitable for DCB treatment, ensuring comparable clinical outcomes with DES, with adequate anatomical and physiological patency during follow‐up re‐assessment [7]. Moreover, FFR‐guided DCB angioplasty has proven to be both safe, without evidence of acute vessel occlusion and elastic recoil, and effective, demonstrating favorable vessel remodeling throughout the follow‐up period [8]. Of note, the rate of bailout stenting was low, ranging from 0% to 6%. Chung et al. [7] demonstrated a considerable rate of discordance between angiographic and physiological assessment to define the lesion severity after adequate lesion preparation. The authors showed a mismatch rate (i.e., residual DS > 30% and FFR ≥ 0.75) in 68.7% of the cases, with a reverse mismatch (residual DS ≤ 30% and FFR < 0.75) in 7.1%. Interestingly, if the mismatch lesions were treated exclusively using the angiographic criteria to define device success recommended by the DCB‐ARC (i.e., residual DS < 40% reported by QCA), all of them would have received a stent, even though the FFR was > 0.75 [1]. Hence, the use of FFR safely reduced the number of unnecessary DES in this mismatch population by an overall rate of 66.7% [7]. In the reverse mismatch group, angiographic criteria would favor DCB treatment (residual DS < 40%), yet these lesions showed an FFR < 0.75, suggesting hemodynamic significance. It is well established that, in patients with functionally significant coronary stenoses, FFR‐guided PCI reduces the need for urgent revascularization compared to medical therapy alone [14]. Therefore, such cases would more appropriately benefit from DES implantation to reduce the risk of future adverse cardiovascular events [7]. It is also crucial to underscore that in the left anterior descending artery (LAD), the majority of lesions with angiographically severe dissection had an FFR ≥ 0.75, suggesting that angiographic judgment of dissection severity has no corresponding hemodynamic impact.

Resting Physiological Parameters and DCB

iFR and Pd/Pa are resting physiological indexes that do not require adenosine‐induced maximal hyperemia to test the severity of coronary lesions.

Measurement of iFR immediately after lesion preparation demonstrated to be a valid alternative to FFR for addressing the quality of lesion preparation during PCI of de novo coronary lesions [10]. Chung et al. tested a revascularization strategy guided by an iFR threshold ≥ 0.86 to identify functionally non‐significant residual lesions and to guide treatment with DCB. This approach demonstrated to be safe and effective with improved patency after 9 months and excellent 3‐year clinical outcome [10].

Another study reported that iFR value, assessed 0 and 15 min after DCB angioplasty, was significantly lower in the bailout stenting group, and higher iFR 15 min after PCI was associated with positive late lumen enlargement (LLE) at OCT [11]. Finally, the most accurate model to predict LLE after DCB angioplasty included percentage area stenosis on OCT, iFR gradient, and difference between measurements, underscoring the importance of combining physiological and imaging tools for confirming an optimal and durable DCB angioplasty result.

A study involving 106 de novo coronary lesions tested the use of resting Pd/Pa, either after lesion preparation, to guide the choice between DES and DCB treatment and following DCB angioplasty, to identify segments requiring further balloon dilation or targeted DES implantation [13]. A Pd/Pa ≥ 0.90 was selected as a surrogate of optimal result in both PCI phases [14]. DES implantation was also performed in the presence of coronary dissections > type B according to the National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute, regardless of functional assessment. As a result, 72.5% of the lesions were treated with DCB alone, 10.1% with final DES implantation covering the treated segment. Notably, a hybrid approach was implemented in 12.8% of the cases, whereby a short proximal DES was deployed in vessels with a diameter ≥ 3.0 mm in response to angiographic deterioration observed within the treated area. Dissections were observed in 70.6% of lesions, and at a median follow‐up of 246 days, no cases of acute vessel occlusion were reported. Target lesion failure and revascularization occurred in 13.2% and 8.7% of the cases, respectively. This physiologically guided approach showed safety in terms of acute vessel closure, favorable healing of coronary dissections after DCB angioplasty, and effective minimization of DES length.

Angiography‐Derived Physiological Parameters and DCB

The most validated angiography‐derived physiological index is the QFR, which estimates FFR from invasive coronary angiography without the need for a pressure wire or pharmacologically induced hyperemia [15]. More recently, Murray's law‐based QFR (μQFR) has been developed to address limitations associated with linear vessel tapering, particularly in the context of bifurcation lesions. By incorporating fractal geometry principles, μQFR has demonstrated promising results and may offer improved accuracy [16].

Several retrospective studies have investigated the optimal cut‐off value of QFR, measured immediately after DCB angioplasty (DCB‐QFR), for predicting clinical events either in de novo CAD or ISR lesions (Table 2). Three studies proposed different QFR cut‐offs (≥ 0.90, 0.92, or 0.94) to predict the occurrence of cardiovascular events after DCB angioplasty in patients with ISR. The analysis of the Restore ISR China trial, classified into recurrent and non‐recurrent ISR, demonstrated that a DCB‐QFR ≥ 0.90 was an independent risk factor for recurrent ISR [17]. In addition, compared to the non‐recurrent ISR group, the recurrent ISR group exhibited lower initial and suboptimal final QFR values [20, 23]. The other two studies demonstrated that post‐DCB QFR was strongly associated with adverse outcomes, serving as an excellent predictor of 1‐year composite events and 3‐year target vessel revascularization (HRs: 6.53 and 5.15, respectively) (Table 2).

The safety and efficacy of DCB‐QFR were also assessed in de novo CAD. A DCB‐QFR ≤ 0.90 demonstrated better performance compared to angiographic DS% post‐DCB dilatation, in predicting target vessel DS ≥ 50% at mid‐term follow‐up [19]. In addition, in a post‐hoc analysis from a prospective, randomized trial focused on small vessel disease, Lin et al. [24] identified a post‐DCB QFR ≤ 0.86 (HR: 3.6) and a ΔQFR ≤ 0.57 (defined as post‐PCI QFR –pre‐PCI QFR, HR: 4.7) as significant predictors of major adverse cardiovascular events (Table 1). Finally, an analysis of the RESTORE SVD China trial reported that both 9‐month QFR and ΔQFR were correlated with angiographic %DS, late lumen loss, and were also predictive of 2‐year clinical outcomes [18] (Table 2).

The recently published PICCOLETO VI study [27], which included paclitaxel and sirolimus‐coated balloon for patients with SVD, identified a μFR ≤ 0.86 as a reliable cut‐off for predicting follow‐up ischemia, with an accuracy of 80%. Finally, a sub‐analysis of the PICCOLETO II randomized trial demonstrated that post‐PCI, the DCB group had a lower acute μFR gain compared to DES, whilst at 6‐month follow‐up, μQFR values were similar between groups with a comparable late functional loss [26] (Table 2).

Physiology‐Based Decisional Algorithm for DCB Angioplasty

The possibility of guiding DCB angioplasty with physiological parameters is intriguing and could overcome some inherent limitations of solo‐angiographic parameters, however this field is still lacking robust evidence‐based data. Therefore, we propose a decision‐making algorithm grounded in physiological cut‐off values derived from various methodologies and applied across different CAD settings (Figure 1). In the context of de novo CAD, DCB treatment should be pursued only after adequate lesion preparation, with post‐preparation physiological indices meeting at least one of the following thresholds: FFR ≥ 0.86, iFR ≥ 0.90, or Pd/Pa ≥ 0.90, in the absence of flow‐limiting dissections and with residual DS ≤ 40%, according to the DCB ARC paper [1].

After DCB deployment, there is more paucity of data, limited on angiography‐derived physiological parameters. Available evidence suggests that target vessel QFR ≥ 0.90 or μQFR ≥ 0.86 may define procedural success. If these physiological targets are not met, bail‐out stenting should be advised.

For patients with ISR, where more robust evidence is available, QFR is the most used physiological tool, with a threshold of ≥0.90 indicating an optimal physiological result. The cut‐off values for other physiological indices and the criteria for bail‐out stenting remain consistent with those applied in de novo CAD.

Gaps in Research

Physiological‐guided angioplasty represents a promising and relatively novel area within the field of DCB, warranting more research to further elucidate its clinical implications and optimize its application. First, it is expected a final validation of the physiological cut‐off values from randomized, powered clinical data. Second, the role of physiology‐guided DCB angioplasty in patients with acute coronary syndrome has not been investigated, and whether different cut‐off values apply in this setting is not known. Third, it is unclear whether focal or diffuse disease may have different cut‐off values to predict future clinical events, thus leading to actual DCB‐treatment (does the “from healthy‐to‐healty” axiom valid for stents apply to DCB as well?). Fourth, dedicated studies on physiology‐guided DCB angioplasty for chronic total occlusions are awaited to clarify the role of the physiological assessment in this specific setting. Fifth, ad hoc investigations are needed to assess the functional relevance of non‐flow‐limiting type C–E dissections: unlike QCA, physiological indices are able to quantify flow resistance and energy loss of fluid even in lesions with dissections, potentially identifying cases in which bailout stenting may be unnecessary despite angiographic appearance. Sixth, a physiology‐guided approach may be employed to identify segments requiring further balloon dilation or targeted DES implantation after lesion preparation, and potentially guide a blended DCB‐DES strategy. Seventh, it remains unknown whether different types of DCB (i.e., limus vs. paclitaxel) may require distinct physiological cut‐off values, as the majority of current evidence is derived from studies involving paclitaxel‐coated balloons. Eight, the impact of alternative measurements, such as delta FFR/QFR and percent increase of FFR/QFR should be further clarified. Finally, it is still unclear if and when to integrate intravascular imaging and physiology to guide DCB‐angioplasty, and when should these tools be implemented to add informations to angiographic parameters. Emerging technologies, such as IVUS‐FFR and OCT‐FFR fusion, may represent a paradigm shift with the potential to significantly enhance procedural and clinical outcomes for DCB angioplasty.
