Should we consider ankle fractures as osteoporotic fractures?
Authors: Steffi S. I. Falk, Thomas Mittlmeier
Published: 2025

Introduction

Ankle fractures are a frequent occurrence in the daily practice of orthopaedic surgeons and represent one of the most common lower limb injuries in adults, particularly in the elderly. The Weber classification, which categorises fibular fractures based on fracture height in relation to the syndesmosis, is one of the most commonly used classifications in the literature. Also widely used are the AO classification and the Lauge and Hansen classification. Except for Weber type A fractures and non-displaced type B fractures with an intact syndesmosis, ankle fractures are indicated for surgery to restore ankle stability and anatomical alignment. A wide variety of techniques are described in textbooks, ranging from plates and nails for the fibula, to screws and wire traction tape for the medial malleolus, to plates and screws for the distal dorsal tibia. Notably, these sources rarely address the importance of diagnosing concomitant metabolic bone disease.

Most ankle fractures result from supination trauma. This mechanism is observed across all age groups, which makes it difficult to determine whether the trauma alone is sufficient to explain the fracture. Unlike distal radius fractures, which show a distinct age-related incidence peak, ankle fractures do not demonstrate such a pattern. This complicates the differentiation between fractures associated with osteoporosis and those unrelated to bone fragility. Consequently, the question arises of how to identify patients with compromised bone quality outside the operating theatre. In terms of patient quality of life, treatment should therefore not only focus on anatomical reduction and stable fixation, but also on the detection of concomitant metabolic bone disorders. The most frequent conditions in this context are vitamin D deficiency and osteoporosis.

It is not uncommon for patients with osteoporosis to be first identified by so-called indicator fractures. The DVO (German Osteological Association) guideline published in 2017 mainly mentions vertebral body fractures [1]. However, fractures of the large long bones following low-energy trauma should also be considered in this context, according to the DVO guideline. Specifically, these are fractures of the proximal femur, proximal humerus, and distal radius. However, under non-vertebral fractures, the guideline recognises exceptions such as fractures of the toes or the skull, and also specifically mentions fractures of the ankle.

Knowing that ankle fractures are the most common fractures of the lower extremity, the question arises as to how high the proportion of osteoporosis-related fractures is in this patient group. In the study, patients with ankle fractures were screened for osteoporosis to see if this would be a good approach for osteoporosis case finding.

Methods

All patients over the age of 45 who underwent surgical treatment for an ankle fracture during a 24-month period were consecutively screened and included in this study. All patients gave informed consent. Patients with pathological fractures, high-energy trauma, or those with severe renal insufficiency were excluded. Renal osteoporosis has an additional negative effect on bone metabolism. The participants were asked about their risk factors for osteoporosis [1] and the Fracture Risk Assessment Tool® (FRAX) [2] was used to estimate an indication for therapy.

The patients underwent an osteoporosis assessment and were questioned regarding any screening examinations (such as laboratory analyses and/or DXA scans) they had prior to the fracture. Osteoporosis assessment was in accordance with the DVO (Dachverband Osteologie, German Osteology Society) guidelines for osteoporosis diagnostic testing [1], which were valid at the time. This included a risk factor questionnaire and baseline laboratory.

The information from the questionnaire was used to determine whether there was an indication for prior diagnostic testing for osteoporosis according to the DVO guideline, independent of vitamin D intake. Patients were also asked about previous laboratory tests and DXA scans, and about medications and supplements they took. It was also recorded whether, based on the risk factors assessed, the patient had an indication for osteoporosis diagnostic testing according to the DVO guideline, independent of the current fracture. The term ‘diagnostic gap’ was defined as the proportion of patients who, according to the DVO guideline, had an indication for osteoporosis diagnosis but had not yet undergone such a diagnosis.

The assessment was completed by calculating the FRAX® value. FRAX calculates the risk of experiencing a fracture in the next 10 years for patients ranging from 40 to 90 years of age. If the patients are older than 90 years, their age is automatically set to 90 for the calculation. The result includes two numbers: the 10-year risk of a proximal femur fracture and the 10-year risk of a major osteoporotic fracture. It can be used with or without DXA results. The current ankle fracture was not counted as a previous fracture for the FRAX calculation. This was to avoid confounding by the current ankle fracture and because this fracture was explicitly excluded as an osteoporosis-related fracture according to the DVO guideline valid at the time of the study.

Patients were classified according to the American Association of Clinical Endocrinologists (AACE) treatment recommendations [3]. The AACE guidelines state that a diagnosis of osteoporosis should be made, even if bone density measurements are normal, in cases where there is an elevated risk of fracture. The recommended threshold for initiating drug therapy is a 3% risk of proximal femur fracture and a 20% risk of major osteoporotic fracture over a 10-year period. Drug therapy in this context refers specifically to bisphosphonate therapy or osteoanabolic therapy, and does not include vitamin D or calcium substitution. We have categorized patients based on these risk thresholds, as we do not have DXA scans for the patients described here. The term 'therapeutic gap' was defined as the proportion of patients who, according to the calculated risk, had an indication for osteoporosis drug therapy but had not yet undergone such therapy. Descriptive data were analyzed using Microsoft Excel 2019.

Results

A total of 131 fractures of the ankle joint were included. All fractures were closed fractures. An overview of the fracture types of the included patients is shown in Fig. 1. There were 82 women, resulting in a male-to-female ratio of 1:2. The mean age was 65 years (45–94). Sixty-seven patients were over the age of 65. The answer to the question whether they had had a diagnostic test for osteoporosis (laboratory test or DXA scan) prior to the accident was negative in 75% of the cases. A combination of laboratory and DXA scan was reported by 15% of the patients, and the remaining 10% had received either a DXA scan or a laboratory scan. This suggests that there is a 67% diagnostic gap in patients indicated for basic diagnostic testing.

Based on the drug therapy indication, the cut-offs given in the Methods section resulted in a recommendation for specific osteoporosis therapy in 33% of patients. Taking age into account, it was found that no patient aged under 61 was indicated, but all patients aged over 74. Among the group of patients aged 61 years and older, 55% had a specific therapy indication prior to the occurrence of their fracture. The therapeutic gap was 97% for all patients with ankle fractures and an indication for a specific therapy.

Of the patients included, 18 (14%) had a previous fracture. The most common fracture was ankle fracture (n = 5), followed by distal radius fracture (n = 4) and proximal femur fracture (n = 3). Overall, 50% of those with previous fractures had experienced a major osteoporotic fracture.

Discussion

In the group of patients over 45 with ankle fractures, two out of three should be screened for osteoporosis. In the over-60 s with ankle fractures, one in two patients has an indication for specific therapy, but almost no one receives it.

The inclusion criterion was adults over the age of 45. The group was as expected in terms of gender and age distribution. Given an increasingly ageing population, an average age of 66 is representational [4]. A majority of women was also expected due to the gender distribution in this age group. This means that the cohort studied represents the expected population.

The fact that 85% of the patients aged 60 years and older in the study group had an indication for a diagnostic test of osteoporosis, even without considering the ankle fracture as an osteoporosis-associated fracture, is evidence for the authors of the vulnerability of this patient group. According to the still valid DVO guidelines, age only leads to a purely age-related indication for diagnostic testing for osteoporosis starting at 70 for women and 80 for men.

To the authors’ knowledge, there is no literature on the diagnostic gap for ankle fractures or other fracture entities. For this reason, the comparison was made with internal, unpublished data. For the whole group of ankle fractures reported here, the proportion of patients with an indication for baseline assessment was lower (61%) compared with 73% for radial fractures in the same age group and time period. However, the diagnostic gap of 67% was higher than the 53% for radius fractures. The diagnostic gap underlines the fact that the osteoporotic risk is still significantly underestimated in the ankle fracture group, according to the authors.

In terms of treatment, the picture is similar to the basic diagnostic testing. For the authors, the fact that only one patient received osteoporosis drug therapy is an expression of the 78–80% therapeutic gap in Germany, as reported in the literature [5]. Again, there is a lack of studies on individual fracture entities, so a comparison is also made with the osteoporotic pattern fracture of the distal radius. According to unpublished data from the same period in our clinic, 56% of patients with surgically treated distal radius fractures have an indication for specific therapy, which is lacking in 84% of these patients (= therapy gap). For ankle fractures, there is an indication for treatment in 33% of patients, which is significantly lower. This lower proportion can be explained by the incidence of both types of fracture. While there is a clear peak in the incidence of distal radius fractures in the second age group, the incidence of ankle fractures is more or less the same in all age groups. However, looking at the therapeutic gap between patients, it was 95% for ankle fractures and 84% for distal radius fractures. Thus, the exclusion of ankle fractures as osteoporosis-related seems to lead to an underestimation of the likelihood of osteoporosis in patients, both in terms of therapy and diagnostic testing.

Court-Brown and McQueen have shown that the incidence of ankle fractures does not increase with age, but remains constant in both men and women. This argues against the use of ankle fracture as an osteoporosis-related fracture. They were also able to show that the classic osteoporosis-associated fractures such as the distal radius fracture or the proximal femur fracture as well as the proximal humerus fracture are part of the same fracture type, characterized by an increase in incidence with age, and this is independent of gender [6]. This is a significant difference from ankle fractures.

In a retrospective analysis, Lee et al. [4] found that patients with ankle fracture did not have a lower bone density index than the control group; rather, they had a higher body mass index. From these findings, they concluded that there is no association between ankle fractures and osteoporosis.

Hjelle et al. [7] demonstrated that, compared to a control group, patients with a high BMI had an increased risk of ankle fracture, while the bone density in the fracture group was similar to that in the control group. The authors therefore conclude that there is no association with osteoporosis. The DXA scans of the patients reported that only 30% had normal bone density, whereas 22% had osteoporosis and the remaining 48% had osteopenia. In view of these results, ankle fractures are also suitable for identifying patients with osteoporosis (case finding).

Therdyothin et al.’s [8] systematic review only identified 19 studies on whether there is a higher occurrence of subsequent fractures or lower bone density in individuals with ankle fractures, in comparison to those without. The results of the studies vary. Therefore, there is no consistent evidence for or against the hypothesis that ankle fractures are associated with osteoporosis. However, it is worth noting that five of the eleven studies showed an increased risk of subsequent fractures. However, not all of the studies addressed this question. Overall, the authors conclude that the literature is inconclusive, nevertheless suggesting an association for geriatric patients. Therefore, this notion supports the employment of ankle fractures as a case finding measure, instead of disregarding them as non-osteoporotic from the beginning.

The data collected on indications for diagnostic testing and treatment for osteoporosis, which are lower than those for distal radius fractures, may also explain why ankle fractures are, by definition, not osteoporosis-associated fractures. Nevertheless, the figures prove that it is currently very reasonable in everyday clinical practice to treat them as such with regard to diagnostic screening for osteoporosis. The authors’ view is supported by the new draft guideline on osteoporosis therapy in postmenopausal women. This guideline no longer explicitly excludes ankle fractures as osteoporosis-associated fractures and focuses more on case finding.

Limitation

With 131 patients, the group presented here is small. However, it is a representative group due to the two-year time period. This period avoids seasonal bias. Furthermore, with 66 patients, the statistical power is already sufficiently high at 0.8 for an expected therapeutic gap of 80% in the total population. Another limitation is the lack of DXA scans of the patients. This would have supported the diagnostic testing for osteoporosis according to the current gold standard. However, it was not possible to integrate this measurement into the clinical routine. The lack of DXA scans is a common phenomenon in everyday clinical practice. Therefore, this study used the FRAX calculator risk calculation, which is known to have a good correlation with osteoporosis in the absence of a bone density measurement [3, 9, 10].

In the present study, no comparison was made with the normal population in the sense of a control group. This was not done because the question was about the incidence of osteoporosis in the group of patients with ankle fractures and, in addition, it was investigated whether the ankle fracture is suitable for case finding in diagnosing osteoporosis. Neither of these questions requires a control group.

Conclusion

Ankle fractures occur with nearly constant frequency across all age groups and therefore cannot be regarded as classical “sentinel fractures” of osteoporosis, such as distal radius fractures. Nevertheless, our findings demonstrate that a substantial proportion of patients with ankle fractures fulfill the criteria for osteoporosis screening and, in many cases, for specific therapy. Excluding ankle fractures from the spectrum of osteoporosis-associated fractures may therefore underestimate the risk of underlying bone fragility in this patient population.

We suggest that ankle fractures, particularly in patients over 60 years of age, should be considered a relevant indicator for osteoporosis case finding. Integrating routine osteoporosis screening into fracture management could help reduce the considerable diagnostic and therapeutic gap currently observed in Germany and Europe.
