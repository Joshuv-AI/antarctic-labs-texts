Microbiome composition in grapevine trunk diseases symptomatic plants is modulated by genotype and region
Authors: Filipe Azevedo-Nogueira, Ana Gaspar, Kateřina Štůsková, Ana Margarida Fortes, Ales Eichmeier, Cecília Rego, David Gramaje, Paula Martins-Lopes
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Grapevine health is influenced by microbiome composition, which is affected by region and several plant features such as cultivar, age and rootstock. Grapevine Trunk Diseases (GTDs) are caused by several wood-colonizing fungi, leading to imbalances in microbiome composition. Here, we performed next-generation sequencing of fungal and bacterial microbiomes present in trunk samples of ninety-seven symptomatic grapevines, from two cultivars (cv. Touriga Nacional and cv. Aragonez), collected in eight Portuguese wine-producing regions. The influence of wine-producing regions, grapevine genotype, rootstock, and age was analyzed. Results indicate that microbiome composition is largely influenced by region and cultivar, with more pronounced alterations in cv. Touriga Nacional. Furthermore, relationships between microbes were characterized, revealing that several genera could engage in competitive interactions with the pathogens. We postulate that environmental conditions associated to the wine-producing regions modulate trunk endosphere and microbiome composition. Plant cultivar, age and rootstock also influence the trunk microbiome assembly, leading to distinct taxa composition in the trunk, and also altered microbe relationships.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-13962-9.

Introduction

Grapevine Trunk Diseases (GTDs) are composed of up to six fungal diseases that encompass several syndromes, caused by more than one hundred fungal species1. These fungal species belong to several families and genera, with multiple – usually closely related – fungal species being responsible for a given GTD. These pathogens colonize grapevine wood tissues, mostly the spurs, the vine arms and the trunk, but in some cases can also infect the root system1–5. The vast colonization of plant woody tissues leads to the appearance of several symptoms, which evolve to plant decay and death, as the final stage of infection. At this point, plant fitness and yield are largely reduced, even virtually null; the best course of action being to discard GTD infected plants, as they provide an additional source of pathogenic inoculum for upcoming years1,4. Nevertheless, even when wine growers apply the best management practices, the losses associated with GTDs are still substantial.

Therefore, to limit pathogens propagation and colonization, delay outbreaks, and minimize greater losses, several strategies are employed such as: the use of phytopharmacological and chemical compounds; the adaptation of cultural practices (e.g., pruning time and practices, vineyard sanitation and debris removal, pruning wound protection); the improvement of nursery propagation practices (e.g., frequent mother block rotation, regular cleaning of tools and spaces); the use of Biocontrol Agents (BCAs); and physical treatments (like hot-water treatment). For more effective results, these measures are best employed in an integrated disease management system, which combines preventive and control practices to reduce GTDs impact in nurseries and vineyards4,6–9.

In recent years, several research groups have been committed to unravel the microbial composition of grapevine plant compartments, with emphasis on the rhizosphere, root endosphere, and the phyllosphere. Studies on other grapevine plant compartments (carposphere, trunk endosphere, and cane endosphere) have had a smaller focus, since they are not immediately responsible for grapevine establishment, grape maturation and, wine fermentation10–20. Environmental conditions and anthropogenic activities largely influence the microbial composition of grapevines plant compartments, affecting microbiome and plant tissue colonization by pathogens and endophytes12,21–23. Trunk endosphere, composed by woody and vascular tissues of the plant, is one of the less studied grapevine plant compartments10,17; yet, it is subjected to pressure from various environmental conditions, while being directly influenced by grapevine-related factors such as genotype and age11,24,25. Plant compartment colonization by pathogenic and/or endophytic microbial species leads to microbiome alterations and abnormal microbial behaviors, and a similar trend is observed in trunk endosphere9,26. However, there is still a lack of knowledge about the microbial composition of grapevine trunk endosphere and the effects on microbiome regarding woody tissue invasion by fungal pathogens responsible for GTDs.

Moreover, pathogen and endophyte behavior can be affected either by environmental conditions or by plant health status. Studies were conducted to understand the role of several microbial species in symptomatic and asymptomatic grapevines10,14,19,26, by resourcing culture-independent methods for microbiome analysis, which offer greater resolution when compared to culture-dependent methods15. Additionally, understanding the roles of endophytes in the trunk endosphere may provide another tool for GTDs control and mitigation, with the discovery of new BCAs.

Despite the knowledge regarding microbiome changes and dynamics due to either host features or environmental conditions, there is a lack of multifactor large-scale studies focusing on the influence of GTDs on the trunk microbiome. Therefore, in this work, we unravel and compare the microbiome changes within the trunk endosphere of two commercially relevant Portuguese grapevine cultivars – cv. Aragonez (syn. Tempranillo) and cv. Touriga Nacional – across various wine-producing regions by using a metabarcoding approach in a Next Generation Sequencing (NGS) platform. We also examine additional variables that may influence the grapevine trunk microbiome, such as vine age and rootstock. We analyzed the relations between colonizing microbial taxa considering these factors to understand if microbial interactions are affected by changes in the wine-producing region, grapevine genotype, age and the rootstock. Also, we investigate correlations between taxa, both pathogenic and endophytic, to elucidate potential antagonistic or mutualistic relationships.

Next generation sequencing

A total of 6,542,683 fungal ITS sequences and 5,154,717 bacterial 16S rRNA sequences were generated from 97 samples after paired-end alignments, quality filtering and deletion of singletons, chimeric, chloroplast and mitochondrial sequences, leading to 9 samples from fungal library and 22 samples from bacterial library being removed due to low number of reads. Good’s coverage values in all samples ranged from 99.3 to 100%, in fungal samples, and 92.5–100%, in bacterial samples. For fungal sequences, Chao1 diversity estimator ranged from 21.5 to 130, while Shannon diversity estimator ranged from 0.06 to 2.73. For bacterial sequences, Chao1 diversity estimator ranged from 67.2 to 493.9, while Shannon diversity estimator ranged from 1.30 to 4.28 (Supplementary information 2).

The 97 sampled grapevines presented symptoms related to Esca or Botryosphaeria dieback. From all grapevines, sawdust trunk samples were retrieved and NGS results showed that 64% of the identified Operational Taxonomic Units (OTUs) were from three fungal families (Phaeomoniellaceae, Togniniaceae and Botryosphaeriaceae), composed by 5 genera (Phaeomoniella, Phaeoacremonium, Neofusicoccum, Diplodia and Botryosphaeria) that are related to both diseases. Most grapevines sampled were simultaneously infected by pathogens related to Esca and Botryosphaeria dieback. On the other hand, the analysis of fungal and bacterial trunk microbiomes of cv. Touriga Nacional grapevine samples showed statistically significant differences between regions and between vine age (Table 2; Supplementary information 4). The type of rootstock did not lead to differences in alpha-diversity of trunk microbiome. Regarding beta-diversity, cv. Touriga Nacional, presented r-squared values between 0.092 and 0.459 (Table 2; Supplementary information 4).

Both alpha-diversity and beta-diversity revealed that cv. Aragonez samples grouped by region, vine age, or rootstock presented similar trunk microbiomes, contrasting with cv. Touriga Nacional, where trunk microbiomes composition differed among distinct regions. Also, regarding vine age, only cv. Touriga Nacional presented statistically significant differences, in both fungal and bacterial microbiomes (Table 2; Supplementary information 4), indicating that vine age affects the microbiome composition in specific genotypes, mainly in the distribution of counts per taxa. The type of rootstock did not lead to any differences in alpha-diversity indexes in fungal and bacterial trunk microbiomes of both cultivars. Also, beta-diversity r-squared values were low, as expected, since all samples presented GTDs symptoms, and were largely colonized by GTDs pathogens. However, a higher beta-diversity r-squared value was observed in cv. Touriga Nacional, mostly in the rootstock variable, but also considering region, when compared to cv. Aragonez (Table 2). The data demonstrate that both grapevine cultivars respond distinctively according to region, resulting in alterations of endospheric conditions and overall plant fitness, which leads to distinct microbiome development.

Overall, we observed that trunk microbiome composition differed among regions in cv. Touriga Nacional in both fungal and bacterial microbiomes (Chao1 index p-value: 4.84E−6, and 0.015; Shannon index p-value: 9.4E−4, and 3.4E−5; beta-diversity r-squared: 0.315, and 0.403, respectively). Also, beta-diversity r-squared values showed that microbiome composition was usually more dissimilar in cv. Touriga Nacional than in cv. Aragonez, indicating that the grapevine trunk microbiome of cv. Aragonez was less plastic, despite the type of rootstock used or the wine-producing region.

Influence of wine-producing region on microbiome changes

The datasets were divided to understand the effects of the wine-producing region on trunk microbiome considering distinct grapevine cultivar, age, and type of rootstock. Since the dataset size (Supplementary information 1) was very heterogenous, only some regions were considered in the analyses: Protected Denomination of Origin (PDO) Alentejo, Protected Geographical Indication (PGI) Algarve, PDO Dão, PDO Douro and PGI Lisboa. The most abundant fungal taxa were Phaeomoniella (22.5%) and Phaeoacremonium (15.2%) in PDO Alentejo, Phaeomoniella (28.2%) and Phaeoacremonium (15.1%) in PGI Algarve, Phaeoacremonium (26.9%) and Cladosporium (15.0%) in PDO Dão, Phaeoacremonium (38.1%) and Neofusicoccum (27.3%) in PDO Douro, and Phaeomoniella (47.9%) and Neofusicoccum (18.8%) in PGI Lisboa.

The datasets analyzed by regions revealed differences in the microbiome. The calculated alpha-diversity revealed that fungal microbiomes are statistically different between both cultivars in the regions of PGI Algarve (Table 3; Supplementary information 5–9.) and PDO Alentejo (Table 3; Supplementary information 6.). In contrast, in PDO Douro (Table 3; Supplementary information 7) and PGI Lisboa (Table 3; Supplementary information 8), the fungal microbiome of both cultivars is similar. The PDO Alentejo region also presented differences among fungal microbiomes considering the variables vine age and rootstock. Discussion

Throughout this work, we analyzed the fungal and bacterial microbiome in the trunks of symptomatic plants from two grapevine cultivars across various Portuguese wine-producing regions, examining the influence of grapevine age and the type of used rootstock. The results demonstrate distinct microbiome compositions influenced by several factors, mainly the grapevine cultivar and environmental conditions associated with the wine-production region. The alterations on climacteric conditions are translated into distinct reactions at the microbiome level when comparing both cultivars. Our results show that cv. Touriga Nacional exhibits unique behaviors at the trunk endosphere level, responding significantly to alterations in the region, in contrast to cv. Aragonez, which appears to be unaffected (Fig. 4). These observations suggest that regional changes influence microbial colonization and growth through plant response mechanisms to changes in external conditions (e.g., drought or temperature)13,20,29, rather than having a direct effect (as seen in the phyllosphere and carposphere)20,22,23.Fig. 4Graphical representation of the interaction between microbiome, endospheric conditions, host, and environment. We observed that the cultivars’ interaction with endospheric microbiota occurs distinctly, probably caused by alterations in environmental conditions resulting in changes in the endosphere.

Our model suggests that the observed differences in trunk microbiome composition and endospheric microbiota relations are highly dependent of grapevine cultivar genotype. We observed that the cultivars’ interaction with endospheric microbiota occurs distinctly, probably caused by alterations in environmental conditions resulting in changes in the endosphere.

In the case of cv. Touriga Nacional, the microbiome composition varied according to the wine-producing region, likely due to the distinct environmental conditions, which is consistent with patterns noted in trunk microbiome in Greece10. Interestingly, such impact was not observed in cv. Aragonez.

A similar phenomenon was reported by Mezzasalma et al.22, who identified distinct bacterial microbiomes in grape berries (cv. Cabernet Sauvignon, cv. Syrah, and cv. Sauvignon Blanc) and soils from Northern Spain, Italian Alps, and Northern Italy, which varied according to the producing regions.

An analysis performed in closely located vineyards revealed that microbiome composition in soil and root samples were consistent across vineyards, indicating minimal impact at a micro-geographical scale27. A similar pattern was observed in the berry microbiome, which is greatly affected by regional environmental conditions, thus influencing fermentation processes; where closely located vineyards exhibit fewer differences28. Interestingly, our results demonstrate that, in PDO Alentejo and PGI Algarve, bacterial populations are less susceptible to environmental changes compared to fungal population. This is consistent with findings from another work20, where fungal populations exhibited greater variability than bacterial populations throughout the seasons. Additionally, it was generally observed that the bacterial microbiome – in soil, rhizosphere, roots, cordon, canes and sap – displayed greater taxonomic richness than the fungal microbiome, a trend also noted in cv. Syrah grapevines in California30.

Moreover, our results show that the cultivar influences microbial changes, corroborating findings from other studies showing that distinct genetic pools can affect microbial composition in the phyllosphere and carposphere25,31. As expected, the two cultivars studied exhibited different microbiome compositions. Cv. Aragonez is less susceptible to GTDs compared to cv. Touriga Nacional, which can be due to physiological, genetic, or immunological traits32,33. These differences are also modulated by environmental conditions, highlighting the complex interplay between a cultivar’s adaptation to a specific climate and the associated influence on colonization by endophytes and/or pathogens32. Interestingly, while the microbiomes of both cultivars showed variations in taxa distribution and evenness in certain regions, other regions showed similar microbiome compositions. This suggests that microbiome assembly mechanisms are shaped by genotype-environment interactions25. These factors (region and cultivar) modulate the response of grapevines and, subsequently, their endosphere, exerting a more significant effect than other variables, such as rootstock and vine age.

Regarding vine age, our analysis also yielded results suggesting its influence on the trunk microbiome, particularly for taxa distribution in cv. Touriga Nacional, in contrast to cv. Aragonez. The effects of vine age appear to be only minimally influenced by the wine-producing region, which is primarily noted in bacterial communities in PDO Douro. In a study performed in vineyards closely located in Spain24, the authors theorized that differences in the trunk microbiome could be attributed to the varying ages of the grapevines analyzed. We observed that vine age has a direct impact on the microbiome composition, as shown in a study performed in groups of 3-, 8-, and 13-yeard-oldgrapevines; this study observed an increased colonization of endophytic species with advancing vine age, however some taxa varied in relative abundance (e.g., increasing relative abundance of Cladosporium and a decreasing relative abundance of Cadophora with age)15. Hence, due to the distinct phenological profiles at different developmental stages, vine age can modify the endosphere environment, influencing microbiome composition within the trunk. Accordingly, several studies have indicated a more complex microbial composition in the wood organs of older vines15,24 and, to a lesser extent, in the grapevine rhizosphere11.

Furthermore, our results show that the rootstock used can significantly influence the composition of the trunk microbiome through an impact on root microbiome assembly30, as observed in both cultivars but more pronounced in cv. Touriga Nacional. Notably, rootstock development propagation affect the fungal taxa present, as Phaeomoniella and Phaeoacremonium genera are more abundant after the propagation program, and Neofusicoccum is more abundant after a cold storage and hydration step17. Several studies highlight a strong genetic component of the host influencing rootstock microbiomes. In soils with similar bacterial composition, particular rootstocks were distinctly colonized by bacterial taxa: 140Ru (e.g. Rokubacteriales), 1103P (e.g. Pseudomonadaceae, Thioalkalispiraceae, and Methylophilaceae), Kober 5BB (e.g. Vermiphilaceae), and 161-49C (Rhodothermaceae)34. Also, different rootstocks were reported to exhibit varying levels of susceptibility to GTD pathogens; in particular, rootstock 41B presented a higher abundance of Black Foot disease pathogen Dactylonectria torresensis than rootstocks 140Ru, 1103P, 161-49C, and 110R11. The influence of rootstock on the trunk microbiome is also affected by environmental conditions, which can cause regional variations in taxa richness and distribution within both fungal and bacterial microbiomes. While the rootstock microbiome is influenced by environmental and soil conditions11, only minor effects were noted in closely located vineyards27. In this study, trunk microbial communities categorized by rootstock showed a higher beta-diversities when samples were grouped by region (Table 3) rather than by cultivar (Table 2). This highlights that the combined effect of rootstock and regional conditions has a higher impact in trunk endosphere colonization than the combined effect of rootstock and cultivar. This aligns with research suggesting that the rootstock genotype – along with the scion genotype and their interaction – affects microbial selection, which is hypothesized to occur via root exudates and immune responses. The latter lead to changes in microbial communities that can influence nutrient fixation and solubilization, thereby affecting plant fitness, health, and responses to abiotic and biotic stresses30,34–36.

Additionally, we investigated the microbial interactions between GTD-causing pathogens and other microbial taxa to elucidate unknown relationships and their potential modulation by region and plant features. Our findings demonstrate that regional environmental conditions differentially affect trunk colonization by fungal genera, beneficially impacting some taxa. However, the underlying mechanisms are complex, and the taxa that benefit vary accordingly. These factors, whether biotic or abiotic, influence microbial selection and relations and can compromise plant health by promoting the development of pathogens that can co-infect with other GTD-causing fungal species. Hence, understanding the balance and maintenance of the holobiont is crucial for sustainable precision viticulture, as plant health is closely connected to host-microbe and microbe-microbe interactions24,37. We observed that wine-producing regions are responsible for such alterations, with an intricated influence of the cultivar. Nonetheless, some genera, such as Phaeoacremonium and Neofusicoccum, exhibit more cultivar genotype dependency, while others, like Eutypa, show more regional influence, indicating deterministic impacts on fungal growth and colonization. Also, rootstock and vine age introduce an additional layer of pathogenic fungi selection, especially concerning Esca pathogens. Interactions within the trunk microbiome, including pathogen-pathogen or pathogen-endophyte relationships, are modulated by the analyzed factors (Fig. 3; Supplementary information 15, 16.), which is concordant with other studies10,19. While interactions between GTD-related pathogens generally remain constant, with mutualistic (e.g. Diaporthe and Diplodia) or competitive (e.g. Phaeomoniella and Diplodia, or Botryosphaeria and Eutypa) genera preserving their relationships, the intensity of these interactions can vary with changing any of the analyzed factors (Supplementary information 15). For pathogen-endophyte interactions, a similar pattern is observed, since the strength of the interaction is contingent to the analyzed factors. Interestingly, some microorganism interactions may shift between mutualistic or competitive states in response to an alteration of any factor, such as region, affecting pathogen and endophyte metabolism due to variations in nutrient availability and plant condition (e.g. Phaeoacremonium and Aspergillus).

In summary, our study shows that the trunk microbiome is highly dynamic, influenced by region, plant age, cultivar, and rootstock. These factors collectively impact the trunk endosphere, though some, such as region and plant genotype, have a more profound effect. The trunk microbiome composition and diversity are also shaped by microbiome assembly mechanisms, as its structure is influenced by microbiomes of other plant compartments30. Additionally, we also noted that environmental conditions related to the wine-producing region affect plant microbiome composition and microbial interactions. Other endophytes can also affect holobiont balance, hence affecting plant fitness and health24,37. Lastly, our assessment of microorganism interactions has pinpointed several genera (e.g. Cladosporium, Pseudophytomyces, Alternaria, and Filobasidium, among others) with potential biocontrol activity or with capacity to enhance pathogen colonization, as already observed for symptomatic and asymptomatic grapevines19. Understanding microbiome composition, diversity, and associated endophyte relations is essential for developing new strategies to mitigate the impact of GTDs in vineyards.

Material and methods

Samples

During 2021, from May to August, 97 grapevines (Table 1) from two commonly used cultivars, 35 cv. Touriga Nacional grapevines and 61 cv. Aragonez grapevines (syn. cv. Tempranillo), with GTDs symptoms, were identified from vineyards in 30 locations in 8 Portuguese wine producing regions (5 Protected Denominations of Origin and 3 Protected Geographic Indications). All samples were collected with the consent of the respective vineyard owners. Grapevines 10 years old and under were considered young and those over 10 years were considered mature for an easier analysis, and due to the fact that some GTDs are only present in young or mature grapevines1. These grapevines are subjected to double cordon training system and pruned on the spur. All grapevines are under integrated pest management. Symptoms observed in the field were recorded and a section of the trunk of each grapevine was taken. Trunk and arms sections were cut longitudinally to show the presence of symptoms such as discoloration and wood necrosis.

DNA extraction, metabarcoding library preparation and NGS

Sawdust was recovered from the wood tissues affected with discoloration and necrosis of the longitudinal cuts of the trunk sections, with a 0.5 mm micro drill MICROMOT50/EF (Proxxon micromot, Madrid, Spain)17. DNA was extracted with the Fast DNA™ Spin Kit (MPBiomedicals, Irvine, CA, USA) following manufacturer instructions. For fungal library preparation, the complete fungal ITS2 region was amplified using the primers ITS86F38 and ITS4R39. Bacterial libraries were prepared with the primers 515F-Y40 and 806R41. Primers were modified to include the Illumina sequencing primers. PCR were carried out in a final volume of 25 μL, containing 2.5 μL of template DNA, 0.5 μM of the primers, 12.5 μL of Supreme NZYTaq 2 × Green Master Mix (NZYTech), and ultrapure water up to 25 μL. PCR amplifications consisted of an initial denaturation step at 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 35 cycles of 95 °C for 30 s, 49 °C (fungi) and 47 °C (bacteria) for 30 s, 72 °C for 30 s, and a final extension step at 72 °C for 10 min. The oligonucleotide indices that are required for multiplexing different libraries in the same sequencing pool were attached in a second amplification step with identical conditions but only 5 cycles and 60 ºC as the annealing temperature. Libraries were purified using the Mag-Bind RXNPure Plus magnetic beads (Omega Biotek, Norcross, GE, USA), following the instructions provided by the manufacturer. The purified libraries were pooled in equimolar amounts according to the quantification data provided by the Qubit dsDNA HS Assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Samples were sequenced using the NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) at the AllGenetics and Biology SL (Galicia, Spain) (www.allgenetics.eu), accessed on 15 December 2023, using a paired-end 2 × 250 bp (PE250) sequencing. A negative control that contained no DNA (BPCR) was included in every PCR round to check for contamination during library preparation.

Sequencing data are deposited under BioProject acc. No. PRJNA1081177, where the SRA experiments are available by acc. Nos. SRX23777254-SRX2377735042.

Data analysis

FastQ files quality was assessed using FastQC43 and the output was summarized using MultiQC44. Further data processing of the sequences was performed in SEED v2.1.245. Raw sequences for each sample were assembled into paired-end reads using fastq-join tool integrated in the SEED software46. The fungal sequences obtained were filtered by quality (Q > 30) and length trimmed (above 250 bp) and bacterial sequences were filtered by quality (Q > 30) and length (above 40 bp). In both ambiguous sequences were removed. Sequences were grouped by barcode motives and labelled by sample name. For fungal sequences, Internal Transcribed Spacers (ITS) were extracted using ITSx (perl v5.18.1)47. Sequences were then clustered in Operational Taxonomic Units (OTUs) using Usearch-UPARSE 8.1.186148 and chimeric sequences removed with a threshold of 97% pairwise identity against UNITE (v8.2) fungal dynamic database49. OTUs sequences obtained were extracted from the clusters using MAFFT 7.22250. OTU identification was performed by blastn megablast against UNITE (v8.2) database for fungal OTUs and SILVA (release 119.1) for bacterial OTUs (https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Blast.cgi, accessed on 14 July 2021)13,44,45,47–50. Unclassified taxa at kingdom level, taxa from kingdoms which were not targeted, mitochondrial and chloroplast sequences were removed.

Microbial diversity and statistical analysis

Processed data was analyzed in MicrobiomeAnalyst 2.051. Samples with less than 10,000 reads were readily removed from the datasets. After, datasets were also filtered regarding read low counts (minimum count = 2 and prevalence > 10%) and low variance (percentage > 5% based on Inter-quartile range). Datasets were rarefied to minimum library size and were normalized by Total Sum Scaling13. Whenever necessary, samples with an unknown feature, namely rootstock used or grapevine age (Table 1), were removed. The highest level of resolution used for taxa identification was genus level, since for fungal libraries, ITS sequences do not usually provide enough definition for an accurate species identification in several taxonomic fungal clades15. Alpha-diversity was calculated using Chao1 and Shannon index with default ANOVA statistical analysis with a confidence interval of 95% (p-value < 0.05). Beta diversity was estimated using principal coordinates analysis (PCoA) based on Bray–Curtis Index with PERMANOVA statistical analysis (p < 0.001)13,17. Correlation analysis between fungal taxa was performed with SparCC algorithm that is integrated in MicrobiomeAnalyst, with a p-value threshold of 0.05 and a minimum correlation threshold of 0.3. Also, univariate analysis were performed to identify fungal taxa that were differently observed among variable factors (FDR-adjusted p-value < 0.05) employing the metagenomeSeq (fitZig) statistical method.

The correlations between fungal and bacterial taxa were performed in Cytoscape V3.10.1, with the Conet plugin52. Pearson and Spearman correlations were performed with the total dataset. Correlations presented were filtered to have a minimum threshold of 0.3 (p-value < 0.05). GTDs-causing taxa were selected and only correlations with other taxa were presented.
