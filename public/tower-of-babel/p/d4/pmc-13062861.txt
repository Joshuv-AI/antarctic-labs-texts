Primary adenoid cystic carcinoma of the thyroid gland: a case report and literature review
Authors: Yueyao Zhao, Lina Zhao, Zhihong Wei, Quanfeng Huang, Bei Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPrimary thyroid adenoid cystic carcinoma (PT-ACC) is an extremely rare malignant tumor, accounting for a negligible proportion of thyroid malignancies. It predominantly affects men and lacks unified clinical diagnostic criteria, resulting in a high rate of misdiagnosis. Owing to the scarcity of reported cases in female patients, we present this case with detailed ultrasonographic findings, pathological features, and 18-month follow-up data to supplement clinical evidence in this underrepresented population and to aid differential diagnosis and treatment strategies.Case DescriptionA 39-year-old woman with a 2-year history of hypertension, well controlled with oral medication, was found to have a thyroid nodule [Thyroid Imaging Reporting and Data System (TI-RADS) category 4B] during a routine physical examination in May 2021. Ultrasonography revealed a hypoechoic nodule measuring 20 mm × 16 mm × 24 mm located in the right thyroid lobe and isthmus, with an irregular shape and increased intranodular vascularity. Preoperative differential diagnoses included follicular carcinoma, atypical adenoma, and medullary carcinoma; the latter was excluded based on normal serum calcitonin levels. The patient underwent total thyroidectomy with central lymph node dissection and bilateral recurrent laryngeal nerve exploration, followed by postoperative radiotherapy (total dose of 60 Gy delivered in 30 fractions, 5 fractions per week). Chemotherapy was not administered. Postoperative pathological examination and immunohistochemical analysis confirmed the diagnosis of PT-ACC. During an 18-month follow-up period (up to December 2022), no evidence of local recurrence, distant metastasis, or treatment-related adverse events was observed, and the patient reported a normal quality of life.ConclusionsUltrasonography combined with TI-RADS classification is valuable for the preliminary screening of PT-ACC. In female patients presenting with thyroid nodules characterized by irregular morphology and abnormal vascularity, ACC should be considered in the differential diagnosis. Early radical surgical resection combined with postoperative radiotherapy may improve prognosis. This case adds to the limited clinical data on thyroid ACC in female patients and provides a useful reference for clinical diagnosis and management.Keywords: Thyroid, adenoid cystic carcinoma (ACC), case report

Key findings

• A rare female primary thyroid adenoid cystic carcinoma (PT-ACC) case was diagnosed via ultrasound (Thyroid Imaging Reporting and Data System category 4B) and pathology [p63 (+), thyroglobulin (−)/thyroid transcription factor-1 (−)]. What is known and what is new?

• PT-ACC is extremely rare, male-prevalent, easily misdiagnosed, and mainly treated with surgery.

• This study supplements female PT-ACC clinical data and validates surgery plus radiotherapy for favorable short-term prognosis.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Clinicians should suspect PT-ACC in females with irregular thyroid nodules; establish multicenter registries and conduct prospective studies to optimize treatment protocols.

Introduction

Adenoid cystic carcinoma (ACC) most commonly arises in the salivary glands of the head and neck and accounts for approximately 1–2% of all head and neck malignancies (1). Primary thyroid ACC (PT-ACC) is an exceptionally rare thyroid malignancy, and to date, no large-scale case series or unified clinical diagnostic criteria have been established. Clinically, PT-ACC is prone to misdiagnosis because of its insidious onset, often asymptomatic or presenting with nonspecific symptoms such as neck swelling or hoarseness, and its imaging features, which overlap with those of more common thyroid tumors (2). Regarding prognosis, the rarity of PT-ACC has precluded large-scale survival analyses. However, existing sporadic case reports suggest that complete surgical resection is associated with favorable short-term outcomes, whereas high-risk factors, including positive surgical margins, nerve invasion, and distant metastasis, may correlate with poorer prognosis (3). Epidemiologically, PT-ACC predominantly affects male patients, and reports involving female patients are exceedingly rare. In light of this limited evidence, we report a case of PT-ACC in a female patient, accompanied by a systematic literature review, to enrich the clinical data available for this underrepresented population. We present this case in accordance with the CARE reporting checklist (available at https://gs.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/gs-2025-aw-501/rc).

Case presentation

Patient information

A 39-year-old woman was found to have a right thyroid nodule classified as Thyroid Imaging Reporting and Data System (TI-RADS) category 4B during a routine physical examination at our hospital in May 2021. She subsequently underwent an outpatient follow-up evaluation in July 2021. The patient denied pain, dysphagia, dyspnea, heat intolerance, excessive sweating, chest tightness, palpitations, hand tremor, or other related symptoms. Her medical history was notable for vocal cord polyps diagnosed 3 years earlier and hypertension diagnosed 2 years earlier, which was well controlled with regular oral antihypertensive medication. She reported no family history of thyroid cancer or hereditary disorders and had no history of prior thyroid-related interventions. All procedures performed in this study were in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional and/or national research committee(s) and with the Helsinki Declaration and its subsequent amendments. Written informed consent was obtained from the patient for publication of this case report and accompanying images. A copy of the written consent is available for review by the editorial office of this journal.

Clinical findings

Physical examination of the neck revealed a supple neck with normal carotid artery pulsation and a midline trachea. A palpable mass measuring approximately 2.0 cm × 2.5 cm was detected in the right thyroid lobe extending to the right side of the isthmus. The mass had a well-defined margin and smooth surface, was non-tender, non-fluctuant, and mobile with swallowing, and was not associated with an audible vascular murmur. No palpable mass or vascular murmur was identified in the left thyroid lobe.

Color Doppler ultrasonography performed in July 2021 revealed a hypoechoic nodule measuring approximately 20 mm × 16 mm × 24 mm located in the right thyroid lobe and the right side of the isthmus. The lesion exhibited a distinct margin, irregular shape with shallow lobulation, and heterogeneous internal echogenicity (Figure 1A). Color Doppler flow imaging (CDFI) demonstrated increased intranodular vascularity (Figure 1B). Contrast-enhanced neck computed tomography (CT) performed in July 2021 revealed a nodular, hypoenhancing lesion measuring approximately 18 mm × 15 mm in the right thyroid lobe adjacent to the medial edge of the isthmus. The lesion was clearly demarcated from the tracheal wall, with no evidence of tracheal mucosal thickening, luminal narrowing, or tracheal wall invasion. No abnormal enlargement or enhancement of the bilateral parotid glands, submandibular glands, or other salivary glands was observed. Fiberoptic nasopharyngoscopy and laryngoscopy revealed left vocal cord polyps, with intact tracheal mucosa showing no irregular protrusions or lesions. No abnormalities were identified in the ducts of the oropharyngeal or hypopharyngeal salivary glands. Laboratory examination revealed urinary red blood cells at 15.0 cells/µL and white blood cells at 586.00 cells/µL. No significant abnormalities were detected in parathyroid hormone (PTH), thyroid function tests [thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH), free triiodothyronine (FT3), and free thyroxine (FT4)], thyroglobulin (TG), thyroid peroxidase antibody (TPOAb), thyrotropin receptor antibody (TRAb), or serum calcitonin levels.

Diagnostic assessment

Diagnostic assessment included thyroid ultrasonography (TI-RADS category 4B), neck CT, laboratory testing (including thyroid function tests and serum calcitonin), intraoperative frozen-section analysis, postoperative histopathological examination, and immunohistochemical analysis. The primary diagnostic challenge was the extreme rarity of the disease. In particular, the ultrasonographic features of the lesion, hypoechogenicity and irregular morphology, overlap with those of more common thyroid malignancies, such as papillary thyroid carcinoma. In addition, it was essential to exclude the possibility of metastatic ACC originating from the salivary glands or trachea, which required a comprehensive evaluation of adjacent organs to determine the primary tumor origin. Preoperative differential diagnoses included follicular carcinoma, suggested by possible capsular invasion on frozen-section examination; atypical adenoma, based on the presence of follicular proliferative lesions; and medullary carcinoma, which was excluded because of normal serum calcitonin levels. A primary thyroid origin was subsequently confirmed through multimodal evaluation. First, imaging and endoscopic examinations demonstrated no evidence of tracheal wall invasion or salivary gland abnormalities on neck CT, and laryngoscopy confirmed intact tracheal mucosa. Second, pathological examination showed that the tumor was closely adherent to the thyroid parenchyma and clearly demarcated from adjacent tracheal cartilage and salivary gland tissues, with no tumor infiltration into tracheal or salivary gland structures on hematoxylin-eosin (HE) staining. Histologically, the tumor predominantly exhibited a cribriform growth pattern, characterized by sieve-like cystic spaces lined by epithelial and myoepithelial cells, with focal tubular components; no solid growth pattern was observed. Third, immunohistochemical analysis revealed that the tumor was negative for TG and thyroid transcription factor-1 (TTF-1), excluding tumors of thyroid follicular epithelial origin, and positive for p63, a marker commonly associated with ACC. Moreover, no pathological features indicative of tracheal epithelial origin (such as respiratory epithelial metaplasia) or salivary gland epithelial differentiation (such as mucous gland differentiation) were identified. Collectively, these findings confirmed the diagnosis of primary ACC of the thyroid. From a prognostic perspective, there was no invasion of surrounding organs, apart from dense adhesion to the trachea and cricoid cartilage, and no lymph node metastasis was identified (0/1 lymph nodes involved). The tumor was therefore classified as early stage, and the prognosis was considered favorable.

Therapeutic intervention

The patient underwent comprehensive therapeutic management, including surgical resection in July 2021, followed by postoperative radiotherapy. Surgical treatment consisted of total thyroidectomy, central lymph node dissection, and bilateral recurrent laryngeal nerve exploration. The procedure was performed under general anesthesia and lasted approximately 3 hours. Postoperative radiotherapy was subsequently administered at a total dose of 60 Gy, delivered in 30 fractions (2 Gy per fraction, five fractions per week over 6 weeks). In addition, the patient received oral levothyroxine at a daily dose of 100 µg for postoperative TSH suppression therapy. Throughout the entire treatment course, no modifications were made to the surgical plan or radiotherapy regimen. Chemotherapy was not administered, given the early tumor stage and the absence of high-risk factors for metastasis.

Follow-up and outcomes

Intraoperative frozen-section analysis revealed follicular proliferative lesions with partial capsular invasion in the right thyroid lobe and isthmus. Owing to limited frozen-section material, an atypical adenoma was initially considered; follicular carcinoma could not be excluded; and medullary carcinoma was ruled out. Postoperative routine pathological examination demonstrated a tumor located in the right thyroid lobe and isthmus, for which immunohistochemical analysis was required to establish a definitive diagnosis. No metastatic carcinoma was identified in the central lymph node examined (0/1), and no malignant lesions were found in the left thyroid lobe (Figure 1C). Immunohistochemical staining showed positivity for cytokeratin (CK; partial +), CK7 (+), CD117 (partial +), p63 (partial +), and CK19 (partial +), while CK20, smooth muscle actin (SMA), CD10, CK5/6, CD43, epithelial membrane antigen (EMA), TG, TTF-1, hector Battifora mesothelial-1 (HBME-1), thyroid peroxidase (TPO), and CD56 were negative. The Ki-67 labeling index was approximately 10% (Figure 1D). Combined with the HE morphological features, these findings confirmed the diagnosis of primary ACC of the thyroid, with no evidence of tumor involvement in adjacent tissues, including the salivary glands or trachea. During the follow-up period extending to December 2022 (18 months postoperatively), the patient adhered to regular oral levothyroxine therapy and completed all scheduled follow-up visits. Mild fatigue was noted during radiotherapy, but no other treatment-related adverse effects were observed. No surgery-related complications, such as recurrent laryngeal nerve injury, or severe radiotherapy-related toxicities were reported. Follow-up evaluations demonstrated normal thyroid function at all visits, no abnormal nodules or lymphadenopathy on neck ultrasonography, and no evidence of pulmonary metastasis on chest CT. Clinical assessment confirmed the absence of tumor recurrence or metastasis. The patient reported no hoarseness or dysphagia and had returned to normal work and daily activities, with a good quality of life.

Literature review methods

A systematic literature search was conducted across four databases, PubMed, Web of Science, China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI), and Wanfang, to identify relevant case reports and case series published between January 2000 and December 2022. The search strategy used the following terms: (“thyroid” OR “thyroid gland”) AND (“adenoid cystic carcinoma” OR “ACC”) AND (“primary”). The inclusion criteria were as follows: (I) pathologically confirmed PT-ACC; (II) case reports or case series providing available clinical, imaging, pathological, or follow-up data; and (III) publications written in English or Chinese. The exclusion criteria were: (I) metastatic ACC involving the thyroid and originating from the salivary glands, trachea, or other organs; (II) reviews, meta-analyses, basic research studies, or editorials without original case data; (III) duplicate publications reporting the same case in multiple journals; and (IV) studies with incomplete or non-extractable data. From the eligible studies, the following variables were extracted: age, sex, tumor location, clinical symptoms, invasion of surrounding structures, presence of metastasis, tumor size, treatment modalities, diagnostic basis, histological recurrence, treatment for recurrence, and clinical outcomes. All included studies were organized alphabetically by author name and year of publication. A flowchart illustrating the study selection process for thyroid ACC is presented in Figure 2. Two independent researchers applied the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria during the title and abstract screening process. Subsequently, both reviewers independently assessed the full texts of potentially eligible studies to determine final inclusion. Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion and consensus.

Literature review results

A total of 126 records were identified through the database search, including 66 from PubMed, 22 from Web of Science, 8 from CNKI, and 30 from Wanfang. After removing 26 duplicate publications and one article published in a language other than English, 99 records remained for screening. Of these, 69 articles were excluded for not meeting the inclusion criteria, and 18 reports could not be retrieved in full. Ultimately, five cases of PT-ACC were included in the analysis, comprising four cases from the literature and the present case. The clinical and pathological characteristics of these cases are summarized in Table 1 (2-5). The present case involved a female patient, whereas three of the previously reported cases involved male patients; in one remaining report, patient sex and age were not specified. Among the four cases with available age data, the median age was 50.75 years. Tumor location varied among cases: one tumor was located in the left thyroid lobe, one in the right lobe, and two involved multiple regions, including bilateral thyroid lobes or the right lobe and isthmus. All cases were confirmed postoperatively by HE staining and immunohistochemical analysis. The initial clinical manifestations primarily included abnormalities of the neck, such as unilateral or bilateral neck enlargement. In one patient, tumor invasion of the recurrent laryngeal nerve was the sole cause of hoarseness. Patients presenting with bilateral neck enlargement exhibited not only adjacent nerve involvement but also intravascular tumor thrombus. In the present case, intraoperative exploration and postoperative pathological examination revealed an indistinct tumor boundary, irregular morphology, and dense adhesion to the trachea, cricoid cartilage, and thyroid cartilage. During follow-up, no recurrence was observed, and the patient remained alive at the last follow-up. Four of the five reported cases underwent surgical treatment; among them, one male patient (n=1, 25%) also received TSH suppression therapy. No tumor recurrence was reported in these surgically treated cases.

Discussion

In this report, we describe a case of primary ACC of the thyroid in a female patient. The tumor was located in the right thyroid lobe and isthmus and exhibited dense adhesion to the trachea, cricoid cartilage, and thyroid cartilage. During the follow-up period, no tumor recurrence was observed, and the patient remains alive at the time of reporting. According to the standards of the American Thyroid Association, the tumor demonstrated morphological and immunohistochemical features characteristic of ACC (6).

PT-ACC is an exceedingly rare entity. Existing literature indicates that the majority of ACCs arise from salivary gland tissue or represent metastases from primary lesions in other organs, whereas tumors originating within the thyroid gland are extremely uncommon (7,8). Through our systematic literature review, we identified four reported cases of PT-ACC. Only one publication described clinical features similar to those observed in our case (4), including comparable tumor size, close adherence to the trachea, and favorable survival outcomes. The remaining reported cases differed with respect to tumor location, extent of local invasion, and therapeutic approaches. Based on the present case and previously reported cases, PT-ACC appears to predominantly affect male patients, with female cases being exceptionally rare.

The early development of ACC of the thyroid gland is typically insidious and characterized by mild or nonspecific symptoms. Consequently, many patients are diagnosed at a relatively advanced stage, by which time the tumor may have invaded adjacent structures such as the trachea or regional lymph nodes, thereby increasing the difficulty of treatment. Early detection is therefore critical throughout the diagnostic and therapeutic process. In both the report by Sun et al. and the present case, thyroid masses were incidentally discovered during routine physical examinations (4). The diagnosis of thyroid ACC appears to be closely related to health awareness in the affected population, and regular physical examinations may significantly improve treatment efficacy. When symptoms such as hoarseness or dysphagia occur, relatively few patients seek prompt medical attention. This may be attributable to the slow-growing nature of the tumor, which leads to compressive symptoms resembling those of benign thyroid nodules or neck masses, or to direct invasion of peripheral nerves by the tumor, resulting in corresponding neurological manifestations. Perineural invasion (PNI) is a hallmark pathological feature of ACC and reflects its distinctive biological behavior. PNI refers to tumor cell infiltration along the perineural space and has been reported in approximately 60–80% of head and neck ACCs, most of which originate from the salivary glands. This feature contributes to the tumor’s local aggressiveness, delayed recurrence, and characteristic symptoms, including hoarseness, pain, or sensory disturbances. In previously reported cases of PT-ACC, PNI was documented in two cases (4,5). In contrast, no evidence of PNI was identified in our case, which is consistent with the absence of neurological symptoms and the patient’s favorable short-term prognosis. Notably, site-specific anatomical differences may account for variations in PNI-related clinical manifestations between PT-ACC and salivary gland ACC. Compared with salivary gland ACC, PT-ACC is less likely to present with PNI-related symptoms, which are observed in approximately 60–70% of salivary gland ACC cases, owing to the dense periglandular nerve plexus in salivary glands. In contrast, the thyroid gland has a relatively sparse distribution of adjacent nerves, potentially contributing to the more occult clinical presentation of PT-ACC. However, the lack of specific early symptoms further complicates timely diagnosis, underscoring the importance of auxiliary diagnostic examinations in the identification of this rare disease.

Ultrasonography, CT, and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) are the most commonly used adjunctive imaging modalities for the evaluation of thyroid lesions. Real-time ultrasonography enables dynamic assessment of thyroid nodules and their relationships with surrounding structures. CT imaging is useful for evaluating tumor location, extrathyroidal extension, involvement of adjacent structures such as the trachea or carina, and the presence of distant metastases (9). MRI provides superior soft-tissue contrast and can help assess inflammatory changes, the extent of PNI, and differentiation between benign and malignant lesions. Given the anatomical location of the thyroid gland and the limited ability of CT and MRI to dynamically evaluate tumor characteristics and their interactions with surrounding tissues, ultrasonography remains an inexpensive modality with few contraindications and is therefore a cornerstone in the detection and diagnosis of thyroid disease. The ultrasonographic features of ACC typically include solid thyroid nodules, occasionally presenting as mixed cystic-solid lesions, with a diameter of approximately 2 cm. These nodules often exhibit relatively clear margins with an incomplete capsule, irregular morphology with shallow lobulation, visible small lacunae, heterogeneous internal echogenicity, punctate hypoechoic or anechoic areas, partial posterior acoustic attenuation, and abundant intranodular blood flow signals. However, these ultrasonographic characteristics overlap substantially with those of many other thyroid malignancies, making it difficult to establish a definitive pathological diagnosis based solely on conventional ultrasound findings. According to current TI-RADS classification guidelines, features such as an aspect ratio greater than 1, ill-defined margins, irregular edges, solid composition, hypoechogenicity, microcalcifications, and abnormal internal vascularity aid in distinguishing benign from malignant nodules and may improve the diagnostic accuracy for ACC to some extent (10). In the present case, although no typical malignant ultrasonographic signs were identified, the nodule was relatively large and exhibited shallow lobulation and strip-like intranodular blood flow, leading to its classification as TI-RADS category 4B, indicating moderate suspicion of malignancy. While this assessment was less definitive than the postoperative pathological diagnosis, it nevertheless contributed to reducing the risk of misdiagnosis. Overall, ultrasonography plays a crucial role not only in preliminary screening but also in the evaluation of regional lymph node involvement and in monitoring tumor progression during clinical treatment, underscoring its importance in the comprehensive management of PT-ACC.

When evaluating thyroid ACC, it is essential to consider and exclude tumors originating outside the thyroid gland, particularly direct invasion or metastatic involvement from the parotid gland or trachea (11,12). Accordingly, the possibility of non-thyroid primary tumors in adjacent structures should be carefully assessed in patients presenting with highly invasive cervical tumors, a history of prior or distant malignancies, or fine-needle aspiration specimens demonstrating atypical cytological features (13,14). In the present case, postoperative pathological examination and complementary investigations confirmed that the tumor originated from the thyroid gland rather than the parotid gland or trachea. Similarly, the cases identified in our literature review were all confirmed to be of thyroid origin. In one previously reported case, the tumor exhibited marked invasion of the trachea, necessitating differentiation from a primary tracheal carcinoma. In our case, tumor size, location, and metastatic status were not directly correlated with the histological tumor type. Morphologically, the histopathological features of ACC may resemble those of papillary or follicular thyroid carcinoma, which complicates diagnosis based on morphology alone. However, immunohistochemical analysis provides critical discriminatory value. Papillary and follicular thyroid carcinomas typically express TG and TTF-1 but do not express markers such as SMA or p63, whereas ACC characteristically expresses SMA, p63, and related markers (15). In the present patient, immunohistochemistry demonstrated partial positivity for p63 and negativity for TG and TTF-1, thereby clarifying both the nature and the primary origin of the tumor. In conclusion, in addition to clinical manifestations and imaging findings, histopathological evaluation using HE staining combined with immunohistochemical analysis is indispensable for establishing an accurate diagnosis of PT-ACC.

At present, there is no standardized treatment protocol for primary ACC of the thyroid. Owing to its high degree of local invasiveness, this tumor frequently exhibits perineural spread and a strong propensity for hematogenous dissemination. Surgical resection remains the primary treatment modality, particularly for tumors with regional lymph node involvement. Postoperative radiotherapy and chemotherapy have been reported to reduce the risk of hematogenous metastasis and improve local disease control (16). In the present case, preoperative evaluation revealed tumor involvement of the trachea, cricoid cartilage, and thyroid cartilage adjacent to the trachea. To minimize the risk of local invasion and distant dissemination, a comprehensive treatment strategy combining radical surgical resection with postoperative radiotherapy was adopted, resulting in a favorable therapeutic outcome. Nevertheless, PT-ACC is characterized by a high risk of distant metastasis despite effective local control. The lung is the most common site of distant spread, followed by bone and liver involvement, which significantly affects patient survival. Reported data indicate a 5-year local recurrence rate of approximately 40% and a distant metastasis rate of up to 60% (14). In the case reported by Sun et al., the tumor did not invade the thyroid capsule and was classified as an early-stage lesion based on tumor size (4); however, disease recurrence occurred 11 months after the initial diagnosis. The absence of postoperative radiotherapy may have contributed to this outcome, as several reported cases have documented subsequent distant metastases. Therefore, standardized postoperative management—particularly the incorporation of adjuvant radiotherapy—may play a critical role in reducing tumor recurrence. In addition, long-term and rigorous follow-up is essential for the early detection of distant metastasis in patients with PT-ACC.

The principal strength of this study lies in its comprehensive documentation of clinical presentation, imaging findings, pathological characteristics, and follow-up outcomes in a rare female case of PT-ACC, thereby enriching the limited clinical evidence available for this underrepresented population. Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, as a single-center, single-case report, the generalizability of the findings is inherently limited. Second, the follow-up duration of 18 months is relatively short, and long-term outcomes, including late recurrence, remain to be determined. Third, molecular genetic analyses, such as assessment of the MYB-NFIB gene fusion, were not performed and could have provided further insights into tumor pathogenesis. Fourth, the small number of cases included in the literature review (n=5) may introduce selection bias. Future multicenter studies incorporating larger patient cohorts, extended follow-up periods, and molecular genetic profiling are warranted to overcome these limitations and to further elucidate the biological behavior, optimal management, and long-term prognosis of PT-ACC.

Patient perspective

“I first discovered the thyroid nodule during a routine physical examination in 2021. I felt anxious when I was informed that it was classified as TI-RADS category 4B, but the medical team provided clear explanations regarding the disease and treatment plan, which greatly relieved my concerns. The surgery was successful, and during the 6-week course of radiotherapy, I experienced only mild fatigue. Follow-up visits were convenient, and the physicians offered careful guidance on medication use and daily self-care. Now, 18 months after surgery, I have returned to my normal work and daily life. I am grateful for the early detection and timely treatment, and I hope that my experience may encourage other patients to face this disease with confidence and seek medical care promptly.”

Conclusions

PT-ACC is a rare malignant tumor that predominantly affects middle-aged men, with female cases being exceptionally uncommon. Diagnosis relies mainly on histopathological examination combined with immunohistochemical analysis, whereas ultrasonography serves as a valuable tool for lesion detection and characterization. The application of the TI-RADS classification system may aid in raising clinical suspicion of malignancy in thyroid nodules, although it cannot independently establish a definitive diagnosis of ACC. In addition, ultrasonography may assist in evaluating the relationship between the tumor and adjacent structures, such as the trachea, larynx, and salivary glands, as well as in monitoring regional lymph nodes and potential distant metastases during postoperative follow-up. Therefore, clinicians should consider the possibility of ACC in female patients presenting with thyroid nodules, particularly those exhibiting irregular morphology and increased intranodular vascularity. Early recognition and individualized treatment strategies may contribute to improved disease control and quality of life for affected patients.
