Effects of different tooth sites and ages on the remodeling patterns of human mandibular alveolar bone
Authors: YiQing Wang, Wei Liang, Longlong He, Jianfei Liang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThis study aimed to investigate the effects of tooth sites (incisors, canines, premolars, molars) and aging on the remodeling patterns of the human mandibular alveolar bone, including the buccal cortical bone, lingual cortical bone, and cancellous bone.MethodsA total of 600 eligible patients (300 males and 300 females, aged 20–80 years) were randomly selected from the Stomatological Hospital of Xi’an Jiaotong University between January 2023 and December 2024. All subjects underwent cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) scans using a Planmeca ProMax 3D scanner. They were stratified into age groups (20–29 to 70–80 years, in 10-year intervals) and by tooth position (incisors, canines, premolars, molars). The measurement plane was standardized using InVivoDental 7 software. Cortical bone thickness was measured as the average of six points on both the buccal and lingual sides below the root apex. Cancellous bone density was analyzed using threshold analysis in ImageJ software.ResultsThe results indicated that within the same age group, the buccal cortical bone thickness followed a distinct pattern across different tooth positions: molars (LM) > premolars (LP) > canines (LC) > incisors (LA). In contrast, no clear patterns were observed for lingual cortical bone thickness or cancellous bone amount across tooth positions within the same age group. With increasing age, the buccal cortical bone thickness at the same tooth position exhibited a gradual thinning pattern. However, no obvious patterns of change with age were found for either the lingual cortical bone thickness or the amount of cancellous bone at the same tooth position.ConclusionsThe remodeling pattern of the mandibular alveolar bone is significantly influenced by both tooth position and age, with the most pronounced effects observed on the buccal cortical bone.Clinical relevanceThese findings suggest that clinical practices such as oral implantology and orthodontics should pay particular attention to the status of the buccal cortical bone, especially in the anterior region and in older patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-026-08263-0.Keywords: Mandible, Alveolar bone remodeling, Cortical bone thickness, Cancellous bone, Tooth position, Age

Introduction

Population aging is a global phenomenon that underscores the importance of understanding age-related physiological changes, particularly in the alveolar bone [1]. As a fundamental component of the masticatory system, the alveolar bone provides crucial support for teeth, transmits masticatory forces, and maintains the architectural integrity of the dental arch and facial contours [2–4]. With advancing age, however, the alveolar bone undergoes progressive physiological resorption [5], a process driven by an imbalance in bone remodeling where resorption by osteoclasts outpaces formation by osteoblasts [6]. This imbalance leads to microarchitectural deterioration, including trabecular thinning, increased marrow space, and reduced bone mineral density, which collectively compromise bone strength and resilience [7]. Notably, alveolar bone mass begins to decline from approximately 40 years of age, with postmenopausal women experiencing accelerated bone loss at a rate 2–3 times faster than men [8]. Furthermore by 70 years of age, individuals may have lost 30%–50% of their alveolar bone volume compared to youth [8].

Such extensive bone loss has profound clinical implications, contributing to tooth mobility and loss, impaired masticatory function, occlusal disturbances, and loss of facial skeletal support, which manifests as an aged appearance [9–11]. Moreover, adequate alveolar bone volume and quality are essential prerequisites for the success of common dental rehabilitative treatments, such as implant therapy and fixed prosthodontics [12]. Previous research has quantified age-related alveolar changes, demonstrating a progressive reduction in alveolar crest height and bone width [13–16]. For instance, one study reported that the distance from the cementoenamel junction to the alveolar crest in maxillary anterior teeth increases significantly with age [13]. These changes are influenced by disuse atrophy, reduced vascular supply, chronic inflammatory response, and systemic bone mineral density decline, which contributed to the stability of natural tooth and prosthesis [14–17].

Furthermore, gender and anatomical site-specific differences exist. For example, women exhibit significant thinning of the palatal cortical bone in the posterior maxilla, potentially linked to hormonal changes, while men maintain greater buccal cortical bone thickness compared to same aged women in the same region, which possibly due to anatomic and biomechanical factors [18, 19].While previous studies have often relied on linear measurements of bone height or width, there is still a lack of systematic three-dimensional analysis. To address this, the present study utilized cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) combined with precise three-dimensional reconstructions in InVivo Dental 7 software and quantitative bone density analysis in Image J software. With a large sample size (n = 600) and strict inclusion criteria to minimize confounding factors, this study aims to provide a refined analysis of the physiological aging remodeling patterns of the human mandibular alveolar bone. The primary objectives are: (1) to characterize and compare age-related variations in buccal cortical bone thickness across different mandibular tooth positions; (2) to examine sex-based differences in these parameters; and (3) to investigate site-specific and age-related changes in both cortical bone thickness and cancellous bone density. The findings are expected to construct a detailed “tooth-position–age–gender” model, providing valuable data for clinical assessments and personalized dental treatment plans.

Materials and methods

Data collection

Subject selection

​A total of 600 patients meeting the inclusion criteria were randomly selected between January 2023 and December 2024 at the Stomatological Hospital of Xi’an Jiaotong University. The cohort comprised 300 males and 300 females, aged 20 to 80 years, whose maxillofacial cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) images were collected. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Stomatology of Xi ‘an Jiaotong University (2024-XJKQIEC-KY-QT-0041-002) in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants were informed and willing to cooperate.

Grouping basis

​This study performed intergroup comparisons of alveolar bone quality across different teeth and tooth positions in men and women across various age groups. The age categories included 20–29, 30–39, 40–49, 50–59, 60–69, and 70–80 years. Tooth positions were classified into anterior, canine, premolar, and molar regions. The primary outcome measure was buccal cortical bone thickness at these tooth sites in both sexes. Inter-group comparisons of buccal bone thickness were planned across the afore mentioned age groups for each tooth site. Based on prior studies, the mean ± standard deviation values for the smallest inter-group differences between 20 and 29 years and 30–39 years in buccal bone thickness were assumed as follows: 1.23 ± 0.225 and 1.14 ± 0.216 (male anterior teeth), 1.24 ± 0.328 and 1.10 ± 0.355 (male canines), 1.36 ± 0.430 and 1.51 ± 0.310 (male premolars), 1.62 ± 0.448 and 1.78 ± 0.349 (male molars), 0.75 ± 0.337 and 0.87 ± 0.248 (female anterior teeth), 1.26 ± 0.432 and 1.11 ± 0.311 (female canines), 1.31 ± 0.459 and 1.48 ± 0.357 (female premolars), and 1.30 ± 0.548 and 1.50 ± 0.449 (female molars). A two-sided significance level of 0.05 and a statistical power of 80% were applied. The sample size was estimated using PASS 2021 (v21.0.3) with a two-sided two-sample unequal-variance t-test. The required sample sizes per group were calculated as 96, 95, 100, 96, 97, 100, 93, and 100, respectively. To ensure data uniformity, a total of 100 subjects were planned for enrollment in each age group, resulting in a final sample size of 600 participants.

Study methods

Equipment and scanning protocol

All cone-beam computed tomography scans were obtained as part of the routine clinical care for patients undergoing preoperative assessment for dental implant surgery. No additional CBCT scans were performed solely for research purposes, thereby ensuring that no subject was exposed to unnecessary radiation. This retrospective analysis utilized existing clinical data, and the study protocol, including the use of these clinically indicated CBCT images for research, was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of Stomatology of Xi’an Jiaotong University (Approval Number: 2024-XJKQIEC-KY-QT-0041-002) in strict accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

All scans were performed using a Planmeca ProMax 3D unit. To adhere to the ALARA principle, standardized, low-dose scanning protocols were employed for full-arch examinations (tube voltage: 120 kV; tube current: 10 mA; scanning time: 20–30 s, adjusted for patient size; slice thickness: 0.25 mm). All subjects wore lead thyroid collars and aprons for protection during scanning. The procedures were conducted by an experienced radiologist following established clinical protocols. Cases with artifacts or incomplete scans were excluded. All subjects had provided informed consent for the clinical CBCT procedure, which included explanation of the radiation exposure. The scanned data were imported into InVivoDental 7 in DICOM format for reconstruction and measurement.

​Measurement methods

In the Multiplanar Reconstruction (MPR) interface of InVivo Dental 7 software, the teeth to be measured were identified on the horizontal cross-section of the tooth neck. The sagittal slice was adjusted to make the sagittal section bisect the mesiodistal plane of the measured tooth. Then, the coronal section was adjusted in the coronal position to make the sagittal tangent pass through the cusp and the midpoint of the incisal edge of the measured tooth. Finally, the coronal tangent was adjusted on the sagittal plane to pass through the cusp and the incisal edge. This process was repeated to obtain the maximum buccolingual sagittal view of the maxillary central incisor, which was used as the measurement plane.

Measurement: On the buccal and lingual sides of the lower anterior teeth (LA), lower canines (LC), lower premolars (LP), and lower molars (LM) from below the tooth root to above the apical cortical bone), an average of six measurement points were selected on each side to measure the thickness of the alveolar bone. The average value of the 6 micro-point measurements was taken as the final thickness of the alveolar bone at that site; The corresponding region of interest (ROI) was selected on the intercepted plane. In ImageJ software, the ROI was converted into an 8-bit grayscale image, followed by threshold analysis, and finally, the trabecular bone density of cancellous bone was measured (Supplementary Figure). All measurements were performed independently by a single researcher.

Statistical analysis

All data analysis was performed using SAS® Statistical Analysis Software Version 9.4. Descriptive analysis followed these principles: quantitative data were described using the number of non-missing observations (missing), mean, standard deviation, median, Q1, Q3, minimum, and maximum values. Decimal presentation rules are as follows: The number of decimal places for max/min values matches the precision of the original data. Mean, median, Q1, and Q3 display one additional decimal place compared to the original data. Standard deviation displays two additional decimal places compared to the original data. In principle, no more than five decimal places are displayed.

In this study, comparisons between groups of quantitative data employed either analysis of variance (ANOVA) or the Kruskal-Wallis test. If the data meet the assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variance, a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA; superscript *a*) was used for group comparisons, with post-hoc pairwise comparisons conducted using Tukey’s test. If the data violated the assumptions of normality or homogeneity of variance, the non-parametric Kruskal-Wallis test (superscript *b*) was employed for group comparisons, followed by Dunn’s test (DSCF) for pairwise comparisons (see Supplementary Tables). When data satisfied normality and homogeneity of variance, overall group comparisons used ANOVA, P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for intergroup differences.

Results

The buccal cortical bone thickness at different tooth sites within the same age group

To investigate the influence of tooth sites on buccal cortical bone thickness, we measured buccal cortical bone thickness across various age and sex groups. In the 20–29 age group, buccal cortical bone thickness exhibited a posterior-to-anterior gradient in both sexes, with the highest values at LM (males: 2.091 ± 0.4674 mm; females: 2.159 ± 0.3478 mm) and the lowest at LA (males: 1.558 ± 0.3554 mm; females: 1.401 ± 0.2852 mm) (Fig. 1.A, B); In the 30–39 age group, a consistent posterior-to-anterior gradient was maintained, with LM showing the greatest thickness (males: 2.116 ± 0.4817 mm; females: 2.176 ± 0.4940 mm) and LA the least (males: 1.516 ± 0.3182 mm; females: 1.506 ± 0.2943 mm) (Fig. 1.C, D). Within the 40–49 age group, thickness continued to follow the LM > LP > LC > LA sequence in both sexes (LM—males: 1.980 ± 0.4518 mm, females: 1.863 ± 0.3887 mm; LA—males: 1.342 ± 0.2852 mm, females: 1.257 ± 0.2995 mm) (Fig. 1. E, F). For participants aged 50–59, the gradient persisted (LM—males: 1.773 ± 0.3954 mm, females: 1.807 ± 0.4797 mm; LA—males: 1.235 ± 0.3352 mm, females: 1.316 ± 1.5158 mm) (Fig. 1.G, H); In the 60–69 age group, thickness values declined further but retained the posterior-anterior order (LM—males: 1.777 ± 0.4502 mm, females: 1.445 ± 0.5067 mm; LA—males: 1.146 ± 0.3109 mm, females: 0.882 ± 0.3520 mm) (Fig. 1.I, J); Finally, in the 70–80 age group, the gradient remained evident (LM—males: 1.567 ± 0.5295 mm, females: 1.310 ± 0.4708 mm; LA—males: 1.021 ± 0.3467 mm, females: 0.866 ± 0.2622 mm) (Fig. 1.K, L). Overall, these results suggest the buccal cortical bone thickness at different tooth sites as followed trend: LM > LP > LC > LA.

The lingual cortical bone thickness at different tooth sites within the same age group

To investigate the influence of tooth sites on lingual cortical bone thickness, we measured lingual cortical bone thickness across various age groups. In the 20–29 age group, the thickest lingual cortex was located at the LC in males (2.102 ± 0.4184 mm) and at the LA in females (2.143 ± 0.4128 mm), with the thinnest at the LM in both sexes (males: 1.838 ± 0.3828 mm; females: 1.862 ± 0.3256 mm)(Fig. 2A, B).Within the 30–39 age group, the LA was thickest in males (2.031 ± 0.4013 mm) and females (2.211 ± 0.4202 mm), while the LM remained thinnest (males: 1.766 ± 0.3555 mm; females: 1.864 ± 0.4055 mm) (Fig. 2C, D). For the 40–49 age group, the LC was thickest in males (1.950 ± 0.3730 mm) and the LP in females (2.051 ± 0.4361 mm), with the LM being thinnest (males: 1.741 ± 0.3950 mm; females: 1.780 ± 0.3562 mm) (Fig. 2E, F). In participants aged 50–59, the LC was thickest (males: 1.880 ± 0.3514 mm; females: 2.083 ± 0.3996 mm) and the LM thinnest (males: 1.655 ± 0.3847 mm; females: 1.677 ± 0.4261 mm) (Fig. 2G, H). Within the 60–69 age group, the thickest site was LA in males (2.466 ± 6.2551 mm) and LC in females (1.751 ± 0.5058 mm), with the LM being thinnest (males: 1.760 ± 0.3904 mm; females: 1.352 ± 0.3941 mm) (Fig. 2I, J). Finally, in the 70–79 age group, the LC was thickest in both sexes (males: 1.804 ± 0.3485 mm; females: 1.835 ± 0.4764 mm), and the LM was thinnest (males: 1.472 ± 0.4687 mm; females: 1.286 ± 0.3836 mm) (Fig. 2K, L). These results suggest the lingual cortical bone thickness of at different positions does not show a regular pattern at every age group.

The mass of cancellous bone at different tooth sites within the same age group

To assess the impact of tooth sites on cancellous bone mass, the percentage of cancellous bone was measured across various age groups. In the 20–29 age group, the highest percentage of cancellous bone was observed at the LC in both sexes (males: 35.95 ± 15.89%; females: 31.18 ± 14.49%), while the lowest was at the LM (males: 25.39 ± 16.96%; females: 25.13 ± 14.44%) (Fig. 3A, B).Within the 30–39 age group, the highest values were located at LC in males (36.87 ± 15.75%) and the LA in females (40.17 ± 15.13%), with the lowest at the LM in both sexes (males: 26.17 ± 17.63%; females: 29.83 ± 18.85%)(Fig. 3C, D). In the 40–49 age group, the peak percentages were at the LC in males (41.31 ± 10.69%) and the LA in females (39.95 ± 13.12%), whereas the LM showed the lowest values (males: 29.25 ± 17.34%; females: 33.59 ± 14.00%) (Fig. 3E, F).For the 50–59 age group, the highest percentages were in the LC/LA (males: approx. 42.8%; females: 42.22 ± 11.23%) and the lowest at the LM (males: 34.75 ± 16.62%; females: 27.61 ± 13.84%)(Fig. 3G, H). In the 60–69 age group, the LA showed the highest values (males: 48.55 ± 13.02%; females: 41.49 ± 19.24%), and the LM the lowest (males: 39.20 ± 14.46%; females: 29.26 ± 13.32%) (Fig. 3I, J). Finally, in the 70–79 age group, the LC was highest in males (46.00 ± 15.06%) and the LA highest in females (34.26 ± 14.59%), with the LM being the lowest in both (males: 36.60 ± 15.96%; females: 24.11 ± 12.99 (Fig. 3K, L). These results suggest the percentage of cancellous bone does not show a regular pattern at every age group.

Change of the buccal cortical bone thickness at different age group

To evaluate the effect of age on buccal cortical bone thickness, we measured buccal cortical bone thickness at various age in each site. At LA, thickness progressively decreased with age in males, from 1.558 ± 0.3554 mm in the 20–29 group to 1.021 ± 0.3467 mm in the 70–79 group. In females, values were highest in the 30–39 group (1.506 ± 0.2943 mm) and lowest in the 70–79 group (0.866 ± 0.2622 mm), with a non-linear decline across the decades (Fig. 4A, B). At LC, a clear age-related reduction was evident in males, from 1.684 ± 0.3122 mm (20–29 years) to 1.124 ± 0.3127 mm (70–79 years). In females, thickness was highest in the 30–39 group (1.555 ± 0.2557 mm) and lowest in the 60–69 group (1.010 ± 0.3349 mm) (Fig. 4C, D). At LP, males exhibited a steady decline from 1.822 ± 0.3205 mm (20–29 years) to 1.278 ± 0.3518 mm (70–79 years). Similarly, females showed a gradual reduction, with the highest value in the 20–29 group (1.703 ± 0.2630 mm) and the lowest in the 70–79 group (1.120 ± 0.2909 mm) (Fig. 4E, F). For LM, thickness decreased consistently in males from 2.091 ± 0.4674 mm (20–29 years) to 1.278 ± 0.3518 mm (70–79 years). In females, the highest value was observed in the 30–39 group (2.176 ± 0.4940 mm) and the lowest in the 70–79 group (1.310 ± 0.4708 mm) (Fig. 4G, H). These results suggest the buccal cortical bone thickness exhibits a pattern of gradual reduction with age.

Change of the lingual cortical bone thickness at different age group

The impact of age on lingual cortical bone thickness was evaluated in different tooth sites. At LA, lingual cortical bone thickness was relatively stable in males from 20 to 40 years (approximately 1.88–2.03 mm), peaked notably in the 60–69 group (2.466 ± 6.2551 mm; note high variability), and declined thereafter. In females, values were highest in the 30–39 group (2.211 ± 0.4202 mm) and lowest in the 60–69 group (1.605 ± 0.4808 mm) (Fig. 5A, B). At LC, thickness in males was highest in the 20–29 group (2.102 ± 0.4184 mm) and lowest in the 70–79 group (1.804 ± 0.3485 mm), with intermediate ages showing similar values. In females, peak thickness occurred in the 30–39 group (2.191 ± 0.4082 mm), with the lowest value in the 60–69 group (1.751 ± 0.5058 mm) (Fig. 5C, D). At LP, males showed the highest thickness in the 20–29 group (2.037 ± 0.3747 mm) and the lowest in the 70–79 group (1.720 ± 0.4450 mm). Females exhibited peak values in the 30–39 group (2.098 ± 0.4579 mm) and the lowest in the 70–79 group (1.656 ± 0.4409 mm) (Fig. 5E, F).For LM, a gradual age-related decline was observed in both sexes. In males, thickness decreased from 1.838 ± 0.3828 mm (20–29 years) to 1.472 ± 0.4687 mm (70–79 years). In females, the decrease was from 1.864 ± 0.4055 mm (30–39 years) to 1.286 ± 0.3836 mm (70–79 years) (Fig. 5G, H). These results suggest the lingual cortical bone thickness does not show the decreasing trend with increased age.

Change of the cancellous bone mass at different age group

The effect of age on cancellous bone mass was also assessed in different tooth sites. At LA, percentage of cancellous bone in males increased from 32.24 ± 14.33% (20–29 years) to 48.55 ± 13.02% (60–69 years), then slightly decreased in the oldest group. In females, values rose from 30.44 ± 14.92% (20–29 years) to 42.22 ± 11.23% (50–59 years), with a subsequent decline (Fig. 6A, B). At LC, a progressive age-related increase was evident in males, from 35.95 ± 15.89% (20–29 years) to 47.50 ± 13.78% (60–69 years). In females, the percentage peaked in the 60–69 group (40.53 ± 13.87%) and was lowest in the 70–79 group (30.71 ± 13.36%) (Fig. 6C, D). At LP, percentage of cancellous bone in male increased steadily from 29.30 ± 13.66% (20–29 years) to 43.74 ± 12.55% (60–69 years). Females showed a more variable trend, with the highest value in the 40–49 group (36.99 ± 12.28%) and the lowest in the 20–29 group (27.71 ± 13.98%) (Fig. 6E, F). For LM, percentage of cancellous bone in males increased from 25.39 ± 16.96% (20–29 years) to 39.20 ± 14.46% (60–69 years). In females, the highest value occurred in the 40–49 group (33.59 ± 14.00%) and the lowest in the 70–79 group (24.11 ± 12.99%) (Fig. 6G, H). These results suggest the cancellous bone does not show the decreasing trend with increased age.

Discussion

This study provides a systematic three-dimensional analysis of mandibular alveolar bone remodeling patterns, revealing significant variations associated with tooth site, age, and sex. The findings elucidate distinct remodeling behaviors between cortical and cancellous bone components, offering insights into their underlying biomechanical and physiological mechanisms.

The observed tooth-specific gradient in buccal cortical bone thickness—thinnest at the incisors and thickest at the molars—aligns with the functional demands of mastication. Incisors, subjected to relatively low cutting forces, require less robust cortical support [20]. In contrast, canines and premolars, involved in tearing and grinding, exhibit moderate thickening. The molars, as the primary load-bearing units enduring occlusal forces 3–5 times greater than those on anterior teeth [21, 22], undergo pronounced adaptive thickening to resist deformation [23]. The lack of a clear pattern in lingual cortical bone thickness across tooth positions may be attributed to complex biomechanical influences. In the anterior region, attachments of the genioglossus and suprahyoid muscles may stimulate bone deposition, while in the posterior region, the dominant masseter muscle action on the buccal side and the absence of strong lingual muscle attachments may explain the relative thinning [25]. The minimal correlation between cancellous bone density and tooth position, with significant differences emerging only in the elderly, suggests that trabecular bone remodeling is predominantly influenced by systemic metabolic factors rather than localized biomechanics [24].

Age exerted a profound effect on buccal cortical bone thickness, which peaked in the 20–30 age group and declined steadily after 40. This reduction (e.g., in males from 1.62 ± 0.45 mm to 1.01 ± 0.32 mm) mirrors systemic osteoporotic patterns [26], highlighting buccal cortical bone as a sensitive indicator of age-related resorption under mechanical loading. In contrast, the relative stability of lingual cortical and cancellous bone proportions with age suggests slower remodeling rates, potentially due to mechanical protection from tongue function during swallowing.

Gender differences were consistently observed, with males exhibiting greater buccal and lingual cortical thickness across all tooth positions (by 0.3–0.5 mm and 0.4–0.8 mm, respectively), most notably in the molar region. This may be attributed to a more robust zygomaticomaxillary strut and greater bite forces in males [27, 28], triggering more pronounced adaptive bone formation [29].

Our findings are consistent with and extend previous biomechanical models. The 1.3-fold greater cortical thickness in the mandibular molar region compared to the anterior region, as observed here, corroborates finite element analyses indicating that the molar region bears approximately 62% of masticatory forces [30]. Furthermore, the higher von Mises stress on the buccal aspect (12.5 MPa vs. 5.8 MPa lingually) provides a direct mechanical explanation for its heightened susceptibility to age-related resorption [31]. The annual buccal cortical bone loss rate of 0.025 mm after age 40, observed in our study, resonates with reports of bone density reduction in canine regions, reinforcing the principle of preferential remodeling in stress-concentration zones [32]. At the molecular level, this localized resorption may be driven by age-related dysregulation of the RANKL/OPG pathway and elevated inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-6), which promote osteoclast activity, particularly in mechanically stressed areas [33]. The anatomical protection offered by tongue movements may create a “stress-masking effect” on the lingual cortex, contributing to its slower remodeling rate [34]. The subtle age-related decrease in cancellous bone proportion might represent a compensatory response to cortical thinning [35].

These findings have direct clinical relevance. The thick buccal cortex in the molar region is ideal for implant primary stability, yet surgeons must be cautious of the 30–40% thickness reduction in patients over 70. In the anterior region, where cortical thickness often falls below 1 mm in the elderly, meticulous implant placement planning and guided bone regeneration (GBR) may be necessary to prevent perforation. Regular CBCT monitoring of buccal bone volume is advisable for patients over 40, and early interventions like alveolar ridge preservation should be considered, particularly for postmenopausal women. Although cancellous bone density shows less age-related decline, its maintained proportion (around 60% in the elderly anterior region) indicates preserved potential for implant osseointegration.

Despite the significant findings outlined above, this study still has several limitations. Its cross-sectional design precludes the establishment of causal relationships, necessitating longitudinal studies to validate the dynamics of bone remodeling. The single-center sample may limit the generalizability of the findings. And the sample size, while substantial, could be expanded in future multi-center studies. The absence of systemic bone density and direct occlusal force measurements limited a comprehensive analysis of contributing factors. Future research should integrate molecular biomarkers of bone metabolism with biomechanical modeling to elucidate the mechanisms behind the observed three-dimensional remodeling patterns. Longitudinal follow-up of implant patients could help establish age-specific prosthetic protocols. Also, incorporating multi-regional cohorts and systemic health data will be crucial for building comprehensive oral-systemic bone health models to advance precision dentistry.

Conclusion

​ This study systematically demonstrates that buccal cortical bone thickness in the mandibular alveolar ridge is significantly influenced by both age and tooth position. Specifically, for the same tooth position, buccal cortical bone thickness exhibits an age-dependent reduction, whereas lingual cortical bone thickness and cancellous bone volume remain relatively stable. These findings provide a rationale for studying the mechanisms of mechanical bone regulation. From a clinical perspective, this study suggests that during guided bone regeneration (GBR), the buccal aspect may require more substantial augmentation, and implant placement should be biased toward the linguistically stable bone.
