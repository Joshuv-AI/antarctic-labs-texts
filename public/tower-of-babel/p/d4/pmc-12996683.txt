Safety, feasibility and impact of a 12-week yoga program in adults Fontan patients: a pilot study
Authors: Ashish H Shah, Pradeepkumar Charla, Kohsaku Goto, Purvi A Shah, Erwin N Oechslin, John Granton, Rachel M Wald
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundReduced exercise capacity and compromised pulmonary function are common findings following Fontan palliation. Although yoga practice has been associated with improved respiratory function in individuals with acquired heart disease, little is known about the potential impact in the Fontan population.MethodsWe assessed the safety, feasibility, and efficacy of a 12-week in-person yoga program in adult Fontan patients. Participants with stable Fontan physiology and without limiting comorbidities were included. Study patients were encouraged to continue yoga practice regularly after study completion. Study outcomes included cardiopulmonary exercise testing (CPET) and pulmonary function testing (PFT). Comparisons were made within group (pre/post intervention) at 12 weeks and between group (study patients compared with non-intervention controls) at last follow-up.ResultsTen patients were enrolled and 5 completed the program. No adverse events were reported. There were no statistically significant changes in CPET or PFT measures after the 12-week intervention. At last follow-up [median 77 months (IQR 61–91)], delta forced vital capacity (FVC) differed between the yoga and usual care groups (−0.1L [-0.1, 0.2]) versus −0.2L [-0.4, −0.2], p = 0.032). There were no other significant between-group differences.ConclusionsA 12-week yoga program was well-tolerated in adult Fontan patients. While there was no apparent short-term improvement in CPET or PFT parameters, regular yoga practice may contribute to stabilization of FVC at longer term follow-up. Further studies are warranted to evaluate the clinical impact of yoga post Fontan palliation.Keywords: Fontan circulation, Yoga intervention, Lung functions, Exercise capacity

Introduction

The Fontan procedure is a surgical strategy used to palliate patients with univentricular physiology. Although the Fontan circulation (FC) restores oxygen saturations and reduces volume loading, it inevitably increases lifetime risk of multiorgan dysfunction [1]. Given the absence of a subpulmonary ventricle, the FC relies on passive pulmonary blood flow driven by chronically elevated central venous pressures and negative intra-thoracic pressures (ITP) during inspiration [2,3]. Because of the heightened dependence on optimal cardiorespiratory interactions to enable efficient functioning of the FC, impairment in respiratory mechanics can adversely affect cardiac output and exercise tolerance.​ Furthermore, adults with a FC often exhibit reduced respiratory muscle strength [4,5], leading to compromised pulmonary function and impaired exercise capacity. Therefore, optimizing respiratory function in this population may translate into improved clinical outcomes.

Accumulating evidence suggests that yoga benefits patients with acquired heart disease, including those with acute myocardial infarction [6] and heart failure [7]. Targeted yogic breathing exercises can strengthen respiratory muscles and positively influence cardiovascular health among patients with biventricular physiology [8]. Based on these observations we hypothesized that yoga may benefit patients with a FC who specifically rely on optimal cardio-respiratory interactions.

Methods

Study design and participants

This prospective pilot study was conducted at a single tertiary center and patients were recruited from our ambulatory cardiology clinics. Adults with a FC (≥18 years) were eligible if they could attend 12 consecutive weekly in-person yoga sessions led by a certified and experienced yoga instructor. Exclusion criteria included decompensated FC failure, recent hospitalization (≥1 day), pulmonary disease (chronic obstructive pulmonary disease or asthma), or inability to complete the 12-week program (Supplementary Table S1). We sought to evaluate (1) within group differences after the 12-week yoga intervention and (2) differences between study patients who completed the yoga intervention compared with a control group who received usual care. The study received institutional research ethics board approval, and written informed consent was obtained (study number 15-9818).

Intervention group

This 12-week, in-person yoga program included three distinct breathing exercises, each performed in two 5-min sessions (10 min per exercise; 30 min in total; Supplementary Fig. 1).

1.Alternate nostril breathing (anulom-vilom) to facilitate balanced breathing.

Alternate nostril breathing (anulom-vilom) to facilitate balanced breathing.

2.Forceful exhalations (kapalbhati) followed by passive inhalations, to strengthen abdominal and diaphragmatic muscles.

Forceful exhalations (kapalbhati) followed by passive inhalations, to strengthen abdominal and diaphragmatic muscles.

3.Forceful and rapid respiration (bhastrika) to enhance respiratory endurance.

Forceful and rapid respiration (bhastrika) to enhance respiratory endurance.

In addition to the in-person sessions, participants were encouraged to practice at home for 30 minutes, 4 times per week. Compliance was monitored via attendance and self-reported logs throughout the 12-session program. Pulmonary function testing (PFT) and cardiopulmonary exercise testing (CPET) were performed at baseline and following the yoga intervention (pre/post intervention) (Supplementary methods). After program completion, participants were encouraged to continue yoga practice at home; CPET and PFT studies were then scheduled as per usual care as directed by their cardiologists. Serial CPET and PFT results were recorded and delta (Δ) measurement comparisons were made between baseline and last follow-up.

Usual care group

Individuals who qualified for the yoga intervention and consented for the study, but did not participate in the 12-week yoga program and did not report participation in any yoga activities, were studied as a usual care comparison group. They received standard care as directed by their cardiologists. Serial CPET and PFT tests were recorded and Δ comparisons were made at baseline and last follow-up.

Outcomes

All pertinent clinical data were extracted from electronic medical records. Safety was assessed based on occurrence of significant adverse events (Supplementary Table S2). Feasibility was assessed based on the reasons for discontinuing the yoga program. Impact was measured based on Δ in CPET and PFT measurements.

Statistical analysis

Measurements are reported as mean with standard deviation, or median with interquartile (IQR) range, as appropriate. Within-group changes were analyzed using Wilcoxon signed rank test, and between-group comparisons with the Mann-Whitney test. A two-tailed p-value <0.05 was considered significant. Prism 10 was used for statistical analysis (GraphPad Software, Inc., La Jolla, CA, USA).

Results

Safety and feasibility of yoga intervention

Ten patients were enrolled and five completed the full 12-week program. Five patients enrolled but failed to complete the program due to issues unrelated to cardiovascular status or to the practice of yoga (unable to arrange travel [n = 4] and an unrelated sports injury [n = 1]). Characteristics of the yoga and usual care groups were comparable (Table 1). All study participants were asymptomatic throughout the study. No significant adverse events occurred.

Within group comparisons: pre/post intervention

Among patients who completed the yoga program, there were no significant changes in Δ CPET or PFT parameters after the 12-week intervention as compared with baseline.

Between group comparisons: yoga intervention versus usual care

The yoga and usual care groups were compared at median follow-up of 77 months (IQR 61, 91) from baseline testing. Three out of five patients in the yoga group (60%) reported continued yoga practice, and four patients (80%) reported participating in regular aerobic activities at the time of their last clinical follow-up visit. In the usual care group, no individuals practiced yoga; three (60%) reported participating in regular aerobic activity. Following comparison of Δ CPET and PFT parameters between groups, absolute FVC(L) demonstrated a greater decline (ΔFVC) in the usual care as compared with the yoga group which appeared to be relatively stable (ΔFVC -0.2 [−0.4, −0.2] versus 0.1 [−0.1, 0.2], p = 0.032) (Table 2). In the usual care group, within group ΔFVC decline trended towards statistical significance (3.9 versus 3.6L, p = 0.063, while there was no change in the yoga group 2.9 versus 2.9L, p = 0.813).

Discussion

This pilot study demonstrates that a structured, 12-week yoga program centred on breathing exercises appears to be feasible and safe for adult Fontan patients in the short-term. While there did not appear to be any apparent impact of yoga on CPET and PFT measurements at 12-weeks, at longer-term follow-up patients who learned and practiced yoga had relatively preserved FVC compared to the usual care group. These findings point toward the potential salutary benefit in yoga practice for prevention of the progressive decline in pulmonary function in Fontan patients which is known to occur over time [5]. Although 50% of consented patients did not commit to participation, reasons were unrelated to cardiac status. Future studies may benefit from improved participation if virtual sessions are offered, in whole or in part, for the yoga program.

The rationale for focusing on respiratory muscle strengthening through yoga relates to physiological premises unique to the Fontan population, including restrictive lung disease and high residual lung volumes due to repeated sternotomies along with diaphragmatic impairment [9]. Inspiratory muscle weakness can compromise the ability to generate sufficiently negative ITP, thereby impairing venous return and elevating central venous pressure, which may translate into multi-organ dysfunction. A decline in FVC was documented over a 10-year period in pediatric Fontan patients [5], suggesting that deteriorating pulmonary function is associated with advancing age. Maintaining or enhancing respiratory muscle strength is therefore critical in this patient population. Prior interventions, including inspiratory muscle training, can improve ventilatory efficiency, resting cardiac output [10] and exercise capacity [11], and we have demonstrated that non-invasive biphasic ventilation can improve pulmonary and systemic output in Fontan patients [12]. Collectively these findings support the potential role for yoga in preserving pulmonary function, maintaining pulmonary blood flow, and sustaining overall cardiac output [13].

Limitations of this pilot study include small sample size and non-randomized design. In addition, a formal assessment of quality of life as well as Borg scale scores during CPET were not collected. Patient reported outcomes and/or feedback from participants could further enhance our understanding of yoga in the Fontan population in future studies. Nevertheless, findings are hypothesis-generating and should be confirmed in larger cohorts with a wider range of ages with a focus on relevant patient-reported and clinical outcomes.

Conclusion

This pilot study suggests that a 12-week yoga program may be safe and feasible for adult Fontan patients. While short-term impact remains inconclusive, regular yoga practice may help preserve pulmonary function, specifically FVC, over longer-term follow-up. Further study is warranted to clarify the relationship between yoga practice and clinical outcomes.
