Hemopneumothorax After Carotid Endarterectomy
Authors: Simona Formánková, Michael Rousek, Jiří Pudil, Radek Pohnán
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTSpontaneous hemopneumothorax (SHP) is a rare condition characterized by the accumulation of air and blood in the pleural cavity without prior trauma. We present a case of a patient who underwent carotid endarterectomy for carotid artery stenosis. Two days postoperatively, she developed sudden respiratory distress, hypotension, tachycardia, and a decrease in hemoglobin levels. A chest X‐ray revealed a massive left‐sided hemopneumothorax. A chest tube was inserted, but no active bleeding source was identified on subsequent imaging. Despite initial stabilization, the patient required intubation and underwent video‐assisted thoracoscopic surgery for residual hemothorax and empyema. She fully recovered. In this case report, we provide an overview of the diagnostic and treatment options for SHP. As SHP is a potentially life‐threatening condition, we emphasize the importance of including it in the differential diagnosis of sudden patient deterioration. In our case, the patient had undergone vascular surgery; however, the cause of hemorrhagic shock was unexpectedly SHP.

Introduction

Spontaneous hemopneumothorax (SHP) is an extremely rare condition that can lead to hypovolemic shock in 30%–40% of cases and may be life‐threatening [1, 2]. The definition of SHP is the accumulation of more than 400 mL of blood and air in the pleural cavity without previous trauma or any apparent cause [3]. The true incidence of SHP is currently unknown. Affected patients are typically male and smokers [3]. Patients with SHP often present with shortness of breath, tachycardia, hypotension, decreased hemoglobin levels, decreased oxygen saturation, and chest pain.

Spontaneous hemopneumothorax is most likely caused by the tearing of an aberrant vessel between the parietal pleura and adhesion bullae due to lung collapse [1]. A pneumothorax after mechanical ventilation is not uncommon [4]. However, during our literature review, we found no cases describing a hemopneumothorax occurring after mechanical ventilation.

Treatment options include surgical and non‐surgical approaches, with video‐assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) being the preferred method [4]. Early diagnosis is crucial, and SHP should be considered in the differential diagnosis. Our case report highlights the vital role of chest X‐ray and computed tomography (CT) in the early diagnosis and management of thoracic hemorrhage. The systematic review of the literature provides information on the etiology, epidemiology, and treatment options for SHP.

Case Presentation

A 74‐year‐old female with a past medical history significant for poorly controlled hypertension, diabetes mellitus, dyslipidemia, ischemic heart disease, and tobacco use presented to the Emergency Department with a 72‐h history of expressive aphasia and central facial nerve paresis. Additionally, she exhibited weakness in the distal left upper extremity. Her prior medical history includes a myocardial infarction in 2017 and iliofemoral bypass surgery in 2018 for critical limb ischemia.

A non‐contrast computed tomography (CT) scan showed a small hypodense lesion on the convexity of the left frontal lobe. CT angiography demonstrated 70% stenosis of the left common carotid artery, consistent with the North American Symptomatic Endarterectomy Carotid Trial criteria. Consequently, an urgent carotid endarterectomy was performed. The patient's early postoperative course was uneventful; however, on postoperative day two, a sudden clinical deterioration occurred.

On reassessment, the patient was alert and oriented, with a blood pressure of 95/60 mmHg, a sinus tachycardia with a frequency of 120 beats per minute, and a respiratory rate of 20 breaths per minute. Her oxygen saturation was 85% on 4 L per minute (L/min) of supplemental oxygen. There were no signs of bleeding from the surgical wound or surrounding soft tissues of the neck. Rectal examination was negative for occult blood. Auscultation of the chest revealed diminished breath sounds over the left hemithorax. The abdominal examination was unremarkable. The patient remained in a semi‐seated position due to dyspnea and complained of chest pain.

Diffrential Diagnosis, Investigations and Treatment

Upon clinical evaluation, hemorrhagic shock was strongly suspected. Given the recent surgical intervention, the primary differential diagnosis was a complication related to the carotid endarterectomy. In the light of the patient's chest pain and dyspnea, acute myocardial infarction or unstable angina pectoris was also considered, particularly given her history of prior myocardial infarction. Continuous ECG monitoring revealed no pathological changes, ruling out ischemic events. Pulmonary embolism was another consideration, particularly due to the immobilization and abrupt onset of symptoms. However, typical ECG findings such as deep S waves in lead I, deep Q waves in lead III, T wave inversions in lead III, or right bundle branch block were absent, and D‐dimer levels were negative.

After excluding these possibilities through clinical assessment, a chest X‐ray was performed.

The imaging revealed a massive left hemothorax (Figure 1). A chest drain was promptly inserted, draining 900 mL of blood and air. Volume resuscitation was initiated, and the patient demonstrated a favorable clinical response to initial fluid resuscitation. The bleeding from the chest tube stopped spontaneously. Laboratory tests revealed anemia, with a hemoglobin level decreasing from 132 g/L to 85 g/L (reference range: 120–168 g/L). Hepatic and renal function remained within normal parameters.

Chest X‐ray. A massive left‐sided fluidothorax is present (white arrow).

Following initial hemodynamic stabilization, a contrast CT scan was performed, which excluded postoperative hemorrhage as a complication of endarterectomy (Figure 2). No convincing source of bleeding in the chest was identified. The chest drain was correctly positioned, and the imaging revealed a small residual hemothorax.

A CT scan of the chest. A complication at the site of the surgery was ruled out. No soft tissue haematoma was present (white arrow).

The patient remained hemodynamically stable with no signs of ongoing bleeding. Empirical broad‐spectrum antibiotics were initiated, and she was admitted to the Intensive Care Unit for high‐flow oxygen therapy. On postoperative day five, her clinical condition deteriorated, with the onset of dyspnea, pyrexia, and elevated inflammatory markers. Progressive respiratory failure ensued, requiring endotracheal intubation. Repeated chest CT imaging demonstrated dystelectasis and residual hemothorax.

Video‐assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) was undertaken, revealing pleural adhesion, blood clots in the pleural cupula and lower lung fields, and empyema. Surgical management included lower lobe decortication, evacuation of the blood clots, empyema drainage, and thorough pleural lavage. Intraoperatively, no pulmonary bullae were identified. The patient was subsequently managed with targeted antimicrobial therapy based on intraoperative findings and cultures.

Outcome and Follow‐Up

The chest drain was removed on postoperative day seven following VATS, and her subsequent hospital stay was uneventful. Serial follow‐up chest X‐rays demonstrated complete resolution of previous changes. The patient was discharged 19 days after undergoing carotid endarterectomy. Although the precise perioperative etiology of SHP remains unclear, retrospective review of the initial CT scan identified small, vascularized pulmonary bullae (Figure 3), which may have contributed to the event. No complications or adverse events were observed during the follow‐up period.

Discussion

Although the precise etiology of spontaneous hemopneumothorax remains incompletely understood, bleeding into the chest cavity and hemothorax development can complicate 0.5%–12% of pneumothoraxes [2, 5]. SHP is more frequently observed in males and individuals with a history of smoking [5]. The pathophysiology of SHP is primarily attributed to the three mechanisms: (1) hemorrhage resulting from a torn adhesion between the parietal and visceral pleurae; (2) bleeding of the visceral pleura from rupture of vascularized bullae or lung parenchyma; (3) bleeding resulting from a torn congenital aberrant vessel following pneumothorax‐induced lung collapse [1].

Subpleural bullae are identified in 81%–90% of patients via CT imaging or intraoperative findings [6, 7]. Contributing factors to the formation of pleural porosity, blebs, and bullae may include distal bronchial tree anomalies, distal airway inflammation, malnutrition, and local ischaemia or connective tissue disorders. Increased pleural porosity secondary to inflammation may be another mechanism for the occurrence of the SHP [6, 7, 8, 9].

Tearing of aberrant vessels between the parietal pleura and adhesion bullae due to lung collapse is the most frequent cause of bleeding in patients with SHP [10, 11]. These vessels have a minimum amount of muscle fiber in their tunica media, and with the negative pressure in the thoracic cavity, they create bleeding that is difficult to stop [10]. Smoking has been strongly linked to SHP, likely due to increased production of inflammatory cytokines such as interleukin‐8, which can induce lung parenchyma changes such as porosity, bullae, or aberrant vessel formation [12]. Notably, in 29.6%–35% of cases, no source of bleeding is identified [7, 10].

The incidence of barotrauma is significantly increased in mechanically ventilated patients, especially those with underlying pulmonary pathology. Pneumothorax is among the most common complications associated with barotrauma [13]. In the present case, tearing of aberrant pleuroparenchymal vessels following lung collapse secondary to barotrauma‐induced pneumothorax represents a plausible mechanism for the development of SHP.

Currently, no standardized treatment guidelines for SHP exist. Therapeutic options include chest tube insertion, video‐assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS), or thoracotomy. VATS or thoracotomy is generally indicated in the presence of ongoing bleeding exceeding 100 mL per hour, an initial output of more than 1000 mL, persistent air leak, incomplete lung re‐expansion, or recurrent pneumothorax [14]. In patients not meeting these criteria, conservative management with chest drainage may be appropriate. However, early VATS may offer distinct advantages, including reduced duration of hospitalization and drainage, decreased blood loss and transfusion requirements, and expedited recovery [7, 15]. Additional benefits of VATS include direct visualization and control of bleeding sources, efficient evacuation of clotted hemothorax, potential resection of bullae, and atraumatic placement of chest drains under visual guidance [7].
