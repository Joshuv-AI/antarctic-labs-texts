Inuit experiences of and expertise on the 21st-century tuberculosis epidemic in Nunavik, Quebec: a qualitative community-based participatory study
Authors: Ben Geboe, Glenda Sandy, Daphne Tooktoo, Sophie Tukalak, Stephanie Law, Shirley White-Dupuis, Qiallak Nappaaluk, Nellie Aliqu, Eva Quananack, Maggie Emudluk, Larry Watt, Pasa Mangiok, Anna Dunn-Suen, Madeline Yaaka, Natasha Ita MacDonald, Faiz Ahmad Khan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:In Nunavik, the Inuit lands in Quebec, the incidence of tuberculosis has been rising and is currently 1000 times that seen among non-foreign-born Quebec residents. To inform tuberculosis policies aligned with Inuit self-determination, we sought to explore Nunavimmiut (Inuit of Nunavik) experiences with tuberculosis care.Methods:We undertook a community-based participatory action research project in an Indigenous research methodology (IRM) framework. From 2022 to 2023, Indigenous researchers (First Nations and Inuit) conducted interviews and focus groups with Nunavimmiut about tuberculosis and health care experiences. We also interviewed health care workers. In our data analyses, we used constructivist grounded theory integrated with IRM to identify constructs. Findings derived from Nunavimmiut-reported perspectives and experiences informed calls for policy and service changes.Results:We interviewed 156 Inuit (37% aged ≤ 35 yr, 61% women), in 5 Nunavik communities and in Montréal, and 21 health care workers. Nunavimmiut shared a strong desire to protect individual and community health, which they reported was undermined by under-resourced health services. Contemporary tuberculosis care itself was identified as a source of hardship, with contributing factors including displacement outside of community, isolation, directly observed therapy, fear of coercive measures, and threat of culturally unsafe experiences in health care settings. Information gaps undermined agency and caused heightened anxiety and stigma related to tuberculosis. The rarity of the Inuktitut language in health services contrasted with its predominance in community life. Health care workers echoed these concerns. Recommendations by Nunavimmiut for improving tuberculosis care were grouped into 7 calls to action: increasing Inuit control over services and data; providing person-centred care; increasing local services to minimize displacement; using community-wide screening, adapted locally; training and hiring more Inuit health care workers; reducing stigma; and implementing Inuit-led cultural safety training for health care workers.Interpretation:Nunavimmiut reported experiencing program-centred tuberculosis care, requiring Inuit to adapt to services that are shaped by resource scarcity. By contrast, Inuit recommended person-centred tuberculosis care that is supportive and responsive to community needs. Changes will necessitate reconciliation- and decolonization-aligned policy changes and increased resources.

Tuberculosis continues to disproportionately harm the Inuit more than non-Inuit people in Canada.1,2 Canada remains far from achieving its 2030 deadline for eliminating tuberculosis from Inuit Nunangat,1,3 the 4 Inuit regions in Canada. The situation is particularly dire in Nunavik, the Inuit region in northern Quebec. In 2025, Nunavik experienced a record number of Inuit diagnosed with active tuberculosis.4 Compared with non-foreign-born Quebecers, Nunavimmiut (for this study, Nunavimmiut refers to Inuit from Nunavik) face 1000 times greater risk of active tuberculosis.5,6

Health surveillance monitoring describing rising levels of tuberculosis infection in Inuit Nunangat during the 21st century starkly contrasts with an absence of documentation on Inuit experiences during this contemporary epidemic. Respecting Inuit self-determination over health necessitates development of policies and services grounded in accurate and culturally meaningful health assessments.7 Addressing health inequities and ensuring appropriate health services are among the Truth and Reconciliation Commission’s Calls to Action.8 Inuit Tapiriit Kanatami, the national representational organization for Inuit in Canada, advocates for alignment of tuberculosis policies with Inuit self-determination.9 For Nunavik, we sought to inform such policies through a community-based participatory research study to explore Nunavimmiut experiences with tuberculosis care. The study was co-conceived by regional health authorities, practitioners, and research partners working in Nunavik. Nunavimmiut are 14 000 strong10 and account for around one-quarter of the population of Inuit Nunangat.

Methods

The strain of Mycobacterium tuberculosis circulating today in Nunavik was first introduced through interactions with European or European-descended settlers in the early 20th century.11 This led to a major epidemic with high mortality.12–14 Federal interventions to address the disease in the mid-20th century included sending Inuit thousands of kilometres away to sanitoria in southern Canada, causing substantial psychological trauma and social disturbance.9,15,16 Large-scale testing and treatment campaigns for active and latent tuberculosis led to falling rates through the late 20th century.9 The rising incidence since the early 21st century is considered to be driven by adverse social determinants of health, including insufficient housing17 and availability of health services.9 The incidence has been rising in Nunavik since 2003, with sharper increases in the last 10 years, including record numbers in 2023, 2024, and 2025.4,18

Nunavimmiut live in 14 villages geographically isolated from the rest of Quebec (Figure 1). More than 95% of Nunavimmiut speak Inuktitut,19 and most participate in traditional land-based activities.20 Negotiations increasing Inuit self-governance are under way.21 The Nunavik Regional Board of Health and Social Services (NRBHSS), in partnership with Nunavik’s 2 health networks (Tulattavik and Inuulitsivik), organizes and provides health services. Although these institutions have Inuit executives and boards of directors, most health care administrators, policymakers, and nurses, and all physicians, are non-Inuit, and service delivery must comply with Quebec government directives.

Map of Nunavik (shaded area), Quebec.

Each of the 14 communities has an outpatient clinic (often referred to as “clinic,” “nursing,” or “nursing station”) staffed by nurses and interpreters, with general practitioners also present intermittently (10 communities) or continuously (4 communities).

Kuujjuaq and Puvirnituq also have in-patient facilities (hospitals). At the time of the study, radiography was routinely available at the 2 hospitals and intermittently in 4 communities. Since 2021, recurrent and severe shortages of health care personnel and resources in Nunavik have attracted media attention.22,23

We developed our study in 2019, with interviews and focus groups undertaken between March 2022 and March 2023, after travel restrictions related to the COVID-19 pandemic were lifted. In Nunavik, accessing important aspects of tuberculosis care often requires travel to 1 of the 2 villages with a hospital to get chest radiographs (for contact evaluation, screening, diagnosis, and any tuberculosis-related follow-up), or for inpatient isolation. In other communities, there is limited access to radiography owing to both a lack of equipment and technicians. Tuberculin skin testing and sputum collection are available in each community, with specimens undergoing rapid polymerase chain reaction testing at either regional hospital, while smears and cultures are done in Montréal. Directly observed therapy (DOT), wherein a clinic worker watches someone ingest their medication, is mandatory for most patients and mostly performed at the clinic, with alternative locations offered in certain circumstances. Anyone diagnosed with active tuberculosis in Nunavik must sign a contract informing them that tuberculosis treatment is mandatory by law in Quebec and acknowledging that court or police intervention may be used to enforce testing and treatment (Appendix 1, available at www.cmaj.ca/lookup/doi/10.1503/cmaj.251824/tab-related-content).

Overview of Inuit-centred research approach

Our research was guided by the 4 Rs — respect, relevance, reciprocity, and responsibility — initially described in the context of Indigenous higher education and since adapted as Indigenous research methodology (IRM).24,25 The research addressed an Inuit-determined priority topic.9,26 At the project’s inception, we established a steering committee of Inuit with experience in community or health leadership who ensured that Inuit values guided the research team. Through relationship-building in the region, the steering committee expanded from 3 to 7 people and named itself Puvaqatsianirmut (The Committee for Healthy Lungs) by the time data collection began. We considered that ascertaining Inuit experiences was necessary for identifying Inuit-informed policies. Inuit and Indigenous researchers facilitated Inuit access and meaningful research participation through shared identities and commonalities of lived experiences with participants, use of Inuktitut, and community affiliations — all contributing to creating safe spaces for data collection and accurate data synthesis. As part of our IRM, we used constructivist grounded theory27 to prioritize Inuit voices and permitted participant responses to reshape data gathering, analysis, and theory development. Constructivist grounded theory is general in approach and targets participant data as an opportunity for inclusion; however, the operational aspects of going to Nunavik with Inuit researchers and conferring frequently with Inuit representatives were key aspects that arose out of the IRM. In line with the 4 Rs and principles of respecting Inuit self-determination over research and health,28 and aligned with reciprocity and responsibility, we member-checked the results with the Inuit community members, Inuit leadership, and Inuit health board representatives before sharing with other partners, and to broader audiences, in Inuktitut and English.

Positionality statement

The mostly Indigenous core research team included 3 Nunavik Inuit peer researchers, 1 of whom is an academic professor; all had Inuktitut fluency. They ensured meaningful integration of community concerns well beyond translation. Core research teammates included a Cree-Naskapi nurse advisor to the NRBHSS, a Dakota Sioux social worker, and 2 racialized minority settler Canadians. Throughout the study, 2 principal investigators (G.S. and F.A.K.) supported Nunavik health services and clinicians in tuberculosis management and clinical care, generating additional insights into health service delivery challenges. The all-Inuit steering committee, with representation from villages across the region, brought extensive community and health leadership experience.

Community and participant recruitment

Four communities experiencing recent or ongoing tuberculosis outbreaks were identified and prioritized by Nunavik health authorities as data-collection sites. A fifth community, home to 2 of the Inuit researchers, was added per their recommendation. Any community resident aged 14 years or older was eligible for enrolment, and we used targeted (purposive)29,30 recruitment to favour enrolment of Inuit community members and, in later visits, of those aged 35 years and younger. In a second phase, we recruited health care workers to collect perspectives from the health care sector. Demonstrating respect and relevance, Indigenous researchers coordinated convenience sampling through outreach and recruitment in close coordination with local Inuit leadership and health committees, along with key community organizations (e.g., family houses, youth centres, Elder homes, hunter support programs, and sewing centres). Inuit researchers conducted outreach on local radio, with daily announcements in Inuktitut, and created posters in syllabics (Inuktitut orthography) that they placed in local stores. Culturally relevant activities to further community engagement included craft workshops, community feasts, and raffle events promoting outreach. Community member recruitment relied on self-referral and consisted of snowball sampling — that is, word-of-mouth referrals by community members, leaders, and clinic staff. We used direct referral and snowball recruitment31 to enrol any health care worker (e.g., nurse, doctor, manager) with prior or current experience in tuberculosis care in Nunavik. All participants were offered an honorarium out of respect for their time.

Data collection

We undertook interviews and focus groups — the former when people presented alone to enrol, the latter when they presented together and expressed a preference for doing a focus group. Topics included experiences with tuberculosis (personally and in their community), care pathways, and health services. Culturally grounded data collection privileged participant storytelling32,33 with semistructured interview and focus group guides (Appendix 2, available at www.cmaj.ca/lookup/doi/10.1503/cmaj.251824/tab-related-content). The guides were developed with iterative input from representatives of the NRBHSS and our steering committee to comprehensively cover experiences related to tuberculosis in one’s community, with separate guides for community members who had never experienced tuberculosis and those who had experienced tuberculosis themselves (or were parents of children who had experienced tuberculosis). Because our researchers favoured conversational interviews and focus groups as culturally relevant and more likely to lead to insightful discussions, the interview guides served more as a reference list the researchers looked at to ensure coverage of key topics. We adapted interview questions throughout the data-collection process, based on Indigenous researcher experiences and reflections, participant responses, and ongoing tuberculosis outbreaks, during which health board and community representatives requested we expedite data collection in a specific community with a new outbreak. We fostered cultural safety by centring relationality33: Inuit and First Nations researchers (B.G., D.T., N.I.M., and S.T.) led enrolment, interviews, and focus groups of Inuit participants, with activities undertaken in familiar settings (e.g., community centres, family homes, cooperative hotels, and participant homes), and ensuring Inuktitut could predominate in the conversations. Interviewers offered information about themselves as part of natural conversation and sharing throughout the interviews, including their Inuit or First Nations community affiliation, their motivation for doing this research (including personal anecdotes when relevant), and their own personal or community experiences with tuberculosis. Most health care worker participants were interviewed by telephone or videoconference (Zoom or Microsoft Teams by S.L.) with a minority interviewed in Nunavik (B.G., D.T., and A.D.-S.). Informed consent was obtained before all interviews, including an option to opt out of audio recording and withdraw from participation without consequence. Audio recordings were transcribed and those in Inuktitut were translated to English by research team members (D.T. and S.L.). Interviewers took summary notes and key quotations verbatim for unrecorded interviews.

Data analysis

Following Indigenous scholars Shawn Wilson,34 Margaret Kovach,35 and Linda Tuhiwai Smith,36 we applied an inductive approach using constructivist grounded theory as outlined by Kathy Charmaz.27 Kovach explicitly describes how IRM and constructivist grounded theory relate through promotion of community contributions in the development of data gathering, analysis and theory creation, coding, and theory development.35 Interviewers themselves (B.G., D.T., S.T., N.I.M., and S.L.) conducted initial coding and direct coding with Inuktitut transcripts (D.T. and S.T., initially supported by non-Inuktitut-fluent team members who used the English translations) to identify initial themes. These were iteratively reshaped through research team discourse (including reflections on lived experiences from the Indigenous team members and team reflections on health care service challenges occurring during the ongoing outbreaks). In addition, themes were reshaped by feedback during member-checking with communities, undertaken through in-person discussions with our all-Inuit steering committee; through local FM radio call-in shows, in which we discussed findings in communities where data collection had taken place; through in-person and video meetings to review findings with Inuit health board leaders; through in-person presentations to a regional committee addressing tuberculosis, which included community mayors and was co-chaired by coauthors (Q.N. and G.S.); and through presentations with discussion of findings at board of director meetings of health boards. The finalized constructs describing current Inuit experiences in relation to tuberculosis health care were verified with steering committee members, Inuit health leadership, and Inuit health and hospital board representatives. Data from the fourth construct were further analyzed and operationalized into community-derived and constructed policy directions — that is, calls to action — by 2 of the senior authors (N.I.M. and F.A.K.). Visualizations to summarize the recommendations were created by an Inuit artist (P.M.), supported by other team member (A.D.-S. and F.A.K.).

Ethics approval

The research ethics board of the McGill University Health Centre provided ethics overview of the project, as no Nunavik-based institutional review board exists.

Results

We provide a description of participants, interviews, and focus groups. We then present the overarching paradigm about health care and tuberculosis, followed by the constructs and representative quotations upon which the paradigm is built.

Participants

Between March 2022 and March 2023, we interviewed 156 Nunavimmiut in 5 Nunavik communities and in Montréal and 21 non-Inuit health care workers (Table 1). Most Inuit participants were women (61%), more than one-third were in the age range commonly referred to as “youth” in Nunavik (≤ 35 yr), and 82 (53%) had experience with active or latent tuberculosis (either their own or in children they cared for). Among health care workers, we interviewed 7 doctors and 13 nurses, with experience in Nunavik ranging from less than 1 year to more than 10 years providing front-line tuberculosis care (in clinical or public health roles). Thirty-six of 80 interviews and 20 of 32 focus groups were conducted in Inuktitut. In 20 of 80 interviews and 5 of 32 focus groups, participants and at least 1 interviewer were fluent in Inuktitut while choosing to converse in English.

Overview of qualitative results

Nunavimmiut shared extensive and vivid descriptions about contemporary experiences related to tuberculosis, medical services, and life in communities affected by tuberculosis. Strikingly, several interviews emphasized contemporary tuberculosis care and interventions as sources of hardship. Health care workers acknowledged the hardship tuberculosis interventions bring to patients and families. We heard inadequate health care resourcing described as a key structural determinant of hardship-within-care, manifesting in participant reports (described in detail in constructs 1 through 4), and observed by research team members (F.A.K. and G.S.) in their roles as consultants or advisors to health centres during the study period. Integrating key findings, we described 2 contrasting paradigms for tuberculosis care, depicted in Figure 2. On the left — or backward-facing arrow — is the contemporary paradigm that we term “program-centred”: an approach seeking to minimize the burden that providing tuberculosis services places on health services. Program-centred care is driven by resource availability, expects Inuit to adapt to the services offered, and does not seek community feedback. The right — or forward-facing arrow — indicates the Inuit desire for tuberculosis care in alignment with community values and Inuit identity and actively seeking to alleviate tuberculosis-related hardship by adapting to meet individual and community needs, supporting Inuit agency, and building Inuktitut proficiency into services, consistent with “person-centred” care. Rather than making Inuit fit into existing services, person-centred and Inuit-driven care would adapt services to meet Inuit needs. The validity of the program-centred versus person-centred paradigm resonated with Inuit in numerous forums, including cross-regional representation (mayors’ meetings, health executives, boards of directors, and our steering committee).

The following examination describes 4 constructs identified from the data leading to the above theory, with illustrative quotations. Only certain participant characteristics are listed, to maintain anonymity.

Construct 1: Nunavimmiut express a strong desire for healthy communities amid chronically underresourced health services

Participants discussed valuing one’s own health and individual responsibility toward community well-being (Box 1). Leaders and other community members described valuing tuberculosis elimination. We did not hear outright rejection of the importance of biomedical treatment or diagnostics, though participants spoke about how medical care was difficult (as described in construct 2).

Participants described that medical resource constraints ran counter to the community sense of urgency surrounding tuberculosis (Box 1). They reported that limitations undermined the deployment of needed interventions and efforts to ease challenges in tuberculosis care. Participants described that resource-constrained contexts led to deprioritizing tuberculosis or underestimating its threat. For example, a team member reported hearing that health care workers in a community were reluctant to launch screening for active tuberculosis because clinic resources were overburdened by the existing case load.

Construct 2: Nunavimmiut report experiences of serious undue hardships during tuberculosis care

Participants reported various sources of hardship within tuberculosis diagnosis and care pathways, encompassing disruption to people’s daily lives; these included social and economic vulnerability, potential impacts on traditional livelihoods, cultural safety concerns, and feeling loss of autonomy over one’s body or one’s children due to the potential of mandated treatment through court or police involvement (Box 2). Participants described that community displacements to undergo screening, testing, and isolation disrupt lives and compromise confidentiality, as intervillage travel is a highly public event.

Participants reported that stringent DOT and potential for court or police intervention in the case of noncompliance contributed to hardships (Box 2).

Respondents also described difficult health care experiences in general (not specific to tuberculosis; Box 2). Participants reported that health care contexts could not uphold cultural safety because of community or personal experiences with discrimination, dismissiveness toward patient requests, and a lack of health care worker understanding of culture. Health care workers also shared these concerns.

In contrast to the dominance of Inuktitut in day-to-day life in Nunavik, the language was reported to be inadequately present in health care contexts, leading a First Nations researcher (B.G.) to coin this as “illiteracy of health services in Inuktitut.” It was also noted that English is often a second language to both patients and the predominantly Francophone staff, further compromising patient safety (Box 2).

Some individuals did not express experiences of hardship from tuberculosis care (Box 2). Respondents were not always explicit as to what factors made treatment smooth. We inferred the following as potential facilitating factors based on participant comments: material resources (notably, access to vehicles), strong support networks (enabling child care, transport, purchasing of food), “lucky” circumstances (living close to the clinic), and health care professional familiarity with the community and effectiveness at demonstrating supportive care.

Construct 3: Nunavimmiut reported experiences of undermined agency, heightened anxiety, and stigma related to broad information gaps on tuberculosis

Participants reported stress and worry related to tuberculosis in Nunavik, which we identified as relating to information and knowledge gaps (Box 3). For example, we met with individuals aware of tuberculosis circulating in their community through rumours rather than formal announcements from health services and not being aware of interventions to address tuberculosis. Some participants expressed concerns about their own vulnerability of being exposed to tuberculosis, some wondered who might have exposed them or their children, others asked about how tuberculosis develops, and some expressed fear about infecting others. Evidence of concern about tuberculosis was also shown in respondents asking the Indigenous interviewers about physical symptoms such as coughing or shortness of breath. Indigenous researchers were struck by these exchanges, which reflected the desire for information and supported their own observations that there were few accessible resources for patient education and the potential for ineffective communication in clinical encounters. Inuit desire for tuberculosis prevention and treatment information demonstrates interest and a lack of avoidance.

Stigma was mostly rooted in fears of infection and blame for infecting others (Box 3). Potential stigmatization was the main reason cited for ensuring that tuberculosis diagnoses are kept confidential. Reported nervousness about community gossip was mentioned by respondents specifically as a concern if others knew about one’s infection. Yet, several respondents advocated for knowing about other community members’ diagnoses, saying even though public knowledge would create unease, it would be a way to help prevent spread. Interpretation

In this study, we explored Inuit experiences during the tuberculosis epidemic that has been ongoing in Nunavik since the early 2000s, using an IRM approach and with involvement of Nunavik Inuit as researchers, steering committee members, and community research partners. Interviews and focus groups with Nunavik Inuit and health care providers revealed tuberculosis care as a source of hardship, owing to a program-centred paradigm that prioritizes minimizing the burden caused by tuberculosis care on resource-constrained health services. Key sources of hardship included compulsory displacements out of community for diagnosis and treatment, lack of support services to facilitate engagement in isolation and with lengthy treatment protocols, the potential for police or court interventions, inadequate sharing of information and data on tuberculosis in affected communities, and a paucity of Inuktitut within health care services. Participants expressed a strong desire to protect their and their community’s health. They also described how current health care structures can fail at cultural safety. Nunavik Inuit, sharing their expertise on tuberculosis within their communities, articulated tangible and practical changes to tuberculosis policies and care pathways that would meet Inuit needs, consistent with person-centred care paradigms, 37–39 that we grouped into 7 calls to action.

We identified 1 other study reporting on Inuit experiences with tuberculosis in the last 20 years in Canada.40 Between 2003 and 2004, a Danish nurse and anthropologist working in Iqaluit sought to understand how Inuit “make meaning of [tuberculosis]” using ethnography.40 Møller interviewed 29 Inuit in Iqaluit (3 in Inuktitut), 8 of whom had active or latent tuberculosis between 1998 and 2004. In Møller’s article, Inuit reported public health messaging as not being impactful, and shared experiences of racism within and outside of health care services. Møller called for decolonization through “embracing Inuit ways and values in educational, health, and political matters,” for culturally acceptable public health messaging and training, and for greater reflexivity and respectful practice by Euro-Canadian health care workers.40 Our work is an inquiry using IRM into Inuit experiences with tuberculosis covering the post-2003 period, when incidence has sharply risen. Our work supports the findings of Møller while contributing novel insights and expanding the evidence base by interviewing a large number of Inuit from multiple Nunavik communities, adding their detailed reports on how contemporary tuberculosis-specific care paradigms can result in hardship, providing Inuit insights into the interplay between information gaps and stigma, and adding Inuit-issued calls for improving tuberculosis services. The substantial presence of Inuktitut in our data collection and reporting, and involvement of Inuit peer researchers and community research partners, helped centre the evidence on Inuit voices.

The 7 calls to action are in alignment with Nunavik-specific decolonization models25,41 and with the holistic conceptualization of health shared by Nunavik Inuit in a regional consultation evaluating general health services from 2017 to 2019.42 The calls for supportive and needs-based care are also consistent with person-centred and people-centred health care delivery models that prioritize respecting autonomy, understanding people within their social context, and offering tailored approaches to comprehensively support tuberculosis-affected individuals and communities.37–39,43

Inuit participants explicitly detailed tangible — and often very practical — changes to tuberculosis policies within the 7 calls to action. Certain suggestions were similar to approaches used by the Taima TB (Stop Tuberculosis) group in Iqaluit — specifically, door-to-door case-finding and increasing Inuit involvement in tuberculosis-awareness efforts.44,45 Some changes are already being implemented by the NRBHSS. The public health department now regularly updates local leaders with statistics on tuberculosis in their communities, and health centres have been hiring local community members in a new role as public health officers who will support individuals and communities in tuberculosis care.46,47 Training for health care workers has been launched by the Public Health Department that covers the history of tuberculosis in Nunavik and promotes cultural safety training. There is also a greater push to establish radiography capacity to more communities to reduce displacement for screening and diagnosis, and to use video-assisted DOT. Further integration of the 7 calls to action can be done by establishing routine protocols to assess and address social, economic, and logistical needs of tuberculosis-affected individuals and families — through offering practical support such as child care, rides, financial or food support, and incentives. Integrating these within DOT could ease negative perceptions around adherence monitoring. While social support interventions are occasionally provided to people with tuberculosis in Nunavik, incorporating them in clinical protocols will ensure they become the standard of care.

Important knowledge gaps remain. Further community-led research is needed to outline Inuit-specific approaches to replace the use of court orders and police intervention when people stop engaging in treatment. Improving our understanding of the interplay between confidentiality and stigma in small communities will be important for developing culturally acceptable support mechanisms. Interventional operational research through an Indigenous lens can play a role in increasing participation in community-wide screenings, and completion of latent tuberculosis treatment.

Limitations

Because of concerns about protecting confidentiality, transcripts were not shared with participants; however, interviewers leading transcription safeguarded for accurate recollection of context, and we used multiple other rigorous means of member-checking, as described in the Methods. Those who reported no or few difficulties related to tuberculosis care provided limited reflections on what contributed to their positive experiences. However, inferences about facilitating factors were readily made from other interviews. Advertising and word-of-mouth recruitment may have led to under- or overrepresentation of some experiences. This may have particularly affected those who experienced the most difficulties with tuberculosis care. Nevertheless, we successfully enrolled many individuals who reported difficult experiences, including those who struggled substantially with adherence.

Conclusion

Nunavimmiut experience program-centred tuberculosis care, requiring Inuit to adapt to services that are shaped by resource scarcity. Nunavimmiut prioritize ending tuberculosis. They advocate for changes to tuberculosis-related health care services and policies that contribute to care being a source of hardship. In the face of a rapidly worsening tuberculosis epidemic, the governments of Quebec and Canada must respond urgently by addressing chronic health care underresourcing to enable implementation of a robust and supportive approach to ending this epidemic, as called for by Nunavimmiut.
