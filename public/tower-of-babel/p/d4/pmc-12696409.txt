Monoclonal antibodies against human TMPRSS2 prevent infection by any SARS-CoV-2 variant
Authors: Michishige Harada, Takehisa Matsumoto, Mizuki Yamamoto, Jin Goda, Akiko Idei, Kenichi Ohtaki, Natsuki Kojima, Natsumi Yoneda, Kosuke Miyauchi, Kazushige Katsura, Mariko Ikeda, Kazuharu Hanada, Yoshiko Ishizuka-Katsura, Toshiaki Hosaka, Tamao Hisano, Toshie Kaizuka, Takako Yamamoto, Masashi Matsuda, Manabu Nakayama, Akiko Sugimoto-Ishige, Machie Sakuma, Rina Hashimoto, Kazuo Takayama, Misako Nakayama, Cong Thanh Nguyen, Hirohito Ishigaki, Yasushi Itoh, Yoshinobu Hashizume, Minoru Yoshida, Yasushi Kawaguchi, Makoto Takeda, Haruhiko Koseki, Mikako Shirouzu, Jun-ichiro Inoue, Takashi Saito
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryThe transmembrane serine protease 2 (TMPRSS2) plays a critical role in SARS-CoV-2 infection by priming the viral Spike (S) protein for host cell entry and thus represents a potential target for COVID-19 therapy. Here monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) against human TMPRSS2 were established for therapeutic application. In vitro infection by SARS-CoV-2 of cell lines and human lung organoids was strongly inhibited by the TMPRSS2 mAbs. These mAbs inhibited infection of all SARS-CoV-2 variants tested including omicron. mAbs recognized epitopes different from the enzymatic active site and did not inhibit protease activity, suggesting blockade of steric interactions of S protein-ACE2/TMPRSS2. The inhibitory activity of the mAbs in vivo was examined in human ACE2/TMPRSS2-double knock-in mouse and macaque models. Analysis of viral titers and histopathological analysis of the lung in these infected animals indicated that the TMPRSS2 mAb effectively suppressed viral titers and induction of inflammation in vivo.

Introduction

Pandemic COVID-19, which is caused by the severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2), began in 2019 and has expanded, and 778 million people were diagnosed worldwide, causing over 7 million deaths.1,2 Various types of vaccines have been developed to induce potent neutralizing antibodies (Ab) and T cell response in vivo to combat the virus. Particularly, the mRNA vaccines BNT162b2/Comirnaty and mRNA-1283/Spikevax are able to generate neutralizing anti-Spike protein antibodies (Abs) and have been approved with over 90% efficacy after a second dose.3,4,5 In addition to such newly developed potent vaccines, monoclonal Abs (mAbs) against the Spike protein of the virus have been developed as neutralizing mAbs for the prevention or treatment of COVID-19 patients.5,6,7 Despite the development of these vaccines and neutralizing mAbs, breakthrough infections occur especially by emerging virus variants, which makes these therapeutic agents less effective. During the first 3 years of the pandemic, the original Wuhan virus (wild-type, WT) has changed to multiple variants with many mutations, particularly in the S protein, and named alpha, beta, gamma, delta, epsilon, kappa, lambda, and omicron.8,9 The variant omicron, which harbors an unusually high numbers of mutations in the spike protein10 and thus can escape from currently available vaccines and anti-Spike mAbs,8 are now spreading worldwide. Therefore, additional new therapeutic interventions are required for the prevention and treatment of COVID-19.

Infection by SARS-CoV-2 is initiated by the attachment of the virus to mucosal epithelial cells through the binding of the S1 domain of the viral surface Spike (S) protein to host angiotensin-converting enzyme 2 (ACE2) as the cellular receptor.11 During cellular attachment, the S protein has to be processed to be digested at the S1/S2 site by furin and then at S2 by the membrane-type serin protease TMPRSS2.12,13 This activity generates an S protein fusion peptide able to fuse with the host cell membrane for viral entry into host cells.14 Since TMPRSS2 is essential for completing the virus/cell fusion process, it may represent a promising target for COVID-19 therapy.

TMPRSS2 is a type II transmembrane serine protease bearing a serine protease domain (SPD) with the catalytic site at the C-terminus and is expressed on the cell surface in various tissues such as lung, gastrointestinal tract, kidney, and pancreas. The critical function of TMPRSS2 for the entry of SARS-CoV-2 into host cells has been shown in vitro in cell lines as well as in vivo using Tmprss2-deficient (KO) mice.11,15,16,17 SARS-CoV-2 infection is inhibited in Tmprss2-KO mice.16,17 Based on the essential role of TMPRSS2 for successful SARS-CoV-2 infection, TMPRSS2 inhibitors such as nafamostat and camostat have been developed as potential therapeutic reagents.11,18,19 Indeed, both camostat and nafamostat have been under clinical trials, but the results have not been ideal.20,21,22,23,24 Nafamostat has been used for a long time in the clinic for the treatment of pancreatitis but has various known side effects and is also known to have a rapid turnover in vivo.24,25

Therefore, based on the idea that mAb against TMPRSS2 as a host protein instead of viral protein may represent a potentially safe and stable target to inhibit SARS-CoV-2 infection in vivo and to treat an ongoing COVID-19 infection, we have tried to generate TMPRSS2 mAbs that could inhibit infection as therapeutic agents for COVID-19.

Results

Establishment of TMPRSS2 mAbs that block SARS-CoV-2 infection

Because human and mouse TMPRSS2 have high homology, we utilized TMPRSS2-deficient (KO) mice to generate anti-human TMPRSS2 antibodies. We prepared recombinant human TMPRSS2 ectodomain protein and A20 and EL4 mouse cell lines expressing human TMPRSS2 by transfection. Tmprss2-KO mice were immunized five times alternatively with the recombinant protein and each transfectant at 2-week intervals. Three days after the final immunization with TMPRSS2 protein IV without adjuvant, splenic B cells were purified and subjected to electro-fusion with P3U1 myeloma cells (Figure S1A). After culture in HAT medium, 11000 wells were screened to identify hybridomas with blocking activity. We screened the hybridomas for specific Ab production by three procedures: first, binding to TMPRSS2 proteins by ELISA; second, surface staining by flow cytometry; and third, by a cell-fusion-based blocking assay. ELISA screening for culture supernatants was performed on plates coated with recombinant TMPRSS2 SPD protein. Among 11,328 wells, 3,813 were positive by ELISA. The supernatants of wells with positive ELISA results were further analyzed by flow cytometry and a cell-fusion-based assay. TMPRSS2-transfected human Daudi cells were stained with the supernatants and analyzed by flow cytometry. Among 3813 wells, the supernatants from 20 wells brightly stained, and those of 671 wells weakly stained the transfected cells. The cell-fusion system for screening consisted of two different 293FT cells; one expressing SARS-CoV-2 S protein and DSP8-11, and the other expressing human TMPRSS2, ACE2, and DSP1-7. Fusion of two cells through binding of ACE2/TMPRSS2 and S protein induces luminescence as described16 (Figure S1B). All supernatants were subjected to high throughput screening for their ability to inhibit the cell fusion, and 80 samples showed strong inhibition (>75%) (Figure S1C). These 80 samples were further subjected to cloning by limiting dilution, and the cell fusion inhibitory function of all 80 clones was reconfirmed. Although the cell-fusion inhibitory function and surface staining of TMPRSS2-transfected Daudi cells were not well correlated, we selected 20 clones exhibiting both bright cell surface staining (Figure S2A) and strong inhibition of cell fusion (Figure S2B).

In vitro inhibition of SARS-CoV-2 infection by the TMPRSS2 mAbs

To analyze the inhibitory function of these established anti-TMPRSS2 mAbs, we first examined SARS-CoV-2 virus infection on Calu-3 human lung cell lines. We first used the original Wuhan (WT) clone for infection. When four representative mAbs (752, 1864, 2020, and 2228 clones) were added to Calu-3 cells prior to infection with the WT SARS-CoV-2 authentic virus, the expression of viral antigen RNA in infected cells was dramatically inhibited in a mAb dose-dependent manner (Figure 1A). Inhibition by nafamostat, a synthetic TMPRSS2 inhibitor used as a positive inhibition control, was similar to that by mAbs at 10 μg/mL (Figure 1A). We further analyzed the inhibitory function of these mAbs upon infection with authentic SARS-CoV-2 virus instead of the pseudo-typed viruses (Figure S3A). Similar to the results with pseudo-virus infection, the 1864 and 2228 mAbs could strongly inhibit infection by all live virus variants tested, WT, α, γ, and δ. TMPRSS2-specific inhibitory effect by mAbs was shown by the fact that the entry of virus into TMPRSS2-negative ACE2-expressing cells was not blocked byTMPRSS2 mAbs (Figure S3B).

The inhibitory function of these mAbs was further analyzed using human iPS-derived lung organoids as a representative normal human tissue instead of cell lines. The B.1.1.214 and delta and omicron SARS-CoV-2 variants were used to infect the lung organoids (Figure 2B). The mAbs (2020, 2228, and 752) were added and the viral titers were analyzed 96 h later. All infections were strongly inhibited with around 1 μg/mL of mAbs. These results indicate that the established mAbs efficiently inhibit a broad range of SARS-CoV-2 infections in human lung tissue cells.

Characterization of the TMPRSS2 mAbs

We analyzed several characteristics, such as the recognition epitope, affinity and specificity of the representative four TMPRSS2 mAb clones.

First, the affinity of mAbs to recombinant TMPRSS2 was measured by surface plasmon resonance method using BIAcore (Table S1). The KDs of the four mAbs were in the nanomolar range, indicating sufficiently high to utilize.

Second, the epitopes of TMPRSS2 recognized by mAbs were determined by competitive staining and cryogenic electron microscopy (cryo-EM), followed by flow cytometry using cells expressing TMPRSS2 with mutation of epitope candidates. Surface staining of TMPRSS2 on Daudi cells with each mAb was competed by adding a 10-fold excess of other mAbs. Staining with 2,228, 2,020, and 1,864 mAbs was competed by each other but not by the 752 mAb. On the other hand, staining with the 752 mAb was competed only by the 752 mAb itself but not by the other three mAbs (Figure 3A). Therefore, we analyzed the structure of the ternary complex of TMPRSS2, Fab fragments of 2,228 and 752 mAbs by cryo-EM. The ectodomain of the recombinant TMPRSS2 was prepared, Fab fragments of 2228 and 752 mAbs were added and the EM data were analyzed (Figure 3B and S4) the structural analysis revealed that the 2228 mAb binds to R316, F319, and F321 of TMPRSS2, whereas 752 mAb reacts with the region composed of N177-Y180, R182, and R186. To confirm these interactions, we performed site-directed mutagenesis of these amino acids for both 2228 and 752 mAbs (Figure 3C). For the 752 mAb epitope, the N179A mutant was not stained by the 752 mAb but stained the other three mAbs. In contrast, the R316S and F319L mutants were not stained by the 2020, 1864, or 2228 mAbs but stained with the 752 mAb. Analysis of these mutants confirmed the existence of two epitopes of mAbs on TMPRSS2: one within the protease domain for the 2228 mAb and the other in the C-terminus region for the 752 mAb. Since mAbs recognize two distinct epitopes represented by 2228 and 752 mAbs, whether the combination of both mAbs could induce additive inhibitory effect of infection was examined. The inhibitory activity of 2228 mAb was slightly enhanced by the addition of 752 mAb (Figure S5A), indicating weak additive effect.

Third, we investigated whether mAbs affect TMPRSS2 protease activity. The recombinant TMPRSS2 ectodomain, which contains the serine-protease activity, was mixed with mAbs before addition of its substrate, a synthetic peptide containing a protease-sensitive site. As shown in Figure S5B, none of the four mAbs inhibited the TMPRSS2 serine protease activity, while the addition of nafamostat completely blocked the activity. Thus, even though the epitope of mAb 2228 was shown to be in the catalytic domain near the protease active site (Figure 3B), it does not inhibit the enzymatic activity.

Next, we analyzed the specificity of TMPRSS mAbs among species, mouse, cat, hamster, monkey, and human. Of the 20 anti-human TMPRSS2 mAbs, four cross-reacted with mouse, one with hamster, one with cat, and 6 with monkey (Figure S6A). Since these mAbs were generated by immunizing mice with human TMPRSS2, it is reasonable that a considerable proportion of mAbs cross-react with monkey, which is closest to human. Since only the 752 mAb reacted with monkey among the finally selected four mAbs, this mAb was used for in vivo infection experiments in macaques.

There are currently 14 different TMPRSS family proteins: TMPRSS2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 9, 11A, 11B, 11D, 11E, 11F, 12, and 13. Because it has been reported that a SARS-CoV-2 pseudo-typed virus can infect a cell line in vitro via TMPRSS2 11D and 13,26 we also tested the specificity of our TMPRSS2 mAb against these TMPRSS family proteins. All four TMPRSS2 mAbs reacted only with TMPRSS2 but not TMPRSS11 or 13, indicating that they are specific for TMPRSS2 (Figure S6B).

In vivo inhibition of SARS-CoV-2 infection by TMPRSS2 mAbs

To evaluate the efficacy of the mAbs in blocking SARS-CoV-2 infection in vivo, we utilized two in vivo settings, mouse, and cynomolgus monkey.

The mouse model was established by generating knock-in (KI) mouse of both human (h) ACE2 and hTMPRSS2 genes (Figures S8 and S9). Homozygous KI mice expressing hACE2 and hTMPRSS2 were prophylactically administrated 1 mg of the 2228 mAb intravenously twice on the day of virus infection and one day later. The mice were infected intranasally with the SARS-CoV2 delta variant (1 × 105 TCID50) and the virus titer in the bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) and lung tissue pathology were analyzed on days 4, 6, and 10 (Figure 4A). The virus copy number was assessed by measuring virus RNA. Body weights of mice were not changed over the period and all mice were survived (Figure 4B). In the control group, the virus titers in BALF on day 4 surged to over 107 RNA copies whereas the 2228 mAb-treated group had a significantly decreased viral load, with more than a 10-fold reduction. On day 6, control mice showed still high titers of virus (∼106) while mAb-treated mice showed rather dispersed responses, a half with high titer and a half with no virus. On day 10, no virus was detected regardless of the mAb treatment (Figure 4C). Histological analysis of the lungs of these mice revealed that control mice showed mild pneumonia, which was characterized by inflammatory cell infiltration, alveolar septal thickening, and vascular system injury. In contrast, mice prophylactically treated with the 2228 mAb showed minimal evidence of such pneumonia and inflammatory pathogenesis (Figure 4D and 4E). Similar to virus titer, these were no significant inflammation on day 10 anymore. These results demonstrate that the 2228 mAb effectively reduced virus replication and infection-induced inflammation in the lung. Finally, we examined the blocking effect of mAbs in a cynomolgus macaque model. For this study, we used the 752 mAb because it cross-reacts best with monkey TMPRSS2. For use in monkeys, the constant region of the 752 mAb was changed to human IgG4. The reactivity of the human chimeric IgG4 752 mAb to human and macaque TMPRSS2 and inhibitory activity for SARS-CoV-2 infection in vitro were confirmed (Figure S7). Macaques received 100 mg of the 752 human IgG4 mAb twice on day 0 and day 1 after infection with the SARS-CoV-2 delta variant (1 × 107 TCID50). Viral load was collected by oral swab on days 0, 1, 3, 5, and 7, and the animals were subjected to necropsy on day 7 (Figure 5A). Body temperature was significantly lower in mAb-treated groups than in control groups (Figure 5B) and virus titers were dramatically increased on day 1 in the control groups whereas the mAb-treated groups showed significantly reduced virus titer, about an 18-fold reduction (Figure 5C). Pathological examination of lung sections after 7 days of virus inoculation revealed that the control groups had moderate alveolar injury and vascular injury while the 752 mAb treated groups showed much less infiltration of inflammatory cells around the periphery of bronchioles. Average pathological scores were also significantly reduced in 752 mAb-treated monkeys (Figure 5D). Viral antigen-positive cells were also enumerated in the specimens. While the control groups showed significantly higher numbers of antigen-stained cells, these were hardly observed in the 752 mAb-treated groups (Figure 5E). Discussion

We here describe the development of TMPRSS2 mAbs as stable and potent inhibitors of infection by all variants of SARS-CoV-2. Extensive mutations within the Spike protein generate new SARS-CoV-2 escape variants, which has made it difficult for vaccines to maintain efficacy and anti-Spike Abs to attack the virus. In contrast, since TMPRSS2 mAbs are directed against a host and not a viral protein, they function to block virus infection regardless of mutations within S or any viral proteins. Indeed, the TMPRSS2 mAbs that we have established can block infection by SARS-CoV-2 Wuhan authentic wild-type, Alpha, Beta, Gamma, Delta, Kappa, and Omicron variants, all variants that we tested without exception. Among latent variants of the virus, Omicron has been the toughest to fight using a Spike-based vaccine or Abs because Omicron harbors an unusually high number of mutations in the S protein.8,29

Accordingly, some recent studies have shown that the TMPRSS2-dependent pathway plays a minor role in infection by the Omicron variant.16,17,30,31,32 However, in the murine airway infection model, Omicron infection was significantly reduced in Tmprss2-KO mice as compared to wild-type mice.16,17 Our analysis of Omicron infection of Calu-3 cells and human lung organoids showed strong inhibition of infection by the TMPRSS2 mAbs, similar to inhibition of infection by other SARS-CoV-2 variants. Stronger inhibition of infection by TMPRSS2 mAbs as compared to that seen in KO mice may reflect the structural importance of the interaction between Spike-ACE2/TMPRSS2.

We originally expected that TMPRSS2 mAbs recognizing the enzymatic active site and inhibiting the protease activity would possess SARS-CoV-2 infection inhibitory activity because the TMPRSS2 inhibitor nafamostat strongly inhibits SARS-CoV-2 infection in vitro. However surprisingly, almost all of the tested mAbs that could inhibit cell fusion through the Spike-ACE2/TMPRSS2 interaction did not inhibit the enzymatic activity. Therefore, blocking of SARS-CoV-2 infection by these TMPRSS2 mAbs is not mediated by inhibiting the TMPRSS2 protease activity. Although the trimolecular structure of Spike-ACE2-TMPRSS2 has not been solved yet, TMPRSS2 may not be accessible to the digestion site (S2) of the Spike protein due to steric hindrance after mAb binding, which is a unique inhibitory mechanism, unlike other anti-viral protein Abs.

TMPRSS2 consists of several domains: SPD, SRCR (Scavenger receptor cysteine-rich), LDLRA (LDL receptor class A) and transmembrane.33,34 The role of individual domains in TMPRSS2 function remains unclear except for the catalytic site, SPD domain. Recent studies indicated the involvement of the LDLRA domain in TMPRSS2 enzymatic activity, as in its activation of related enzymes like matriptase.35 The precise role of the SRCR domain is still not fully understood, though it appears to play a role in the interaction with cell surface or extracellular molecules.34 A 21-amino acid region (residues 150–170) within the SRCR domain of TMPRSS2 was identified to bind to the spike protein, restricting its access to the endosomal pathway.36 Our cryo-EM study showed that the 2228 and 752 mAbs bind to the SPD and SRCR domains of TMPRSS2, respectively, but neither directly bind to the active site.

In our in vivo infection experiments using macaque and knock-in mouse models, inhibition of infection by the TMPRSS2 mAb was shown to have both preventive and protective activity. In our animal infection models, we performed inhibition of infection by pre-administration (preventive) of mAb in the mouse model and by post-administration (protective) in the monkey model, indicating that the TMPRSS2 mAbs work in both ways. Although we have not yet tested both preventive and protective protocols in the same system, such experiments will follow soon. The fact that the mAbs can inhibit infection even when administered after infection suggests that the TMPRSS2 mAb functions even after the initial entrance of the virus into cells, probably by preventing further transmission of the virus from cell to cell. Since the TMPRSS2 mAb 2228 has already been changed to human IgG4 and bears the humanized VH and VL regions, it is ready to be used in a clinical study after preclinical testing.

TMPRSS2 inhibitors, nafamostat and camostat have been thought to be useful for clinical use for COVID-19 but they were rather short-lived in vivo and known to show side effects which appeared to be independent from inhibition of TMPRSS2 activity.20,21,22,23,24,25 Considering that TMPRSS2-KO mice are normal and do not have any abnormality in any tissue37 and that TMPRSS2 mAbs do not block the protease activity, TMPRSS2 mAb treatment in vivo may be free from any side effect in clinical application. Furthermore, since TMPRSS2 play a critical role in the infection of influenza virus, TMPRSS2 mAbs may function to inhibit influenza virus infection similar to SARS-CoV-2.38,39

Limitations of the study

TMPRSS2 mAbs showed inhibitory function of SARS-CoV-2 infection in vivo using two animal models. Whereas 752 mAb was therapeutically administrated after infection in macaque model, 2,228 mAb was preventively administrated prior to viral infection in murine model due to the cross-reactivity to animal TMPRSS2 and a limitation of the utility of the P3 facility. We have to extend the analysis by administrating mAbs in both preventive and therapeutic ways in the same system. In addition, our study evaluated a broad range of SARS-CoV-2 variants, from the original Wuhan strain to the highly immune-evasive Omicron lineage, and further validation against current XBB variants is needed.

Experimental model and study participant details

Animal experiment approval, animal care, and animal studies

In vivo animal studies

Cynomolgus macaque model

Six female cynomolgus macaques were obtained from Indonesia, Vietnam and the Philippines. They were 17 - 18 years old and weighed between 2.70 and 3.86 kg at the time of challenge. All procedures were performed under ketamine/xylazine anesthesia, and all efforts were made to minimize suffering as previously described.44 The experiments with macaques were performed in accordance with the Guidelines for the Husbandry and Management of Laboratory Animals of the Research Center for Animal Life Science at Shiga University of Medical Science and standards relating to the Care and Fundamental Guidelines for Proper Conduct of Animal Experiments and Related Activities in Academic Research Institutions under the jurisdiction of the Ministry of Education, Culture, Sports, Science and Technology, Japan. The protocols were approved by the Shiga University of Medical Science Animal Experiment Committee.

Mouse model

C57BL/6 and BALB/c mice were purchased from CLEA Japan Inc. The Tmprss2 knockout mice on a C57BL/6 background were kindly provided by M. Takeda.16 All mice were maintained in a pathogen-free animal facility and were used at 8–13 weeks of age. All mouse experiments were carried out according to the in-house guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals of RIKEN, Yokohama Institute, Japan.

Generation of human TMPRSS2 (hTMPRSS2) and ACE2 (hACE2) double knock-in mice

The generation of hTMPRSS2 and hACE2 knock-in (KI) mice is described in Figure S7. The mouse genomic BAC clones containing the mouse Tmprss2 and Ace2 gene were provided by the RIKEN BRC through the National BioResource Project of the MEXT, Japan. The hTMPRSS2 (NM_005656) and hACE2 (NM_021804) cDNAs were purchased from Genscript Biotech Corporation and Sino Biological Co. Ltd., respectively. A targeting vector for KI each human cDNA into the homologous mouse locus was constructed according to the previously reported “two-step Red recombination method”.55 The DNA constructs and sequences of hACE2 and hTMPRSS2 knock-in targeting vectors (pDT-hACE2Kineo and pDT-hTMPRSS2Kineo) are shown in Figures S8A and S8B.

The targeting vectors were linearized with NotI and transfected into mouse embryonic stem (ES) cells by electroporation. Neo-resistant ES cells were selected by PCR using primers for the Neo-resistance gene. Correctly targeted ES cells were injected into blastocyst derived from BALB/c mice, and chimeric mice were crossed with ACT-FLPe mice B6;SJL-Tg(ACTFLPe)9205Dym/J (The Jackson Laboratory, Bar Harbor, Maine, USA) to delete the neomycin resistance cassette. After five generations of backcross with C57BL/6, the resultant mice were used for various experiments. The hTMPRSS2 and hACE2 double knock-in mice (hACE2xhTMRSS2 KI mice) were generated by crossing hACE2 KI mice and hTMRSS2 KI mice.

Cell line and transfectants

The A20 and EL4 cell lines were obtained from the RIKEN-BRC Cell Bank. The Daudi cell line was obtained from the JCRB Cell Bank (JCRB9071). These cells were maintained in RPMI-1640 containing 10% Fetal calf serum (FCS) at 37°C in a 5% CO2 incubator.

Calu-3 cells were obtained from the ATCC (HBT-55). The X293T cell line, a subclone of HEK293 cells, was purchased from Takara bio (Japan). 293TF-Spike, 293TF-TMPRSS2/ACE2 transfectants was provided by J. Inoue.56 These cell lines were cultured in Dulbecco Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM), high glucose supplemented with 2 mM Glutamax I, MEM non-essential amino acids solution, 1 mM sodium pyruvate, 55 μM 2-mercaptoethanol and 10% FCS.

Cells were transduced by using retrovirus vectors as previously reported.57 Briefly, the cDNAs were ligated into the multi-cloning sites of pMys-IRES-GFP or pMys-IRES-blasticidin resistant gene for retrovirus vector by the In-Fusion™ cloning system (Takara Bio Inc.).

The retroviral constructs and the envelope expression vectors, p10A1 were co-transfected into GP2–293 packaging cells using the Retro-X Universal Packaging system (Takara Bio Inc., #631530), and the cell lines were transformed by retroviral vectors. The transfectants were finally sorted with >98% purity by FACSAria III (BD Biosciences) or selected by 5μg/mL blasticidin.

Method details

Plasmid constructions

Amino acid and cDNA sequences encoding the TMPRSS family were obtained from NCBI and UniProtKB and listed in Table S2. We synthesized these cDNAs (Eurofins Genomics) and generated recombinant proteins and transfected cell lines. PCR products were inserted into the pFastBac1 vector for recombinant proteins and into the multi-cloning sites of pMys-IRES-GFP or pMys-IRES-blasticidin resistant gene for retrovirus vector by the In-Fusion™ cloning system (Takara Bio Inc.), respectively.

TMPRSS2 protein preparation

A PCR fragment containing the ectodomain of TMPRSS2 (residues 109–492) fused to an N-terminal GP64 (baculovirus envelope glycoprotein) signal peptide and a C-terminal 8×His tag was amplified and inserted into the pFastBac1 vector using In-Fusion cloning (Clontech). The sequence responsible for TMPRSS2 autoactivation (250SSRQSR255) was substituted with the 6 residues containing an enterokinase cleavage site (DDDDDK). The recombinant bacmid DNA was generated by transforming it into DH10MultiBac E. coli competent cells (ATG:biosynthetics GmbH, Germany). Bacmid preparation and P0 to P4 viral stock production were performed as described previously.58 For protein overexpression, High Five cells were infected, and after 72 hours, the supernatant was harvested, filtered, and purified using Ni-NTA Superflow resin (Qiagen, 30430). The protein was activated by recombinant enterokinase (Enterokinase Cleavage Capture Kit, Millipore 69067) and further purified. The final protein was stored at −80°C.

Cell-free production of TMPRSS family proteins

Full-length TMPRSS family proteins were expressed using a cell-free protein synthesis system with an expression vector containing N-terminally fused with a poly-histidine affinity tag, a TEV protease recognition site, and the linker sequence GSSGSSG, and C-terminally fused with TEV protease recognition site and Streptag II sequences in the pCR2.1 vector. The cell-free synthesis reaction was performed as described previously59 with some modifications. The reaction mixture was incubated for 5.5 hours at 25°C. The mixture was ultracentrifuged, and the supernatant was incubated with Streptactin Sepharose (Cytiva, 28-9356-00) for purification. The protein was eluted with PBS buffer containing 0.04% DDM, 0.005% CHS, and desthiobiotin and stored at −80°C.

The expression vectors for the serine protease domain of TMPRSS2 (aa 256-492 with mutation C365S) which was N-terminally fused with the N11 affinity tag and SUMO protein or the N11 affinity tag and maltose-binding protein (MBP), a TEV protease recognition site, and the linker sequence GSSGSSG, were constructed using the pCR2.1 vector. Each protein was produced in the cell-free synthesis reaction. The protein was purified using a HisTrap™ HP column (Cytiva) and eluted with 500 mM imidazole. The eluted fractions were concentrated and subjected to size-exclusion chromatography using a Superdex 200 column Cytiva. The purified protein was then concentrated and used for mouse immunization.

Ab sequence analysis and humanization

Using SMARTer RACE 5'/3′ Kit (Clontech 634858), the first-strand cDNAs of IgH and IgL of mouse antibodies 2228 and 752 were synthesized with total RNA extracted from respective hybridoma cells and were amplified by 5′-RACE PCR with the kit’s Universal Primer Mix and reverse primers carefully designed based on the sequences of IgG2c, IgG1 and Igκ determined by mouse antibody isotyping, mIgG2cCH3_pRACE-Rv: GATTACGCCAAGCTTATCATTTACCC AGAGACCGGGAGATGGTCTTAG, mIgG1CH3_pRACE-Rv:GATTACGCCAAGCTTATC ATTTACCAGGAGAGTGGGAGAGGC and mIgkc_pRACE-Rv: GATTACGCCAAGCTTACT AACACTCATTCCTGTTGAAGCTCTTGAC. The PCR products were cloned into the linearized pRACE vector by In-Fusion® HD Cloning. The complete VH and VL sequences of the antibodies were obtained as two pieces of overlapping DNA sequence.

The variable regions of mouse mAbs 2228 and 752 were used to perform a homology search for human light and heavy chain germline genes using “abYsis” (http://www.abysis.org/abysis/) as an annotation tool. One hundred germlines respectively for the light chain and heavy chain were evaluated with percent sequence identity to variable regions of mouse antibodies 2228 and 752. Each three acceptor framework sequences were selected from the human light chain and heavy chain variable region germlines encoding antibody sequences with high homology to mouse antibodies 2228 and 752, respectively.60 CDRs from mouse mAbs 2228 and 752 were respectively grafted onto the selected 3 light and 3 heavy chain germlines resulting in a combination of respective 9 candidates of humanized Abs. From these 9 candidates, we screened the humanized Abs for in vivo use by the reactivity to TMPRSS2 and production rate of Ab.

Recombinant Ab preparation

The expression vectors for Abs were constructed using PCR with synthesized genes or mutated primers. The variable regions (VH and VL) were cloned into the pcDNA3.4 vector with a human IgG4 constant region (S108P mutation to prevent Fab-arm exchange) and Trastuzumab CL. Recombinant Abs were produced via transient co-transfection of IgH and IgL vectors using the Expi293 or Expi-CHO expression systems. Ab was purified from culture supernatant in an endotoxin-free environment and sterilized through 0.22 μm- filtration. Fab heavy chains (VH-CH1) attached with the C-terminally TEV protease cleavage site and 6 x His tag and IgL were, respectively, subcloned downstream of the Ig kappa signal peptide into the pcDNA3.4 vector. Fab proteins were produced via the Expi293 system and purified from the supernatant using a HisTrap HP column and size-exclusion chromatography and concentrated for use in cryo-EM.

Immunization and cell fusion

We produced hybridoma cells as previously reported61 with some modifications. Briefly, TMPRSS2-KO mice were subcutaneously primed with human TMPRSS2-Serine protease domain recombinant protein (50 μg/mouse) in TiterMax Gold. They were alternately immunized intraperitoneally with TMPRSS2 transfectants (2x107 cells/mouse) and subcutaneously with recombinant protein (50 μg/mouse) at 2-week intervals. TMPRSS2 transfectants were treated with gamma irradiation (10 Gly) before immunization. ODN 1826 (Nacalai tesque, Japan) was used as an adjuvant for transfectants. Finally, the mice received a tail vein boost with full-length TMPRSS2 recombinant protein 3 days before cell fusion (Figure S1A).

IgG+ mature B cells were separated from splenocytes of the immunized mice by negative selection using the MACS system (Miltenyi Biotec), targeting unwanted mononuclear cells with a biotin-labeled Ab cocktail. Enriched IgG+ B cells were mixed with P3U1 myeloma cells at a 1:1 ratio and subjected to electrofusion using a Nepagene ECFG21 electroporator (Nepa Gene Co. Ltd., Ichikawa, Chiba, Japan) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The cells were seeded in 96-well plates and selected in HAT medium. Supernatants were tested by ELISA for reactivity with TMPRSS2 recombinant protein. Positive hybridomas were frozen, subcultured, and used for flow cytometric and competitive screenings as described below.

Dual Split Protein (DSP) assay

The DSP assay using 293FT cells was performed as described previously18 to monitor SARS-CoV-2-S-protein-mediated membrane fusion. Briefly, effector cells expressing S-protein with DSP8-11, target cells expressing ACE2, and transmembrane serine protease 2 (TMPRSS2) with DSP1-7 were cultivated one day before the assay. Two hours before the DSP assay, cells were treated with 6 μM EnduRen (Promega), a substrate for Renilla luciferase. 30 μL of each anti-TMPRSS2 hybridoma supernatant was added to the 384-well plates. Next, 30 μL of 293FT-DSP1-7 (TMPRSS2 + ACE2) single-cell suspension (effector cells) was added and incubated for 1 h on ice, and then 30 μL of 293FT-DSP8-11 (S-protein) single-cell suspension (target cells) was added to the wells using a Multidrop dispenser (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). After incubation at 37°C for 4 h, luciferase activity was measured using a Centro xS960.

Flow cytometry

For flow cytometry screening for ELISA-positive supernatants, a mixture of Daudi parental cells and TMPRSS2 transfectants expressing GFP were stained with the 100μL of hybridoma supernatant on ice for 20 min, followed by incubation with allophycocyanin (APC)-conjugated anti-mouse IgG (BioLegend, Poly4053). Cells were washed and resuspended in propidium iodide (Nacalai tesque, Japan) and analyzed for TMPRSS2 staining using a LSRFortessa X-20 (BD Biosciences, Franklin Lages, NJ). Hybridomas whose supernatant reacted with TMPRSS2+ Daudi transfectants but not the parental cell line was selected.

Competitive flow cytometry analysis was used as follows: the staining concentration of APC-direct labeled mAbs was determined at concentrations showing around 80% value of maximal fluorescence intensity against the TMPRSS2 transfected Daudi cell line. Competitor non-labeled mAbs were incubated for 30 min on ice at 3-fold dilutions in 0.1 to 10-fold concentrations of APC-labeled mAb. Subsequently, the detection-labeled mAb was added to cell suspension for a further 30 min. Cells were washed and resuspended in propidium iodide (PI) and analyzed.

ELISA for TMPRSS2 family proteins

TMPRSS family proteins with a biotinylated Avitag at C-terminus were immobilized individually on Nunc Immobilizer streptavidin-coated plates (ThermoFisher #436014) at 78nM equivalent protein concentrations. The bound protein was reacted with anti-TMPRSS2 mAbs (10 ug/mL), followed by detection using HRP-conjugated Goat anti-Mouse IgG (H+L) antibody.

Surface-plasmon resonance (SPR) analysis

The kinetic constant measurement of antibodies and TMPRSS2 extracellular domain protein were examined by the Biacore T200 System. A CM5 sensor chip was activated according to the standard procedure for amine coupling and immobilized with anti-human IgG antibodies as the first ligands using Human antibody capture kit (cytiva BR-1008-39) according to the manufacturer’s instruction. Humanized anti-TMPRSS2 Abs were then immobilized onto the chip. Subsequently, sequentially diluted enterokinase-cleaved recombinant extracellular domain of TMPRSS2 (0 to 1000 nM) were injected over the chip to measure the association (Ka) and dissociation (Kd) rates. The chip surface was regenerated using 3 M magnesium chloride solution, and the data were analyzed using Biacore T200 Evaluation Software.

Enzymatic TMPRSS2 assay

The peptide substrate, SSRQSRIVGGE, for the protease of TMPRSS2 was labeled with a fluorescence quenching pair (Dabcyl and Edans) at both ends. The enterokinase-digested extracellular domain of TMPRSS2 (1 nM) was premixed with antibodies in assay buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0), 150 mM NaCl). After 30 min at room temperature, the mixture was added to the substrate solution. The decrease in fluorescence due to cleavage of the peptide by TMPRSS2, was sequentially measured at an excitation wavelength of 340 nm and an emission wavelength of 486 nm. Inhibitory activities of the antibodies were calculated with values measured at 30 min.

Cryo-EM data acquisition and image processing

To prepare complexes of recombinant proteins for cryo-EM, the above TMPRSS2 ECD digested with enterokinase, and two kinds of Fab proteins were mixed with a molar ratio of 1:3:3 and incubated at 4°C for 30 min. The complexes were purified by size-exclusion chromatography using a Superose 6 Increase10/300 GL column equilibrated in 20 mM Tris-HCl (pH8.0) ,150 mM NaCl and then concentrated. The purified complex was applied onto a freshly glow-discharged holey carbon grid (Quantifoil 0.6/1, Cu, 300 mesh) and subsequently vitrified for 3 s at 4°C in 100% humidity and plunge-frozen in liquid ethane using the Vitrobot Mark IV.

Cryo-EM imaging of the complex was performed on a 300 kV Titan Krios G4 equipped with a BioQuantum K3 direct electron detector in the electron counting mode. Cryo-EM reconstitution of TMPRSS2-ECD Fab complex was described in Figure S4. The imaging was performed at a nominal magnification of ×105,000, corresponding to a calibrated pixel size of 0.829 Å per pixel. Each micrographic movie was recorded for a total of 15.7 electrons per pixel per second for 2.2 sec, resulting in an accumulated exposure of 50.1 e-/Å2. A total of ∼3,000 movies were collected per data set. All the data were automatically acquired using the EPU software, with a defocus range of -0.8 to -2.2 μm.

Image processing was mainly performed with the RELION-3.1.3 package46 except for generating the initial 3D map which was obtained from a preliminary 3D reconstruction with cryoSPARC.47 All of the dose-fractionated movies were subjected to beam-induced motion correction using MotionCor248 and the contrast transfer function (CTF) parameters were estimated using CTFFIND4.49 Particles were picked and extracted from the micrographs and subjected to 2D and 3D classification to select good particles, and subsequently subjected to 3D refinement, Bayesian polishing and CTF refinement. To further improve the density of binding site, the particles were subjected to focused 3D classification using a mask encompassing TMPRSS2 and variable domains of Fabs. In the last cycle, 80,052 particles were refined, yielding an overall map with an overall resolution of 3.1 Å according to the Fourier shell correlation (FSC) = 0.143 criterion. The final map was sharpened with ResolveCryoEM and Autosharpen in Phenix package50 for model building.

Model building and validation

The model of the TMPRSS2 ECD was constructed based on reported structures (PDB ID: 7MEQ) as the initial model. The model of each antibody was built by AlphaFold ver2.3.251 and manually fitted into each map in Coot52 and ChimeraX.53 The model of the complex of the variable domains and TMPRSS2 ECD and Fabs was iteratively refined by using phenix.real_space_refine of the Phenix package with secondary structure restrains. Molecular graphics and density map were prepared with UCSF ChimeraX.

Preparation of SARS-CoV-2 and its pseudo-typed VSV viruses

Strain UT-NCGM02/Human/2020/Tokyo from the Institute of Medical Science at University of Tokyo was used for the in vitro SARS-CoV-2 entry inhibition assay.

For the human lung organoid study, the SARS-CoV-2 strains B.1.1.214 (GISAID accession number: EPI_ISL_2897162), B.1.617.2 (GISAID accession number: EPI_ISL_9636792), and XBB.1.1 (GISAID accession number: EPI_ISL_15907741) were used. Infection experiments using lung organoids were done in biosafety level 3 facilities at Kyoto University. In vivo infection studies, SARS-CoV-2 delta strain (B.1.617.2) (TY11-927-P1/2021) was kindly provided by Ken Maeda, Mutsuyo Takayama-Ito, Masaaki Sato, and Masayuki Saijo, National Institute of Infectious Disease.

SARS-CoV-2 viruses were expanded in VeroE6-TMPRSS2 (JCRB1819) cells in DMEM containing 5% FBS. The SARS-CoV-2 virus stock titer was determined by performing a plaque assay in VeroE6-TMPRSS2 cells, aliquoted, and stored at -80 °C. SARS-CoV-2 pseudo-typed VSV viruses were prepared as described previously.56 Briefly, 293T cells were transfected with an expression plasmid for S or VSV G using calcium phosphate precipitation. At 16 h post-transfection, the cells were inoculated with a replication-deficient VSV, which encodes firefly luciferase, at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 1. At 2 h after infection, the cells were washed and further incubated for 16 h before the supernatants containing the pseudo-virus were harvested.

SARS-CoV-2 entry inhibition assay

A virus neutralization assay was performed as previously described and with some modifications.40 In brief, Calu-3 cells were seeded one day before infection in 96-well plates (5×104 cells/well). Cells were treated with serially diluted antibody for 1 h and then SARS-CoV-2 at an MOI of 0.1 was added and incubated for 30 min. The cells were then washed with PBS and incubated in fresh medium for 24 hours. The amount of SARS-CoV-2 was measured using viral RNA amplified in cells by RT-qPCR. Cell lysis and cDNA synthesis were performed using SuperPrep II Cell Lysis & RT Kit for qPCR (SCQ-401, TOYOBO, Osaka, Japan) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Quantitative real-time RT-PCR for SARS-CoV-2 N and ribosomal protein L13a (Rpl13a) was performed using THUNDERBIRD SYBR qPCR Mix (TOYOBO) with CFX ConnectTM Real-Time PCR detection system (Bio-Rad, CA, USA) at 95°C for 3 min, followed by 50 cycles of 95°C for 10 s and 60°C for 1 min. The following primer sets were used: SARS-CoV-2 N (5′-AAATTTTGGGGACCAGGAAC-3′, 5′-TGGCAGCTGTGTAGGTCAAC-3′), and human Rpl13a (5′-TGTTTGACGGCATCCCAC-3′, 5′-CTGTCACTGCCTGGTACTTC-3′). For a pseudo-virus infection assay, cells were treated with serially diluted antibody for 1 h and the pseudo-viruses were added. At 16 h postinfection, luciferase activity was measured using the Bright-Glo Luciferase Assay system (Promega) and Centro xS960 luminometer (Berthold).

Human lung organoids and SARS-CoV-2 infection

Lung organoids were generated from human iPS cells as previously reported.43 Lung organoid cells were cultured in a 96-well plate (2.0×104 cells/well) with anti-TMPRSS2 mAbs for 1 hr on ice and then infected with 2.0×103 TCID50/well of SARS-CoV-2 for 2 hr. The culture medium was replaced with fresh medium containing anti-TMPRSS2 mAbs at 1 dpi. At 2 dpi, the viral RNA copy number in the cell culture supernatant was measured by RT-qPCR.

The cell-culture supernatant was mixed with an equal volume of 2×RNA lysis buffer (distilled water containing 0.4 U/μL SUPERase·In Rnase Inhibitor (Thermo Fisher Scientific), 2% Triton X-100, 50 mM KCl, 100 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.4), and 40% glycerol) and incubated at room temperature for 10 min. The mixture was diluted 10 times with distilled water. Viral RNA was quantified using a One Step TB Green PrimeScript PLUS RT-PCR Kit (Perfect Real Time) (Takara Bio) on a QuantStudio 1 Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The primers used in this experiment are as follows: (forward) AGCCTCTTCTCGTTCCTCATCAC and (reverse) CCGCCATTGCCAGCCATTC. Standard curves were prepared using SARS-CoV-2 RNA (105 copies/μL) purchased from Nihon Gene Research Laboratories.

In vivo SARS-CoV-2 infection

hTMPRSS2 and ACE2 double KI mice (female) were anesthetized with intraperitoneal injection of 150 μl ketamine (10 mg/ml)/xylazine (1 mg/ml) solution. Five mice were dosed with anti-TMPRSS2, humanized 2228 Fv-mouse IgG2b (ADCC-, CDC-) at 1 mg x 2 per mouse (100 mg antibody/kg) and four with saline through intravenous administration on 1 day before and the day of virus challenge. After four hours, in total, the SARS-CoV-2 delta strain (1x104 TCID50) was administered intranasally in a total volume of 50 μl DMEM (Thermo Scientific) per mouse delivered equally to both nostrils.

Viral RNA samples from euthanized mice were recovered by instillation of lungs with 1 ml sterile PBS followed by aspiration to collect ∼0.5 ml volume of fluid on day 4 post-infection. Then the animals were euthanized, and the lungs were harvested. Mouse infection experiments were performed in the RIKEN-IMS BSL3 facility.

In vivo infection of cynomolgus macaques was performed as previously described44 with some modifications. Briefly, 1 × 107 TCID50 of SARS-CoV-2 delta strain was inoculated into the conjunctiva, nasal cavity, oral cavity, and trachea of six cynomolgus macaques. The next day after viral inoculation, 100 mg (57–62 mg/kg) of anti-TMPRSS2, 752 mAb (cross-species reactive with cynomolgus macaque) or saline was injected intravenously. Swabs were collected on day 0 (before infection), day 1 (before mAb injection), days 3, 5, and 7. Macaques were sacrificed on day 7 and lung tissues were collected. Total RNA was extracted from swab samples using the QIAamp Viral RNA Mini Kit and RNeasy Plus Mini Kit (Qiagen, Venlo, Netherlands) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The levels of RNA corresponding to SARS-CoV-2 N protein were measured using the TaqMan Fast Virus 1-step Master Mix (Thermo Scientific). The primers and probe used for detecting the SARS-CoV-2 delta (B.1.617.2) strain were as follows: forward primer, 5′-GACCCCAAAATCAGCGAAAT-3′; reverse primer, 5′-TCTGGTTACTGCCAGTTGAATCTG-3′; and probe, 5′-(FAM)-ACCCCGCATTACGTTTGGTGGACC-(BHQ-1)-3′.

Infection experiments of macaques were performed in the ABSL3 facility, Shiga University of Medical Science.

Histological analysis

Lung histopathological analysis was performed as described previously with modification.62 Lungs were obtained at necropsy and lung tissue slices were collected from mice and macaques. These slices were fixed in 10% formalin for 72 h, embedded in paraffin, and cut into 3-μm-thick sections, which were mounted on glass slides. Sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin and analyzed by light microscopy. Histological evaluation was performed by two pathologists, both blinded to sample identification, based on criteria established for influenza virus infection63 as follows: 0, normal lung; 1, mild destruction of bronchial epithelium; 2, mild peribronchiolar inflammation; 3, inflammation in the alveolar walls resulting in alveolar thickening; 4, mild alveolar injury accompanied by vascular injury; 5, moderate alveolar injury and vascular injury; and 6 and 7, severe alveolar injury with hyaline membrane-associated alveolar hemorrhage (under or over 50% of the section area, respectively). The mean score of the two pathologists was defined as the histological score. After autoclaving the slides in citrate buffer (pH 9) for antigen retrieval, SARS-CoV-2 N antigen was detected using monoclonal anti-N antibody 8G8A (Bioss, Inc., Boston, MA, USA) and a secondary antibody.
