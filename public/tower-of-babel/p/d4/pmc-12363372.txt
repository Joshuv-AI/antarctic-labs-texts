Long-term functional and quality of life outcomes after cementless minimally invasive extendable endoprosthesis replacement in skeletally immature patients with bone sarcomas at the lower limb
Authors: Yusuke Tsuda, Yoshihiro Nishida, Akio Sakamoto, Koichi Ogura, Tomohiro Fujiwara, Tetsuya Sekita, Hirotaka Kawano, Hiroshi Kobayashi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsExtendable endoprostheses are utilized to reconstruct segmental defects following resection of bone sarcomas in skeletally immature children. However, there remains a paucity of data regarding long-term functional and quality of life outcomes.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective, multicentre study and reviewed 45 children who underwent cementless minimally invasive extendable endoprosthetic replacement. Anatomical sites included the distal femur (n = 29), proximal femur (n = 4), proximal tibia (n = 11), and total femur (n = 1). The mean follow-up period was 12 years. The mean age at extendable endoprosthetic replacement was ten years (5 to 15). Most patients (96%, 43/45) had reached skeletal maturity at the final follow-up.ResultsThe ten-year endoprosthetic failure-free survival rate was 60%. Of the 45 patients, 25 (56%) had 42 complications which were frequently related to structural failure (45%, 19/42), with extension mechanism jamming being the most common (n = 7, 17%). Excluding lengthening procedures, 20 patients (44%) underwent additional surgery with a mean of two surgeries per patient. The mean limb-length discrepancy at the final follow-up was 2.3 cm. Limb salvage was achieved in 44 (98%) patients. The mean Musculoskeletal Tumor Society (MSTS) scores, Toronto Extremity Salvage Score (TESS), and EuroQol five-dimension five-level questionnaire (EQ-5D-5L) were 78%, 92%, and 92% at the last follow-up, respectively. Multiple additional surgeries (≥ 2 times) for complications were associated with worse MSTS scores compared with those without multiple additional surgeries (p = 0.009). Moreover, limb-length discrepancy > 3 cm showed significantly worse MSTS scores compared with those ≤ 3 cm (p = 0.019).ConclusionExtendable endoprostheses were associated with a high complication rate and need for additional surgeries over time, especially for structural-related complications. Despite this, successful limb salvage with reasonable function/quality of life and small limb-length discrepancy were achievable in the long term. Introduction

Primary bone sarcomas in children often involve the physis. The epiphyses of the distal femur and proximal tibia contribute approximately 35% and 30% to growth of the lower limb.1 Therefore, limb-length discrepancy (LLD) is a common challenge in children following bone sarcoma resection at the lower limb. Restoring limb length is essential to preclude gait abnormalities, leg pain, back pain, and cosmetic issues. Reconstruction methods after excision of bone sarcomas in children include extendable endoprostheses, allografts or autografts, epiphyseal distraction, or bone transport.2-9 Extendable endoprostheses enable early weightbearing, LLD correction, predictable function, and reportedly a low risk of early complications.10

Effective chemotherapy improved patient outcomes. The five-year overall survival for non-metastatic osteosarcoma and Ewing’s sarcoma is 60% to 80%.11,12 Implant-related complications arise with time, and complications may be associated with decreased functional outcomes. However, there are limited data on long-term complications or functional/quality of life (QoL) outcomes of cementless minimally invasive extendable endoprostheses at the lower limb,12,13 which is crucial information for patients and surgeons in selecting reconstruction options.

The aims of this study were to report the long-term functional and QoL outcomes and implant-related complications in children treated with extendable endoprostheses after bone sarcoma resection. Second, we sought to determine the risk factors which were associated with worse functional and QoL score.

Methods

We conducted a multicentre, retrospective, observational study by chart review through the Japanese Musculoskeletal Oncology Group (JMOG), a group for multicentre clinical studies organized by orthopaedic oncologists in Japan. After the institutional review board approval for each hospital, we analyzed the data of 45 patients who underwent cementless minimally invasive extendable endoprosthesis replacement (EPR) at the lower limb after primary bone sarcoma resection between January 1998 and January 2018. Patients with more than a five-year follow-up period were included in this study. These patients were treated at 13 JMOG-affiliated institutions, including Nagoya University (n = 8), Kyoto University (n = 6), Okayama University (n = 5), National Cancer Centre (n = 5), Keio University (n = 4), Osaka University (n = 3), Kobe University (n = 3), and Tokyo University (n = 3). We also recruited two patients each from the following institutions: Gifu University, Kinki University, and Kanazawa University. One patient each was recruited from Nara Medical University and Shinsyu University. Ultimately, we included 45 patients who underwent extendable EPR of the distal femur (n = 29), proximal femur (n = 4), proximal tibia (n = 11), and total femur (n = 1). The indication for EPR depended on the surgeon’s decision. Generally, a projected LLD > 3 cm at skeletal maturity was considered an appropriate indication.10

Operative technique and endoprostheses

All endoprostheses were minimally invasive Kotz Growing prostheses (Stryker, USA), matching the fixed hinge Howmedica Modular Resection System (Figure 1). Lengthening with these prostheses required a minimally invasive open procedure. Extension mechanisms involved worm drive, a threaded spindle driven by a bevel gear pair, which moves a sleeve by a threaded bush. Adjustment is performed by unlocking a small fixation screw that gives access to an adjustable screw. This adjustment allows the prosthesis to lengthen by 0.9 mm per turn without the need for spacers. All endoprostheses were fixed using a screw without cement (the implant design is restricted to cementless fixation). A non-cement polished femoral component was inserted to opposite epiphysis (e.g. proximal tibial epiphysis in a distal femoral arthroplasty). The patella was not replaced at the initial EPR. The Kotz Growing prosthesis is the only extendable prosthesis available in Japan. Patients with a smaller bone diameter were considered fit for other reconstructive methods, because the minimum component diameter of this system was 8 mm. In our series, five patients underwent biological reconstruction using frozen or irradiated autograft before using an extendable prosthesis. Moreover, a temporal ceramic spacer was used in two patients before using an extendable prosthesis.

Anteroposterior radiographs of a ten-year-old female patient with osteosarcoma of the distal part of the femur treated with a minimally invasive extendable endoprosthesis. The radiographs were made a) preoperatively, b) one month postoperatively, and c) ten years postoperatively with four lengthenings. Ultimately, a total of 48 mm of lengthening was achieved over four procedures.

Limb growth was monitored clinically and radiologically. Generally, we aimed to reduce LLD to ≤ 3 cm. Function was assessed using Musculoskeletal Tumor Society (MSTS) score14,15 and Toronto Extremity Salvage Score (TESS).16 QoL was evaluated using EuroQol five-dimension five-level questionnaire (EQ-5D-5L).17 Herein, stiffness is defined as < 80° of flexion or a fixed flexion deformity > 10°.18

Statistical analysis

Numerical variables were compared using Mann-Whitney U tests. All time-to-event endpoints were calculated using the Kaplan-Meier method. A two-tailed p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.2.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Austria).

Modified Henderson classification was used to categorize all complications requiring surgical or non-surgical management.19,20 Endoprosthetic failure-free survival (EFFS) was defined as the time from implantation to the first failure, which required additional surgeries. The first event leading to additional surgeries, complete endoprosthesis removal, unplanned revision of a failed portion of the endoprosthesis, or the following events was registered:19 soft-tissue reconstruction to restore joint stability (type 1), arthrolysis for stiffness (type 1), resurfacing and realignment for subluxation of the patella or acetabulum (type 1), revision due to aseptic loosening (type 2) or extension mechanism jamming (type 3), rebushing (type 3), fixation or revision due to periprosthetic or prosthesis fracture (type 3), and revision due to infected endoprosthesis (type 4) or tumour progression (type 5).

Revision-free implant survival was defined as the time from implantation to the first major revision procedure with partial/total replacement of major components or removal without revision, including amputation. We also calculated the number of revision surgeries based on this definition.

Results

Evaluation of EFFS and implant-related complications

The five- and ten-year EFFS rates for all patients were 70% (95% CI 54 to 82) and 60% (95% CI 43 to 73), respectively (Figure 2a). The five- and ten-year revision-free survival rates for all patients were 84% (95% CI 69 to 92) and 71% (95% CI 54 to 82), respectively (Figure 2b). The ten- and 20-year amputation-free survival rates for all patients were 97% (95% CI 83 to 100) and 97% (95% CI 82 to 100), respectively (Figure 2c).

Table III shows the incidence of each failure mode. During the follow-up period, 19 patients (43%) had 29 failures requiring additional surgery, with a mean of 1.5 failures (1 to 5) per patient. Structural failures (type 3) occurred most frequently (62%, 18/29). Specifically, prosthesis fracture and breakage (21%, 6/29), extensor mechanism jamming (21%, 6/29), and periprosthetic fracture (17%, 5/29) were common. Extensor mechanism jamming occurred at a mean of 49 months after EPR. Of the 29 failures, aseptic loosening occurred in 14% (n = 4). Of the five periprosthetic fractures, four were successfully treated with internal fixation, and one was managed with revision surgery. Revision surgeries, lengthening procedures, and temporary epiphysiodesis were performed in two patients, two patients, and one patient with extensor mechanism jamming, respectively. Cumulative infection risk was 10% at ten years. Two (50%, 2/4) type 4 (infection) failures were categorized as late (> 2 years) failures. Single-stage revision; two-stage revision; debridement, antibiotics, and implant retention; and internal fixation of the knee joint were performed.

Table V shows each patient’s outcome. Of the 45 patients, 83% had implant lengthening at least once during the follow-up period. A LLD of > 6 cm was observed in two cases. Case 24 underwent reconstruction with a spacer at the age of ten years, and arthroplasty with an expandable prosthesis at age 14 years. However, postoperative infection occurred due to skin necrosis, and extension was not possible.21 At the last follow-up, the patient was aged 26 years and had a LLD of 8.7 cm. In Case 41, the patient underwent reconstruction with a spacer at the age of five years, before undergoing arthroplasty with an expandable prosthesis at 11 years old. Component fracture and component sinking were observed at age 15 and 17, respectively. At the last follow-up, the patient was aged 19 years and had a LLD of 10.5 cm.

Functional and QoL outcomes

We assessed postoperative functional scores using the MSTS score (n = 36), TESS (n = 21), and EQ-5D-5L (n = 20). Final MSTS scores were obtained at a mean of 12 years after EPR. At that time, patients had revised (32%) or original (68%) endoprosthesis. Mean MSTS scores, TESS, and EQ-5D-5L were 78% (40% to 93%), 92% (70% to 98%), and 92% (66% to 100%), respectively, at the final follow-up. Multiple additional surgeries (≥ 2 times) for complications were associated with worse MSTS scores compared with those without multiple additional surgeries (median 20 vs 25; p = 0.009, Mann-Whitney U test) (Table VI). Moreover, LLD > 3 cm showed significantly worse MSTS scores compared with those ≤ 3 cm (median 21 vs 25; p = 0.019, Mann-Whitney U test). Of 35 patients with available data, seven (20%) had < 80° of flexion. The mean of the final knee flexion was 92° (10° to 135°) after distal femur arthroplasty.

Discussion

We reported the long-term complications and functional/QoL outcomes of cementless minimally invasive extendable endoprostheses. This reconstruction option provides excellent long-term limb salvage rates, which compensates for progressive LLD, and has acceptable functional and QoL outcomes. The ten-year amputation-free survival rate was 97%, whereas 80% of patients reached ≤ 3 cm LLD. The mean MSTS score, TESS, and EQ-5D-5L were 78%, 92%, and 92%, respectively. However, these successes come at a price, as many patients treated with extendable endoprostheses experience complications or failures and require several revision procedures over multiple years. In our cohort, 43% experienced a mean of 1.5 failures. While this reconstruction option offers some advantages, risks of major complications and the subsequent impact on the patient’s physical and psychosocial health should be communicated with greater emphasis. Acknowledging these challenges not only informs clinical practice, but also helps set realistic expectations for patients and their families.

Our study showed a similar implant survival rate compared to other studies. Structural failures (type 3) requiring additional surgery occurred most frequently (62%, 18/29), resulting from high loading stresses on these prostheses in our cohort. Specifically, extension mechanism jamming, prosthesis fracture, and periprosthetic fracture frequently occur. The extension mechanism should be modified to meet the high functional demands of this patient group. Aseptic loosening accounted for 14% of 29 failures, and the occurrence of aseptic loosening for cemented extendable endoprostheses ranged from 19% to 30%.15,22 Schinhan et al23 reported on extendable endoprostheses in 71 children after 10.9 years of follow-up, wherein only 59% of patients reached skeletal maturity. They showed that soft-tissue failure was the most common complication affecting 46% of patients. By contrast, Dotan et al13 found that infection was the most common adverse event occurring in 47% of 38 patients. These differences may be attributed to the difference of follow-up period or the non-cement fixation in our cohort (Supplementary Table i).

The cumulative risk of infection was 10% at ten years. The deep infection rate in children with extendable prostheses was between 18% and 38%.15,23 A previous report of adult endoprostheses described a high incidence of infection within two years.24 By contrast, our results showed a high late infection rate (> 2 years; 50%), which was related to additional invasive procedures and patient longevity. These results emphasize the importance of patient education and preventative strategies. A noninvasive grower may contribute to decreasing the rate of infection.25,26

Our study showed a high incidence of cortical atrophy or stress shielding, which could lead to future complications, such as periprosthetic/prosthesis fracture or aseptic loosening in longer follow-ups. Two previous studies reported the incidence of stress shielding after EPR.27,28 Kim et al28 reported a 42% incidence of stress shielding in skeletally immature patients with bone sarcoma. Herein, patients aged < ten years were associated with a high risk of stress shielding. A similar correlation between age and stress shielding was observed in another study.29 Compressive osteointegrative endoprosthetic devices may help reduce late revisions due to periprosthetic fracture, aseptic loosening, or prosthesis breakage, which showed no evidence of late-onset cortical atrophy or stress shielding at the bone–prosthesis interface in the long term.30

Patient-reported functional/QoL outcomes were insufficient in previous reports (Supplementary Table i). Our results showed that cementless minimally invasive extendable endoprostheses offered acceptable functional/QoL outcomes in the long term. However, multiple additional surgeries for complications were associated with worse MSTS. Modifications in the extension mechanism and the introduction of compressive osteointegrative endoprosthetic device or noninvasive grower may contribute to the reduction of additional surgeries for complications. Moreover, our data indicated that restoration of limb length within 3 cm was crucial to maintain patients’ function.

The principal alternative for reconstruction in children is biological reconstruction using allografts or autografts.31-34 However, biological reconstructions have not been proven as reliable in lower-limb osteoarticular resections due to chondrolysis, osteonecrosis, LLD, and high failure rates (40% to 70%).33,35 Rotationplasty is a well-established and functional alternative, but many patients and parents do not prefer this option for cosmetic reasons.36,37 Distraction osteogenesis may be suitable when a joint-sparing resection can be performed in skeletally immature patients with a projected LLD and expected long-term survival.38

We acknowledge limitations to our study. First, our study is a retrospective review, and selection, indication, and expertise biases exist with regard to the initial surgery and additional procedures. In particular, selection or indication bias exists when multiple reconstruction options are available. Moreover, our data lack a cemented EPR comparison group. Second, patients were treated over two decades; we were unable to adjust for important confounders, including evolution in treatment strategies and medical/surgical management/techniques. Third, similar to many endoprosthesis studies this study contained a heterogeneous group of patients with small patient numbers.13,15,23,24 Our study may be underpowered to detect other factors independently associated with worse MSTS or revision-free survival rates. Considering the rarity of bone sarcoma in skeletally immature children, this is one of the largest studies focusing on cementless minimally invasive extendable endoprostheses. Fourth, it is important to recognize that while the amputation rate at the time of last follow-up remains low, the trajectory for many of these patients is far from straightforward, including the possibility of amputation or further reconstructive surgeries. These factors must be viewed not only through the lens of clinical outcomes, but also in terms of the broader physical, psychological, and social implications.

Cementless minimally invasive extendable endoprostheses are reliable reconstruction options in children achieving effective restoration of limb length, high rate of limb salvage, and acceptable function/QoL in the long term. However, these procedures are associated with a high failure and complication rate, particularly for structural failures. Multiple additional surgeries due to postoperative complications and LLD > 3 cm were associated with worse MSTS. Future research should continue to explore better endoprosthesis designs or alternative procedures to mitigate these risks and improve the long-term outcomes.

Take home message

- A retrospective, multicentric study was conducted and reviewed 45 children who underwent cementless minimally invasive extendable endoprosthetic replacement.

- We reported the long-term functional, quality of life (QoL) outcomes, implant-related complications, and the risk factors which were associated with worse Musculoskeletal Tumor Society scores.

- These are reliable reconstruction options achieving effective restoration of limb length, high rate of limb salvage, and acceptable function/QoL in the long term, although these procedures are associated with a high failure and complication rate.
