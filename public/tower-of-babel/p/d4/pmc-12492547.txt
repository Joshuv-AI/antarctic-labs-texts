Effects of different land use on functional genes of soil microbial carbon and phosphorus cycles in the desert steppe zone of the Loess Plateau
Authors: Weijie Jin, Yubao Zhang, Xuesi Su, Zhongkui Xie, Ruoyu Wang, Yajun Wang, Yang Qiu, Yuhui He
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractDesert grassland ecosystems on China’s Loess Plateau are characterized by diverse land use types and varying human disturbances. We aimed to evaluate how land use influences soil microbial communities and functional genes related to carbon (C) and phosphorus (P) cycling. To do this, we selected five representative land use types: natural grassland, 20-year abandoned farmland, 12-year alfalfa grassland, 5-year Lanzhou lily farmland, and 17-year Platycladus orientalis forest. High-throughput metagenomic sequencing and soil physicochemical analyses were conducted. Proteobacteria dominated the nutrient-rich lily soil, while Actinobacteria were more abundant in the other soils. Available phosphorus (AP) had the strongest influence on microbial community structure and gene composition (p < 0.01). The relative abundance of ppdK, rpiB, glpX, and epi (C fixation genes), and purS (purine metabolism) was significantly higher in forest soil than in abandoned farmland (p < 0.05). Similarly, forest soil showed elevated levels of mttB and acs (methanogenesis), sdhA (TCA cycle), pstS (P transport), and pps (pyruvate metabolism) compared to alfalfa soil. Lily soil exhibited significantly higher abundance of acr genes (involved in the hydroxypropionate–hydroxybutylate cycle) and phnE (an ATP-binding cassette transporter) than natural grassland and alfalfa soils (p < 0.05). Microbial networks involved in C and P cycling were simpler but more functionally specialized in forest soil. Positive microbial interactions related to C and P cycling were strongest in lily soil. These findings provide important insights into soil microbial functional adaptation and offer a foundation for sustainable land use management on the Loess Plateau.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-025-04305-9.Keywords: Metagenomes, Land use types, Carbon cycle, Phosphorus cycle

Background

The Loess Plateau desert grassland is an important part of China’s interior and is an important representative ecosystem of northwestern China. The region’s low precipitation, arid climate, and sparse vegetation make the ecosystem very fragile [1]. Human intervention to protect natural vegetation is necessary to improve the ecological environment and promote regional vegetation recovery [2, 3]. Diverse land uses are a typical feature of the Loess Plateau under human disturbance [4, 5]. Different land uses may have long-term effects on soil structure [6], physicochemical properties [7, 8], plant and animal residues, and particularly on soil microbial communities [9], including their α-diversity (e.g., Shannon index), community composition, and the abundance of functional genes related to biogeochemical cycling [10, 11]. For instance, among five land-use types in China’s Pearl River estuary, microbial diversity was highest in planted woodland, followed by cropland, natural wetland, and wasteland, and lowest in construction land [12]. These shifts are primarily driven by differences in surface vegetation, disturbance intensity, and external organic matter inputs and transformation [13, 14]. Microbial indicators such as diversity, taxonomic composition, and functional potential are sensitive to land-use changes and are directly linked to key ecological processes. Specifically, changes in microbial composition and gene expression influence soil carbon cycling (e.g., organic matter decomposition, carbon fixation, and methane metabolism) and phosphorus transformation (e.g., organic P mineralization via phosphatase-encoding genes). Therefore, understanding microbial responses under different land-use patterns is essential to assess ecosystem function and sustainability.

Changes in land use caused by human activities lead to significant alterations in soil organic carbon (SOC), profoundly influencing the soil carbon cycle. This, in turn, impacts soil fertility, agricultural productivity, food security, and broader concerns such as climate change and environmental health [15, 16]. Empirical studies have shown that paddy and vegetable fields in China accumulate SOC more rapidly than drylands, with increases of 37.1% and 25.5%, respectively, indicating that farmland type significantly influences SOC content [17]. To better understand the underlying processes of SOC dynamics, it is essential to investigate the functional genes involved in microbial carbon cycling. Microorganisms are the primary drivers of soil carbon transformation, and their encoded genes regulate the enzymatic steps responsible for carbon fixation, organic matter decomposition, and greenhouse gas fluxes. Thus, examining the prevalence and diversity of microbial functional genes provides insight into the potential capacity of soils to store carbon, release CO2 or CH4, and respond to land-use changes [18, 19]. The soil microbial C cycle mainly encompasses carbon fixation, central carbon metabolism, and methane metabolism [20]. Central metabolism includes pathways such as the Entner–Doudoroff (ED) pathway, gluconeogenesis, the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, and the Embden–Meyerhof pathway, which are fundamental for the degradation and transformation of organic carbon substrates. Methane metabolism includes both methane production (via hydrogenotrophic, acetoclastic, and methylotrophic pathways) and methane oxidation (e.g., conversion from methane to methanol, and subsequently to formaldehyde). These pathways are ecologically significant, as they regulate carbon fluxes, influence soil carbon stabilization or loss, and contribute to the emission or mitigation of greenhouse gases.

Phosphorus (P) is a key nutrient for maintaining ecosystem development and stability, and is one of the limiting nutrients that strongly affects ecosystem productivity [21, 22]. P exists in organic and inorganic forms in the soil and the transformations among different forms of P are achieved through 10 biological processes involved in P metabolism [23]. The alteration of land use significantly impacts the abundance of soil phosphatases, as well as phosphatase-encoding genes and associated microorganisms. This results in notable alterations in the distribution and availability of phosphorus [24, 25]. In recent years, studies on the Loess Plateau have shown that with the progression of vegetation restoration, the diversity and abundance of microbial functional genes related to phosphorus (P) cycling—such as inorganic phosphate-solubilizing genes (gcd, ppx) and organic phosphorus-mineralizing genes (phnM, phnI)—have significantly increased [26, 27]. These gene changes are positively correlated with soil available phosphorus (AP) content. These functional genes participate in the release of mineral-bound phosphorus and the degradation of complex organic phosphorus compounds, thereby enhancing the bioavailability of soil phosphorus and promoting plant nutrient acquisition and ecosystem recovery. These findings suggest that changes in land use not only affect soil physicochemical properties but may also drive soil phosphorus cycling by regulating the functional structure of microbial communities. Therefore, exploring the distribution patterns and ecological responses of microbial P-cycling genes under different land use types is of great significance for understanding nutrient reconstruction and microbially mediated processes during ecosystem restoration.

Previous studies have shown that the status and distribution of carbon (C) and phosphorus (P) in soil are strongly influenced by land use and associated management practices—such as fertilization, cultivation, vegetation restoration, and land abandonment. These elements are tightly coupled in biogeochemical cycles, and understanding their interactions is essential for predicting ecosystem function and sustainability [28, 29]. However, these studies focused on the impacts of changes of land uses on soil C and P fractions in a specific region, with limited understanding of how microbial functional genes related to C and P cycling respond to land use changes [30]. To fill this knowledge gap, our study aims to investigate how different land use types influence the composition and abundance of soil microbial communities and their functional genes involved in carbon and phosphorus cycling in the desert grasslands of the Loess Plateau. We hypothesize that land use changes significantly affect microbial gene patterns associated with C and P metabolism, and that these changes correlate with variations in soil physicochemical properties. Using high-throughput metagenomic sequencing combined with soil physical and chemical analyses, we expect to elucidate the mechanistic links between land management, microbial functional potential, and soil biogeochemical cycling.

Methods

Study site and soil sampling

We selected five land use types at the Gansu Gaolan Field Scientific Observation and Research Station for Agricultural Ecosystem: barren hills and slopes of natural grassland, abandoned farmland, alfalfa (Medicago sativa L.) grassland, Lanzhou lily (Lilium davidii var. unicolor) farmland, and artificial forest (Platycladus orientalis (L.) Franco) (Fig. S1). The station is located at 36º13'N latitude, 103º47'E longitude, at an elevation of 1700 to 1800 m. The predominant soil type is a silty loam derived from loess bedrock, classified as Calcisol (WRB) and Arenosol (FAO) with sandy loam texture. For each land use type, one independent site was selected. Within each site, three independent plots were established as biological replicates. At each plot, soil samples were collected from five randomly selected points at a depth of 0–10 cm. The five samples from each plot were thoroughly mixed to form one composite sample per plot. Thus, a total of three composite samples were obtained per site. After collection, each composite sample was homogenized and then divided into two parts: one portion was air-dried and sieved (< 2 mm) for physicochemical property analysis, and the other portion was immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at –80 °C for metagenomic sequencing.

Metagenomes sequencing

Soil genomic DNA was extracted from each composite sample using a commercial soil DNA extraction kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Each land use type included three biological replicates, resulting in 15 total samples. The quality of extracted DNA was assessed by (1) quantifying DNA concentration with a fluorometer, (2) evaluating purity using a spectrophotometer (260/280 and 260/230 ratios), and (3) checking DNA integrity on 1% agarose gel electrophoresis. High-quality DNA was then sheared to ~ 400 bp fragments using an ultrasonicator. DNA libraries were prepared using a commercial DNA library preparation kit, which included end repair, adapter ligation, PCR amplification, and Purification. The quality and concentration of the constructed libraries were assessed using a Qubit 3.0 fluorometer and an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer. Metagenomic sequencing was performed using the Illumina HiSeq 2500 platform, generating an average of ~ 6.5 Gb of clean data per sample. Sequencing was carried out by Guangdong Megger Gene Technology Co., Ltd. (Guangdong, China).

Bioinformatics analysis

Quality control of raw reads was performed using Trimmomatic (v0.32) with the following parameters: LEADING:3 TRAILING:3 SLIDINGWINDOW:5:20 MINLEN:50 [31]. The minimum length threshold (MINLEN:50) was used to exclude short reads (< 50 bp), which are more likely to yield low-confidence annotations in metagenomic analyses. High-quality reads were assembled de novo using MEGAHIT (v1.1.2) with a k-mer range of 35–95 (step size = 20). Contigs shorter than 300 bp were removed using the filterbylength.sh script from BBMap. Open reading frames (ORFs) were predicted using MetaGene (v2.34.0) [32], and ORFs ≥ 100 bp were translated into amino acid sequences. All predicted gene sequences were clustered using CD-HIT (v4.6.1) [33] to generate a non-redundant gene catalog, with the longest sequence in each cluster used as the representative. Taxonomic annotation was performed by aligning representative amino acid sequences to the NCBI-NR database using BLASTP (v2.2.31 +, e-value ≤ 1e − 4). Functional annotation was conducted using DIAMOND (v0.8.35, e-value ≤ 0.001) against the KEGG database (v94.2) to assign gene functions [34]. Although KEGG is a broad-spectrum functional database, it includes a wide array of well-curated genes and modules related to microbial carbon and phosphorus cycling. For carbon cycling, we focused on KEGG modules associated with carbon fixation pathways, central carbon metabolism (e.g., glycolysis/gluconeogenesis, TCA cycle), and methane metabolism (e.g., methanogenesis and methane oxidation). For phosphorus cycling, we examined KEGG Orthology (KO) entries related to phosphatase activity (e.g., alkaline phosphatase, K01077) and phosphate transport (e.g., Pst system, K02036–K02040). These annotations enabled us to track microbial functional potential related to C and P transformations under different land use types in the Loess Plateau. While we recognize the value of specialized databases, KEGG provided sufficient resolution for our comparative metagenomic analysis.

Determination of the physical and chemical factors of the soil

Soil pH and electrical conductivity (EC) were measured using the electrode method (Mettler Toledo S475, Columbus, OH, USA). Soil salinity content (SSC) was determined by the gravimetric method [35] and soil organic carbon (SOC) was measured using the K2Cr2O7–H2SO4 oxidation method [36]. Soil inorganic carbon (SIC) was analyzed using the carbonate gasometric method [37]. while total nitrogen (TN) was measured through Kjeldahl digestion with concentrated sulfuric acid. Total phosphorus (TP) was quantified via the sodium hydroxide fusion technique [38], and total potassium (TK) was analyzed using sodium hydroxide fusion followed by flame photometry [39]. Ammonium nitrogen (NH4+-N) was extracted with 0.01 mol/L calcium chloride and determined by the phenol disulfonic acid colorimetric method. Nitrate nitrogen (NO3--N) was extracted with 2 mol/L KCl and measured using NaCl colorimetry. Available phosphorus (AP) was determined by sodium bicarbonate extraction and ultraviolet spectrophotometry, while available potassium (AK) was measured via ammonium acetate extraction and flame photometry. Total sulfur (TS) was determined by magnesium nitrate oxidation and quantified using barium sulfate turbidimetry.

Statistical analysis

We tested the normality of soil physicochemical data using the Shapiro–Wilk test and assessed homogeneity of variance with Levene’s test prior to conducting parametric tests such as ANOVA; where these assumptions were not met, we employed the non-parametric Kruskal–Wallis test using the rstatix package. To explore relationships among soil properties, microbial communities, and functional genes, we conducted Spearman correlation analysis using the psych package and applied Mantel tests (via the linkET package) to evaluate the correlation between environmental and microbial community distance matrices. Redundancy analysis (RDA) was conducted with the vegan package to identify how environmental variables influence microbial community structure. Prior to RDA, variance inflation factor (VIF) scores were used to check for multicollinearity, and variables with VIF > 10 were excluded. Forward selection was applied using the ordistep function in vegan to retain significant variables for the final RDA model. To identify the main predictors of microbial functional profiles, we employed random forest analysis using the rfPermute package, which provided variable importance scores and permutation-based significance tests. We also performed Analysis of Similarities (ANOSIM) based on Bray–Curtis distances to compare differences in functional gene composition across land-use types. For microbial interaction networks, co-occurrence networks were constructed based on Spearman correlations (r > 0.9, p < 0.01) among the top 300 most abundant genera. To reduce compositional bias, only edges with FDR-adjusted p-values < 0.01 were retained. Network topological properties were calculated, and the networks were visualized using Gephi software. Additionally, LEfSe analysis and further visualization of functional biomarkers were performed using the Tutools platform. All plots and data visualizations were produced using the ggplot2 package unless otherwise specified.

Results

Differences in soil physical and chemical factors between land uses

Soil EC, SSC, TN, NO3--N, AP, AK, and TS were significantly higher in lily soil compared to other land use types (Table 1; Table S1). Statistical significance was determined using one-way ANOVA followed by Duncan’s multiple range test (P < 0.05), and different lowercase letters in Table S1 indicate significant differences among land use types. Abandoned farmland had the highest soil TP and TK. Soil TP was lowest in natural grassland, with significant differences (p < 0.05) between it and abandoned farmland, lily soil, and forest soil, but non-significant differences between it and alfalfa soil. Alfalfa soil had the lowest soil SIC content, with significant differences (p < 0.05) between it and abandoned farmland, lily soil, and forest soil, and no significant difference (p < 0.05) from natural grassland. Forest soil had the lowest soil AP content, with significant differences from abandoned farmland and lily soil and no significant difference compared with natural grassland and alfalfa soil. The soil pH levels were notably elevated in natural grasslands and deserted farmlands compared to other types of land use, it was lowest in forest soil and significantly different from all other land uses. Soil AK, NO3-N, SOC, NH4-N and did not differ significantly between land uses. The heatmap illustrating correlations among soil physicochemical properties (Fig. S2) indicated that pH exhibited strong negative correlations with EC, SSC, and NO3--N. In contrast, SSC showed a strong positive correlation with EC, and SIC was negatively correlated with TP. Additionally, AP was positively correlated with TS, TP, and TK. The strong positive correlation between AP and TS may indicate that inputs such as organic amendments or plant-derived residues contribute to the concurrent accumulation of both nutrients, likely by stimulating microbial activity and organic matter turnover. This co-variation suggests that the observed microbial functional responses to AP may be partially driven by or confounded with TS dynamics. Therefore, when interpreting the influence of AP on microbial community structure and function, it is important to consider such interactions among environmental variables. These findings underscore the importance of integrated analysis when evaluating environmental controls on microbial biogeochemical functions.

Differences in microbial communities across land uses

We obtained metagenomes data for the 15 samples from soils with the five land uses (Table S2). The principal coordinates analysis (PCoA) at the genus level (Fig. S3A) indicated a clear separation of microbial community β-diversity across different land uses along the PCoA1 axis, accounting for 45.2% of the total variation, and the PCoA2 axis explained 27.0% of the variance. PERMANOVA (Adonis) analysis showed that land use significantly influenced the microbial community composition with R2 = 0.396 and p = 0.065. For functional genes (Fig. S3B), PCoA also revealed clear clustering patterns among land uses, with Adonis results indicating a stronger effect (R2 = 0.645, p = 0.02). The ANOSIM analysis revealed notable variances among microbial populations in distinct land utilization practices (R = 0.243, p = 0.032). According to Mantel tests (Fig. S4), there was a significant correlation between the microbial community and AP and SSC at the genus level, while the correlation with other environmental factors was not significant. Although the ANOSIM test showed statistical significance (p = 0.032), the relatively low R value (0.243) suggests only weak dissimilarity between microbial communities across land uses, which may reflect overlapping environmental drivers or inherent microbial resilience. This limitation has been acknowledged in the discussion. At the family level, the microbial community showed a significant correlation with pH and TK, with non-significant correlations identified with the remaining environmental factors.

RDA analyses showed that soil pH and available phosphorus (AP) significantly influenced microbial communities at the genus level (Fig. 1A). After screening for multicollinearity (excluding variables with VIF > 10) and forward selection, only pH and AP were retained for the RDA. Variance partitioning analysis revealed that AP and pH together explained 20.75% of the variation in microbial community composition, with AP and pH contributing 11.8% and 9.1%, respectively (Fig. 1C). Mantel tests further indicated that AP and soil salinity content (SSC) had significant effects on microbial genus-level community composition, while other environmental factors showed no significant influence.

Analysis of the species distribution in various land uses at the phylum level (Fig. 2A) showed that the dominant microbes in lily farmland soils were copiotrophic Proteobacteria, while oligotrophic Actinobacteria dominated the other soils. The proportion of Acidobacteria was greater in both abandoned farmland and alfalfa soil, but the differences were not significant. At the taxonomic genus level, the 40 most prevalent species included Nocardioides, Rhodoplanes, Sphingomonas, Iiumatobacter, and Frankia, which differed significantly among land uses (Fig. 2B). We selected genera with a relative prevalent greater than 0.05% as the key taxa under the five land uses, and analyzed them using random forest models, which were established with an estimated out-of-bag (OOB) error rate of 26.7%. The key taxa were sorted based on the mean decrease of the Gini index, and we selected the 17 top genera (Fig. 2C) for the species abundance difference tests. Caulobacter, Skermanella, Nakamurella, and Phenylobacterium were key taxa under all land uses, and the relative abundance in the different land uses also differed significantly among land uses (Fig. 2D). Response of C and P cycling functional groups to different land uses

A total of 9572 KEGG Orthology identifiers (KOs) were identified in the merged samples. PCoA based on KEGG orthologs revealed distinct clustering patterns of microbial functional β-diversity across land uses along the principal coordinate axes (Fig.S3B), which explained 61.4% of the variation in the overall microbial functional genes; the PcoA2 axis explained 17.4%. The ANOSIM analysis indicated that microbial functional gene profiles significantly differed among the five land uses (R = 0.483, p = 0.002). The RDA model cumulatively explained 40.4% of the variation in microbial functional gene composition across different land uses, indicating that environmental factors strongly influence functional gene distribution and potentially affect ecosystem functioning. Soil AP, TN, and TK significantly affected microbial functional genes in all samples (Fig. 1B). The variance decomposition showed that AP explained 13.6% of the variation, versus 12.0% for TN, 0.4% for the interaction between AP and TN, 9.1% for TK, and 11.4% for the interaction between TK and TN (Fig. 1D).

We identified 83 genes that were involved in C fixation, 72 in carbon center metabolism, and 55 in methane metabolism (Table S3). Key carbon fixation pathways showed distinct gene abundance differences across land uses. The dicarboxylate-4 hydroxybutyrate cycle was characterized by higher abundance of genes such as sdhA and pps, while tktA and rpiB were more abundant in the Calvin–Benson–Bassham cycle. Other important genes included epi in the hydroxypropionate–hydroxybutyrate cycle, ppdK in the reductive citrate cycle, and acsB in the Wood–Ljungdahl pathway. These key genes exhibited significant variation across land uses (Fig. 3). Specifically, the abundance of sdhA was markedly greater in forest soil compared to alfalfa soil. ppsA abundance was highest in forest soil. rplB, ppdK, and epi abundance was highest in forest soil and was significantly greater levels than in abandoned farmland. ppdK abundance was also notably higher in forest soil than in natural grassland soil. The relative abundance of epi was significantly higher in forest soil than in lily soil (p < 0.05). Likewise, glpX was most abundant in natural grassland and forest soils, with forest soils showing a significantly greater level than abandoned farmland. In the lily soil, the relative abundance of the acryloyl-CoA reductase gene was significantly elevated compared to that in natural grassland and alfalfa soils. Among the carbon-centered metabolic pathways, the abundance of devB, which is involved in the Entner–Doudoroff pathway; the abundance of sdhA and sdhC, which are involved in the TCA cycle; and the abundance of fbp3, glpX, and glpX-SEBP, which are involved in the gluconeogenesis pathway, differed significantly among the five land uses. Among the methane metabolism pathways, the abundance of mcrA and mttB, which are involved in the methylotrophic methanogenesis pathway, and the abundance of acs, which is involved in the acetoclastic methanogenesis pathway, differed significantly among the land uses. The acs and mttB abundance was highest in forest soil and both were significantly higher than in alfalfa soil (Fig. 3A). Rather than focusing on the full completeness of entire pathways, we specifically evaluated genes representative of major carbon metabolic routes to detect shifts in key functional potentials associated with land use change. Although mttB and acs are classically associated with methanogenesis, taxonomic annotation indicated that these genes were predominantly carried by bacterial taxa such as Pseudonocardia, Streptomyces, and Nocardioides, rather than methanogenic archaea. This implies that these genes may participate in non-classical methylotrophic or acetogenic processes under micro-anaerobic conditions. There were significant correlations between C cycle genes and AP (Fig. 4A). We used random forest models to identify pinpoint essential functional genes associated with C cycling in the five land uses. The estimated OOB error rate was 23.1% for the established random forest. This revealed that acsE, mcl, mcrA, aclB, pmoC, sdhA, IDH3, pta, acs, and mttB might be the pinpoint essential functional genes associated with C cycling in the five land uses (Fig. 5A).

We found 111 genes associated with the P cycle (Table S4). Significant variation (p = 0.036) was observed in the abundance of ppa, a key player in the oxidative phosphorylation pathway, across different land uses. It was highest in natural grassland and lowest in abandoned farmland. deoB abundance (p = 0.021) and gnd (p = 0.039), which are involved in the pentose phosphate pathway, differed significantly among the land uses, with the highest abundance in natural grassland and forest soils. The abundance of deoB in the alfalfa soil was noticeably greater compared to that in the lily soil. purQ and purS, which are associated with the purine metabolism pathway, also differed significantly among land uses. The abundance of purQ in abandoned farmland was notably lower compared to that in forest soil. The forest soil exhibited a notably higher abundance of pps and pstS genes compared to the alfalfa soil. In the lily soil, the phnE gene was the most abundant, significantly surpassing its presence in natural grassland and abandoned farmland soils (Fig. 3B). Notably, the elevated abundance of pstS and phnE genes, especially in soils with low available phosphorus (e.g., forest and grassland), may reflect microbial strategies to cope with P limitation, rather than indicating enhanced P turnover. This suggests possible nutrient stress responses rather than increased mineralization activity. P cycling genes were significantly correlated with AP, TK, and pH (Fig. 4B). Random forest models were employed to pinpoint the essential functional genes associated with the cycling of phosphorus. The estimated OOB error rate of the established random forest model was 37.5%. The model identified pps, prsA, php, ushA, pbfA, phoA, and ugpC as potential pinpoint essential functional genes associated with in P cycling under the five land uses (Fig. 5B). Additionally, phoD and phoA—key genes involved in organic phosphorus mineralization—were detected in the dataset, although their abundance was generally low across all land use types. This may suggest limited microbial mineralization activity or reflect the dominance of inorganic phosphorus forms in these soils.

Although this study focused on individual carbon cycling genes, we acknowledge that evaluating the completeness and activity potential of entire metabolic pathways (e.g., using pathway inference tools) would offer a more comprehensive understanding of microbial functional capacity. This represents a direction for future studies aimed at assessing holistic C-cycling efficiency and redundancy under varying land use types.

Responses of C and P cycle microorganisms to the different land uses

We identified 70 phyla, 165 orders, and 968 genera through the metagenomes data that were involved in the C cycle, and 80 phyla, 172 orders, and 970 genera that were associated with the P cycle. The Mantel test examination (Fig. 4B) revealed a notable positive correlation at the genus level between the microbial communities participating in C cycling and AP as well as SSC. Moreover, the microbial communities engaged in P cycling exhibited a significant positive correlation with AP, TP, and TK. To identify the signature microorganisms involved in C and P cycling in the five land uses, we performed LefSe analysis at the genus level to determine taxonomic biomarkers (Fig. 6). The biomarkers involved in C and P cycling were Sphingosinicella, Phenylobacterium, Gaiella, and Nakamurella. Biomarkers specific to the C cycle were Rhodoplanes, Mycolicibacter, Lacipirellula, Aestuariivirga, and Pilimelia in the forest soil. Biomarkers specific to P cycling were Pseudorhodoplanes, Lihuaxuella, and Alsobacter. In natural grassland, Pyrinomonas was a biomarker associated with both C and P cycling. Sphingomonas was specific to the C cycle. Biomarkers specific to the P cycle were Mumia and Caulobacter. In the lily soil, the biomarker involved in the C cycle was Sedimenticola. The biomarkers involved in the P cycle were Roseomonas, Aquabacterium, and Burkholderia. In abandoned farmland, the biomarkers involved in the C cycle were Nitrososphaera and Chitinophaga, but we found no biomarkers involved in the P cycle. In the alfalfa soil, we found no biomarkers involved in the C and P cycles.

We further analyzed the taxonomic attribution of key carbon cycling genes to microbial genera. Microorganisms with an acs function (Fig. 7A) were mainly Solirubrobacter, Nocardioides, Streptomyces, and Pseudonocardia, with Actinophytocola and Pseudonocardia contributing more in natural grassland and abandoned farmland, and Streptomyces contributing more in forest soil. Nocardioides, Mesorhizobium, Nordella, Hypericibacter, Pseudonocardia, and Afifella were the main genera contributing to mttB in the five land uses (Fig. 7B), with Pseudonocardia showing higher abundance in natural grassland. Luteimonas was the major contributor to sdhA in the abandoned farmland and lily soils, but not prominent in the other land uses (Fig. 7C). Mycobacterium had the strongest contribution to pps in forest and alfalfa soils (Fig. 7D), while Mycolicibacterium also contributed more in forest soil. However, it is important to note that taxonomic attribution alone does not confirm functional expression or ecological impact. The functional redundancy observed in microbial communities may result in different taxa performing similar ecological functions. Therefore, future research should combine metatranscriptomic or metaproteomic approaches to validate Gene expression and clarify the ecological roles of these microbial taxa in situ. To explore microbial interactions associated with C and P cycling, we conducted network analysis based on the 300 most abundant genera under different land uses (Fig. 8, Table S5). The results showed that the network structure varied greatly across land use types. Forest soil networks had the lowest complexity and fewest edges (484 for C cycling and 400 for P cycling), along with the lowest average degree and clustering coefficient. In contrast, lily soil networks showed the highest complexity and the greatest percentage of positive correlations (81.3% for C and 73.2% for P). Alfalfa soils had the lowest proportion of positively correlated edges (54.7% for C and 54.1% for P), indicating weaker microbial co-occurrence patterns.

Discussion

Influence of soil characteristics on microbial composition and functional genes under the different land uses

Proteobacteria, Actinobacteria, and Acidobacteria were the most prevalent phyla in the microbial populations across the five different land uses, collectively making up over 80% of the total relative abundance. This is consistent with other studies on the Loess Plateau on microbial community composition [40, 41]. Eutrophic Proteobacteria were found to be the predominant bacterial species in the soil of the lily farmland, whereas oligotrophic Actinobacteria dominated the soil in areas with different land uses. In previous research, Ascomycetes were also dominant in lily test fields in Cao Yuan and Yuan Jia Wan villages in Lanzhou City [42]. This could be attributed to the frequent fertilization and irrigation practices typically employed in agricultural fields. Compared to other land uses, lily soil had higher TN, SSC, TN, NO3-N, AP, and AK. The selective enrichment of the 3-hydroxypropionate/4-hydroxybutyrate (3-HP/4-HB) cycle in lily farmland soils may be linked to specific environmental characteristics such as higher total nitrogen (TN), soil salt content (SSC), and available phosphorus (AP), as well as frequent irrigation and fertilization. These factors can create oligotrophic microzones due to rapid nutrient turnover and transient nutrient Limitation, which in turn may favor autotrophic microorganisms that utilize energetically efficient carbon fixation pathways like the 3-HP/4-HB cycle. Moreover, these soils may also experience intermittent anaerobic conditions, promoting the presence of microorganisms adapted to low-energy or stress environments, where this pathway has been previously observed. The Proteobacteria comprise a typical eutrophic taxon that reproduces quickly and is better suited to eutrophic environments [43, 44].

Previous studies have indicated that the transition of an ecosystem from sandy to forested leads to a decline in the proportion of Actinobacteria from 40.2% to 14.7% as precipitation levels rise. Meanwhile, there is an observed increase in the proportion of Proteobacteria from 21.4% to 35.4% [40]. Caulobacter (Alphaproteobacteria) abundance was greater in forest soil and natural grassland compared to other land uses, aligning with the discovery that the highest mean relative abundance of Caulobacter was observed in forest soils [45, 46]. Caulobacter has cellulose, lignin, and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbon degradation functions [47–49]. This aligns with the finding that woodland soil microbial communities have higher activity of ligninolytic enzymes [45]. Methylobacterium, a type of Alphaproteobacteria, consists of parthenogenetic methylotrophs, which are capable of utilizing methanol and other one-carbon compounds for energy and carbon. Additionally, these microorganisms have the ability to produce growth factors that enhance plant growth [50].

The composition and structure of soil microbial communities are significantly influenced by soil nutrient availability and other physicochemical attributes [51]. Our RDA analyses confirmed this, as soil AP and pH significantly affected microbial communities at the genus level in all land uses. Similarly, soil AP, TN, and TK significantly affected microbial functional genes in all land uses. The microbial processes related to pH levels and the biogeochemical cycling of carbon, nitrogen, and sulfur generate significant quantities of hydrogen and hydroxide ions. Consequently, these activities have an impact on soil pH, emerging as a key determinant influencing the composition of soil microbial communities [15, 52].

P limitation is prevalent in temperate climates with calcareous soils, such as in China’s Gansu Province [53], where our study sites were located. Although considerable total P reserves exist in most soils, ecosystems often encounter P availability limitations in calcareous soils due to the prevalence of P fixation [54, 55]. TP and AP differed significantly among the land uses. The microbial communities were significantly correlated with AP and SSC at the genus level, and with pH and TK at the family level but not significantly correlated with other environmental factors, that there was a significant positive correlation between AP and TK, and that there was a significant correlation between C and P cycling genes and AP. Thus, AP was the most significant soil physicochemical factor influencing microbial community composition and functional genes.

Effect of different land uses on C cycle gene abundance

Differences in the abundance of functional genes associated with C cycling will therefore strongly affect the C metabolism function of microbial communities, consequently influencing soil C cycling [56, 57]. The C cycle genes abundance is closely related to the vegetation type [58] and soil nutrients [59]. Our findings indicate that fluctuations in the levels of important genes in CO2 fixation pathways have an impact on the alterations in soil microbial C metabolism. The preference for specific CO2 fixation pathways might be influenced by the type of ecosystem, given that the constituents of microbial communities play a pivotal role in directing these pathways [60].

In semi-arid grassland soils, the rTCA cycle is the preferred CO2 fixing pathway when low-energy is the prevailing condition [61]. ppdK genes and pycA are involved in the rTCA cycle, and differed significantly among the five land uses. The abundance of ppdK was significantly higher in forest soil than in abandoned farmland and natural grassland soils. In our study, the differences among land uses in the functional genes for soil C fixation focused on the dicarboxylate–hydroxybutyrate cycle and the Calvin–Benson–Bassham cycle. Microorganisms fix CO2 to oxaloacetate via the dicarboxylate–hydroxybutyrate cycle [62]. sdhA and sdhC, which are involved in this pathway, are also involved in the TCA cycle, and pps is also involved in the pyruvate metabolism pathway of the P cycle.

The Calvin–Benson–Bassham cycle, widely found in plants, algae, and phototrophic autotrophic microorganisms in nature, serves as a vital CO2 fixation pathway [63]. tktA, rpiB, glpX, and glpX-SEBP, which are associated with the Calvin–Benson–Bassham cycle, differed among the land uses in their abundance, and the abundance of rpiB and glpX was significantly higher in abandoned farmland than in forest soil. The 3-hydroxypropionate/4-hydroxybutyrate cycle stands out as the most energy-efficient aerobic carbon fixation pathway, making it particularly well-adapted for environments with limited nutrients [64]. The pathway uses HCO3− as a substrate, and has been found to exist in oligotrophic lakes, hot springs, thermal springs, oceans, and agricultural soils. The abundance of epi genes involved in this pathway was also significantly higher in the forest soil than in abandoned farmland soil. glpX encodes the sugar isoform-limiting enzyme fructose 1, 6-bisphosphatase, which catalyzes the irreversible conversion of fructose 1,6-bisphosphate to fructose 6-phosphate [65]. glpX was significantly more abundant in forest soil than in abandoned farmland soil. The abundances of glpX, fbp3, and glpX-SEBP were all highest in natural grassland. The TCA cycle provides precursors for biosynthesis and provides energy [66]. sdhABCD encodes the succinate dehydrogenase part of this pathway [67]. sdhA and sdhC abundance differed significantly among the land uses, and sdhA abundance was significantly higher in forest soil than alfalfa soil.

Methane metabolism involves both methanogenic and methylotrophic oxidizing bacteria, and includes methane production, oxidation, and homeostasis in terrestrial ecosystems. Methanogenic bacteria contain enzymes capable of producing methane via CO2 and H2, via methylotrophic methanogenesis of monocarbon compounds, or by aceticlastic methanogenesis of acetate [68, 69]. TMA methyltransferase is a homologous member of the widely distributed MttB COG5598 enzyme superfamily, which spans hundreds of species of bacteria and archaea and participates in the methylotrophic methanogenesis pathway [70]. A significantly higher abundance of mttB was observed in forest soils relative to alfalfa soils (p < 0.05). There was a notably higher presence of aceticlastic methanogenesis-related acs in forest soil compared to alfalfa soil.

Impact of various land utilization on the abundance of P cycling genes

By solubilizing inorganic P (Pi) and mineralising organic P (Po), micro-organisms play an important role in maintaining and regulating soil P status, thereby increasing nutrient availability to plants [71, 72]. Therefore, it is critical to understand microbially driven P cycling and its ecological effects and responses. In this study, the abundance of pgtB, phoB, phoP, phoR, phoU, regX3, and senX3 genes did not differ significantly among the five land uses. phoB and phoR are also involved in the regulation of P starvation [73]. In bacteria, the uptake and utilization of Pi is mainly accomplished by phosphate-specific transport (Pst) and Pi transport. The Pst system generally consists of five proteins, of which PstS is a peripheral binding protein encoded by pstS; its function is mainly to bind Pi in the periplasmic space [74, 75]. The abundance of pstS was significantly higher in forest soil than in alfalfa soil, and the abundance of pstA, pstB, pstC, and pstS did not differ significantly among the land uses. While higher pstS and phnE gene abundance may reflect greater microbial activity in phosphorus uptake and cycling, it could also indicate microbial stress due to phosphorus limitation. In calcareous soils, such as those of Gansu Province, phosphorus is often poorly available due to strong fixation despite high total P. Therefore, the upregulation of phosphate transporter genes like pstS or 2-AEP transporters (e.g., phnE) in forest and lily soils might represent an adaptive response to phosphorus scarcity rather than simply more efficient cycling. This interpretation is supported by prior evidence showing that pstS expression increases under P-limited conditions.

The abundance of phnE was highest in lily soil. There were no significant difference in phnC, phnD, phnE, phnS, phnT, phnU, and phnV among the different land uses, which suggests that the 2AEP transporter rate was significantly higher in the lily soil than in natural grassland soil and alfalfa grassland [76]. ppdK is associated with the reductive citrate cycle and in pyruvate metabolism. Its abundance differed significantly among the land uses. The abundance of pckG and pps, which are involved in pyruvate metabolism, also differed significantly among land uses. Microbial PPS (pyruvate, water dikinase) and PPDK (pyruvate, phosphate dikinase) catalyze the phosphorylation of starch via a dikinase-type reaction [77]. pps abundance was higher in forest soil than in alfalfa, natural grassland, and abandoned farmland soils. PurQ is responsible for the production of ammonia through the conversion of glutamine to glutamate. PurL transfers ammonia molecules to formylglycinamide ribonucleotide in an ATP-dependent manner to produce formylglycinamide ribonucleotide and then to form formylglycinamidine ribonucleotide. It is hypothesised that PurS plays a role in facilitating the transfer of ammonia molecules from PurQ to PurL [78, 79]. Forest soil exhibited a greater prevalence of purS and purQ compared to abandoned farmland. Oxidative phosphorylation in soil is responsible for the production of ATP, which mainly involves ppa and ppk, with ppa abundance (but not ppk) differing significantly among the land uses.

Forest soil had simpler but more specialized microbial C and P cycling networks

Analysis of the species that contributed to differences in gene abundance showed that the key functional genes were contributed by two or three dominant microbial genera in the forest soil, but by no significant dominant microbes in natural grassland, abandoned farmland, alfalfa, and lily soils (Fig. 7, Fig. S5-12). Analysis by means of LEfSe showed that forest soil had the highest number of biomarkers (12) associated with C and P cycling, compared to 4, 2, 0, and 3 in natural grassland, abandoned farmland, alfalfa, and lily soils, respectively. This suggests that the microorganisms involved in forest soil are more specialized. In addition to network topology, trait-based and functional gene evidence also supports the conclusion that forest soil microbial communities exhibit greater specialization. First, LEfSe analysis revealed a higher number of C and P cycling biomarkers in forest soils. Second, dominant taxa contributing to functional gene abundance were fewer but more consistent in forest soils, suggesting functional redundancy is replaced by specialization. Third, genes related to pathways requiring higher biochemical investment (e.g., the 3-HP/4-HB cycle, acs, mttB) were more abundant in forest soils, implying adaptation to niche-specific ecological functions. Together, these trait and gene-based indicators reinforce the inference of higher specialization. Analysis by means of LEfSe and the species contributions showed that the identified biomarkers were not the dominant populations involved in differences in species contributions to the functional genes. This also suggests that the dominant taxa influence microbiome function due to their high relative abundance, whereas keystone taxa may be able to selectively alter specific members of the microbiome [80, 81].

We used network analysis to identify highly connected taxa in the identified communities [82, 83]. The number of edges in the microbial coexistence networks associated with the C and P cycles in forest soil was 484 and 400, respectively. This was the lowest among the five land uses. The number of edges in the coexistence network of microorganisms involved in the C and P cycles in lily soil were much higher, at 1628 and 1444, respectively. This was the highest number of edges among the five land uses. Avg.Degree, average path length, and network diameter for the microorganisms associated with C and P cycles were lowest in the forest soil. The strongest positive correlation was for the lily soil, which accounted for 81.3 and 73.2% of the positive correlations in the association curves for the microbial coexistence networks involved in the C and P cycles, respectively. Discovering microbial interactions using network analyses yields statistically significant findings that may not always accurately reflect genuine biological relationships. Therefore, it is important to emphasize that network results indicate potential associations rather than direct causal or functional mechanisms. Substantiating these connections necessitates additional validation through empirical testing and experimentation [84]. Based on the data collected in the current study, it is not feasible; therefore, this aspect should be taken into account for future research endeavors. However, our analysis findings indicate that the forest soil may have simpler but more specialized microbial networks for C and P cycling in the five land uses. This specialization likely reflects the forest soil’s adaptation to more stable nutrient inputs and efficient resource utilization, which can enhance carbon sequestration and nutrient retention. Such specialized networks contribute to ecosystem resilience and sustainable soil functioning, consistent with the results of straw and lignocellulose degradation experiments conducted in woodland, farmland, and abandoned farmland soils [85].

Conclusions

This study used metagenomic techniques to examine how different land uses shape soil microbial communities and functional genes involved in carbon (C) and phosphorus (P) cycling in the desert steppe region of the Loess Plateau. We identified available phosphorus (AP) as the most influential soil property driving microbial composition and gene abundance. Notably, eutrophic Proteobacteria dominated the microbial community in lily farmland, while oligotrophic Actinomycetes were more abundant in other land use types. Functional genes such as sdhA and pps, which play key roles in C and P metabolism, exhibited significant variation across land uses. Forest soil harbored higher abundances of C-fixation- and methanogenesis-related genes (ppdK, rpiB, glpX, epi, purS, mttB, acs), indicating enhanced microbial capacity for organic matter turnover. Meanwhile, lily farmland showed the highest 2AEP transporter gene abundance and the strongest positive microbial interactions related to C and P cycling. Furthermore, the forest soil exhibited a more specialized yet simplified microbial network structure. These findings provide new insights into the biogeochemical implications of land use change and offer valuable guidance for the sustainable management of soil resources in arid and semiarid ecosystems.
