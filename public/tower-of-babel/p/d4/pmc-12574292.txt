Reproducibility and precision of renal perfusion quantification with contrast enhanced ultrasound in critically ill patients: a prospective observational study
Authors: Hongye Ma, Pierre Frossard, Giuseppe Gullo, Bastien Trächsel, Jean-Yves Meuwly, Antoine G Schneider
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveContrast-enhanced ultrasound (CEUS) is a promising tool to assess renal microcirculation. However, its reproducibility has never been formally assessed. We aimed to assess the reproducibility and precision of CEUS measurements in critically ill patients.MethodsWe conducted an observational study in 20 critically ill patients in stable cardio-pulmonary condition. In group 1 (n = 10), to assess CEUS reproducibility, we performed three CEUS scans within a one-hour time frame in rigorously similar conditions and settings. In group 2 (n = 10), to assess the influence of ultrasound contrast agent rate, we performed two CEUS scans, one with standard settings and the second with a doubled ultrasound contrast agent rate. CEUS-derived parameters included relative blood volume (RBV), mean transit time (mTT) and perfusion index (PI). We used linear quantile mixed models to assess their reproducibility (stability across different time points), precision (variability of consecutive measurements), and the influence of a change in ultrasound contrast agent (UCA) infusion rate. Finally, we sought to determine patients’ related factors associated with signal instability.ResultsIn Group 1, CEUS derived parameters remained similar across the three study timepoints (3 289 vs. 3 680 vs. 3 590 for RBV, 4.1 vs. 4.2 vs. 4.2 for mTT and 684 vs. 762 vs. 735 for PI, all p values > 0.05), indicating stability of these parameters within individual patients over time. Intra-measure coefficient of variability was 23.2% for RBV, 13.9% for mTT and 33.8% for PI. The median of five CEUS assessments yielded reliable estimates. In Group 2, doubling the UCA rate resulted in a significant increase in RBV (3 207 vs. 1 624, p = 0.01), and in PI (977 vs. 617, p = 0.01), while mTT remained stable (4.6 vs. 3.8, p = 0.08). No patients-related parameter could be significantly associated with CEUS parameters variability.ConclusionsUnder controlled conditions, CEUS derived parameters are reproducible and have a fair precision in critically ill patients in stable hemodynamic condition. mTT might be more reliable under varying infusion conditions. Further studies are required to establish the clinical utility of CEUS in critically ill patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13054-025-05692-1.Keywords: Renal perfusion, Ultrasonography, Contrast enhanced imaging, Destruction replenishment sequences, Reproducibility, Precision

Background

Acute kidney injury (AKI) is a common and severe complication in patients admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU) [1]. To date the contribution of renal perfusion alterations to its pathophysiology remains poorly understood [2]. This is largely related to the absence of an adequate tool to evaluate renal perfusion in critical illness. Imaging techniques (scintigraphy, blood oxygen level-dependent magnetic resonance imaging (BOLD MRI)) are expensive and require extensive and prolonged patients’ mobilization which limits its usability in ICU [3]. Experiments have been conducted with renal vein catheterization, however, this procedure is too invasive to be used outside the context of a study [4]. A reproducible, quick and easy method allowing assessment of renal microcirculation could improve our understanding of renal perfusion alterations during AKI and facilitate its management.

Contrast-enhanced ultrasound (CEUS) is an ultrasound based imaging modality, which makes use of dedicated contrast agents [5, 6]. CEUS enables blood flow visualisation and techniques have been proposed to quantify organ perfusion [7–9] including the kidney [5, 10]. Such techniques have been shown to be safe and feasible even in critically ill patients [11–14]. CEUS is applicable at the bedside and could enable to study renal perfusion in critical illness [15]. Such technology could guide fluid or vasopressors management in critically ill patients with or without AKI. However, before this technique enters clinical practice several practical elements need to be clarified.

Issues have been raised about the repeatability of CEUS measurements. Indeed, previous experiments [16] were associated with high variability in results. Measurement variability can be explained by minute changes in ultrasound probe angle (patient or investigator movement), the evaluation of a different area of the kidney or simply inadequate image quality. This can be minimized by thorough quality criteria for image selection, precise anatomical landmarks and probe position and the use of median values as final output. Another difficulty is associated with the fact that CEUS cannot deliver absolute measure of flow. Indeed, in the absence of a reference value, CEUS is only able to perform semi-quantification. Hence, patients cannot be compared to each other but only changes between two measurements can be informative.

To date, no standardized dosing protocols for UCA have been made available. While some studies have suggested a variety of infusion rates [17], others have proposed alternative parameters [18, 19]. Given the relatively high cost of contrast agents, optimizing infusion protocols to balance dosage reduction and measurement reliability is relevant. However, the impact of infusion rate variations on CEUS-derived parameters remains unclear.

Accordingly, we designed a prospective observational study to evaluate the reproducibility (stability across different time points for a given subject) of CEUS-derived parameters, their precision (variability of consecutive measurements for a given subject), the influence of the UCA infusion rate and the influence of external factors on signal stability.

Methods

Study design

This prospective observational study took place in the adult intensive care unit (ICU) of Lausanne University Hospital between October 2017 and January 2019. The target population included patients admitted to the ICU but in a stable hemodynamic situation. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee Vaud (2016–02182) [20–23].

Participants recruitment and selection

Twenty patients admitted to the ICU, with standard monitoring (including invasive blood pressure monitoring) and in a stable cardio-pulmonary condition for > 12 h were approached and consented. Stable condition was assessed by the treating physician based on vasopressor and fluid requirement as well as patient’s clinical trajectory. Patients who were unconscious or unable to provide informed consent, could be enrolled provided a physician not involved in the clinical investigation and who safeguards the participant interests would provide a written authorization to enroll the participant. In this case, post-hoc consent needed to be obtained for data to be utilized. Exclusion criteria were: planned diagnostic or therapeutic intervention during the study period (1 h), inadequate kidney visualization on conventional ultrasound, Sonovue® or any UCA intolerance, end-stage renal disease (plasma creatinine concentration > 300 µmol/l or need for maintenance hemodialysis), inability to obtain informed consent and enrolment in a conflicting research study. Adequate kidney visualization is defined as the ability to view a large and significant portion of the kidney on US imaging.

Study procedure

CEUS scans

For the purpose of this study, we used Sonovue® (Bracco, Milano, Italy) as an UCA. A total volume of 4.8 ml was infused at each scan session into a peripheral or central vein (according to availability) using a dedicated syringe pump VueJect™ (Bracco Research, Geneva, Switzerland). Low mechanical index (MI: 0.06) ultrasound of the kidney was performed with a Philips IU22® ultrasound machine and a convex 5 − 1 MHz probe. A long axis view of the left kidney was obtained by placing the transducer probe over the lower back of the subject. Once adequate images of the kidney were obtained, the probe was stabilized using a dedicated mechanical arm. UCA infusion was then started at 1 ml/min using the syringe pump. This rate was used in previous works from our team and enabled adequate insonification in most situations. It appears to represent a cost-effective compromise.

Image depth, focus, gain and frame rate were optimized at the beginning of each experiment and held constant during the study. After a two minutes period required to obtain a steady state, a minimum of five consecutive destruction/refilling sequences (with >15 s refilling time) were obtained [15]. Destruction was obtained by applying a flash of increased ultrasound intensity (5 pulses with high mechanical index (> 1.0)). Full destruction of contrast agent in the scan plane was ascertained between consecutive destruction-reperfusion sequences.

Procedure sequence

As shown in Figure S1 (Supplementary Material) in the first ten patients (Group 1), three CEUS scans (T0, T1 and T2) were performed within a one-hour time frame (wash-out periods of 30 min between scans). In the following ten patients (Group 2), two CEUS scans were performed (T0, T1), the first in standard conditions and the second (after a 30-minute wash-out period) with a doubled (2 mL/min) rate of UCA infusion.

No other intervention or changes in patient’s management were allowed during the scans or the wash-out periods.

Safety parameters

All studies were performed within the ICU. Patients received full monitoring according to their clinical situation at the time of the examination as evaluated by the treating physician. At a minimum, invasive blood pressure, blood oxygen saturation (via pulse oximetry), and continuous three leads electrocardiograms were available for all patients throughout the experiment. In addition, urinary output was monitored on an hourly basis and blood samples regularly drawn for routine blood tests as per clinical practice.

Sequences analyses

Ultrasound data sets were exported in a digital imaging and communication in medicine (DICOM) format and analysed offline using a dedicated software package, VueBox 6.0® (Bracco Research, Milano, Italy). An example of offline analysis is presented in Fig. 1. For each sequence, one region of interest (ROI) was drawn. To minimize the influence of local perfusion heterogeneities, this ROI was drawn so that it enclosed the largest area of visible renal cortex on the surface of the kidney closest to the ultrasound probe without artifacts. After software motion compensation, cortical regions that were only intermittently visible because of breathing or other artefacts were not included in the ROI. The software generates a perfusion index (PI), which is thought to be proportional to perfusion within a region of interest. Such PI is calculated by dividing the relative blood volume (RBV) by the mean transit time (mTT) and is expressed in arbitrary units (a.u.). These parameters have been described in detail elsewhere [6, 15]. In brief, the RBV is a measure of pixel luminance, proportional to contrast agent concentration within the ROI (increases with higher level of perfusion) and is expressed in arbitrary units (a.u.). The mTT is a measure of the time to replenishment after flash destruction of the contrast agent (a shorter time indicates higher level of perfusion). mTT is expressed in seconds.

Sequence analysis with Vuebox General Imaging Perfusion. A region of interest was drawn (yellow line) in the largest possible area of renal cortex closer to the ultrasound probe. The software generates a time intensity curve. This curve is used to generate CEUS-derived parameters

Suboptimal sequences with inadequate insonification or excessive movement artefact were excluded after review by two investigators (JYM, PF) blinded to quantification results.

Data collection

In addition to CEUS derived parameters, we collected patients’ characteristics at ICU admission and at the time of examination. In addition, to document hemodynamic stability across the evaluation periods, we collected, at each study time point, heart rate, blood pressure, need for vasopressors, SaO2, FiO2 and serum lactate level. Finally, to evaluate for factors potentially associated with data heterogeneity, we collected body mass index, number of days since admission, cumulative fluid balance, in ICU weight gain (weight on study day - weight on admission day), presence of large abdominal dressing and positioning limitations.

Outcomes assessment

We first assessed the reproducibility (stability across different time points for a given subject) of CEUS derived parameters for RBV, mTT and PI, by comparing values obtained at the three different time points in Group (1) Then, we used all time points in Group 1 to assess the precision (variability of consecutive measurements for a given subject) of CEUS-derived parameters, and calculate their coefficient of variation (CV). We then assessed the influence of the UCA rate by comparing values obtained at T0 and T1 in Group (2) Finally, we assessed whether some patients’ related factors could be associated with signal instability.

Statistical analyses

A convenience sample size of twenty patients was considered adequate. Continuous variables are reported using mean and standard deviation (SD) or median and interquartile range (IQR) or proportion [95% confidence interval (CI)] according to data distribution. Categorical variables are described using absolute frequencies and relative percentages. For baseline characteristics comparisons, differences between groups were analysed using t-test or Mann Whitney U test for continuous variables and Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables.

To assess reproducibility i.e. stability across time points for a given subject, we evaluated the effect of time on median measurements. For this purpose, we built a quantile linear mixed model, accounting for the correlation of measurements for each subject. The first analysis explored the relationship between time and RBV, mTT and PI values. A second analysis focused on the effect of doubling the dose leveraging data obtained in patients from Group 2.

To assess precision (variability of consecutive measurements for a given patient), we derived from these models, for RBV, mTT and PI, a coefficient of variation for the median and confidence intervals. This analysis leverages the fact that multiple measurements were taken at a given time point for each patient. These analyses were repeated as a sensitivity analysis after excluding outliers using the IQR method, defined as values outside the range of Q1–1.5 × IQR and Q3 + 1.5 × IQR.

To evaluate the number of measurements required to obtain a sufficient precision, we derived confidence intervals for the median as a function of the number of measurements from the first quantile linear mixed model.

To assess factors associated with signal instability, we assigned to each patient for RBV, mTT and PI, a “stable” or “unstable” label. This was achieved by calculating, for each patient, the coefficient of variation (CV) and compare it to the data distribution. For each parameter, patients with a CV greater than the third quartile (Q3) of all values were defined as unstable. We then conducted univariate analysis to assess for patients’ factors associated with signal instability. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

We used R version 4.4.2 [24] and the extension package “lqmm“ [25] for calculations. All analyses were conducted with BT.

Reproducibility: effect of time

In Group 1, none of the three parameters (RBV, mTT, and PI) presented significant differences across the three time points (Fig. 2a). Specifically, RBV did not differ significantly between T0 and T1 (3289 vs. 3680, p = 0.17) or between T0 and T2 (3289 vs. 3590, p = 0.34). Similarly, mTT showed no significant differences between T0 and T1 (4.1 vs. 4.2, p = 0.38) or T0 and T2 (4.1 vs. 4.2, p = 0.63). Finally, PI values were not significantly different across the three timepoints: T0 and T1 (684.0 vs. 762.3, p = 0.17) or between T0 and T2 (684.0 vs. 734.9, p = 0.59).

Precision: coefficient of variability

CV was 23.2% (IC95 [19.2, 27.3]) for RBV, 13.9% (IC95 [7.0, 20.8]) for mTT and 33.8% (IC95 [25.2, 42.4]) for PI. Similar results were obtained after sensitivity analysis, 25.9% (IC95 [23.4, 28.5]) for RBV, 11.8% (IC95 [10.6, 13.0]) for mTT and 33.4% (IC95 [30.6, 36.2]) for PI (Fig. 3). Pooled CV was 26.4% (IC95 [22.6, 30.1]) for RBV, 14.9% (IC95 [11.4, 18.3]) for mTT and 34.5% (IC95 [24.5, 44.5]) for PI, which are consistent.

Dose effect: effect of doubling the UCA dose

Doubling the contrast agent infusion rate, as performed in group 2 between T1 and T0, was associated with a significant increase in RBV from 1623 (T0) to 3207 (T1), p = 0.01 and PI from 616.7 to 976.6, p = 0.01. On the other hand, mTT remained unchanged (3.8 vs. 4.6, p = 0.08) (Fig. 2b).

Factors associated with unstable measures

We used visual assessment of Figure S2 and Figure S3 to classify patients into stable or unstable for RBV, mTT and PI. Through this approach, we determined that RBV measurements were unstable in 4 patients and mTT measurements in 6.

No single parameter was associated with unstable measurements for RBV or PI (Supplementary Tables 5,7). Similarly, there was no significant differences between patients with stable and unstable mTT values, except for a lower proportion of males in the group of patients with unstable values (33.3 vs. 92.9%, p = 0.01) (Supplementary Table 6).

Discussion

Key findings

We conducted 50 CEUS scans in 20 patients admitted to the ICU in stable cardio-pulmonary condition. We analyzed more than 350 destruction reperfusion sequences. We found that all three CEUS derived parameters remained stable within patients over time. We were able to assess these measurements precision and found that their coefficient of variation was 15% (mTT) and 25% (RBV). An increase in UCA rate was associated with a large increase in RBV and PI parameters, but no change in mTT. Finally, we failed to identify a particular characteristic associated with unstable measurements.

Comparison with previous studies

Assessing renal microcirculation has historically been challenging due to the lack of standardized, methodologies. Studies assessing the repeatability of CEUS in evaluating renal microcirculation remain limited, with existing research employing varied methodologies to assess measurement consistency. For example, Almushayt et al. [26] assessed intra-individual repeatability in healthy adults, where 10 participants underwent two renal CEUS scans within 14 days, reporting ICC values of 0.71 for mTT and 0.65 for PI, suggesting good reproducibility over time. This finding aligns with our conclusions. Zhang et al. [27] evaluated renal perfusion repeatability by placing three ROIs in both the cortex and medulla, and also suggested good intra-individual reproducibility of CEUS-derived parameters. Other studies have reported significant fluctuations in CEUS parameters. For instance, the study by Hillaert et al. [28] highlighted the low inter-observer agreement for most CEUS parameters—including PI. These findings suggest that physiological factors, technical variability, and contrast agent dynamics contribute to measurement inconsistencies, highlighting the challenges of using CEUS for reliable longitudinal monitoring of renal microcirculation. In the present study, we have selected patients in stable cardio-pulmonary conditions, repeated the measurements within a 30 min time frame and optimized all parameters to minimize variability.

Further studies are required to assess the influence of macrocirculatory changes on the precision of CEUS parameters. A porcine model has shown that CEUS could reliably track renal microcirculatory changes across shock and fluid resuscitation, indicating that hemodynamic instability does not preclude robust signal acquisition [29]. Larger, human, studies should include hemodynamically unstable patients (e.g., sepsis, cardiogenic shock). Those studies should include a thorough assessment of cardiac output and renal perfusion to adequately report on macrocirculation status.

To date, there is no standardized protocol or consensus regarding the optimal dose of UCAs for CEUS examinations. Existing studies have employed heterogeneous approaches, including both bolus injection [29] and continuous infusion, with infusion rates varying widely, e.g., fixed rates such as 1 mL/min [30, 31] or weight-based dosing [32]. This variability introduces potential inconsistencies in quantitative CEUS parameters, limiting comparability across studies. Our study is among the first to systematically evaluate the impact of UCA dose on CEUS-derived measurements. Notably, we observed that while RBV and PI demonstrated sensitivity to dose variation, mTT remained independent of UCA infusion rate. This finding suggests that mTT may be a more robust and reliable indicator of renal perfusion in clinical and research settings. These results highlight the importance of dose-related considerations in CEUS protocols and support the use of mTT as a dose-independent perfusion metric.

While bolus injection has been utilized for renal perfusion visualization and even semi-quantitative assessment, as demonstrated by Ma et al. [33], such an approach remains suboptimal for perfusion quantification. In contrast, this study has used destruction replenishment techniques to perform perfusion quantification. This technique requires a continuous infusion of UCA, which provides a more robust and reproducible framework for quantitative evaluation of renal microcirculation.

To our knowledge, no previous studies have addressed the determinants of intra-individual instability in CEUS parameters. While no independent predictors were identified in multivariate analysis, perhaps due to the limited number of patients, we observed significant differences in fluid balance and body weight between patients with stable versus unstable CEUS measurements.

Implications for clinicians and further studies

Results heterogeneity and the lack of a standardization of the measurements have limited the application of CEUS in a clinical setting for the time being [17, 34]. We demonstrate that, under controlled conditions, CEUS derived parameters were reproducible and present acceptable precision. The observed CV of 25% for RBV and 15% for mTT will enable power calculation for further studies and set a gold standard for significant changes. Indeed, any observational study testing renal microcirculation response to an intervention should consider these margins of errors to draw a meaningful conclusion. More specifically, to reliably demonstrate a change in renal microcirculation, a >25% change in RBV (15% for mTT) must be observed. Any lower increment or decrement falls within the area of uncertainty.

Our observation that a doubling of contrast agent rate was associated with an increased RBV is unsurprising since this parameter is a function of the intensity of the perceived signal. However, the lack of change in mTT under these conditions is new and of interest. This suggests that mTT may represent a more robust parameter, less dependent on measurement technique than RBV.

Based on the confidence intervals for the median as a function of the measurements, we recommend five measurements as the incremental precision gain past five measurements was minimal.

Strengths and limitations

Our study presents several strengths. To our knowledge, this is the first study to systematically evaluate the reproducibility and precision of three CEUS parameters in ICU patients, providing a foundation for the broader clinical use of CEUS in renal perfusion assessment. Second, our experiment, although in very controlled conditions, were performed in critically ill patients demonstrating the applicability of this technique in this patient group. Third, we applied robust statistical methods to derive from our data the reproducibility and precision of CEUS derived parameters.

This study also has several limitations. First, the small sample size may limit the generalizability of our findings and, in particular, limits our ability to demonstrate factors associated with signal instability. Further studies should assess the role of potential confounders such as mechanical ventilation settings, renal microcirculation, fluid balance and body weight. Second, our examinations were performed using a mechanical arm to minimize motion artifacts, ensure that a similar zone was scanned throughout experiments and improve internal validity. Such device might not be available under normal conditions. Third, the applicability of our findings in patients in unstable condition remains to be demonstrated. However, our data confirms that, with a strict protocol, adequate reproducibility can be achieved, hence any further variation is likely to be associated with changes in patient’s condition.

Conclusion

Under controlled conditions, CEUS-derived parameters are reproducible and have a fair precision in critically ill patients in stable cardio-pulmonary condition. mTT might be more reliable under varying infusion conditions. Further studies are required to establish the clinical utility of CEUS in critically ill patients.
