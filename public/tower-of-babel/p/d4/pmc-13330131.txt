New insights into the sorption mechanism of charcoal-based activated carbon as an air humidity control material: surface properties and water vapor adsorption behaviour
Authors: Maurice Thésée Mekouebe, Frank Dorinel Solefack Feudjio, Christian Emmanuel Fils Manga, Cyrille Ghislain Fotsop, Donald Raoul Tchuifon Tchuifon, Thomas Djiako, Frédéric Lonsti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This research focuses on the adsorption of water vapor by activated carbons derived from charcoal (CAC0) purchased from local stores in Cameroon, following activation with KOH (potassium hydroxide) and H3PO4 (phosphoric acid). The activation was carried out at temperatures of 400 and 600 °C at various concentrations of an activating agent (0.5, 1 and 1.5 M). Several physicochemical analysis techniques (XRD, SEM, EDX-mapping, N2 adsorption/desorption, FTIR spectroscopy and Raman spectroscopy) were used on the materials to evaluate the characteristics of the carbons following their activation. All materials presented a high concentration of inorganic compounds, and significant changes in surface properties were observed. Furthermore, activation significantly improved the textural characteristics, increasing the specific surface area from 217 m2 g−1 for CAC0 to 353, 331, 409 and 362 m2 g−1 for CACH400, CACK400, CACH600 and CACK600, respectively. Additionally, an increase in microporosity was observed, with estimated micropore surface area values of 0.076, 0.119, 0.106, 0.149 and 0.110 cm3 g−1 for CAC0, CACH400, CACK400, CACH600 and CACK600, respectively. The increased porosity of the carbons endowed them with superior desiccant properties and significantly higher adsorption capacities compared with CAC0. The adsorption process was strongly influenced by the concentration of the activating agent used and the carbonisation temperature. For CACH600, the highest adsorption capacity observed was 19.01 mmol g−1. All adsorption curves were of type V, showing minimal adsorption at P/P0 < 0.3, followed by intense adsorption at P/P0 > 0.3. The analysis of the adsorption isotherms showed that the Freundlich, Sips and Toth models best described the steady-state adsorption process.

Introduction

Technologies dedicated to eliminating water molecules are gaining more and more momentum. Indeed, the presence of these molecules in airflows can pose a risk to equipment (e.g. corrosion) and various items, such as food and pharmaceutical products, which can have consequences for human health.1 To this end, dehumidification using a solid desiccant is an effective and economical solution, which makes it possible to improve conventional drying systems, such as solar drying. The improvement of solar drying offers shorter drying cycles and better preservation of dried products through dehumidification by the adsorption of water vapour from the hot air, enabling.2 Adsorption drying is also a strategy for recovering residual energy from industrial processes, which can be used in thermal transformation systems.3 To this end, thermal transformation by adsorption is based on the management of heat flows from adsorption and desorption cycles of water vapor on adsorbent materials, such as metal–organic frameworks (MOFs),3 zeolites,4,5 silica gel,6 activated alumina, hopcalite and activated carbons.7 Among these materials, activated carbon stands out for its ease of design and its particular properties, which include its large specific surface area, its pore volume and its high stability in humid environments. These properties justify its use in various industrial and non-industrial processes, such as water treatment, energy storage, catalysis and detoxification.8–10 The preparation of activated carbons from lignocellulosic biomass makes them available, renewable and accessible materials at low cost. For this purpose, activated carbons have been developed from biomasses, such as rubber seed hulls,11 cola nut shells (Garcinia),12 coconut shells,13 peanut shells14 and coffee shells.2

The hydrophilic properties of these materials are mainly dependent on the presence of active sites on their surfaces, such as oxygenated groups, which act as nucleation sites capable of binding to water molecules even at low relative pressure values, depending on the concentration of the functional groups.10,15,16 In other words, activated carbons' ability to adsorb water molecules strongly depends on their chemical composition. To illustrate this, Chairunnisa et al. (2022)17 observed a significant increase in the water vapor adsorption capacity of an activated carbon by increasing the oxygenated groups on its surface through ozone treatment. Similarly, Velasco et al. (2021)16 reported that fluorine doping could improve the hydrophilicity of the surface of an activated carbon at low relative humidity values. Other factors related to textural properties, such as specific surface area or microporosity, have also been shown to be relevant in the adsorption of water vapor on these materials. This observation was highlighted by Ridassepri et al. (2020)18 when studying the adsorption of water vapor on activated carbons from sugarcane bagasse. The results obtained revealed a higher adsorption capacity of 0.223 g g−1 for carbon activated at 600 °C, which featured a more developed BET surface area and microporosity. Thus, this study notes the close link that exists between textural properties and activation temperature, also establishing an indirect link between this parameter and the hydrophilic properties of the carbons. Sun et al. (2019)2 also studied the influence of activation temperature on the capacity of activated carbons to adsorb water vapor. They observed that for values lower than 799.85 °C, an increase in the activation temperature had a positive effect on the adsorption of water vapor by activated carbons based on coffee hulls. In addition to temperature, this study also highlighted the preponderant role of the proportion of activating agent in the textural properties of activated carbon and its impact on the hydrophilic properties of these materials. This brief review of previous studies highlights the need to seek the optimal conditions for the preparation of activated carbons both for a rational use of the activating agent and energy and to obtain a well-developed and stable porous structure.

In view of the above, we propose in this study to develop activated carbons intended for the elimination of water vapors from air flows. For this, activated carbons are developed by the chemical activation of commercial biochar derived from Cameroonian wood, commonly used as a heat source for cooking. The choice of this raw material lies in its abundance, low cost and large-scale availability. Chemical activation is carried out with potassium hydroxide (KOH) and phosphoric acid (H3PO4). The choices of these activating agents are based both on their ability to generate oxygenated functions on the surface of the carbon and to develop a porous structure under flexible preparation conditions in terms of both time and energy, while allowing the easy control of the process (Table 1). The search for the best adsorbent will involve the study of temperature and the effect of the concentration of the activating agent on the physicochemical and hydrophilic properties of coal. In order to elucidate the adsorption mechanism, several isotherm models (Sips, Langmuir, Freundlich, Toth and DSL) are fitted to the equilibrium adsorption data. This study contributes to the valorization of lignocellulosic biomass from Cameroon.

Materials and methods

Collection and pre-treatment of raw materials

The wood charcoal was collected from the PK14 market in Douala, in the Douala 5 district, Wouri department, Littoral region, Cameroon. After cleaning the charcoal waste properly with distilled water to remove any earthy contaminants, it was left to dry in the sun. After completing this process, the dried charcoal, coded CAC0, was obtained. Once crushed and sieved to a size of 100 µm, the material was stored for future use.

Synthesis of activated carbons

Two activating agents (KOH and H3PO4) were used in the preparation of various activated carbons. 50 mL of solutions containing 0.5, 1.0 or 1.5 mol L−1 of either KOH or H3PO4 were used to impregnate 20 g of the CAC0 material. After impregnation, the mixtures were shaken for 24 hours, then filtered and dried in an oven at 105 °C for 12 hours. The impregnated materials were then calcined in a Select Horn oven for two hours at two different temperatures (400 °C and 600 °C) with a heating rate of 5 °C min−1. The selection of activation temperatures was based on a combination of guidance from the literature and energy-efficiency considerations. Although previous studies commonly report carbon activation within the broad range of 400–800 °C (Table 1), we deliberately selected the low end of this range to minimize energy consumption and reduce the environmental footprint of the process, in line with sustainable and cost-effective material synthesis. Following cooling, the calcined material was thoroughly washed with distilled water to eliminate any remaining H3PO4 or KOH molecules. The resulting activated carbon was ground into a fine powder using a porcelain mortar and stored after being oven-dried for 24 hours at 105 °C to eliminate water molecules. As control samples, activated materials with 0.5 M activating agent concentrations were employed to assess how the carbonization temperature affected the coals' physicochemical characteristics. The coals impregnated with orthophosphoric acid (H3PO4) and calcined at 400 and 600 °C were designated CACH400 and CACH600, respectively. The coals impregnated with potassium hydroxide (KOH) and calcined at 400 and 600 °C were designated CACK400 and CACK600, respectively.

Characterization method

Several analytical techniques were employed to evaluate the physicochemical properties of the activated carbons, including scanning electron microscopy coupled with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (SEM-EDX), powder X-ray diffraction (XRD), Fourier-transform infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy, and N2 adsorption–desorption analyses. SEM-EDX observations were performed using a TESCAN VEGA 3-LMU electron microscope operating at 8 kV. Prior to analysis, the samples were mounted on aluminum holders and then coated with a thin layer of gold via sputtering to make them conductive. The XRD diffractograms were obtained using a PAN analytical XPERT Pro diffractometer with Cu-Kα radiation (λ = 1.54056 Å), at a voltage of 40 kV and a current of 40 mA. The analyses were performed in a 2θ angular range between 4° and 90°, with a step size of 0.026° and a counting time of 27.54 s. Surface functional groups were identified by FT-IR spectroscopy in attenuated total reflection (ATR) mode, using a Nicolet Thermo Scientific IS5 spectrophotometer, in the spectral range of 4000–400 cm−1. The textural properties (specific surface area, volume, and pore distribution) were determined from N2 adsorption–desorption isotherms obtained using a BELSORP MAX analyzer (Bel Japan Inc.) and analyzed according to the BET (Brunauer–Emmett–Teller) and BJH (Barrett–Joyner–Halenda) models. Finally, Raman spectroscopy was performed using a RAM HR Evolution system equipped with a 532 nm monochromatic laser.

Water adsorption measurement

A Dynamic vapor sorption (DVS) device (DVS Adventure, England) was used to conduct gravimetric water vapor adsorption and desorption studies on the samples. For each experiment, the material was cleaned with ethanol and then dried in an oven set at 250 °C for 10 hours. The climatic chamber of the DVS apparatus was then filled with a metal pallet containing 30 grams of the material. An automatic balance with 0.0001 mg precision was used to quantify mass variations at regular intervals. Relative humidity cycles ranging from 0% to 98% were tested at 25 and 40 °C. The adsorption capacity of water molecules was assessed in relation to the activation temperature and the concentration of the activating agent.

Water vapor adsorption isotherm modeling

Understanding the mechanisms governing different adsorption systems can be accomplished by the modeling of equilibrium adsorption isotherms.29 As a result, the nonlinear versions of many isotherm models, such as Langmuir, Freundlich, Sips, Toth, and DSL, were used to describe the water vapor adsorption isotherms. Results and discussion

Characterization of materials

Fourier transform infrared analysis

Fig. 1 shows the FTIR spectra of CACH400, CACH600, CACK400, CACK600, and CAC0, demonstrating the significant surface modifications that occurred during activation. Several absorption bands on the activated carbons' spectra, particularly those at 3913.06, 1448.35, 688.49, and 539.99 cm−1, vanished as a result of these changes. The stretching vibrations of O–H bonds (carboxylic acids and phenols) were responsible for the first band, whereas C–H bond vibrations were responsible for the bands at 688.49 and 539.99 cm−18. This observation is equivalent to supposing that the numerous C–H bonds on CAC0 have been replaced by other bond types, most likely C–O and O–H. This hypothesis makes sense on the spectra of materials activated with phosphoric acid (CACH400 and CACH600), where the intensification of the band located at 1091.59 cm −1 and the appearance of a new band at 2659.49 cm −1 are observed.8,34 In addition, these absorption bands were more intense on the CACH600 sample, which suggested that a carbonization temperature of 600 °C allowed better oxidation of the coal surface. X-ray diffraction of the samples

The strong presence of minerals in the samples was confirmed by XRD analysis, which presented numerous diffraction lines (Fig. 2). For this purpose, the diffraction lines observed at 54.99°, 59.94° and 75.55° can be attributed to the (0 2 2), (2 1 1) and (0 3 2) planes of the hexagonal structure of quartz, respectively.38 The characteristic lines of the γ-Al2O3 phase were observed around 36.52°, 39.46°, 45.72° and 67.74°.39,40 In addition, the characteristic lines of calcite (CaCO3) were observed at 29.40° and 39.46°.41,42 The presence of K2O in the CACK400, CACK600 and CAC0 samples was confirmed by the observation of lines at diffraction angles of 42.49°, 43.15°, 48.46° and 75.55° on the XRD patterns of these samples.43,44 The absence of these lines on the diffractograms of the phosphoric acid-activated samples suggested that KOH impregnation contributed to maintaining this oxide on the surface of CACK400 and CACK600, thereby corroborating the results of the FTIR spectroscopic analysis. An attenuation of the characteristic lines of the other minerals was also observed on the diffractograms of CACH400 and CACH600, which could result from the leaching of these minerals during impregnation. The intense line observed at 26.42° reflected the presence of a graphitic structure in the CAC0 sample and activated carbons.45 This idea was supported by the observation of another line around 21°, which could highlight a structure made of sp2 hybridized carbon.46 These findings were confirmed by the results of Raman spectroscopy, the spectra of which, for the different samples, are shown in Fig. 3.

Raman spectroscopy

The Raman spectra of all materials exhibited two main bands located between 1300 and 1600 cm−1, characteristic of a graphitic structure punctuated by irregularities. The G mode, which corresponded to the in-plane vibrations of the strongly coupled carbons in the hexagonal sheets, was highlighted by the band at 1585 cm−1. The crystallites present in these materials were relatively small compared to the broad bands presented by the spectra. The band around 1362.73 cm−1 on the CAC0 spectrum, which appeared as a shoulder, indicated a strong presence of interstitial defects (D mode) responsible for disorder in the carbon structure.47 In the spectra of activated samples, this band was relatively more prominent, surpassing that of the G mode in the case of the CACK400 and CACK600 samples. This observation suggested that activation caused a greater amorphization of the material, particularly when it was done with KOH as the activating agent.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) characterization and mapping of the samples

The morphology of the coals given by SEM (Fig. 4) revealed that the samples were all made up of particles of different sizes and shapes. The abundance of small, almost identical particles, with sizes less than 2 µm, on the SEM image of the CAC0 material could result from the strong presence of minerals in this material. In this logic, the absence or rather the weak presence of these particles on the SEM images of CACH400 and CACH600 suggested a low mineral content, which coincided with the observations made on the XRD diagrams of these materials. This hypothesis was reinforced by the results of the EDX analysis of the materials, which are represented in Fig. 5. Indeed, the activation of biochar with phosphoric acid led to the disappearance or reduction of certain metallic species on its surface. This trend was accompanied by a more or less significant increase in the percentage of phosphorus and oxygen. The high mineral content of all samples was confirmed by the high percentage of metallic species (K, Si, Al and Ca), which represented between 15% and 31% of the elements present on the surface of these materials. Each of these metals occupied particles where it was the main constituent, which reinforced the idea that some of the observed particles were minerals. On the other hand, the percentage of oxygen, which was much lower than those of the metallic species, offered a great possibility that the latter were found in ionic form on the surface of the materials, which could further promote the adsorption of water molecules.4,48 These results also highlighted the carbonaceous nature of the samples, revealing a high carbon content, estimated to be between 51% and 62%. Furthermore, the activation resulted in the formation of a microporous structure, as demonstrated by the presence of micropores on the surfaces of the particles that comprise the CACH400, CACK400 and CACK600 samples (Fig. 4). The CACH600 sample appeared to have more developed microporosity, with particles that had a spongy appearance, a sign of a carbonaceous matrix made up of interconnected micropores. These results were confirmed by the textural properties of the different materials, the N2 adsorption/desorption isotherms and pore distribution of which are shown in Fig. 5.

Specific surface area and pore distribution

The N2 adsorption and desorption isotherms of the materials were all type I (Fig. 6a), suggesting that the materials were microporous.4,49 Microporosity is confirmed by the concentration of pores in the size range below 20 Å (Fig. 6b). The activation of CAC0 led to a considerable increase in the amount of N2 adsorbed, reflecting an increase in the specific surface area of the activated samples. The specific surface areas of the activated carbons, as determined by the BET method, were significantly higher than those of CAC0, as illustrated in Table 4. As highlighted by scanning electron microscopy, activation generated more micropores, which was manifested by a substantial increase in the surface area and volume of the micropores. The latter, as well as the total surface area, was significantly greater for samples activated with phosphoric acid, with a maximum obtained at 600 °C. This observation suggested that a high temperature further promoted thermal degradation, resulting in the development of pores, which was in agreement with recent work for the temperature range studied.8,50 However, KOH activation promoted, in addition to micropores, the development of relatively large pores. This observation could result from the presence of potassium atoms, which were intercalated between the pores of the precursor material, promoting their enlargement during thermal decomposition. This transition to a mesoporous structure was more evident at a high temperature, as illustrated by the appearance of hysteresis on the N2 adsorption/desorption isotherm for the CACK600 sample.

Adsorption of water vapor

Water vapor adsorption isotherm characterization

The equilibrium adsorption isotherms of water vapor at 25 °C on the different carbons shown in Fig. 7a showed that activation resulted in a substantial improvement in the adsorption capacity of water molecules. This adsorption capacity was lower for materials activated at 400 °C, probably due to their relatively low specific surface areas. As in most existing reports, the isotherm curves were of type V, according to the IUPAC classification.7,17,18,51–53 This isotherm curve suggested the existence of relatively weak interactions between water molecules and the surface of the coals, due to the combination of the hydrophobic properties of carbon and the hydrophilic properties of the functional groups present on the surface of these materials. In this context, the adsorption of water vapor on the materials began with the fixation of the molecules on the active sites at low relative pressure (P/P0 < 0.2 for CACH400 and CACH600 and P/P0 < 0.3 for CACK400 and CACK600).7,17 The lower values of P/P0 at the end of the occupation of the active sites for the samples activated with phosphoric acid suggested a great affinity between these materials and water molecules. Thus, the improvement in the adsorption capacity observed for CACH600 in this range probably resulted from an increase in the number of active sites on this material. This also suggested that the activation did not lead to a significant variation in the number of active sites on the surface of the CACH400, CACK400 and CACK600 samples. In view of the distribution of the elements on the surface of these samples, it was possible to highlight the synergy between the metal species and the phosphorus and oxygen groups in the adsorption process. Indeed, in addition to the oxygen atoms whose importance in the adsorption process was explored upstream of this study, numerous studies have focused on the participation of phosphorus and metal species in the water vapor adsorption process. This is the case of Rosas et al. (2008)52 who highlighted the ability of phosphorus groups to improve the adsorption of water vapor on activated carbon monoliths. Later, Okada et al. (2010)54 reported that the impregnation of a tissue paper-based activated carbon with hygroscopic CaCl2 salt can lead to a significant improvement in the adsorption capacity. A similar study was carried out by Wang et al. (2016),55 who observed an increase in the amount of adsorbed water molecules by modifying activated carbon fibers with CaCl2. Recently, Yang et al. (2022)56 improved the adsorption of water vapor by modifying carbon fibers with a silica sol and LiCl. The distinctive feature of CACH600 lay in the high abundance of oxygen- and phosphorus-containing species on its surface, together with mineral cations that were likely to enhance its hydrophilic character. The observed adsorption performance could, therefore, be attributed to a synergistic mechanism involving hydrogen bonding between water molecules and surface functional groups, as well as ion–dipole interactions between water molecules and the cations present on the carbon surface.

Water vapor isotherm curve of CAC0, CACH400, CACK400, CACH600 and CACK600 (a). Curves of the Langmuir, Freundlich, Sips, Toth and DSL models at 25° for CAC0 (b), CACH400 (c), CACK400 (d), CACH600 (e) and CACK600 (f).

When the active sites were fully occupied (P/P0 > 0.2 for CACH400 and CACH600 and P/P0 > 0.3 for CACK400 and CACK600), the adsorbed molecules behaved as new adsorption sites by interacting with other molecules through the formation of hydrogen bonds. This resulted in the formation of aggregates of water molecules on these sites, followed by the filling of the micropores.17,52,54 Thus, in this region, adsorption strongly depended on the textural properties of the carbons and more precisely on the micropore volume. To this end, the improvement in adsorption capacity observed in this region for the activated carbons could be linked to their large micropore volume, which allowed a great accumulation of water molecules. Logically, the high number of molecules adsorbed on P/P0 < 0.2 CACH600 further promoted pore filling in this material, giving it a maximum adsorption capacity of 19.014 mmol g−1.

The Langmuir, Freundlich, Sips, Toth, and DSL models were used to model the equilibrium adsorption isotherms, and the fitting curves are shown in Fig. 7b–f. This fitting process required the determination of certain parameters, whose values are shown in Table 5. Based on the values of χ2, the Freundlich model was the one that best described the adsorption process on CAC0, CACK400, and CACK600. This model highlighted the diversity of the hydrophilic sites on the surface of these materials and was consistent with the cooperative adsorption described previously. The constant (n) of this model, greater than 1, suggested that the adsorption process took place easily on the surface of these materials. This was also true for all other samples, particularly CACH400, which also had a relatively low χ2 value for the Toth model. Like the Freundlich model, the Toth model is ideal for describing adsorption on sites of varying affinity to the adsorbate. The values close to unity of R2 for this model for the CAC0, CACK400 and CACK600 samples were, therefore, not surprising. In addition, the value of the homogeneity constant (n) of this model, different from 1, for CAC0, CACH400, CACK400 and CACK600, confirmed the presence of sites of different energies on these materials. Moreover, the adsorption isotherm curves of water molecules on these samples presented inflection points, which highlighted the difference between the adsorption energies of the different sites, thereby agreeing with the R2 high values of the DSL model.57 These inflection points were more pronounced for the CACH600 sample, suggesting that the difference in adsorption energies was greater in this material.

Although the Langmuir isotherm had the lowest χ2 value of 3.307 for CACH600, the root mean square error (4.596) of this model was relatively high, and its R2 (0.962) relatively low. In this context, it would be absurd to conclude that a monolayer was formed on the hydrophilic sites of the same energy. On the other hand, the higher value of the constant (KL) (0.007) for this material may confirm a great affinity between the latter and water molecules, which would be consistent with the high adsorption capacity observed for this material. On the other hand, the Sips model, which reconciles low values of χ2 (3.429) and RMSE (1.821) with a high R2 value (0.995), would be more suitable for describing the adsorption on this sample. This was evidenced by the shape of the fitting curve of this model, as shown in Fig. 7e, and the calculated maximum adsorption capacity (20.109 mmol g−1), which was close to the experimental value (19.014 mmol g−1). These results would, therefore, confirm the existence of several sites of different energies on the surface of this material. The Sips model also appears to be a good mathematical tool to describe the adsorption of water vapor on other materials with regard to the values of R2, RMSE and χ2. Influence of the concentration of the activating agent at different temperatures

For each of the carbonization temperatures used during the activation of the carbons, the effect of the activating agent concentration on the capacity of the activated carbon to trap water molecules was studied. For this, activated carbons were prepared at different concentrations of activating agent under the previous temperature conditions. The materials obtained were named CACHT-x and CACKT-x for the samples activated with phosphoric acid and potassium hydroxide, respectively. Here, T denotes the carbonization temperature (T = 400, 600 °C) and x the activating agent concentration (x = 0.5, 1.0 and 1.5 M). The water vapor adsorption isotherms on these samples are shown in Fig. 9. These isotherms revealed that, with the exception of CACH400-x, the increase in the activating agent concentration led to a reduction in the capacity of the produced carbon to adsorb water molecules. This observation reflected both a reduction in the concentration of active sites on the surface of the samples and a potential alteration of the textural properties. These phenomena would be more marked on the CACH600-x sample in view of the more marked reduction in the adsorbed quantity. Similar observations were made by Sun et al. (2019),2 who reported a decrease in the adsorption capacity of coffee hull-based activated carbons with the increase in the KOH : char mass ratio when the latter was greater than 3 : 1. This team explained this observation by the collapse of the micropores formed in the presence of excess activating agent. On the other hand, an improvement in the desiccant properties of CACH400-x was observed when the activating agent concentration increased from 0.5 M to 1.0 M with a change from 8.93 to 10.84 mmol g−1. This resulted in a reduction in adsorption capacity when the concentration of the activating agent increased from 1.0 to 1.5 M, probably for the same reasons mentioned previously.

Influence of vapor adsorption temperature

Water vapor adsorption isotherms were performed while maintaining the temperature constant at 25 and 40 °C, in order to evaluate the effect of temperature on the performance of activated carbons. For this purpose, only the CACH400-x and CACK400-x materials were used. The effects of adsorption temperature on the amount of water vapor adsorbed by the CACH400-x and CACK400-x samples are shown in Fig. 10. These results showed that an increase in temperature led to a reduction in the adsorption performance of these materials. This trend has been observed in many works, including those of Nguena et al. (2023)5 and Feudjio et al. (2025),4 who each observed a reduction in the amount of water vapor adsorbed by zeolites with an increase in temperature between 25 and 45 °C. The explanation provided to clarify this phenomenon was a reduction in the affinity between water molecules and adsorbents due to the desorption of these molecules by evaporation at relatively high temperatures. In addition, the variation in temperature influenced the effect of the activating agent, modifying the order of effectiveness of the CACH400-x and CACK400-x materials. Thus, the best desiccant properties were observed at CACH400-1.0 M and CACH400-0.5 M at 25 °C, while the CACH400-0.5 M and CACH400-1.5 M materials offered the best performance at 40 °C. This observation was is probably based on the structural differences that existed between the materials impregnated with different concentrations of activating agents.

Conclusion

This study explored the desiccant properties of activated carbons derived from commercially available charcoal in Cameroon. The search for the best desiccant was carried out by varying the nature of the activating agent, its concentration and the activation temperature. In order to evaluate the evolution of the physicochemical properties of the carbons after activation, samples activated at 400 and 600 °C, with an activating agent concentration of 0.5 M, were taken as controls and subjected to several characterization methods. For this purpose, XRD analysis showed a strong mineralogical presence in the initial biochar, as well as in the activated carbons. The mineralogical composition of the initial material was strongly disturbed by the activation process, also leading to a modification of the surface functions and an alteration of the carbon structure. The activated carbons exhibited significantly higher specific surface areas and micropore volumes than charcoal, substantially improving their capacity to capture water molecules. According to the IUPAC classification, the adsorption isotherms of the water molecules on the different samples were type V, thus highlighting the hydrophobic behavior of the activated carbons. This was manifested by weak adsorption (P/P0 < 0.3), corresponding to the occupation of the active sites, followed by strong adsorption (P/P0 > 0.3), related to the cooperative filling of the pores. Modelling the adsorption isotherms of the samples impregnated with an activating agent at a concentration of 0.5 M revealed that the Freundlich, Sips and Toth models were suitable for establishing a relationship between the quantity adsorbed and the equilibrium pressure, given the low values of χ2, RMSE and R2. Due to the strong impact of the activating agent on the microstructure of the carbons, increasing the agent concentration usually reduces the adsorption capacity of the materials. The highest adsorption capacity was obtained with CACH600 at 19.01 mmol g−1. Performing DVS measurements at different temperatures showed that increasing the temperature from 25 to 40 °C led to a significant reduction in the adsorbed amount.
