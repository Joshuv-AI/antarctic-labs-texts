Older caregivers and care recipients in Europe: a cluster analysis according to health and social outcomes
Authors: Alba Ayala, Maider Mateo-Abad, Amaya Bernal-Alonso, Diego Sánchez-González, Fermina Rojo-Perez, Gloria Fernandez-Mayoralas, Maria João Forjaz, Carmen Rodríguez-Blázquez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe deficit of long-term care workers has intensified reliance on informal care, often provided by older adults, reflecting the demographic changes taking place in Europe. Both caregivers and care recipients in this context tend to share a deterioration in their health, well-being, functional limitations and increased social isolation. This study aims to identify and characterize older caregivers and care recipients in Europe according to their quality of life (QoL), health and loneliness.MethodsWave 8 (2019–2020) of the Survey of Health, Ageing and Retirement in Europe (SHARE) provided data of a sample of people aged 50 and over from 26 countries. Multiple correspondence analysis (MCA) and a cluster analysis (k-means) were applied to classify people according to QoL (CASP-12), life satisfaction, self-perceived health, loneliness (R-UCLA), depression (EURO-D), memory test and limitation in activities of daily living (ADLs). Patterns were characterized according to sociodemographic characteristics and caregiving status. Multinomial regression was used to assess the association between caregiving status and cluster membership.ResultsTotal sample was 41,999 individuals, with 57.2% women and a mean age of 71 years. The cluster analysis identified three profiles, with good (42.6%), moderate (50.6%) and poor health (6.8%). The poor health cluster was characterized by having a higher mean age (75.8 years), a higher percentage of women (66.6%), care recipients (42.7%) and live-in caregivers (6.3%), mainly, from Eastern and Southern European countries, such as Bulgaria and Cyprus.ConclusionMeasures of QoL, physical and mental health and loneliness classify more than half of the sample in the moderate and poor health groups in Europe. Health-related profiles of older adults vary significantly by caregiving status and highlight the heterogeneity of ageing across Europe. These results are useful to identify the most vulnerable population and help health and social care interventions focused on improving the living conditions of both older caregivers and care recipients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13690-026-01912-4.

Introduction

In Europe, demographic aging is reshaping long-term care needs. It is estimated that 2.3 million formal long-term care workers would be needed to meet these needs, highlighting the weakness of care and support systems [1]. Due to the paucity of formal long-term care provision, countries will continue to depend on informal caregiving, which traditionally relies on the family [2], as an exponent of informal care [3].

Informal caregivers are defined as people who regularly offer unpaid, non-professional work in the provision of care to those in need of care due to illness or disability [4]. In Europe, informal caregivers provide up to 80% of all long-term care, and most of them are family members, often aged 65 and over [1]. Despite their important role in care systems, older caregivers are a highly diverse group whose health, quality of life (QoL), and caregiving conditions vary across countries.

Providing care that promotes health and facilitates the performance of activities of daily living has a significant impact on QoL and solidarity between different generations. Both global and health-related QoL measures are commonly used to understand the impact of the care received by older people at home [5]. Among caregivers who share home with care recipients (live-in caregivers), the risk of experiencing adverse conditions [6], including loneliness, is greater than those among non-live-in caregivers [7]. Caregiving would contribute to emotional health and social engagement, but it also limit other relationships and lead to social isolation [8]. Health problems, such as chronic pain, illness and poorer self-rated health, also affect caregivers [7], reducing levels of well-being and QoL [9].

Studies indicate that caregivers tend to have better health than those cared for [10]. These outcomes may be partially explained by the ‘healthy caregiver’ hypothesis, which proposes that healthy individuals are more likely to assume caregiving tasks than those with health issues. Furthermore, caregivers’ physical and mental health may be preserved by utilising the physical, mental, and cognitive skills required for care tasks [11].

The impact of caregiving on the caregiver varies according to socio-demographic characteristics [12] and circumstances, such as whether caregivers have to combine caregiving with other work [13]. Time and duration of informal caregiving differs by gender [14], as caregivers are typically women from the family setting, often providing care at a high intensity which, in turn, negatively impacts their QoL. Similarly, older people receiving informal care reported higher feelings of loneliness and poorer QoL than those receiving formal care, while people in formal care showed higher social connectedness and reduced feelings of being a burden on the family in Canada [15]. Research shows that the QoL perception of informal care recipients differs between European countries, being the highest in Northern Europe and the lowest in Southern Europe, while receiving formal support was associated with worse QoL perceptions in Northern, Central and Eastern Europe [16].

There is wide scientific evidence on long-term care at home provided to older people, especially taking into account the care conditions (for example, intensity, frequency and type of tasks), intersectionality and impact on the caregivers’ situation and QoL. However, less is known about the characteristics and living conditions of older people according to informal, or unpaid, care status at home and how this differs across European countries, which is the goal of the present study. Therefore, the use of a database makes it possible to address the proposed objective, especially from a comparative approach in the context of Europe. This makes it possible to transfer scientific knowledge to support the design and implementation of public policies. Moreover, most existing studies focus on the experience or burden of caregiving, rather than identifying comprehensive profiles of health and QoL for both caregivers and care recipients. Limited research has examined differences between live-in and non-live-in caregivers, an important contextual issue in caregiving research. This study addresses this gap by exploring multidimensional health patterns in different caregiving status in the European context.

From this perspective, it was expected that health-related indicators would reveal two distinct dimensions—physical and mental health—and that patterns would either be differentiated along these dimensions or follow a general health gradient. Additionally, the presence of a “healthy caregiver” effect was hypothesized, in which individuals in better health would be more likely to provide care, particularly in non-live-in informal caregivers. For this, multiple correspondence analysis (MCA) was considered an appropriate exploratory technique to identify underlying dimensions and to visualize potential patterns of association with caregiving status.

The aim of this study was to describe the health profiles of older adults in terms of their mental and physical health, loneliness and QoL, in the context of countries included in the SHARE survey. Furthermore, we characterized these profiles based on participants’ caregiving status, exploring how receiving care, providing care, or neither of these is related to relevant aspects related to aging. This may identify key issues on which to base health plans to promote healthy ageing, a key objective in European health agendas.

Methods

Participants and data collection

Data came from the survey SHARE [17, 18] that is “a research infrastructure for studying the effects of health, social, economic and environmental policies over the life-course of European citizens and beyond […] and enables policymakers to tackle socio-economic and public health challenges with the help of scientific evidence” [19]. Underlying the development of this survey is the interest in understanding the differences in QoL in the most relevant spheres of this construct, namely health conditions and socio-economic status. At present, nine waves have been carried out, in addition to two relating to COVID-19.

We used wave 8 at the time of the study was developed, to provide the most up-to-date insights possible. A sample of the 8th wave people aged 50 years and older was recruited using face-to-face interviews based on computer-assisted personal interviewing (CAPI) from 2019 to March 2020 and computer assisted telephone interview (CATI) between June and July 2020. The sampling design used in the SHARE countries was a multi-stage stratified [20]. The final number of participants in this wave was 46,500 from 25 European countries and Israel.

The SHARE project has been approved under the Ethics Committee of the University of Mannheim and the Ethics Council of the Max Planck Society, Germany, and by national ethics committees or institutional review boards whenever this was required. SHARE data are anonymized.

Variables and measures

Socio-demographic variables: sex, age, current job situation (retired or self-employed, unemployed, permanently sick or disabled and homemaker), location (urban and rural area) and frequency of grandchildren care in the last twelve months (never, less often, about every month, about every week, about daily). Although people without grandchildren have been taken into account, geographical proximity may also be influencing caregiving dynamics for those with grandchildren.

Caregiving status: Caregivers were differentiated according to two questions related to giving help inside or outside the household. Non-live-in caregivers were identified by the question: “In the last twelve months, have you personally given any kind of help listed on this card to a family member from outside the household, a friend or neighbour?”. The response options included personal help (e.g. dressing, showering, eating, getting in or out of bed and using the toilet), practical household help (e.g. gardening, transportation, shopping and household chores) and help with paperwork (e.g. filling out forms, settling financial or legal matters). Live-in caregivers were identified using the question: “Is there someone living in this household whom you have helped regularly during the last twelve months with personal care, such as washing, getting out of bed, or dressing?”. The participants who did not respond affirmatively to both questions were considered non-involved in caregiving. Non-involved in caregiving and those who received help were classified as care recipients. The final variable was defined as caregiving status with four mutually exclusive categories: non-involved in caregiving (no help received), care recipient, non-live-in caregiver and live-in caregiver. More information about the variable can be found in (Fig. 1).

Health and social variables: practice of vigorous or moderate physical activity, the Global Activity Limitation Index (GALI) [21], number of chronic diseases, the instrumental activities of daily living (IADL) index [22], number of medicines, pain, wearing glasses/contact lenses or use hearing aid, ever smoked daily, family and friend network, satisfaction with personal network (score 1–10), and the scale of social connectedness (score 0–4) [23] with greater scores indicating higher social connectedness.

To explore health-related profiles, we included a set of the following measures: QoL was assessed using the CASP-12 scale [24, 25], EURO-D [26] for depression into three categories [27], R-UCLA for loneliness [28], life satisfaction scale based on the SF-36 questionnaire [29], activities of daily living (ADL) index [30] and subjective perception of memory problems. A full list of these measures and their description is available in Supplementary Table S1.

Statistical analysis

Frequencies and percentages were calculated to describe the categorical variables and the means and standard deviations (SD) for the continuous variables. A comparison between the four groups of participants based on the caregiving status was performed, using chi-square test and analysis of variance (ANOVA) for categorical and continuous variables, respectively.

To identify the caregiver profiles, a combination of MCA and cluster analysis were used [31, 32]. The MCA is a method used for dimensionality reduction of categorical variables, providing a graphical representation. Scales scores were included in the MCA: CASP-12 QoL, life satisfaction, R-UCLA Loneliness, self-perceived health, limitations with ADL, EURO-D depression, and memory test. Both continuous variables, CASP-12 and life satisfaction were categorized into quartiles, widely used method when no determined categorization exist. In order to analyse the relationship between caregiving status and the rest of variables used to construct the MCA, it was introduced in the analysis as an illustrative variable. Each category of the variables represents a point on the MCA graph, showing a stronger relationship with those points that are closer.

Cluster analysis was used to assign the individuals into groups, based on the association between the variables provided by the MCA. The classification was performed using a k-means non-hierarchical cluster analysis with the first two dimensions of the MCA results. The number of clusters were selected by the Calinski-Harabasz criterion. Clusters with average Jaccard similarities scores greater than 0.85 were considered highly stable [33, 34]. The clusters were represented graphically using the MCA dimensions. Chi-square test and ANOVA were used to characterize the observed groups and analyse the differences between the clusters.

Currently, the available software packages do not support the complex sampling design in MCA analysis. Given that the objective of this study is to conduct an exploratory analysis of health patterns, we considered appropriate the use of the unweighted analysis.

A stepwise multinomial logistic regression model was performed to identify factors associated with in the probability of belonging to each cluster. Independent variables included caregiving status, sociodemographic, health, and social network variables.

For the final data analysis, 9.7% individuals were removed due to missing values in the variables included in the MCA and cluster analysis.

Statistical analyses were performed using statistical software R version 4.3.2 and RStudio 2023.12.1 Build 402, using the packages “ca” “fpc” and “factoextra”.

Results

Complete data was available for 41,999 individuals, with a mean age of 71 and SD of 9.1 years and 57.2% of women (Table 1). The percentage of non-involved in caregiving was 56.2%; care recipient, 21.4%; non-live-in caregiver, 18.0%; and live-in caregiver, 4.4%. The 69.5% of live-in caregivers were the partners of the care recipients, and their average age was slightly higher than that of other live-in caregivers (72.5 vs. 69.5 years). The 50.4% never provided care for grandchildren, and 17.7% had no grandchildren. Over two thirds of the sample (68.7%) was retired, while 18.8% were employed, and 32.3% lived in rural areas. Regarding health variables, almost half of the sample (47.9%) reported having limitation in the GALI index and 44.5% of the participants reported pain. The average number of chronic conditions and medications was 1.9 and 2.0, and a large percentage used glasses or hearing aids (92.2%). Social support indicators, including satisfaction and network size, showed small differences between caregiving groups. Considering the results of the MCA, the CH index indicated that 3 clusters were optimal using k-means method. The three clusters revealed high stability measured with the Jaccard index (Supplementary material Table S2). This indicates that the three clusters identified are internally consistent and reproducible. The distribution of the clusters also showed a gradient across the variables included in the analysis, so they could be named as “good health”, “moderate health” and “poor health”, representing the 42.6%, 50.6%, and 6.8% of the total sample, respectively (Fig. 2B).

Cluster characterization

Table 3 presents the differences between the three clusters according socio-demographic characteristics, health and social network variables. The cluster 1 (good health) showed a lower mean age (69.2 years) and a lower percentage of women (54.8%) than the other clusters. People in this cluster were mostly non-involved in caregiving who did not receive help (56.5%) and non-live in caregivers (24.2%). They also practiced vigorous sports or activities (69.2%) and reported no limitations according to GALI (71.2%), and no pain troubles (71.1%). In addition, this group presented higher social network satisfaction (mean = 9.3) and social connectedness (mean = 2.2).

Discussion

This study focused on identifying profiles of older adults living in Europe based on their health status and QoL and characterizing these profiles according to their caregiving status. Our study suggests that measures of physical and mental health, loneliness and QoL categorize more than half of the sample in the moderate and poor health groups in Europe. Furthermore, the results of this research allow to detect patterns by caregiving status, indicating a higher percentage of non-live-in caregivers in the good health cluster, non-involved in caregiving in the moderate health cluster and care recipient and live-in caregivers in the poor health cluster. Although the study of population health is a topic of deep interest and the subject of numerous studies, the analysis based on the care profile, both of care recipients and caregivers, is more novel. Few studies have addressed the care profile of informal caregivers when residing or not in the home of the person receiving care [35–37].

The results of MCA plot showed response categories of the variables follow a curved trajectory along a gradient ranging from excellent to poor QoL, physical and mental health. This pattern is named “horseshoe effect” or “Guttman effect” and it usually appears between the first two components of the MCA. This curved effect is due to mathematical artefacts [38], where the first dimension presents an ordered structure, and the second identifies more extreme values. This shape suggests that there is a dominant one-dimensional structure, which represents a gradient over the variables included in the analysis.

Poor health is an explanatory factor for being recipient of care [39]. Therefore, we expected that people who receive care have worse health than those who do not. In our study, women were more likely to be care-receptors or live-in caregivers. This is consistent with previous studies that show that women at older ages have poorer health [40, 41] and a greater involvement in care tasks than men [42–44]. Participants who worked (employed or self-employed) represented 18% of our sample, with a higher proportion among non-live-in caregivers and non-involved in caregiving who did not receive help. This is explained by the younger age of active workers, but active ageing could also play a role in this distribution [45].

Results indicates that, in Europe, one out of three older people is an informal caregiver. This fact is related to the increase in life expectancy and that more than one generation may live in the same home, as well as the expansion of the individual network of exchanges in care [46]. Similarly to other studies [47], we observed a predominance of non-live-in informal caregivers over live-in family caregivers.

Concerning the group of caregivers, the association between caregiving and health deterioration is probably more related to the care model than to the fact of caring. That is, the conditions under which care is provided influence the health and QoL. The care model includes factors such as live-in informal caregivers, the intensity and the duration of care tasks and the availability of formal support. Research has been done on the health of live-in caregivers, often concluding that care tasks, especially when prolonged over time, have a negative impact on their health [48, 49]. However, the health of non-live-in caregivers is similar to those non-involved in caregiving. This would be due to lower care burden, in terms of hours and type of tasks performed [50], and to the existence of the healthy caregiver bias [11, 51–53]. The fact that the care receptor does not live at home would also allow the caregiver to maintain certain freedom of choice regarding the moments and care tasks they assume. This freedom of choice, which is often enjoyed to a lesser extent by live-in caregivers, minimize the negative impact of care tasks on the health of non-live-in caregivers [48]. Furthermore, non-live-in caregivers have more access to leisure activities outside the home than live-in caregivers, which seems to have a protective effect on the health of caregivers in general [54].

Live-in informal caregivers showed worse health than non-live-in informal caregivers in our study. In our sample, the most live-in caregivers were partners of the care recipients and tended to be older. This suggests that many of them are older adults with potential physical limitations, which may contribute to the poorer health outcomes observed in this group. These findings support previous evidence indicating that live-in family caregivers are probably very overburdened [55]. In this regard, few countries have developed support services aimed at informal caregivers [56]. Therefore, it is necessary to generate formal support strategies (social services) and informal support (family network) to reduce the vulnerability of the cohabiting caregiver, by providing information and different types of practical assistance [57]. Multinomial regression confirmed these patterns, with live-in caregivers showing significantly higher odds of belonging to the poor health cluster, compared to non-involved in caregiving. This reinforces the health gradient observed in the clusters and highlights the relevance of differentiating between caregiving status in public health strategies.

The data reveal the differences across European countries in the profile of elderly caregivers and non-involved in caregiving according to health, QoL, and loneliness, and this could have implications for social and health systems. Public data on long-term care expenditure support this pattern: countries such as the Netherlands, Sweden, and Norway allocate over 3% of their GDP to long-term care, whereas countries like Bulgaria, Cyprus and Latvia spend less than 0.5% [58, 59]. Thus, in countries in Northern and central Europe, such as Denmark, Switzerland, Austria and Sweden, higher public spending on health and long-term care is related to a higher proportion of older people with good health and QoL, fewer feelings of loneliness and higher proportion of informal care [47]. Meanwhile, in Eastern and Southern European countries, such as Bulgaria, Cyprus, Lithuania and Latvia, lower public spending on health and long-term care is associated with a higher proportion of older people with poor health and QoL, more feelings of loneliness and more proportion of caregivers, especially cohabiting caregivers at risk of being overburdened. The findings highlight the importance of social protection policies, investing in public spending on health and long-term care, to promote active and healthy ageing, as well as reduce the vulnerability of informal caregivers [60].

Our study has some limitations. The information analysed comes from SHARE as collected in a broad survey. In this sense, we do not have information about the person receiving care, their level of dependency or underlying illness or diseases, which would have been useful to us. Also, some information, such as daily hours of care or help with non-basic activities of daily living, which is registered for non-live-in caregivers, is not available for live-in caregivers, which has conditioned our possible analyses. As explained before, the rural/urban dichotomy used in our analysis may have resulted in an excessive simplification of the concept and can present important differences between countries. Also, there are differences between the social and healthcare systems of different countries that are not included among the variables in the study. MCA analysis have been used to analyse all health-related variables together, as it gives the opportunity to analyse categorical variables, which other methods cannot. Hence, two continuous variables have been categorised. Categorizing continuous variables often results in the loss of some information, but in this case, as the results have presented the expected gradient between the variables, the underlying distribution have somehow preserved. In addition, given the cross-sectional nature of our data, we cannot determine the causal relationship. In this study, the sampling weights provided by SHARE survey were excluded from the statistical analysis due to limitations of the MCA analysis. Further methodological developments are needed to integrate the weights in future MCA studies.

Conclusions

This research highlights the factors that shape caregiving status and may guide the development of care models aimed at maintaining caregivers’ health. The observed differences in health status across the various categories of caregivers and care recipients identified in this study provide valuable insights for public policy, helping to target support towards the most vulnerable subpopulations. Given the progressive ageing of the population, implementing policies focused on sustainable care models could have a widespread impact. Moreover, identifying patterns or profiles of adults over 50 years old based on health and QoL indicators enables the design of personalized care interventions that address specific needs. This approach not only benefits caregivers but also improves health outcomes for care recipients and society as a whole. Future research should aim to replicate the results using longitudinal SHARE data, examine causal relationships, and explore the moderating roles of gender and socioeconomic status in caregiving patterns.
