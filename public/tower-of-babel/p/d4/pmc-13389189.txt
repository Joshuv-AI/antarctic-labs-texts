Establishing demographic-based normative values for neurofilament light chain and glial fibrillary acidic protein in serum and plasma samples
Authors: Tyler L Borko, Alanna Ritchie, Sean Selva, Stefan Sillau, Eric Engebretson, Andrew B Wolf, Enrique Alvarez, Robert Gross, Eric Gutierrez, John Corboy, Diego Silva, Ryan Kammeyer, Amanda L Piquet, Timothy L Vollmer, Salim Chahin, Gregory Owens, Jeffrey L Bennett, Kavita V Nair
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInterpretation of neurofilament light chain (NfL) and glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) levels in blood requires reference ranges from healthy cohorts, accounting for demographic features.ObjectiveTo compare serum and plasma NfL and GFAP in healthy individuals, accounting for age, sex, and body mass index (BMI).MethodsAdolescents through older adults were recruited at Colorado community events and a senior clinic. A neurologist reviewed self-reported medical histories. Paired serum and EDTA plasma samples were assayed using the Quanterix SR-X Simoa Neuro 4-Plex. Log-linear models estimated percent change per year of age, per BMI unit, and sex ratios. GFAP used a piecewise age model (knot at 30 years). Spearman correlation assessed serum–plasma concordance.ResultsWe enrolled 143 participants (ages = 16.4–85.9; 63.6% female; mean BMI = 26.7). Serum–plasma correlations were strong (NfL ρ = .923; GFAP ρ = .833; both p < .001). In plasma, age was associated with a 2.96% increase in NfL and 2.34% in GFAP per year for ages ≥30. In serum, NfL rose 2.90% and GFAP 2.69% per year (both p < .0001).ConclusionNfL and GFAP are strongly age-dependent and concordant across serum and plasma in healthy individuals. An online calculator provides age-, sex-, and BMI-adjusted reference estimates and serum/plasma conversions.Keywords: Multiple sclerosis, biomarkers, demyelination, NfL, GFAP

Introduction

Neurofilament light chain (NfL) and glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) are increasingly recognized blood-based biomarkers in multiple sclerosis (MS) and other autoimmune neurologic diseases. NfL, a neuronal cytoskeletal protein, reflects neuroaxonal injury, while GFAP, an astrocytic cytoskeletal protein, indicates glial injury or microglial activation. Although cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) sampling provides direct measurement, blood testing is more practical and has been studied across dementias, stroke, traumatic brain injury, neuropathies, and neuroimmunologic disorders.1–3 In MS, blood NfL predicts relapse risk and disability progression, while GFAP correlates with disease activity.4–8 Despite growing use, reference ranges in healthy individuals remain insufficiently defined.

Clinical application is limited by several challenges. First, these biomarkers are mechanism-specific but not disease-specific, with elevations across neurologic conditions. Levels vary with demographic and physiologic factors such as age, body mass index (BMI), renal function, and hemoglobin A1C.9–11 Second, healthy-control reference ranges remain incomplete. Prior studies often relied on broad age ranges, convenience samples, or incomplete screening, limiting generalizability. Third, the inconsistent use of serum versus plasma further complicates comparisons, as most studies of NfL rely on serum.4,6,9,12–14 While strong intraindividual correlations exist between serum and plasma NfL and GFAP,14–16 absolute values differ, preventing interchangeability. Some studies report median differences,15–18 and only one has proposed a serum–plasma conversion for NfL in older adults, 19 with none for GFAP. This prevents comparison between studies in which different preparations of blood samples are used.

To address these gaps, we prospectively enrolled adolescents through older adults at community events and a senior's clinic. Our objectives were to measure paired serum and plasma NfL and GFAP in screened healthy controls, quantify effects of age, sex, and BMI, establish reference estimates, and characterize serum–plasma relationships to support cross-study interpretation.

Methods

Healthy controls recruitment

We enrolled individuals at various community events and a senior's clinic to obtain a representative sample of the Colorado population based on the 2020 US census (Figure 1). 20 Events included local college campuses, a 5 K race, outside a community of independent eateries, the Rocky Mountain Multiple Sclerosis Center Education Summits and kickoff events, the Anschutz Medical Campus Block Party, and the University's recreational center. Individuals aged 65 years and older were additionally recruited through the Seniors Clinic at the University of Colorado Anschutz Medical Campus (Table 1). We created a screening tool (Appendix A in the supplementary materials) to determine the presence or absence of self-reported comorbidities including other major neurologic or autoimmune disease which were reviewed by a neurologist (Figure 2).

Community data collection

At community events, we operated a staffed booth with privacy screens for specimen collection and research education. Interested individuals reviewed study information and provided e-consent on REDCap generated forms. After consent, participants completed a screening questionnaire; eligibility was confirmed on site. For eligible participants, height, weight, and seated blood pressure were obtained before venipuncture. A serum separator tube and one K2-ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) plasma tube were collected per participant, stored on wet ice (≤4 h), then transported to the University of Colorado Anschutz Medical Campus for processing and −80 °C storage. Participants received a $20 gift card. The goal was to recruit up to 20 participants per decade of age (13–85 years).

Measurement of NfL and GFAP

Details of assay setup are in Appendix B in the supplementary materials. Briefly, paired EDTA plasma and serum were analyzed on the Simoa SR-X platform (Quanterix, Billerica, MA, USA) using the Neuro 4-Plex B™ Advantage kit for plasma and the Neuro 2-Plex B™ Advantage kit for serum, following the manufacturer's two-step protocols. Assays were run with lot-specific calibrators and results were generated from extrapolated standard curves.21,22

Statistical methods

Analyses were performed using complete-case data. NfL and GFAP concentrations in both serum and plasma were natural log-transformed to reduce right-skew and support log-linear modeling. Geometric means and 95% confidence intervals were summarized by sex within age decades beginning at age 13 years. For each biomarker and sample type, graphical diagnostics and spline-based assessments were used to evaluate the adequacy of a log-linear age effect and to inform covariate selection. The log-transformed biomarker values were regressed on age, BMI, and sex; observations were weighted by the number of replicate measurements. Piecewise log-linear models were used when departures from log-linearity were important, and sex-stratified models were also fit. Regression coefficients for effects of age, BMI, and sex were tested for statistical significance. Model results are reported as percent change per year of age, percent change per unit BMI, and male-to-female ratios, with simultaneous 95% Scheffé confidence bands for predicted geometric means. Based on these models, we developed algorithms to estimate conditional geometric means as a function of age, BMI, and sex with simultaneous 95% Scheffé confidence bands and 95% prediction intervals.

Serum and plasma NfL and GFAP concentrations were compared using Spearman correlations. To derive conversion formulas between samples we fit linear regression models of log(serum) on log(plasma) and of log(plasma) on log(serum) for each biomarker. All tests were two-sided with α = 0.05 unless otherwise specified. Analyses were conducted in SAS 9.4, R, and Stata 17.

Results

Neurofilament light (NfL)

Geometric mean NfL and GFAP were measured in plasma and serum for all participants. Plasma NfL ranged from 2.48 pg/mL (95% CI: 1.87–3.29) at ages 16–19 to 19.55 pg/mL (95% CI: 9.56–39.99) at ages 80–85 (Table 2). A log-linear age effect fit the data. Adjusted for BMI and sex, plasma NfL increased 2.96% per year (95% CI: 2.55–3.36, p < .0001); results were similar by sex (Figure 3). Plasma NfL decreased 1.70% per BMI unit (95% CI: 0.42–2.96, p = .0095). The male:female ratio, adjusted for age and BMI, was 1.14 (95% CI: 0.99–1.32, p = .0625) (Table 3).

GFAP

Plasma GFAP ranged from 49.99 pg/mL (95% CI: 41.74–59.87) at ages 16–19 to 121.77 pg/mL (95% CI: 91.07–162.82) at ages 80–85 (Table 2). Diagnostics supported a piecewise age model with a knot at 30 years: ≤30 years, GFAP decreased 2.28%/year (95% CI: 0.39–4.13, p = .0185); ≥30 years, GFAP increased 2.34%/year (95% CI: 1.86–2.82, p < .0001; difference in slopes p < .0001) (Figure 3). The BMI effect was not significant (−0.89% per unit; 95% CI: −2.09–0.32, p = .1488). Adjusted plasma GFAP did not differ meaningfully by sex (male:female 0.91, 95% CI: 0.80–1.04, p = .1808) (Table 3).

Serum GFAP ranged from 54.11 pg/mL (95% CI: 42.28–66.11) at ages 16–19 to 154.47 pg/mL (95% CI: 121.58–196.26) at ages 80–85 (Table 2). With the same piece-wise model, ≤30 years, GFAP decreased 2.71%/year (95% CI: 0.64–4.73, p = .0108); ≥30 years, GFAP increased 2.69%/year (95% CI: 2.19–3.19, p < .0001; difference in slopes p < .0001) (Figure 4). The BMI effect was not significant (−1.07% per unit; 95% CI: −0.25 to 2.38, p = .1112). Adjusted serum GFAP was lower in males (male:female 0.82, 95% CI: 0.71–0.95, p = .0077) (Table 4).

Effects of age and other factors

Age was the dominant predictor for both biomarkers across sample types. NfL increased with age in plasma and serum (≈2.9–3.0% per year; Tables 3 and 4). GFAP demonstrated a non-linear relationship: levels declined modestly before age 30 and rose thereafter, consistent with the piecewise models used for inference (Tables 3 and 4). Only two participants in the entire cohort endorsed regular tobacco use, which limits the ability to understand the impact of this covariate on GFAP and NfL.

Effect of BMI

Higher BMI was associated with lower NfL concentrations in the overall cohort, although sex-stratified analyses showed variability in the estimated strength and statistical significance of these associations. GFAP demonstrated a similar inverse directional trend, but BMI effects were not statistically significant (Tables 3 and 4).

Online tool

We provide an online calculator (age = 17–85 years; BMI = 17–44; sex female/male) that returns geometric mean estimates with simultaneous 95% Scheffé confidence bands, and 95% prediction intervals based on the fitted models: https://eric-gutierrez.shinyapps.io/HCdata_tool/.

The tool enables researchers to generate sample-specific (serum/plasma) reference values for NfL and GFAP matched on age, BMI, and sex, and to compare expected values across plasma and serum. We also provide an algorithm for converting plasma concentrations into serum concentrations, and vice-versa, with accompanying simultaneous 95% Scheffé confidence bands for the estimates.23,24

Discussion

Our principal findings were consistent across biomarkers and sample types. Age was the dominant driver: NfL increased by approximately 3% per year in both plasma and serum, whereas GFAP showed a non-linear relationship, decreasing before age 30 and increasing thereafter. Age was the dominant driver: NfL increased by approximately 3% per year in both plasma and serum, whereas GFAP showed a non-linear relationship, decreasing before age 30 and increasing thereafter. Our findings regarding BMI should be interpreted with some nuance. Higher BMI was associated with lower NfL (and a similar direction for GFAP) concentrations in the overall cohort for both plasma and serum samples; however, sex-stratified analyses demonstrated variability in the estimated magnitude and statistical significance of these associations. Additional exploratory analysis found no statistically significant effect modification for age and BMI by sex (results not shown and can be provided if needed). Given the modest sample size within sex-stratified subgroups, these differences may reflect limited statistical power and wider confidence intervals rather than definitive biologic effect modification by sex. Nonetheless, the findings suggest that potential sex-specific relationships between BMI and circulating neurologic biomarkers merit further evaluation in larger, adequately powered cohorts.

When compared to prior research summarized in Table 5, Fitzgerald et al. leveraged the U.S.-nationally representative NHANES dataset to model serum NfL and identify which demographic, clinical, and laboratory factors explain variability beyond age, helping contextualize NfL in a broad general-population setting. Khalil et al. analyzed serum NfL in the Austrian Stroke Prevention cohort and linked longitudinal NfL trajectories to subclinical MRI brain changes and cognitive outcomes over ∼6 years, positioning NfL as a marker of brain aging and cerebrovascular risk rather than a reference-range tool. 25 Vermunt et al. presented age- and disease-specific NfL reference values in an interactive support interface, addressing the need to contextualize NfL across healthy and diseased cohorts. 26 Benkert et al. derived age- and BMI-adjusted percentiles and z-scores for serum NfL and validated clinical relevance in MS cohorts, enabling standardized interpretation across individuals and therapies. 27 Our work complements these efforts by adding GFAP and by providing paired plasma and serum reference estimates derived from a screened, community-based cohort. Our work also highlights sample-specific differences whose considerations remain important for harmonizing the literature. While within-person correlations between serum and plasma are typically strong, systematic differences in absolute values and pre-analytic factors can influence measurements.14–16,19 When possible, clinicians and researchers should interpret values using reference estimates derived for the same sample type and assay platform.

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered. First, the data are cross-sectional; therefore, we report associations across age rather than within-person longitudinal change. Second, medical history and symptom screening were self-reported and may not fully exclude subclinical conditions that influence biomarker levels. Third, despite community outreach, the cohort may not fully represent all Colorado demographic groups; future studies could improve representativeness by partnering with community clinics, using bilingual materials, mobile or home phlebotomy, and targeted recruitment or oversampling of underrepresented groups. Fourth, sample sizes in the youngest and oldest strata were smaller, leading to wider confidence intervals at the extremes. Fifth, although participants with a history of diabetes were excluded, renal function was not systematically incorporated into the analyses. Because biomarkers such as NfL and GFAP may be influenced by kidney function, and renal function changes with age, the absence of adjustment for measures such as estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) could have introduced residual confounding and may partially influence the observed age-related associations. Sixth, while we can estimate expected values of the biomarkers with decent precision, variability among individual patients renders the prediction intervals wide. Physicians and researchers who use our predictions should be aware of the heterogeneity of biomarker levels among individual patients.

Conclusions

In screened, community-based healthy controls, both NfL and GFAP increase with age in plasma and serum, with GFAP demonstrating a piece-wise relationship before and after age 30. Higher BMI was modestly associated with lower biomarker concentrations, particularly for NfL. Independent sex effects were generally limited after adjustment for age and BMI, although serum GFAP concentrations were significantly lower in males compared with females. Qualitatively, results were mostly similar by sex.

Strong serum-plasma correlations support concordant trends across matrices but do not imply interchangeability of absolute values. Together, these data provide practical, sample-specific reference estimates and an online tool to support interpretation of NfL and GFAP in research and clinical contexts.
