Enhanced Peribiliary Gland Detachment in Aged Donor Grafts After Rat Liver Transplantation
Authors: Kaoru Katano, Shinichi Nakanuma, Takashi Sugio, Junichi Ogawa, Naoto Nishida, Hiroaki Horio, Ryohei Takei, Satoshi Takada, Mitsuyoshi Okazaki, Kaichiro Kato, Yasuhito Iwao, Isamu Makino, Kenichi Harada, Shintaro Yagi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background.Older donor livers have higher rates of biliary complications after transplantation, but early mechanisms of bile duct injury remain incompletely understood. Peribiliary glands (PBGs) in the bile duct wall contain progenitor-like and secretory elements that may support epithelial maintenance and repair. We examined whether donor age is associated with early PBG injury and recovery and explored links with the basement-membrane component laminin.Methods.Livers from young or aged rats were transplanted into young recipients. Extrahepatic bile ducts were evaluated before transplantation and at 24, 72, and 168 h after liver transplantation. PBG morphology and detachment, laminin γ1 expression, and markers related to progenitor-cell characteristics, mucus production, microvascular density, and apoptosis were assessed histologically and immunohistochemically.Results.In aged donor grafts, PBGs showed progressive structural injury after transplantation, with marked cell detachment and loss of glandular architecture at 168 h. In contrast, PBGs in young donor grafts were largely preserved. Areas of PBG cell detachment in aged grafts were spatially associated with reduced laminin γ1 expression. Recovery of laminin γ1 expression over time was less evident in aged grafts and was accompanied by impaired progenitor-cell marker expression, reduced mucus production, persistently decreased microvascular density, and increased shedding of apoptotic PBG cells.Conclusions.Aged donor grafts showed more pronounced early structural PBG injury after transplantation, together with persistent reduction of laminin γ1 expression.

INTRODUCTION

Liver transplantation (LT) is the definitive treatment for end-stage liver disease, acute liver failure, and hepatocellular carcinoma. Despite various recent advances in LT, including improvements in surgical and organ-preservation techniques and immunosuppressive drug strategies, the incidence of biliary complications remains high and has been reported to be 10%–32%.1 Among these, ischemic-type biliary lesions (ITBLs)—defined as diffuse, nonanastomotic strictures of the donor bile ducts in the presence of patent hepatic arterial flow—represent one of the most serious late complications, leading to graft loss and mortality.2-5 Multiple factors are associated with the development of ITBLs, including ischemia/reperfusion injury (IRI), bile salt-mediated injury, and immune-mediated injury.3,6,7 In addition, aged donors have been reported to be an independent risk factor for posttransplant ITBLs.8-10

Peribiliary glands (PBGs) are tubular alveolar glands located within the large intrahepatic and extrahepatic bile duct (EHBD) wall.11-13 They harbor stem/progenitor cells that contribute to epithelial regeneration and repair.13-17 Recent studies have shown that ischemia-induced damage to the PBGs during LT and chronic hypoxia of the bile duct wall because of the loss of the peribiliary vascular plexus are involved in the development of ITBL.18,19 However, the pathogenesis and detailed mechanisms underlying this risk in aged donors are not fully understood. In our previous study, we reported the effects of aging on IRI in the PBGs using a rat model.20 Although young rats showed few morphological changes in the perihilar bile ducts after IRI, older rats showed high levels of oxidative stress, PBG proliferation, bile duct wall thickening, and increased mucin production. However, that model did not fully reproduce the IRI that occurs in LT and did not show the PBG destruction or epithelial loss characteristic of clinical ITBLs.

Therefore, we investigated age-related differences in PBG injury and regeneration in a rat LT model, with particular attention to the association between laminin γ1 in the gland basement membrane and PBG integrity after transplantation.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Animals

Male Lewis rats (SLC Inc., Shizuoka, Japan) were used in this study. The animals were housed under a 12-h light/dark cycle at the Animal Experiment Laboratory of Kanazawa University. They were allowed free access to water and standard chow throughout the experiment. All the experiments and procedures were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of the University of Kanazawa (AP-224335; Kanazawa, Japan).

Experimental Protocol

The experiment used 2 age groups of Lewis donor rats: young donors (YDs; 8–9 wk; 250–280 g) and old donors (ODs; 44–52 wk; 460–620 g). Livers from both donor groups were transplanted orthotopically into young Lewis recipients (8–9 wk; 260–300 g) using a standard non-donation after circulatory death rat LT model. Cold ischemia time was fixed at 180 min in histidine-tryptophan-ketoglutarate (HTK) solution (Custodiol; Dr. Franz Köhler Chemie GmbH, Alsbach-Hähnlein, Germany) at 4 °C. Because whole-liver grafts from ODs were too large for the recipients, we used a 60% graft (right portion of the middle, right, and caudate lobes). The same 60% graft model was also used for young-to-young transplantation to keep the surgical model consistent between groups (Figure 1A). The graft-to-recipient weight ratio (GRWR) was calculated as graft weight (g)/recipient body weight (g) × 100 (%). Animals were excluded if technical problems occurred during surgery, if anhepatic time exceeded 20 min, or if blood loss exceeded 2 mL. Anhepatic time was defined as the interval from portal vein clamping to portal reperfusion. Recipients were euthanized at 24, 72, and 168 h after transplantation (n = 5 per group at each time point), and liver samples including the EHBD were collected; the evaluated EHBD segment is shown in Figure 1B. As controls, additional donor rats were euthanized and liver samples including the EHBD were collected before transplantation (n = 5 per group).

Experimental design and EHBD sampling site. A, Livers from young or old Lewis donors were transplanted orthotopically into young Lewis recipients using a 60% reduced-size graft model. B, The analyzed EHBD segment is indicated (arrow/bracket). EHBD, extrahepatic bile duct.

Partial Orthotopic Liver Transplantation

Donor Operation

As described previously,21 the whole liver was explanted after intravenous administration of 300 international units of heparin sodium through the penile vein, followed by perfusion of the liver with 50 mL of cold HTK solution through the aorta.

Ex Vivo Graft Preparation Including 40% Liver Resection

The donor liver was immersed in HTK solution on ice. A 14-gauge catheter cuff was attached to the portal vein. The left portion of the median lobe and the left lateral lobes were removed after ligating the pedicles. The graft was kept in HTK solution at 4 °C for 180 min.

Recipient Operation

After removing the recipient’s liver, a 60% partial graft was implanted. The suprahepatic and infrahepatic vena cava, portal vein, hepatic artery, and common bile duct were reconstructed as described in detail previously.21 The rats were allowed to recover after abdominal closure with 4-0 nylon sutures.

Light Microscopy, Histopathology, and Immunohistochemistry

The EHBD between the branches to the right and caudate lobes and the branches to the middle and left lobes were assessed histologically (Figure 1B). Liver tissues were fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin, processed, embedded in paraffin, and serially sliced into 4-μm sections. Slides were stained with hematoxylin and eosin according to standard procedures. Analysis of the histomorphology of the EHBD revealed detachment and shedding of PBG cells from the basement membrane and destruction of the PBG after LT. For quantitative evaluation, these findings were subdivided into 5 categories according to the degree of PBG cell detachment, and the “PBG detachment score” was defined (Figure 2A). All evaluable PBGs within the EHBD wall were scored, and the mean detachment score per animal was calculated by averaging scores across PBGs.

PBG, peribiliary gland.

Periodic acid-Schiff (PAS) staining was performed to assess mucus production by PBGs. The percentage of PAS-positive acini among all acini was converted into a semiquantitative score: 0, <5%; 1, 5%–10%; 2, 10%–30%; 3, 30%–50%; and 4, >50%.19,22

Immunohistochemistry

Immunohistochemical analysis was performed to examine the expression levels of cytokeratin 7 (CK7), Ki-67, SRY-box transcription factor 9 (SOX9), laminin γ1, CD31, and cleaved caspase-3 (cCaspase-3). The tissue sections were initially deparaffinized with xylene and rehydrated using a graded ethanol series. Antigen retrieval for Ki-67 and cCaspase-3 was performed in citrate buffer (pH 6.1), while CK7, SOX9, laminin γ1, and CD31 required Tris-EDTA buffer (pH 9); both retrieval steps were conducted by autoclaving at 120 °C for 15 min. Endogenous proteins were blocked using a protein block solution (Dako Cytomation, Kyoto, Japan) for 10 min at room temperature, followed by incubation with normal goat serum. The sections were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: rabbit monoclonal anti-CK7 (1:8000), anti-Ki-67 (1:200), anti-SOX9 (1:2000), anti-laminin γ1 (1:2000), anti-CD31 (1:2000; all from Abcam, Cambridge, United Kingdom), and rabbit polyclonal anti-cCaspase-3 (1:200; Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA). A horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibody was applied for 30 min, and the immunoreactivity was visualized using 0.02% 3,3′-diaminobenzidine tetrahydrochloride solution supplemented with 0.1% hydrogen peroxide. Finally, the sections were lightly counterstained with hematoxylin and examined under a light microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

The anti-CK7 antibody was used to evaluate the volume occupied by the PBG (PBG area) in the EHBD and was expressed as a percentage of the total duct wall.19,22 The areas with destroyed PBGs were excluded. PBG cell proliferation was assessed by immunostaining with a Ki-67 antibody. The proliferation index was determined by calculating the proportion of Ki-67+ cells within 5 randomly selected PBGs. All PBG cells within each selected gland were manually counted.20 The number of stem/progenitor cells in the PBG was assessed using a SOX9 antibody. SOX9 expression was quantified by calculating the percentage of SOX9-positive PBG cells among 5 randomly selected PBGs. All PBG cells were counted manually. The anti-laminin γ1 antibody was used to evaluate the expression of laminin in the basement membrane surrounding the PBGs. For each PBG, the area of preserved laminin-positive area was traced, and the percentage of preserved laminin area was calculated.23 For quantitative evaluation, the degree of laminin expression was subdivided into 5 categories, and the “laminin expression score” was defined as follows: 0, <10%; 1, 10%–25%; 2, 25%–50%; 3, 50%–75%; and 4, >75% (Figure 2B). All evaluable PBGs were scored, and the mean laminin expression score per animal was calculated. Microvessels within the bile duct wall were examined using the CD31 antibody. CD31−positive vessels with a short-axis diameter of <20 µm within the bile duct wall were counted in 5 randomly selected fields at ×40 magnification, and the mean value was calculated. Images were acquired such that they excluded the bile duct lumen from the field of view.20 Histological images were obtained and quantitatively analyzed using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD). All evaluations were performed under the supervision of an expert hepatopathologist (K.H.). Histological and immunohistochemical evaluations were performed in a blinded manner by 2 observers. For quantitative analyses, the mean of the 2 scores was used for analysis.

Blood Biochemistry

At each predefined time point (24, 72, and 168 h after transplantation), recipient rats were euthanized, and blood samples were collected by cardiac puncture. Serum levels of aspartate aminotransferase, alanine aminotransferase, total bilirubin, and alkaline phosphatase were analyzed by SRL Inc. (Tokyo, Japan).

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables are presented as median (interquartile range) and were compared using the Mann-Whitney U test. A 2-sided P value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS software, Version 24 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL).

Artificial intelligence (ChatGPT, OpenAI) was used to improve language clarity and readability; no artificial intelligence tool was used for data collection, analysis, or figure generation.

RESULTS

Donor body weight and graft size were significantly greater in the OD group than in the YD group. Recipient body weight and explanted whole liver weight were comparable between groups. Notably, the GRWR was higher in the OD group than in the YD group (2.81% [2.73%–2.93%] versus 2.37% [2.27%–2.45%]; P < 0.001). The anhepatic time showed a nonsignificant trend toward prolongation (Table S1, SDC, https://links.lww.com/TXD/A883).

All recipient rats (n = 30; 15 rats per group) survived throughout the observation period. Hepatic arterial patency was macroscopically confirmed before euthanasia in all recipients. No bile duct stent obstruction was observed in any of the rats at the time of euthanasia. In the OD group, marked bile duct dilatation on the liver side of the stent was observed in 2 of 5 cases at 168 h after LT.

Review of pretransplant donor liver hematoxylin and eosin sections from all animals (n = 5 per group) did not reveal an apparent between-group difference in macrovesicular or microvesicular steatosis. Representative images are provided in Figure S1 (SDC, https://links.lww.com/TXD/A883).

PBG Detachment Score

Hematoxylin and eosin staining of the EHBD revealed morphological differences between the 2 groups. Before transplantation, no detachment of PBG cells from the basement membrane was observed in either group. In the YD group, the PBG structure remained intact at 24 and 72 h after LT, with minimal detachment observed at 168 h after LT.

The OD group also showed no PBG cell detachment at 24 h post-LT, although some glandular degeneration was observed. Slight detachment was noted at 72 h post-LT (detachment score, 0.03 [0–0.13]). At 168 h post-LT, significant PBG damage was evident, including cell detachment, destruction, and complete loss of glands, with a detachment score significantly higher than that in the YD group (1.67 [1.53–1.87]; P = 0.009; Figure 3A).

Laminin Expression Score

Before LT, laminin expression scores showed no significant difference between the YD (3.26 [3.25–3.45]) and OD (3.03 [2.48–3.13]) groups (P = 0.117). In the YD group, the score significantly decreased at 24 h (1.89 [1.26–2.59]; P = 0.028) and 72 h (1.30 [1.25–1.50]; P = 0.009) post-LT in comparison with the pretransplant levels. A similar decline was observed in the OD group at 24 h (1.94 [0.92–2.60]; P = 0.076) and 72 h (1.03 [0.77–1.11]; P = 0.009) post-LT.

At 168 h post-LT, laminin expression partially recovered in the YD group (2.61 [2.40–3.26]), whereas it remained low in the OD group (1.04 [0.82–2.02]). The score in the OD group at 168 h post-LT was significantly lower than the corresponding score in the YD group (P = 0.047) and the pretransplantation score in the OD group (P = 0.009). Notably, detachment of PBG cells from the basement membrane was observed in regions with reduced laminin expression (Figure 3B).

Stem/Progenitor Cells in PBGs

Before LT, SOX9 expression was detected in nearly all PBG cells in both the YD and OD groups. In the YD group, expression levels remained stable at 24 h (98.7% [97.1%–100%]), 72 h (100% [100%–100%]), and 168 h (94.8% [93.5%–100%]) post-LT.

In contrast, while SOX9 expression in the OD group was preserved at 24 h post-LT (100% [97.0%–100%]), a significant reduction was observed at 72 h (81.7% [70.2%–82.3%]; P = 0.009) and 168 h (60.0% [56.6%–64.6%]; P = 0.009) post-LT in comparison with the pretransplant levels. These values were also significantly lower than those in the YD group at the corresponding time points (P = 0.016 and P = 0.009, respectively; Figure 3C).

PBG Hyperplasia in EHBD

Before LT, PBG area did not differ between groups. After LT, the OD group showed a transient increase in PBG area at 24 h (P = 0.047 versus pre-LT), followed by a decline toward 168 h with evidence of glandular disruption. In contrast, the YD group showed no significant temporal change. No significant intergroup differences were observed at any time point (Figure 4A).

PBG Cell Proliferation in EHBD

The percentage of Ki-67-positive PBG cells increased after LT in both groups, but the OD group showed a more pronounced early increase at 24 h, which was higher than that in the YD group (P = 0.047). Thereafter, Ki-67 positivity declined over time but remained above pre-LT levels in the OD group (Figure 4B).

PBG Mucus Production

PAS scores showed modest changes after LT. Notably, at 168 h, the OD group exhibited a significant decline in PAS score (P = 0.028 versus pre-LT), consistent with PBG destruction, and this value was lower than that in the YD group (P = 0.012; Figure 4C).

Microvascular Density in the EHBD After LT

CD31+ microvessel counts decreased early after LT in both groups. The YD group showed recovery toward 168 h, whereas the OD group remained reduced at 168 h and was still lower than pre-LT (P = 0.047; Figure 4D).

Apoptosis of PBG Cells in the EHBD

cCaspase-3-positive PBG cells were not observed before LT. After LT, apoptotic PBG cells were detected only in the OD group at 168 h, primarily as shed cells in the bile duct lumen (Figure 4E).

Serum Biochemistry

Serum aspartate aminotransferase and alanine aminotransferase peaked at 24 h and declined over time in both groups. Cholestatic markers (total and direct bilirubin and alkaline phosphatase) did not differ significantly between groups at each time point (Table S2, SDC, https://links.lww.com/TXD/A883).

DISCUSSION

To our knowledge, this is the first study to report the effect of donor age on PBG injury after LT in a rat model. We observed clear morphological differences in PBGs between young and aged liver grafts post-LT. In young grafts, PBGs remained largely intact, whereas in aged grafts, PBG cells detached from the basement membrane, and the luminal structure became deformed, disrupted, and ultimately vanished, as shown in Figures 2A and 3A. We refer to this degenerative phenomenon as “ghost PBG.”

PBG cells restore biliary integrity through proliferation and migration following injury.15-17 In both young and aged grafts, the PBG area and cell proliferation increased after LT, which is consistent with the results of previous studies.15,17,22 Together with reduced SOX9 expression and decreased PAS staining, these findings resemble PBG abnormalities reported in patients with ITBL.19 Collectively, these observations suggest that aged donor grafts may have limited capacity to maintain PBG integrity during the early posttransplant period, which may be relevant to the increased susceptibility to biliary injury in older donor livers.

To explore the mechanism underlying PBG cell detachment, we focused on laminins, which are essential basement membrane glycoproteins composed of α, β, and γ chains and play key roles in epithelial adhesion and signaling.24-26 Among various isoforms, laminin-511 (α5, β1, γ1) is the predominant isoform in the liver.27 In our study, laminin-γ1 expression around PBGs decreased after LT in both groups, consistent with previous reports identifying laminin as an early target of IRI in multiple organs.28-30 Importantly, in aged grafts, PBG cell detachment and apoptosis occurred in areas of laminin loss at 168 h, as evidenced by cCaspase-3 expression. This aligns with previous findings in mouse gallbladders, which showed epithelial detachment at laminin-deficient areas.31 Because detachment from the basement membrane can induce apoptosis via anoikis, these results are consistent with the possibility that IRI-mediated laminin degradation may promote PBG cell detachment, contributing to loss of glandular architecture.

Aging itself is associated with diminished laminin levels in various tissues, including the liver.32-34 Qian et al32 showed significantly lower laminin expression during liver injury in aged mice than in young mice. Consistent with this, our study showed comparable laminin levels between groups before LT, but posttransplant recovery of laminin expression occurred only in young grafts. In contrast, aged grafts exhibited persistent laminin loss together with frequent PBG cell detachment and loss of glandular architecture. These findings are consistent with the possibility that impaired laminin restoration is associated with structural PBG injury after LT.

This study had several limitations. First, the present observations were limited to the early posttransplant period and do not demonstrate late-stage biliary complications such as ITBLs. Therefore, the relationship between the observed PBG changes and later biliary complications remains uncertain. Longer-term studies beyond 168 h after transplantation will be necessary to determine whether these early histological changes persist, progress, or resolve over time. Second, the GRWR was significantly higher in the aged-graft group than in the young-graft group. Although the grafts were reduced to the same proportion (60%), the larger absolute graft size in aged donors resulted in a higher GRWR. Larger graft size itself may affect microcirculation, IRI, and subsequent biliary repair. Previous clinical studies have also identified higher donor weight as a predictor of ischemic cholangiopathy, although the underlying mechanisms remain unclear.35 Thus, graft size and donor weight may have acted as confounders, and the observed biliary phenotypes cannot be attributed to donor age alone with complete certainty. In addition, although the present rat LT model included arterial reconstruction, the dependence of the biliary system on arterial blood supply may differ between rats and humans. Therefore, the translational relevance of these findings to human biliary complications should be interpreted with caution. Third, detailed mechanisms of laminin degradation and synthesis could not be clarified because of the small amount of available tissue. In addition, although laminin loss was spatially associated with PBG cell detachment, this finding remained descriptive, and mechanistic or functional analyses to support a causal relationship were not performed. Furthermore, because the present study relied primarily on histopathological and immunohistochemical assessments, and no significant between-group differences were observed in serum biochemical parameters, the functional relevance of these structural changes could not be fully established. Additional multimodal analyses, such as cytokine profiling, ultrastructural evaluation, or transcriptomic studies, will be needed in future investigations. Fourth, the combination of partial hepatectomy and LT in our model may have influenced the biliary environment and regeneration capacity of the PBGs. Although the same 60% graft reduction was applied to both young and aged groups for consistency, the independent effect of hepatic resection on PBG injury could not be assessed in this study and warrants further investigation. Despite these limitations, our study provides descriptive evidence of age-related PBG injury in a rat model of LT and may serve as a foundation for future investigations into the mechanisms of biliary injury after transplantation.

In conclusion, IRI-induced laminin degradation in the basement membrane may promote PBG cell detachment and loss, contributing to progressive loss of PBG architecture. Impaired laminin recovery in aged grafts may be relevant to a higher risk of biliary injury after transplantation.
