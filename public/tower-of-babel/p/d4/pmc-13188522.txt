Association of mid-pregnancy remnant cholesterol levels with adverse pregnancy outcomes in women with gestational diabetes mellitus
Authors: Yufei Fan, Yanfei Lu, Xiaoqing Fan, Yingying Wu, Yuan Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRemnant cholesterol (RC), representing the cholesterol content of triglyceride-rich lipoproteins, has emerged as an essential metabolic risk marker. However, evidence regarding the association between mid-pregnancy RC levels and adverse pregnancy-related outcomes (APO) among women with gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) remains limited.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study included 823 singleton pregnant women diagnosed with GDM who received routine antenatal care at Hangzhou Women’s Hospital between January 2023 and July 2024. Fasting serum lipid profiles were measured at 24–28 gestational weeks. RC was categorized into quartiles (Q1-Q4). The primary outcome was a composite APO, including placental abruption, premature rupture of membranes, oligohydramnios or polyhydramnios, postpartum hemorrhage, shoulder dystocia, preterm birth (< 37 weeks), stillbirth, fetal distress, fetal malformations, and fetal growth restriction. Multivariable logistic regression models were used to evaluate the association between RC quartiles and APO. Restricted cubic spline (RCS), receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve, subgroup analyses, and sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess robustness.ResultsThe overall incidence of APO was 33.9%. After adjustment for age, pre-pregnancy BMI, FPG, 2 h-PG, nulliparous, prior abortion, conceived by IVF, antenatal comorbidities, and laboratory parameters (MO, LY, PLT, ALT), the highest RC quartile was significantly associated with increased risk of APO (adjusted OR = 5.75, 95% CI: 3.60–9.18; P < 0.001). RCS analysis demonstrated a linear dose–response relationship between RC and APO risk (non-linearity P = 0.239). The area under the ROC curve for RC was 0.676, higher than those of conventional lipid parameters. Category-specific analyses showed more consistent associations for fetal and obstetric APO, whereas placental APO showed a weaker association. These associations were generally consistent across subgroups and sensitivity analyses.ConclusionsElevated mid-pregnancy fasting RC levels were independently associated with a higher risk of APO among women with GDM. RC may serve as a clinically relevant lipid-related marker for risk stratification during pregnancy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12884-026-09031-2.Keywords: Remnant cholesterol, Gestational diabetes mellitus, Adverse pregnancy outcomes, Dyslipidemia, Risk factor

Introduction

Gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) is a prevalent metabolic disorder during pregnancy, associated with elevated risks of maternal complications, as well as adverse fetal and neonatal outcomes [1]. Although hyperglycemia has long been considered the primary driver of adverse pregnancy outcomes (APO), mounting evidence indicates that dysregulation of lipid metabolism may also be involved in GDM-related complications, potentially through pathways related to inflammation, oxidative stress, endothelial dysfunction, and altered placental function [2, 3]. Consequently, identifying lipid-related biomarkers that more accurately reflect atherosclerotic lipoprotein burden and metabolic risk may be clinically valuable for early risk stratification and individualized management of women with GDM [4].

Remnant cholesterol (RC) is defined as the cholesterol content of triglyceride-rich lipoproteins, such as very-low-density lipoproteins (VLDL) and intermediate-density lipoproteins (IDL) [5]. RC has been increasingly recognized as a lipid-related marker associated with inflammation and atherogenesis [6]. In non-pregnant populations, elevated RC levels have been associated with cardiovascular events, diabetes, and related complications [7, 8]. Pregnancy is characterized by physiological hyperlipidemia, and women with GDM may exhibit a more pronounced accumulation of triglyceride-rich lipoproteins due to insulin resistance, leading to increased RC levels [9, 10]. However, the evidence regarding the association between mid-pregnancy RC levels and APO in women with GDM remains limited, and the comparative value of RC relative to conventional lipid parameters has not been fully elucidated [11].

The present study aimed to investigate the association between mid-pregnancy fasting RC levels and composite APO among women with GDM, to evaluate potential dose–response relationships, and to compare the discriminatory performance of RC with that of traditional lipid parameters. This will provide evidence for improved risk assessment during pregnancy.

Methods

Study design and participants

This retrospective cohort study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Hangzhou Women’s Hospital, with a waiver of informed consent. We consecutively enrolled pregnant women with GDM who attended routine antenatal care and delivered at Hangzhou Women’s Hospital between January 1, 2023, and July 31, 2024 (Fig. 1).

Flowchart of the study

Inclusion criteria were: (1) singleton pregnancy; (2) diagnosis of GDM based on a 75-g oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) performed at 24–28 gestational weeks according to the International Association of Diabetes and Pregnancy Study Groups (IADPSG) criteria (fasting plasma glucose (FPG) ≥ 5.1 mmol/L, and/or 1-hour plasma glucose (1 h-PG) ≥ 10.0 mmol/L, and/or 2-hour plasma glucose (2 h-PG) ≥ 8.6 mmol/L) [12]; and (3) maternal age > 18 years. Exclusion criteria included: (1) pregestational diabetes or other metabolic diseases; (2) severe hepatic or renal dysfunction; (3) thyroid disease or autoimmune disorders; (4) severe infection; (5) prior history of GDM; (6) recent use of antihypertensive, lipid-lowering, or glucose-lowering medications before the index mid-pregnancy laboratory assessment (prespecified as within 1 month); (7) prior history of APO; and (8) incomplete clinical data.

Data collection

Fasting venous blood samples were collected at 24–28 gestational weeks for lipid measurements, including total cholesterol (TC), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), and triglycerides (TG). Remnant cholesterol was calculated as: RC (mmol/L) = TC − HDL-C − LDL-C. Participants were categorized into quartiles based on RC levels. The primary outcome was a composite APO, defined as the occurrence of at least one of the following events: placental abruption, premature rupture of membranes, oligohydramnios, polyhydramnios, postpartum hemorrhage, shoulder dystocia, preterm birth (< 37 weeks), stillbirth, fetal distress, fetal malformations, and fetal growth restriction.

Demographic and clinical data were extracted from the electronic medical record system. These variables included maternal age, height, pre-pregnancy weight, nulliparous, history of abortion, conception by in vitro fertilization (IVF), and gestational age at delivery. Information on antenatal comorbidities (uterine scar, chronic hypertension, chronic lung disease) and pregnancy complications (insulin therapy, gestational hypertension, intrahepatic cholestasis of pregnancy, gestational hyperthyroidism, gestational hypothyroidism, post-term pregnancy, and preeclampsia) was also collected. In addition, laboratory parameters measured concurrently with lipid sampling were obtained, including OGTT values (FPG, 1 h-PG, and 2 h PG), white blood cell count (WBC), neutrophils (NE), monocytes (MO), lymphocytes (LY), red blood cell count (RBC), hemoglobin (Hb), red cell distribution width (RDW), platelet count (PLT), platelet distribution width (PDW), albumin (ALB), albumin-to-globulin ratio (A/G ratio), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), and creatinine (Cr).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.4.3). Missing data < 10% were handled using multiple imputation. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± SD (for normally distributed variables) or median (interquartile range, for non- normally distributed variables), and compared using t-tests or Wilcoxon rank-sum tests. Categorical variables were expressed as frequency (percentages) and compared using chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests. Variance inflation factors (VIF) > 5 were considered indicators of multicollinearity. RC was divided into quartile groups (Q1–Q4), with Q1 serving as the reference group. Multivariable logistic regression models were constructed: Model 1 (unadjusted); Model 2 (adjusted for age and pre-pregnancy BMI); and Model 3 (further adjusted for nulliparous, prior abortion, conceived by IVF, antenatal comorbidities, FPG, 2 h-PG, MO, LY, PLT, and ALT). Given the clinical heterogeneity of composite APO, we further performed category-specific analyses by grouping outcomes into placental APO, fetal APO, and obstetric APO. Additional multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed using the same covariate-adjustment framework as in the primary model, to explore whether the association between RC and APO differed across outcome domains. Trend tests were conducted to assess dose–response relationships across RC quartiles. ROC curve analysis was performed to evaluate and compare the predictive performance of RC and conventional lipid parameters (TC, TG, HDL-C, LDL-C) for APO. Subgroup analyses were conducted according to age, pre-pregnancy BMI, nulliparous, prior abortion, mode of delivery, and antenatal comorbidities, with interaction terms tested. Sensitivity analyses were performed by sequentially excluding women aged ≥ 35 years, overweight, nulliparous, those with abortion history, those conceived by IVF, and those with antenatal comorbidities. Restricted cubic spline (RCS) models were fitted within the fully adjusted framework to assess dose–response relationships and test for non-linearity. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 823 women with GDM were included in the analysis. As shown in Table 1, no statistically differences were identified across RC quartiles in terms of age, gestational age, prior abortion, pre-pregnancy BMI, or pregnancy complications. However, compared with the lowest RC quartile (Q1), the highest quartile (Q4) had a higher proportion of nulliparous women (72.3% vs. 63.6%, P = 0.003) and a lower prevalence of antenatal comorbidities (8.3% vs. 12.6%, P = 0.001). In addition, MO, LY, PLT, and ALT differed across RC quartiles (all P < 0.05), while other hematological and biochemical indices did not differ significantly between groups. The overall incidence of composite APO was 33.9%, increasing significantly across RC quartiles (Q1–Q4: 17.0%, 29.2%, 33.2%, and 56.3%, respectively; P < 0.001).

Association between RC and APO

Multivariable logistic regression analyses (Table 2) demonstrated a significant positive association between RC levels and APO risk. After adjustment for age, Pre-pregnancy BMI, FPG, 2 h-PG, Nulliparous, Prior abortion, Conceived by IVF, Antenatal comorbidities, and laboratory parameters (MO, LY, PLT, ALT), higher RC quartiles were independently associated with an increased risk of APO. In particular, women in the highest RC quartile exhibited a markedly elevated risk compared with those in the lowest quartile, with a significant dose–response trend observed across increasing RC categories (P < 0.001). Furthermore, the incidences of fetal APO and obstetric APO increased significantly across RC quartiles (P < 0.05), whereas placental APO showed a weaker between-group difference (P = 0.056) (Table S1). In multivariable logistic regression analyses, higher RC quartiles were independently associated with fetal APO and obstetric APO (P < 0.05), while the association with placental APO was weaker and reached statistical significance only in Q4 (P = 0.035) (Table S2).

Predictive performance of RC for APO

ROC curve analysis (Fig. 2) indicated that RC had modest discriminatory performance for APO, with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.676 (95% CI: 0.640–0.711). Compared with conventional lipid parameters, RC yielded a higher AUC than TC, TG, and LDL-C.

Dose–response relationship assessed by RCS

RCS analysis (Fig. 3) demonstrated a linear increasing relationship between RC levels and APO risk within the fully adjusted model (adjusted for age, pre-pregnancy BMI, FPG, 2 h-PG, nulliparous, prior abortion, conceived by IVF, antenatal comorbidities, MO, LY, PLT, ALT), with no significant evidence of non-linearity (non-linearity P = 0.239).

Subgroup analysis

Subgroup analyses (Fig. 4) indicated that the positive association between RC and APO was generally consistent across subgroups stratified by age, pre-pregnancy BMI, nulliparous, prior abortion, mode of delivery, and pregnancy complication. No significant interactions were observed (all P for interaction > 0.05).

Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analyses (Table 3) showed that the strong association between the highest RC quartile (Q4) and APO remained stable after sequential exclusion of age ≥ 35, pre-pregnancy BMI > 23.9, nulliparous, prior abortion, conceived by IVF, and antenatal comorbidities (adjusted OR range: 3.21–6.32; all P <0.05), supporting the robustness of the primary findings.

Discussion

In this retrospective cohort study of women with GDM, we systematically investigated the association between mid-pregnancy RC levels and the risk of APO. Our findings demonstrate that elevated RC levels are independently associated with a higher risk of composite APO among women with GDM, with a clear linear dose-response relationship. In addition, RC showed modest discriminatory performance for APO compared with conventional lipid parameters, such as TC, TG, and LDL-C.

Previous investigations examining lipid metabolism and pregnancy outcomes in women with GDM have mainly focused on TG or TC, and the reported associations have been inconsistent [9, 13, 14]. While some studies have suggested that elevated triglyceride levels during pregnancy are associated with increased risks of preterm birth, fetal growth restriction, or hypertensive disorders of pregnancy [14–16], others have reported attenuation or loss of significance after adjustment for glycemic indices and pre-pregnancy BMI [9, 14, 17]. Such discrepancies may be partly explained by the limited ability of traditional lipid measures to capture the cholesterol burden carried by TG-rich lipoprotein remnants [18]. As an integrated marker of remnant lipoprotein cholesterol, RC may better reflect the atherogenic and metabolically adverse lipid profile characteristic of insulin-resistant states [10]. Our study demonstrates a stable, gradient-like association between RC and APO risk.

In the current study, the association between RC and APO remained significant after comprehensive adjustment for OGTT-derived glucose measures, indicating that RC is not merely a concomitant phenomenon of hyperglycemia but may represent an independent metabolic risk marker [19]. Notably, the excess risk of APO was most pronounced in women with the highest RC levels, suggesting that higher RC levels may identify a subgroup of women with GDM at particularly increased risk of APO. This observation provides a potential explanation for the inconsistent findings in prior studies using conventional lipid parameters and supports the relevance of focusing on RC in this population.

Given the clinical heterogeneity of the composite APO endpoint, we further examined category-specific outcomes. The findings showed that the association between RC and APO was not uniform across different outcome domains. Higher RC quartiles were more consistently associated with fetal APO and obstetric APO, whereas the association with placental APO was weaker. This pattern suggests that the relationship between RC and APO is unlikely to be explained by a single common pathway. Rather, RC may be better interpreted as an integrated marker of adverse metabolic status in women with GDM, while the specific biological pathways linking RC to individual APO domains remain uncertain.

Additionally, RC showed better discriminatory performance for APO than conventional lipid parameters. This finding is consistent with evidence from non-pregnant populations, where RC is more strongly associated with cardiometabolic outcomes than LDL-C or TG alone [7, 20, 21]. In contrast to LDL-C, which primarily reflects cholesterol mass, RC reflects the cholesterol content of TG-rich lipoproteins [22]. These lipoproteins have been linked to inflammation and endothelial dysfunction, and such mechanisms may contribute differently across APO domains [10, 22–24]. Moreover, placental tissue is susceptible to changes in maternal lipid metabolism [25]. Therefore, RC may be better interpreted as an integrated marker of adverse metabolic disturbance rather than a direct indicator of any single biological pathway. Because this was an observational study, our findings cannot determine whether RC is a causal mediator or a proximal biological marker. Further mechanistic and prospective studies are needed to clarify the pathways linking RC to individual APO components.

Subgroup analyses indicated that the association between RC and APO risk was generally consistent across strata defined by age, pre-pregnancy BMI, nulliparous, prior abortion, mode of delivery, and pregnancy complication, with no significant interactions observed. These findings suggest that the association between RC and APO was not confined to a single clinically defined subgroup within this cohort. Similar patterns have been reported in previous studies of women with GDM [19], although the generalizability of these findings still requires confirmation in broader prospective populations.

Sensitivity analyses further supported the stability of the primary results after exclusion of women with potential sources of confounding, including age ≥ 35, pre-pregnancy BMI > 23.9, nulliparous, prior abortion, conceived by IVF, and antenatal comorbidities. After excluding nulliparous women, the associations in the moderate RC categories were attenuated, whereas the highest RC category remained significantly associated with APO. This attenuation may partly reflect reduced sample size and statistical power after restricting the cohort, particularly because nulliparous women constituted a substantial proportion of the study population and were more frequent in the highest RC quartile. Overall, these results support the robustness of the association between elevated RC and APO risk, while suggesting that the estimates for moderate RC categories are less stable under reduced sample size.

This study has several limitations. First, as a retrospective study, residual confounding from unmeasured factors cannot be excluded despite multivariable adjustment. Second, serum cholesterol was measured only once during mid-pregnancy, precluding assessment of longitudinal lipid changes throughout pregnancy. Finally, although the use of a composite APO improved statistical efficiency, it may have obscured heterogeneity across individual outcomes. Therefore, our findings should be interpreted cautiously and confirmed in larger, multicenter prospective studies.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the present study demonstrates that elevated mid-pregnancy remnant cholesterol levels are independently associated with an increased risk of adverse pregnancy-related outcomes among women with GDM, with a clear linear dose–response relationship. RC showed modest discriminatory performance and had a higher AUC than conventional lipid parameters, and the observed associations were consistent across subgroups and sensitivity analyses. In addition to glycemic control, it is imperative to closely monitor lipid metabolic abnormalities, particularly remnant cholesterol, to enhance our understanding of risk assessment and management in women with GDM.
