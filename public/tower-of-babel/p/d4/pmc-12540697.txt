Teamwork and job satisfaction among nurses in pediatric intensive care unit
Authors: Sabeti Fahimeh, Rostampur Niusha, Faghir-Ganji Monireh, Aliakbari Mahbobeh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractTeamwork in the pediatric intensive care unit (PICU) is crucial due to the high-pressure environment, which requires swift decisions, multiple concurrent care interventions, and effective real-time communication. Job satisfaction is a multifaceted concept influenced by various factors, and emerging evidence highlights a link between teamwork and job satisfaction. This study aimed to examine teamwork, job satisfaction, and their relationship among PICU nurses. A descriptive cross-sectional design was employed. The sample consisted of 200 nurses from PICUs, selected through census sampling. Inclusion criteria comprised voluntary participation and a minimum of 6 months of PICU experience. Data were collected using a demographic-occupational characteristics questionnaire, the Nursing Teamwork Scale (NTS), and the Minnesota Job Satisfaction Questionnaire (MSQ-short form). Analyses were conducted in SPSS-26 using independent t-tests, ANOVA, Pearson’s correlation, and multiple linear regression. Results revealed a mean teamwork score of 107.13 (SD = 19.20) and a job satisfaction score of 61.74 (SD = 10.00). The highest teamwork score was observed for the “shared mental model”, while the lowest was for “team orientation”. A significant positive correlation was found between overall teamwork (and all its subscales) and job satisfaction (r = 0.402–0.485, p < 0.001). The results of a multiple linear regression model showed that teamwork emerged as the only statistically significant predictor of job satisfaction (B = 0.190, p < 0.001). Additionally, shift type, education, and interest in the nursing profession were significant predictors of teamwork. This study emphasizes teamwork’s key role in PICU nurses’ job satisfaction. Healthcare policymakers should prioritize teamwork development to boost clinical and occupational outcomes.Keywords: Teamwork, Job satisfaction, Pediatric intensive care unit (PICU), NurseSubject terms: Paediatric research, Paediatric research

Introduction

In recent decades, profound transformations in healthcare systems, increasing complexity of therapies, and diverse professional roles have emphasized the vital importance of teamwork. Nurses, as core team members, play a crucial role in advancing organizational goals and improving patient outcomes. In high-pressure environments such as the Pediatric Intensive Care Unit (PICU), team synergy is critical for ensuring quality, safety, and efficiency of care1,2.Teamwork in nursing encompasses interactive processes among team members, mutual trust, effective communication, task coordination, distributive leadership, and group support. Studies indicate that nursing teams with high functional cohesion are more successful in minimizing clinical errors, enhancing patient satisfaction, and fostering positive work environments2,3.Teamwork plays a vital role in ensuring patient safety and is acknowledged as a cornerstone of a healthy nursing work environment4,5.

PICUs are specialized hospital units that care for children in critical and unstable conditions. In these units, team members must make quick decisions, work together on treatments, and share information right away, since poor coordination can have serious consequences1. Nurses in PICUs often face heavy workloads, complex cases, and ongoing stress, which increases their risk of burnout5. Both technical and emotional support are important. Research shows that when nursing teams work well together, patients have better outcomes, such as shorter hospital stays, improved safety, and fewer errors6.

Job satisfaction shows how content people are with their jobs. It usually means how well job expectations match what the workplace is actually like7,8. In healthcare, job satisfaction is an important sign of a stable workforce. It is linked to better staff retention, less turnover, improved mental health, higher quality of care, and better patient results3. In nursing, researchers have looked at job satisfaction as both a result and a cause of how well organizations perform. Nurses who feel appreciated, supported by their teams, and given chances to grow professionally are more likely to stay in their jobs and take an active role in patient care. Job satisfaction is not the same everywhere3,9. For instance, Aiken et al. (2011) found that nurses in the United States had the highest dissatisfaction (41%), while those in Germany had the lowest (17%). Overall, satisfaction was highest in Germany, followed by the United States, New Zealand, Canada, South Korea, the United Kingdom, China, and Japan10.In Iran, Akbari et al. (2020) found that 58% of female nurses and 47% of male nurses had moderate job satisfaction11.

Nurse shortages, challenging work conditions, low pay, irregular shifts, long hours, and job stress are major human resource issues in Iran’s healthcare system12,13, often leading to dissatisfaction among providers. While teamwork and job satisfaction are known to improve quality of care, there is limited research on these topics in specialized, high-stress settings like pediatric intensive care. In particular, little is known about how team interactions relate to job satisfaction for nurses in Iran’s PICUs. Studying these factors, with attention to Iran’s unique cultural and organizational context, can help shape better management policies to support teamwork, boost job satisfaction, and improve care quality. This study aims to assess teamwork and job satisfaction among PICU nurses and explore the relationship between them.

Literature review

Research on teamwork and job satisfaction in PICU is limited, but existing studies demonstrated a positive correlation between the quality of team interactions and nurses’ job satisfaction. When nurses work in environments characterized by participation, trust, and effective collaboration, they report greater feelings of belonging, security, and job satisfaction5. The results of4 study revealed that when accounting for unit type, role, work experience, and staffing levels, those who reported better teamwork had significantly higher job satisfaction. So, facilitating effective teamwork should be a priority for every nurse manager4. The study by Akyüz et al. (2022) revealed a significant positive correlation between nurses’ attitudes toward teamwork and their job satisfaction2. The findings from a mixed-methods exploratory study by Goh et al.,2020 among nursing staff showed nurses who were dissatisfied with their jobs had significantly lower teamwork scores compared to satisfied nurses14. Trybou et al. (2015) examined the impact of nurse team on intention to leave and job satisfaction. Their results demonstrated that nursing staff experiencing higher-quality team-member exchange (TMX) reported greater job satisfaction, stronger affective organizational commitment, and lower intention to leave. The quality of relationships between nursing staff and their team members plays a pivotal role in nurse retention15. The results of Kalisch et al. (2010) demonstrate that in acute care patient units nursing teams, higher levels of teamwork and perceived adequate staffing lead to greater job satisfaction with both current position and employment situation16. Organizational psychology research further suggests that positive team dynamics can mediate the relationship between occupational stress and job satisfaction. A significant inverse relationship was observed between occupational stress levels and job satisfaction, mediated by positive team climate. Specifically, reduced work-related stress coupled with improved team dynamics directly enhanced job satisfaction. These results confirm teamwork’s dual mediating role in both stress reduction and satisfaction enhancement17. Ultimately, Lu et al. (2019), identified two key components effective teamwork and nurse’s job satisfaction—as critical factors in workforce retention and patient safety18. Most research so far has looked at how important teamwork and job satisfaction are, especially in general hospital wards or adult settings. But there’s not much work done specifically on PICU. Considering how crucial these units are for taking care of seriously ill children, and how different cultures and healthcare systems vary around the world, it’s clear that this study is both important and necessary.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

This study used a cross-sectional descriptive design. The ethics committee of Iran University of Medical Sciences approved the research and confirmed that all research was performed in accordance with relevant guidelines/regulations. We used census sampling, so all nurses working in pediatric intensive care units at Tehran’s teaching hospitals who met the inclusion criteria were selected. Nurses could participate if they volunteered and had at least six months of experience in the PICU. We excluded anyone who did not complete the questionnaires. Based on a 95% confidence level, a margin of error of 0.1 points on a 1 to 5 scale, and an assumed standard deviation of 0.7 for teamwork scores19. We calculated that at least 200 nurses were needed for the sample.

.

Measurements

The data collection tools included a demographic-occupational questionnaire, Nursing Teamwork Survey (NTS), and the Minnesota Satisfaction Questionnaire-Short Form (MSQ-SF). The demographic-occupational questionnaire asked questions about age, gender, education, marital status, employment status, overall work experience, work experience in PICU units, shift type, patient number per shift, monthly work hours, nursing profession interest and PICU work interest. NTS was specifically developed in 2010 by Kalisch et al. for nurses working in inpatient units. It consists of 33 items and five subscales: Mutual trust (7 items: 4, 23, 24, 25, 31, 32, 33), Team orientation (9 items: 5, 11, 13, 15, 16, 17, 18, 22, 26), Backup (6 items: 3, 9, 19, 20, 28, 30), Shared mental model (7 items: 1, 7, 10, 12, 14, 21, 29) and Team leadership (4 items: 2, 6, 8, 27). Items were scored on a 5-point Likert scale: 5(Always), 4(75% of the time), 3(50% of the time), 2(25% of the time), 1(rarely). The total score ranges from 33 to 165. A higher score indicates better teamwork. The NTS exhibited acceptable psychometric properties16. The reliability and validity of the NTS were assessed through psychometric testing by Najafi Ghezeljeh et al. (2021) in Iran. The translation-back translation method of the tool was conducted using a standardized protocol. Content validity of questionnaire was approved by an expert panel of faculty members of School of Nursing and Midwifery at Iran University of Medical Sciences. The instrument demonstrated excellent reliability, with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.9519. In the present study, the Cronbach’s alpha of the scale was calculated at 0.90. The MSQ developed by Weiss et al. in 1967. It consists of both long and short forms. In this study, the short form was employed, comprising 20 items rated on a 5-point Likert scale: 1(Very dissatisfied), 2(Dissatisfied), 3(Neutral) 4(Satisfied)), 5(Very satisfied). The total score ranges from 20 to 100, with higher scores indicating greater job satisfaction. The internal consistency reliability coefficient (Cronbach’s alpha) of the MSQ-SF was reported by Weiss et al. (1967) in various studies to range between 0.84 and 0.9120. In Iran, the psychometric properties of the instrument were evaluated by Hajibabaee et al. (2016). Reliability was reported as good, with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.8221. In the present study, the Cronbach’s alpha of the scale was calculated at 0.89.

Procedure

Following approval of the study by the university’s ethics committee, the researcher, equipped with an official introduction letter, visited pediatric teaching hospitals with intensive care units. After meeting with the head nurses and nursing staff of the wards, the researcher explained the objectives of the study and invited nurses to participate based on the inclusion criteria. In this process, nurses who were willing to participate were asked to sign a written informed consent form. Subsequently, the researcher visited the wards during different shifts and distributed the questionnaires among the participants. The participants were instructed to complete the questionnaires anonymously, thoroughly, and at their convenience (e.g., during break time), and to submit them in an envelope placed with the ward secretary. This procedure of distributing and collecting questionnaires was repeated across multiple shifts and time points to ensure comprehensive data collection. The average time required to complete each questionnaire was 5–10 min. Data was collected from March 2023 to January 2024.

Ethical considerations

The ethics committee of Iran University of Medical Sciences approved this study (Ethical code: IR.IUMS.REC.1401.744). We assured all participating nurses of data confidentiality. Therefore, the nurses completed all questionnaires anonymously and voluntarily joined the study, retaining the right to withdraw at any time. Every participant signed an informed consent form.

Data analysis

We performed the data analysis using SPSS software (version 26. Descriptive statistics, including frequency, mean, and standard deviation, were applied. Additionally, inferential statistical methods such as independent t-tests, Pearson’s correlation coefficient, analysis of variance (ANOVA). Linear regression was also used to control for the effects of confounding variables. The significance level was set at 0.05.

Results

Participant characteristics

The majority of participants (86.5%) were female, most (49%) were under 30 years old, more than half (55%) were married, and 82% held a bachelor’s degree in nursing. Most nurses (87%) worked rotating shifts. The majority of nurses (41%) had less than 5 years of total work experience, and similarly, 63.5% had fewer than 5 years of experience working in the PICU. Permanent employment was the most common (42%), and 52.5% reported caring for 3 patients per shift. Additionally, 54% worked fewer than 200 h per month. Most nurses reported moderate interest in nursing (40%), as well as in working in the PICU (34.5%), (Table 1).

Item analysis revealed that the highest satisfaction was reported for Item 9: " The chance to do things for other people” (M = 3.95, SD = 0.58). Correlation of characteristics of participants with nursing teamwork and job satisfaction

Teamwork showed statistically significant negative correlations with age (r = -0.146, p < 0.039) and education (r=-0.256, p < 0.001) indicating that teamwork scores decreased with advancing age (younger nurses demonstrated better teamwork than old nurses), and higher educational attainment (BSN nurses demonstrated better teamwork than MSN nurses). Significant correlations were observed between teamwork and other work-related factors such as shift type, nursing profession interest and PICU work interest. Nurses working rotating shifts reported significantly higher teamwork scores than fixed-shift nurses (t = 3.057, p < 0.003). Teamwork showed statistically significant correlations with nursing profession interest (F = 5.426, p < 0.001). Tukey’s post hoc comparisons revealed that nurses with “very high” nursing interest showed superior teamwork compared to those with “very low” interest (Mean Difference = 16.51, p < 0.001) and those with “very high” interest in nursing (vs. “low” interest) reported better teamwork (Mean Difference = 29.33, p < 0.036). Teamwork showed statistically significant correlations with PICU work interest (F = 3.541, p < 0.008). Tukey’s post hoc comparisons revealed that nurses with ‘very high’ PICU work interest showed significantly better teamwork than those with ‘very low’ interest (Mean Difference = 20.37, p < 0.073) and ‘low’ interest (Mean Difference = 13.95, p < 0.038). Job satisfaction showed a statistically significant negative correlation with age (r= -0.227, p < 0.001), education (r= -0.18, p = 0.007), total work experience (r= -0.21, p < 0.003) and PICU work experience (r = − 0.158, p < 0.025), indicating that as age and education increases, job satisfaction decreases, and that as work experience increased, job satisfaction decreased. Job satisfaction was also significantly associated with employment status (F = 6.179, p < 0.001). Tukey’s post hoc comparisons revealed that job satisfaction was higher among mandatory nurses compared to those with contractual (Mean Difference = 8.95, p < 0.001), temporary (Mean Difference = 5.28, p < 0.048), and permanent positions (Mean Difference = 6.14, p < 0.03) (Table 1).

Predictive variables of teamwork and job satisfaction

As seen in Table 5, a multiple linear regression model was employed to identify predictors of teamwork, incorporating the following independent variables: age, education, shift type, nursing profession interest and PICU work interest. Results showed each unit increase in educational attainment (from BSN to MSN) predicted a significant 9.598-point decrease in teamwork scores (B =-9.598, p < 0.004). Also, nurses working rotating shifts demonstrated 10.814-point higher teamwork scores than fixed-shift counterparts (B = 10.814, p < 0.004) and each unit increase in nursing profession interest corresponded to a 4.830-point increase in teamwork (B = 4.830, p < 0.007). To identify predictors of job satisfaction, a multiple linear regression model was designed that included the following independent variables: teamwork, PICU work interest, nursing profession interest, employment status, PICU work experience, total nursing experience, education and age. The dependent variable was job satisfaction. Result of a multiple linear regression model showed teamwork emerged as the only statistically significant predictor of job satisfaction (B = 0.190, p < 0.001). Every 1-unit increase in teamwork scores, job satisfaction increased by 0.190 points, holding other variables constant.

Discussion

In the PICU, teamwork plays a critical role. In this setting, clinical healthcare teams are responsible for caring for highly vulnerable patients, where even minor errors in care delivery may lead to serious consequences1,22. The aim of the present study was to evaluate teamwork and job satisfaction among nurses working in PICUs. The findings revealed that the overall teamwork score among PICU nurses was at a moderate-to-high level. The mean subscale scores for teamwork, in descending order, were: Shared Mental Model (highest), Team Leadership, Backup, Mutual Trust, and Team Orientation (lowest). The overall teamwork score was lower than those reported in comparable studies by Mousazadeh et al. (2018), Goh et al. (2020), and Bragadóttir et al. (2023)4,14,16. Factors such as culture, teamwork training, and environmental conditions may contribute to this difference in teamwork scores between the present study and previous research. The healthcare system in Iran is traditionally hierarchical, with a high respect for authority and seniority. While this structure may facilitate hierarchical leadership, it may also reduce open communication and hinder the active participation of younger staff in decision-making. At the same time, Iran’s collectivist cultural values ​​emphasize group coordination and shared responsibility, which may foster dimensions such as a shared mental model but discourage open conflict, disagreement, or supportive behaviors that are also important for effective teamwork. Together, these cultural dynamics provide the context for the mixed pattern of teamwork scores observed in our study. The result of mean subscale scores for teamwork is consistent with previous studies, as Kohanová et al. (2024), Goh et al. (2020), Costello et al. (2021), and Taskiran et al. (2021) reported Shared Mental Model as the highest-scoring and Team Orientation as the lowest-scoring dimension14,23–25. These findings suggest that, while nurses possess a shared understanding of processes and responsibilities, the areas of team cohesion and synergistic collaboration remain in need of further development and reinforcement. Our findings are aligned with the study by Najafi Ghezeljeh et al. (2021), highlighted that some aspects of teamwork most notably Backup and Team Leadership remain underdeveloped within Iranian nursing settings, pointing to the need for targeted interventions in these domains26. Concerning job satisfaction, our study corroborates earlier Iranian research11–13, all of which documented a moderate overall level of satisfaction among nurses. One of the sources of job dissatisfaction was the item “My pay and the amount of work I do”, which revealed a marked misalignment between the scope of responsibilities and the level of remuneration. This imbalance is not only detrimental to nurses’ psychological health, but also threatens workforce retention and stability3,27. By contrast, the highest satisfaction was associated with the item “The chance to do things for other people”, underscoring the continuing influence of intrinsic and altruistic values in sustaining nursing practice4,28. From a practical perspective, interventions such as spiritual intelligence training appear promising in enhancing intrinsic motivation, strengthening organizational commitment, and ultimately improving job satisfaction28.

Correlation analysis revealed a significant positive association between teamwork and job satisfaction, with the strongest correlation observed in the Mutual Trust subscale. Regression analysis further indicated that teamwork was the only significant predictor of job satisfaction, a finding consistent with previous studies such as Kalisch et al. (2010) and Bragadóttir et al. (2023)4,16.This association can be explained through organizational theories. According to the Job Characteristics Model (Hackman & Oldham, 1976), teamwork provides social enrichment that fulfills psychological needs, while the Workplace Social Exchange Theory (Chiaburu & Harrison, 2008) highlights how trust-based interactions enhance psychological safety and motivation29,30. From a managerial perspective, investing in teamwork development emerges as an effective strategy to enhance job satisfaction, reduce turnover2,4,14, and help address the global nursing shortage18,31. Studies conducted during the COVID-19 pandemic have highlighted the critical role of teamwork in enhancing nurses’ job satisfaction6,32,33. Evidence shows that in high-risk settings such as PICU, collaboration and coordination among team members not only reduce stress and clinical errors but also strengthen nurses’ sense of professional valueerrors4,24. Overall, organizational support for teamwork should be recognized as a key strategy for retaining the nursing workforce, reducing burnout, and improving job satisfaction3. Although a positive association between teamwork and job satisfaction has been widely reported in previous studies4,16, the present study adds value by examining this relationship within the specific cultural and organizational context of Iran. Pediatric intensive care units in Iran face a persistent nursing shortage31, demanding working conditions with long hours12, and high levels of occupational stress13. Furthermore, the lack of structured teamwork training26and hierarchical management styles, may undermine certain aspects of teamwork, such as team orientation. Our findings indicate that environmental and cultural factors in Iran significantly impact teamwork, job satisfaction within PICUs, and the relationship between them.

The results of the multivariate linear regression analysis revealed that work shift type, education, and nursing profession interest were significant positive predictors of teamwork. Nurses working rotating shifts demonstrated better teamwork, likely because they had more opportunities to interact with colleagues across different shifts. In such situations, team dependency and information exchange are enhanced, which leads to greater team cohesion. This finding aligns with Dinç & Yıldız (2023), who emphasized the importance of shift dynamics in strengthening nurses’ interpersonal and teamwork skills22. Nurses with a strong interest in their profession exhibited higher levels of teamwork and reported greater job satisfaction. This result is consistent with motivational theories such as Herzberg’s Two-Factor Theory, where intrinsic motivation (e.g., passion for the job) directly influences job performance and satisfaction28. The study showed nurses with higher education levels reported lower teamwork engagement. The results also indicated that teamwork and job satisfaction declined with age. Although the correlation between teamwork and job satisfaction and variables such as age and education are statistically significant, but this correlation is weak, which limits their practical significance and should be interpreted with caution. In the Iranian healthcare system, nurses with higher education (e.g., a master’s degree versus a bachelor’s degree) are sometimes assigned to administrative roles, which reduced flexibility and opportunities for direct interaction and clinical collaboration. Additionally, hierarchical structures and the underutilization of advanced skills can lead to decreased motivation and a sense of role incongruence. These factors limit the autonomy and opportunities for collaboration among highly educated staff, ultimately resulting in reduced participation in teamwork activities and job dissatisfaction. However, the studies are few and these results highlight the need for future research. The result of some studies contradicts the present findings, indicating that nurses with higher education levels placed greater importance on teamwork and achieved higher teamwork scores24,34.

Also, our study showed that younger nurses tended to score higher in teamwork, likely due to their greater flexibility and willingness to collaborate. In contrast, older nurses exhibited lower team cohesion, possibly due to burnout, unmet expectations, or diminished motivation. The studies by Akyüz et al. (2022) and Kohanová et al. (2024) suggest that generational diversity within teams can have both strengthening and weakening effects on performance2,24.While different age groups bring diverse perspectives, knowledge, and skills potentially fostering creativity, innovation, and trust, they may also differ in work values, communication styles, and attitudes, leading to misunderstandings, conflicts, and reduced team effectiveness5,35. Correlation analysis further revealed that interest in the nursing profession and PICU work was positively correlated with teamwork and job satisfaction. This suggests that higher interest may lead to increased teamwork and satisfaction. Due to the critical nature of the PICU, this environment demands high levels of commitment and passion, potentially reinforcing the positive effects of initial interest. Nurses who make an informed and motivated choice to work in this setting tend to demonstrate more active and positive team engagement and report higher job satisfaction, possibly because their motivation aligns with the demands of the environment. These findings are consistent with previous studies18,22,36, which also observed greater job satisfaction among pediatric nurses who had willingly chosen their profession. Notably, job satisfaction was higher among nurses with less experience in the PICU and in the nursing profession overall. One possible explanation is that, in the context of nursing shortages and retention concerns, nurse managers in Iran may provide more support and autonomy to less experienced nurses, which can positively influence their job satisfaction. A systematic review by Lu et al. (2019) also suggested that high job satisfaction in novice nurses may arise from their energy, optimism, and enthusiasm, which often diminish over time as workplace challenges accumulate18.

Limitations

This study has limitations. First, this study was conducted with a small sample size and only in teaching hospitals, which limits the generalizability of the results. Also, the prolonged data collection period is another limitation, as the study results may have been influenced by seasonal, temporal factors, or changes in hospital policies. Additionally, self-reported questionnaires were used in this study, which may have introduced bias in the responses.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that effective teamwork is a vital foundation for enhancing job satisfaction among nurses in the pediatric intensive care unit. Beyond confirming previous evidence, the findings also highlight the influence of Iran’s cultural and organizational context. The healthcare systems need to strengthen organizational structures and supportive culture, for example, emphasizing transformational leadership styles and creating an environment that fosters both teamwork and professional development of nurses. This comprehensive approach can lead to increased job satisfaction and improved quality of care. Future interventions should prioritize structured teamwork training and the adoption of transformational leadership styles, which can inspire, motivate, and empower nurses while addressing cultural and organizational challenges in Iran. It is recommended that future studies be conducted in a greater number of hospitals, including private, non-teaching and teaching hospitals, and with a shorter data collection period. Additionally, other factors related to teamwork and job satisfaction, such as seasonal effects, cultural factors, nurses’ stress levels, nurse-to-patient ratio, and hospital reward and punishment policies, should be clearly examined. It is also recommended that future studies be conducted with larger samples and different research designs to better determine the practical applicability of the weaker correlation results.
