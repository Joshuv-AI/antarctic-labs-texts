Atrial Fibrillation Beyond the Atrium
Authors: James Cranley
Published: 2026

Atrial fibrillation (AF) is traditionally framed as an atrial disease, with ventricular dysfunction usually understood to arise in the setting of poor rate control or unchecked atrial enlargement. That view sits uneasily with epidemiological data showing that sudden cardiac death (SCD) is a major mode of cardiovascular death in AF, and with the clinical observation that some patients develop ventricular systolic dysfunction despite apparently adequate rate control. What has been missing is a convincing mechanistic link between AF and ventricular electrical instability. In this issue of JACC: Basic to Translational Science, Spangler et al1 shed light on that link.2

Using human left ventricular cardiomyocytes from patients undergoing aortic valve replacement, the authors show that rate-controlled AF is associated with more delayed afterdepolarizations and spontaneous calcium waves—canonical triggers of ventricular arrhythmia. They then ask a more fundamental question: is irregularity itself sufficient? In a reductionist murine model, irregular pacing at an identical mean rate recapitulates the phenotype. This is accompanied by increased oxidative activation of CaMKII, phosphorylation of RyR2 and phospholamban, reduced SERCA2a, and up-regulated NCX expression and function. Pharmacological inhibition of NCX and genetic ablation of CaMKII oxidation both markedly attenuate the phenotype. The implication is clear at the cellular level: irregular ventricular activation can itself be proarrhythmic.1

These findings extend a broader conceptual shift. AF is increasingly recognized not merely as an atrial disorder with downstream hemodynamic consequences, but as a condition that can directly remodel the ventricle. Prior work by these investigators has shown impaired excitation–contraction coupling in the human ventricle during AF and, in atrial myocardium, Ca2+-dependent triggered activity linked to sarcoplasmic reticulum leak and increased NCX function. The present study carries that narrative from ventricular contractile dysfunction to ventricular arrhythmogenic triggering. In doing so, it offers a plausible cellular substrate for the epidemiological association between AF and SCD.2, 3, 4

The strengths of the study are considerable. First, the use of primary human ventricular cardiomyocytes grounds the observations in clinical reality rather than model systems alone. Second, the focus on rate-controlled AF with preserved ejection fraction helps isolate AF-related effects from overt tachycardiomyopathy. Third, the mechanistic dissection is multifaceted and cohesive: electrophysiology, calcium imaging, protein biology, pharmacology, and genetics converge on a coherent CaMKII-NCX axis. The oxidation-resistant CaMKII knock-in model is particularly persuasive, moving the argument from association toward causation.

The findings must nevertheless be interpreted within the study's limitations. The human data are observational, derived from a small cohort with aortic stenosis, and therefore vulnerable to limited power and residual confounding. Larger ventricular dimensions in the AF group hint at structural remodeling not fully captured by preserved ejection fraction. The murine model, while elegant, is necessarily reductionist: 24 hours of irregular pacing in isolated myocytes cannot reproduce tissue-level features such as conduction heterogeneity, dispersion of repolarization, or re-entry. Although the CaMKII-NCX pathway is compelling, it is unlikely to be the only mechanism operative in a syndrome as heterogeneous as AF.

The clinical implications should therefore be interpreted with restraint. This work does not support AF-specific ventricular arrhythmia prophylaxis, nor does it alter current indications for implantable defibrillators. It does, however, sharpen the argument that ventricular irregularity matters even when the average rate appears controlled. Contemporary trials have already shifted practice toward earlier or more active rhythm-control strategies, with signals for improved cardiovascular outcomes in selected populations. The present study adds a ventricular mechanistic rationale that may help explain some of those benefits. It also raises a more provocative possibility: if sinus rhythm cannot be maintained, might regularization of ventricular activation through strategies such as “pace and ablate” mitigate arrhythmic risk?5, 6, 7

Several questions follow. Does AF burden correlate with ventricular ectopy, ventricular tachyarrhythmias, and SCD risk in a dose-dependent manner? Are the cellular changes described here reversible with restoration of sinus rhythm, or indeed with regularization of ventricular activation alone? And can the CaMKII oxidation pathway be safely targeted in humans? Recent advances in small-molecule CaMKII inhibition and preclinical gene editing are encouraging, but translation will require considerable caution.8,9

For now, the principal contribution of this study is conceptual. It challenges the acceptability of “adequate rate control” as a complete endpoint and reframes AF as a disorder capable of directly reshaping ventricular excitability. That shift matters. If AF can generate ventricular triggers through irregular activation alone, then the boundary between atrial and ventricular disease is more porous than traditionally assumed. Spangler et al1 make that argument with unusual clarity and make it increasingly difficult to ignore.
