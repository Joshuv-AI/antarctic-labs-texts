Cyanidin-3-O-glucoside-mediated reduction of regulatory T cells: a therapeutic strategy for multiple myeloma
Authors: Huixian Chen, Kehua Fang, Jinbao Zong, Xiaotian Chang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMultiple myeloma (MM) is a malignant plasma cell tumor. Regulatory T cells (Tregs), key components of the immunosuppressive tumor immune microenvironment (TIME), play a significant role in MM progression. One mechanism of anti-MM activity of anti-CD38 monoclonal antibodies is reducing CD38+ Tregs. Cyanidin-3-O-glucoside (C3G), a natural CD38 inhibitor, effectively suppresses enzymatic activity of CD38. Consequently, we investigated whether C3G could serve as a novel therapeutic agent for MM by targeting Tregs.MethodsA MM-bearing mouse model was established and treated with C3G to investigate whether C3G can modulate the TIME for therapeutic effects. Tumor growth was monitored, and changes in peripheral blood lymphocyte subsets were assessed to evaluate the immune-modulating effects of C3G. In addition to that, C3G was applied to Naive CD4+ T cells derived from the peripheral blood of healthy individuals in vitro to verify its impact on level of Tregs. Network pharmacology and RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) were employed to explore the potential mechanisms by which C3G targets Tregs, and validation was performed using real-time PCR.ResultsC3G demonstrated therapeutic effects in MM-bearing mice, reducing the proportion of Tregs in peripheral blood while showing no significant effects on T cells, B cells, or NK cells. In vitro studies revealed that C3G’s effects on Tregs were concentration-dependent and capable of reducing Tregs even in the presence of MM cells. Integration of network pharmacology and RNA-seq results suggested that downregulation of granzyme B (GZMB) and upregulation of heme oxygenase 1 (HMOX1) at the transcriptional level are key mechanisms by which C3G influences Tregs.ConclusionC3G exerts therapeutic effects on MM by reducing the proportion of Tregs and thereby restoring anti-tumor immunity. The mechanism may be related to the decrease of GZMB level mediated by the inhibition of CD38.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12865-025-00756-x.

Introduction

Multiple myeloma (MM) is an incurable chronic hematologic malignancy. The tumor immune microenvironment (TIME), comprising various immune cells and cytokines around tumor cells, affects the inhibition and progression of tumor growth [1]. Regulatory T cells (Tregs), which are associated with the formation of an immunosuppressive TIME, can impair antigen presentation [2, 3] and suppress the function of immune effector cells such as natural killer (NK) cells and plasma cells by secreting immunosuppressive cytokines [4, 5]. Additionally, Tregs can suppress the expansion of effector T cells [6, 7] and mediate their death through perforin and granzyme [8]. Moreover, Tregs have the ability to suppress their own apoptosis and induce myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) [9].

In MM, an increase in the number and activity, as well as the inhibition of apoptosis of Tregs, can be observed. However, some researchers have proposed that Tregs may be reduced or have functional deficiencies [10]. What is more consistent is that a elevated level of Tregs correlate with shorter disease progression time and survival period, as well as a poorer treatment response. Consequently, reducing the proportion of Tregs or inhibiting their suppressive function may contribute to the treatment of MM [10]. Daratumumab, an anti-CD38 monoclonal antibody (mAb), has been widely applied in the treatment of MM [11]. It can reduce the number of CD38+ immunosuppressive cells, particularly CD38+ Tregs, thereby helping to restore anti-tumor immunity [12, 13]. Studies have shown that compared with CD38-negative Tregs, CD38+ Tregs exhibit stronger immunosuppressive functions [14, 15].

Cyanidin-3-O-glucoside (C3G) is a major constituent of anthocyanins and is widely used as a natural health supplement [16]. Previous studies have demonstrated that C3G acts as an inhibitor of CD38 enzyme activity [17, 18]. Our research group has also validated the inhibitory effect of C3G on CD38 enzyme activity in prior investigations [19, 20]. Therefore, we propose the question of whether C3G can exert anti-tumor effects by modulating TIME, particularly by targeting CD38+ Tregs.

In this study, a MM tumor-bearing mice model was established and intervened with C3G. The proportions of T cells, B cells, NK cells, and Tregs in the peripheral blood were monitored. We found that both the healthy control mice and the MM mice treated with C3G exhibited a decrease in the proportion of Tregs. In in-vitro experiments, we sorted Naive CD4+ T cells and co-cultured them with MM cells. After C3G treatment, we detected the change in the proportion of Tregs and used network pharmacology analysis and transcriptome sequencing analysis to explore the possible mechanisms.

Materials and methods

Detailed descriptions of reagents, cell culture, model construction, flow cytometry, Network pharmacology, RNA sequencing (RNA-seq), and statistical analysis are provided in the Supplementary Data 1.

Results

C3G inhibited MM progression by reducing the proportion of Tregs

A subcutaneous xenograft mouse model of MM was successfully established and treated with either PBS or C3G. Subcutaneous tumor dimensions were assessed biweekly with a caliper, and the tumor volume was determined using the formula: volume = length × width²/2. At the experimental endpoint, mice were euthanized, and tumors were excised, weighed, and photographed. The experimental workflow is schematically shown in Fig. 1A. Results indicated that by day 13, the subcutaneous tumor in MM-C3G group was visibly smaller than that in MM group (1037 ± 53.40 mm³ vs. 527.7 ± 56.39 mm³, P < 0.01, Fig. 1B). At the experimental endpoint, the tumor weight was significantly reduced in MM-C3G group (1157 ± 64.08 mg vs. 507.8 ± 72.64 mg, P < 0.001, Fig. 1C, D). These findings demonstrate that C3G has a therapeutic effect on the mouse model of MM, inhibiting disease progression.

To investigate whether C3G exerts therapeutic effects on MM through immune regulation, flow cytometry was conducted on peripheral blood samples from the experimental mice to examine the proportions of T cells, B cells, NK cells, and Tregs. Results showed that for the proportion of peripheral blood CD3⁻CD19⁺ B cells (Fig. 2A) and CD3⁺ T cells (Fig. 2B) among lymphocytes, no significant differences were observed between HC (22.39 ± 2.99%, 65.51 ± 3.83%) and HC + C3G group (18.24 ± 0.59%, 72.98 ± 0.30%) or the MM group (24.28 ± 3.03%, 72.47 ± 3.05%). Additionally, C3G treatment had no significant impact on MM mice (19.06 ± 3.41%, 78.19 ± 3.92%). For peripheral blood NK cells (Fig. 2C), a significant decrease was observed in MM tumor-bearing mice (1.01 ± 0.12%) compared to HC group (3.88 ± 0.68%, P < 0.01). Nevertheless, C3G treatment did not significantly affect the proportion of NK cells in either HC mice (3.20 ± 0.60%) or MM mice (1.21 ± 0.29%). For peripheral blood Tregs (Fig. 2D), there was no significant difference between MM group (6.38 ± 0.31%) and HC group (5.79 ± 0.60%). However, a notable reduction in Tregs was observed in HC + C3G group (3.94 ± 0.41%, P < 0.05) compared to HC group. Moreover, C3G treatment significantly decreased the proportion of Tregs in MM mice (4.76 ± 0.15%, P < 0.05) compared to the untreated MM group. Immunofluorescence staining was further performed to quantify tumor-infiltrating Tregs in subcutaneous tumors of MM-bearing mice. Consistently, a significant reduction in the proportion of Foxp3⁺CD4⁺ Tregs among CD4⁺ T cells was observed in the MM + C3G group compared with the MM group (Figure S1). These findings suggest that C3G inhibits disease progression in MM tumor-bearing mice by reducing the level of Tregs.

C3G reduced the proportion of Tregs in CD4+ T cells in vitro

To determine the safe concentration of C3G for Naive CD4+ T cells, the cytotoxicity of different concentrations of C3G was evaluated using the CCK-8 assay. Naive CD4+ T cells were exposed to various concentrations of C3G (0 µM, 50 µM, 100 µM, 200 µM, 400 µM, and 600 µM) for 48 h. Results showed that concentrations of C3G up to 400 µM did not significantly impact cell viability. Conversely, a substantial decrease in cell viability was observed at 600 µM C3G compared to 0 µM control group (59.81 ± 2.15%, P < 0.001, Fig. 3A). Therefore, concentrations of 400 µM and below were selected for follow-up experiments.

To determine the optimal concentration of C3G, after 48 h of treatment with C3G final concentrations of 0 µM, 25 µM, 50 µM, 100 µM, 200 µM, and 400 µM, the proportion of Tregs in the sorted Naive CD4+ T cells was detected by flow cytometry. The results revealed that the effect of C3G on Treg cells was concentration-dependent. Compared with untreated cells (29.36 ± 1.47%), 400 µM C3G significantly reduced the proportion of Tregs (1.04 ± 0.16%, P < 0.001, Fig. 3B, C). Therefore, 400 µM was used as the final concentration of C3G in follow-up experiments.

To verify the function of C3G on Tregs in the presence of MM cells, MM.1 S cells were co-cultured with Naive CD4+ T cells in direct contact and treated with 400 µM C3G for 48 h. The proportion of Tregs was then measured (Fig. 4A). Results indicated that compared to CD4+ T cells cultured alone (29.59 ± 0.94%), C3G significantly reduced the proportion of Tregs (10.43 ± 0.93%, P < 0.01), while the presence of MM.1 S cells did not significantly affect Tregs’ proportion (39.04 ± 5.12%, P = 0.17). Additionally, C3G decreased the proportion of Tregs in CD4+ T cells co-cultured with MM.1 S cells (25.01 ± 2.31%, P < 0.05). Similar results were obtained in the co-culture experiments of RPMI-8226 cells with Naive CD4+ T cells (Figure S2). These results were consistent with the observations in peripheral blood of mice. When MM.1 S cells pre-treated with 400 µM C3G for 12 h were co-cultured with Naive CD4+ T cells, no significant difference in proportion of Tregs was observed compared to MM.1 S cells not treated with C3G (27.87 ± 1.10% vs. 30.62 ± 0.54%, P = 0.36, Fig. 4B). Furthermore, the effects of C3G treatment on the levels of Th1, Th2, and Th17 cells in CD4+ T cells were assessed, with results represented by the mean fluorescence intensity of INF-γ, IL-4, and IL-17 A, respectively. C3G treatment significantly increased the levels of Th1 cells (6102 ± 93.65 vs. 6963 ± 142.3, P < 0.01) and Th2 cells (905.3 ± 3.18 vs. 1497 ± 8.41, P < 0.0001), while no notable variation was observed for Th17 cells (Fig. 4C).

Network Pharmacology analysis

After searching the six databases, we got a total of 477 possible targets of C3G, and 460 after de duplication (Table S1). When searching Treg-related genes, 2056 genes with a correlation score greater than 1.777 were obtained by median screening of results from GeneCards database for three times, and 2158 genes (Table S2) related to Tregs were obtained after combining with OMIM database results. When C3G predicted targets and Treg-related targets were crossed, 41 overlapping genes were found (Fig. 5A, Table S3). The protein-protein interaction (PPI) network diagram of these 41 genes was obtained in Cytoscape 3.9.1 software, which showed 38 nodes and 323 edges (Table S4). Using the Centiscape tool for screening, the conditions were “Closeness unDir > 0.0177, Betweenness unDir > 21.789, Degree unDir > 17.0”, and 13 core genes were obtained, which were AKT1, TNF, IL6, STAT3, CASP3, BCL2, HIF1A, IFNG, CTNNB1, mTOR, PPARG, RELA, and MCL1 (Fig. 5B). Go analysis was performed on 41 overlapping genes, and the visualization of three items including biological processes, cellular components and molecular functions involved was obtained (Fig. 5C, Table S5). In terms of biological processes, genes were mainly involved in apoptosis, regulation of epithelial cell migration, inflammatory response to injury and oxidative stress response. In cellular components, it was mainly related to the outer membrane of organelles, the outer membrane of mitochondria and BCL-2 protein family complexes; In terms of molecular function, it mainly involved the binding of transcriptional co regulators, cytokine receptors, nuclear factor activity, etc. KEGG enrichment analysis showed that these 41 overlapping genes were mainly related to apoptosis, HIF-1 and TNF signal transduction, inflammatory bowel disease, PD-L1 and PD-1 pathways in tumors, and Th17 cell differentiation (Fig. 5D, Table S6).

RNA-seq analysis

RNA-seq analysis was employed to explore the possible pathways through which C3G affects Naive CD4+ T cells. It was revealed that there are DEGs between Naive CD4+ T cells treated with C3G and those without treatment (Table S7). Compared to untreated Naive CD4+ T cells, the C3G-treated group had 252 DEGs, including 82 down-regulated genes and 170 up-regulated genes (Fig. 6A). To further predict the functions of these DEGs, GO analysis was performed. The DEGs were classified into three categories based on their functions and pathways, including biological processes, cellular components, and molecular functions. The most representative differences in each group were “mitotic spindle organization, mitotic spindle assembly checkpoint signaling, and cell division,” “spindle, outer kinetochore, and kinetochore,” and “ATP binding, protein binding, and ATP-dependent protein folding chaperones,” respectively (Fig. 6B, Table S8).

Fig. 6RNA-seq analysis of naive CD4+ T cells treated with C3G. A Volcano plot of DEGs. B Bar chart of GO enrichment analysis for DEGs. C, D. Bubble charts of KEGG pathway enrichment analysis for DEGs

RNA-seq analysis of naive CD4+ T cells treated with C3G. A Volcano plot of DEGs. B Bar chart of GO enrichment analysis for DEGs. C, D. Bubble charts of KEGG pathway enrichment analysis for DEGs

To identify the signaling pathways that may play a key role in C3G intervention, we conducted KEGG enrichment analysis on the DEGs. The results of the KEGG enrichment analysis of the differential genes were represented by a scatter plot. The larger the point, the more enriched genes there were; the redder the point, the more significant the enriched KEGG pathway. Compared to Naive CD4+ T cells that were not treated with C3G, the up-regulated KEGG enrichment pathways of C3G-treated group were mainly focused on the P53 signaling pathway, apoptosis, ECM-receptor interaction, NF-κB signaling pathway, cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction, and basal cell carcinoma (Fig. 6C, Table S9). While the down-regulated enrichment pathways were mainly concentrated on oocyte meiosis, cell cycle, cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction, eukaryotic ribosome biogenesis, nucleocytoplasmic transport, cysteine and methionine metabolism, and purine metabolism (Fig. 6D, Table S10).

In addition, gene-set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was employed to delineate the potential biological implications of differentially expressed genes based on GO and KEGG terms. Representative GO biological process sets that were up-regulated included negative regulation of Wnt signaling pathway, cell-cell adhesion via plasma-membrane adhesion molecules, and antibacterial humoral response, whereas rRNA processing, rRNA metabolic process and ribosomal small subunit biogenesis were significantly down-regulated (Figure S3, Table S11). Among GO cellular component sets, enrichment was observed for vacuolar membrane, lysosomal membrane, and autophagosome, while preribosome, small-subunit processome and ribosome were markedly reduced (Figure S4, Table S12). GO molecular function sets showed increased DNA-binding transcription activator activity, RNA polymerase II-specific, DNA-binding transcription activator activity and guanyl-nucleotide exchange factor activity, concomitant with decreased ATP-dependent protein folding chaperone, ribonucleoprotein complex binding, and ribosome binding (Figure S5, Table S13). KEGG-based GSEA further revealed significant enrichment of several signaling pathways. Notably, gastric cancer, basal cell carcinoma, and p53 signaling pathway were up-regulated, whereas nucleocytoplasmic transport, ribosome biogenesis in eukaryotes, and spliceosome pathways were down-regulated (Figure S6, Table S14).

Verification with real-time PCR

We intersected the overlapping genes predicted by network pharmacology to be potential targets of C3G in Tregs with the DEGs obtained from RNA-seq analysis, identifying two genes: heme oxygenase 1 (HMOX1) and granzyme B (GZMB) (Fig. 7A, B; Table S15). Transcripts per million (TPM) is a standardized expression measure used to compare the abundance of transcripts between different genes or between different samples. Based on TPM values obtained by RNA-seq, we found that the transcription level of GZMB was decreased and that of HMOX1 was increased after C3G intervention in Naive CD4+ T cells. And the transcriptional level of GZMB was significantly higher than that of HMOX1 (Fig. 7C, Table S16). The transcriptional levels of GZMB and HMOX1 genes were detected by Real-time PCR for verification, showing the same trend as RNA-seq (Fig. 7D). We also compared the transcriptional levels of CD38 and found that C3G intervention had no significant effect on it (Figure S7). GZMB and HMOX1 genes may be key to the reduction of Tregs caused by C3G.

Discussions

Tregs are vital for maintaining self-tolerance and modulating the overall immune response to infections or tumor cells, and they contribute to the formation of an immunosuppressive TIME, which is inversely correlated with patient survival in various cancers [21]. Elevated proportions or activities of Tregs have been observed in several solid tumors, including head and neck squamous cell carcinoma, breast cancer, pancreatic cancer, and colorectal cancer [22–24]. Although the role of Tregs in MM remains controversial, depletion of Tregs through exogenous interventions has demonstrated therapeutic efficacy in multiple animal models of MM [10].

In the in-vivo mouse experiment of this work, after C3G intervention, the subcutaneous tumor volume and tumor weight in MM-bearing mice were significantly reduced, which proved the therapeutic effect of C3G on MM. The changes in the levels of common immune cells including CD3+ T cells, B cells, NK cells, and Tregs were detected. Although the proportion of peripheral blood NK cells in MM-bearing mice was found to be reduced, C3G intervention had no notable impact on it. However, it was observed that there was a decrease in level of Tregs in both healthy mice and MM-bearing mice after C3G intervention. The reduction of Tregs may be one of the potential therapeutic mechanisms of C3G for MM. To clarify whether C3G directly acts on Naive CD4+ T cells to reduce Tregs, in-vitro verification was conducted. Naive CD4+ T cells with different concentrations of C3G were intervened, and it was found that it had concentration-dependent impact on Tregs. Myeloma cell lines were co-cultured with Naive CD4+ T cells to simulate the in-vivo environment, and it was observed that C3G could also reduce Treg cell levels in the presence of myeloma cells. In contrast, co-culturing MM.1 S cells pre-treated with C3G with Naive CD4+ T cells could not reduce Tregs, indicating that C3G could not indirectly mediate the reduction of Tregs through affecting myeloma cells.

According to different differentiation processes, CD4+ Tregs can be categorized into natural Tregs and induced Tregs [25]. Natural Tregs primarily originate from thymus cells, while induced Tregs are produced from Naive CD4+ T cells in the periphery [26]. It has been proved by the latest research of Dong Chen and others that natural Tregs were mainly distributed in lymph nodes and spleens in tumor-animal models, and about 50% of Tregs in tumors were induced Tregs, which significantly contribute to the formation of an immunosuppressive TIME and facilitate tumor progression [27]. After activation in the peripheral circulation, Naive CD4+ T cells can differentiate into Tregs, Th1 cells, Th2 cells, and Th17 cells, etc [28, 29]. Tregs have a regulatory effect on other subgroups of CD4+ T cells (including Th1, Th2, and Th17 cells) [30], inhibiting their expansion and survival [31], and promoting apoptosis [32]. In the detection of each subgroup of CD4+ T cells, it was also found that after C3G intervention, there was a decrease in the proportion of Tregs and an increase in the levels of Th1 and Th2 cells, and there was no statistically notable difference in Th17 cells.

In this study, the reduction in level of Tregs may be attributed to multiple factors, including decreased differentiation, functional suppression, or increased apoptosis. By combining network pharmacology with RNA-seq, the possible mechanisms of C3G in reducing level of Tregs were further explored. By integrating the predicted targets obtained from network pharmacology analysis with DEGs in RNA-seq, the potential key targets of C3G action were identified. That is, GZMB and HMOX-1. GZMB is a serine protease, which is often combined with perforin and released by cytotoxic T cells and NK cells. It mediates the apoptosis of target cells via caspase-dependent and caspase-independent pathways [33], thus playing an anti-tumor role [34, 35]. Nevertheless, Grossman and others found that GZMB had a high expression on Tregs induced by Naive CD4+ T cells, while its expression on Th1, Th2, and Th17 cells appears to be uncommon [36–38]. Different from its role in immune-active cells such as NK cells, the expression of GZMB is one of key mechanisms through which Tregs control immune responses [31, 32, 39]. Increased GZMB expression in Tregs is associated with higher tumor metastasis risk and shorter survival [40], while it can also reduce the incidence of graft-versus-host disease [41]. Moreover, the reduction of its transcription or expression level would exacerbate the progression of autoimmune-related diseases [42, 43]. In animal experiments, increasing the expression of GZMB in Tregs could also help to alleviate the symptoms of colitis [30]. HMOX1 is responsible for catalyzing the rate-limiting step in the heme degradation process, and plays an important role in protecting cells from oxidative and inflammatory damage and maintaining cellular homeostasis. In tumor cells, upregulation of HMOX1 has been shown to induce ferroptosis [44, 45]. In Tregs, one study suggests that upregulation of HMOX1 increases the expression of FoxP3 [46], while another indicates that HMOX1 is not essential for the development, maintenance, and function of Tregs [47]. A study has reported a negative correlation between GZMB expression and HMOX1 in CD8+ T cells, whereas no correlation was found in CD4+ T cells [48].

In the analysis of DEGs in RNA-seq, it was found that GZMB was down-regulated after C3G intervention on Naive CD4+ T cells. Considering C3G as an inhibitor of CD38 enzyme activity and based on the target prediction results of network pharmacology, we believe that CD38 may mediate the effect of C3G on treg, although the results show no significant change in the transcriptional level of CD38. In a study pre-published at the 24th European Hematology Association Congress, Ruonan Li et al. reported that high expression of CD38 is associated with increased GZMB expression in leukemia cells [49]. In the PPI network, we observed an interaction between CD38 and GZMB, with a combined score of 0.786 (high reliability). Compared with GZMB, HMOX1 exhibited a lower TPM value, indicating a lower transcriptional level. However, following C3G intervention, the transcriptional level of HMOX1 increased slightly. A previous study reported that the positive expression rate of HMOX1 in Th1 cells was significantly higher than that in Tregs [50]. Consistent with this, our study also observed an elevated level of Th1 cells, which may account for the activation of HMOX1. Additionally, no interactions between CD38 and HMOX1 or between GZMB and HMOX1 were showed in the PPI network. Therefore, it was speculated that C3G might reduce GZMB expression by inhibiting CD38 enzyme activity, thereby attenuating the immunosuppressive capacity of Tregs.

There are the following limitations in this work. First, the current findings need further validation. Second, although the proportion of Tregs among CD4+ T cells were detected, the absolute counts and functional researches of Tregs were not conducted. Finally, the RNA-seq data represent the changes in the entire CD4+ T cells, and single-cell transcriptomics needed to be further distinguished and explored each subpopulation in the future. Despite these limitations, it has been demonstrated that C3G can exert a therapeutic effect on MM by targeting Tregs in this work, which provides a basis and more possibilities for the immunotherapy of MM.

In this study, we primarily focused on Tregs, while analysis of CD4+ T cells revealed upregulated HMOX1 transcription. Given the differential HMOX1 expression between Treg and Th1 cells, coupled with C3G-induced elevation of Th1 levels, we propose that C3G may not exclusively target Tregs but rather modulate the balance across CD4+ T cell subsets - a phenomenon warranting further investigation. Furthermore, current anti-CD38 mAbs exert anti-myeloma effects by targeting both CD38-high myeloma cells and CD38-positive immunosuppressive cells. Our data demonstrate that C3G does not alter CD38 transcriptional levels in CD4+ T cells, suggesting minimal risk of off-target interference when combined with anti-CD38 mAbs. KEGG enrichment and GSEA analyses identified C3G-mediated upregulation of the tumor suppressor p53 signaling pathway. This finding indicates C3G may directly induce pro-apoptotic and anti-proliferative effects in myeloma cells (a conclusion supported by our ongoing studies), positioning C3G as a promising candidate for combination therapy with anti-CD38 mAbs.

Conclusion

Overall, this study demonstrates that C3G exerts therapeutic effects on MM by reducing the level of Tregs. The effects of C3G on Tregs may be associated with the down-regulation of GZMB mediated by the inhibition of CD38. Although the underlying mechanisms require further investigation, this study provides a novel direction for the immunotherapy of MM.
