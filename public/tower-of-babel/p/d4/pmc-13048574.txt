Glycan pairing in therapeutic IgG orchestrates Fcγ receptor engagement and ADCC: an integrated structure-function approach for thorough evaluation of Fc N-glycans as critical quality attributes
Authors: Maximilian Meudt, Julia Baumeister, Antoinette Cassiopeia Russell, Lindsey Dzielak, Gale Hansen, Boris Mizaikoff, Sybille Ebert, Frank Rosenau, Michaela Blech, Fabian Higel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMonoclonal antibodies (mAbs) can mediate their therapeutic activity through antigen binding and by engaging Fc receptors (FcRs) or the complement system. FcRs are expressed on immune cells and can initiate effector functions such as antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC) and antibody-dependent cellular phagocytosis. The conserved Fc N-glycans of immunoglobulin G (IgG) at N297 modulate the strength and stability of Fc–FcγR interactions and the resulting effector responses. Conventional analysis typically considers individual glycans in isolation and neglects the fact that each Fc part carries two N-glycans, that may be paired symmetrically or asymmetrically. Recent studies with IgG Fc regions have demonstrated that glycan pairing can significantly shape Fc–FcR interactions. Here, the effect of glycan pairing was evaluated with full-length IgG using rituximab as a model. A set of pure, homogeneous, symmetrical, and asymmetrical Fc glyco-pair variants was generated by controlled chemoenzymatic remodeling and purification. Surface plasmon resonance binding studies across a comprehensive panel of human FcγRs revealed receptor- and allotype-specific effects: FcγRI binding was essentially unaffected, FcγRIIa showed allotype dependence, with FcγRIIa-H131 predominantly modulated by galactosylation and FcγRIIa-R131 by afucosylation, whereas FcγRIIIa required only a single afucosylation to reach maximal binding. Galactosylation exerted modest and receptor-dependent effects, most pronounced for FcγRIIIb. High-mannose species reduced binding to FcγRI and FcγRII, but displayed intermediate affinity to FcγRIIIa, which was lower than afucosylated complex types yet higher than fully fucosylated variants. To evaluate the biological relevance, cell-based ADCC reporter gene assays confirmed that single afucosylation was sufficient to drive the increase in ADCC potency, with galactosylation and high-mannose contributing only modest or context-dependent effects. Together, these findings establish glycan pairing as a critical quality attribute of therapeutic antibodies and provide a framework for precise evaluation of Fc N-glycan criticality and for tailoring mAbs with defined effector function profiles.

Introduction

Monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) represent a major class of therapeutic proteins with broad clinical applications in oncology, autoimmune disorders, and infectious diseases.1 Their therapeutic activity is defined not only by antigen recognition through the antigen-binding fragment (Fab) domain, but also by the crystallizable fragment (Fc) domain, which mediates effector functions via interactions with Fc receptors (FcRs) and the complement system. These Fc-mediated interactions can contribute directly to the mechanism of action, clinical efficacy, and safety profile of therapeutic mAbs, making a detailed understanding of Fc biology an essential component of antibody development.2,3

Immunoglobulin G (IgG) Fc domains carry a single, conserved N-glycosylation site at asparagine 297 (EU numbering) on each heavy chain. As Chinese hamster ovary (CHO) cells are the predominant production host for therapeutic antibodies (76% of all US Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-approved antibody therapeutics and 78% of FDA-approved IgG1 products 4), the Fc glycan repertoire of CHO-derived IgGs is particularly relevant. In such antibodies, the attached Fc N-glycans are predominantly complex-type, biantennary structures, with variable levels of galactosylation, core fucosylation, and a usually smaller proportion of high-mannose-type glycans.4 These glycans contribute to Fc structural stability and can influence both pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic properties of a biotherapeutic.5,6 Fc N-glycans are key modulators of Fcγ receptor (FcγR) engagement, a family of receptors that link antibodies to immune cell effector functions.7,8 Human FcγRs are classified into activating receptors (FcγRI, FcγRIIa, FcγRIIIa, and FcγRIIIb) and the inhibitory receptor FcγRIIb, which differ in their affinity, cellular distribution, and contribution to processes such as antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC), and antibody-dependent cellular phagocytosis (ADCP).9 FcγRI binds monomeric IgG with high affinity and participates in antigen presentation and immune activation. FcγRIIa and FcγRIIb regulate immune complex handling and phagocytosis, with FcγRIIb providing inhibitory feedback. FcγRIIIa, expressed on natural killer (NK) cells, is a primary mediator of ADCC, while FcγRIIIb, anchored to neutrophils via a glycosylphosphatidylinositol linker, contributes to immune complex clearance and inflammatory responses.10 Except for FcγRI, the magnitude of Fc-FcR binding is strongly influenced by the Fc glycan composition. For example, the absence of core fucose drastically increases FcγRIIIa binding and ADCC activity, whereas galactosylation and high-mannose content exert more subtle, receptor-dependent effects.6,11

Conventional Fc N-glycan analysis usually reports the relative abundance of individual glycan species, without accounting for the fact that each IgG Fc domain carries two N-glycans, which can pair either symmetrically (identical) or asymmetrically (non-identical). However, N-glycan pairing is not random, but partially directed through steric hindrance or host cell enzyme level and specificity.12,13 Consequently, the apparent pairing distribution or its functional impact cannot be predicted from bulk glycan percentages. The functional relevance of high-mannose glycan pairing is illustrated by mannose receptor (CD206) interaction, which recognizes and binds high-mannose glycans. Symmetrical high-mannose pairs bind with substantially higher avidity than asymmetrical combinations, influencing mAb clearance and therefore pharmacokinetics.14 Furthermore, recent work has shown that both the composition and the symmetry of Fc glycan pairs influence FcγR binding, adding a previously underappreciated layer of structural and functional complexity.15 Asymmetrical pairing can alter Fc domain conformation and modulate the accessibility of receptor-binding sites, thereby affecting binding strength and kinetics. Building on these principles, we used defined mAb glyco-pair Fcs with affinity chromatography (AC) and surface plasmon resonance (SPR) analyses to demonstrate that FcγR binding is influenced by glycan pairing.15 The present study extends this concept to full-length IgG, using rituximab as a clinically relevant model. Rituximab is a chimeric anti-CD20 monoclonal antibody widely used in the treatment of CD20-positive B-cell malignancies and selected autoimmune diseases, mediating rapid B-cell depletion through Fc-dependent effector functions such as ADCC and complement activation.16 A panel of pure, homogeneous, symmetrical, and asymmetrical glyco-pair variants was generated, including complex-type galactosylated, afucosylated, and high-mannose species. The variants were produced using controlled enzymatic and purification strategies to ensure structural homogeneity and integrity, enabling targeted functional evaluation. Binding properties toward a comprehensive set of human FcγRs, including major allotypes, were characterized by SPR. To translate the observed FcγRIIIa binding differences into a biological context, a cell-based ADCC reporter gene assay was performed. By combining precise glyco-pair generation with detailed receptor-binding characterization and functional assessment, this work delivers a targeted variant analysis not previously achieved for full-length antibodies. The resulting insights refine the understanding of Fc glycan criticality and highlight opportunities to deliberately tailor mAbs for defined effector function profiles.

Results

Generation and quality control of rituximab asymmetrical Fc glyco-pairs

Starting from rituximab drug product, pure, homogenous, asymmetrical as well as symmetrical Fc glyco-pairs were generated and isolated as indicated in Figure 1. Briefly, for full-length asymmetrically fucosylated variants, native Fc N-glycans were completely removed down to the core GlcNAc-F (shown in Figure S1), and core fucose partially removed to produce the hemi-fucosylated intermediate GlcNAc-F:GlcNAc. Formation of the hemi-fucosylated intermediate was monitored by intact mass spectrometry (MS) and the material used as substrate for subsequent transglycosylation. To our knowledge, this controlled chemoenzymatic route to full-length, asymmetrically fucosylated IgG has not been reported previously. Homogeneous complex-type glycans were then restored by glycan transfer to yield defined glyco-pairs. The resulting variants G0:G0, G0F:G0, and G0F:G0F were separated using FcγRIIIa AC as previously described 15 and shown in Figure S2, and fractions were collected. A subset of the asymmetrical material was further galactosylated, and/or selectively trimmed, subjected to targeted glycan transfer, and isolated by FcγRIIIa AC, yielding the variants G2F:G2, G0F:G2, and G2F:G0. To produce asymmetrical non-fucosylated or high-mannose species, rituximab N-glycans were fully trimmed to the core GlcNAc prior to transglycosylation with combinations of complex-type (G0, G2) and high-mannose (M5) donors. Because transfer efficiencies differed between donor substrates, reaction conditions were adjusted to obtain balanced product distributions and enable isolation of asymmetrical species by FcγRIIIa AC (Figure S2). Using this strategy, seven distinct asymmetrical rituximab glyco-pairs (G0F:G0, G2F:G0, G0F:G2, G2F:G2, G0:G2, G0:M5, and G2:M5) were generated, and the corresponding symmetrical controls (G0:G0, G0F:G0F, G2:G2, G2F:G2F, M5:M5) were isolated in parallel, yielding a panel of 12 purified glyco-pair variants. Identity and purity of all isolated variants were confirmed by intact liquid chromatography (LC)-MS (Figure 2) and analytical size-exclusion chromatography (SEC) (Table S1 and Figure S3). The MS data enabled unequivocal identification of the desired rituximab glyco-pairs and were further evaluated for glycosylation-related, generation process-derived impurities. While minor additional glycoform species were detected in some samples, the overall purity of the isolated variants remained very high across the panel. SEC clearly showed that no significant aggregates or fragments were formed during the generation process, meaning that the variants are present as intact, monomeric molecules and therefore suitable for follow-up functional analysis. Affinity of asymmetrical glyco-pair mAbs toward human FcγRs

To assess the functional relevance of Fc N-glycan pairing in a clinically established antibody context, symmetrical and asymmetrical glyco-pair variants of full-length rituximab were analyzed for their binding properties toward a panel of human FcγRs, including major allotypes. These comprised FcγRI, FcγRIIa-H131, FcγRIIa-R131, FcγRIIb, FcγRIIIa-F158, FcγRIIIa-V158, and FcγRIIIb. Due to different affinities of Fc to different FcγRs and their allotypes, assay conditions were adapted for certain receptors. This experimental design emphasized comparative affinity trends between glyco-pair variants rather than the determination of absolute KD values.

FcγRI:Fc binding is insensitive to Fc glycoforms

FcγRI is the only high-affinity FcR and primarily expressed on monocytes and macrophages, with a well-established role in IgG uptake and antigen presentation.9,17 Fc binding measurements were conducted using prolonged dissociation times and a 1:1 kinetic fit model to more accurately assess the slow off-rates (Figure 3). Across all glyco-pair variants, including afucosylated, galactosylated, and high-mannose forms, no statistically significant differences in affinity were detected with the employed conditions. However, subtle trends suggested slightly enhanced binding for afucosylated or galactosylated variants. G0:G2 and G2:G2 exhibited slightly stronger binding compared to double-fucosylated forms like G0F:G0F. High-mannose variant M5:M5 showed modest reductions in affinity relative to their complex-type counterparts. FcγRII reveals allotype-specific sensitivity to core fucosylation and galactosylation

FcγRIIa is a low-affinity receptor expressed on myeloid cells, central for ADCP. Its H131 and R131 allotypes differ in IgG subclass preference and therapeutic outcomes.18 FcγRIIa-R131 was assessed via steady-state affinity fitting and exhibited a stepwise dependency on fucosylation level (Figure 4). Affinity increased progressively from G0F:G0F to G0F:G0 and further to G0:G0, indicating an incremental impact of core fucose removal. Galactosylation had no detectable effect on either fucosylated or afucosylated variants. In contrast, incorporation of high-mannose glycans diminished binding; M5:G0 had lower affinity than G0:G0, and M5:M5 displayed the weakest interaction of all tested variants. Using kinetic fitting at reduced temperature, FcγRIIa-H131 showed a different Fc binding pattern. While overall affinity for Fcs was higher than FcγRIIa-R131, afucosylation did not substantially affect binding (KD G0F:G0F ≈ G0:G0). However, galactosylation contributed modestly to increased binding, particularly double galactosylated variants displayed the strongest FcR interaction. High-mannose containing species again impaired binding, with M5:M5 showing the significantly lowest binding strength. FcγRIIb is the only inhibitory Fcγ receptor, playing an important regulatory role in immune complex handling and B cell feedback inhibition.19 Notably, FcγRIIb also carries an Arg at position 131, consistent with its structural similarity to FcγRIIa-R131 and the related binding trends observed here. Steady-state evaluations revealed clear relative differences. Similar to FcγRIIa-R131, afucosylation enhanced binding stepwise from G0F:G0F through G0F:G0 to G0:G0. Galactosylation did not improve binding across any glycoform. High-mannose glyco-pairs showed diminished affinities, with M5:M5 again exhibiting the weakest interaction. ADCC-relevant FcγRIII binding is driven by single afucosylation

FcγRIIIa is the principal mediator of ADCC through NK cell activation. The F158 and V158 allotypes differ in IgG1 affinity and clinical responsiveness.17,20,21 FcγRIIIa-F158 measurements revealed that core fucosylation is the dominant modulator of binding (Figure 5). In contrast to FcγRIIa-R131 and FcγRIIb, a single afucosylation (G0F:G0) was sufficient to elicit the full affinity enhancement, while double afucosylation (G0:G0) provided no additional benefit. Galactosylation had no consistent impact throughout all variants except for the low affinity, fucosylated G2F:G2F variant, which was increased compared to G0F:G0F. The M5:M5 glyco-pair trended toward reduced binding compared to other afucosylated glyco-pairs, but differences did not reach significance. The higher-affinity FcγRIIIa-V158 allotype mirrored the relative binding pattern of FcγRIIIa-F158 for fucose and galactose variants and pairings. Only the reduction in affinity for M5:M5 in relation to other afucosylated variants was statistically significant and different from FcγRIIIa-F158. FcγRIIIb is structurally similar to FcγRIIIa, but functionally distinct.22,23 However, the binding pattern of FcγRIIIb toward rituximab glyco-pair variants was almost identical to FcγRIIIa; a single afucosylation (G0F:G0) significantly increased binding, and further afucosylation (G0:G0) showed only a non-significant trend toward higher affinity, while galactosylation and high-mannose variants were not relevant except for G2F:G2F. Assay benchmarking and control

All SPR assays shown in Figures 3–5 were benchmarked and material integrity verified by including two controls alongside the engineered glyco-pair variants: 1) untreated rituximab starting material, predominantly G0F:G0F with expected glycan heterogeneity, and 2) a fully afucosylated rituximab produced in a FUT8-knockout (KO) CHO cell line. Across all FcγRs and allotypes, the untreated starting material matched the engineered G0F:G0F variant with minor deviations resulting from the presence of glycan heterogeneities in the bulk material. This expected, small deviation reflects the heterogeneity of the starting glycan profile rather than any loss of quality and confirms that the chemoenzymatic workflow preserved receptor-binding competence. Likewise, the FUT8-KO control aligned with the respective afucosylated complex-type pairs, such as G0:G0, G0:G2, and G2:G2, for every receptor tested. Notably, this agreement held despite differences in batch and cell-line origin, underscoring the robustness and comparability of the assay. Together, the two controls also span the method’s dynamic range, with the heterogeneous starting material anchoring the lower-affinity end and the FUT8-KO material defining the higher-affinity end across receptors. These concordant behaviors validate the assay conditions and support that the observed differences among glyco-pair variants arise from defined Fc glycan composition and pairing rather than from process or matrix effects.

Implications from SPR kinetics for the Fc-FcγR binding mechanism

Equilibrium dissociation constants (KD) can be derived from the interplay of the association rate constant (ka) and the dissociation rate constant (kd). A high-affinity interaction can result from either a rapid association, a slow dissociation, or a combination of both. For FcγR interactions that could be described by a 1:1 kinetic model, ka and kd were obtained by kinetic fitting and are shown for FcγRIIIa-V158 in Figure 6 (additional receptors and allotypes in supplementary Figure S4). Kinetic analysis of FcγRIIIa-V158 binding revealed that specific Fc N-glycan structures and pairings differentially influenced these two kinetic components, providing insight into the underlying binding mechanism. The fastest association rates were observed for asymmetrically fucosylated variants, including G2F:G0, G2F:G2, and G0F:G0. This pattern suggests that asymmetrical fucosylation may promote a more accessible Fc conformation, facilitating faster FcγRIIIa engagement. Within this group, galactosylation further accelerated association, as reflected by higher ka values for G2F-containing pairs compared to their agalactosylated counterparts. Galactosylation also appeared to enhance association in fully fucosylated variants, with G2F:G2F associating more rapidly than either G0F:G0F or even G0:G0. Among fully afucosylated variants, only double galactosylation (G2:G2) provided a notable increase in association rate compared to G0:G0 or G0:G2. High-mannose incorporation in an asymmetrical pairing had little effect on ka, but symmetrical M5:M5 exhibited the slowest association of all tested variants. In contrast, dissociation rates generally correlated inversely with afucosylation level. Stepwise removal of fucose led to progressively slower dissociation (kd G0F:G0F > G0F:G0 > G0:G0), indicating more stable Fc-FcγRIIIa complexes in the absence of core fucose. Galactosylation exerted minimal influence on dissociation for afucosylated or high-mannose containing variants. Notably, G2F:G2F displayed the fastest dissociation of all variants, whereas asymmetrically fucosylated species G2F:G2, G2F:G0, and G0F:G0 exhibited the same, tighter binding, despite their differences in galactosylation levels. These findings point toward a role for galactosylation in accelerating receptor association, likely through conformational modulation of the Fc, rather than directly stabilizing the bound complex. M5-containing variants displayed kd values comparable to symmetrical afucosylated complex-type glycans, suggesting that their reduced affinity is primarily driven by slower association. Summary

A summary and rating of the SPR-based analysis of full-length rituximab glyco-pair variants across all major FcγRs is given in Table 1. The data reveal a consistent influence of core fucosylation on receptor affinity, particularly for FcγRIIa-R131 and FcγRIIIa. A single afucosylation generally confers the full affinity gain for FcγRIIIa, with no to little additive effect from full afucosylation, while a stepwise affinity increase was true for the FcγRII class receptors FcγRIIa-R131 and FcγRIIb. Galactosylation effects were modest and receptor-dependent, contributing most significantly to FcγRIIIb binding. High-mannose glyco-pairs, particularly symmetrical M5:M5 variants, reduced FcγR engagement across all FcγRIIs, but increased binding of FcγRIIIs compared to the G0F:G0F variant. Kinetic analysis of FcγRIIIa-V158 binding provided further mechanistic insight. Asymmetrical fucosylation accelerated receptor association, an effect amplified by galactosylation, whereas dissociation rates decreased with decreasing fucose content. Galactosylation primarily influenced association rather than complex stability, indicating a likely conformational effect on Fc accessibility. In contrast, reduced FcγR binding of M5:M5 variants was largely attributable to slow association rather than rapid dissociation. ADCC reporter gene assay reveals distinct effector functionalities of asymmetrical glyco-pair mAbs

SPR-based affinity measurements and kinetic analyses demonstrated that Fc glycan pairing can influence both FcγR binding strength and the underlying association-dissociation dynamics. While these binding assays were optimized to highlight differences between glyco-pair variants, the extent to which such differences translate into biological effector functions remains uncertain. ADCC is primarily mediated by NK cells through Fc-FcγRIIIa engagement upon target cell opsonization and is a critical consideration in the clinical performance of therapeutic antibodies. Depending on the therapeutic’s intended mode of action, the presence or absence of ADCC activity can either enhance efficacy or cause adverse effects such as cytokine release syndrome or unwanted target cell cytotoxicity.24 To assess the functional relevance of the observed FcγRIIIa binding differences under more physiologically relevant conditions, cell-based ADCC reporter gene assays were performed with the panel of rituximab glyco-pair variants. Rituximab is well established to elicit ADCC in clinical settings, making it a suitable model to evaluate the impact of Fc glycan pairing on NK cell-mediated effector activity.16 Suitability of the generated rituximab material for cell-based assays was furthermore confirmed by endotoxin testing (Table S 1).

Analysis of EC50 values (Figure 7), derived from dose–response curves (given in supplementary Figure S5) revealed clear, glycan-dependent differences in ADCC potency across the rituximab panel. Two controls were included to supported assay and material quality: Untreated rituximab starting material comprising a relatively heterogenic glycosylation pattern, and afucosylated rituximab produced in a FUT8-KO cell line. The starting material showed EC50s that matched the range of the fully fucosylated benchmark G0F:G0F, underscoring the comparability of the generated variants with untreated material in the ADCC assay. The FUT8-KO control showed significantly increased ADCC compared to the reference materials, but closely aligned with the engineered G0:G0 variant, further indicating suitability of the assay and the chemoenzymatic remodeled material. Across all variants, core fucosylation was the dominant determinant of ADCC. The two symmetrically fucosylated pairs, G0F:G0F and G2F:G2F, were significantly less potent than all afucosylated, hemi-afucosylated, and high-mannose constructs. Introducing a single afucosylated glycan (G0F:G0) lowered the EC50 by roughly six-fold relative to G0F:G0F, whereas complete afucosylation (G0:G0) did not yield an additional significant improvement, indicating that one afucosylated glycan in a pair is sufficient to achieve near-maximal ADCC. Galactosylation did not yield statistically significant differences, but showed a consistent trend of pairing-sensitive enhancement within low-EC50 backgrounds. In the symmetrically afucosylated series, EC50s decreased numerically from G0:G0 to G0:G2 and G2:G2. A similar pattern was observed for hemi-afucosylated pairs: G0F:G2 displayed the lowest EC50 in the panel, whereas G2F:G0 and G2F:G2 were modestly higher. These observations suggest that total galactose content and the positioning of the core fucose relative to the galactosylated glycan in asymmetrically fucosylated glyco-pairs specify, rather than determine, ADCC performance within the afucosylated range. Relative to fucosylated complex-type controls, high-mannose variants showed intermediate to high potency. M5:M5 yielded significantly higher activity than the fully fucosylated pairs, but did not surpass the lowest EC50 of afucosylated, galactosylated constructs. Asymmetrical M5 pairings, M5:G0 and M5:G2, fell within the low-EC50 group and were not significantly different from several afucosylated complex-type variants. A subtle trend toward higher ADCC activity of asymmetrically paired M5 variants relative to M5:M5 could be observed, while galactosylation did not result in any difference comparing M5:G0 to M5:G2. Discussion

Several approaches for the generation of asymmetrically glycosylated Fc parts or antibodies have already been described, including Fab-arm exchange,25,26 IgG2 disulfide shuffling,27 and knob-into-hole half-molecule expression.28 While each of these strategies demonstrated feasibility, their applicability is often limited by the need for protein engineering, specific expression systems, a restricted range of accessible glycoforms, or simply a rather heterogenous resulting glycoform mixture. A comprehensive discussion of these methods and their limitations has recently been provided elsewhere.15 The approach by Manabe et. al,29 which used ENGase-based glycan remodeling combined with FcγRIIIa AC to generate asymmetrically glycosylated trastuzumab variants, is in some respects similar to our work. However, their reliance on custom-made glycosynthase mutants, which may be difficult to obtain or reproduce, contrasts with our strategy, which uses broadly available commercial enzymes. In addition, their method could not produce asymmetrically fucosylated or high-mannose variants, limiting its utility for comprehensive IgG N-glycan assessment. Furthermore, the methodology we applied in the present work avoids harsh reductive or denaturing conditions and preserves antibody integrity, making it broadly applicable to IgG and potentially to other Fc-bearing molecules. The approach is readily scalable and yields symmetrical as well as asymmetrical variants in defined form, providing a robust platform for systematic analysis of glycan pairing effects. By extending glyco-pair generation from previously reported Fcs 15 to intact antibodies, the strategy enables functional interaction studies under conditions comparable to native IgG.

The systematic SPR analysis of the generated glyco-pair panel provided detailed insight into the receptor- and allotype-specific effects of Fc glycosylation. Binding to FcγRI was largely unaffected by glycan composition. Only subtle trends were noted, with modestly stronger binding for galactosylated or afucosylated pairs and reduced binding for high-mannose pairs. Previous work has shown that Fc glycans can contribute indirectly to the high-affinity FcγRI recognition,30,31 although this interaction is generally regarded as glycosylation-type independent.32,33 More recent findings using elevated assay temperatures and Fcs revealed glycosylation-dependent differences in FcγRI complex stability.15 Under the conditions applied here and with full-length IgG, however, glycan-pairing effects on FcγRI binding appeared negligible and are unlikely to be biologically meaningful. Nevertheless, the contribution of even subtle affinity shifts cannot be fully excluded and would require confirmation in respective cellular assays.

For IgG binding to FcγRIIa-H131, galactosylation was the dominant modulator. In the present dataset, adding galactose increased affinity, and a single galactosylated glycan was sufficient to capture most of the effect. Afucosylation had little impact, whereas high-mannose pairs decreased binding. This pattern recapitulates the findings from evaluation of the Fc,15 where hemi-galactosylation already maximized the galactose effect and fucose contributed only modestly to FcγRIIa-H131 binding. In contrast, FcγRIIa-R131 showed a different behavior in which afucosylation primarily determined affinity, while galactosylation had no measurable effect. The progressive affinity increase from incremental afucosylation contrasts with the behavior observed for other receptors, where the presence of a single afucosylated glycan already accounts for most of the affinity gain, and suggests a distinct binding mechanism. These observations are consistent with results from FcγRIIa AC,34 which identified afucosylation as the more prominent determinant for R131 and a marked galactose effect for H131. In line with the Fc region data 15 and prior reports,35 afucosylation was also the main driver of increased Fc-FcγRIIb binding. Removal of core fucose strengthened affinity in a stepwise manner, galactosylation had only a minor impact, and high-mannose pairs reduced binding. Notably, FcγRIIb and FcγRIIa-R131 share an Arg at position 131, and are considered structurally similar in the Fc-binding interface. This provides a plausible molecular rationale for the comparable glycoform ranking observed for FcγRIIb and FcγRIIa-R131 in the present dataset. Glycoform-driven affinity changes may therefore affect not only activating receptor engagement (FcγRIIa), but also inhibitory signaling (FcγRIIb) at the same time.

For FcγRIIIa, the data confirmed afucosylation as the principal lever for affinity, with galactosylation providing only fine-tuning within that framework, consistent with Fc region experiments.15 Differences between the F158 and V158 allotypes were attributable to the generally higher affinity of FcγRIIIa-V158, which rendered otherwise small differences, such as the relative behavior of high-mannose pairs, more apparent. FcγRIIIb closely mirrored these relative affinity differences across glyco-pairs, while having markedly lower absolute affinity toward Fcs than FcγRIIIa. Notably, kinetic analysis of FcγRIIIa-V158 suggested that the affinity differences we observed were driven mainly by dissociation behavior, while association rates were less discriminatory across the key glycoform comparisons. This is exemplified by G0F:G0F versus G0:G0: both variants showed comparable ka values, yet differed more markedly in kd, indicating that enhanced complex stability is primarily characterized by slowed dissociation. A similar pattern was observed for asymmetrical variants such as G0F:G0, consistent with the 1:1 stoichiometry of the IgG-FcγR interaction, where a single afucosylated Fc glycan was sufficient to substantially prolong complex lifetime.

The biological consequences of the observed Fc-FcR affinity properties remain context dependent. Non-significant differences, like for FcγRI, are unlikely to translate into functional changes, whereas for other receptors modest shifts may contribute to altered outcomes, noting that effector functions arise from the combined engagement of receptors with distinct expression patterns on different immune cells. In clinical settings, the interplay of FcγRIIIa and the inhibitory FcγRIIb is particularly relevant for ADCC, since both receptors show increased affinity for afucosylated Fc. In principle, enhanced FcγRIIb engagement could dampen cytotoxic responses, yet the magnitude of the affinity gain is greater for FcγRIIIa, which taken together could promote increased ADCC. Thus, quantitative assessment of individual glyco-pairs is important for functional interpretation, while in vivo predictions remain challenging.

To address this, the observed impact of specific glyco-pairs on FcγRIIIa affinity differences were evaluated for biological consequence in a cell-based ADCC reporter gene assay. The results reinforced the SPR findings, identifying single afucosylation as the key determinant not only of affinity gain, but also of increased ADCC in a cellular context. Similar conclusions were drawn by Freimoser-Grundschober et al. using FcγRIIIa AC enriched fucose-glycoforms 36 and by Shatz et al. using knobs-into-holes generated hemi-afucosylated mAbs.28 Although the latter used heterodimeric Fc and rather heterogeneous glycan profiles, our study confirms these conclusions with high confidence through the use of pure, homogeneous IgG glycoforms. Moreover, the obviously limited impact of varying galactosylation reported in those studies is consistent with the present targeted dataset. Here, no statistically significant additional benefit of full afucosylation over single afucosylation was detected, and neither galactosylation nor high-mannose content produced a consistent, significant change in ADCC. The influence of galactosylation on ADCC remains the subject of discussion, with reports showing no effect 37 and others describing an increase.38,39 Wada et al. 39 applied a similar strategy to generate pure glycoforms and used a comparable assay, reporting EC50 patterns that closely align with those observed here for most variants. In detail, their absolute values for the controls, fully fucosylated variants, and fully afucosylated galactosylated variants were almost identical to the results in this study. Solely a deviation concerning the G0:G0 (A2G0 in the cited study) species underpinned their conclusion of a galactose effect on ADCC activity. Dekkers et al. 38 also reported galactose-dependent increases, although their assay format differed substantially and might not be comparable, which is reflected by the absence of ADCC activity of fucosylated species. Furthermore, they used heterogeneously glycosylated material, and apparent glycan distribution was not considered, which complicate interpretation of individual glycan properties. Overall, conclusions about clinical relevance remain contingent on assay format (reporter gene assays or peripheral blood mononuclear cell-based assay) and the resulting consequences like indirect readout or donor variability. The evidence for the impact of specific glycovariants on effector functionality has continued to grow, with several observations aligning well across studies while others remain under active discussion. Although assay formats and their performance are debated and clearly leave room for improvement, our study robustly underscores that using pure, homogeneous glycoforms, as well as explicit consideration of glycan pairing, are prerequisites for accurate assessment of Fc N-glycan criticality.

Beyond the ADCC-focused case study presented here, our findings have broader implications for Fc N-glycan criticality assessment in chemistry, manufacturing, and controls (CMC) procedures. Released glycan profiling remains the gold standard for routine Fc glycan monitoring, but it inherently reports bulk percentages and cannot resolve how glycans are distributed across the molecules. Accordingly, correlations between bulk glycan percentages and functional outcomes can miss mechanistically relevant information and may lead to misleading conclusions. Glycan pairing, in contrast, represents a higher-order attribute that links released glycan composition to glycosylation-dependent functional outcomes at the molecule level. Future studies would particularly benefit from including asymmetrical variants that share identical bulk glycan percentages, but differ in their apparent distribution across the two Fc domains, as this would furthermore illustrate the limitations of inferring function from released glycan readouts alone and highlight the added value of glyco-pair-resolved analytics.

From a risk assessment perspective, glycan pairing should therefore be considered in a context-dependent manner wherever Fc glycosylation drives biological activity or exposure. On the potency side, this includes not only FcγRIIIa-mediated effector functions, but, in principle, all glycosylation-dependent Fc functions, such as interactions across Fcγ receptor classes/allotypes and complement-related mechanisms, depending on the intended mode of action. Beyond effector function, exposure-related mechanisms should be considered as well, since high-mannose Fc glycans have been associated with increased serum clearance, and pairing-specific effects for high-mannose species have been reported.14 In this view, glycan pairing could be used as a complementary characterization layer that improves mechanistic interpretability and, where relevant, strengthens CMC risk evaluation for Fc glycosylation-related critical quality attributes.

Materials and methods

Proteins

Rituximab drug product was purchased from Comtur Pharmaceuticals (Freiburg, Germany). The human receptor FcγRIIa-R131 was purchased from ACRO Biosystems (Cat. No. CDA-H5221, Lot. No. 1822–229HF1-1CQ, Newark, DE, USA). For the remaining hFcγRs, cDNA was codon optimized for mammalian expression with a signal sequence inserted at the N-terminus and a 6xHis tag inserted at the C-terminus. The ORF was gene synthesized with XbaI and HindIII restriction sites, which was then used for restriction-enzyme-based cloning into transient pTT5 or pcDNA3.1 stable vector. FcγRIIIb was produced by transiently transfecting CHO 3E7 cells with pTT5 plasmid DNA via the TransIT-PRO system (Cat. No. MIR 5750; Mirus Bio, Madison, WI, USA). Cells were harvested 6-day post-transfection by centrifugation for 20 min at 6000 rpm. Supernatant was collected, 0.22 µm filtered prior to purification. To generate stable cell lines for FcγRI, FcγRIIa-H131, FcγRIIb, FcγRIIIa-V158, and FcγRIIIa-F158, FreeStyle CHO-S cells were transfected with pcDNA3.1 plasmid DNA via the FreeStyle Expression system (Cat. No. K900020, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Two rounds of combined puromycin and methotrexate (Cat. Nos. P8833 and A6770, MilliporeSigma, Burlington, MA, USA) drug selection resulted in final stable cell lines that were scaled up for large-scale expression and purification. Cells were harvested on day 8 to 13 by centrifugation for 20 min at 4000 to 6000 rpm and supernatant was collected, 0.22 µm filtered prior to initiation of 1st step purification. Secreted proteins were purified from cell culture supernatant via first step Ni-NTA agarose AC. Fractions were eluted with imidazole (250 mM in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS); pH 7.4) and those containing the recombinant protein (as analyzed by SDS-PAGE) were pooled. Products of first step purification were polished with either a second step ion exchange chromatography or a gel filtration chromatography using a Hi-load 26/600, 320 mL column with Superdex 200 pg matrix (Cytiva, Marlborough, MA, USA). Final proteins were assessed for monomer quality via analytical SEC followed by analytical ultracentrifugation (AUC) and LC-MS for sequence confirmation.

Generation of full-length asymmetrical N-glycosylated IgG

TransGLYCITTM (Genovis, Kävlinge, Sweden) kits were used for all deglycosylation steps (GlycINATOR®/+ FucosEXO™ 16; according to the manufacturers protocol), and all transglycosylation reactions (TransINATOR™ + oxazoline glycans; with an 25% increased enzyme and oxazoline glycan to antibody ratio). For the generation of full-length asymmetrically fucosylated glyco-pairs, Fc N-glycans were first trimmed to the core GlcNAc-F state using Endo S (GlycINATOR®), thereby exposing the core fucose. Restricted afucosylation of trimmed GlcNAcF:GlcNAcF glyco-pairs was performed by adding 1 unit of α1–2,4,6 Fucosidase O (NEB, Ipswich, MA, USA) per 5 µg antibody and incubation in 1x GlycoBuffer 1 (5 mM CaCl2, 50 mM sodium acetate pH 5.5) for about 1 h at 37°C. The reactions progress was monitored by reversed-phase chromatography (RPC)-MS. Incubations were terminated by directly initiating the subsequent transglycosylation step once the desired GlcNAc-F:GlcNAc distribution had formed. Transglycosylation was then carried out by addition of TransINATOR™ together with the respective oxazoline glycan donor, which restored homogeneous complex-type glycans and simultaneously quenched further afucosylation. For hypergalactosylation of complex type N-glycans, a ratio of 30 µg β1–4 galactosyltransferase 1 (MilliporeSigma) per 1 mg of mAb was incubated in 100 mM MES buffer pH 6.5 containing 10 mM UDP-galactose and 20 mM MnCl2 for 16 h at 37°C. Selective trimming of fucosylated complex type glycans was done with Endo F3 (NEB) according to the manufacturers protocol and increased incubation time to last overnight. For the generation of asymmetrical non-fucosylated and high-mannose glyco-pairs, rituximab Fc glycans were trimmed to the core GlcNAc using Endo S combined with α1–6 fucosidase treatment to remove core fucose. Transglycosylation was performed with combinations of G0/G2 or G0/M5 oxazoline donors; G0 and G2 were added simultaneously, whereas for G0/M5 the reaction was staged by adding M5 with half of the enzyme 20 min prior to addition of G0 with the remaining enzyme to counteract preferential G0 transfer.

Intact RPC-MS of mAbs

A BioAccord LC-MS system (Waters, Milford, MA, USA), comprising an ACQUITY UPLC I-Class PLUS system and an electrospray ionization time-of-flight-based ACQUITY RDa detector, controlled by UNIFI Scientific Information Software (Waters) was utilized. Reversed phase chromatography was conducted using a BioResolve RP mAb Polyphenyl Column (Waters) at 80°C and a flow rate of 0.25 mL/min. Mobile phase A was 0.1% formic acid (FA) in water and mobile phase B was 0.09% FA in IPA. The system was equilibrated with 15% B prior to injection of 2 µg mAb sample. 15% B was held for 1 min before a linear gradient to 35% B in 8 min followed by a washing step at 95% B for 1 min and re-equilibration at 15% B for 3 min. Elution was monitored using UV detection at 214 nm. MS data were acquired in full scan mode within a mass range of 400 to 7000 m/z at positive polarity, using a 2 Hz scan rate, 140 V cone voltage, 1.5 kV capillary voltage, 550°C desolvation temperature, and standard lock mass correction mode. Data analysis was performed with the UNIFI Scientific Information System using the MaxEnt1 deconvolution algorithm.

SEC of mAbs

SEC was performed on a Waters ACQUITY Arc system using a ACQUITY UPLC BEH200 (4.6 × 300 mm, 1.7 µm, Waters) column. Analysis was carried out in isocratic elution mode at 0.3 mL/min with a mobile phase consisting of 40 mM sodium phosphate and 400 mM sodium perchlorate at pH 6.8 and at room temperature. Samples were diluted to a concentration of 1 to 5 mg/mL in mobile phase and 15–30 µg were injected. Elution was monitored with UV detection at 280 nm.

FcγRIIIa affinity chromatography

Analytical and preparative FcγRIIIa AC was performed on a Waters ACQUITY Arc system equipped with a Waters Fraction Manager Analytical. A TSKgel® FcR-IIIA-NPR column (TOSOH Bioscience, Griesheim, Germany) was used at 25°C and a flow rate of 0.65 mL/min. Sodium acetate/citric acid buffers pH 6.5 (A) and 4.0 (B) containing 150 mM NaCl were prepared according to Bouvarel et al. 40 After injection, the separation started with a hold at 0% B for 2 min, followed by a linear gradient to 55% at a steepness of 1.8% B/min. The column was then washed with 100% B for 3 min and re-equilibrated at 0% B for 5 min. Elution was monitored with intrinsic fluorescence detection with an excitation wavelength of 280 nm and emission wavelength of 350 nm for analytical AC, and with UV detection at 280 nm for preparative application.

aSEC of hFcγRs

For aSEC, 10 µg of protein were injected into an analytical SEC column, ACQUITY UPLC Protein BEH SEC Column, 200 Å, 1.7 µm, 4.6 mm × 150 mm (part no: 186,005,225, Waters), using a Waters UPLC-system. The system was run at 0.5 ml/min for 5 min and the mobile phase consisted of 50 mM Na-Phosphate, 200 mM Arginine-HCl, 0.05% Na-Azide; pH 6.8. The elution of hFcγR was detected via absorbance measurements at 280 nm using a UV absorbance detector. BioRad molecular weight standards were used to calculate the molecular weight based on retention times. Percentage of monomer is reported in Table S2.

Analytical ultracentrifugation of hFcγRs

For AUC of hFcγRs, experiments were performed with a Beckman XL-I analytical ultracentrifuge (Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA). On the day of experiment, samples stored at −80°C were thawed, mixed gently by pipette, and diluted to a concentration resulting in an absorbance signal within the linear range of the detector. Samples were loaded into the sample sectors of charcoal-filled epon cells with sapphire or quartz windows, and matched buffers were used in the reference sector of the cell. Experiments were carried out using an analytical four-hole titanium An-60 Ti rotor (Beckman Coulter). The calibration counterweight was placed in hole 4. An initial scan at 3000 rpm was performed to verify cell integrity and to perform a radial calibration for the instrument. Also, a wavelength scan was performed on each cell at 3000 rpm to verify absorbance. Samples were allowed to reach temperature equilibrium at 20°C for at least 2 h before initiation of centrifugation at high speed, typically 40,000 to 60,000 rpm. The data scans of each cell reporting concentration as a function of radial position were collected by absorbance scanning at 280 nm with a radial step size of 0.003 cm, continuous scanning, 1 flash per channel.

A subset of the collected scans was analyzed by selecting the first scan to about the 10th scan after the sample had pelleted. Data was analyzed, producing continuous c(s) distributions using SEDFIT version 15.01b.3 The sample vbar, signal of average sedimentation coefficient (S), as well as percentage of monomer is reported in Table S2.

LC-MS of hFcγRs

For LC-MS of hFcγRs, material was prepared with a Tecan Freedom Evo and then analyzed at both intact and subunit levels. Each sample was normalized to 1 mg/mL and then incubated with PNGaseF to generate de-glycosylated intact molecules. Intact and de-glycosylated intact molecules were reduced by tris(2-chloroethyl) phosphate. Separate injections were made of native (intact or reduced) and PNGaseF-treated (de-glycosylated intact or reduced) molecules. Each injection was desalted and introduced into the mass spectrometer using an Agilent 1290 HPLC with a 1.0 mm by 30 mm POROSHELL 300SB-C3 reversed phase column. A 2.5-minute binary gradient consisting of mobile phase A (98.9% water, 1% acetonitrile, 0.1% formic acid) and mobile phase B (70% isopropanol, 20% acetonitrile, 9.9% water, and 0.1% formic acid) increasing from 5% to 90% of mobile phase B at 1.20 ml/min was used to trap, desalt, and elute the protein from the column. Mass spectra data of the eluted material were acquired using an Agilent 6545XT LC/Q-TOF and processed using the maximum entropy algorithm within the Mass Hunter analysis software (Agilent). All hFcγRs matched the expected amino acid sequences.

hFcγr SPR analysis

SPR was performed using a Biacore 8K+ and a Series S CM5 sensor chip (Cat No. 29,104,988, Cytiva). The running buffer for these experiments and all dilutions were performed in 1x HBS-EP+ prepared by diluting 10x HBS-EP+ (Cat. No. BR100669, Cytiva) in pharmaceutical grade water and 0.22 µm filtering. Pierce Protein AG (Cat. No. 21,186, Thermo Fisher Scientific) was immobilized to the surface of a CM5 sensor chip according to the manufacturer’s instructions. In short, using the Amine Coupling Kit (Cat. No. BR100050, Cytiva) the CM5 sensor chip was activated with an equal mixture of 1-ethyl-3-(3-dimethylaminopropyl)carbodiimide hydrochloride and N-hydroxysuccinimide for 420 s at a flow rate of 10 µl/min. Protein AG, diluted to 10 µg/ml in 10 mM sodium acetate buffer pH 4.5 (Cat. No BR100350, Cytiva), was immobilized for 420 s at a flowrate of 10 µl/min resulting in 1000 to 1500 RU on the chip surface. The sensor chip was deactivated with 1 M ethanolamine HCl for 420 s at 10 µl/min. The sensor chip was stabilized with 15 start-up cycles at the beginning of each run.

To prevent buffer mismatch, FcγRIIa-R131, FcγRIIb, FcγRIIIa-V158, and FcγRIIIb were concentrated using Amicon Ultra centrifugal filters with 10 kDa molecular weight cutoff (Cat. No. UFC8010, MilliporeSigma) and proteins were reassayed for percentage monomer via aSEC prior to proceeding with assay (Table S 2). All subsequent steps were performed at a temperature of 4°C to detect binding differences that might be missed at higher temperatures. Antibodies were captured on the Protein A/G surface at a concentration of 1 µg/mL for 120 s at a flowrate of 10 µL/min resulting in 245 to 975 RU on the chip surface. The analytes, FcγRs, were diluted 10-fold with a 1:2 dilution factor in running buffer with the following starting concentrations: 20 µM for FcγRIIIb, 0.2 µM for FcγRI, and 10 µM for the remaining receptors. Varying concentrations of analytes were injected over the captured ligand for 120 s at a flowrate of 30 µL/min followed by a dissociation of 120 to 650 s. Two injections of 0.85% phosphoric acid for 30 s at a flowrate of 30 µl/min were used to regenerate the surface.

Each glycoform was tested in three independently prepared experiments except for G0F:G2, which only had enough material for ADCC Jurkat reporter assay, and G2F:G0, which only had enough material for one SPR run along with Jurkat reporter assay.

Evaluation was performed using Biacore Insight Evaluation software v5.0.18.22102. Raw sensorgram data was processed by subtracting signals from the reference flow cell (flow cell 1) and blank injections (either HBS-EP+ buffer or 0 nM analyte). Steady-state binding affinities were determined for FcγRIIb and FcγRIIa-R131 by plotting response units versus analyte concentration and fitting the data assuming a 1:1 binding interaction. For the remaining receptors, sensorgrams were analyzed using global fitting to a 1:1 kinetic binding model, yielding the kinetic constants: on rate (ka), off rate (kd), and affinity (KD).

ADCC Jurkat effector cell reporter gene assay

To assess receptor binding in a cellular context, an ADCC reporter assay consisting of Raji B cell lymphoma (ATCC CCL-86) as target cells and genetically engineered Jurkat T cells stably expressing FcγRIIIa-V158 receptor that signals through the NFAT response element driving expression of firefly luciferase (Cat. No. G7010, Promega, Fitchburg, WI, USA) as the effector cells. Raji cells were cultivated in RPMI 1640 with L-glutamine (Cat. No. 11,875,093, Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific) supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (HI FBS, Cat. No. 10,082–147, Gibco). Jurkat cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 with L-glutamine supplemented with 100 µg/mL hygromycin B (Cat. No. 10,687,010, Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific), 250 μg/ml Antibiotic G-418 Sulfate Solution (Genetecin, Cat. No. 10,131–035, Gibco), 1 mM sodium pyruvate (Cat. No. 11,360–070, Gibco), and 0.1 mM MEM nonessential amino acids (Cat. No. 11,140–050, Gibco). All cell culture and ADCC assays were performed at 37°C, 5% CO2.

The ADCC assay was initially optimized using effector:target cell ratios ranging from 6:1 to 12:1 using the manufacturer’s recommended 96-well protocol for a final E:T ratio selection of 6:1 (data not shown). A Hamilton Microlab STAR liquid handler was used to transition to a 384-well format by creating a drug dilution plate with a top concentration of 6 µg/mL (3x top tested conc. of 2 µg/mL) diluted 10-fold in RPMI 1640 + 4% Hyclone Super Low IgG FBS (ADCC Assay Buffer, Cat. No. SH3089803HI, Cytiva) using a 1:5 dilution factor. Raji and Jurkat cells were centrifuged and washed with PBS prior to resuspension in ADCC Assay buffer. An intermediate plate (Cat. No. P-96-450 V-C-S, Axygen, Corning, Corning, NY, USA) was first created consisting of 40 μL each of drug followed by target cells (approx. 4,375 cells/well in assay plate) and effector cells (approx. 26,250 cells/well in assay plate) for a total volume of 120 μL. After mixing the intermediate plate thoroughly, the final 384-well assay plate (Cat. No. 3824, Corning) was prepared by transferring 20 μL to each of four wells in a quadrant for each condition or concentration tested. Assay plates were incubated at 37°C, 5% CO2 for 6 h. At the end of incubation, assay plates were removed from incubator and allowed to equilibrate to room temperature for 10 min prior to adding 20 μL of room temperature BioGlo Luciferase Assay reagent (Cat. No. G7940, Promega) to each well and incubating for 5 min before reading luminescence on a PerkinElmer EnVision (Model number 2104 software v1.14.3049.1534) plate reader. Each glycoform was tested in three independently prepared experiments. Raw RLU data was exported and analyzed in GraphPad Prism v10.4.1 using the analysis log(agonist) vs. response – Variable slope (four parameters) to generate nonlinear fits and calculate EC50 values.
