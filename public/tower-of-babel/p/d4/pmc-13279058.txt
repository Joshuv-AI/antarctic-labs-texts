Increased Risk of Disabilities in Children and Adolescents With Congenital Heart Disease of Any Severity
Authors: Samantha J. Lain, Wen‐Qiang He, Gillian M. Blue, David S. Winlaw, Gary Sholler, Edwin P. Kirk, Sally L. Dunwoodie, David S. Celermajer, Natasha Nassar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe aim of this study was to determine the prevalence of overall and specific disability service use among children with congenital heart disease (CHD) compared with matched controls and siblings.MethodsThis was a retrospective data‐linkage study of children diagnosed with CHD (excluding chromosomal abnormality/syndrome and extracardiac congenital anomalies) in a hospital in New South Wales, Australia from 2002 to 2014. CHD was stratified into severity groups based on CHD diagnosis and age at cardiac procedure. Disability services data identified intellectual disability, autism, sensory, speech, learning, and physical disability. Multivariable logistic regression models, adjusted for clinical and sociodemographic factors, were used to examine odds of disability compared with children without CHD, with generalized estimating equations used in sibling models.ResultsThere were 4420 children with CHD, 37 931 without CHD, and 4606 siblings. The proportion receiving a disability service was 7.0% for severe CHD, 5.6% for other CHD with a cardiac procedure, and 5.1% for nonsevere CHD, compared with 2.5% children without CHD and 3.3% siblings. Adjusted odds ratios (aORs) of any disability service compared with those without CHD were as follows: severe CHD (aOR, 2.82 [95% CI, 2.19–3.63]), other CHD with a procedure (aOR, 2.20 [95% CI, 1.58–3.05]), and nonsevere CHD (aOR, 1.75 [95% CI, 1.42–2.15]). These associations remained in sibling analyses for severe CHD (aOR, 2.47 [95% CI, 1.57–3.88) and other CHD with a procedure (aOR, 1.63 [95% CI, 1.02–2.61]) but not nonsevere CHD.ConclusionsChildren with CHD, regardless of severity, had a significantly higher risk of receiving a disability service than the general population and a greater risk for those who had undergone a cardiac procedure compared with their siblings.

Congenital heart disease (CHD), a collective term for structural abnormalities of the heart and associated large blood vessels present at birth, is the most common congenital anomaly. 1 , 2 There have been many medical and surgical advances in the past few decades that have increased the survival rate of children with CHD, with >95% surviving to adulthood. 3 However, the impact of this disease persists through the life span. Not only are medical problems prevalent, such as hemodynamic and electrical conduction abnormalities, but many adult patients with CHD have special health care needs, including disabilities. 4

One of the most common disabilities affecting the lives of individuals with CHD is neurodevelopmental disorders, including intellectual disability (ID), autism, and cerebral palsy, estimated to occur in 10% to 40% of cases. 5 However, the prevalence of other disabilities, such as physical disabilities and sensory impairment, among people with CHD has not been well characterized. Previous population surveys have been biased due to reliance on self‐report of disability and low response rates, 4 , 6 whereas other population‐based studies lack clinical data on extracardiac congenital anomalies or other comorbidities that may impact the need for disability services. 7 , 8 Population‐based data on the disability needs of people with CHD are required to understand their service requirements.

This study therefore aims to examine the prevalence of use of disability services among children with CHD compared with the general population and siblings, including across different phenotypes of CHD and by type of disability.

METHODS

Due to regulations on confidentiality of data by the New South Wales Ministry of Health and New South Wales Department of Family and Community Services, individual records used in this study will not be available for sharing.

Study Population and Data Sources

A population‐based cohort study was conducted of children born between January 1, 2002, and December 31, 2014, in New South Wales, the most populous state in Australia, with a recorded diagnosis of CHD before age 1 year. Children who died before age 5 years were excluded.

The study population was identified using record linkage of New South Wales Perinatal Data Collection (birth data) and the New South Wales Admitted Patient Data Collection (hospital data). The Perinatal Data Collection is a population‐based surveillance of all births in New South Wales of at least 400 g birth weight or 20 weeks’ gestation. Each birth record includes information about both the infant and mother. The hospital data include all hospital discharge records from public and private hospitals in New South Wales. The principal diagnosis and up to 50 secondary diagnoses for each admission are coded on the basis of the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision, Australian Modification (ICD‐10‐AM). All inpatient procedures are also recorded and coded according to the Australian Classification of Health Interventions. Individual records from each of the data sources were linked by the New South Wales Centre for Health Record Linkage using probabilistic linkage methods, with a linkage rate of 99% between birth and hospital data.

The hospital and birth data were also linked to the New South Wales Family and Community Services Disability Dataset (disability services). Disability services data were available from July 1, 2003, to December 31, 2017. During the study period, under the National Disability Agreement, data from agencies providing publicly funded disability services were collected. 9

Study Exposure and Comparison Groups

Infants with CHD were identified as having at least 1 recorded diagnosis of CHD (ICD‐10‐AM codes from Q20 to Q26.9), excluding isolated patent ductus arteriosus (Q25.0), in their hospital record from birth to age 1 year. Children with “unspecified CHD” (Q24.9) were also excluded, as this code has been previously shown to have poor reliability in identifying CHD in hospital admission data. 10 Children were identified as having a syndrome, chromosomal abnormality, or extracardiac congenital anomaly if they had a recorded diagnosis in a hospital record in the first year of life (ICD‐10‐AM codes from Q‐chapter 11 ; see Data S1). The frequency of disability service use was examined for this group of children with CHD and additional anomalies, and then they were excluded from further analysis.

Infants with CHD without a syndrome, chromosomal abnormality, or extracardiac congenital anomaly were grouped into 3 categories based on severity of CHD diagnosis and age at the first cardiac procedure. Severe CHD was classified as those with a diagnosis from a previously defined list of ICD‐10 codes 12 and had undergone a first cardiac procedure before age 1 year (Data S1). The list of relevant cardiac procedures and related codes was developed with clinicians and previously applied. 13 The other CHD diagnoses, those with a diagnosis code not defined as severe, were divided into 2 groups: those who underwent a first cardiac procedure before age 5 years (other CHD with a cardiac procedure), and those without a cardiac procedure (nonsevere CHD) (Data S1). These groups were separated on the basis of the need for a cardiac procedure, as it may infer severity; however, a record of a cardiac procedure also validates that the CHD diagnosis is true. To ensure that those with “nonsevere CHD” were a true case, they were included only if they had >1 admission with a CHD diagnosis or the CHD diagnosis was recorded in the primary or first 2 secondary diagnosis fields of the admission record.

Children with CHD were compared with children without CHD and with siblings, also without a syndrome, chromosomal abnormality, or other congenital anomaly. For each child with CHD, 5 children without CHD were identified from birth data matched for sex, gestational age, and year of birth. Through the birth data, siblings were identified among live births if they had the same mother.

Study Outcomes

Disability service use was defined on the basis of children with a record in the disability services data set, which includes a record for each occasion of disability service an individual receives. Information recorded includes the primary and significant other disability service group (Data S1). There are 6 disability service groups that relate to a broad categorization of disabilities in terms of underlying health condition, impairment, and activity limitations; ID, autism spectrum disorder, learning disability (specific learning/attention deficit disorder), physical disability, and speech and sensory disability (vision, hearing, deaf–blind impairment).

Children can have multiple primary disability groups recorded during different occasions of service, so they are included in >1 disability service group. However, as children are more likely to receive a service for an additional disability once they are already receiving a disability service, a sensitivity analysis was performed examining use of disability service groups when those who received a service for autism and intellectual disability were excluded. There is significant evidence that children with CHD have increased risk of neurodevelopmental disorders, 14 so autism and ID were excluded to see if the proportion who received services for learning disability, physical disability, and speech and sensory disabilities was impacted.

Confounding Factors

Information on potential prenatal and sociodemographic confounders associated with both CHD and disabilities 14 , 15 were available from various linked data sets. Variables ascertained from the birth record included infant sex, gestational age, 5‐minute Apgar score, maternal age, maternal smoking, and postcode at birth. The postcode at birth was used to classify socioeconomic status using the Socio‐Economic Index for Areas, which was reported in quintiles from the most disadvantaged (quintile 1) to the most advantaged (quintile 5). 16 From the children's hospital admission record, the total hospital length of stay in the first year of life were identified.

Statistical Analysis

The total frequency of services for any disability and by each disability group was examined for each CHD category, for children without CHD, and for siblings. As a large proportion of children with syndromes and chromosomal anomalies receive disability services, they were excluded from the subsequent statistical analyses. Multivariable logistic regression models, adjusting for confounders, were used to examine the association between CHD and disability. For the control comparison, the 3 CHD groups were included in the same model and compared with controls. For sibling comparison, separate models were applied and included only those children with CHD who had corresponding siblings with generalized estimating equations used to adjust for clustering within families. Odds ratios with 95% CIs were calculated. As the prevalence of the outcomes is <10%, odds ratios can be used as an approximation for risk ratios.

For both models, we first examined the crude association between CHD and any disability service and each type of disability service group. The models were then adjusted for potential confounding by sociodemographic variables, sex, year of birth, gestational age, and 5‐minute Apgar score at birth. For children with mild CHD, the total hospital length of stay during the first year of life was also adjusted for, as a proxy for infant morbidity.

Ethical approval was obtained from the New South Wales Population and Health Services Research Ethics Committee (2019/ETH11615). A waiver of consent for participants was received.

RESULTS

The study population comprised 4420 children with a CHD diagnosis without a related syndromic diagnosis, 37 931 matched children without CHD, and 4606 siblings (Figure 1). In addition, there were 1498 children with syndromic CHD and 466 children with CHD with an extracardiac congenital anomaly (Figure 1). More than half (54.8%) of children with syndromic CHD had a recorded disability, and 12.2% of children with CHD and an extracardiac anomaly received a disability service. There were 208 children with CHD without a syndrome or extracardiac anomaly who died before age 5 years and were excluded; 189 (90.1%) died in the first year of life, and 139 (66.8%) had a severe CHD diagnosis.

Overall Disability Service Use

Children with CHD had higher prevalence rates of all disability service use than children without CHD and siblings (Figure 2). The range of follow‐up time was 3 to 14 years, with the median age at first disability service ranging from 3.0 years for severe CHD to 3.6 years for children without CHD and 3.7 years for siblings. Seven percent of children with severe CHD, 5.6% of children with “other CHD with a procedure,” and 5.1% of children with nonsevere CHD received a disability service compared with 2.5% of those without CHD and 3.3% of siblings (Figure 2). Children with severe, other, and nonsevere CHD also have higher prevalence of receiving ≥3 disability services (2.3%, 1.6%, and 1.5%, respectively), compared with <1% of children without CHD and siblings (Table 2).

The odds of receiving a disability service for children with other CHD or severe CHD were 2.20 (95% CI, 1.58–3.05) and 2.82 (95% CI, 2.19–3.63) times higher than those without CHD, respectively, after adjustment for confounding factors (Table 2). When compared with siblings, these associations reduced but were still significant; children with severe CHD had an adjusted odds ratio (aOR) of 2.47 (95% CI, 1.57–3.88) and children with other CHD had an aOR of 1.63 (95% CI, 1.02–2.61) of receiving a disability service (Table 2). Children with nonsevere CHD had increased odds of receiving a disability service compared with children without CHD (aOR, 1.75 [95% CI, 1.42–2.15]); however, there was not a significant increase in odds compared with siblings (aOR, 1.16 [95% CI, 0.82–1.63]; Table 2). Smaller sample size for the sibling comparison (Table S2) resulted in less precise estimates and wider CIs for sibling models.

Intellectual Disability

Children with severe CHD had >5 times the odds (aOR, 5.25 [95% CI, 3.52–7.84]) of receiving a service for ID compared with those without CHD, although the odds more than halved when compared with siblings (aOR, 2.22 [95% CI, 1.16–4.28]; Figure 3), while children with other CHD had >3 times the odds of ID compared with children without CHD (aOR, 3.51 [95% CI, 2.07–5.94]) or siblings (aOR, 3.07 [95% CI, 1.25–7.58]; Figure 3). Nonsevere CHD had a significantly increased odds of a disability service for ID compared with children without CHD but not compared with siblings.

Autism and Learning and Speech Disability

Children with CHD had 1.5 to 2.5 times the odds of receiving a service for autism or learning or speech disability compared with those without CHD in the adjusted models (Figure 3). However, when compared with siblings, only children with severe CHD had significantly increased odds of a service for autism (aOR, 2.23 [95% CI, 1.16–4.28]). Children with nonsevere CHD did not have increased odds for receiving a service for autism (aOR, 0.94 [95% CI, 0.57–1.52]) or speech disability (aOR, 1.07 [95% CI, 0.64–1.81]) compared with siblings (Figure 3). Children with “other CHD with a procedure” had 3 times the odds of receiving a disability service for learning disabilities compared with children without CHD and twice the odds compared with siblings (Figure 3).

Sensory and Physical Disability

All children with CHD had increased odds of receiving a disability service for a sensory disability; however, due to small numbers, the analysis compared with siblings could not be undertaken. Children with severe CHD had >8 times the odds of receiving a service for a physical disability compared with both children without CHD (aOR, 8.82 [95% CI, 5.32–14.63]) and siblings (aOR, 8.16 [95% CI, 2.75–24.12]), while children with nonsevere CHD had 2.5 times the odds (aOR, 2.55 [95% CI, 1.54–4.23]) of a physical disability service compared with children without CHD (Figure 3).

In the sensitivity analyses, when examining disability service use for those without ID or autism, compared with children without CHD, similar patterns were observed to the models examining all disability service use. Children with severe CHD still had 5 times (aOR, 5.75 [95% CI, 2.86–11.58]) the odds of receiving a service for a physical disability (Table S3), and children with other CHD with a procedure had >3 times (aOR, 3.48 [95% CI, 1.60–7.55]) the odds of receiving a service for a learning disability.

DISCUSSION

In our study, one of the first population‐based studies that robustly identifies children with disabilities for which they have received a service and includes important clinical information that may impact disabilities, children with CHD had substantially increased odds of receiving a disability service, compared with matched children without CHD, including those with nonsevere CHD. After excluding those with identified syndromes and other congenital anomalies, children with severe CHD and other CHD had 2.2 to 2.8 times the odds of receiving a disability service compared with those without CHD; even children with nonsevere CHD had an ≈1.75 times increase in odds. Although the odds of different types of disability services were significantly increased among various CHD subtypes, all CHD groups had increased odds of services for ID, learning, speech, and sensory disabilities compared with children without CHD. The increased association with receiving a disability service remained for children with severe or other CHD with a procedure when compared with their siblings.

This study found that ≈7% of children with severe CHD received a disability service, compared with 2.5% of children without CHD. This may underreport the true prevalence of disability among children with CHD, as we recorded only publicly funded services, which may be received by children with severe disabilities. Two studies performed in schools in the United States found that 13% to 15% of children with CHD without chromosomal abnormalities or extracardiac congenital anomalies had a disability or received special education services but may overestimate rates. 8 , 17 As these studies were performed in the education setting and may be identified by teachers, they had higher rates of children with developmental delay, 8 learning disability, and speech/language impairment, 17 with potentially different thresholds and definitions compared with our study.

Although services received for some disability groups may be underestimated, the rates for services received for ID were similar to those receiving special education services for ID, 17 although the severity of ID could not be identified. Population studies have reported that children with severe and moderate CHD are 3.5 to 5 times more likely to have mild to moderate ID, 7 , 17 similar to results found in our study when examining severe and other CHD with a procedure compared with children without CHD and siblings. Children with CHD who have undergone cardiac procedures in infancy and early childhood will be exposed to known risk factors for neurocognitive deficits such as cardiopulmonary bypass, postoperative seizures, mechanical ventilation, and longer intensive care unit stay. 18 , 19 , 20 However, it seems unlikely that these factors fully account for the differences observed in this study; other factors, such as a shared genetic basis for CHD and neurocognitive phenotypes, are important.

Previous population‐based studies of children with isolated noncritical CHDs have contained limited clinical information; these have also shown a 3 to 5 times increased risk of ID compared with children without CHD. 7 , 8 , 17 Our study found that children with nonsevere CHD had increased odds of a disability service for ID compared with children without CHD but not compared with siblings. Children with nonsevere CHD in our study have not received a cardiac procedure before age 5 years but do have increased morbidity in the first year of life, with 20% having >14 days in the hospital before age 1 year. This increased contact with the health system allows more chance for a CHD diagnosis to be recorded, and therefore case identification may be vulnerable to ascertainment bias, as there may be missed cases in the control and sibling groups who were not admitted to the hospital in the first year or more false‐positive CHD cases recorded. When models were adjusted for hospital length of stay during the first year, those with nonsevere CHD still had increased odds of ID compared with children without CHD (aOR, 2.08 [95% CI, 1.43–3.03]); however, there still may be unmeasured confounding. Socioeconomic status and parental education of children with CHD are associated with neurocognitive outcomes. 21 When familial or shared environmental factors of the home are adjusted for in sibling models, there was no longer a significant association between nonsevere CHD and ID. This pattern is also seen for nonsevere CHD and use of disability services for autism, learning and speech disabilities, whereby the aORs compared with siblings are close to 1.

A number of other associations between CHD and disability were significant in the models comparing with children without CHD but not significant when compared with siblings, such as autism and speech impairment in patients with other CHD and nonsevere CHD. As such, shared genetic factors may be important. 22 However, sibling comparisons can contain biases, including a crossover effect, whereby the exposure or outcome of the first sibling influences the second sibling. 23 Frequent barriers to accessing government‐funded disability service include knowledge of the process and delays in diagnosis, 24 such that once 1 family member has received a disability service, access for a sibling may be more straightforward. This crossover effect will attenuate the association with disability services compared with siblings without CHD. The sibling models also have a smaller sample size, so some estimates are less precise with wide CIs.

The main strength of this study is the use of the population‐based, longitudinally linked data sets over 15 years. The use of routinely collected data avoids recall bias and loss to follow‐up and reduces potential selection bias, as even marginalized groups who are often excluded from research studies are included. To our knowledge, this is the first study to compare disabilities among children with CHD with both children without CHD and siblings. Sibling models are important to control for family and sociodemographic factors, such as individual‐level socioeconomic status and parental education, and for the triangulation of results between different epidemiological methods. 25 The main limitation of using routinely collected data in research is data quality. As such, to classify CHD severity, we have included age at cardiac procedure, as procedures are more reliably collected than diagnoses, 26 and there is no consensus on how to define severe CHD using ICD codes for research. 27 We also used a restricted definition of nonsevere CHD to minimize false‐positive cases. Given the observational design of our study, we do not suggest that the association between CHD and increased disability services is causal, and we cannot draw specific conclusions related to causal mechanisms between CHD and disabilities.

In conclusion, children with CHD have a significantly increased risk of using a disability service compared with children without CHD, and children with severe and moderate CHD have increased disability compared with siblings. The ascertainment of disabilities goes beyond the often‐reported neurodevelopmental disabilities, with children requiring services for physical and sensory disabilities, even after controlling for prematurity. Screening, identification, and early intervention for children with CHD and disabilities is recommended to improve long‐term outcomes. 5 Future research to explore specific comorbidities and clinical or genetic risk factors for different disabilities is required to improve the identification of at‐risk individuals.
