Causal links between multi-domain risk factors and intervertebral disc degeneration: a systematic review and meta-analysis of Mendelian randomization studies
Authors: Hang Zhang, Jingnan Tian, Yuchi Lu, Dongxun Yu, Hailong Wu, Yan Lu, Lianhe Yu, Zongjian Luo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe causal roles of modifiable risk factors in intervertebral disc degeneration (IVDD) remain incompletely understood. Mendelian randomization (MR) studies have advantages over traditional observational studies. However, given the heterogeneity of current MR study designs for IVDD, the causal estimates of these reported effects remain uncertain, thereby obscuring the true biological impact of factors such as bones, metabolism, inflammation, gut microbiota, and lifestyle on IVDD.PurposeThis study synthesized Mendelian randomization (MR) evidence to evaluate the causal relationship between exposure factors across multiple domains and intervertebral disc degeneration(IVDD).MethodsWe conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis of MR studies published up to September 30, 2025, with reporting completeness assessed using STROBE-MR. Twenty studies (12 “Excellent”, 8 “Good”) were included.ResultsHigher bone mineral density significantly increased IVDD risk, including total body BMD (OR = 1.20, 1.15–1.25), lumbar spine BMD (OR = 1.18, 1.13–1.24), heel eBMD (OR = 1.06, 1.03–1.10), and femoral neck BMD (OR = 1.16, 1.09–1.24). Inflammatory cytokines IFN-γ (OR = 0.87, 0.83–0.91) and IL-18 (OR = 0.96, 0.93–0.98) showed inverse associations with IVDD risk. Specific gut microbiota were causally implicated: genus Marvinbryantia (OR = 1.14, 1.07–1.21) and phylum Bacteroidetes (OR = 1.15, 1.06–1.24) increased risk, whereas Order Rhodospirillales (OR = 0.93, 0.90–0.96), Family Rhodospirillaceae (OR = 0.92, 0.88–0.97), and genus Ruminococcaceae UCG003 (OR = 0.92, 0.87–0.97) were protective. Key lifestyle and metabolic risk factors were time watching TV (OR = 1.78, 1.52–2.08), smoking initiation (OR = 1.22, 1.12–1.33), greater height (OR = 1.14, 1.09–1.20), larger waist circumference (OR = 1.26, 1.04–1.53), body mass index (OR = 1.26, 1.14–1.38), triglycerides (OR = 1.08, 1.03–1.13), and Type 2 diabetes (OR = 1.05, 1.03–1.07).ConclusionThis synthesis summarizes current MR evidence suggesting potential causal links between multi-domain factors and IVDD. Among modifiable lifestyle-related factors, genetically predicted time spent watching TV showed a particularly prominent association with IVDD risk. These findings may help prioritize hypotheses for future mechanistic and prevention-oriented studies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12891-026-10040-7.

Introduction

Intervertebral disc degeneration (IVDD) is a predominant underlying cause of low back pain, a condition that imposes a staggering global socioeconomic burden and is a leading contributor to years lived with disability worldwide [1, 2]. The pathophysiology of IVDD is multifactorial, involving a complex interplay of genetic predisposition, mechanical stress, inflammatory processes, and metabolic disturbances [3, 4]. Despite its high prevalence, the modifiable risk factors and causal mechanisms driving IVDD progression remain incompletely elucidated, hampering the development of effective preventive and therapeutic strategies.

Traditional observational studies have identified numerous associations between various exposures—such as obesity, lifestyle habits, and biochemical traits—and IVDD risk [5, 6]. However, inferring causality from these associations is notoriously challenging due to residual confounding and reverse causation [7]. Mendelian randomization (MR) has emerged as a powerful methodological approach to address these limitations. By using genetic variants as instrumental variables for exposures, MR can provide more reliable evidence for causal inferences, as genetic alleles are randomly assigned at conception and are generally independent of confounding environmental factors [8, 9].

The application of MR has rapidly expanded in the field of IVDD, with recent studies investigating the causal roles of a wide spectrum of factors [10, 11]. Nevertheless, the evidence from individual MR studies is often fragmented and sometimes inconsistent. These discrepancies may arise from differences in statistical power, genetic instrument selection, population ancestry, or methodological heterogeneity. A meta-analysis of MR studies offers a robust means of synthesizing these findings, enhancing statistical power and providing more precise and generalizable causal estimates [12, 13].

To address this critical knowledge gap, we conducted a comprehensive systematic review and meta-analysis of MR studies to evaluate the causal relationships between a wide range of exposures and IVDD. Our study possesses several key strengths and innovations: first, it integrates evidence across diverse exposure domains (Lifestyle, Bone mineral density(BMD), Gut microbiota, Metabolic, and Inflammatory), providing a unified causal landscape of IVDD. Second, it implements a rigorous quality assessment using the Strengthening the Reporting of Mendelian Randomization Studies (STROBE-MR) Guidelines, ensuring a transparent evaluation of the methodological robustness of included studies [14, 15]. Third, it directly addresses inconsistencies in prior research, yielding more stable causal estimates and clarifying uncertainties. By synthesizing published MR evidence, this study aimed to identify potentially causal risk and protective factors for IVDD and to inform future mechanistic, epidemiological, and intervention studies. The findings are intended to support hypothesis generation rather than provide immediate clinical guidance.

Methods

This systematic review and meta-analysis were conducted in accordance with the PRISMA-P 2020 guidelines. The study protocol was registered in the PROSPERO database (CRD420251169418).

Data sources and search strategy

The process illustrated in Fig. 1 outlines the procedures for literature retrieval and for inclusion and exclusion. A systematic literature search was conducted in electronic databases (including PubMed, Embase, Medline, Web of Science, and the China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI)) as of September 30, 2025. The search strategy was designed to identify all published Mendelian randomization studies investigating the association between any exposure and IVDD. The full search strategies for all databases are provided in the Supplementary Table 1. No restrictions on language or publication status were applied. Additionally, the reference lists of included articles and relevant review papers were manually screened to identify any potentially eligible studies that might have been missed.

Study Selection

Study selection criteria

Two investigators independently screened the titles, abstracts, and full texts according to the pre-defined criteria. Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion or by consulting a third reviewer.

The inclusion criteria: (1) employed an MR design to estimate the causal effect of any exposure on IVDD; (2) used genetic variants (single nucleotide polymorphisms, SNPs) as instrumental variables for the exposure; (3)Any studies that include MR as part of their analysis; and (4) reported summary-level data with effect estimates (e.g., odds ratios, ORs) and their corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) or standard errors.

The exclusion criteria: (1) non-MR studies (e.g., reviews, case reports); (2) studies where full text was unavailable or essential data could not be extracted; and (3) studies with overlapping or duplicate data.

Data extraction

A standardized data extraction form was used to collect relevant information from each included study by two independent reviewers. Extracted data included: first author, publication year, source of exposure and outcome data (e.g., GWAS consortium, cohort), population ancestry, number of instrumental variables, MR analysis methods (the main type: inverse-variance weighted, IVW), and the causal estimate with its 95% confidence interval and P-value. The extracted result data are shown in Table 1 and Supplementary Tables 6–10. Because the included studies reported different MR designs and multiple MR estimators, we prespecified an extraction hierarchy. For the primary meta-analysis, we extracted the forward exposure to IVDD estimate from univariable MR analyses using the inverse-variance weighted (IVW) method, as IVW was the primary estimator in most original studies. Estimates from MR-Egger, weighted median, weighted mode, multivariable MR, mediation MR, and reverse-direction MR analyses were not pooled in the primary analysis. For each association, the MR design, MR direction, extracted estimator, and effect unit or scale were recorded in Supplementary Tables 6–10.

Assessment of reporting completeness

The reporting completeness of each included MR study were critically appraised using the STROBE-MR Guidelines [14]. The assessment tool comprised 50 items across multiple domains: Title and Abstract, Introduction, Methods (Study Design, Variable Definitions, Statistical Methods), Results, Discussion, and Other Information(Supplementary Table 4). Each item was scored on a 3-point scale (0 = not reported, 1 = partially reported, 2 = fully reported), with particular attention paid to critical items such as the assessment of instrumental variable strength (e.g., F-statistic), MR methods, pleiotropy testing, sensitivity analyses, and the discussion of limitations. The overall quality score was calculated, and studies were graded as Excellent (≥ 85 points), Good (70–84 points), Fair (55–69 points), Poor (40–54 points), or very Poor (< 40 points)(Supplementary Table 5) [36]. Discrepancies in scoring were resolved through discussion to reach a consensus by two independent reviewers. The reporting completeness of each included MR study was assessed using the STROBE-MR checklist. STROBE-MR was used as a reporting guideline rather than a formal risk-of-bias assessment tool. Therefore, higher STROBE-MR scores were interpreted as indicating better adherence to recommended reporting items, but not necessarily lower risk of bias. Potential sources of bias, including weak instruments, horizontal pleiotropy, sample overlap, population stratification, and outcome misclassification, were considered qualitatively when interpreting the findings. STROBE-MR scores were not used to weight studies in the meta-analysis or as exclusion criteria.

Statistical analysis

Meta-analysis was performed for each unique exposure that was investigated by two or more studies. The pooled odds ratio (OR) and its 95% confidence interval (CI) were calculated using the inverse-variance weighted method. Heterogeneity was quantified using the I² statistic and Cochran’s Q test, with I² > 50% or a P-value for the Q test < 0.05 indicating substantial heterogeneity. A fixed-effects model was applied when between-study heterogeneity was low (I² ≤ 50%, P > 0.05); otherwise, a random-effects model was used. To assess the robustness of the pooled estimates, sensitivity analyses, including leave-one-out analysis, were conducted. Funnel plots and Egger’s regression tests were not performed because the number of MR estimates available for each exposure was limited. Under these conditions, formal tests for funnel plot asymmetry would have low statistical power and could yield unstable or misleading results. All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3.3) with the meta package. A two-sided P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. For continuous exposures, pooled ORs represent the change in IVDD odds per genetically predicted increase in the exposure on the scale reported by the original GWAS or MR study. For binary exposures, such as smoking initiation and type 2 diabetes, pooled ORs represent the effect of genetically predicted liability to the exposure. When the original study did not explicitly report a clinical unit, the effect scale was described according to the original GWAS/MR scale.

Results

Study selection and reporting assessment

The initially retrieved database yielded 373 records using the pre-established retrieval strategy. However, upon closer inspection, only 20 articles were ultimately included in this review, as illustrated in Fig. 1 [16–35].

Based on the STROBE-MR checklist, the reporting completeness of the 20 included MR studies was generally good. Twelve studies were rated as “Excellent” and eight as “Good” according to our predefined reporting score categories. These ratings reflect adherence to reporting items rather than a formal assessment of risk of bias. Common reporting strengths included well-structured abstracts, detailed methodology, and sensitivity analyses. Frequently underreported items included sample overlap, participant flow, baseline characteristics, data availability statements, and covariate definitions. (Supplementary Table 3).

Study characteristics

This systematic review and meta-analysis encompassed a total of 20 Mendelian randomization studies that investigated the causal effects of various exposures on intervertebral disc degeneration(Table 1). All studies utilized summary-level data from large-scale genome-wide association studies (GWAS), with the vast majority of participants being of European ancestry. The sample sizes of the underlying GWAS cohorts for both exposures and outcomes varied substantially, ranging from approximately 8,300 to over 800,000 individuals.

The included studies sourced their genetic instruments from a variety of publicly available consortia and biobanks, including the IEU OpenGWAS project, UK Biobank, FinnGen, the GEFOS consortium, MiBioGen, and others. The exposures under investigation were diverse, covering domains such as lifestyle factors, body composition metrics, metabolic traits, inflammatory biomarkers, and gut microbiota.

All studies provided comprehensive information on the genetic instruments and sample sizes. The primary statistical method employed for the Mendelian randomization analysis was the IVW method. To ensure the robustness of the findings, the majority of studies conducted extensive sensitivity analyses. These included weighted median and MR-Egger regression methods to test for and account for potential horizontal pleiotropy. This rigorous methodological approach enhances the reliability and validity of the causal inferences drawn from this body of evidence, although they do not eliminate potential bias in the MR estimates. Details of the extracted MR estimate, MR direction, and effect unit or scale for each included association are provided in Supplementary Tables 6–10.

Meta-analysis results

The relationship between BMD and IVDD

Our meta-analysis of Mendelian randomization studies elucidated the causal relationships between various BMD phenotypes and IVDD, as illustrated in Fig. 2 and Supplementary Fig. 1. The results demonstrate that total body bone mineral density(TB BMD) is a significant risk factor for IVDD (OR:1.20 [1.15–1.25]). Similarly, elevated lumbar spine bone mineral density (LS BMD) (OR:1.18 [1.13–1.24]) and femoral neck bone mineral density (FN BMD) (OR:1.16[1.09–1.24]) also served as significant risk factors. A significant, though modest, risk-increasing effect was also identified for heel bone mineral density (eBMD) (OR:1.06 [1.03–1.10]). In contrast, the analysis indicated no evidence for a causal role of forearm bone mineral density(FA BMD) (OR:1.11 [0.93–1.32]), which was neither a risk nor a protective factor.

The results of the sensitivity analysis indicated that after repeatedly deleting individual studies, the estimated impact of all important risk factors remained stable and statistically significant(Supplementary Fig. 7). Meanwhile, heterogeneity was low across all BMD analyses: TB BMD (I² = 0%, P = 0.9977), LS BMD (I² = 0%, P = 0.9302), FN BMD (I² = 0%, P = 0.9945), FA BMD (I² = 0%, P = 0.9967), and eBMD (I² = 0%, P = 0.9809). Therefore, fixed-effect models were used for these analyses (Supplementary Table 2). Meanwhile, the close alignment between individual and pooled estimates further strengthens the reliability of these causal inferences.

The relationship between inflammatory factors and IVDD

The meta-analysis of inflammatory factors identified inverse associations between IL-18 and IFN-γ levels and IVDD risk, as illustrated in Fig. 3 and Supplementary Fig. 2. The levels of Interleukin-18 (IL-18) were associated with a significant reduction in the odds of IVDD (OR:0.96[0.93–0.98]). A stronger inverse association was observed for Interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) (OR:0.87[0.83–0.91]).

The pooled estimates for both cytokines were consistent with the direction of effects in the individual studies, and the forest plots demonstrated a high degree of result alignment. Furthermore, no substantial heterogeneity was observed for IFN-γ (I² = 0%, P = 0.7656) or IL-18 (I² = 0%, P = 0.4392), and fixed-effect models were used (Supplementary Table 2). Meanwhile, sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of these causal estimates(Supplementary Fig. 8).

The relationship between metabolic factors and IVDD

The meta-analysis of metabolic factors identified several significant causal risk factors for IVDD, as illustrated in Fig. 4 and Supplementary Fig. 3. Body mass index (BMI) demonstrated a substantial risk-increasing effect (OR:1.26[1.14–1.38]). Similarly, elevated levels of triglycerides (OR:1.08[1.03–1.13]) and the presence of Type 2 diabetes (T2D) (OR:1.05[1.03–1.07]) were also established as significant risk factors.

The meta-analysis of gut microbiome factors revealed distinct causal roles for specific bacterial taxa in IVDD, as illustrated in Fig. 5 and Supplementary Fig. 4. Genus Marvinbryantia (OR: 1.14[1.07–1.21]) and Phylum Bacteroidetes (OR:1.15[1.06–1.24]) were identified as significant risk factors for IVDD. Discussion

This meta-analysis of published MR studies systematically evaluated the causal relationships between multiple exposures—including BMD, gut microbiota, inflammatory cytokines, lifestyle behaviors, and metabolic factors—and IVDD. Overall, we found that TB BMD, LS BMD, eBMD, and FN BMD were positively associated with IVDD risk. Certain gut microbial taxa (e.g., Marvinbryantia, Bacteroidetes) increased IVDD risk, whereas others (Rhodospirillales, Rhodospirillaceae, Ruminococcaceae UCG003) showed protective effects. IL-18 and IFN-γ exhibited inverse associations with IVDD. Lifestyle factors and metabolic factors, including sedentary behavior, smoking, height, waist circumference, BMI, triglycerides, and T2D, also showed significant causal effects.

Bone mineral density and IVDD

Our MR meta-analysis showed that higher systemic and site-specific BMD was causally associated with increased IVDD risk. This finding aligns with several recent MR studies and genetic epidemiological analyses. However, clinical observations suggest that IVDD predominantly affects middle-aged and elderly individuals, among whom BMD typically declines—especially in postmenopausal women [37, 38]. Several epidemiological and imaging studies have also reported the coexistence of low BMD/osteoporosis and spinal degenerative changes [39, 40]. However, we believe that this is not a real contradiction, but rather that BMD may play a “two-way role” in intervertebral disc health.

First, the lifelong genetic predisposition and age-related changes in BMD represent different time scales. MR estimates reflect the long-term effect of an individual’s lifelong genetic predisposition on BMD and its impact on IVDD [25], whereas the co-occurrence of low BMD and degeneration observed in clinical cohorts among older adults is largely driven by age-accumulated factors such as hormonal changes (e.g., postmenopausal estrogen decline), metabolic alterations, and cumulative mechanical loading [41]. Therefore, these two lines of evidence can coexist without contradiction. Second, measurement artifacts may inflate local BMD values in degenerative spines. Osteophytes and endplate sclerosis can artificially elevate lumbar BMD, potentially creating the appearance of “higher BMD” in subjects with advanced degeneration [42, 43]. Third, increased vertebral stiffness alters load transmission across the endplate–disc interface, promoting endplate microdamage, impaired nutrient diffusion, cell dysfunction, and disc matrix breakdown [44, 45]. Our findings challenge the simplistic notion that “higher bone mineral density is always better for the spine.” However, these findings should not be interpreted as indicating that high BMD alone warrants clinical surveillance or intervention for IVDD. Rather, they suggest that the relationship between BMD and disc degeneration may be more complex than traditionally assumed and should be further evaluated in longitudinal imaging cohorts and mechanistic studies. Potential measurement confounding in BMD assessment should also be considered, as degenerative spinal changes such as osteophytes and endplate or subchondral sclerosis may inflate DXA-based BMD measurements, particularly at the lumbar spine. Therefore, the MR association between genetically predicted BMD and IVDD should not be interpreted as definitive evidence that higher true bone mass directly causes disc degeneration. Additionally, although age may modify the relationship between BMD and IVDD, the present study could not perform age-stratified analyses because the original MR studies did not consistently provide age-stratified estimates. Thus, the proposed explanation regarding lifelong genetic predisposition and age-related BMD decline should be considered hypothesis-generating and requires validation in future age-stratified MR studies and longitudinal imaging cohorts.

Inflammatory cytokines and IVDD

In this meta-analysis, IL-18 and IFN-γ demonstrated protective effects against IVDD—a result that appears inconsistent with the conventional understanding that inflammation promotes disc degeneration. Emerging mechanistic studies suggest that the role of inflammatory cytokines within intervertebral disc tissue is dynamic and context-dependent, exerting either protective or detrimental effects influenced by the specific disease stage and the local immune and tissue microenvironment.

We believe it can be explained by the following aspects. Firstly, Transient and moderate inflammation may facilitate debris clearance, immune modulation, and early repair. As a hallmark cytokine of Th1 cell responses, IFN-γ enhances macrophage activity, thereby promoting the clearance of apoptotic cells and facilitating local repair. IL-18 also plays a key role in inducing immune responses from T cells and natural killer cells, thereby modulating the immune system and potentially promoting the repair process in degenerated disc tissue [46, 47]. Furthermore, chronic and excessive inflammation may accelerate tissue degeneration and pathological progression. Specifically, IL-18 and IFN-γ, when involved in sustained inflammatory processes, can trigger excessive matrix degradation and cell apoptosis. Under persistent immune stimulation, these cytokines may promote the breakdown of the disc’s extracellular matrix by activating degrading enzymes such as Matrix Metalloproteinases (MMPs), ultimately leading to further structural deterioration of the intervertebral disc [3, 48, 49]. Finally, the biological effects of inflammatory cytokines on the disc are closely linked to the polarization state of immune cells. The polarization of macrophages into the M1 (pro-inflammatory) or M2 (pro-repair) phenotype exerts markedly different influences on disc degeneration. IFN-γ typically drives the polarization towards the M1 phenotype, which primarily amplifies the local immune response through the release of pro-inflammatory factors. In contrast, within a more balanced immune microenvironment, IFN-γ may alternatively enhance the reparative functions of M2-like macrophages, thereby facilitating tissue repair and regeneration [49]. Additionally, research indicates that the role of IL-18 may be beneficial for tissue repair during the early stages of inflammation. However, in the context of persistently high inflammatory levels, it is likely to contribute to accelerated tissue destruction [47, 50].

Integrating the current evidence, the inverse associations observed for IL-18 and IFN-γ may suggest context-dependent roles of these two cytokines in IVDD. This observation generates the hypothesis that IL-18 and IFN-γ may have stage-dependent effects during IVDD progression. However, these inverse MR associations require cautious interpretation; MR estimates reflect lifelong genetic predisposition to cytokine levels rather than transient inflammatory changes during a specific stage of IVDD. Therefore, the observed associations do not imply that short-term increases in IL-18 or IFN-γ are protective. Although the original studies conducted sensitivity analyses, residual pleiotropy cannot be fully excluded, and most studies did not perform multivariable MR to account for other correlated immune factors.

Metabolic factors and IVDD

In this meta-MR analysis, we identified stable and directionally consistent causal signals between metabolic factors and IVDD. Body mass index (BMI) significantly increased the risk of IVDD, while elevated triglycerides (TG) and type 2 diabetes (T2D) were also significant risk factors. In contrast, 25-hydroxyvitamin D (25-OH-D) showed no significant causal association. But it is worth noting that the association between BMI and IVDD should be interpreted with caution because substantial heterogeneity was observed across the included MR estimates. This heterogeneity may reflect differences in GWAS sources, exposure scaling, sample overlap, instrument selection, covariate adjustment, or outcome definitions.

Existing studies have demonstrated that increased body weight directly elevates axial and shear stress on the spine, accelerating the accumulation of microdamage in discs and inducing degenerative changes [51, 52]. It is also important to note that obesity-associated chronic low-grade inflammation can alter disc cell metabolism, promote the expression of MMPs/ADAMTS, and accelerate matrix degradation [53]. Therefore, the finding that BMI significantly increases IVDD risk is well supported. Moreover, current evidence suggests that dyslipidemia may lead to lipid deposition in the vertebral bodies/endplates, inducing local oxidative stress and inflammatory responses, contributing to endplate lesions, vascularization, and matrix degradation, thereby accelerating IVDD [54, 55]. This highlights that, beyond weight control, lipid management should also be emphasized in the prevention and treatment of disc degeneration. Another notable finding is the role of T2D. Prolonged hyperglycemia in diabetic patients provides a favorable environment for the formation of Advanced glycation end products (AGEs). AGEs accumulate in the disc matrix, cross-link collagen, alter matrix mechanics, and—via the receptor for Receptor for Advanced Glycation End products (RAGE)—activate pro-inflammatory and oxidative stress signaling, promoting cell apoptosis and matrix degradation. Multiple experimental and review studies have elaborated the central role of AGEs in diabetes-related IVDD [54, 56], supporting the conclusion that T2D is a reliable risk factor for IVDD. For diabetic patients, long-term glycemic control and potential inhibition of AGEs may represent promising strategies to slow IVDD progression. In the present study, 25-OH-D did not demonstrate a significant causal effect, and thus, it cannot be confirmed as a protective factor. Based on the current MR evidence, we did not find support for a causal protective effect of genetically predicted 25-hydroxyvitamin D on IVDD risk in the studied populations. This finding does not address vitamin D supplementation in individuals with clinical deficiency or other bone-health indications. However, in populations with clear vitamin D deficiency or at high risk for bone health issues, evaluating the effect of vitamin D supplementation on spinal imaging outcomes remains a reasonable direction for future research [57, 58].

In summary, based on this meta-MR evidence, BMI, triglycerides, and T2D were identified as causal risk factors for IVDD. These findings support further investigation of weight management, lipid regulation, and glycemic control as potentially relevant components of IVDD prevention research, while recognizing that the present MR synthesis alone cannot establish clinical intervention efficacy.

Gut microbiota and IVDD

Our MR findings suggest potential causal associations between gut microbiota and IVDD. Genetically predicted abundance of Bacteroidetes and Marvinbryantia was associated with increased IVDD risk, whereas Ruminococcaceae UCG003, Rhodospirillaceae, and Rhodospirillales showed inverse associations. These findings support a potential “gut–disc axis,” but the underlying mechanisms remain to be clarified.

Although the intervertebral disc is largely avascular, it depends on diffusion through the cartilaginous endplate for nutrient and metabolite exchange. Gut microbiota may therefore influence the disc microenvironment indirectly by modulating intestinal barrier permeability, circulating microbial metabolites, systemic low-grade inflammation, immune activation, and metabolic homeostasis. For example, lipopolysaccharide (LPS) derived from Gram-negative bacteria such as Bacteroidetes can induce chronic low-grade inflammation, which may affect endplate permeability, oxidative stress, and extracellular matrix metabolism in disc tissue. In contrast, short-chain fatty acid (SCFA)-producing bacteria, such as those from the family Ruminococcaceae, help maintain intestinal barrier integrity and promote an anti-inflammatory state [59, 60]. Furthermore, animal and clinical studies have observed reduced gut microbial diversity in IVDD patients, characterized by decreased butyrate-producing bacteria and increased abundance of pro-inflammatory bacteria [10].

Therefore, the associations observed for specific taxa should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating evidence for a potential gut–disc axis rather than proof of direct microbial effects on the avascular disc. Further experimental and longitudinal studies are needed to determine whether these taxa influence IVDD through specific metabolites, immune-inflammatory pathways, or endplate-mediated nutrient exchange.

Lifestyle factors and IVDD

Our study provides genetic evidence supporting significant causal relationships between modifiable lifestyle factors and IVDD. Specifically, sedentary behavior—such as prolonged television viewing—was positively associated with IVDD risk, consistent with mechanical studies showing that sustained static loading reduces disc height and promotes degenerative changes [61, 62]. Among sedentary behaviors, genetically predicted time spent watching TV was positively associated with IVDD risk, whereas computer use showed an inconsistent and non-significant association. This discrepancy suggests that different sedentary behaviors may not represent the same underlying exposure pattern. TV watching may capture a broader sedentary and lifestyle-related profile, including lower physical activity, poorer diet, adiposity, smoking, mental health status, and socioeconomic factors, rather than the isolated effect of TV viewing itself. In contrast, computer use may be more closely related to different educational or occupational contexts. Therefore, the association observed for TV watching should be interpreted cautiously and considered hypothesis-generating, particularly because multivariable MR adjustment for correlated lifestyle and socioeconomic factors was not consistently available across the included studies. Smoking predisposition was also identified as a causal risk factor, likely mediated by nicotine-induced vasoconstriction, carbon monoxide–related hypoxia, oxidative stress, and subsequent matrix degradation [63, 64]. These findings align with systematic reviews and imaging studies confirming more severe disc degeneration in smokers [65, 66]. Additionally, anthropometric traits, including height, waist circumference, and BMI, showed positive genetic correlations with IVDD. Taller stature increases spinal axial load, while elevated waist circumference and BMI raise abdominal pressure and mechanical stress, disturbing disc homeostasis [67]. Obesity-related chronic low-grade inflammation and metabolic dysregulation may further promote IVDD progression [68]. These findings suggest that lifestyle-related and anthropometric traits may be relevant to IVDD risk. However, whether modifying these factors can reduce IVDD incidence or progression requires confirmation in longitudinal and interventional studies.

Collectively, this study further clarified from a genetic perspective the significant role of factors related to prolonged sitting, smoking, and physical load in the occurrence and development of IVDD, strengthening the key entry point provided by “modifiable lifestyle factors” for the prevention and control of IVDD.

Overall, this synthesis provides a preliminary multidomain map of genetically predicted factors associated with IVDD. Rather than providing immediate clinical guidance, these findings may help prioritize exposures and pathways for future validation, including longitudinal cohort studies, mechanistic experiments, and, where appropriate, intervention trials. Given the nature of MR evidence, the observed associations should not be directly equated with the expected effects of clinical or lifestyle interventions. First, it identifies priority areas for intervention: weight control, regulation of blood lipids and glucose, smoking cessation, and reduced sedentary behavior should be established as core public health messages for the primary prevention of IVDD. Second, it reveals potential novel therapeutic targets, including specific gut microbiota (e.g., modulable through probiotics or dietary interventions) and protective inflammatory pathways. Finally, it introduces the concept of an “individual risk profile,” which integrates an individual’s genetic predispositions (such as high BMD or specific microbial traits) and metabolic status to identify populations at high risk for IVDD, thereby enabling the implementation of early and targeted management strategies.

Despite the methodological strengths of this study, several limitations should be acknowledged. The analysis relied on published MR studies. Although the exposure spectrum was broad, it remained constrained by instrument selection in the original MR analyses; thus, some potentially relevant causal factors may not have been captured. In addition, STROBE-MR is a reporting checklist rather than a formal risk-of-bias tool. Therefore, high STROBE-MR scores should not be interpreted as evidence that the included MR estimates were unbiased. Genetic instruments reflect lifelong exposure tendencies rather than actual exposure levels, and therefore cannot fully represent exposures that change rapidly with age or environmental influences. The present study could not assess segment-specific or severity-specific causal associations of IVDD. Although MR reduces confounding to a large extent, residual horizontal pleiotropy may still influence certain causal estimates. In addition, it is worth noting that MR estimates reflect genetically predicted lifelong differences in exposures and should not be interpreted as equivalent to the effects of modifying these exposures through clinical or lifestyle interventions. Another limitation is the potential overlap of GWAS participants across some included MR studies. Several studies used the same or related GWAS resources, such as UK Biobank, IEU OpenGWAS, MRC-IEU, and FinnGen, for similar exposures. Because the exact degree of participant overlap could not be quantified from published summary-level data, some pooled estimates may be affected by non-independence of MR estimates and should therefore be interpreted cautiously. Meanwhile, most included studies used GWAS data from individuals of European ancestry. Therefore, the findings may not be generalizable to non-European populations. Future trans-ethnic MR studies and population-specific GWASs are required to assess whether these associations are ancestry-specific or shared across populations. Despite these limitations, this systematic MR meta-analytic framework provides a structured cross-domain synthesis of current MR evidence for IVDD. Future studies integrating longitudinal cohorts, animal models, and randomized controlled trials are warranted to validate these associations, clarify key mechanistic pathways, and determine whether modifying these exposures during midlife or later life can reduce IVDD incidence or progression.

Conclusion

This systematic evaluation of published MR evidence suggests that bone density, inflammatory cytokines, metabolic traits, gut microbiota, and lifestyle-related factors may be associated with IVDD risk. Among modifiable lifestyle-related factors, time spent watching TV showed a particularly prominent association with IVDD risk and may warrant further prevention-oriented research. These findings provide a basis for hypothesis generation and future validation, but should be interpreted cautiously given heterogeneity in MR designs, exposure scaling, GWAS populations, and outcome definitions.
