MR Imaging of Wallenberg Syndrome Using Accelerated 3D-FLAIR with T2 Preparation and Magnetization Transfer Contrast: A Case Report
Authors: Yusuke Ayabe, Noriko Oyama-Manabe, Nobuyoshi Ohmi, Yoshimasa Ikeda, Yoshimasa Koyama
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
We report a case of Wallenberg syndrome associated with vertebral artery occlusion and suspected atherosclerotic changes. The patient was evaluated using accelerated 3D fluid-attenuated inversion recovery MRI incorporating T2 preparation and magnetization transfer contrast pulses. This technique enabled a shortened acquisition time while preserving high image contrast, allowing clear visualization of the lateral medullary infarction. This approach provides a non-contrast, high-resolution method for assessing brainstem infarcts and may contribute to improved diagnostic accuracy in the acute setting.

Introduction

Wallenberg syndrome, also known as lateral medullary syndrome, is a neurological disorder caused by ischemia in the lateral medulla.1 The most common etiologies include occlusion of the posterior inferior cerebellar artery (PICA) or vertebral artery (VA), with vertebral artery dissection (VAD) being a frequent underlying cause. Previous reports suggest that Wallenberg syndrome is associated with VAD in approximately 26%–43% of cases, particularly among younger individuals.2

Imaging evaluation of VAD involves assessing morphological abnormalities and occlusion of the VA, as well as the presence of thrombus in a false lumen or intramural hematoma.3 These findings are often difficult to visualize using CT, making MRI the preferred modality. While magnetic resonance angiography (MRA) is commonly used to assess vessel morphology and occlusion, fat-suppressed black-blood T1-weighted imaging (BBT1) has been reported to be effective in evaluating thrombosed false lumens and hematomas. Although non contrast-enhanced (Non-CE) MRI is frequently employed, contrast-enhanced (CE) MRA has also shown diagnostic utility in select cases.4

Diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) has the highest sensitivity for detecting infarction in Wallenberg syndrome. However, its sensitivity may be reduced during the hyperacute phase.4 Fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) imaging is useful for estimating the time of onset but is often compromised by cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) flow artifacts in the brainstem. To address this, 3D-FLAIR imaging has been proposed as a solution, though its long acquisition time limits its use in acute settings.5 T2 preparation (T2 Prep) pulses enhance tissue T2 contrast, while magnetization transfer contrast (MTC) pulses improve image contrast via magnetization exchange effects.6,7 Both techniques are known to improve white matter (WM) -to-gray matter (GM) contrast in FLAIR imaging, but no prior reports have described the combined use of T2 Prep and MTC pulses.

Here, we report a case of Wallenberg syndrome associated with a VA lesion suspected to represent atherosclerosis. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first report demonstrating that Non-CE 3D-FLAIR imaging incorporating both T2 Prep and MTC pulses on a 1.5 Tesla (1.5T) MRI scanner enabled clear visualization of the medullary infarction within a scan time of only 2 min.

Case Description

A 65 year-old man with a history of hypertension presented to our hospital with dysarthria, left facial droop, and left upper limb weakness. On arrival, the National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) score was 2 points, consisting of 1 point for facial palsy and 1 point for dysarthria. MRI was performed using a 1.5T scanner (Vantage Orian 1.5T; Canon Medical Systems, Tochigi, Japan) 16 hr after symptom onset. MRI revealed a hyperintense lesion in the left lateral medulla on DWI (Fig. 1a, b) suggestive of Wallenberg syndrome. On conventional 2D-FLAIR imaging, visualization of the infarct was limited due to CSF flow and motion artifacts (Fig. 1c, d). In contrast, 3D-FLAIR imaging incorporating T2 Prep and MTC pulses distinctly visualized the infarct in the left lateral medulla (Fig. 1e, f). The imaging parameters for each FLAIR sequence are summarized in Table 1. Time-of-flight (TOF) MRA failed to visualize the left VA (Fig. 2a), but basi-parallel anatomical scanning (BPAS) successfully depicted it (Fig. 2b, c), raising suspicion of left VA occlusion or severe stenosis. Additional Non-CE 3D-BBT1 (Fig. 3a, b) showed the left VA as isointense, indicating reduced flow and possible occlusion; however, neither thrombus formation nor an intimal flap could be clearly identified. On Non-CE 3D-FLAIR imaging, a hyperintense area was observed within a portion of the vascular lumen of the left VA (Fig. 3e). These hyperintense regions matched those observed on CE 3D-BBT1 imaging performed the following day (Fig. 3c). Additionally, a hypointense region in the VA seen on the 3D-FLAIR image (Fig. 3f) corresponded to the flow void seen in the CE 3D-BBT1 image (Fig. 3d). These findings are also evident on the coronal images reconstructed by multiplanar reformation (MPR) (Fig. 4). In addition, an overview of MPR images of the 3D-FLAIR is shown in Figure 5.

Quantitative Evaluation

To quantitatively evaluate lesion conspicuity and CSF flow artifacts across different FLAIR sequences, contrast ratios (CRs) were calculated between the lesion and the contralateral medulla, as well as between WM and CSF. In the MRI system used in this study, background noise is processed as zero when parallel imaging is applied; therefore, CR was used for evaluation instead of the contrast-to-noise ratio (CNR). Signal intensity (SI) was measured by placing rectangular ROI in the lesion, the contralateral medulla, WM, and CSF. Figures 6 and 7 shows the ROIs placement. The derived SI are used to calculate CR as follows: CR=(SIA-SIB)/(SIA+SIB)

Here, SIA represents the SI of the lesion and WM, measured using rectangular ROIs. SIB represents the SI of the contralateral medulla and CSF, also measured using rectangular ROIs. The ROI size for the lesion and contralateral medulla was set to 6 × 14 mm, while the ROI sizes for WM and CSF were set to 16 × 13 mm and 8 × 17 mm, respectively. Each ROI was placed at the identical location across all sequences using the copy function on the MRI console. ROIs for the lesion were placed on DWI at the lesion site and then copied to each sequence. The results obtained using the above method are presented below. The lesion-to-contralateral medulla CR was 0.087 on 2D-FLAIR and 0.117 on 3D-FLAIR, demonstrating an approximately 1.3-fold improvement with 3D-FLAIR (Fig. 6). Additionally, the WM-to-CSF CR increased from 0.23 on 2D-FLAIR to 0.78 on 3D-FLAIR, representing a 3.3-fold improvement, indicating a substantial reduction in CSF flow artifacts (Fig. 7).

Discussion

This case suggests the potential utility of a 3D-FLAIR sequence incorporating both T2 Prep and MTC pulses for the evaluation of brainstem lesions in patients with Wallenberg syndrome. A key feature of this technique is that the combination of T2 Prep and MTC pulses enables acquisition of high-resolution 3D-FLAIR images with a shortened TR (3500 ms) while preserving WM-to-GM contrast. On quantitative evaluation, the cerebellar vermis CSF flow artifacts were reduced, and the 3D-FLAIR sequence demonstrated a significantly higher CR. These findings suggest that this technique can reduce clinically significant CSF flow artifacts compared to 2D-FLAIR, thereby enhancing lesion detectability in the cerebellar region.

To date, only 1 report has described the use of 3D-FALIR imaging for evaluating the intraluminal hematoma in VA dissection.8 In that report, the intraluminal hematoma exhibited hyperintensity on 3D-FLAIR earlier than on BBT1, suggesting that the signal changes on 3D-FLAIR may reflect biochemical changes associated with hemoglobin degradation. In our case, the hyperintense signal on 3D-FLAIR corresponded precisely to the region of enhancement on CE 3D-BBT1. According to previous reports, these findings may reflect reduced blood flow, and vessel wall enhancement may be attributable to contrast enhancement of the vasa vasorum in the adventitial layer.4 For differentiation from an intramural hematoma, the SI on BBT1-weighted imaging and its longitudinal changes over time should be assessed comprehensively, and the diagnosis should be made with caution. In our case, the hyperintense signal on 3D-FLAIR showed spatial correspondence with the region of enhancement on CE 3D-BBT1, which may reflect slow or stagnant blood flow rather than definitive intramural hematoma. Cho et al. reported that among 7 patients with hyperacute medullary infarction not detected on DWI, unilateral VA enhancement was observed on CE 3D-BBT1 in 5 cases.4 In these patients, the time interval from symptom onset to hospital arrival and the initial MRI examination was significantly shorter. These enhancement effects are considered to correspond to the hyperintense areas observed on 3D-FLAIR and CE 3D-BBT1 in the present case. DWI remains the first-line modality for detecting medullary infarction. In contrast, 3D-FLAIR may provide limited but supplementary information suggestive of vascular involvement in cases of DWI-negative hyperacute medullary infarction. However, it remains unclear whether 3D-FLAIR offers additional vessel wall information beyond that obtained from 3D-BBT1. Further accumulation of cases and systematic evaluation are required to clarify its incremental diagnostic value. FLAIR imaging is useful for detecting ischemic lesions and estimating time of onset; however, in the brainstem, image quality often deteriorates due to CSF flow artifacts. Although 3D-FLAIR provides isotropic spatial resolution and supports MPR and artifact reduction making it well-suited for brainstem lesion evaluation its long acquisition time has traditionally limited its use in acute settings. Multi-coverage 2D-FLAIR has been reported to reduce CSF flow artifacts, but it doubles scan time and may reduce MTC effectiveness due to an increased number of segments, potentially degrading WM-to-GM contrast. Incorporation of MTC pulses into 2D-FLAIR can shorten TR while maintaining contrast6; however, this approach does not adequately address CSF flow artifacts. In acute cases, reduction of MRI examination time is essential; therefore, acceleration of 3D-FLAIR acquisition is indispensable. Currently, several fast 3D-FLAIR techniques have been reported, including 3D Sampling Perfection with Application-optimized Contrasts using different flip angle Evolutions (SPACE),9 and Volume ISotropic T2-weighted Acquisition (VISTA).10 The reported acquisition times for these techniques are 1 min 53 s for SPACE-FLAIR,9 1 min 52 s for VISTA.10 All of these reports were obtained using 3T scanners, where scan time reduction was achieved primarily by increasing the parallel imaging acceleration factor. In comparison, the acquisition time achieved by our proposed method (2 min 3 s) is nearly equivalent. Given the inferior coil performance typically encountered in 1.5T systems, our approach is considered highly effective for achieving accelerated 3D-FLAIR imaging under such conditions.

In contrast, the 3D-FLAIR sequence used in this case, combining T2 Prep and MTC pulses, benefits from the intrinsic artifact-reducing properties of 3D acquisition and allows TR shortening while maintaining image contrast. While previous studies have reported the use of T2 Prep to shorten TR to 3000 ms on 3T scanners, concerns about image degradation remain.11 In this case, TR was set to 3500 ms. The reduction in WM-to-GM contrast caused by incomplete T1 recovery under short TR conditions was compensated by the combination of T2 Prep and MTC. T2 Prep enhances tissue T2 differences (with reported values at 1.5T of approximately 70 ms for WM and 90 ms for GM), while MTC pulses selectively saturate macromolecular-bound water, which is abundant in WM, thereby improving contrast relative to GM. A schematic diagram illustrates the underlying physical mechanisms of each pulse (Figs. 8 and 9). Compared to conventional 2D-FLAIR, improved contrast was observed not only in the brainstem but also in the basal ganglia, suggesting enhanced lesion detectability throughout the brain (Fig. 10). Although further evaluation is required to fully assess lesion conspicuity under shortened TR conditions, our results confirmed that lesion detectability remained unchanged when TR was reduced from 5000 ms to 3500 ms through the combined application of T2 Prep and MTC pulses (Fig. 11).

A limitation of this study is that conventional 3D-FLAIR images were not acquired. Because this case involved acute cerebral infarction, prompt diagnosis and treatment were required. Therefore, the examination was completed using only the proposed method incorporating T2 Prep and MTC pulses, and conventional 3D-FLAIR images were not obtained for comparison. This limitation should be addressed in future studies.

Conclusion

3D-FLAIR imaging incorporating T2 Prep and MTC pulses enabled clear visualization of a lateral medullary infarction associated with VA occlusion or suspected atherosclerotic changes. This technique may serve as a useful non-contrast MRI method that allows high-resolution evaluation with reduced acquisition time and may provide clinically valuable imaging in lesions suspected of brainstem infarction.
