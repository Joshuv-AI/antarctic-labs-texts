Genetically Encoded FRET-Based Nanosensor for Insect Juvenile Hormone
Authors: Shingo Kikuta, Takahiro Shiotsuki, Wataru Tsuchiya, Rintaro Suzuki, Takahiro Kikawada, Zui Fujimoto, Toshimasa Yamazaki
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Juvenile hormone (JH) plays a central role in insect development and reproductive maturation. However, the molecular transport mechanism remains unclear because JH is difficult to detect nondestructively and in real-time. A new genetically encoded fluorescent biosensor based on Förster resonance energy transfer (FRET) is composed of a JH-binding protein (JHBP) derived from Bombyx mori, which is fused with two fluorescent protein pairs (mTFP1 and mVenus) and exhibits a ratiometric readout. The mTFP1-JHBP-mVenus proteins, flanked by the two fluorescent proteins at the N- and C-termini, respectively, exhibited no discernible FRET as a primary FRET JH sensor. This suggests that inducible FRET by JH binding may have been compromised. Optimizing the insertion of mTFP1 into JHBP enabled inducible FRET in the presence of JH. The resulting construct, the FRET JH Indicator Agent (FREJIA), is the first ratiometric, genetically encoded, and fluorescent biosensor for JH detection. FREJIA interacts with the binding protein and emits a resonance energy transfer signal in response to binding to JH I, II, and III and methoprene at nanomolar ranges. FREJIA enables ratiometric imaging of JH III in live mammalian cells. Our results showed that FRET can be applied to the selective and ratiometric monitoring of JH levels.

Introduction

Juvenile hormone (JH) is a sesquiterpenoid hormone synthesized by the corpora allata, a pair of small endocrine glands near the brain in insects. It plays a central role in regulating insect development and reproductive maturation, acting in concert with ecdysteroids. Pupal differentiation is initiated in the presence of ecdysteroids and the absence of JH. JH modulates gene expression through direct nuclear receptor signaling and indirect signaling mechanisms involving secondary messengers. − One well-characterized receptor is methoprene-tolerant (Met), a bHLH-PAS transcription factor activated by JH-bound cofactors. In Lepidoptera, JH is transported from the corpora allata to peripheral tissues via high-affinity binding proteins known as hemolymph JH-binding proteins (JHBPs). These proteins are thought to stabilize JH in the hemolymph and mediate its delivery to target cells. Despite the physiological importance of JH, its transport and intracellular activities remain poorly characterized in insects. The real-time, nondestructive detection of JH dynamics has been limited by the lack of suitable biosensors for in vitro analysis.

Current techniques for detecting JH, such as liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC-MS), reverse-phase high-performance liquid chromatography (RP-HPLC) coupled with fluorescence detection, and gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC-MS), offer high sensitivity. − However, these methods require cell lysis and complex sample preparation, making them unsuitable for real-time monitoring in live organisms. In contrast, biosensors based on Förster Resonance Energy Transfer (FRET) have been successfully applied to track the dynamics of various small molecules, including calcium ions, sugars, and plant hormones, in live cells and in vitro settings with high spatial and temporal resolution. − These FRET-based biosensors typically consist of a ligand-binding domain flanked by donor and acceptor fluorescent proteins. Ligand binding induces conformational changes in the sensor, modulating the efficiency of energy transfer between fluorophores. Despite the proven utility of FRET biosensors in plant and mammalian systems, no such tool exists for monitoring invertebrate hormones such as JH. This highlights the critical unmet need for a genetically encoded biosensor capable of nondestructive, ratiometric detection of JH and its analogs in real-time.

In this study, we developed an in vitro FRET-based biosensor for JH detection using JHBP as a ligand-sensing domain. In this design, ligand binding induces conformational changes in the JHBP, which in turn alters the energy transfer between a donor and an acceptor fluorescent proteins fused to its termini. This change enabled ratiometric detection of JH through FRET. To ensure high sensitivity and specificity, we selected JHBP derived from Bombyx mori, whose crystal structures in both apo and JH-bound states have been elucidated. Based on these structural changes, we engineered a sensor construct named the FRET JH Indicator Agent (FREJIA), designed to report JH binding via an increase in FRET efficiency. FREJIA responds to nanomolar concentrations of JH with a measurable emission ratio change, enabling real-time, nondestructive monitoring of hormone levels. Additionally, we investigated the response of FREJIA to various insect growth regulators, including methoprene, which is structurally similar to JH, and pyriproxyfen, which is not structurally similar to JH. This allowed us to evaluate the specificity of the sensor for JH and its potential utility in screening JH analogs in the future.

Experimental Section

Materials

A FRET-based biosensor expression construct was designed by incorporating fluorescent proteins that formed efficient FRET pairs. The genes encoding mTFP1 (donor) and mVenus (acceptor) were inserted into the multiple cloning site of the pRSET-A bacterial expression vector (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Carlsbad, CA, USA). − The suitability of the mTFP1/mVenus FRET pair was validated as described by Day and Booker. To allow the insertion of a ligand-binding domain, a unique multiple cloning site was designed by introducing XhoI, EcoRV, and KpnI restriction sites between the two fluorescent proteins of interest. This configuration enabled the construction of original FRET-based sensors by fusing fluorescent proteins to the N- and C-termini of the protein of interest, allowing ligand-induced FRET ratio changes.

Generation of the Original FREJIA

The coding sequence of the mature form of Bombyx mori juvenile hormone-binding protein II (BmJHBP II, accession number AF098305) reported by Vermunt et al., excluding its signal peptide, was amplified using high-fidelity PCR with PrimeSTAR HS polymerase (TaKaRa Bio, Shiga, Japan). The position of the signal peptide was determined according to the method. Primers were designed with 7–8 bp overlaps to facilitate seamless cloning using the GeneArt Seamless Cloning and Assembly Enzyme Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The amplified PCR product was inserted into the EcoRV site of a previously constructed FRET-based sensor expression vector. Sanger sequencing was performed using BigDye Terminator v3 chemistry and analyzed using the Genetyx-MAC software (Genetyx, Tokyo, Japan).

Generation of FREJIA Variants

To generate FREJIA variants, site-directed mutagenesis was performed using the original FREJIA expression vector as the template DNA. After PCR amplification, the parental plasmid was digested with DpnI to eliminate methylated template DNA. The reaction products were removed by digesting the remaining template DNA with the DpnI restriction enzyme and self-ligated with the GeneArt Seamless Cloning and Assembly Enzyme Mix. To produce recombinant FREJIA variants, expression vectors encoding FREJIA and its mutants were transformed into Escherichia coli BL21­(DE3) cells (EMD Millipore, Darmstadt, Germany). Single colonies of transformed cells were cultured in 100 mL LB medium supplemented with 100 μg/mL ampicillin. When the cultures reached mid log phase (OD600 ≈ 0.6), protein expression was induced by adding 1 mM isopropyl β-d-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG). The cells were then incubated at 16 °C for 16 h in the dark to minimize photobleaching of the fluorescent proteins. Sensor protein purification was performed based on previously described, with minor modifications. E. oli cells were harvested by centrifugation, resuspended in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.5), and lysed by ultrasonication on ice for 30 min. The supernatant was applied to a Ni–NTA affinity column (HisTrap HP; GE Healthcare, Piscataway, NJ, USA), and the bound proteins were washed with a buffer containing 20 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0), 200 mM NaCl, and 20 mM imidazole. Elution was carried out using a buffer containing 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 200 mM NaCl, and 400 mM imidazole. For further purification, the samples were applied to a HiLoad 26/60 Superdex 200 prep-grade column (GE Healthcare). The purity of the FREJIA variants was assessed by SDS-PAGE followed by Coomassie Brilliant Blue staining. The concentrations of the FRET sensors were determined using UV–visible spectrophotometry.

Measurements of FRET

Purified FREJIA variant proteins were dissolved in 10 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 150 mM NaCl, and 1% ethanol to a final concentration of 2–5 μM. Each ligand, including a series of juvenile hormone (JH) compounds and related analogs, was prepared in 100% ethanol and added to a 96-well clear-bottom polystyrene microplate (Falcon; Thermo Fisher Scientific) for the assay. The tested compounds included JH I and JH II (SciTech, Czech Republic), JH III (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO), methyl farnesoate (Echelon Biosciences, Salt Lake City, UT), methoprene (AccuStandard, New Haven, CT), methoprene acid, oleic acid (Sigma-Aldrich), fenoxycarb, and pyriproxyfen (MedChemExpress, NJ). Fluorescence spectra were acquired at 25 °C using a fluorescence spectrophotometer (F-4500, Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan). FRET efficiency was determined ratiometrically as the emission intensity ratio of mVenus to mTFP1. Fluorescence readings were measured for the donor fluorophore (mTFP1: excitation at 450 nm, emission at 480 nm), acceptor fluorophore (mVenus: excitation at 500 nm, emission at 530 nm), and FRET (mTFP1 excitation at 450 nm, mVenus emission at 530 nm; bandwidth set to 15 nm) using a fluorescence microplate reader (ARVO MX 1420 Multilabel Counter; PerkinElmer, Waltham, MA, USA). All measurements were performed at 25 °C.

JH imaging of cultured cells. Human embryonic kidney (HEK) 293T cells were cultured and transfected as described previously. The FREJIA construct (JH sensor-mTFP1-CΔ5 variant, NCBI accession number: LC859350) was subcloned into the multiple cloning site of the pcDNA3.1 mammalian expression vector (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Transfection was performed using PEI Max (Polysciences, Warrington, PA, USA). Fluorescence imaging was performed 48 h after transfection using a fluorescence microscope (IXplore Pro; Olympus, Osaka, Japan). The imaging parameters were set according to a previously reported method. Juvenile hormone III (JH III; 100 μM in ethanol) was added directly to the imaging chamber during the observation period. Cells transfected with empty pcDNA3.1 and mTFP1-mVenus expression vectors were used as negative controls.

Results and Discussion

Creation and Structural Optimization of FRET-Based JH Sensors

The successful development of FRET-based biosensors for small molecules requires simultaneous optimization of multiple parameters, including ligand-binding affinity, fluorescence brightness, and dynamic range. , To enable the specific detection of JH, we selected B. mori JHBP as the sensory domain based on its pronounced conformational change upon ligand-binding. According to the crystal structure of JHBP, the N-terminal α-helix (Met14–Ser29) is exposed to the solvent in the apo-state. Upon JH binding, this helix undergoes a dramatic ∼70° swing to cover the ligand-binding pocket. This substantial conformational transition was hypothesized to be suitable for modulating FRET between mTFP1 and mVenus fluorophores fused to the JHBP. As a first approach, we constructed a tandem fusion protein consisting of mTFP1–JHBP–mVenus, expressed it in E. coli, and purified the recombinant protein (Figure A,B).

However, this initial construct did not exhibit a discernible change in FRET upon ligand addition. Structural analysis suggested that in the JH-bound state, the N- and C-termini of JHBP adopt opposing orientations owing to the antiparallel hydrogen bonds between Cys9 and Phe220/Glu222, resulting in an increased distance between the fluorophores and inefficient energy transfer. To address this, we engineered a second construct by inserting mTFP1 into the loop region between Leu11 and Gly12, which is located in close proximity to the C-terminus of the JH-bound structure. This modified sensor displayed JH-induced FRET ratio changes, indicating effective conformational coupling between JH binding and fluorophore positioning. However, this construct was found to be structurally unstable during purification, with partial proteolysis leading to the detachment of mTFP1 from the sensor. Among the engineered constructs, one sensor variant exhibited superior responsiveness to JH but was unstable during bacterial expression and purification. To improve its structural integrity, we introduced sequence-level optimizations to the mTFP1 domain, including modifications to the N-terminal composite linker between the fluorescent proteins and JHBP. Linker shortening is known to enhance FRET signal strength across various nanosensor designs. Specifically, two amino acid residues were deleted from each side of the composite linker, and a conservative five-residue deletion variant was selected as the most promising configuration. To further stabilize the construct, glycine residues were inserted between mTFP1 and JHBP, resulting in an optimized sensor named FREJIA (Figure A–C).

Ligand-Induced FRET Response and Sensor Stabilization

Next, we evaluated FREJIA for JH binding by measuring changes in the FRET efficiency. FRET ratio analysis revealed a ligand-dependent increase in the emission ratio (mVenus/mTFP1), consistent with the expected conformational rearrangement of the JHBP domain upon hormone binding. This indicates successful signal transduction from ligand recognition to optical output. To assess the stability of the sensor, we examined the effects of several commonly used additives. FREJIA retained its FRET responsiveness in the presence of 1% dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) or 0.005% Tween 20, with no measurable effects on emission ratios (Supplemental Table 1). These results confirmed that FREJIA maintained structural integrity and ratiometric response under mild detergent or solvent conditions, supporting its robustness in vitro.

Specificity and Sensitivity of the Optimized FREJIA Sensor

FREJIA exhibited robust ligand-dependent FRET responses and a dissociation constant (K d) of 293 nM for JH III, as determined from ratiometric emission changes (Figure D). This affinity was comparable to the K d of 450 nM reported for recombinant JHBP using a dextran-coated charcoal (DCC) assay with tritiated JH III. The K d of the recombinant JHBP for JH III, as determined by the DCC assay, represents the value of JHBP itself. However, the FRET response obtained with FREJIA does not always correspond to the dissociation constant of JHBP because the FRET response differs from the binding activity of JHBP. The performance of sensors, including their ligand specificity and sensitivity, can be altered by applying protein-engineering techniques. The present study showed the ligand specificity of FREJIA. The sensor also responded to other biologically active JHs, including JH I, JH II, and the synthetic analogs methoprene and fenoxycarb, which structures resemble those of JHs (Figure A). In contrast, no significant FRET changes were observed with 1.38 μM methoprene acid, methyl farnesoate, or pyriproxyfen. FRET responses were detected against the ligands at a concentration of 10 μM, suggesting that FREJIA exhibits high ligand specificity within a defined concentration range (Figure B). FREJIA did not respond to oleic acid (Figure B). The alteration of the sensor concentration should make it possible to prevent nonspecific, off-target binding. The FRET response to non-JH small molecules suggests that JHBP may exhibit a broader binding capacity in insects.

It has been shown to be very responsive to externally applied JH, leading to the measurement of endogenous changes in JH levels in insects. In Manduca sexta, the maintenance of JH in the hemolymph at approximately 150 nM during active feeding stages suggests the potential functionality of FREJIA in vivo, because the sensor responded to approximately 10 nM JH (Figure B). Thus, far, the molecular interactions between sugars and hormones have been visualized and quantified using ratiometric imaging in mice, rats, zebrafish, and plants. ,,, In insects, the FRET glucose sensor was expressed in Drosophila, and its transport to the brain was visualized. It is speculated that JHBP releases JH via conformational changes triggered by specific electrostatic and hydrophobic interactions on the target cell membrane. , To date, the release mechanism remains unknown. The JH sensor developed in this study will allow the imaging of insect-specific molecular targets. ,

The output from the sensors can be tracked by exciting the donor and measuring the ratio of acceptor emission to donor emission; this emission ratio is independent of biosensor expression levels and is proportional to the ligand concentration. Theoretically, any conformational changes in the sensory domain upon ligand binding are translated into a change in the distance (<10 nm) and/or orientation of the two fluorophores, thereby altering the FRET efficiency. The entry of the JH-binding pocket is surrounded by a gate helix α1 along with the N-terminal arm on one side, and on the other side, by a latch-like region formed by the C-terminal tail. The positions of the residues in the C-terminal tail do not substantially change between the apo- and ligand-bound structures. In contrast, the largest difference is the length and orientation of the gate α1 helix, which is 1.5-turn shorter at its N-terminus in the apo form than in the JH-bound form. The amino acid residues between the two fluorophores generate energy transfer, effectively converting JH-dependent intermolecular interactions into structural rearrangements. In addition, JHBP can be obtained by bacterial expression as well as fluorescent proteins. Based on the characteristics of JHBP, it would allow us to obtain the FRET sensor in vitro, to perform the measurement easily, and to decrease the cost in the high-throughput screening format. FREJIA functioned reproducibly in a 384-well microplate format with analyte concentrations up to 10 μM, demonstrating its suitability for in vitro screening assays. Ratiometric-based FRET, which exhibits fluorescence changes upon ligand binding with high affinity, can also be used as a tool to identify novel insect growth regulator candidates in high-throughput screening platforms.

Conclusion

In this study, we developed FREJIA, a genetically encoded FRET-based biosensor that enables the ratiometric, nondestructive detection of JH and its analogs, both in vitro and in living cells. FREJIA is based on the conformational change of Bombyx mori JHBP upon ligand binding, which modulates energy transfer between fused fluorescent proteins. The sensor exhibited a ligand-dependent increase in the FRET emission ratio, a dissociation constant of 293 nM for JH III, and high specificity for JH-related compounds. Importantly, FREJIA functions in live HEK293T cells, allowing real-time hormone detection. This demonstrates FREJIA’s potential as a valuable tool for studying insect endocrine signaling. The platform also enables future applications, such as high-throughput screening for JH analogs and real-time imaging of hormone activity in live organisms, thereby addressing a longstanding technical gap in insect molecular biology.
