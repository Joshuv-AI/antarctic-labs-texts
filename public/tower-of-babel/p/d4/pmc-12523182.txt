Understanding suicidal ideation in southeastern Iran: a cross-sectional analysis of demographic and socioeconomic correlates
Authors: Sajad Khosravi, Saeid Mirzaei, Mahdi Ahmadi, Ayda Nasrizadeh, Rashed Pourhamidi, Nadia Oroomiei
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSuicide and suicidal ideation are critical public health issues worldwide, especially in low- and middle-income countries such as Iran, where cultural and socioeconomic factors complicate prevention efforts. Understanding the regional correlates of suicidal ideation is essential for informing interventions. This study assessed the extent of suicidal ideation and examined its associations with demographic and socioeconomic factors among individuals aged ≥ 15 years in southeastern Iran.MethodsA cross-sectional survey was conducted across five counties under Bam University of Medical Sciences, enrolling 2,008 participants via stratified random sampling. Data were collected using the Beck Scale for Suicidal Ideation (BSSI). Descriptive statistics and inferential tests (chi-square, ANOVA, t-tests) examined associations between suicidal ideation and gender, age, education, marital status, employment, and residence.ResultsOf 2,008 participants, 120 (5.9%) reported suicidal ideation. Mean BSSI scores differed significantly by marital status and employment in univariate analyses: single and divorced individuals scored higher than married participants, and students and unemployed participants scored higher than employed and homemakers. No differences were observed by gender, residence, or education. In multivariable linear regression, higher education (B = –0.270, p = 0.001) and being married (B = –1.155, p < 0.001) were associated with lower BSSI scores, whereas age, gender, residence, and employment were not significant. The model was statistically significant but explained only a small proportion of variance (R2 = 0.028).ConclusionsIn this population-based cross-sectional survey, 5.9% of participants endorsed suicidal ideation. After adjustment, being married and higher educational attainment were associated with lower BSSI scores, whereas gender, age, residence, and employment showed no independent associations. The model’s limited explanatory power (R2 = 0.028) indicates that demographic variables alone have limited value for risk stratification; confidential, stigma-sensitive screening and linkage in primary care, universities, and employment-related settings may be informative for service planning. Future studies should incorporate psychological and social measures and consider multilevel or longitudinal designs to better characterize correlates; given the cross-sectional design, no causal inferences are implied.Keywords: Suicidal Ideation, Demographic Factors, Socioeconomic Factors, Iran, Cross-Sectional Studies, Beck Scale for Suicidal Ideation (BSSI), Multivariate Analysis

Background

Mental health is increasingly recognized as a critical component of overall well-being, with suicide emerging as one of the most pressing global mental health challenges [41]. When individuals are unable to effectively cope with daily stressors and life challenges, their mental health may deteriorate, increasing the risk of behaviors that compromise psychological, physical, and social functioning [29].

Suicidality is commonly conceptualized as a continuum that includes suicidal ideation, suicide attempts, and death by suicide; suicide refers specifically to death resulting from self-directed injurious behavior with intent to die. These behaviors often arise from a complex interplay of psychological, social, and cultural stressors [14]. Although not every suicide attempt results in death, suicide claims hundreds of thousands of lives globally each year and leaves profound and lasting impacts on families and communities. Understanding the factors that contribute to suicide is vital for developing effective prevention strategies, particularly in regions where mental health services are underdeveloped or stigmatized [36].

Globally, the average suicide rate stands at 11.4 per 100,000 people, ranking it as the fifteenth leading cause of death worldwide. Approximately 800,000 people die by suicide each year, accounting for 1.5% of all global deaths [30, 35]. In the United States, suicide rates rose by 35% between 2000 and 2018, contributing to stagnation—and even a decline—in national life expectancy, particularly among vulnerable social groups [21]. Suicide remains the leading cause of death by violence globally, leaving a lasting impact across generations. Although global suicide mortality has declined over the past three decades [23], trends vary by country. Notably, 75% of suicides worldwide occur in low- and middle-income countries [15], including Iran.

In Iran—a Middle Eastern, Islamic country—suicide is not criminalized but carries significant stigma due to religious beliefs, traditional norms, and limited mental health resources [11, 33]. Despite its relatively low national suicide mortality rate, Iran continues to grapple with serious concerns about suicide attempts, especially among young people [42–44]. National data show that Iran’s suicide rate declined from 5.9 per 100,000 in 2000 to 3.5 per 100,000 in 2020, before rising again in 2021 to 4.34 per 100,000 [44]. These fluctuations, particularly the recent increase, have reignited concern among health professionals and policymakers. Although data specific to the Bam region are limited, both national and provincial patterns underscore the need to investigate the underlying determinants of suicidal behavior in southeastern Iran.

A recent systematic review and meta-analysis by Asadiyun and Daliri [3] analyzed data from 20 studies and found an overall suicide mortality rate in Iran of 8.14 per 100,000, with a suicide attempt rate of 131 per 100,000. Attempt rates were higher among women, while mortality rates were higher among men. Although Iran’s suicide mortality rate remains lower than the global average, the increasing trend of suicide attempts, particularly among young people, is a growing concern [3]. A study by Hajebi et al. revealed substantial provincial disparities in Iran, with suicide attempt rates ranging from 0.7 to 271.1 per 100,000 and mortality rates from 0.17 to 17.7 per 100,000. Suicide lethality was higher among men, older adults, widowed or divorced individuals, unemployed people, and rural residents. The most common methods included drug overdose and hanging [10]. While suicide rates in the United States have increased significantly in recent decades—particularly among disadvantaged groups [21]—Iran’s relatively low suicide mortality may mask significant psychosocial distress, underscoring the need for culturally contextualized research like the present study.

Although some national and regional studies in Iran have examined suicide rates, most have primarily focused on the prevalence of suicide itself. In contrast, suicidal ideation—a deeply contextual and culturally shaped phenomenon—has not received comparable attention. Monitoring the presence of suicidal thoughts is crucial for understanding the current landscape and informing effective policy and intervention efforts. Therefore, this study aims to assess the extent of suicidal ideation and examine its sociodemographic associations in the population served by Bam University of Medical Sciences.

Given these gaps in existing research, there is a clear need to investigate suicidal ideation and its potential associations within the local context of southeastern Iran, particularly in areas like Bam where suicide remains a sensitive and stigmatized issue. By focusing on this culturally specific setting, the present study seeks to assess the extent of suicidal ideation and examine its associations with demographic and socioeconomic factors in southeastern Iran. This study represents one of the first systematic efforts to explore suicidal ideation in this region, providing critical data for local health authorities and contributing to evidence-based mental health strategies.

Context of the study

This study was conducted among the population served by Bam University of Medical Sciences, covering roughly 500,000 residents across five counties—Bam, Rigan, Fahraj, Narmashir, and Gonbaki—in Kerman Province, the largest province in southeastern Iran[22]. According to the 2016 national census, this region faced significant structural challenges, including a reported unemployment figure of 1,204 and approximately 4,752 officially recorded divorces. These socioeconomic stressors exist within a broader context of growing concern over suicide in the province (Iran Statistics Center 2012). According to local reports from 2023, the suicide rate in Kerman Province was about 6.5 per 100,000. Although region-specific data for the Bam catchment area remain limited, these provincial trends highlight the urgency of investigating the underlying factors driving suicidal behaviors in this setting [24].

Methods

This study employed a quantitative, cross-sectional design.

Sampling and representativeness

The study employed a stratified random sampling method, selecting both health centers and participants within those centers at random. These health centers are public and government-operated, providing essential health and medical services to both urban and rural populations. As such, they serve as a broad representation of the local population, including individuals from diverse socio-economic and demographic backgrounds who seek healthcare services. While it is acknowledged that individuals who visit health centers may differ systematically from those who do not (for example, in terms of health-seeking behavior and mental health status), the random selection of individuals within health centers reduces selection bias by ensuring that the sample represents a wide spectrum of individuals who access healthcare services. Furthermore, the inclusion of both urban and rural health centers ensures wide geographic representation, reflecting the diversity of the population across different regions. Although it is possible that individuals who visit health centers may have specific health concerns, this sampling approach still provides valuable insights into the mental health status of individuals in southeastern Iran. Nevertheless, it is important to note that the findings may be more applicable to populations who access healthcare services, and future research could explore other methods of sampling to enhance generalizability to the broader population.

Data collection

After determining the sample size by county, the research team visited health facilities under the coverage of Bam University of Medical Sciences—including health posts, health houses, comprehensive urban and rural health service centers, and health centers—to invite individuals aged 15 years and older to participate. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

To minimize potential distress associated with sensitive questions on suicidal ideation, the research team collaborated with health center staff to provide a private and supportive environment for participation. Individuals were informed that participation was entirely voluntary and would not affect the receipt or quality of their current or future health services. Questionnaires were anonymous and completed in a designated area where participants could sit comfortably and respond without observation. A sealed box was provided for returning completed questionnaires to ensure confidentiality. For participants with limited literacy or those preferring assistance, trained staff read the questions aloud and recorded responses verbatim, according to the participant’s direction. Participants were also informed that they could skip any question or withdraw at any time without consequence.

Because questionnaires were completed anonymously and returned in sealed boxes specific to each health center, individual follow-up was not possible. However, this approach allowed us to identify centers whose catchment populations showed relatively higher levels of suicidal ideation. In such cases, aggregated findings were confidentially communicated to health center leadership and the university administration to encourage strengthening of local mental health services. This strategy ensured that while individual confidentiality was fully protected, the evidence could still inform targeted service planning. This procedure was consistent with the study’s ethics approval, which emphasized both the protection of individual confidentiality and the utility of aggregate findings for improving community mental health services.

Data were collected using the Beck Scale for Suicidal Ideation (BSSI), which includes 19 items assessing the intensity of suicidal thoughts, suicidal plans, fear of death, hope for the future, and fear of the consequences of suicide.

Instruments

The Beck Scale for Suicidal Ideation (BSSI) is a standardized tool for evaluating the severity of suicidal thoughts and tendencies. It comprises 19 items designed to assess levels of hopelessness, concerns, planning, and suicide-related desires. The questionnaire covers multiple dimensions, including the wish to die, active and passive suicidal thoughts, control over thoughts, mental barriers, and readiness to act. Five screening items are reverse-scored; respondents who endorse any of these (scores 1 or 2) proceed to the remaining 14 items. Items are rated on a scale from 0 to 3, with higher scores indicating greater severity of suicidal ideation. This scale was originally developed by Beck et al. and has been validated and widely used in clinical research (de Beurs et al. 2015; Kliem et al. 2017; Lee et al. 2020). The BSSI has been translated into Persian and psychometrically validated for use in the Iranian population, including studies conducted in Tehran (Esfahani et al. 2015). The full version of the scale is available from the Beck Institute (Beck Institute 1998).

Scoring and interpretation

The BSSI total score ranges from 0 to 38, with higher scores reflecting greater severity of suicidal ideation. Following the scoring manual, we classified scores into three levels: 0–5 = ideation, 6–19 = preparation, and 20–38 = intention. In addition, Item 5 of the BSSI (“I have no desire to commit suicide” = 0; “I have a weak desire to commit suicide” = 1; “I have a strong desire to commit suicide” = 2) functioned as a screening item. Respondents scoring 0 on this item were considered as having no suicidal ideation and were not required to complete subsequent items, while those scoring ≥ 1 proceeded to the remaining 14 items. This procedure ensured consistency with the standardized scoring protocol and has been applied in prior Iranian validation studies.

Reliability and validity of the questionnaire

Previous studies have confirmed the reliability and validity of the BSSI [2, 25, 45]. Test–retest reliability coefficients range from 0.90 to 0.94, indicating strong measurement stability. Additionally, its correlations with the Beck Depression Inventory (0.60 to 0.68) and the Beck Hopelessness Scale (0.75 to 0.80) demonstrate high convergent validity with related psychological measures. Cronbach’s alpha for internal consistency has been reported between 0.97 and 0.98, reflecting excellent precision and validity of the tool in assessing suicidal ideation across diverse populations [4].

Data analysis

After data collection and cleaning, the final dataset was entered into SPSS for analysis. Descriptive statistics, including tables and charts, were generated, and inferential analyses were conducted using independent t-tests, ANOVA, and chi-square tests. To ensure the validity of these tests, we first checked the assumptions of normality using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test, which confirmed that the data followed a normal distribution (p = 0.200), supporting the use of parametric tests (e.g., t tests, ANOVA).

Additionally, post hoc tests, including Tukey HSD and Sidak, were applied to adjust for multiple comparisons and to reduce the risk of Type I errors. These adjustments were made to ensure the validity of the statistical conclusions drawn from the analysis.

Furthermore, to explore the relationships between suicidal ideation and a range of demographic and socio-economic variables, a linear regression model was conducted. The dependent variable was the suicidal ideation score, while the independent variables included gender, age, education, marital status, employment status, and residence. To assess the goodness of fit of the model, R2 was calculated, and the model’s statistical significance was evaluated using the F-test. Linear regression was used to estimate associations for each independent variable while adjusting for others. Assumptions of linearity, independence, and homoscedasticity were visually assessed; no notable violations were detected.

A total of 2,008 individuals participated in the quantitative phase of this study, with 120 individuals reporting suicidal ideation. This translates to a suicidal ideation prevalence of 5.9% among participants.

Statistical analyses were conducted on the full sample (N = 2,008) as well as on the subsample of participants who endorsed suicidal ideation (n = 120; Item 5 score ≥ 1). Participants who scored 0 on Item 5 were not required to complete subsequent BSSI items, but they were retained in descriptive analyses of the full sample, while comparative analyses focused on subgroups with reported suicidal ideation. Subsample analyses were applied to evaluate mean scores and associations among those with reported suicidal ideation.

Age grouping

Participants’ ages were categorized into four groups (15–25, 26–40, 41–60, and 61 years and older), based on life cycle stages commonly applied in demographic and mental health research. This classification facilitated interpretation of age-related patterns in suicidal ideation rather than ensuring equal group sizes.

Results

A total of 2,008 participants were included in the study, of whom 120 (5.9%) reported suicidal ideation. Based on BSSI scoring, 0–5 indicated suicidal ideation, 6–19 suicide preparation, and 20–38 suicide intention. Before conducting inferential analyses, we assessed the distribution of suicidal‐ideation scores to confirm the suitability of parametric tests. The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test indicated that the data were normally distributed (p = 0.200), supporting the use of t-tests and ANOVA.

Among participants with suicidal ideation, the largest proportion was in the 26–40-year age group (48.3%), whereas very few were aged ≥ 61 years. The mean age of participants with suicidal ideation was 32.9 years (median 32; range 15–74 years), consistent with the predominance of cases in the 26–40-year group. Women represented a slightly higher proportion than men (57.5% vs. 42.5%). Most participants with suicidal ideation had education up to the high school level, with very few holding higher degrees. Regarding employment, employed individuals and homemakers together comprised the majority, while retirees were least represented. Over half were married (54.2%), followed by singles (40.8%). In terms of residence, 58.3% lived in urban areas and 41.7% in rural areas. A full distribution of demographic characteristics is presented in Table 3.

Multiple linear regression assessed associations between demographic/socioeconomic variables and suicidal-ideation scores. The model was statistically significant (F = 9.560, p < 0.001) but explained a small proportion of variance (R2 = 0.028). Higher educational level was associated with lower suicidal-ideation scores (B = − 0.270, p = 0.001). Being married (vs. single/divorced/widowed) was also associated with lower scores (B = − 1.155, p < 0.001). Residence (rural vs. urban) was not associated with mean scores in the regression model. Age, gender, and employment status were not statistically significant (all p > 0.05). Given the small R2, the model’s explanatory power is limited; additional variables and alternative designs (e.g., longitudinal studies) may be needed to account for more variance.

Discussion

In this population-based cross-sectional study from southeastern Iran using the Beck Scale for Suicidal Ideation (BSSI), 5.9% of respondents reported suicidal ideation, and mean scores differed across select sociodemographic strata. In multivariable analyses, higher education and being married were associated with lower BSSI scores, whereas gender, residence, age, and employment status were not independently associated after adjustment; in unadjusted comparisons, students and unemployed participants had higher mean scores than employed individuals. These patterns are broadly consistent with reports from Iran and other low- and middle-income settings in which marriage correlates with lower levels of suicidal ideation—often discussed in relation to social support, role meaning, and network integration. Supporting this, Shiraly et al. reported that depression, not being married, low perceived social support, and limited social networks were more salient correlates of suicidal ideation in southwestern Iran [34]. In a similar direction, Stephenson et al. found that being married was associated with a lower likelihood of suicidal ideation across age [37], and Cranney & Wilkinson reported that, in unadjusted data, unmarried individuals had approximately threefold higher prevalence of thinking about, planning, and/or attempting suicide (all ps < 0.001) at a given time [5].

Likewise, the inverse association between education and ideation aligns with literature linking educational attainment to cognitive/behavioral coping resources and health literacy. For example, Ludwig et al. described an inverse association consistent with a protective pattern of education for the emergence of suicidal ideation in Europe [19]. This accords with findings from the United States indicating that individuals with only a high school education showed higher suicide mortality compared with those with higher education [26]. Similarly, Zhu et al. found that a reduction in educational attainment was linked to an increase in the risk of suicide attempts [46]. Taken together, these studies consistently report inverse associations between education and suicidality, suggesting that education-related contexts are relevant for outreach and prevention planning.

In the adjusted models, no significant gender differences were detected, although some unadjusted comparisons suggested slightly higher mean scores among women. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted with caution. At the same time, the null adjusted associations for gender and urban–rural residence differ from reports describing higher ideation among women or notable urban–rural disparities. For instance, Yoon Young Kim et al. observed higher prevalence of suicidal ideation among women across age groups [17], and Lauren N. Forrest et al., using national U.S. data, reported that women more often endorsed suicidal thoughts and planning [8]. Although early marriage—particularly among women in Iran—and challenges of married life have been noted in the literature as being associated with women’s suicidal thoughts [7, 13], the results of this study did not show a significant association in this sample. Differential disclosure remains a possibility: despite assurances of anonymity, women may have been less inclined to self-disclose due to social stigma. Consistent with these findings, evidence from Kerman Province reported no significant difference in suicidal tendencies between men and women [27].

Our study also found no significant difference in suicidal ideation between urban and rural areas. A plausible contextual explanation is shared exposures—such as the lasting impact of the 2003 Bam earthquake, regional climatic stressors, and water scarcity—as well as sparse rural settlements with close proximity to urban centers. In line with this, Hoffmann et al. reported no meaningful differences in suicidal ideation by urban versus rural residence [12].

Several nonexclusive explanations may account for these patterns. First, our outcome—ideation rather than attempts or mortality—addresses a specific segment of the suicidality continuum, for which gender patterns may diverge from those observed for deaths by suicide. Second, local norms around disclosure may shape self-report. Third, a binary urban–rural indicator may mask within-urban deprivation (e.g., small cities and peri-urban settlements) and within-rural protective factors (e.g., kinship networks), attenuating detectable differences. The higher bivariate means among students and unemployed participants—but absence of independent associations after adjustment—may reflect overlap with education, age structure, and marital status, or heterogeneity in job quality not captured by a simple employment-status measure. Relatedly, Raschke et al. reported associations of low income, unemployment, and financial difficulties with multiple forms of suicidal behavior [31].

The small explained variance (R2 = 0.028) indicates that demographic markers account for only a limited portion of heterogeneity in ideation, suggesting that while the observed associations are statistically significant, their practical significance is limited. Prior work points to psychological constructs (depressive symptoms, hopelessness, perceived burdensomeness) [18], recent stressors [18], interpersonal dynamics (social isolation, intimate partner violence) [9], and substance use as salient correlates that merit inclusion in future models [6].

These considerations are especially relevant in southeastern Iran, where economic volatility, drought-related livelihood stress, and geographic dispersion intersect with strong family and tribal structures, yielding a risk landscape that does not map neatly onto an urban–rural binary [28]. The association of marriage with lower scores is consistent with robust kin support and community embeddedness, whereas the lack of a residence gradient may reflect convergence in material hardship across smaller urban centers and surrounding rural districts.

Programmatically, higher mean scores among unmarried adults and individuals in education or unemployment contexts may point to pragmatic targets for enhanced screening and linkage within primary care, universities, and employment services, provided that implementation safeguards confidentiality and addresses stigma [1, 32, 38]. Given the limited explanatory power of demographic covariates, future studies in this region would benefit from integrating validated measures of depression, hopelessness, and substance use,disaggregating employment by job quality/security; applying multilevel or longitudinal designs to separate individual- from area-level correlates; and capturing locally relevant stressors (e.g., debt, migration, climate-related shocks) [20, 39]. Such work can inform locally grounded risk profiles to support tailored prevention, without implying causation.

Conclusion

In this population-based cross-sectional survey from southeastern Iran using the BSSI, 5.9% of participants reported suicidal ideation. In adjusted models, being married and having higher educational attainment were associated with lower ideation scores, whereas gender, age, place of residence, and employment status showed no independent associations. Unadjusted comparisons showed higher mean scores among students and unemployed participants, but these differences did not remain after adjustment. The model’s small explained variance (R2 = 0.028) suggests that the demographic variables captured here account for limited heterogeneity in ideation, highlighting the likely relevance of psychological, interpersonal, and contextual factors not measured.

Within this regional context—marked by economic volatility and environmental stressors—service planning could emphasize confidential, stigma-sensitive screening and referral pathways in primary care, universities, and employment-related settings, while avoiding causal interpretations. Given the cross-sectional design and reliance on a single instrument, future studies should incorporate validated measures of depression, hopelessness, and substance use; refine socioeconomic indicators (e.g., job quality/security); and apply multilevel or longitudinal designs to better examine individual- and area-level correlates. Such evidence can inform locally grounded prevention efforts aligned with the needs of southeastern Iran.

Limitations

This cross-sectional design and single time point preclude causal inference and limit conclusions about temporality; findings pertain to ideation rather than attempts or mortality. Data were self-reported on a sensitive topic, so underreporting or differential disclosure (e.g., by gender or marital status) is possible despite anonymity. We used stratified random sampling within public health facilities across five counties to reduce selection bias; nevertheless, this frame may over-represent health-service users, and individuals who do not seek care may differ, which constrains generalizability to the broader population. Measurement was limited to the BSSI and a set of demographic/socioeconomic variables; some exposures were coarse (e.g., binary urban–rural, employment without job quality/security). Finally, analyses did not explicitly model clustering by facility/county, and the regression explained modest variance (R2 = 0.028), indicating unmeasured heterogeneity. Despite these considerations, the study provides regionally informative estimates of suicidal ideation and its correlates in southeastern Iran. Findings should be interpreted cautiously in light of potential underreporting due to stigma around suicide.
