Stress hyperglycemia ratio improves 28-day mortality prediction beyond GRACE score in critically ill patients with acute coronary syndrome: a retrospective cohort study from MIMIC-IV
Authors: Jiaxin Yang, Yuxin Ma, Jia Wang, Shuhui Shen, Tong Zou
Published: 2026

Background

Acute Coronary Syndrome (ACS) imposes a substantial burden on healthcare systems [1, 2], and accurate risk stratification is essential for guiding therapy and optimizing patient outcomes. Currently, international guidelines recommend the Global Registry of Acute Coronary Events (GRACE) score as the standard tool for ACS risk assessment [3, 4]. Although the GRACE score primarily reflects hemodynamic and renal markers [5] and has demonstrated robust predictive performance in multiple external validation studies [6, 7], it may not fully capture the pathophysiological impact of acute metabolic derangements in critically ill patients.

Stress hyperglycemia is closely associated with adverse outcomes in critically ill patients. Compared to admission blood glucose alone, which is influenced by chronic glycemic fluctuations, the Stress Hyperglycemia Ratio (SHR) corrects for baseline glucose using Glycosylated Hemoglobin(HbA1c), serving as a more specific biomarker of critical illness-related metabolic stress [8]. This is particularly relevant in critically ill ACS patients, where acute metabolic stress is more pronounced and may not be captured by conventional hemodynamic-based scores such as GRACE.

Although prior studies have suggested SHR adds predictive value to the GRACE score [9–11], evidence on its incremental predictive value beyond established risk scores remains limited. Many prior studies were constrained by small sample sizes and single-center designs, and primarily focused on in-hospital mortality, which is affected by variations in length of stay. Addressing this specific knowledge gap, the present study provides the first systematic evaluation of integrating SHR with the GRACE score for predicting 28-day mortality in critically ill ACS patients, thereby complementing the predominantly hemodynamic GRACE framework with a metabolic dimension.

Using the extensive clinical data from the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV (MIMIC-IV) database, this study aimed to: (1) investigate the association between SHR and 28-day mortality in critically ill ACS patients; (2) evaluate the incremental predictive value of SHR over the traditional GRACE score; and (3) provide a complementary validation of these findings through machine learning algorithms and sensitivity analyses.

Methods

Data source and study population

This retrospective cohort study utilized data from the MIMIC-IV database (version 3.1). This large-scale, freely accessible critical care database contains comprehensive, de-identified clinical records of patients admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU) at Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center (BIDMC) in Boston, Massachusetts, between 2008 and 2022.

The initial screening identified 6,681 adult patients (aged ≥ 18 years) who were admitted to the ICU for the first time with a primary diagnosis of ACS. To ensure the reliability of the analysis, the following exclusion criteria were applied: (1) patients with an ICU length of stay of less than 12 h; (2) individuals with missing data on admission blood glucose or HbA1c; and (3) patients with a co-existing diagnosis of malignancy. Ultimately, a total of 2,763 eligible participants were enrolled in the final analysis (Fig. 1). The study population was stratified into four groups (Q1–Q4) based on the quartiles of the SHR.

Flowchart of patient selection. Abbreviations: ACS, Acute coronary syndrome; HbA1c, Hemoglobin A1c; ICU, Intensive care unit; MIMIC-IV, Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV

This study was conducted in strict accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The establishment of the MIMIC-IV database was approved by the Institutional Review Boards of the Massachusetts Institute of Technology and BIDMC. The requirement for individual informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the research and the use of de-identified data. One author (J.Y.) completed the required Collaborative Institutional Training Initiative curriculum (Certification Number: 13257386) to gain authorized access to the database.

Variable extraction and definition

Data extraction was performed using Structured Query Language (SQL) via Navicat Premium (version 16.1.10). A comprehensive set of covariates was retrieved, including: (1) Demographics: age, gender, ethnicity, and body mass index (BMI); (2) Comorbidities and acute clinical characteristics: myocardial infarction (MI) type (ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction [STEMI] and non-STEMI), hypertension, diabetes mellitus, congestive heart failure (CHF), cerebrovascular disease, atrial fibrillation, and renal disease, identified using International Classification of Diseases, 9th Revision(ICD-9) and ICD-10 codes; (3) Vital signs: heart rate, systolic blood pressure (SBP), and respiratory rate, recorded within the first 24 h of ICU admission; and (4) Therapeutic interventions and advanced life support: revascularization status (including percutaneous coronary intervention [PCI], coronary artery bypass grafting [CABG]), mechanical circulatory support [MCS], encompassing intra-aortic balloon pump [IABP], extracorporeal membrane oxygenation [ECMO] and Impella), mechanical ventilation, use of vasoactive agents, and other pharmacological treatments.

Laboratory parameters, such as white blood cell (WBC) count, platelets, hemoglobin, and creatinine, were extracted from the first measurement within 24 h of ICU admission. Admission glucose was defined as the measurement closest to ICU entry within 24 h, using laboratory results as the primary source and bedside records as a substitute for missing data. HbA1c was extracted through a hierarchical approach where the first record within 7 days post-admission was utilized first, followed by a 90-day pre-admission look-back for remaining cases. Over 99% of these measurements were obtained between 30 days prior to and 7 days after ICU admission. The SHR was calculated using the standard formula: SHR = Admission Glucose (mg/dL)/(28.7 × HbA1c—46.7).

The GRACE risk score was calculated according to its established algorithm. Objective variables (age, heart rate, SBP, and initial creatinine) were extracted directly from the database. Given that specific components such as Killip class, cardiac arrest, and ST-segment deviation were not explicitly structured in the MIMIC-IV database, we derived them using validated mapping algorithms based on diagnostic codes. Detailed definitions and extraction strategies for these reconstructed components are provided in Table S6.

Outcomes

The primary endpoint was 28-day all-cause mortality following ICU admission, a standardized metric for assessing short-term critical care outcomes. Secondary endpoints included ICU mortality and in-hospital mortality. Survival data were obtained from hospital records and the linkage to state death registries.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were first assessed for normality. Normally distributed variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and compared using One-way ANOVA, while non-normally distributed variables were presented as median [interquartile range, IQR] and compared using the Kruskal–Wallis test. Categorical variables were reported as frequencies (percentages) and analyzed using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Variables with a missing rate exceeding 25% (such as AST and ALT, which approached 50%) were only analyzed descriptively based on available cases and were strictly excluded from predictive modeling to ensure data reliability. For the remaining baseline covariates, all of which had missing rates below 25%, missing values were addressed using K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN) imputation.

To evaluate the independent dose–response association between SHR and outcomes, Restricted Cubic Splines (RCS) regression with four knots (at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles) was employed, adjusting for all covariates included in the fully adjusted model (Model 4). To facilitate clinical risk stratification, the optimal prognostic cutoff value for SHR was determined using the Youden index derived from the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve. Survival patterns were compared using Kaplan–Meier curves and the Log-rank test. To minimize residual confounding inherent to the retrospective design and further evaluate the independent prognostic value of SHR, we constructed four progressive multivariable Cox proportional hazards models to estimate Hazard Ratios (HRs) and 95% Confidence Intervals (CIs): Model 1 (unadjusted); Model 2 (adjusted for age and gender); Model 3 (further adjusted for chronic comorbidities, including hypertension, renal disease, CHF, and cerebrovascular disease); and Model 4 (additionally adjusted for acute clinical characteristics and intensive treatments, including MI type, revascularization status, MCS, vasoactive agents, beta-blockers, and mechanical ventilation). The proportional hazards (PH) assumption was evaluated using the Schoenfeld residuals test. Variables violating this assumption were adjusted using stratified Cox regression, ensuring that all final fully adjusted models strictly satisfied the PH assumption. Furthermore, to evaluate the consistency of the association between SHR and 28-day mortality across diverse clinical profiles, we conducted subgroup analyses. Patients were stratified by age, gender, diabetes mellitus, hypertension, renal disease, CHF, MI type, revascularization status, MCS, and vasoactive agents. All subgroup analyses were adjusted for the covariates included in the fully adjusted model (Model 4), and P-values for interaction were calculated using likelihood ratio tests to evaluate potential heterogeneity.

The predictive performance was quantified using the Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUC). The incremental value of adding SHR to the GRACE score was evaluated using the DeLong test, Net Reclassification Improvement (NRI), and Integrated Discrimination Improvement (IDI) based on 1,000 bootstrap resamples. A prognostic nomogram was developed, with calibration assessed via calibration plots, the Hosmer–Lemeshow test, and the Brier score. Clinical utility was further evaluated using Decision Curve Analysis (DCA).

To corroborate the robustness of our findings, machine learning algorithms were employed as a complementary validation strategy. The cohort was randomly partitioned into a training set (70%, N = 1,935) and an independent test set (30%, N = 828). Three models—Logistic Regression, Random Forest, and Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost)—were trained using 18 clinical features (Table S7) determined based on clinical significance and early baseline availability, excluding downstream treatments. To rigorously prevent potential overfitting, hyperparameters were optimized exclusively within the training set via 5-fold cross-validation with grid search (Table S8), and final model performance was evaluated solely on the unseen independent test set. The SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) framework was applied to the best-performing model to interpret feature importance.

To address potential selection bias inherent to our retrospective cohort, sensitivity analyses were conducted. We performed multiple imputation for the 3,030 patients initially excluded due to missing HbA1c, utilizing Predictive Mean Matching (PMM) within the Multiple Imputation by Chained Equations (MICE) framework. The primary analyses were then repeated in this imputed full cohort.

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.4.2), with a two-sided P-value < 0.05 considered statistically significant. During the preparation of this work, the authors used ChatGPT 5.2 (OpenAI), Claude Opus 4.5 (Anthropic), and Gemini 3 Pro (Google) to assist in drafting and refining the language of the manuscript. The authors confirm that all statistical analyses were performed independently using R software, and all AI-generated text was rigorously verified against the original data to ensure accuracy. The authors take full responsibility for the final content of the publication.

Results

Baseline characteristics of the study population

As illustrated in the study flowchart (Fig. 1), a total of 6,681 patients with a first ICU admission for ACS were initially screened. Following the rigorous application of exclusion criteria, 3,918 individuals were excluded, resulting in a final analysis cohort of 2,763 patients. The median age of the study population was 69 years, with a male predominance (70.9%).

The detailed baseline characteristics, stratified by SHR quartiles, are summarized in Table 1 and Table S2. The study groups were comparable regarding demographic parameters, with no significant heterogeneity observed in age, gender, or BMI across the quartiles (all P > 0.05). Conversely, markers of disease severity exhibited distinct patterns. Notably, patients in the highest SHR quartile (Q4) had a significantly higher prevalence of STEMI (34.5% vs. 14.1%) and CHF (60.4% vs. 41.0%) compared to those in the lowest quartile (Q1) (all P < 0.001). Furthermore, the GRACE risk score demonstrated a significant upward trend parallel to increasing SHR levels (P < 0.001), suggesting a correlation between stress hyperglycemia intensity and baseline clinical risk. In terms of clinical interventions, patients in the highest SHR quartile more frequently required MCS (19.6% vs. 9.1%) but underwent revascularization significantly less often (59.9% vs. 86.7%) (P < 0.001).

Regarding clinical outcomes, the overall incidence of the primary endpoint, 28-day mortality, was 8.3% (229/2,763). A pronounced mortality gradient was observed across the SHR groups, with the risk of death rising progressively from 4.6% (31/668) in the lowest quartile (Q1) to 16.8% (117/695) in the highest quartile (Q4) (P for trend < 0.001). Consistent hierarchical patterns were also identified for secondary endpoints, including ICU mortality and in-hospital mortality.

Association between SHR and clinical outcomes

Fully adjusted RCS analysis demonstrated a continuous linear dose–response association between SHR and the risk of 28-day mortality (P for non-linearity = 0.755, Fig. 2A). The risk profile was characterized by a steady upward trajectory as SHR increased, with no significant physiological inflection point. Comparable linear patterns were also consistently observed for the secondary outcomes of in-hospital mortality (P for non-linearity = 0.872) and ICU mortality (P for non-linearity = 0.982, Figure S1). Based on the Youden index, the optimal prognostic cutoff value for SHR in predicting 28-day mortality was identified as 1.165 (sensitivity: 60.3%, specificity: 68.8%). Kaplan–Meier survival estimates indicated that patients in the highest SHR quartile (Q4) experienced significantly lower cumulative survival rates compared to those in the lower quartiles for 28-day, ICU, and in-hospital mortality (all Log-rank P < 0.0001, Fig. 2B and Figure S1).

Multivariable Cox proportional hazards models corroborated the independent prognostic value of SHR (Table 2). When analyzed as a continuous variable, elevated SHR was significantly associated with an increased risk of 28-day mortality in the unadjusted model (HR 3.04, 95% CI: 2.59–3.56, P < 0.001). Crucially, this association remained robust after full adjustment for potential confounders in Model 4 (adjusted HR 1.60, 95% CI: 1.30–1.96, P < 0.001). Categorical analysis further confirmed these findings, compared with the lowest quartile (Q1, Reference), patients in the highest quartile (Q4) exhibited a significantly elevated adjusted mortality risk (HR 1.73, 95% CI: 1.13–2.64, P = 0.012). Similarly, fully adjusted analyses demonstrated that SHR was an independent predictor of both ICU mortality (HR 1.72, 95% CI: 1.37–2.17, P < 0.001) and in-hospital mortality (HR 1.91, 95% CI: 1.54–2.37, P < 0.001).

Furthermore, subgroup analyses demonstrated the consistency of this association across various clinical strata. As shown in Fig. 3, no significant interactions were observed with respect to age, gender, diabetes, hypertension, renal disease, CHF, use of vasoactive agents, or MI type (all P for interaction > 0.05), indicating that the prognostic value of SHR remains stable across these diverse subpopulations. Incremental predictive value of SHR over the GRACE score

The GRACE score alone demonstrated good predictive discrimination for the primary endpoint of 28-day mortality, with an AUC of 0.796 (95% CI: 0.76–0.83). Adding SHR to the GRACE model significantly improved predictive accuracy, increasing the AUC to 0.816 (95% CI: 0.79–0.85). Although the absolute increase was small (∆AUC = 0.02), the improvement was statistically significant (DeLong test P < 0.001, Fig. 2C). Similar improvements were observed for secondary outcomes, with the combined model showing better discrimination for both in-hospital mortality (AUC 0.839 vs. 0.817, P < 0.001) and ICU mortality (AUC 0.842 vs. 0.815, P < 0.001). In terms of diagnostic performance, the combined model achieved a sensitivity of 73.4%, a specificity of 77.0%, and a negative predictive value of 97.0%.

Reclassification analyses provided further evidence of the model's improvement. The addition of SHR to the GRACE score significantly improved risk prediction, with an IDI of 0.023 (P < 0.001) and an NRI of 0.037 (Table 3). Finally, DCA supported the clinical utility of the combined model. As shown in Fig. 2D, the integrated model (GRACE + SHR) offered a greater net benefit than the GRACE score alone across a wide range of threshold probabilities (3%–15%). This suggests that incorporating SHR into clinical decision-making could improve patient outcomes without increasing unnecessary interventions.

Construction and calibration of the prognostic nomogram

To translate the multivariable results into a practical clinical tool, a prognostic nomogram was constructed to predict the individual probability of 28-day mortality based on the integrated GRACE + SHR model (Figure S2A). The predictive accuracy of the nomogram was rigorously assessed. Bootstrap calibration plots revealed excellent concordance between the predicted probabilities and the actual observed outcomes, as evidenced by a mean absolute error of 0.005 (Figure S2B). Additionally, the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test indicated a robust model fit (P = 0.126), suggesting no significant deviation between predicted and observed risks. Risk stratification analysis further validated the discrimination capacity of the nomogram (Figure S2C). Patients were accurately stratified into four distinct risk categories: low (< 5%), intermediate (5–10%), high (10–20%), and very-high (> 20%). The predicted mortality risks for these groups (2.6%, 6.9%, 13.9%, and 36.2%, respectively) aligned closely with the actual observed mortality rates (2.0%, 8.8%, 13.7%, and 36.0%), thereby confirming the reliability of the nomogram for personalized risk assessment in clinical practice.

Validation via machine learning algorithms

To complement our primary analyses, we further compared the performance of advanced machine learning algorithms (XGBoost and Random Forest) against traditional Logistic Regression in the primary cohort (Table S3, Figure S3A). Among the evaluated models, XGBoost achieved the highest discrimination in the test set (AUC 0.880, 95% CI: 0.841–0.919), followed closely by Random Forest (AUC 0.879). Although these machine learning models were numerically superior to the full-variable Logistic Regression model (AUC 0.858), the difference did not reach statistical significance. However, crucially, the machine learning models significantly outperformed the traditional model based solely on the GRACE score (P = 0.002), highlighting the necessity of multi-dimensional feature integration.

SHAP analysis was employed to elucidate feature importance and interpret the "black box" nature of the XGBoost model (Figure S3C). Consistent with clinical expectations, the GRACE score emerged as the most influential predictor (mean absolute SHAP value = 0.725). Notably, SHR was identified as a pivotal metabolic marker, ranking 7th in overall importance (SHAP value = 0.201) and outperforming traditional parameters such as WBC count, blood pressure, and BMI. The SHAP dependence plot (Figure S3B) further revealed a non-linear positive correlation between SHR and predicted mortality risk, providing independent validation of the prognostic value of SHR beyond the GRACE score.

Sensitivity analysis

To address potential selection bias arising from missing HbA1c data, we compared the baseline characteristics of included and excluded patients (Table S1). The excluded cohort was older (median age 74.1 vs. 69.0 years) and exhibited a heavier burden of chronic comorbidities, including CHF (55.9% vs. 45.5%) and renal disease (33.6% vs. 25.2%). Furthermore, these excluded patients presented with greater acute disease severity, as evidenced by worse Killip classes, a higher incidence of cardiac arrest (10.6% vs. 7.7%), higher GRACE scores (190 vs. 176), a markedly higher 28-day mortality rate (22.7% vs. 8.3%), and a substantially lower revascularization rate (34.3% vs. 75.4%). Consequently, multiple imputation was performed to generate a full cohort (N = 5,793) for sensitivity analysis. Multivariable Cox regression in the imputed full cohort corroborated the primary findings. SHR index was independently associated with an increased risk of 28-day mortality (HR 1.52, 95% CI 1.35 to 1.70, P < 0.001; Table S4). And patients in the highest SHR quartile (Q4) maintained a significantly increased risk of 28-day mortality compared to the lowest quartile (Q1) after full adjustment (HR 2.04, 95% CI 1.65 to 2.51, P < 0.001). Regarding predictive performance, the incorporation of SHR into the GRACE score yielded a significant improvement in discrimination, elevating the AUC from 0.769 to 0.788 (P < 0.001; Table S5). Machine learning validation in the full cohort further reinforced these results, with the XGBoost model demonstrating superior discrimination (AUC = 0.860) and statistically outperforming the traditional Logistic Regression model (P = 0.028; Table S3). In the full-cohort SHAP analysis, the importance of SHR rose to the 4th rank among all clinical features, trailing only creatinine, GRACE score, and age.

Discussion

In this retrospective cohort study utilizing the large-scale MIMIC-IV database, we provided supportive evidence regarding the prognostic significance of SHR in critically ill patients with ACS. The principal findings of our investigation are fourfold: (1) elevated SHR serves as an independent predictor of 28-day, in-hospital, and ICU mortality, exhibiting consistent prognostic utility across diverse subgroups; (2) a continuous linear dose–response association exists between SHR and 28-day mortality, with an optimal cutoff of 1.165 identified to facilitate practical bedside risk stratification; (3) the integration of SHR into the GRACE score provided valuable incremental prognostic information, driven primarily by improved risk reclassification (NRI and IDI) rather than the modest gain in overall discrimination (AUC); and (4) as a complementary analytical approach, machine learning algorithms corroborated the clinical relevance of SHR by ranking it among the top 10 most influential features, thereby supporting its predictive consistency across different modeling methodologies.

Our findings align with existing evidence supporting SHR as a robust metric to quantify acute stress while adjusting for chronic glycemic levels [8, 12–14]. Although previous studies reported lower risk estimates when evaluating in-hospital mortality [15], our standardized 28-day endpoint more comprehensively captures early deaths driven by acute metabolic toxicity irrespective of variable discharge practices. Furthermore, the alignment of our short-term results with established long-term risk profiles [16] suggests that the prognostic impact of stress hyperglycemia is predominantly concentrated in the acute phase.

Our RCS analysis demonstrated a continuous linear dose–response association between SHR and mortality. While previous studies in less severe cohorts reported J-shaped relationships reflecting an adaptive phase of moderate stress hyperglycemia [9, 15, 17], our findings suggest that critically ill ACS patients have already surpassed this compensatory threshold upon admission. In this extreme physiological state, elevated SHR directly drives pathological mechanisms such as profound oxidative stress and endothelial injury. To facilitate practical risk stratification, we identified an optimal prognostic cutoff of 1.165. Although differing slightly from thresholds in general populations (0.57 to 1.20) [9, 18, 19], this value inherently reflects the uniquely high baseline physiological stress of the intensive care environment.

In terms of risk stratification, our study addresses a gap in the application of the GRACE score. Although recommended by international guidelines, the GRACE score primarily evaluates hemodynamic instability and renal dysfunction, lacking indicators of acute metabolic stress [20]. This omission leads to variable performance across different patient groups [21, 22]; even the machine-learning-based GRACE 3.0 score excludes metabolic markers [23]. Conversely, SHR specifically reflects acute metabolic stress and neurohormonal activation. Therefore, adding SHR to the GRACE score integrates the assessment of both hemodynamic and metabolic statuses, providing a more comprehensive risk evaluation. This approach parallels recent efforts to augment the GRACE score with additional bedside parameters. For instance, Demirtaş İnci et al. demonstrated that combining the heart rate-corrected QT (QTc) interval with the GRACE score improved early mortality prediction in patients with NSTE-ACS [24]. In a similar vein, our study underscores the importance of the metabolic dimension. To our knowledge, this is the first study to systematically evaluate this integration for standardized 28-day mortality using a large public database. Building upon previous findings in a smaller cohort of acute myocardial infarction patients [11], our research confirms the incremental value of SHR utilizing a substantially larger sample size and a standardized endpoint. Although the absolute AUC increase of 2.0% was modest, this improvement aligns with prior reports and, alongside significant gains in risk reclassification, supports the potential clinical utility of incorporating metabolic markers into traditional risk scoring systems.

Our complementary machine learning analysis suggests that SHR may offer greater prognostic utility than a history of diabetes. In the SHAP analysis of the primary cohort, SHR was the 7th most important feature, whereas a history of diabetes contributed minimal value to the model. This finding is consistent with our subgroup analysis, which showed no significant interaction between SHR and diabetes status. Similarly, a large meta-analysis [25] confirmed that the mortality risk associated with high SHR remained stable in the diabetic subgroup. Therefore, regardless of diabetes status, the intensity of the acute stress response measured by SHR appears to be the stronger determinant of survival during the acute phase of ACS.

The pathophysiological mechanisms of stress hyperglycemia involve several interconnected pathways. Acute cardiovascular events activate the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis and the sympathetic nervous system. This leads to the release of counter-regulatory hormones, which promote hepatic gluconeogenesis, inhibit peripheral glucose uptake, and induce insulin resistance [17, 26]. At the same time, inflammatory cytokines (such as TNF-α and IL-6) worsen insulin resistance by activating the IκB kinase (IKK) complex [26]. Hyperglycemia activates the PKC, polyol, and hexosamine pathways, promoting the production of advanced glycation end products (AGEs) [27] and leading to oxidative stress [27, 28]. This oxidative stress further fuels a vicious cycle of hyperglycemia, oxidative stress, and inflammation [29]. Additionally, acute hyperglycemia can worsen microvascular obstruction [30], impair endothelium-dependent vasodilation [31], and increase platelet reactivity [32]. By correcting for baseline glucose levels as reflected by HbA1c, SHR provides a more accurate measure of acute metabolic stress [33].

Our findings have significant implications for bedside risk stratification. By requiring only admission blood glucose and HbA1c, SHR is a pragmatic tool for early clinical use. The nomogram developed in this study translates our integrated model into a visual platform for individualized assessment, effectively stratifying patients into four clinically actionable risk categories based on predicted mortality. Additionally, our study offers a new perspective on interpreting blood glucose in the critically ill. The NICE-SUGAR trials [34, 35] showed that intensive glucose control increased 90-day mortality, leading to more relaxed glycemic targets. However, these absolute targets often overlook the individual’s chronic glycemic background. The CONTROLING study [36] showed that setting individual targets based on HbA1c significantly reduces hypoglycemia risk. In this context, the identified threshold of 1.165 serves as a critical clinical marker; patients exceeding this value represent a high-risk phenotype that likely reflects severe underlying physiological distress. In clinical practice, this threshold can help clinicians rapidly prioritize high-risk individuals for intensified monitoring and advanced life support. Although our findings support SHR as a useful prognostic indicator in this population, its transition from a risk-stratification tool to a therapeutic target requires cautious interpretation. Future randomized trials are necessary to determine whether targeted interventions for elevated SHR can improve clinical outcomes.

Limitations

Our study has several limitations. First, the retrospective single-center design precludes causal inference, and residual confounding from unmeasured variables such as reperfusion time and specific imaging parameters may persist despite extensive adjustment. Furthermore, the reconstruction of the GRACE score from retrospective ICD codes may introduce minor deviations from prospective bedside clinical assessments. Second, the requirement for HbA1c testing introduces significant selection bias characterized by a survivor effect. In the ICU, HbA1c measurement is not random but often driven by suspected metabolic abnormalities and the length of hospitalization. As baseline comparisons revealed, excluded patients experienced substantially higher mortality and lower revascularization rates. This inherent exclusion of highly unstable patients who suffer very early mortality before metabolic screening implies that our analyzed cohort represents a clinically selected, relatively stable subgroup with higher treatment intensity, thereby limiting the generalizability of our findings to all unselected critically ill ACS patients. Third, the analyzed population exhibits considerable clinical heterogeneity. The substantial proportion of patients undergoing revascularization procedures, including coronary artery bypass grafting, indicates that post-operative cases constitute a significant segment of our cohort. The distinct pathophysiological and risk profiles of these post-surgical patients compared to primary ACS cases must be considered when interpreting the results. Fourth, although integrating SHR provided independent prognostic value, the absolute improvement in discrimination over the GRACE score was modest. Nevertheless, the significant gains in risk reclassification metrics demonstrate its practical value in refining early bedside assessment. Finally, as our focus was limited to 28-day mortality, the prognostic value of SHR for long-term outcomes warrants further investigation.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this large-scale retrospective analysis of the MIMIC-IV database shows that SHR is an independent predictor of standardized 28-day mortality in critically ill ACS patients. The relationship exhibits a continuous linear dose–response pattern, with an optimal cutoff of 1.165 identified to facilitate practical risk stratification.

Combining SHR with the GRACE score enhances prognostic assessment, refining patient risk stratification and addressing the omission of metabolic factors in traditional models. Machine learning algorithms further support the independent prognostic value of SHR. Simple to calculate and clinically practical, SHR warrants wider application, although future multicenter prospective studies are needed to verify these findings. Ultimately, SHR serves as a valuable tool for early risk assessment rather than a direct therapeutic target.
