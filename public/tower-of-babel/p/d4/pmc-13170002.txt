Healthy ageing and quality of life: exploring factors associated with quality of life among older adults in Tabuk, Saudi Arabia
Authors: Rahaf Alatawi, Sara Aljadrawi, Yara Aljohani, Turki Alanazi, Hanan Alshareef, Ahmed Mohsen Elsaid Hamdan, Kousalya Prabahar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPopulation ageing poses significant challenges to health systems worldwide, emphasizing the need to promote healthy ageing and maintain quality of life (QoL) among older adults. In Saudi Arabia, rapid demographic transitions and health reforms under Vision 2030 highlight the importance of understanding region-specific factors associated with QoL, particularly among community-dwelling older adults. This study aimed to examine the associations between healthy ageing indicators and QoL, and to identify key factors associated with QoL among older adults residing in Tabuk, Saudi Arabia.MethodsBetween February and April 2025, a cross-sectional survey was implemented at the community level involving 372 individuals aged 65 years or above. Information was gathered via in-person interviews using a standardized instrument that assessed demographic and health-related variables, health literacy, lifestyle behaviors, and QoL using the Arabic SF-36 questionnaire. Both correlation testing and multivariable linear regression analysis were applied to identify factors independently associated with overall QoL.ResultsParticipants’ age averaged 71.8 ± 5.3 years, with a high prevalence of multimorbidity and polypharmacy. Overall QoL scores indicated moderate impairment, particularly in physical domains. Physical functioning (β = 0.31) and emotional well-being (β = 0.24) showed the strongest positive associations with QoL, while the number of chronic diseases (β = − 0.28) and polypharmacy (β = − 0.12) were negatively associated with QoL (all p < 0.05). Social engagement and health literacy were also positively associated with QoL. Chronological age was not independently associated after adjustment.ConclusionQoL among older adults in Tabuk is shaped predominantly by modifiable physical, emotional, and social factors rather than age alone. Multidimensional healthy ageing strategies that focus on functional ability, mental health, social participation, and optimized medication use may contribute to improved wellbeing; further research is needed to confirm their impact.Keywords: Healthy ageing, Quality of life, Older adults, SF-36, Multimorbidity, Social engagement, Health literacy, Saudi Arabia

Introduction

Population ageing is a global demographic phenomenon with significant consequences for healthcare systems, social structures, and economic development [1]. As life expectancy increases worldwide, ensuring that individuals age in a healthy and productive manner has become a priority for policymakers and healthcare providers [2]. The World Health Organization (WHO) describes healthy ageing as the ongoing development and preservation of functional ability that promotes wellbeing during older adulthood [3]. It encompasses physical, psychological, and social domains that allow older adults not only to live longer but to maintain autonomy, participate actively in society, and achieve a satisfying quality of life (QoL). With ageing comes increased vulnerability to chronic diseases, functional decline, cognitive impairment, and social isolation, all of which can undermine health and diminish QoL [4, 5]. Thus, identifying the factors associated with QoL in older adults is fundamental for informing strategies and policies that promote wellbeing and social participation in later life.

Saudi Arabia is undergoing a rapid demographic shift attributed to improvements in healthcare services, economic development, and strategic national reforms aligned with Vision 2030 [6–8]. Investments in preventive care, public health initiatives, and expanded access to medical services have contributed to a substantial decline in mortality rates, with the national death rate falling to 2.34 per 1,000 inhabitants in 2023 [9]. As a result, the proportion of older adults is increasing steadily and is projected to reach 18.4% of the population by 2050 [10]. This demographic transformation emphasizes the necessity of evidence-based strategies that target the health and social requirements of the expanding geriatric population. Older adults in Saudi Arabia frequently present with multiple comorbidities and are often exposed to polypharmacy, factors that have been associated with reduced functional capacity, psychological wellbeing, and overall QoL [11, 12]. Understanding how healthy ageing contributes to QoL in this population is therefore vital for enhancing clinical care, optimizing resource allocation, and developing targeted community-based support programs.

Existing international evidence suggests that QoL in older adults is associated with multiple interconnected factors, including physical health, emotional stability, functional ability, and cognitive and sensory capacities [13–15]. Longitudinal research in Europe has demonstrated that these domains exhibit country-specific variations, highlighting the importance of regional and cultural contexts when evaluating QoL [16]. Studies in Asia have shown that active ageing, social engagement, and adequate health literacy significantly enhance QoL among older adults [17]. Systematic reviews further indicate that physical activity, social participation, and mental wellbeing are strongly associated with better QoL in ageing populations [18]. Despite the growing global knowledge base, there remains a lack of region-specific studies in Saudi Arabia, and particularly in Tabuk, examining the associations between healthy ageing indicators and QoL among community-dwelling older adults.

Addressing this gap is crucial for informing national health policies and local public health strategies. As Saudi Arabia advances toward achieving Vision 2030 objectives, understanding the factors associated with QoL in its ageing population can help them inform targeted interventions aimed at promoting wellbeing, supporting functional ability, and encouraging social participation. In light of this, the present study aims to examine the associations between healthy ageing indicators and QoL among older adults in Tabuk, and to identify key factors linked to QoL that may guide future geriatric care initiatives and community health programs.

Methods

Study design

This research utilized a cross-sectional design to examine the impact of healthy ageing on QoL among community-dwelling older adults in Tabuk, Saudi Arabia. Data collection was conducted over a three-month period, from February to April 2025.

Study setting

The study was conducted in the community setting of Tabuk City, located in the northwest region of Saudi Arabia. Older adults were approached in residential neighborhoods, community centers, and primary healthcare outreach areas to ensure broad population representation.

Study population

The study population comprised adults aged 65 years and older residing in Tabuk. All participants were cognitively intact, able to communicate verbally, and willing to provide informed consent.

Sampling technique and sample size

A convenience sampling approach was employed due to the absence of a comprehensive registry of community-dwelling older adults in Tabuk. This method allowed for timely recruitment of a sufficient sample size while ensuring accessibility to participants across multiple neighborhoods and community centers. Although convenience sampling may limit generalizability, it is commonly used in community-based geriatric research where population lists are unavailable [19, 20].

The sample size was determined based on a 95% confidence level, a 5% margin of error, and an assumed population proportion of 50%, yielding a minimum requirement of 372 participants. Recruitment continued until this target was reached.

Data collection procedure

Data were gathered through face-to-face interviews conducted by trained co-investigators, using a structured questionnaire to ensure consistency and reliability. Eligible older adults were identified within the community and were approached individually. The study objectives, procedures, confidentiality, and voluntary participation requirements were explained before obtaining written informed consent.

Co-investigators administered a structured data collection form that included:

Socio-demographic data (age, sex, marital status, education, living arrangement).

Clinical information (chronic illnesses, medication use, functional limitations).

Each participant completed the questionnaire once during an interview lasting approximately 15–20 min.

Study instrument

The SF-36 Health Survey was employed to evaluate health-related QoL. The instrument assessed eight domains: physical functioning, role limitations due to physical health, role limitations due to emotional problems, energy/fatigue, emotional well-being, social functioning, bodily pain, and general health perceptions. Scoring was performed according to the RAND scoring manual, with higher scores indicating better QoL [22]. An overall QoL score was computed by averaging the eight SF-36 domain scores to provide a holistic measure of overall health-related QoL. This approach has been employed in previous studies to summarize multi-domain QoL assessments when a single composite indicator is needed [23, 24]. While this method deviates from the traditional physical and mental component summaries, it allows for straightforward interpretation of general wellbeing and comparison across participants. Domain-specific scores are also reported to provide detailed insights. Health literacy was assessed using a self-reported single-item measure in which participants rated their perceived ability to understand, evaluate, and manage health-related information. Brief, single‑item health literacy screening questions have been used in prior studies and shown to correlate with more comprehensive assessments of health literacy in adult populations, including older adults [25, 26]. Responses were interpreted as ‘adequate’ or ‘inadequate’ health literacy based on the participants’ self-assessment. While this single-item measure does not capture all dimensions of health literacy, prior studies indicate it provides a reasonably valid estimate for use in community-based surveys. Healthy ageing was examined through key functional, clinical, behavioral, and psychosocial indicators rather than a single composite index.

Ethical considerations

The study protocol was approved by the Local Research Ethics Committee prior to initiation [UT-520–270–2025]. All participants provided written informed consent. Participant confidentiality and anonymity were strictly maintained throughout data collection and analysis.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS version 27. Descriptive statistics, including frequencies and percentages for categorical variables and means with standard deviations for continuous variables, were used to summarize participant characteristics. Correlation tests (Pearson or Spearman as appropriate) were conducted to examine associations between healthy ageing factors and QoL. Additional analyses, including multiple linear regression, were performed to examine independent associations with QoL. Standardized beta coefficients (β) were reported to allow comparison of the relative strength of these associations. Multicollinearity was assessed and found to be acceptable (VIF < 5 for all variables). A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Participant characteristics

A total of 372 older adults aged 65 years and above participated in the study. The participants had a mean age of 71.8 years (SD = 5.3), and 54.3% (n = 202) were male. Most were married (62.6%), and 41.1% had not received any formal education (Table 1).

Correlation between healthy ageing variables and QoL

Significant correlations were observed between several healthy ageing indicators and QoL. Age showed a negative correlation with overall QoL (r = -0.29, p < 0.001), indicating lower QoL with increasing age. The number of chronic diseases also correlated negatively with QoL (r = -0.41, p < 0.001), as did polypharmacy (r = -0.33, p < 0.001).

Conversely, physical activity was positively correlated with overall QoL (r = 0.38, p < 0.001). Social engagement demonstrated significant positive correlations with social functioning (r = 0.44, p < 0.001). Higher health literacy was associated with better general health perceptions (r = 0.36, p < 0.001) (Table 4).

Factors associated with quality of life

Multiple linear regression analysis was conducted to examine independent associations with overall QoL (Table 5). The model was statistically significant (F = 36.2, p < 0.001) and explained 42.3% of the variance. Physical functioning showed the strongest positive association with QoL, followed by emotional well-being and social engagement, indicating that higher levels of these factors were linked to better overall QoL. In contrast, the number of chronic diseases and polypharmacy demonstrated negative associations with QoL, suggesting that a greater disease burden and higher medication use were related to poorer wellbeing. Age was not independently associated with QoL, indicating that its univariate correlation may be driven by functional status and comorbidities. Nevertheless, a substantial portion of the variance remains unexplained, suggesting that other factors not included in the model, such as socioeconomic status, diet, environmental factors, or cognitive function, may also contribute to QoL in older adults.

Discussion

The findings of this research enhance understanding of factors associated with healthy ageing and QoL among community-dwelling older adults in Tabuk, Saudi Arabia. The following interpretations distinguish between findings directly supported by the data and contextual explanations that should be interpreted cautiously. In the context of rapid demographic ageing and ongoing health system reforms under Saudi Vision 2030, the findings contribute valuable region-specific evidence that can inform geriatric care planning and community-based health interventions. Overall, QoL among older adults was associated with a combination of physical, emotional, and social factors, rather than chronological age alone.

Moderate impairment was observed in QoL, particularly in physical functioning and role limitations due to physical health, aligning with global evidence that physical decline is a prominent challenge of ageing [27, 28]. These lower physical domain scores likely reflect the high prevalence of chronic conditions such as hypertension, diabetes, and osteoarthritis, which contribute to pain, mobility limitations, and reduced independence [29, 30]. In contrast, social functioning and emotional well-being scores were comparatively higher, possibly reflecting social cohesion in the community. One potential explanation is the influence of strong family structures in Saudi Arabia [31–33], though this was not directly measured and should be interpreted cautiously.

Consistent with prior research [34–36], age showed a significant negative correlation with QoL in univariate analysis but was not independently associated in the multivariate model, suggesting that its effect may be mediated by functional status, comorbidity burden, and medication use. From a policy perspective, these findings suggest that geriatric care models could emphasize functional ability rather than chronological age; however, longitudinal studies are needed before definitive recommendations can be made. Our regression model accounted for 42.3% of the variance in overall QoL, indicating that additional factors such as socioeconomic status, environmental influences, diet, or cognitive function, may also contribute to QoL.

Among the variables examined, physical functioning had the largest effect size, highlighting its importance for independence, autonomy, and engagement in social activities [37]. Despite this, only a minority of participants reported regular physical activity, indicating a potential opportunity for targeted interventions such as age-friendly exercise programs, walking groups, and culturally tailored community-based rehabilitation services. Polypharmacy and multimorbidity were negatively associated with QoL, emphasizing the need for integrated, patient-centered care that addresses multiple chronic conditions simultaneously and includes regular medication reviews [38–41].

Emotional well-being and social engagement were positively associated with QoL, suggesting that even subclinical psychological distress and limited social participation can meaningfully impact overall wellbeing. Interventions incorporating mental health assessments, psychosocial support, and structured social opportunities (e.g., senior clubs or volunteer programs) could enhance QoL and reduce social isolation [42].

Health literacy was also positively related to QoL, particularly in general health perceptions. Older adults who perceive themselves as knowledgeable about their health are more likely to adopt preventive measures and utilize healthcare services effectively, indicating that targeted education programs may serve as a cost-effective strategy to promote self-management [43].

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference, limiting the ability to determine temporal relationships between healthy ageing indicators and QoL. Second, convenience sampling was used due to the absence of a comprehensive registry of older adults, which may introduce selection bias and reduce generalizability beyond the study sample. Third, the study was conducted exclusively in Tabuk, which restricts the applicability of the findings to other regions of Saudi Arabia with potentially different demographic, cultural, or healthcare contexts.

Fourth, data were self-reported, which may be subject to recall bias or social desirability bias, potentially affecting the accuracy of reported chronic conditions, medication use, physical activity, social engagement, and health literacy. Fifth, health literacy was measured using a single-item self-assessment, which may not fully capture its multidimensional nature and could underestimate or overestimate its relationship with QoL.

Sixth, the QoL scoring approach involved averaging the eight SF-36 domains into a single composite score, which deviates from the standard practice of using separate physical and mental component summaries. This approach may obscure domain-specific variations and limit detailed interpretation of specific aspects of QoL. Finally, there is potential for residual confounding, as other relevant variables such as socioeconomic status, dietary habits, nutritional status, environmental factors, or cognitive function were not included in the analysis. These unmeasured factors may influence both healthy ageing indicators and QoL, potentially biasing the observed associations.

Despite these limitations, the study provides valuable region-specific insights into factors associated with QoL among community-dwelling older adults in Tabuk, highlighting areas for future research and informing preliminary health promotion strategies.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that QoL among older adults in Tabuk is primarily associated with modifiable factors such as physical functioning, emotional well-being, social engagement, multimorbidity, and polypharmacy rather than chronological age alone. These findings suggest that integrated, multidimensional healthy ageing strategies focusing on functional ability, mental health, and social participation may be beneficial. Such approaches could help support QoL and enable Saudi Arabia’s ageing population to age with dignity, independence, and wellbeing. Future research should employ longitudinal designs and consider environmental, nutritional, and socioeconomic factors, as well as more comprehensive and validated measures of health literacy, to better understand the relationships between healthy ageing indicators and QoL and enhance the generalizability of findings across Saudi Arabia. Given the cross-sectional design of the current study, these implications should be interpreted with caution.
