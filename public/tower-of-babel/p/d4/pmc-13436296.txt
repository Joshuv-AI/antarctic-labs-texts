Comparison of two menopausal hormone therapy regimens in managing menopausal symptoms: a retrospective study
Authors: Yanqin Yu, Mengqin Lv, Yanhong Fu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFemoston (estradiol/dydrogesterone) and Climen (estradiol valerate/cyproterone acetate) are two widely prescribed sequential menopausal hormone therapy (MHT) regimens that differ in estrogen formulation, progestogen component, and sequential pattern. Head-to-head comparative data on their clinical efficacy and safety remain scarce.MethodsThis single-center retrospective study enrolled 80 perimenopausal and early postmenopausal women who received Femoston (n = 42) or Climen (n = 38) for six consecutive treatment cycles between January 2022 and January 2025. The primary outcome was post-treatment Kupperman Menopausal Index (KMI) score assessed by multivariable linear regression. Secondary outcomes included individual symptom response rates, serum sex hormone levels, lipid profile, hepatic enzymes, fasting plasma glucose, endometrial thickness, and adverse events.ResultsBoth regimens produced substantial reductions in KMI scores from baseline (Femoston: 26.90 ± 5.72 to 10.48 ± 3.72; Climen: 27.53 ± 6.08 to 11.74 ± 4.12; both P < 0.001). After multivariable adjustment, treatment group was not independently associated with post-treatment KMI score (β = 0.58, 95% CI: −0.82 to 1.98, P = 0.412). Response rates for hot flashes/sweating, insomnia, and mood symptoms were comparably high between groups (all P > 0.05). Both regimens significantly suppressed FSH and LH and elevated E₂. Regarding lipid profiles, Femoston significantly increased HDL-C (P = 0.001) whereas Climen did not (P = 0.402); Climen significantly increased TG (P = 0.048) whereas Femoston did not (P = 0.583). After Benjamini–Hochberg correction, the between-group difference in HDL-C remained significant (adjusted P = 0.024). No clinically significant changes in hepatic enzymes, fasting glucose, or endometrial thickness were observed in either group, and no serious adverse events occurred.ConclusionsFemoston and Climen provide comparable short-term menopausal symptom relief. The more favorable lipid profile with Femoston, particularly its HDL-C benefit, may inform individualized regimen selection, especially in women without indications for antiandrogenic therapy. Prospective studies with longer follow-up are warranted to evaluate hard cardiovascular endpoints.

Introduction

The menopausal transition is characterized by progressive ovarian follicular depletion and declining estrogen production, giving rise to a constellation of vasomotor, psychological, musculoskeletal, and genitourinary symptoms that substantially impair quality of life [1]. With global life expectancy continuing to rise, it is estimated that women will spend approximately one-third of their lives in the postmenopausal state [2], making effective symptom management a growing public health priority. Menopausal hormone therapy (MHT) remains the most efficacious intervention for vasomotor symptoms and the genitourinary syndrome of menopause, and is also effective in preventing postmenopausal bone loss and fracture [3]. Current guidelines from The North American Menopause Society (NAMS) and the International Menopause Society (IMS) endorse MHT for symptomatic women younger than 60 years or within 10 years of menopause onset, emphasizing individualized treatment based on the benefit–risk profile [4, 5].

For women with an intact uterus, a progestogen must be co-administered with estrogen to prevent estrogen-induced endometrial hyperplasia and malignancy [6]. However, the clinical implications of MHT extend well beyond endometrial protection: the type of progestogen used can differentially modulate cardiovascular, metabolic, thrombotic, and breast cancer risks [7, 8]. There is now considerable evidence that micronized progesterone and its retroisomer dydrogesterone exhibit more favorable safety profiles with respect to breast cancer, venous thromboembolism, and lipid metabolism compared with androgenic progestogens such as norethisterone and levonorgestrel [5, 9]. This recognition has shifted prescribing patterns in recent years, with increasing uptake of estradiol/dydrogesterone combinations [10], although direct comparative data among commercially available regimens remain limited.

Femoston, containing estradiol and dydrogesterone, and Climen, containing estradiol valerate and cyproterone acetate, are two commonly used sequential MHT regimens in clinical practice [11, 12]. These two products differ in three respects: estrogen formulation, progestogen component, and sequential pattern. Femoston contains micronized 17β-estradiol combined with dydrogesterone in a continuous sequential regimen without a pill-free interval, whereas Climen contains estradiol valerate combined with cyproterone acetate in a cyclic sequential regimen with a 7-day hormone-free period. Dydrogesterone is a retroprogesterone with selective progesterone receptor activity and negligible androgenic, estrogenic, or glucocorticoid effects, and has been shown to preserve the beneficial lipid and hemostatic actions of estrogen [13]. Cyproterone acetate, by contrast, is a pregnane-derived progestogen with potent antiandrogenic properties, making it a preferred option in women with concurrent hyperandrogenic features such as acne, hirsutism, or androgenetic alopecia [14]. Despite these well-characterized pharmacological differences, head-to-head clinical comparisons of these two packaged regimens are scarce. Such comparative data are needed to inform evidence-based treatment selection in real-world clinical practice.

Therefore, the aim of this retrospective study was to provide comparative real-world evidence on the short-term efficacy and safety of Femoston versus Climen. We sought to assess differences in symptom control, metabolic effects, and adverse event profiles between these two commonly prescribed sequential MHT regimens.

Methods

Study design and ethics

This was a single-center retrospective study conducted at the gynecology outpatient clinic of The First People’s Hospital of Fuzhou between January 2022 and January 2025. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of The First People’s Hospital of Fuzhou (approval number: 2026-03-13-01). Given the retrospective nature of the study, the requirement for individual informed consent was waived. This study was reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) statement.

Study population

Inclusion criteria

Patients were eligible for inclusion if they met all of the following criteria: (1) aged 40–60 years; (2) diagnosed with perimenopause or early postmenopause with reference to the Stages of Reproductive Aging Workshop + 10 (STRAW + 10) staging system: [15] (a) perimenopause, defined as persistent menstrual cycle irregularity (a change of ≥ 7 days in cycle length) accompanied by climacteric symptoms (hot flashes, sweating, insomnia, mood disturbances, etc.), with or without elevated serum follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH); or (b) early postmenopause, defined as ≤ 5 years since the final menstrual period (amenorrhea ≥ 12 months), with serum FSH ≥ 40 IU/L as a supportive biochemical criterion; (3) had an intact uterus; (4) received either Femoston sequential tablets (estradiol/estradiol–dydrogesterone, 1/10 or 2/10) or Climen (estradiol valerate/estradiol valerate–cyproterone acetate) for ≥ 6 consecutive treatment cycles; and (5) had complete baseline and follow-up data available in the medical records, including serum hormone levels, Kupperman Menopausal Index (KMI) scores, and transvaginal ultrasound measurements of endometrial thickness.

Exclusion criteria

Patients were excluded if any of the following were present: (1) use of any other hormonal preparations (oral contraceptives, progestins, tibolone, or phytoestrogen supplements) within 6 months prior to MHT initiation; (2) concomitant use of medications known to affect sex hormone metabolism (e.g., anticonvulsants, rifampicin) during the study period; (3) coexisting uncontrolled thyroid dysfunction or other endocrine disorders; (4) diagnosis of uterine fibroids ≥ 4 cm, endometrial polyps, or other uterine pathology requiring surgical intervention during the treatment period; or (5) concurrent participation in any other clinical study.

Group assignment

Patients were assigned to two groups based on their prescribed regimen:Femoston group: Femoston sequential tablets were taken orally, one tablet daily for 28 consecutive days per cycle. During the first 14 days, patients took tablets containing estradiol 1 mg (Femoston 1/10) or 2 mg (Femoston 2/10); during the subsequent 14 days, tablets containing the same dose of estradiol combined with dydrogesterone 10 mg were administered. No pill-free interval was included between cycles (continuous sequential regimen).Climen group: Climen tablets were taken orally, one tablet daily for 21 consecutive days per cycle. During the first 11 days, patients took tablets containing estradiol valerate 2 mg; during the subsequent 10 days, tablets containing estradiol valerate 2 mg combined with cyproterone acetate 1 mg were administered. Each cycle was followed by a 7-day pill-free interval (cyclic sequential regimen).

Data collection

Data were independently extracted from the hospital electronic medical record system by two investigators (YY and YF). Discrepancies were resolved by consensus with a senior attending physician (KY). Collected variables included baseline demographic and clinical characteristics (age, body mass index [BMI], menopausal status, interval from last menstrual period [LMP] to treatment initiation, gravidity, parity, comorbidities, smoking status, alcohol consumption, Femoston dose strength, and presence of hyperandrogenic features), baseline total testosterone, blood pressure measurements, baseline and post-treatment laboratory parameters (serum sex hormones, fasting lipid profile, hepatic enzymes, and fasting plasma glucose [FPG]), transvaginal ultrasonographic measurements of endometrial thickness, and modified Kupperman Menopausal Index (KMI) symptom scores.

Baseline hyperandrogenic features were defined as the presence of any of the following: serum total testosterone above the upper limit of the laboratory reference range, acne (any grade), hirsutism (Ferriman–Gallwey score ≥ 6), or a documented diagnosis of androgenetic alopecia. This variable was recorded as a binary covariate (present vs. absent) and incorporated into the analytic framework as described below.

Outcome measures

Primary outcome

The primary outcome was the post-treatment KMI total score after 6 treatment cycles, with baseline KMI score included as a covariate in the multivariable regression model to adjust for baseline differences. The KMI encompasses 11 symptom domains: hot flashes/sweating, paresthesia, insomnia, nervousness, depressive mood, vertigo, fatigue, arthralgia/myalgia, headache, palpitations, and formication. Each domain is scored as absent (0), mild (1), moderate (2), or severe (3) and multiplied by its respective weighting coefficient to yield a total score. Assessments were performed at baseline and after 6 treatment cycles by the same trained healthcare professional using a standardized face-to-face questionnaire.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA). All statistical tests were two-sided, with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Continuous variables were tested for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Normally distributed data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and compared between groups using the independent-samples t-test; within-group pre–post comparisons were performed using paired t-tests. Non-normally distributed data are presented as median (interquartile range [IQR]) and compared using the Mann–Whitney U test; within-group comparisons were performed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Categorical variables are expressed as frequencies (percentages) and compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate.

For the primary outcome, between-group comparison of post-treatment KMI scores was first performed using the independent-samples t-test. A multivariable linear regression model was then constructed with post-treatment KMI score as the dependent variable and treatment group (Climen vs. Femoston [reference]) as the primary independent variable. Covariates were selected a priori based on clinical relevance and included age, BMI, menopausal status, baseline KMI score, baseline E₂ level, number of comorbidities, baseline total testosterone, estrogen daily dose (1 mg vs. 2 mg; Femoston 1/10 coded as 1 mg, Femoston 2/10 and Climen both coded as 2 mg), and baseline hyperandrogenic features (present vs. absent). The inclusion of baseline hyperandrogenic features served to adjust for potential confounding by indication, given that the antiandrogenic properties of cyproterone acetate may lead to preferential prescribing of Climen in women with hyperandrogenic manifestations. Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIF > 5 indicating concern), and residual independence was evaluated using the Durbin–Watson statistic. For continuous secondary outcomes, within-group and between-group comparisons were performed using the same parametric or non-parametric tests as above. For binary secondary outcomes (symptom response rates and adverse event rates), chi-square tests, Fisher’s exact tests, and multivariable logistic regression were used as appropriate, with adjusted odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) reported. The Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) procedure was applied to adjust P values for multiple between-group comparisons of post-treatment lipid parameters.

Three prespecified sensitivity analyses were performed: (SA-1) restriction to Femoston 2/10 vs. Climen to achieve dose matching; (SA-2) restriction to Femoston 1/10 vs. Climen; and (SA-3) exclusion of patients with baseline hyperandrogenic features to assess confounding by indication. SA-1 and SA-2 were regarded as exploratory given small subgroup sizes, and consistency in effect direction was the primary criterion for assessing robustness. Prespecified subgroup analyses examined potential effect modification by menopausal status, baseline symptom severity (dichotomized at the median KMI score), and baseline hyperandrogenic features, with interaction terms tested in the regression model. The hyperandrogenic subgroup analysis (Femoston n = 3, Climen n = 8) was considered exploratory and underpowered. Variables with < 5% missing data were analyzed using complete case analysis; variables with 5%–20% missing data underwent multiple imputation (m = 5) as a sensitivity check. As this was a retrospective study, a formal a priori sample size calculation was not performed.

Results

Patient screening and enrollment

A total of 128 medical records of perimenopausal and early postmenopausal women who received Femoston or Climen were initially identified from the electronic medical record system.

Of these, 34 failed to meet one or more inclusion criteria: not fulfilling STRAW + 10 diagnostic criteria for perimenopause or early postmenopause (n = 12), prior hysterectomy (n = 5), treatment duration < 6 consecutive cycles (n = 7; mainly due to voluntary discontinuation, loss to follow-up, or transfer of care), or incomplete baseline/follow-up data (n = 10). The remaining 94 patients were further screened against the exclusion criteria, resulting in the removal of 14 cases: use of other hormonal preparations within 6 months before MHT initiation (n = 5), concomitant use of medications affecting sex hormone metabolism (n = 2), uncontrolled thyroid dysfunction or other endocrine disorders (n = 3), and uterine pathology requiring surgical intervention during the treatment period (n = 4).

Ultimately, 80 patients were enrolled and allocated by prescribed regimen into the Femoston group (n = 42; Femoston 1/10, n = 26; Femoston 2/10, n = 16) and the Climen group (n = 38).

Baseline characteristics

Baseline characteristics are summarized in Table 1. The two groups were well balanced with respect to demographic, clinical, and laboratory variables (all P > 0.05). The slightly elevated baseline TC values in both groups (5.36 ± 0.83 and 5.44 ± 0.86 mmol/L, respectively) are consistent with the presence of pre-existing dyslipidemia in 8 and 6 patients in the Femoston and Climen groups, respectively, all of whom were receiving stable lipid-lowering therapy at enrollment. Despite the inclusion of five patients with diabetes mellitus (three in the Femoston group and two in the Climen group), baseline FPG values were within the normal range in both groups, confirming adequate glycemic control at the time of MHT initiation. Baseline hyperandrogenic features were numerically more prevalent in the Climen group than in the Femoston group (21.05% vs. 7.14%, P = 0.104), with a concordant trend in serum total testosterone (0.96 ± 0.41 vs. 0.84 ± 0.33 nmol/L, P = 0.145). Although neither difference reached statistical significance, this pattern is consistent with the expected channeling effect of cyproterone acetate’s antiandrogenic properties on prescribing behavior; both variables were included as covariates in the primary regression model to adjust for this potential confounding by indication. The mean baseline KMI score was comparable between groups (Femoston 26.90 ± 5.72 vs. Climen 27.53 ± 6.08, P = 0.636).

Primary outcome

Both groups demonstrated significant reductions in KMI total scores from baseline after 6 treatment cycles (both P < 0.001) (Table 2). The mean post-treatment KMI score was 10.48 ± 3.72 in the Femoston group (reduction of 16.42 points; 61.04% decrease from baseline) and 11.74 ± 4.12 in the Climen group (reduction of 15.79 points; 57.35% decrease). The unadjusted between-group comparison showed no statistically significant difference (mean difference − 1.26, 95% CI: −3.01 to 0.49, P = 0.156).

Among patients with hyperandrogenic features (Femoston n = 3, Climen n = 8), post-treatment KMI scores were 12.67 ± 4.16 and 12.75 ± 4.52, respectively (mean difference − 0.08, 95% CI: −5.78 to 5.62, P = 0.974). The interaction between treatment group and hyperandrogenic status was not significant (P for interaction = 0.682). These results should be interpreted with caution given the very small sample size in the hyperandrogenic subgroup (n = 11).

Discussion

Menopausal hormone therapy remains the most effective pharmacological intervention for vasomotor symptoms and the genitourinary syndrome of menopause, yet head-to-head comparisons among commercially available combined regimens are uncommon in the published literature [16, 17]. In this retrospective study, both Femoston (estradiol/dydrogesterone) and Climen (estradiol valerate/cyproterone acetate) achieved substantial and comparable reductions in KMI scores after 6 treatment cycles, with no significant between-group difference after multivariable adjustment. The study population included both perimenopausal and early postmenopausal women, consistent with routine clinical practice and current NAMS and IMS guidelines, which endorse identical MHT principles for symptomatic women in both stages. A prespecified subgroup analysis confirmed that the treatment effect did not differ by menopausal status (P for interaction = 0.836), with consistent between-group effects in both the perimenopausal (P = 0.298) and early postmenopausal (P = 0.324) subgroups. These findings thus contribute real-world comparative evidence between two widely prescribed yet pharmacologically distinct sequential MHT regimens, supporting the validity of the combined analysis.

Both groups achieved substantial reductions in KMI scores, consistent with the magnitude of benefit reported in studies of estradiol-based MHT. A recent meta-analysis of 24 randomized controlled trials involving over 5,000 participants confirmed that estradiol-containing MHT significantly reduces KMI scores, with a large pooled effect size [18]. A retrospective study of 342 women receiving estradiol/dydrogesterone (Femoston 2/10) similarly demonstrated rapid and sustained KMI reductions over up to 3 years of follow-up [19]. In the multivariable model, baseline KMI score emerged as the strongest predictor of post-treatment scores (standardized β = 0.70), while treatment group assignment was not independently associated with the primary outcome. This finding is clinically relevant because it underscores the importance of baseline symptom severity in shaping treatment expectations: patients with a greater initial symptom burden tended to retain higher post-treatment scores despite comparable proportional improvements, supporting an individualized, symptom-driven rather than one-size-fits-all approach to MHT initiation. Notably, neither baseline hyperandrogenic features nor estrogen daily dose significantly predicted the primary outcome. The lack of a dose effect should be interpreted with caution, as both doses used in this study (1 mg and 2 mg) exceed the established minimum effective threshold for symptom relief, and the sample size within each dose subgroup was limited. Nonetheless, these results suggest that the observed equivalence between regimens was not materially driven by dose heterogeneity or confounding by indication related to hyperandrogenic status. The comparable KMI improvements observed in both perimenopausal and early postmenopausal subgroups are physiologically coherent rather than a statistical artifact of category overlap. Both groups share the same underlying mechanism, progressive estrogen deficiency, which MHT addresses by restoring circulating estrogen to therapeutic levels. This shared mechanism is reflected in current international guidelines, which recommend MHT for symptomatic women within the same treatment window without distinguishing between perimenopause and early postmenopause [4, 5]. The prespecified subgroup analysis showed no significant interaction between treatment group and menopausal status (P for interaction = 0.836), though the limited subgroup sample size precludes definitive conclusions.

A methodological consideration that distinguishes this study from some prior comparative analyses is the decision to retain patients with baseline hyperandrogenic features rather than exclude them. Cyproterone acetate possesses potent antiandrogenic activity, and in routine clinical practice, the presence of acne, hirsutism, or elevated testosterone may channel prescribing toward Climen — a classic example of confounding by indication [20]. Excluding this population would have improved internal validity but at the cost of external validity and reduced sample size, both of which are already limited in a single-center retrospective cohort. We therefore adopted a statistical adjustment strategy: hyperandrogenic status and baseline testosterone were included as regression covariates, a prespecified sensitivity analysis excluded hyperandrogenic patients entirely (SA-3), and an exploratory subgroup analysis stratified by hyperandrogenic status was performed. All three approaches yielded consistent and directionally stable results. It should be acknowledged, however, that the hyperandrogenic subgroup (n = 3 vs. 8) was too small to draw any conclusions about differential efficacy, and this remains an important unanswered question for future adequately powered studies. Both regimens produced comparable elevations in serum E₂ and comparable suppression of FSH and LH, consistent with their shared mechanism of action. The absence of a significant between-group difference in post-treatment E₂ levels (196.82 ± 49.86 vs. 204.47 ± 53.92 pmol/L, P = 0.512) despite the dose heterogeneity within the Femoston group is likely attributable to two factors: the lower oral bioavailability of estradiol valerate (Climen) relative to micronized 17β-estradiol (Femoston) due to the hepatic hydrolysis step required for prodrug activation [21, 22], and the substantial inter-individual pharmacokinetic variability reflected in the wide standard deviations of both groups [23].

Although the two regimens demonstrated equivalent symptom control, the divergence in lipid profiles warrants clinical attention. Within each group, Femoston produced a significant increase in HDL-C that was not observed with Climen, while Climen was associated with a significant rise in triglycerides that was absent with Femoston. This differential pattern aligns with the established pharmacological profiles of the two progestogens. Dydrogesterone has negligible androgenic and glucocorticoid activity and consistently preserves the beneficial estrogenic effects on lipoprotein metabolism [24]. Cyproterone acetate, though valued for its antiandrogenic properties, exhibits partial glucocorticoid receptor agonism that may attenuate HDL-C elevation and promote hepatic triglyceride synthesis [25]. Notably, after correction for multiple comparisons, only the between-group difference in post-treatment HDL-C retained statistical significance. The clinical significance of this difference should not be overstated: the failure of the Climen group to reach within-group significance for HDL-C (P = 0.402) may partly reflect limited statistical power to detect its small numeric increment (0.03 mmol/L), and the between-group difference, while surviving Benjamini–Hochberg correction, represents a surrogate endpoint whose relationship to hard cardiovascular outcomes remains incompletely defined [26]. The American Heart Association has emphasized that the menopause transition represents a period of accelerating cardiovascular risk [27], and even modest metabolic advantages could become clinically meaningful over extended treatment durations. These lipid findings may therefore serve as one factor among many, including patient preferences, comorbidities, and contraindications, in informing individualized regimen selection.

Beyond lipid metabolism, the accumulating safety data on different progestogens have increasingly influenced treatment selection. A large nested case-control study using UK primary care databases found that among combined MHT regimens used for five or more years, the risk of breast cancer was lowest with dydrogesterone-containing preparations compared with other progestogens such as norethisterone and levonorgestrel [28]. The WHI long-term follow-up data further reinforced that the balance of benefits and risks of MHT depends critically on formulation, timing of initiation, and patient characteristics [26]. Dydrogesterone has also been reported to have a neutral effect on coagulation parameters, a property that may translate into lower venous thromboembolic risk relative to some synthetic progestogens [29]. These considerations collectively support Femoston as an attractive option for the broader menopausal population without specific indications for antiandrogenic therapy, while Climen retains a niche role where concurrent management of hyperandrogenic manifestations is desired. The continuous sequential design of Femoston, which avoids a 7-day hormone-free interval, may also offer practical advantages by maintaining more stable estrogen levels and preventing the symptom recurrence that is occasionally reported during the gap period of cyclic regimens [30].

Both regimens demonstrated reassuring short-term safety over the 6-cycle observation period. No clinically significant changes in hepatic enzymes or fasting glucose were detected, and endometrial thickness remained within normal limits in all patients without evidence of hyperplasia. The breast tenderness rate of approximately 20% observed in both groups is higher than that reported in randomized controlled trials of low-dose continuous combined regimens (2.4%–10.8%) [31], yet is comparable to that documented in Chinese perimenopausal women receiving conventional-dose Femoston (17.5%) [32]. This likely reflects the population characteristics, estrogen doses, sequential regimen design, retrospective study setting, and modest sample size of the present study. Direct comparisons are limited by these methodological differences. All events were mild to moderate and did not lead to treatment discontinuation, consistent with the self-limiting nature of estrogen-related breast tenderness. All adverse events were mild to moderate in severity, and no participant discontinued treatment; the absence of serious adverse events, including venous thromboembolism, is consistent with the low absolute event rates expected in this population [4].

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. The retrospective single-center design introduces the possibility of selection bias and residual confounding that cannot be fully addressed by multivariable adjustment. Although we controlled for key potential confounders including hyperandrogenic features, baseline testosterone, and estrogen dose, unmeasured variables such as physician prescribing preference, patient socioeconomic status, and adherence could not be accounted for. The sample size of 80 patients represents an important limitation; no formal a priori power calculation was conducted. The 95% CI for the between-group KMI difference (− 3.01 to 0.49) indicates that smaller true differences cannot be entirely excluded, and results for secondary endpoints and subgroup analyses should be interpreted with particular caution. The hyperandrogenic subgroup analysis should be regarded as hypothesis-generating only. A further limitation concerns the heterogeneity in cumulative estrogen exposure across dose subgroups. Over a standard 28-day reference cycle, Femoston 1/10 delivers approximately 28 mg of estradiol, Femoston 2/10 approximately 56 mg, and Climen approximately 42 mg of estradiol valerate. Although dose heterogeneity was addressed by including estrogen daily dose as a regression covariate and by conducting dose-stratified sensitivity analyses (SA-1 and SA-2), both of which yielded directionally consistent results, a prospective three-arm trial separating Femoston 1/10, Femoston 2/10, and Climen would be the definitive design to disentangle regimen-specific from dose-specific effects. The requirement of six or more completed treatment cycles may have introduced survivorship bias by excluding patients who discontinued treatment early, who likely represent those with poorer tolerability. The modestly higher attrition rate in the Climen group (6 vs. 4 patients) raises the possibility of differential tolerability, though the numbers were too small for firm conclusions. Treatment adherence could not be reliably ascertained from retrospective records. The inclusion of patients with stable hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and dyslipidemia reflected guideline-concordant real-world prescribing practice. Under current NAMS and IMS guidelines, these conditions are not considered contraindications to MHT when the overall benefit–risk profile is favourable, and baseline TG levels in both groups (1.45 ± 0.40 and 1.50 ± 0.42 mmol/L) were well below the threshold associated with oral estrogen-related hypertriglyceridemia risk [33]. Nonetheless, the six-month observation window limits conclusions about the long-term metabolic safety of these regimens in higher-risk subgroups, and future studies should report outcomes separately for patients with and without relevant comorbidities. Furthermore, comprehensive assessment of glucose metabolism was not possible in this retrospective setting, as insulin levels, homeostatic model assessment of insulin resistance (HOMA-IR), and glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c) were not systematically measured. Given the pharmacological differences between dydrogesterone and cyproterone acetate with respect to glucocorticoid receptor activity, future prospective comparisons should incorporate these parameters to more fully characterise the metabolic profiles of both regimens. The six-month follow-up, while adequate for capturing initial lipid effects, is insufficient for evaluating long-term cardiovascular or oncological outcomes. Finally, the KMI, although widely used in Chinese clinical practice, has been critiqued for its unvalidated weighting system and the absence of domains capturing vaginal dryness and sexual dysfunction; [34] future comparative studies should incorporate more psychometrically robust instruments such as the Menopause Rating Scale (MRS) or the Greene Climacteric Scale [35].

Conclusion

In summary, this study provides preliminary real-world evidence that Femoston and Climen offer comparable short-term symptom relief for perimenopausal and early postmenopausal women. The lipid profile differences favoring Femoston, while consistent with the known pharmacology of dydrogesterone, require prospective validation against hard cardiovascular endpoints. The choice between these two regimens should be individualized, incorporating patient-specific factors such as lipid profiles, the presence of hyperandrogenic features, bleeding pattern preferences, and long-term safety considerations. Larger, multicenter prospective studies with extended follow-up and adequately powered subgroup analyses, particularly in women with concurrent hyperandrogenic manifestations, are needed to confirm and extend these findings.
