Retrospective evaluation of C-reactive protein for discriminating between lower urinary tract infections and acute pyelonephritis in dogs
Authors: Francesca Fidanzio, Greta Colombo, Kateryna Vasylyeva, Alessandro Tirolo, Maria Chiara Sabetti, Isabella Tirelli, Erika Monari, Andrea Corsini, Francesco Dondi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe role of C-reactive protein (CRP) in differentiating pyelonephritis from other types of urinary tract infection in dogs is not well defined.Hypothesis/ObjectivesCompare serum CRP concentrations at diagnosis in dogs with acute pyelonephritis (APy), acute prostatitis (APr), and bacterial cystitis (BC). Serum CRP concentration is hypothesized to be higher in APy and APr than in BC.AnimalsOne hundred fifteen client-owned dogs were included: 56 with BC, 32 with APy, 23 with APr, and 4 with both APy and APr.MethodsRetrospective observational study. Dogs with positive urine culture were included and divided into 3 groups (BC, APy, and APr), based on medical history. Dogs with comorbidities potentially affecting CRP concentrations and dogs with subclinical bacteriuria were excluded. Data were compared among groups, and the diagnostic accuracy of CRP was assessed post-hoc.ResultsDogs with BC had lower median CRP (1.04 mg/dL; range, 0.08-7.21) than those with APy (23.65 ± 11.96 mg/dL; median difference [MD], 22.9 mg/dL; 95% CI, 18.2-26.5; P < .001) and APr (20.9 mg/dL; range, 5.93-55.97; MD, 19.9 mg/dL; 95% CI, 10.7-25.6; P < .001), whereas APy and APr did not differ (MD, −3.0 mg/dL; 95% CI, −10.6 to 4.4; P > .99). Serum CRP concentration had high discriminative ability to differentiate dogs with BC and APy, APr, or both (area under the curve [AUC], 0.99; 95% CI, 0.99-1).Conclusions and clinical importanceSerum CRP concentration evaluated at diagnosis, when highly increased, may help differentiate BC from APy in dogs without concurrent inflammatory diseases.

Introduction

Pyelonephritis is an infection of the renal pelvis, renal parenchyma, or both, that usually occurs from ascending infection or via hematogenous spread.1,2 Ascending infections may arise from clinically evident urologic disease or even in the absence of lower urinary tract signs (LUTS).1 Diagnosis of pyelonephritis can be challenging because of nonspecific clinical signs, laboratory findings, and imaging findings.3,4 Acute pyelonephritis (APy) can be suspected based on positive urine culture results, clinical signs (eg, fever, lethargy, and polyuria/polydipsia [PU/PD]), and laboratory findings (azotemia, leukocytosis, pyuria, and cylindruria).1 Gold standard techniques for diagnosis of pyelonephritis are renal pelvic urine culture and histopathology of the renal parenchyma performed by renal biopsy.4 It is uncommon to perform these tests because of their risks,4,5 and, even in cases with strong clinical suspicion, the diagnosis of pyelonephritis usually remains presumptive.2

Bacterial cystitis (BC) is defined as infection and inflammation of the urinary bladder caused by bacteria; LUTS frequently accompany this condition.1 Prostatitis should be ruled out using an appropriate diagnostic protocol in all intact male dogs with urinary tract infection (UTI) so as to start the correct antimicrobial treatment.2 It is crucial to discriminate among BC, APy, and acute prostatitis (APr) to provide optimal treatment and disease monitoring. The treatment course for BC lasts 3-5 days, whereas longer duration treatment is suggested for APy (10-14 days) and APr (4 weeks).2 Treatment also differs in terms of choice of antimicrobial. International Society for Companion Animal Infectious Diseases (ISCAID) guidelines recommend amoxicillin or trimethoprim-sulfonamides as first-line antibiotics for BC, whereas APy and APr should be treated with fluoroquinolones or third-generation cephalosporins.2 Inappropriate treatment can result in unresolved infections, contribute to antimicrobial resistance, and lead to increased treatment costs and duration.

C-reactive protein (CRP) is an acute phase protein, and its increase in circulation indicates systemic inflammation, although it gives no information on the cause or localization of the disease. Immune-mediated and infectious and non-infectious inflammatory diseases can increase serum CRP concentration.6–11 In human medicine, upper UTI but not lower UTI leads to increased CRP concentrations.12 Acute phase proteins have been linked to upper urinary tract infections in cats, with serum amyloid A (SAA) concentration being helpful to rule out pyelonephritis in cats when the disease is not strongly suspected.13 In dogs, serum CRP concentration data in pyelonephritis are lacking. Medline and Google Scholar databases were searched using the following keywords (canine and pyelonephritis and C-reactive protein), and no report about the use of serum CRP concentration in dogs with APy was found.

We aimed to compare CRP results among dogs with APy, APr, or BC. We hypothesized that dogs with APy and APr would have higher serum CRP concentrations than dogs with BC.

Materials and methods

Study design

Our retrospective cross-sectional study included dogs presented to the Veterinary Teaching Hospital of the University of Parma and the University of Bologna between January 2020 and June 2024. The electronic database (Fenice, Zaksoft Software Technology) was searched for dogs in which a urine culture was performed for any reason. For each dog, signalment, history, and physical examination findings were extracted from the medical records.

Client-owned dogs with UTI, selected based on positive urine culture results and appropriate clinical signs, were retrospectively included in the study and divided into 3 groups: BC, APy, and APr. A detailed description of the criteria used to include dogs in the 3 groups is provided in the Appendix. Dogs that had concurrent APr and APy were excluded from these groups, but they were included in a separate group consisting of dogs with pyelonephritis, prostatitis, or both (APP group).

Dogs were excluded from the study if they had comorbidities potentially affecting serum CRP concentrations, if they had subclinical bacteriuria, if serum CRP was not determined at the time of diagnosis, or if medical records were incomplete. A detailed description of the exclusion criteria is provided in the Appendix.

Clinical and clinicopathologic data

Serum CRP concentrations were measured using 2 immunoturbidimetric assays previously validated for the quantification of CRP in dogs: the Turbovet canine CRP (Acuvet Biotech, Zaragoza, Spain, at the University of Parma; lower limit of detection, 0.1 mg/dL; reference interval [RI], 0-1.0 mg/dL) and the CRP OSR 6147 (Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA, at the University of Bologna; lower limit of detection, 0.05 mg/dL; RI, 0-0.85 mg/dL). Further details on clinical and clinicopathological data are reported in the Appendix.

Statistical analysis

The comparison of CRP concentrations among groups initially was performed on the entire study sample. Subsequently, the same analysis was performed separately according to the CRP measurement method: method 1—CRP OSR 6147 (Beckman Coulter, Brea CA, USA) and method 2—Turbovet canine CRP (Acuvet Biotech, Zaragoza, Spain).

The Kruskal–Wallis test, followed by the Dunn multiple post-hoc comparison test, was used to compare continuous variables among BC, APy, and APr. The Mann–Whitney test was used to compare BC and APP. Categorical variables were compared among BC, APy, and APr using the chi-squared test. A post hoc receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was used to evaluate the sensitivity and specificity of serum CRP in discriminating between BC and APP. Statistical significance was set at P < .05. Further details on statistical analysis are reported in the Appendix.

Results

Population

A total of 1010 dogs underwent urine culture in the study period. Of these, 301 dogs were excluded because they had no growth in urine culture. Of the 709 dogs with a positive urine culture result, 266 were excluded for the presence of comorbidities that could influence CRP concentrations, 158 were excluded for the lack of a complete medical record or because CRP was not measured at the time of diagnosis, 150 did not meet the inclusion criteria (patients with a clinical suspicion of APr or APy that did not fulfill all inclusion criteria [eg, only 3 of 5 required criteria]), and 20 had subclinical bacteriuria. The comorbidities that led to exclusion are described in Supplementary Information S1. A total of 115 client-owned dogs were finally included in the study (Figure 1).

Flow chart illustrating the selection process of dogs included in the study. Abbreviations: APP = acute pyelonephritis and/or prostatitis; APr = acute bacterial prostatitis; APy = acute pyelonephritis; BC = bacterial cystitis.

The BC group included 56 dogs (10 intact males, 11 neutered males, 8 intact females, and 27 spayed females), with 14 mixed breed and 42 purebred dogs. The median age was 8 years (range, 0-15 years), and the median weight was 17.5 kg (range, 4.1-80.0 kg). The APy group included 32 dogs (8 intact males, 7 neutered males, 7 intact females, and 10 spayed females), with 10 mixed breed and 22 purebred dogs. The median age was 11 years (range, 0-15 years), and the median weight was 7.8 kg (range, 2.3-32.0 kg). The frequency of each inclusion criterion in the APy group is reported in Table 1. The APr group included 23 dogs (21 intact males, 2 neutered males), with 7 mixed breed and 16 purebred dogs. The median age was 11 years (range, 4-14 years), and the mean weight was 18.3 ± 11.2 kg. Four dogs had concurrent APy and APr and were included in the APP group, which also included dogs with APy and dogs with APr. Of these 4 dogs, 3 were intact males, and 1 was a neutered male. Two were mixed breed dogs, 1 was a Dachshund, and 1 was a Labrador Retriever. A detailed list of breeds included in the BC, APy, and APr groups is reported in Supplementary Information S1.

Age was significantly different among the 3 groups (P = .001), with BC significantly younger compared to both APy (P = .005) and APr (P = .02). Clinical signs and clinicopathologic variables

Eighty of the 115 dogs (70%) had one or more LUTS or PU/PD. Specifically, LUTS were reported in all 56 dogs with BC, with 10/56 (18%) dogs presenting with stranguria, 23/56 (41%) with pollakiuria, and 30/56 (54%) with hematuria. Twenty-eight of 32 dogs in the APy group (87.5%) had LUTS or PU/PD: 3/32 (9%) dogs had stranguria, 2/32 (6%) dogs had pollakiuria, 5/32 (16%) dogs had hematuria, and 22/32 (69%) dogs had PU/PD. Nine of 23 dogs in the APr group (39%) had LUTS or PU/PD, with stranguria reported in 1/23 (4%) dog, pollakiuria in 3/23 (13%), hematuria in 6/23 (26%), and PU/PD was reported in 5/23 (22%) dogs.

Body temperature was recorded in 52 of 56 dogs in the BC group and in all dogs in the APy and APr groups. In the BC group, temperature > 39.2°C was reported in 4/52 (8%) dogs; mean rectal temperature was 38.5 ± .50°C. In the APy group, 8/32 (25%) dogs had fever, and the mean body temperature was 38.6 ± 1.1°C. In the APr group, 14/23 (61%) dogs had fever, and the median body temperature was 39.7°C (range, 38.4-40.3°C). Of the 4 dogs with concurrent APy and APr, 3 (75%) had fever.

The clinicopathologic variables of the 3 groups are described in Table 2. Leukocytosis was present in 10/55 (18%), 18/32 (56%), and 6/23 (26%) of dogs with BC, APy, and APr, respectively. Leukocyte count was significantly higher in APy compared with BC (P < .001). No significant difference was found between BC and APr or between APy and APr. Neutrophilic leukocytosis was present in 9/55 (16%), 20/32 (62.5%), and 9/23 (39%) of dogs with BC, APy, and APr, respectively. The neutrophil count was significantly different among the 3 groups, between BC and APy (P < .001) and between BC and APr (P = .02). Serum creatinine concentration was above the upper limit of the RI (1.4 mg/dL) in 7/56 (12.5%), 21/32 (66%), and 2/23 (9%) dogs with BC, APy, and APr, respectively. Serum creatinine concentration was significantly higher in APy compared with BC (P < .001) and APr (P < .001). Serum urea concentration was above the RI in 18/51 (35%) dogs, 26/31 (84%) dogs, and 10/22 (45%) dogs in BC, APy, and APr, respectively. Serum urea concentration was significantly higher in APy compared with BC (P < .001) and APr (P < .001). Serum total protein concentration did not differ among groups (P = .79), whereas serum albumin concentration was significantly lower in APy compared with BC (P = .002). Serum phosphorus concentration was significantly higher in APy compared with BC (P < .001) and APr (P = .003).

C-reactive protein

Median serum CRP concentration was 1.0 mg/dL (range, 0.1-7.2 mg/dL) in BC, 23.6 ± 12.0 mg/dL in APy, and 20.9 mg/dL (range, 5.9-56.0 mg/dL) in APr. Dogs with BC had significantly lower serum CRP concentration compared with dogs with APy (MD, 22.9 mg/dL; 95% CI, 18.2-26.5; P < .001) and APr (MD, 19.9 mg/dL; 95% CI, 10.7-25.6; P < .001). No differences were found between APy and APr (MD, −3.0 mg/dL; 95% CI, −10.6 to 4.4; P = .99; Figure 2A). The APP group, with a median serum CRP concentration of 22.2 mg/dL (range, 4.3-56 mg/dL) had a significantly higher serum CRP concentration compared with BC (MD, 21.1 mg/dL; 95% CI, 16.7-24.2; P < .001; Figure 3A).

Considering the 79 dogs in which CRP was measured using method 1, serum CRP concentration was 1.1 mg/dL (range, 0.7-7.2 mg/dL) in BC, 22 ± 11.3 mg/dL in APy, and 27.8 ± 15.3 mg/dL in APr. Dogs with BC had significantly lower serum CRP compared with APy dogs (MD, 22.0 mg/dL; 95% CI, 16-26; P < .001) and APr dogs (MD, 26 mg/dL; 95% CI, 20-28; P < .001). No differences were identified between APy and APr (MD, 4 mg/dL; 95% CI, −3.4 to 15; P = .35; Figure 2B). The APP group, with a serum CRP concentration of 24 mg/dL (range, 4.3-56 mg/dL) had significantly higher serum CRP concentrations compared with BC (MD, 23 mg/dL; 95% CI, 19-27; P < .001) (Figure 3B).

Considering the 33 dogs in which CRP was measured using method 2, serum CRP concentration was 1.2 ± 1.2 mg/dL in BC, 26.8 ± 13.1 mg/dL in APy, and 9.8 mg/dL (range, 6.6-45.5 mg/dL) in APr. Dogs with BC had significantly lower serum CRP compared with dogs with APy (MD, −25 mg/dL; 95% CI, 16-38; P < .001) and APr (MD, 8.9 mg/dL; 95% CI, 6.5-18; P = .003); no differences were identified between APy and APr (MD, −16 mg/dL; 95% CI, −25 to −0.9; P = .53) (Figure 2C). The APP group, with a serum CRP concentration of 18 mg/dL (range, 5.3-46 mg/dL), had significantly higher serum CRP compared with BC (MD, 17 mg/dL; 95% CI, 9.7-25; P < .001; Figure 3C).

Discussion

Our results demonstrate that, within a selected population without inflammatory comorbidities, dogs with BC had significantly lower serum CRP concentrations than dogs with APy and APr, although no difference was found between dogs with APy and APr. Based on these results, CRP represents a potentially useful biomarker to help differentiate dogs with BC from those with APy, APr, or both, provided no concurrent inflammatory conditions are present. Also, the exploratory post-hoc ROC analysis of serum CRP had high discriminative ability in differentiating the BC group from other groups in this specific cohort. Although CRP could not distinguish between APy and APr, these 2 conditions can be easily differentiated based on clinical presentation and ultrasonographic findings. A correct diagnosis is crucial to provide adequate treatment in terms of the choice of antimicrobial and duration of treatment.14 Thus, an inexpensive, minimally invasive biomarker capable of distinguishing upper UTI from lower UTI would be highly beneficial in clinical practice.

Specifically, the CRP concentrations in dogs with BC were significantly lower than those in dogs with APy or APr. Of note, 2 different assays were used to measure CRP, which could prevent defining a general cut-off. Nonetheless, the cut-off extrapolated from the total cohort was very similar to the cut-offs defined by analyzing the 2 assays separately, and CRP proved a valuable marker for differentiation regardless of the measurement method used.14

Dogs with experimentally induced BC have been reported to have markedly increased serum CRP concentrations compared with healthy controls, with median values of 4.38 mg/dL in dogs with positive urine culture versus 0.59 mg/dL in those with negative culture results.15 These results are higher than those observed in our study, but these findings were obtained in an experimental setting that may not fully replicate clinical infections. Specifically, the pattern and severity of inflammation in cases of spontaneous BC can differ substantially from what is observed in experimental models, potentially limiting the direct applicability of these results to clinical cases. In the experimental study, several factors likely contributed to the increased CRP concentrations, including the small number of animals, the controlled experimental conditions, the use of Proteus spp. (a highly pathogenic urease-producing bacterium), and the lack of exclusion of upper urinary tract involvement.

To date, the diagnostic role of CRP in spontaneous UTIs has not been thoroughly investigated in dogs, although it is well documented in human medicine. In one study assessing serum and urinary CRP concentrations in dogs with neurologic disorders, the population included patients with various urologic signs, and some of them were diagnosed with cystitis. Although that study did not specifically address UTIs, its findings suggest that CRP may have broader relevance in dogs with urinary tract diseases.16 Although this study suggested a potential application of CRP as a biomarker in urinary tract diseases of dogs, it was not specifically designed to evaluate UTIs and did not provide clear evidence of the ability of CRP to discriminate between different types of UTIs.

In pediatric human patients, a previous study found that both urinary and serum CRP concentrations were significantly higher in bacterial UTIs compared with nonbacterial infections.17 Urinary CRP differentiated bacterial from nonbacterial infections with high accuracy (AUC up to 0.98 in infants), and serum CRP concentration was particularly increased in patients with renal involvement.17

Notably, serum CRP concentrations are significantly higher in patients with upper UTIs (eg, acute pyelonephritis) compared with those with lower UTIs (eg, cystitis). In adult patients, a recent study reported mean CRP concentrations of 11.35 mg/dL in upper UTI vs 1.28 mg/dL in lower UTI (P = .02).12 Additionally, earlier work found mean CRP concentrations of approximately 12.7 mg/dL in upper UTI, markedly higher than in lower UTI, suggesting a potential diagnostic role.18

Similarly in cats, acute phase proteins correlate with upper UTIs, with SAA concentration being particularly helpful to rule out pyelonephritis. Cats with confirmed or presumed bacterial pyelonephritis have significantly higher median SAA concentrations (86.8 and 98.8 mg/L, respectively) compared with cats in which pyelonephritis was excluded or considered unlikely (4.0 and 5.4 mg/L, respectively), with an optimal diagnostic cut-off of 51.3 mg/L providing 88% sensitivity and 94% specificity, even when the disease is not strongly suspected.13

Together, these data from humans and cats emphasize that acute-phase proteins, including CRP and SAA, can serve as valuable biomarkers to differentiate between lower and upper UTIs across species.

From a future perspective, CRP may be a valid variable not only to differentiate among UTI categories but also for monitoring treatment, as has already been described for other inflammatory diseases.14,15,19 Notably, serum CRP concentration increases in various inflammatory conditions, including immune-mediated diseases, infectious diseases, and noninfectious inflammatory diseases,6,20,21 but it does not correlate with the cause or the location of the inflammation.22,23 Thus, dogs with other conditions that can influence serum CRP concentrations were excluded from the study, and, for these reasons, CRP should be interpreted cautiously in dogs with UTI and concurrent inflammatory diseases not involving the urinary tract.

Several clinical and clinicopathological findings emerged as particularly relevant in characterizing the presentation and diagnosis of APy in dogs. A high proportion of dogs with APy had LUTS. Simultaneous involvement of the lower urinary tract in dogs with APy in our study could explain this result. Alternatively, this finding could be a peculiarity of pyelonephritis in dogs, in which some patients could manifest renal involvement with signs typical of lower urinary tract disease. This finding should be considered by clinicians when interpreting urologic signs in their differential diagnosis.

Polyuria/polydipsia and suboptimally concentrated urine were both common in dogs in our study, being more frequent in dogs with APy (69% and 87%, respectively) compared with other groups. This result is expected considering PU/PD as a sign of renal involvement and decreased urine concentrating ability.3,24 Conversely, 39% of the dogs with BC and 22% of those with APr had PU/PD. A possible explanation is the role of E. coli as a causal agent of these infections. Uropathogenic E. coli can express toxins that interfere with antidiuretic hormone receptors in the kidney, causing secondary nephrogenic diabetes insipidus.3,24 The most frequently isolated bacterial agent in each group was E. coli, as anticipated, considering that E. coli is the most common bacterium responsible for UTIs.3,24–26 Another possible explanation is that some of these dogs may have had other underlying diseases causing decreased urine concentrating ability, such as chronic kidney disease. For these reasons, PU/PD in UTIs is not necessarily suggestive of kidney involvement but also can be present in cystitis or prostatitis.

Fever was more common in dogs with upper UTI in our study, whereas it was reported in only 8% of dogs with BC. Nonetheless, this clinical sign is not specific, and it was observed in only 25% of dogs with APy. This finding is consistent with a previous retrospective study, in which fever was documented in only 21% of dogs with histologically confirmed pyelonephritis, despite being considered a classic sign of upper UTI.4 Leukocytosis was common in the APP group, whereas only 16% of the dogs with BC exhibited leukocytosis, as previously reported. All of these findings confirm that dogs with BC seem to have a less severe inflammatory state than those with APy or APr.

Only 66% of the dogs with APy met the criteria for acute kidney injury according to the International Renal Interest Society (IRIS) guidelines. Indeed, serum creatinine concentration is a marker of glomerular filtration rate and is not specific for APy as the underlying cause of renal injury. In contrast, ultrasonographic findings were observed in more than 80% of the patients with APy.

Leukocyturia and hematuria were common in all groups, as previously described in dogs with UTIs.27,28 However, no difference was found among groups, emphasizing that these findings do not accurately discriminate between the different types of UTIs. Bacteriuria was the most common finding in the urine sediment in dogs with BC and APy. However, 20% of all dogs did not have bacteriuria on urinalysis, and up to 30% of dogs with APr had positive urine culture results without bacteriuria. As is well known, failure to identify bacteria in the urine sediment does not exclude UTI, particularly if APr is suspected, and a diagnosis of UTI must be confirmed by performing a urine culture.2,29

In our cohort, BC occurred more frequently in females, consistent with the well-known anatomical predisposition of female dogs.2 Interestingly, pyelonephritis affected both sexes almost equally, showing a less pronounced sex imbalance than typically reported in the literature, where females usually account for most cases.4 This distribution in our study may reflect the dual pathophysiology of pyelonephritis, which can arise not only from ascending infections but also via hematogenous spread. Additionally, our strict inclusion criteria, which excluded dogs with comorbidities and those lacking CRP measurements, likely influenced the observed sex ratios.

Our study has some limitations. First, the clinical diagnosis of APy in dogs remains challenging because of the lack of universally accepted diagnostic criteria in the veterinary literature. To overcome this limitation, we used a composite clinical reference standard to assess the diagnostic accuracy of CRP, but even if unavoidable in this setting, it does not in itself constitute an unbiased reference standard. In addition, even if CRP was excluded from the composite clinical reference standard, it may have influenced the original clinical suspicion and diagnostic evaluation performed by the attending clinician. As such, misclassification could have occurred, particularly in cases where clinical and laboratory findings overlapped. To maximize the likelihood of an accurate clinical diagnosis of pyelonephritis, we chose to apply stringent criteria to include patients in the APy group. All subjects were required to have a positive urine culture and meet at least 4 of the 5 clinical criteria established based on ISCAID guidelines. Nonetheless, because of the retrospective design of the study, review and incorporation bias could not be completely ruled out. On the other hand, because of these stringent criteria, the samples may not fully represent the broader population of dogs with UTI, which might limit the generalizability of our results to different groups. Second, ours was a retrospective study. Collected data could be incomplete or missing information, and the quality and method of data collection could vary. For example, the study did not distinguish between sporadic and recurrent cases of BC. In dogs with recurrent infections, undetected subclinical pyelonephritis or prostatitis could be present. Based on our results, these subclinical diseases might go undetected if CRP was used to rule them out. Third, variability in urine collection methods among patients might have influenced the risk of sample contamination and, consequently, urine culture results. Specifically, samples collected by urethral catheterization are more prone to false-positive cultures because of potential contamination from the distal urethra. To mitigate this limitation, we applied additional stringent diagnostic criteria and did not rely solely on a positive urine culture to classify patients into the respective groups. Fourth, urothelial carcinoma and concurrent uroliths were not systematically ruled out in all dogs included in the study. However, bladder ultrasonography was performed in all but one BC case, and therefore the presence of bladder neoplasia or urolithiasis was considered unlikely in this population. In addition, in the APr group, prostatic cytology was performed in 6 of 23 dogs. Fifth, rectal temperature was not recorded in 4 dogs in the BC group. Although this factor did not affect inclusion because all other criteria were met, it does represent a limitation of the study. Sixth, the multicenter design of the study inherently introduced variability, particularly because of differences in diagnostic tools, laboratory procedures, and CRP assays used across participating centers. Finally, the optimal CRP threshold was derived and evaluated in the same dataset, which may result in optimistic estimates, and validation in an independent dataset potentially would strengthen the results.

Conclusions

We demonstrated that, among dogs without relevant inflammatory comorbidities, dogs with BC had significantly lower CRP than those with APy or APr, with no significant difference observed between the latter groups. Thus, in the absence of concurrent inflammatory comorbidities, serum CRP concentrations could help differentiate among BC and APy, APr, or both conditions. Given the clinical challenges associated with diagnosing upper UTIs, such as pyelonephritis, serum CRP appears to be a promising biomarker that may help differentiate these conditions from lower UTIs such as cystitis.
