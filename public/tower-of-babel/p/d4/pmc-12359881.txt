Circular RNAs: key regulators of paclitaxel sensitivity and resistance in cancer
Authors: Zahra Tajik, Soudeh Ghafouri-Fard
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPaclitaxel remains a cornerstone of cancer therapy, yet resistance significantly limits its clinical efficacy. Emerging evidence implicates circular RNAs (circRNAs) as key regulators of paclitaxel resistance, offering potential biomarkers and therapeutic targets. This review synthesizes current knowledge on circRNA-mediated resistance mechanisms, categorizing their roles as promoters or suppressors of chemoresistance. We highlight two major pathways—miRNA sponging and protein interactions—through which circRNAs alter drug sensitivity. Moreover, we focus on enhancement of epithelial–mesenchymal transition and modulation of autophagy as two significant downstream pathways influenced by the established primary mechanisms. Notably, our analysis reveals context-dependent dualities, where certain circRNAs exert opposing effects in different cancers, underscoring the need for tissue-specific therapeutic strategies. Furthermore, we identify critical gaps in understanding circRNA regulatory networks and their clinical translatability. By evaluating circRNA signatures associated with paclitaxel response, this review proposes a framework for biomarker development and combination therapies to circumvent resistance. Our findings emphasize the urgency of functional studies and standardized profiling methods to harness circRNAs for personalized oncology.Keywords: RNA, Circular, Paclitaxel, Drug resistance, Neoplasm, RNA, Long noncoding

Introduction

Circular RNAs (circRNAs) are noncoding RNAs characterized by single-stranded, covalently closed circular structures that lack 5′ caps and 3′ poly(A) tails. Owing to these characteristics, they are resistant to digestion by ribonucleases, including RNase R and exonuclease, therefore, they have longer half-lives compared with linear mRNAs [1]. They have wide expression in human cells, and their expression sometimes highly surpasses the levels of their linear isomers [2]. Based on their origins, they are categorized into five groups: (1) exon-only circRNAs (EcircRNAs), (2) intron only circRNAs (ciRNA), (3) those created byback-splicing of upstream exons and intron retention (EIcircRNA), (4) circRNAs made from gene fusion (f-circRNA), and (5) read-through circRNAs formed by polymerase II (rt-circRNAs). Despite their diverse origins, circRNAs are generally made through head-to- tail back splicing [3].

CircRNAs regulate expression of their parental genes, affect alternative splicing, transcription and translation, serve as miRNA sponges, sequester RNA-binding proteins or act as protein scaffolds. They may occasionally be translated into peptides/proteins and make some pseudogenes [4].

Most importantly, the recent decade has witnessed the great improvement in the recognition of the role of circRNAs in regulating cancer progression and chemoresistance [5, 6]. Through acting as miRNA or protein sponges, interacting with proteins, and serving as regulators of transcription or translation, they affect several important aspects of cell physiology and pathology [6]. Besides, circRNAs can modulate response of cancer cells to chemotherapeutics drugs, such as paclitaxel.

Paclitaxel is among taxane chemotherapy agents interfering with microtubules and leading to blockage of mitosis and eventually cell death. In fact, β-tubulin is the main target of the paclitaxel action. The consequent microtubule stabilization results in cell cycle arrest, particularly at the G2/M phase [7], and apoptosis [8]. Additionally, as a taxane, it activates the cleavage of procaspases and proapoptotic proteins [9]. This naturally occurring compound is broadly used for the treatment of ovarian, esophageal, breast, lung, cervical, and pancreatic cancers as well as Kaposi's sarcoma. However, recurrence of cancer occurs in a number of patients treated with paclitaxel as a result of emergence of resistance to this agent. Paclitaxel resistance is evolved through complicated mechanisms involving several genes and numerous steps. These steps lead to establishment of aggressive phenotypes that result in a high mortality rate in advanced cancers [10]. Alterations in β-tubulin, activation of molecular mechanisms of drug efflux, and changes in the regulatory proteins of apoptosis are among mechanisms described for emergence of resistance to paclitaxel [11]. Specifically, the role of ATP-binding cassette (ABC) transporters, the P-glycoprotein multidrug efflux pump, breast cancer resistance protein (BCRP), and paclitaxel-resistance-associated gene-3 (TRAG-3/CSAG2) has been extensively assessed in this regard [11]. Many aspects of these processes can be modulated by circRNAs.

This review is the first to comprehensively categorize circRNA-driven paclitaxel resistance mechanisms across cancer types, critically evaluating their clinical potential as predictive biomarkers and therapeutic targets. By integrating preclinical and emerging clinical evidence, we propose a standardized framework for circRNA validation—addressing key challenges in profiling consistency and functional annotation. Our analysis prioritizes high-confidence circRNAs for translational development, offering a roadmap to overcome paclitaxel resistance through circRNA-based precision oncology.

CircRNAs and paclitaxel resistance in lung cancer

Numerous circRNAs affect response of lung cancer cells to paclitaxel (Table 1 and Fig. 1). This concept has been best evaluated in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC). For instance, circ_ZFR is an example of up-regulated circRNAs in paclitaxel-resistant NSCLC specimens and cells. Silencing of this circRNA has suppressed paclitaxel resistance, cell cycle progression, proliferation, migration and invasive properties and prompted apoptosis in paclitaxel-resistant NSCLC cells. Mechanistically, circ_ZFR silencing has obviously decreased KPNA4 levels through sequestering miR-195-5p, thus stimulating paclitaxel sensitivity and stopping cell progression in paclitaxel-resistant NSCLC cells. Furthermore, circ_ZFR silencing has increased paclitaxel sensitivity in vivo.Taken together, circ_ZFR/miR-195-5p/KPNA4 axis is an important axis for regulation of paclitaxel sensitivity of NSCLC and a therapeutic target for NSCLC [12]. Similarly, hsa_circ_0003220 expression has been reported to be raised in paclitaxel-resistant NSCLC cells and tissues. As expected, down-regulation of hsa_circ_0003220 in these cells has led to reduction of chemoresistance. Mechanistical studies have revealed the importance of hsa_circ_0003220/miR-489-3p/IGF1 axis in the regulation of chemoresistance, implying possibility of circRNA-targeted therapy for NSCLC [13]. Furthermore, hsa_circ_0074027, as another vital modulator of NSCLC progression, has been found to induce chemoresistance in NSCLC. hsa_circ_0074027 level has been meaningfully correlated with resistance to paclitaxel and two other chemotherapeutic agent, namely docetaxel and cisplatin. As anticipated, its silencing has improved chemosensitivity in vitro, inhibited tumor expansion in vivo and simultaneously induced sensitivity to chemotherapy hsa_circ_0074027/miR-379-5p/IGF1 has been recognized as the molecular route functioning in NSCLC chemoresistance [14].

On the other hand, a number of circRNAs with tumor suppressive roles have been found to induce sensitivity to paclitaxel in NSCLC. Typically, expression of these cicRNAs has been reduced in NSCLC cells and tissues. However, forced over-expression of these transcripts has been proposed as a likely strategy for combating paclitaxel resistance. hsa_circ_0002483 [15] and hsa_circ_0030998 [16] are examples of these type of circRNAs in NSCLC. Table 1 shows a comprehensive overview of both up-regulated and down-regulated circRNAs in different cancers, which act as inducers of resistance and sensitivity to paclitaxel, respectively.

Most of circRNAs that have been found to modulate response of cancer cells to paclitaxel serve as molecular sponges for miRNAs. Through sequestering miRNAs, they release mRNA targets from their inhibitory effects (Fig. 1).

CircRNAs and paclitaxel resistance in ovarian cancer

Paclitaxel is regarded as the standard care for primary ovarian cancer [29]. Since resistance to this agent affects survival of patients with ovarian cancer, several researchers have evaluated the role of circRNAs in this process (Table 2 and Fig. 2). Similar to other types of cancers, most of assessed circRNAs in this field are oncogenes that induce resistance to paclitaxel. For instance, circSETDB1 is an over-expressed circRNA in paclitaxel-resistant ovarian cancer. Knockdown of circSETDB1 has led to suppression of cell proliferation and viability, blockade of cell cycle progression, and induction of cell apoptosis in paclitaxel-resistant ovarian cancer cells. These effects are mediated through miR-508-3p/ABCC1 axis [30]. Similarly, circ_0061140 is an upregulated circRNA in the paclitaxel-resistant ovarian cancer tissues and cells compared with controls. Circ_0061140 knockdown has suppressed cell proliferation, migration and invasion, and stimulated cell apoptosis and paclitaxel sensitivity. Since the effects of circ_0061140 knockdown have been restrained by miR-136 RNAi, authors have speculated that circ_0061140 is a sponge of miR-136. Considering the direct effects of miR-136 on CBX2, circ_0061140/miR-136/CBX2 has been suggested as a molecular axis modulating response to paclitaxel in the ovarian cancer cells [31].

Among tumor suppressor circRNAs in ovarian cancer is circEXOC6B. CircRNAs and paclitaxel resistance in breast cancer

Breast cancer, particularly the triple negative subtype (TNBC) has been another focus of researches that seek contribution of circRNAs in the paclitaxel resistance (Table 3 and Fig. 3). The effects of circRNAs on paclitaxel resistance have been assessed in different breast cancer cell lines, such as MCF-10A, MCF‐7 and MDA‐MB‐231 [43, 44]. Several circRNAs are over-expressed in paclitaxel-resistant breast cancer tissues and cells. Circ_0069094 is an example of such circRNAs. Silencing of circ_0069094 has reduced tumor growth, cell proliferation and cell invasion; and induced paclitaxel sensitivity and cell apoptosis in resistant cells. Further assays have suggested that circ_0069094 sponges miR-136-5p and enhanced expression of YWHAZ which is targeted by this miRNA [43]. CircHIPK3 is another up-regulated circRNA in paclitaxel-resistant breast cancer tissues and cells compared with the sensitive equivalents. As expected its silencing has enhanced paclitaxel sensitivity both in vitro and in vivo. The miR-1286/HK2 axis has been found as a downstream pathway in this case [44]. Similarly, circ_0,007,331 contributes to the paclitaxel resistance, proliferation and motility of breast cancer cells through modulation of the miR-200b-3p/ANLN axis [45].

On the other hand, there are few examples of circRNAs that attenuate paclitaxel resistance in breast cancer cells. For example, circDUSP1 is inversely correlated with TNBC tumorigenesis and progression. Up-regulation of this circRNA has significantly reduced cell proliferation, migration, invasion, and epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT), and induced paclitaxel sensitivity. These effects are mainly exerted through sponging miR-761 and reducing its suppression on DACT2 expression. Thus, circDUSP1/miR-761/DACT2 axis might be regarded as a candidate for targeted therapy of TNBC [46].

In breast cancer, circRNAs modulate several aspects of tumorigenesis such as apoptosis, proliferation, migration, autophagy, EMT, and metabolic pathways. Through modulating these processes, they affect response of neighboring cancer cells to paclitaxel (Fig. 3).

CircRNAs and paclitaxel resistance in other cancers

The impact of circRNAs on paclitaxel resistance can be assessed through expression and functional assays in parental cancer cell lines and the corresponding paclitaxel-resistant cell lines of different origins (Table 4). An example of such studies has been conducted in nasopharyngeal cancer (NPC) cell lines using circRNA microarrays and a number of functional assays that facilitate identification of the molecular mechanisms [54]. First, authors have confirmed up-regulation of circRNA_0067717 in NPC paclitaxel-resistant cells and its association with paclitaxel resistance. Further mechanistical studies have revealed that circRNA_0067717 acts as a scaffold for TRIM41 (a ubiquitin E3 ligase) and p53 proteins. In paclitaxel-resistant NPC cells, the over-expressed circRNA_0067717 binds to TRIM41 and p53 proteins, enhancing TRIM41-induced p53 ubiquitination and degradation, leading to reduction of p53 protein levels. Notably, inhibition of circRNA_0067717 with these proteins results in reduction of resistant phenotype of NPC cells [54].

CircMYBL2 is another circRNA that is upregulated in cervical cancer tissues and cells, mainly in paclitaxel-resistant tissues and cells. This stable circRNA is principally located in the cytoplasm where it sponges miR-665 and promotes expression of EGFR, thus enhancing the paclitaxel resistance of cervical cancer cells [55].

It is worth mentioning that circRNAs mostly act as miRNA sponges to release the mRNA targets from the inhibitory effects of miRNAs. Such interactions between circRNAs and miRNAs have important role in paclitaxel resistance in several types of cancers (Fig. 4). Circ_0007534/miR-625/ZEB2 [57], circ_0039569/miR-1271-5p/PHF6 [58], circ_ZFR/miR-944/IL10 [59], circ-CEP128/miR-432-5p/MCL1 [60], circ_0004488/miR-136/MEX3C [61], circMYBL2/miR-665/EGFR [55], circ-BIRC6/miR-877-5p/YWHAZ [62], circ_0005785/miR-640/GSK3B [63], circ_002136/miR-16-5p/HMGA1 [64], circPLEC/miR-198/MUC19 [65], circ-PVT1/miR-124-3p/ZEB1 [66], circ_0006168/miR-194-5p/JMJD1C [67], and cPKM/miR-199a-5p/IGF2BP2 [68] are examples of circRNA/miRNA/mRNA axes contributing to paclitaxel resistance.

In these axes, the expression of circRNAs is increased, leading to the inhibition of miRNA function as they act as suppressors of their target gene expressions. As a result, with the sponging of miRNAs and their subsequent reduction, the expression of their target genes is enhanced. Exosome-derived circRNAs

Exosomal transfer of circRNAs facilitates dissemination of resistance phenotype within the tumor microenvironment. Tumor microenvironment includes several types of cells, such as macrophages, T cells, cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), and tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), with significant roles in therapy resistance. These cells interact with each other and with the tumor cells, influencing the effectiveness of cancer treatments [70]. Table 5 provides a summary of circRNAs that have been detected in tumor-derived exosomes. While all types of cells within tumor microenvironment might secrete exosomes, the presented data in this table describes the exosomes secreted by tumor cells. For instance, circ_0001955 is a highly expressed circRNA in breast cancer tissues and cell lines, as well as exosomes derived from these cells. Down-regulation of circ_0001955 has stopped proliferation, induced paclitaxel sensitivity, and decreased glycolysis in two breast cancer cell lines. This circRNA exerts its roles through sponging miR-708-5p, and releasing PGK1 from its inhibitory effect [71]. CircBACH1 is another example of exosome-transferred circRNAs whose expression is increased in paclitaxel-treated breast cancer-derived exosomes and breast cancer tissues. The mentioned exosomes could promote paclitaxel-resistance and angiogenesis via increasing circBACH1 in the target cells. Downregulation of this circRNA has improved paclitaxel-sensitiveness through suppression of the cell viability, stemness, migration, and angiogenesis. Furthermore, miR-217 has been found to be sponged by circBACH1. Since this miRNA directly targets the GTPase-activating SH3 domain-binding protein 2 (G3BP2), circBACH1/miR-217/G3BP2 axis has been suggested as the molecular axis that induces paclitaxel resistance [72]. Finally, exosomal transfer of circDNER has been found to enhance paclitaxel resistance and tumorigenicity through modulation of miR-139-5p/ITGB8 axis [73].

Concluding remarks

Although chemoresistance has been traditionally attributed to genomic alterations [11], the epigenome has now been found to affect this phenotype [74]. Thus, recent studies have focused on the role of epigenetic regulators in the emergence of resistance. The importance of two groups of epigenetic modulators, namely long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) and miRNAs in paclitaxel resistance has been thoroughly reviewed [75]. CircRNAs, as another type of epigenetic regulators in the context of cancer, have gained attention in this aspect in recent years. In fact, several mechanisms of chemoresistance including EMT, signaling pathway bypass, drug efflux induction, and drug entry defect can be affected by circRNAs. In addition, circRNAs contribute to chemoresistance by modulating critical cellular processes: (1) Cell cycle dysregulation (e.g., circCELSR1 promoting G1/S transition [36]), (2) Inhibition of apoptosis (e.g., circERC1 blocking caspase activation [69]), (3) EMT induction (e.g., circ_0007534 activating ZEB2 (via miR-625) [57], and circPVT1 activating ZEB1 (via miR-124-3p) [66]), (4) Autophagy activation (e.g., hsa_circ_0011298 [21], circ-ABCB10 [47] and circ_0006528 [48], hsa_circ_0011298 [21], circ_0076305 [25], circHIPK3 [44], circ-RNF111 [49] and circ_0006168 [67]), and (5) Angiogenesis promotion (e.g., circ_0010235 [26]).

In addition, circRNAs have crucial roles in the development and regulation of cancer stem cells (CSCs) [76]. Thus, abnormal levels of circRNAs in cancer cells may lead to drug resistance through modulation of CSCs. PI3K/AKT signaling pathway, as a major driver of CSCs [77], has been shown to be affected by a number circRNAs regulating paclitaxel resistance (Fig. 5).

The PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway is indeed a key signaling pathway implicated in the biology of cancer stem cells (CSCs). This pathway is crucial for regulating cell survival, proliferation, and metabolism, and its dysregulation is frequently observed in various cancers. Notably, circWAC induces resistance to paclitaxel in triple-negative breast cancer through sequestering miR-142, upregulating WWP1 and activating the PI3K/AKT pathway [51]. Similarly, circAMOTL1 has an important role in posttranscriptional regulation of AKT in these cells [50]. In cervical cancer, circMYBL2 regulates the resistance to paclitaxel through miR-665-dependent regulation of EGFR, and subsequent effect on PI3K [55]. On the other hand, in ovarian cancer cells, circPLEKHM3 acts as a tumor suppressor negatively regulating AKT1 [42]. Arrows indicate regulatory interactions (↑: activation; ⊥: suppression)

Most notably, a number of miRNAs have been found to mediate the effects of different circRNAs on paclitaxel resistance in different tissues. For instance, miR-136 is the shared miRNA in at least three circRNA/miRNA/mRNA axes influencing paclitaxel resistance, namely circ_0061140/miR-136/CBX2 in ovarian cancer [31], circ_0069094/miR-136-5p/YWHAZ in breast cancer [43], and circ_0004488/miR-136/MEX3C in cervical cancer [61].

More importantly, chemoresistant cells secrete circRNAs via exosomes, disseminating resistance traits within the tumor microenvironment.

From a clinical point of view, since circRNAs are stable in bodily fluids, they are regarded as non-invasive diagnostic/prognostic tools to predict resistance to paclitaxel. Moreover, modulation of Wnt/β-catenin, AKT/ERK, PI3 K/AKT, mTOR, NF-κB and STAT related pathways has been suggested as possible routes of regulation of paclitaxel resistance by lncRNAs and miRNAs [75].

Therapeutic targeting of circRNAs to overcome paclitaxel resistance: clinical perspectives

Silencing oncogenic circRNAs through using antisense oligonucleotides or restoring tumor-suppressive ones are promising strategies to re-sensitize tumors.Several in vitro and in vivo studies have provided evidence for feasibility and efficiency of such methods. Particularly, combination therapies targeting circRNAs and paclitaxel may improve efficacy of this chemotherapeutic agent.

It is also important to emphasize that circRNAs have context-dependent roles. This highlights the need for context-specific approaches. Emerging evidence positions circRNAs as promising therapeutic targets to reverse paclitaxel resistance in clinical oncology. Several strategies are being explored to modulate resistance-associated circRNAs, including short hairpin RNAs (shRNAs) for knockdown (e.g., targeting oncogenic circPVT1 in gastric cancer [66]) and CRISPR/Cas9 systems for precise excision of circRNA-forming loci. While the latter strategy has not been implemented for reduction of resistance to paclitaxel, the feasibility of this technique has been assessed in the mouse models [78]. Notably, nanoparticle-based delivery methods show particular promise for circRNA-targeting therapies, with recent studies demonstrating successful encapsulation of circRNAs into lipid nanoparticles [79]. For diagnostic applications, future research should find circRNAs which exhibit consistent differential expression in liquid biopsies from paclitaxel-resistant patients across multiple cancer types. Such circRNAs will be used as non-invasive biomarkers. However, significant challenges remain in clinical translation, including the need for standardized detection protocols and a deeper understanding of circRNA pharmacodynamics. Future clinical trials exploring circRNA biomarkers and preclinical studies of combination therapies (circRNA inhibitors with albumin-bound paclitaxel) are needed to identify circRNAs'clinical potential. Future efforts should prioritize the development of circRNA-targeting drugs with improved tissue specificity and the validation of predictive circRNA signatures in large, multicenter cohorts to facilitate personalized treatment approaches for paclitaxel-resistant cancers.

In brief, circRNAs have pivotal roles in paclitaxel resistance via different routes. Their dual roles as both promoters and suppressors of resistance highlight their complexity. Evaluation of circRNA profiles for biomarker development and targeted therapies is a promising route for overcoming resistance and personalizing cancer treatment.

Future research opportunities

There are several unexplored avenues about the role of circRNAs in paclitaxel resistance. For instance, further research is needed to elucidate how specific circRNAs regulate drug efflux pumps (e.g., MDR1, ABCB1), apoptosis evasion, and EMT. Moreover, the interactions of circRNA with RNA-binding proteins (RBPs) and their impact on signaling pathways (e.g., PI3K/AKT, Wnt/β-catenin) should be assessed in future studies.

The advent of high-throughput screening methods has facilitated large-scale profiling of circRNAs in paclitaxel-resistant vs. sensitive tumors to identify novel biomarkers. Integration of multi-omics data (transcriptomics, proteomics, epigenomics) using system biology methods would also enable uncovering circRNA-mediated regulatory networks. The obtained data should be further validated using genetically engineered models (CRISPR-based circRNA knockout/overexpression). For identification of clinical correlations, it is necessary to conduct prospective studies to assess circRNA expression patterns in patient-derived xenografts (PDXs) and liquid biopsies (circulating circRNAs). Besides, correlation of circRNA signatures with therapeutic response and survival outcomes should be assessed.

Advancements in sequencing and bioengineering such as single-cell circRNA sequencing are accelerating circRNA research, enabling resolution of circRNA heterogeneity within tumor subpopulations contributing to resistance. Finally, CRISPR-Cas13 can be used for circRNA targeting, allowing selective degradation of oncogenic circRNAs without affecting linear transcripts.

Despite promising findings, key hurdles remain. First, there is no consensus on circRNA detection methods (RT-qPCR, RNA-Seq) and normalization controls. Variability in circRNA expression across tumor subtypes and stages further complicates the situation. Poor stability and off-target effects of circRNA-targeting oligonucleotides are other obstacles for in vivo studies. Taken together, circRNAs hold immense potential as biomarkers and therapeutic targets in paclitaxel resistance, but their clinical translation demands interdisciplinary collaboration. Future studies should focus on mechanistic validation, advanced technologies, and overcoming delivery challenges to harness circRNAs for precision oncology.
