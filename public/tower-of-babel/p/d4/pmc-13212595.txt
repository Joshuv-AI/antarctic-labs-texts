Efferocytosis-related gene PLG in prognosis, immune correlation, and contribution to malignant behavior in vitro and in vivo of clear cell renal cell carcinoma
Authors: Yusong Zhou, Zhun Zhang, Yuqing Liu, Wei Liu, Jing Dai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Efferocytosis plays an important role in tumor progression; however, its specific involvement in clear cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC) remains to be fully elucidated. In this study, efferocytosis-related genes (ERGs) were obtained from GeneCards and KEGG databases, and their expression profiles were analyzed in ccRCC using TCGA and GEO datasets. A total of 35 differentially expressed ERGs (DEGs) were identified. Among them, nine genes were found to be associated with overall survival using univariate Cox regression analysis. Consensus clustering further stratified patients into three molecular subtypes with distinct biological pathway characteristics. Subsequently, a risk score model based on six genes (PLAUR, MIAT, PLG, HAVCR1, ABCA1, and ABCG1) was constructed using LASSO regression and multivariate Cox analysis. This model stratified patients into high- and low-risk groups with significantly different survival outcomes. The risk score was significantly associated with overall survival and remained an independent factor in multivariate analysis. In addition, immune microenvironment analysis indicated that the risk score and model genes were closely related to immune characteristics. Among these genes, PLG was identified as a key gene with lower expression in tumor tissues. Functional experiments showed that PLG overexpression inhibited ccRCC cell proliferation and migration, reduced PD-L1 expression, and enhanced CD8⁺ T cell-mediated cytotoxicity. In summary, this study developed a risk score model associated with survival outcomes in ccRCC and highlighted a potential role of PLG in tumor progression and immune regulation. These findings provide a basis for further mechanistic studies and clinical validation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-46752-y.

Introduction

Renal cell carcinoma (RCC) has more than 400,000 new cases and more than 150,000 deaths every year, ranking 14th among cancers worldwide1. The age at diagnosis is approximately 60 years, and twice as many men are diagnosed as women2. There are several subtypes of RCC, clear cell renal cell carcinomas (ccRCC) are the most common subtype of RCC, and about 70% of patients are diagnosed with ccRCC3. Although ccRCC can often be detected early and successfully managed with surgical or ablation methods, up to one-third of patients may develop metastases4. The development of these diseases will lead to reduced therapeutic efficacy and almost fatality. Therefore, it is crucial to discover their prognostic markers, which will facilitate disease treatment and improve patient cure rates.

Efferocytosis is a mechanism that removes dying cells, playing a vital role in preserving tissue balance and recovering homeostasis post-illness5. The weakened efferocytosis will lead to the accumulation of dead cells and induce the continued necrosis of normal cells, thus leading to diseases such as autoimmunity and tissue necrosis6. Reduced efferocytosis has also been found to be a driving factor in key mechanisms in the development and progression of cancer. Efferocytosis produces an immunosuppressive phenotype within tumors through a series of coordinated signaling events involving multiple parts of the tumor environment7. In the tumor microenvironment (TME), efferocytosis can shift cytokine production from promoting immune responses to suppressing them, altering macrophage and dendritic cell characteristics, which may hinder anti-tumor immunity and speed up tumor progression8. Some studies have found that efferocytosis can reprogram the TME to promote pancreatic cancer liver metastasis, and research on efferocytosis can prevent pancreatic cancer liver metastasis9. However, the role of efferocytosis in ccRCC remains unclear. Therefore, this study aimed to reveal the prognostic and immune value of efferocytosis in ccRCC.

This research utilized extensive bioinformatics techniques to pinpoint and confirm crucial regulators in ccRCC. Further illustrating the value and potential significance of efferocytosis in ccRCC. This research holds considerable importance for identifying prognostic genes in diagnosing and treating ccRCC patients, potentially serving as a target for these patients and offering fresh perspectives for future scientific investigations.

Materials and methods

Information origins and initial processing

As of April 20, 2024, this research employed RNA sequencing data and clinical details for ccRCC patients sourced from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/. The dataset included patient age, survival status, and tumor TNM staging, detailed in Supplementary Table 1. Furthermore, the GSE16449, GSE29609 and GSE40435 dataset from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo) was employed. The GSE16449 dataset contains 40 tumor samples for integration with TCGA data and subsequent analysis; the GSE29609 dataset contains clinical information for 39 ccRCC patients, used to validate the survival status of the risk model; and the GSE40435 dataset contains 101 adjacent normal tissue samples and 101 ccRCC samples for external validation of model genes. This dataset underwent both quantile normalization and log2 transformation before being analyzed. ERGs were sourced from the GeneCards database (https://www.genecards.org/) and the KEGG database (https://www.kegg.jp/entry/hsa04148)10–12. When using KEGG imagery, please cite this source in the appropriate figure legend, as per the citation guidelines: www.kegg.jp/kegg/kegg1.html. After removing duplicate genes from both databases, a total of 272 genes were obtained, as shown in Supplementary Table 2.

Identification and analysis of DEGs and CNVs associated with overall survival

Initially, the “limma” package was employed to detect DEGs between normal and cancerous samples, using criteria of |logFC|> 1 and a p-value less than 0.05. These DEGs were visualized using heatmaps generated by the “pheatmap” package. Univariate Cox regression analysis was used to evaluate DEGs associated with overall survival. We utilized multiple R packages such as “igraph”, “psych”, “reshape2”, and “RColorBrewer” to explore the connections between ERGs and risk factors by creating correlation network diagrams. The model was optimized by employing LASSO regression to eliminate genes that were overfitted. Data on copy number variations (CNVs) were obtained from the UCSC Xena database and analyzed using a Perl script to calculate mutation rates. The “RCircos” package was used to generate circular plots illustrating the mutation positions of DEGs.

Consensus clustering and verification of ccRCC

Cohorts from TCGA and GEO were categorized into the most effective classification based on consensus clustering of ERGs using the R package “ConsensusClusterPlus” with k ranging from 2 to 9. Clustering was conducted using the “Euclidean” distance metric and validated through 1000 iterations to ensure robustness. This approach focuses on enhancing intra-group similarity and reducing inter-group similarity. The effectiveness of the classification was evaluated through principal component analysis (PCA). Survival curves were created with the “survival” and “survminer” packages to evaluate the survival rates across various groups. Additionally, heatmaps and boxplots were created with the “pheatmap” and “boxplot” packages to visualize differences in parameter expressions across clusters.

Pathway enrichment analysis of ERGs

Gene Set Variation Analysis (GSVA) is a method employed to evaluate alterations in pathway activity by examining differences in gene set enrichment among various samples. In contrast to conventional gene set enrichment analysis, GSVA assesses the gradual variations in pathway activity and is capable of categorizing samples according to these changes13. Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) is a technique employed to determine if predefined groups of genes, associated with specific biological functions, pathways, or chromosomal regions, are significantly overrepresented in a ranked gene list from an experiment. It helps identify whether the genes in a particular set are preferentially upregulated or downregulated, providing insights into the underlying biological processes or pathways that may be associated with a particular phenotype or condition14,15. This research utilized KEGG pathway analysis to investigate the variations in pathways among the ERGs. The examination utilized the R libraries “GSEABase”, “GSVA”, and “clusterProfiler”. To determine pertinent pathways, a p-value cutoff of less than 0.05 was used.

Development and validation of risk models associated with efferocytosis

In this study, ccRCC patients were divided into two cohorts: one for training and the other for testing. LASSO regression implemented using the “glmnet” R package was used to identify potential overall survival associated factors in ERGs. These ERGs were then validated by cross-validation and included in the best multivariate Cox model based on the minimum error criterion. Based on the best combination of regression coefficients and λ values, we selected 6 ERGs to construct the risk score model. The risk score was calculated using the formula: risk score = ∑ (coefi × Exp-genei). Using the median risk score as the cutoff value, patients were divided into high-risk and low-risk groups. Overall survival associated markers of ERGs were created using the “survival” and “survminer” libraries, and their effects were evaluated by hazard ratios (HRs). The effectiveness of the risk model was evaluated over time using ROC curves and AUC metrics. Multivariate Cox regression analysis was performed to explore the association between clinical characteristics and outcomes. To predict patient survival using the risk score, a nomogram integrating multiple clinical variables was developed, and its accuracy and reliability were evaluated by C-index, calibration plot, and decision curve analysis (DCA).

Immunoassays and sensitivity analyzes for clinical treatments

The CIBERSORT algorithm was utilized to perform a correlation analysis, examining the link between ImmuneScores and StromalScores in relation to the risk score16. The ESTIMATE algorithm (which stands for Estimation of Stromal and Immune cells in Malignant Tumor tissues using Expression data) was utilized to determine the scores of the TME. This examination enabled us to contrast the immune and stromal scores of high-risk versus low-risk groups. Additionally, we used the “OncoPredict” R package to forecast drug responses and identify relevant biomarkers17 which is used to analyze the sensitivity of tumor therapeutic drugs in the TCGA-ccRCC dataset. The IC50 values of the drugs are calculated to reflect the drug’s sensitivity in the high- and low-risk groups. The “ggplot2” R package creates a box plot to visualize this result.

Identification of genes independently associated with overall survival

Genes independently associated with overall survival were identified using univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses. The results, including p-values, HRs, and 95% confidence intervals (CIs), were visualized using the “forestplot” package. The “ggplot2” package was applied to compare the expression levels of candidate genes between ccRCC and adjacent normal tissues. Differences in survival between groups with high and low gene expression were evaluated using Kaplan–Meier survival analysis and the log-rank test. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Tissue origin

Between June 2023 and May 2024, 12 pairs of clinical tissue specimens from ccRCC and normal renal tissues were collected at the Third Xiangya Hospital of Central South University. These specimens were primarily used for functional validation to investigate the expression level of PLG (plasminogen). Only a subset of samples (n = 3) with adequate tissue quality were used for IHC analysis.

Immunohistochemical staining

Samples were preserved in 4% paraformaldehyde, encased in paraffin, and sliced into 6 μm sections. For immunohistochemical staining, the sections were first deparaffinized and rehydrated. The sections were exposed to Tris–EDTA buffer (10 mM Tris–HCl and 1 mM EDTA) and heated in a pressure cooker for 5 min to extract antigens and block endogenous peroxidase activity. Following three washes, the samples were treated with BSA for half an hour and then left overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies targeting PLG, Ki67, PCNA, or E-cadherin. The following day, the segments were exposed to secondary antibodies at ambient temperature for one hour. Following hematoxylin staining of the nuclei, the samples were dehydrated and then mounted using neutral resin.

Cell culture

The ccRCC cell lines 786-O, ACHN and Normal cell line HK-2 were obtained from Procell Life Science & Technology Co., Ltd (Wuhan, China). Cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 or MEM medium with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS). All cells were incubated at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere.

Establishment of stable PLG-overexpressing cell lines

Lentiviral vectors for PLG overexpression (overexpression Ctrl (OC), overexpression PLG (OE)) were constructed by GeneChem Co. Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Briefly, cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 2.0 × 105 cells per well. After 12 h, the lentiviruses were added to the wells with 1 mL of RPMI 1640 or MEM medium and 40 μL of transfection reagent (GeneChem, Shanghai, China) to incubate the cells for 12 h. Following a washing step with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), the medium was replaced with 5A medium and incubated for an additional 48 h. Finally, puromycin was added to the cell culture flask to eliminate untransduced cells, establishing stable PLG-overexpressing and control cell lines.

qRT-PCR

RNA was isolated using an RNeasy mini kit (QIAGEN, Beijing, China), and cDNA synthesis for PCR was performed with a high-capacity reverse transcription kit (Thermo, Shanghai, China). The TaqMan Gene Expression Master Mix was utilized. Following the manufacturer’s guidelines, TaqMan probes for human PLG and actin (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China) were utilized with Bio-Rad equipment (Shanghai, China). All primers used were synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai) Co., Ltd.

Western blot

Stable cells were washed twice with PBS, and total protein was extracted using RIPA lysis buffer containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors and quantified by BCA. These protein extracts were then separated by SDS-PAGE, transferred to PVDF membranes, blocked at room temperature for 1 h with protein-free rapid blocking buffer, incubated overnight at 4 °C with the primary antibody against the target protein, and further incubated for 1 h with peroxide-labeled secondary antibody. Visualization was performed using the ChemiDoc system. Band intensity was quantified using ImageJ.

MTT

Stable cells were plated into a 96-well plate at a concentration of 6000 cells per well. Afterward, each well received 50 μL of a 2 mg/mL MTT solution and was left to incubate for four hours. Following the removal of the medium, 150 μL of DMSO was introduced into each well and stirred for 10 min. Absorbance was then measured at 490 nm.

Colony formation assay

Stable cells were seeded into 24-well plates at a concentration of 1000 cells per well for cloning. The medium was changed every 3–4 days. After 10 days, the cells in the wells were fixed with 10% formaldehyde solution. Finally, 0.1% crystal violet was added for staining. The cells were washed twice with PBS, air-dried, and photographed. The number of colonies formed was calculated using ImageJ.

Scratch assay

Stable cells seeded into 12-well plates at a concentration of 3 × 105 cells per well. The following day, a monolayer of cells formed at the bottom of the plate. The cell layer was scratched using the tip of a 200 μL pipette to create scratch areas, followed by washing twice with PBS. These cells were then cultured in serum-free medium for the specified time. Cell images were taken under a microscope at the same location at 0 and 24 h. The width of the scratches was measured using ImageJ, and cell migration was calculated.

Transwell assay

Stable cells (40,000 cells) were placed in 200 μL of serum-free medium in the upper chamber of a Transwell, while 600 μL of medium containing 10% serum was added to the lower compartment as a chemical inducer. After 24 h of treatment, the cells were fixed with 4% formaldehyde, stained with 0.1% crystal violet, and the cells located on the upper membrane side were removed using absorbent cotton. Finally, the cells were observed and photographed under a microscope.

In vivo tumor models

5- to 6-week-old female BALB/c nude mice were selected for this study, as female mice are less prone to fighting-related injuries and mortality compared to males, and no significant sex-dependent differences in tumor engraftment or growth have been reported for established RCC cell line-based xenograft models. Mice were purchased from Skajingda Biotechnology and housed them under specific pathogen-free conditions. Environmental parameters were controlled by maintaining a temperature of 22–26 °C, humidity of approximately 55%, and a 12-h light–dark cycle, and the mice were acclimatized to this environment for 5 days. Mice were randomly assigned to cages, with a maximum of five mice per group, and randomly assigned to different experimental groups to minimize bias. To establish a xenograft model, mice were subcutaneously injected with 200 μl of a 2 × 106 cell suspension (786-O-OC or 786-O-OE cells). The mice’s weight and the volume of the tumor (long and short axes) were measured every other day. Tumor size was calculated using the formula: 1/2 × (long diameter × short diameter2). All mice were euthanized on day 25 after implantation, and the tumor tissue was harvested, photographed, and weighed. Finally, the tumor tissues were fixed, embedded in paraffin, and sliced into thin sections for immunohistochemical staining analysis using anti-PLG, Ki67, PCNA, and E-Cadherin antibodies. All experiments were conducted in accordance with Chinese regulations regarding the use and care of laboratory animals.

CD8+ T cell cytotoxic assay

CD8+ T cells were obtained from the peripheral blood of healthy donor according to methods reported in the literature18. In brief, Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated from fresh peripheral blood of healthy donor using Ficoll-Paque Plus (GE Healthcare, USA) via density gradient centrifugation. Briefly, the peripheral blood was carefully layered onto Ficoll-Paque Plus solution, followed by centrifugation at 2000 rpm for 20 min at room temperature to separate PBMCs from other blood components. After centrifugation, the PBMC layer was carefully collected, washed twice with PBS to remove residual Ficoll-Paque Plus, and resuspended in RPMI 1640 medium. CD8+ T cells were then purified from the isolated PBMCs using a CD8+ T cell isolation kit (Miltenyi Biotec, Germany) strictly following the manufacturer’s instructions to ensure high purity of the target cells. Isolated CD8+ T cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium (Procell, China) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco, USA), 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Solarbio, China), 50 U/mL interleukin-2 (IL-2, PeproTech, USA) and 10 ng/mL interleukin-15 (IL-15, PeproTech, USA). The cells were incubated at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ atmosphere for 5–7 days to induce activation and expansion. Prior to use in subsequent experiments, the purity of CD8+ T cells was verified by flow cytometry (BD FACSCanto II, USA), and only samples with a CD8+ T cell purity of ≥ 90% were used for further assays. Tumor cells were transduced with lentivirus-packaged overexpression PLG plasmid or control plasmid and incubated for 24 h. Following the incubation period, the cells were placed into a 96-well plate with a concentration of 1 × 104 cells in each well. CD8+ T cells were introduced to the plate following a 12-h interval. The co-incubation lasted for 48 h, after which the medium was discarded, and the tumor cells were rinsed twice with PBS to eliminate the T cells. The impact of CD8+ T cells on the viability of tumor cells was evaluated through an MTT assay.

Statistical analysis

Data annotation and organization for transcriptome, clinical, and gene expression data were performed using PERL (v5.30.0). Statistical analyses were conducted with R software, specifically versions 4.3.0 and 4.3.3, with the Wilcoxon test applied to assess variations associated with efferocytosis based on sample size. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Result

Identification of differential genes and mutation frequency analysis in ccRCC

The flow chart of this study is shown in Supplementary Fig. 1. First, we employed the “limma” package to conduct differential expression analysis on the TCGA-ccRCC transcriptome dataset in order to determine the quantity of DEGs between ccRCC and normal samples. The analysis identified a total of 35 DEGs. Of these, 5 genes were significantly upregulated in normal samples, while 30 genes showed higher expression in ccRCC tissues. Visualizations used red to represent high expression and blue for low expression (Fig. 1A, B). To further analyze these DEGs, univariate Cox regression identified 9 genes associated with ccRCC prognosis: PLAUR, MIAT, HAVCR1, PLG, PROS1, ABCA1, CD14, ABCG1, and SCARF1. These genes were illustrated in forest plots and corresponding network plots (Fig. 1C, D). Among these, six genes with an HR below 1 are deemed low-risk and highlighted in blue, whereas three genes with an HR above 1 are classified as high-risk and indicated in red. These genes exhibit significant interrelationships and mutual influence. Afterward, we examined CNV data from the TCGA database to evaluate the chromosomal changes in these DEGs. The CNV analysis showed that HAVCR1 and CD14 had a higher frequency of amplification compared to loss, primarily occurring on chromosome 5. Conversely, PROS1 and PLG exhibited more frequent loss than amplification, occurring on chromosomes 3 and 6, respectively. Other genes showed minimal mutations, and no CNV mutation was detected in the MIAT gene (Fig. 1E, F). The differences in the positions of these genes indicate the differences in the genes. Three cluster types identified based on 9 DEGs

To delve deeper into the importance of ERGs in ccRCC, we utilized the R package “ConsensusClusterPlus” to perform consensus clustering on 9 prognosis-related DEGs (p < 0.05). The best categorization was obtained with k = 3, segmenting ccRCC patients into three unique subgroups (Fig. 2A–C). We then plotted the CDF graph, Delta Area graph, and consensus matrix heat map, which also showed that K = 3 was the best classification (Supplementary Fig. 2A–C). The study included 124 samples in Group A, 274 in Group B, and 135 in Group C. Survival analysis showed that as survival time lengthened, the likelihood of survival generally declined; nonetheless, Group A consistently had higher survival rates than Groups B and C (Fig. 2D). PCA, t-SNE, and UMAP analyses validated that the classification based on these 9 DEGs was distinctly separable, proving the precision of this classification approach (Fig. 2E–G). Subsequently, we analyzed the expression levels of these nine genes among the three different subtypes. Notable differences in expression were observed, particularly for PLG, which was highly expressed in subtype A and relatively low in subtypes B and C (Fig. 2H, I). Development and assessment of ERG risk models associated with ccRCC

To prevent overfitting and improve data dimensionality, we utilized LASSO and multivariate Cox regression techniques. These analyses pinpointed 6 ERGs crucial for ccRCC prognosis (Fig. 4A, B). We then used these 6 ERGs to develop a riskscore model. Supplementary Table 3 provides the coefficients for these ERGs used in calculating the risk score. The risk score is computed as follows: Risk score = PLAUR × 0.193234812794492 + MIAT × 0.188115390204241 + HAVCR1 × 0.11712386502624-PLG × 0.156366787553141 − ABCA1 × 0.310479843312512 − ABCG1 × 0.308507451071034. The data was split randomly into training and testing sets, with patients in each set further categorized into high-risk and low-risk groups according to the median risk score, shown in Supplementary Table 4. Kaplan–Meier survival curves revealed that individuals classified as high-risk exhibited notably worse outcomes in both the training and validation cohorts (Fig. 4C, D), showing a significant decline in survival rates over time. The predictive model demonstrated high accuracy, as shown by time-dependent ROC curves for 1, 3, and 5-year overall survival (OS) in both the training and validation cohorts (Fig. 4E, F). These findings affirm that the model effectively predicts patient survival. Subsequently, we validated this risk model using the GSE29609 dataset. Patients were stratified into high- and low-risk groups based on the median risk score; evidently, the survival time of the high-risk group was shorter than that of the low-risk group (Supplementary Fig. 3A, B). Foremore, we used the GSE40435 dataset as an external validation set to verify the area under the ROC curve (AUC) values of the six genes. The results showed that the AUC values of the six genes were all greater than 0.8, indicating that these genes can be used to construct the model with satisfactory results (Supplementary Fig. 3C). Additionally, univariate Cox regression analysis was performed to assess the association between the risk score and clinical factors, including age, sex, and T and N stages. The results indicated that age, T3 and T4 stages, as well as the risk score, were independently associated with overall survival in ccRCC patients (Fig. 4G). This suggests that the risk score, calculated from the six ERGs, serves as a reliable and precise indicator of prognosis in ccRCC patients. The heatmap analysis of risk indicated that, apart from PLAUR and MIAT, identified as high-risk genes, the other genes fall into the low-risk category (Supplementary Fig. 3D). We assessed and contrasted the risk scores among the three recognized clusters to explore the connection between clusters and risk models. Significant variations in risk scores were observed among these clusters (Supplementary Fig. 3E). Moreover, a Sankey plot was used to illustrate the correlations and developmental trends between the clustering results and efferocytosis-related risk scores (Supplementary Fig. 3F). This visualization suggests that a substantial proportion of patients exhibit a relatively favorable survival trend. We developed a nomogram incorporating each clinical feature and risk score to estimate patient survival. For instance, an overall score of 153 points correlates with projected survival rates of 97.7% after 1 year, 93.7% after 3 years, and 89.2% after 5 years (Fig. 5A). To validate the accuracy of this nomogram, we employed a calibration curve, which demonstrated that the nomogram’s survival predictions closely aligned with the ideal curve, confirming its strong predictive value (Fig. 5B). The cumulative risk curve analysis revealed that overall survival (OS) risk for ccRCC patients increased over time, with high-risk patients showing higher vulnerability compared to low-risk patients (Fig. 5C). The nomogram showed superior performance in predicting both short- and long-term survival compared to other clinical features (Fig. 5D–F). In addition, we combined the risk score with ccRCC clinical characteristics (age, sex, T, M stage) for univariate and multivariate Cox regression analysis. The results showed that the risk score can serve as an independent predictor of ccRCC progression (Supplementary Fig. 3G, H). Analysis of immune activity in ccRCC

Tumor cells often display inherent resistance to cancer therapies, a phenomenon frequently affected by the non-malignant cells present in the TME19. To explore the TME differences between high-risk and low-risk groups, we used the “CIBERSORT” R script to quantify immune cell infiltration. Figure 6A shows the relative proportions of various immune cell types associated with different risk scores. Supplementary Fig. 4A highlights how the proportions of 13 immune cell types change with varying risk scores: 7 cell types increased, while 6 decreased as the risk score rose. Comparing immune cell expression scores between high-risk and low-risk groups revealed significant differences in 11 immune cell types, with p-values < 0.05 indicating statistical significance (Fig. 6B). Correlation analysis between the six identified genes and their risk scores, as well as various immune cell types, revealed a close association between these genes and different immune cells (Fig. 6C). Immune cells such as T cell regulatory (Tregs) and Macrophages M0 were positively correlated with the risk score, while immune cells such as Mast cells resting and T cell CD4 memory resting were negatively correlated with the risk score. This difference in correlation indicates that patients with different risk scores have different immune statuses, which has important implications for immunotherapy. Additionally, we conducted a correlation analysis among immune cells to better understand the TME composition (Fig. 6D). We evaluated stromal cell components, immune cell infiltration levels, and tumor purity by calculating Immune scores, Stromal scores, and Estimated scores for both high-risk and low-risk groups based on expression profiles (Fig. 6E). The calculated risk score was also associated with immune checkpoint-related genes (Supplementary Fig. 4B). This shows an association between patient risk stratification and potential response to immunotherapy, indicating that patients with different risks may be suitable for immune checkpoint inhibitor treatment. Drug sensitivity analysis of anticancer drugs

In ccRCC, despite the critical role of drug treatments, their effectiveness remains suboptimal. To address this, we performed a sensitivity analysis of anti-cancer drugs to identify those that are sensitive to different patient risk groups. By examining the relationship between the IC50 values of these drugs and risk scores, we identified a total of 88 anti-cancer drugs whose sensitivity correlates with the risk score. Out of these, 21 medications showed increased sensitivity in the high-risk category, indicating they could be more beneficial for individuals with advanced-stage ccRCC. On the other hand, 67 medications showed increased effectiveness in the low-risk category, suggesting they could be more appropriate for patients with early-stage ccRCC (Supplementary Table 5). Figure 7F–J display five medications that demonstrate significant effectiveness in the low-risk category, indicating they could offer enhanced treatment advantages for patients with early-stage ccRCC. PLG is an independent prognostic protein gene of great value

After systematically evaluating the relevance of the six genes in ccRCC, we performed univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses to identify genes independently associated with overall survival. The results indicated that ABCG1, MIAT, and PLG were independent factors associated with overall survival (Fig. 8A, B). We then compared the expression levels of these three genes in ccRCC tissues versus adjacent normal tissues. Our findings showed that PLG was expressed at lower levels in ccRCC tissues, suggesting it may function as a tumor suppressor gene. Conversely, ABCG1 and MIAT were found to be highly expressed in ccRCC, indicating their role as oncogenes that contribute to cancer progression (Fig. 8C). Further analysis of the impact of these gene expressions on patient survival revealed that patients with high expression of ABCG1 and PLG had significantly better survival rates compared to those with low expression. In contrast, high expression of MIAT was associated with lower survival rates (Fig. 8D–F). This result appears counterintuitive, as ABCG1, being an oncogene, would typically be expected to correlate with poorer survival outcomes. Upon closer examination, it was noted that MIAT is a long non-coding RNA (lncRNA), which does not encode proteins but can still play crucial roles in regulating gene expression and cellular processes20. Given this context, the paradoxical survival association for ABCG1 may be attributed to complex interactions between these genes and their regulatory networks. Additional evaluation of PLG levels was performed on two ccRCC cell lines and one normal cell line utilizing WB and PCR methods. Overexpression of PLG inhibits the proliferation and migration of ccRCC cells in vivo

To further explore the impact of PLG overexpression on tumor development in vivo, we utilized a subcutaneous xenograft model in nude mice. Mice were injected with cells modified to overproduce PLG (OE) as well as with control cells (OC). The findings indicated that tumors originating from cells with high PLG expression demonstrated markedly slower growth than those from the control group (Fig. 11A, B). Moreover, the OE group had a marked reduction in tumor weight relative to the OC group (Fig. 11C). Importantly, the average body weight of the mice remained stable, indicating that the PLG overexpression did not adversely affect overall health (Fig. 11D). Immunohistochemical analysis revealed that, compared to the OC group, the proportion of Ki-67- and PCNA-positive cells was significantly reduced in the OE group, suggesting a decline in cellular proliferation levels (Fig. 11E). Concurrently, immunohistochemical analysis also demonstrated that elevated PLG expression was accompanied by the upregulation of E-cadherin expression (Fig. 11E). Taken together, these results suggest that PLG overexpression is associated with reduced tumor growth and invasive capabilities in ccRCC in xenograft tumors, potentially acting through the regulation of cell proliferation markers and cell adhesion molecules. Discussion

Several methods exist for clearing dead cells, with efferocytosis being a primary mechanism. Efferocytosis is a dynamic process wherein phagocytes first detect and locate apoptotic cells. Following detection, ligands on the apoptotic cells bind to receptors on the phagocytes, which facilitates the identification, engulfment, and removal of the apoptotic cells. This ensures their digestion and helps maintain metabolic homeostasis23,24. Disruptions at any stage of cell clearance can lead to disease development. In particular, cancer is frequently linked to inflammation. When inflammatory factors are not fully removed, they can contribute to both the initiation and progression of cancer25. The TME is populated with numerous macrophages, found at different phases of tumor progression, and they are essential for immune system regulation. These immune cells assist in removing inflammatory agents and aid in resolving inflammation, thus facilitating the healing process of diseases26. The process of efferocytosis is vital in the development and advancement of tumors. Despite its importance, research specifically on efferocytosis in ccRCC is limited. Our research involved an innovative and comprehensive bioinformatics examination, along with validation, of crucial genes linked to efferocytosis in ccRCC. This study sheds light on the relationship between efferocytosis and ccRCC, elucidating the impact of efferocytosis on cancer growth and advancement.

Our study demonstrates that a risk model based on ERGs is associated with overall survival in ccRCC patients and shows significant correlations with the TME. PLAUR plays a key role in tissue remodeling and extracellular matrix degradation and is closely associated with tumor invasion and metastasis. Studies found that PLAUR is highly expressed in ccRCC, affecting not only the prognosis of ccRCC but also the infiltration of immune cells. The proteolytic cascade mediated by PLAUR is a key step in tumor cells breaching the basement membrane barrier and developing distant metastases27,28. HAVCR1, as an immune checkpoint molecule, binds to its ligand to transmit inhibitory signals, leading to CD8+ T cell failure and interrupted IFN-γ production, thereby directly mediating immune escape29. HAVCR1/TIM-3 has also been shown to play a role in regulating macrophage efferocytosis30. MIAT is a long noncoding RNA (lncRNA) that may promote ccRCC progression by regulating gene networks associated with cell proliferation, apoptosis resistance, and epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT)31. However, its relationship with efferocytosis remains unclear. ABCA1 and ABCG1 are key transporters in the reverse cholesterol transport (RCT) pathway. Tumor cells participate in immune escape by upregulating ABCA1/G1. Tumor cells excrete cholesterol through ABCA1/G1, which may cause “cholesterol starvation” in the microenvironment, thereby weakening T cell receptor (TCR) signal transduction and immune synapse formation, leading to T cell inactivation32. Notably, PLG (plasminogen) emerged as a key protein gene in this model. PLG is a prevalent plasma protein that interacts with various immune cells via its receptors. This interaction can activate inflammatory pathways, leading to cytokine production and further influencing innate immunity. Specifically, PLG can enhance phagocytosis by dendritic cells and phagocytes through complement activation, thereby playing a critical role in immune responses and tumor progression33–35. PLG can also bind to extracellular matrix (ECM) components and degrade ECM-related proteins. This deterioration can affect the immune system by enabling the movement of cells and pathogens from the bloodstream to various tissues. Such processes may exacerbate disease progression by altering tissue architecture and promoting further disease development36. In ccRCC, some studies have found that inhibitors targeting PLG can become prognostic and immune markers for ccRCC and participate in the process of cancer immunity and tumor prognosis37. PLG can bind to ECM components and degrade ECM-related proteins. This degradation can influence the immune response by promoting the migration of cells and pathogens from the bloodstream to other tissues. These changes in tissue architecture and immune cell distribution can exacerbate disease progression and contribute to the development and advancement of the disease.

Cell death is prevalent during the malignant progression of solid tumors and often increases with cytotoxic treatments. The clearance of dead cells significantly influences the maintenance of the immune phenotype within the TME. Efficient removal of dead cells can affect inflammation, immune cell activation, and overall immune response, thereby impacting tumor progression and treatment outcomes38. Efferocytosis can induce an immunosuppressive phenotype through various TME-related signaling pathways. This process helps prevent secondary necrosis and limits the release of pro-inflammatory damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) from dead cells that have not been cleared. By efficiently clearing apoptotic cells, efferocytosis prevents the accumulation of these inflammatory signals, thus limiting chronic inflammation and immune activation within the TME7. The process of efferocytosis can enhance the levels and functionality of receptors like MerTK, Axl, and Tyro3 in macrophages and dendritic cells associated with tumors, leading to their shift towards an immunosuppressive state. These antigen-presenting cells are essential for both innate and adaptive immunity. When their phenotypes are altered by efferocytosis, it results in reduced antigen cross-presentation and limited expansion of T cell clones. This shift impairs the development of effective antigen-dependent anti-tumor immunity, weakening the overall immune response against the tumor38–41. In our GSEA enrichment, we found that the T cell receptor (TCR) signaling pathway was relatively active in cluster B. The TCR signaling pathway is involved in both anti-tumor immune response and tumor immune escape in the progression of ccRCC42,43. On the one hand, normal activation of the TCR signaling pathway is essential for CD8+ T cells to recognize ccRCC tumor antigens and initiate tumor cell killing, while the release of IFN-γ can inhibit tumor growth and enhance anti-angiogenic effects44,45. On the other hand, chronic TCR stimulation in the TME can lead to T cell exhaustion, and upregulation of the PD-1/PD-L1 axis inhibits T cell function and promotes immune tolerance43. Some studies have found that plasminogen activator inhibitor-1 can arbitrarily promote the expression of PD-L146. In our study, we found that overexpression of PLG significantly inhibited the expression level of PD-L1, which is basically consistent with the results. In addition, HIF-α accumulation and enhanced glycolysis caused by VHL loss in ccRCC form a hypoxic, acidic microenvironment that inhibits TCR signaling, while tumor-derived lactic acid further weakens T cell activation and proliferation. Therapeutic strategies targeting the TCR signaling pathway, such as immune checkpoint inhibitors (anti-PD-1/PD-L1 or CTLA-4 therapy) and TCR-engineered T cells, have shown significant effects in the clinic, and combined therapies targeting VEGF or HIF-2α may further improve the treatment of ccRCC47–49. Therefore, the cumulative effect of efferocytosis helps limit tumor inflammation and anti-tumor immunity and can become a new immune checkpoint and a potential target for tumor treatment. Our immune checkpoint analysis found a significant association between our risk genes and most immune checkpoint genes, indicating that the progression of ccRCC may be regulated by immune checkpoints. Studies have found that contributions to disease treatment can indeed be discovered by exploring the expression and function of immune-related genes50–54. Therefore, we also investigated the effect of PLG overexpression on immunosuppression and found that it can inhibit the expression of PD-L1, indicating that ccRCC patients can be treated by targeting immune checkpoints.

Therapeutic options for ccRCC patients primarily consist of tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs), mTOR inhibitors, and PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors55. ccRCC is characterized by its rich blood supply, making it particularly responsive to tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs). TKIs primarily target vascular endothelial growth factors (VEGFs) and inhibit related signaling pathways, leading to effective treatment outcomes for ccRCC. Drugs such as sunitinib and pazopanib are particularly effective in managing ccRCC, with these first-line treatments significantly prolonging progression-free survival in patients56. Furthermore, the Akt/mTOR signaling route is identified as a potential treatment target for ccRCC. Focusing on this crucial pathway, essential for cell proliferation and survival, has shown notable effectiveness, especially in the later stages of RCC57. In our study, we identified 88 anticancer drugs whose sensitivity is closely linked to efferocytosis-related risk scores. These drugs show potential as treatments for ccRCC patients. However, the effects of some drugs in ccRCC have not yet been verified. Therefore, further studies are needed to confirm whether these are effective and applicable for the treatment of cRCC patients, which is also the focus of our continued research.

While this study provides insights into efferocytosis-related subgroups and genes associated with survival in ccRCC, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the analyses were primarily based on public databases, with a limited number of samples and RNA sequencing datasets, and lacked validation in independent clinical cohorts. This may affect the generalizability of the findings. Second, some patients had incomplete clinical information, and potential batch effects and confounding clinical variables may have introduced bias into the results. Therefore, further validation in prospective and independent cohorts is warranted. Third, functional validation in this study was mainly focused on PLG, whereas other candidate genes identified in the model were not experimentally investigated. In addition, the small number of samples used for IHC analysis represents a limitation of this study and may introduce potential bias. Further validation in larger cohorts is required. Future studies are needed to explore the biological roles of these genes in greater detail. Finally, although the nomogram demonstrated an association with survival outcomes in the training dataset, overfitting cannot be completely excluded, and its clinical applicability remains to be further validated. In addition, the experimental validation was limited to basic expression and functional assays, and more comprehensive mechanistic studies are required to substantiate the current findings.

Conclusion

In summary, this study identified molecular subgroups associated with efferocytosis and constructed a risk-scoring model based on six ERGs to assess survival outcomes in patients with ccRCC. This model demonstrated a significant association with patients’ overall survival outcomes and exhibited distinct discriminative power across different risk strata. As one of the key genes, PLG may contribute to the pathogenesis, progression, and immune regulation of ccRCC by modulating PD-L1 expression. By examining the perspective of efferocytosis, this study preliminarily elucidated the potential biological significance and immune-related implications of this process in ccRCC, thereby providing a reference basis for subsequent mechanistic investigations and clinical validation.
