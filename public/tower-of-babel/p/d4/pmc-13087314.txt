Demirjian’s and Cameriere’s methods for estimating the 18-year legal age threshold using third molar maturity in a Northern Thai population
Authors: Nattakan Chaipattanawan, Chanakan Chamart, Chayanin Lertromyanant, Nanthatchaphon Guardgasam, Natnapan Intharasing, Siriprapa Pithakam, Boonchanit Nophachatsathid, Wannakamon Panyarak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study evaluated and compared the accuracy of Demirjian’s and Cameriere’s methods for estimating the 18-year legal age threshold using mandibular third molar maturity in a Northern Thai population. It also aimed to establish population-specific cut-offs. A total of 260 orthopantomograms from healthy Northern Thai individuals aged 14–23 years were retrospectively collected. Bilateral mandibular third molars (teeth 38 and 48) were assessed using Demirjian’s stages and Cameriere’s third molar maturity index (I3M). Diagnostic indices and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves with area under the curve (AUC) values were used to evaluate performance and discriminatory ability at the 18-year threshold. Among Demirjian’s stages, only stage H demonstrated consistently high specificity (0.89–0.98) across sexes and tooth sides. Cameriere’s method also showed strong performance, with specificity for I3M < 0.08 ranging from 0.83 to 0.98 and increasing markedly when a stricter cut-off of I3M < 0.02 was applied, reaching up to 1.00 in males and 0.98 in females. ROC analysis indicated excellent to outstanding discriminatory accuracy in both sexes and on both sides of the mandibular third molars. These findings showed that Demirjian’s stage H and Cameriere’s I3M < 0.02 were useful methods for estimating the 18-year legal age threshold. Introduction

Age estimation is a fundamental component of forensic science, as it is used to assist in the identification of deceased individuals and to support the evaluation of living persons in criminal, civil and medico-legal contexts1,2. In Thailand, evaluating the likelihood that an individual has reached 18 years of age is particularly important, as this age threshold defines the legal distinction between juveniles and adults. Such assessments provide supporting evidence in judicial proceedings, particularly in cases involving detainees, victims of human trafficking or individuals claiming juvenile status for legal protection3.

Although the actual chronological age serves as the primary legal reference, it may prove unreliable when identification documents are missing or inaccurate4. In such situations, age estimation based on radiographic evaluation of third molar development provides a non-invasive, reliable and reproducible alternative for assessing biological maturity5–7. The third molars are particularly valuable for estimating age in adolescents and young adults because they are the last teeth to develop and follow a predictable pattern of crown and root formation. Therefore, they may be used to estimate the likelihood that an individual has reached the legal adulthood threshold (≥ 18 years) and to provide probabilistic support for forensic age assessment8; however, such methods cannot replace official documentation or legal determinations.

Demirjian’s tooth development staging system is one of the most frequently employed dental age estimation techniques because of its simplicity, reproducibility and ease of standardization9. This method classifies third molar development into eight stages (A–H) based on the degree of mineralization, ranging from initial crown formation to complete root development and apical closure10. Reaching stage H is considered a key indicator for estimating the likelihood that an individual has turned 18 years of age, as demonstrated in studies conducted in American, European and African populations11–14. However, several studies have reported that earlier developmental stages, such as F or G, demonstrate stronger overall discriminatory ability for probabilistic age estimation around the 18-year age threshold, particularly among East Asian populations, in which third-molar maturation tends to be more variable or delayed15–19.

Cameriere’s method is an alternative approach that provides a quantitative assessment of third molar maturity by measuring the degree of apical closure. This method calculates the third molar maturity index (I3M), defined as the ratio between the sum of the widths of the open apices and the tooth length, yielding a continuous variable that allows for more precise threshold determination20. Several studies have suggested that an I3M cut-off value of 0.08 offers meaningful discriminatory performance for probabilistic age estimation around the 18-year legal threshold across European and East Asian populations21–25. However, this cut-off value may be unsuitable as a universal threshold for confirming legal adulthood because dental maturation varies among ethnic populations and between sexes. Therefore, establishing population-specific cut-off points is essential to enhancing the reliability of this method26–29.

Despite their widespread application, data validating Demirjian’s and Cameriere’s methods at the 18-year threshold among Southeast Asian populations remain limited. Therefore, the present study compared the diagnostic accuracy of Demirjian’s and Cameriere’s methods in estimating the likelihood that individuals have reached 18 years of age, with the aim of identifying population-specific thresholds for a Northern Thai population, enhancing the precision and forensic applicability of dental age estimation.

Methods

Study design and ethical approval

This research was designed as a hospital-based, retrospective, observational, cross-sectional study to compare the diagnostic accuracy of Demirjian’s and Cameriere’s methods in estimating the legal adult age threshold of 18 years, based on the maturity of the mandibular third molars observed in orthopantomograms (OPGs) from the Northern Thai population.

The study protocol was approved by the Human Experimentation Committee of the Faculty of Dentistry, Chiang Mai University, Thailand (registration number: 18/2025). All procedures involving human participants adhered to institutional and national ethical standards and complied with the principles of the 1964 Helsinki Declaration and its later amendments. Because the study used anonymized radiographic and clinical records retrieved retrospectively, the ethics committee granted a waiver of informed consent. The study was reported following the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines30 and the Standards for Reporting Diagnostic Accuracy Studies (STARD) recommendations31.

Study participants and eligibility criteria

OPGs of healthy Northern Thai patients aged between 14 and 23 years were retrospectively collected from the Oral and Maxillofacial Radiology Clinic at the Faculty of Dentistry, Chiang Mai University, between January 2020 and June 2025. OPGs were included if they displayed distinguishable and measurable bilateral mandibular third molars. However, OPGs showing third molar malposition (e.g., severe buccoversion or linguoversion), third-molar dental anomalies (e.g., short root, root dilaceration or root malformation) or any visible dental or bone pathology that could influence the normal development of third molars were excluded.

Sample size and sampling techniques

The required sample size was determined based on the study by Akman et al.32, who reported a specificity of 0.82 for Cameriere’s 0.08 cut-off value in predicting the attainment of 18 years of age, assuming a prevalence of 0.47, a desired absolute precision of 0.07 and a 95% confidence level. Based on these parameters, the minimum required sample size was 218 OPGs. A total of 260 OPGs were included in the study to compensate for potential exclusions of approximately 15%.

Stratified random sampling was applied according to sex (male and female) and chronological age (14–23 years). OPGs were randomly selected within each stratum at a 1:1 proportion relative to the population distribution to ensure representative coverage of the target population.

Data collection and radiographic interpretation

All OPGs were acquired using the manufacturers’ default settings. Two machines were utilized, including the Carestream 9600 (Carestream Dental, Atlanta, GA, USA), with exposure parameters ranging from 60 to 90 kVp and 2–15 mA over an exposure time of 8.1–12.3 s, and the NewTom GiANO 2D (NewTom, Imola, Italy), operating at 60–85 kVp and 2–26 mA with an exposure time of 11.2–12.3 s. All included OPGs were exported from the system as JPEG images.

For each radiograph, the patient’s hospital number, sex, date of birth, and date of radiographic acquisition were recorded. Chronological age was calculated in decimal years by subtracting the date of birth from the date of radiographic acquisition. To ensure anonymity, all OPGs were anonymized by replacing the original identification codes with sequential numbers from 1 to 260.

Dental age estimation was based on assessment of the developmental stages of both the left and right mandibular third molars for each study participant, using Demirjian’s and Cameriere’s methods. In Demirjian’s method, each molar was classified into one of eight mineralization stages (A–H), as shown in Fig. 1. In Cameriere’s method, the I3M was calculated as the ratio of the sum of the mesial (Am) and distal (Ad) open apices to the tooth length (L). In molars without root furcation, the distance between the internal sides of the open apices (A) was measured instead (Fig. 2). All measurements were performed using the ImageJ software (U.S. National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA).

All included OPGs were independently assessed by five undergraduate dental students (N.I., S.P., C.L., N.G., and N.N.). The assessors were blinded from the chronological age and sex of each subject.

Prior to data collection, the undergraduate assessors underwent structured and supervised training conducted by an expert oral and maxillofacial radiologist (W.P.). This training included comprehensive theoretical instruction on the principles and scoring criteria of Demirjian’s method, as well as detailed step-by-step guidance on the application of Cameriere’s method. Practical sessions were performed using reference OPGs to ensure correct stage allocation and accurate measurement procedures. In addition, students received supervised hands-on training in the use of ImageJ software for standardized measurement of open apices.

Due to the distinct methodological characteristics of Demirjian’s categorical approach and Cameriere’s quantitative measurement-based method, assessors were divided into two separate teams before calibration to minimize potential cross-method procedural bias. Specifically, two assessors (N.I. and S.P.) were assigned to Demirjian’s method, whereas three assessors (C.L., N.G., and N.N.) were assigned to Cameriere’s method.

Following allocation to method-specific teams, a calibration process was implemented. Each assessor independently evaluated 30 randomly selected OPGs on two separate occasions, with a two-week interval between assessments to minimize recall bias. Intra- and inter-rater reliability were assessed within each team to determine measurement consistency. Full dataset evaluation commenced only after satisfactory levels of agreement had been achieved.

Statistical analysis

Intra- and inter-rater reliability were evaluated using Cohen’s kappa coefficient for categorical outcomes and the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) for numerical outcomes. The levels of agreement for kappa and ICC were interpreted according to the guidelines of Landis and Koch34 and Koo and Li35, respectively.

The mean chronological age (in years) was calculated for each of Demirjian’s developmental stages and Cameriere’s index group corresponding to the left and right mandibular third molars. The normality of age distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test before comparing mean age differences between males and females. The independent t-test was applied to normally distributed data, whereas the Mann–Whitney U test was used for non-normal distributions. In addition, Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient was calculated to assess the strength and direction of the association between chronological age and dental maturity.

Diagnostic indices, including sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), accuracy, positive likelihood ratios (LR+), negative likelihood ratios (LR−), diagnostic odds ratios (DOR) and Bayes’ post-test probability, were calculated for each cut-off point of Demirjian’s stages (Stages F, G and H) and Cameriere’s index groups (I3M < 0.08, < 0.04 and < 0.02) to compare the performance of the methods in estimating legal age at the 18-year threshold. Moreover, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis and the area under the curve (AUC) were used to assess the overall ability of both methods to discriminate adults from juveniles. All AUC values were interpreted following the guidelines proposed by Hosmer and Lemeshow36. The Supplementary Table provides the definitions and forensic interpretations of these indices.

All statistical analyses were performed using Stata 16.0 (College Station, TX, USA). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. All estimates were reported with 95% confidence intervals (CI).

Results

In Demirjian’s method, intra-rater reliability showed substantial agreement, with kappa values of 0.79 (N.I.) and 0.75 (S.P.). Furthermore, inter-rater reliability demonstrated almost perfect agreement, with a kappa value of 0.83. On the other hand, in Cameriere’s method, intra-rater reliability indicated excellent agreement, with ICC values of 0.99 (C.L.), 0.98 (N.G.) and 0.99 (N.N.). Additionally, inter-rater reliability also showed excellent agreement, with an ICC value of 0.99.

The study flow diagram (Fig. 3) shows that 1,219 OPGs met the initial inclusion criteria. Of these, 397 were excluded based on the specified criteria, leaving 822 eligible OPGs. From these, 260 OPGs were selected using 1:1 stratified random sampling by sex (male and female) and chronological age (14–23 years) for data analysis.

ROC analyses demonstrated excellent to outstanding overall discriminative performance for both Demirjian’s and Cameriere’s methods at the 18-year age threshold (Fig. 5). Among males, Cameriere’s method yielded the highest accuracy for both left and right mandibular third molars (AUC for the left side = 0.91, 95% CI 0.87–0.96; AUC for the right side: 0.91, 95% CI 0.86–0.96), outperforming Demirjian’s method (AUC for the left side = 0.88, 95% CI 0.82–0.94; AUC for the right side = 0.89, 95% CI 0.83–0.95). Discussion

Dental age estimation based on third molar maturity is crucial in forensic and legal contexts, where distinguishing between adults and juveniles at the 18-year threshold impacts rights, protections and criminal liability37. Although Demirjian’s method is widely used in Thailand, its reliance on subjective staging may result in variability and potential misclassification38–40. On the other hand, Cameriere’s method offers a more objective, measurement-based assessment but requires greater technical expertise for accurate application20. These methodological differences highlight the need to evaluate which technique provides more reliable age assessments around the legal age threshold. Hence, this retrospective cross-sectional study aimed to propose population-specific cut-off values for a Northern Thai population, improving the diagnostic accuracy and forensic applicability of both methods, using the left and right mandibular third molars.

In forensic contexts, errors in age estimation can lead to serious consequences. Misclassifying a juvenile as an adult (false positive) may result in the loss of essential legal protections. Conversely, misclassifying an adult as a juvenile (false negative) may compromise judicial processes or impede appropriate legal actions. Therefore, international forensic guidelines recommend prioritizing high-specificity cut-off values when the objective is to estimate the likelihood that an individual has reached adulthood, in order to minimize the risk of incorrectly classifying juveniles as adults. However, more sensitive cut-offs may be acceptable for screening purposes, where the primary goal is to identify individuals who may require further assessment. Therefore, when establishing population-specific thresholds for forensic age assessment, specificity should be prioritized, particularly in analyses designed to minimize false-positive classifications of adulthood, in order to safeguard juvenile protections and maintain the integrity of the justice system41.

Regarding Demirjian’s method, although Duangto et al.38 reported a 100% probability of stage H demonstrating an age of ≥ 18 years in a Thai population, these probabilities are descriptive and sample-dependent and cannot be used as minimum-age cut-offs for forensic purposes. Thus, stage H is primarily used as an indicator providing strong probabilistic support for adulthood, consistent with our findings, in which stage H demonstrated high specificity, PPV, LR + and Bayes’ post-test probability in both sexes and both mandibular third molars. These results align with Upalananda et al.42, who also identified stage H as the only Demirjian stage with high specificity for estimating the likelihood that individuals have reached 18 years of age in a Southern Thai population. By contrast, earlier Demirjian’s stages (F or G) increased sensitivity but reduced specificity, PPV, LR + and Bayes’ probability, indicating weaker confirmatory value and a higher risk of misclassifying juveniles as adults.

Although I3M < 0.08 has been proposed as a reliable threshold for providing probabilistic support for adulthood at the 18-year threshold using Cameriere’s method20,43, its performance in this Northern Thai sample varied by sex. In Thai males, I3M < 0.08 demonstrated balanced diagnostic performance with moderate-to-high sensitivity and specificity. By contrast, in Thai females, this cut-off showed high specificity but similarly low sensitivity for both left and right mandibular third molars. When the threshold was decreased to I3M < 0.02, it provided strong probabilistic support for adulthood, characterized by very high specificity, in both mandibular third molars; however, the low sensitivity of this threshold in both sexes indicates that many adults do not reach this level of root maturation. These trends are consistent with findings from Southern Thailand, reported by Upalananda et al.42, who also observed high specificity but low sensitivity for I3M < 0.08, and strong probabilistic support for adulthood, characterized by very high specificity but minimal sensitivity, for I3M < 0.02.

Although Demirjian’s stage H and an I3M cut-off value of < 0.02 demonstrated high specificity for identifying individuals aged ≥ 18 years, these thresholds should be interpreted with caution in forensic practice. Their very low sensitivity indicates that a substantial proportion of adults may not reach these criteria, thereby limiting their utility as stand-alone indicators of legal adulthood. Accordingly, while Demirjian’s stage H and I3M < 0.02 may provide supportive evidence for suggesting adulthood at the group level, they do not permit definitive age classification in individual cases. For enhanced reliability and forensic robustness, these dental markers should be interpreted in conjunction with other biological age indicators, such as hand–wrist radiographs or medial clavicular epiphyseal ossification, within a multidisciplinary and explicitly probabilistic framework for forensic age estimation.

This is the first study to report the ROC curves and AUC values of Demirjian’s and Cameriere’s methods for estimating the 18-year age threshold in a Thai population. Both methods demonstrated excellent-to-outstanding overall discriminatory performance, with Cameriere’s method showing superior accuracy in males and Demirjian’s method performing slightly better in females. These findings agree with previous studies from other populations. For example, Akman et al.32 reported slightly lower but still excellent AUC values in a Turkish population, with I3M yielding an AUC of 0.85 and Demirjian’s method showing AUCs ranging from 0.80 to 0.83. Costa et al.44 also found that Demirjian’s method applied to all third molars discriminated adults from juveniles in Colombian and Mexican populations, with AUC values ranging from 0.94 to 0.95. Similarly, Khare et al.28 reported high AUC values for the I3M index in an Eastern Chinese population, with AUCs of 0.93 in females and 0.94 in males, demonstrating comparably strong performance in distinguishing adults from juveniles. Although both methods demonstrated strong overall discriminatory performance, their applicability at the critical legal age threshold must be interpreted with caution. High AUC values reflect accuracy at the group level; however, individual misclassifications remain possible. In forensic contexts, high specificity is particularly important to minimize the risk of incorrectly classifying juveniles as adults.

In our study, sex dimorphism was evident in both Demirjian’s and Cameriere’s methods. Females reached advanced Demirjian’s stages and lower I3M values at slightly older ages, indicating earlier third molar development in males. This pattern is consistent with findings obtained using Demirjian’s method in Southern Thailand42, Korea45, Turkey46, Brazil14 and Iran47. By contrast, studies from Basra48, Pakistan49, India50 and Northern China51 found that females developed earlier. Other populations, such as Southern Italy12 and Peru52, reported no significant sex differences. For Cameriere’s method, our results also suggested earlier apical closure in males, consistent with data from Southern Thai42, Italian20, Croatian23, Brazilian53, South Indian54 and multi-ethnic samples comprising European, African, Asian and American populations55. However, in contrast to studies from Southern Italy12, Botswana56, Peru52 and Northern China51, they reported a slightly earlier tendency for females. These findings underscore the importance of considering sex-specific differences when interpreting dental maturity in forensic age estimation.

The diagnostic performance was generally comparable between the left and right mandibular third molars in both sexes, although minor variations in sensitivity and specificity were observed. Based on the high bilateral correlation coefficients and the nearly identical AUC values, the left and right mandibular third molars can be used interchangeably for age estimation in this population. This finding aligns with the results reported by Angelakopoulos et al.55, who observed no significant bilateral differences in third molar development among Asian populations, in contrast with European, American and African groups. However, asymmetrical development between contralateral third molars has been reported in the Australian population57. Such asymmetry may arise from factors including random local genetic variations or environmental influences. Therefore, forensic age estimation based on one side alone may be less reliable in some individuals, and incorporating information from both sides can improve overall accuracy. Moreover, the third molars as a sole criterion cannot be considered sufficiently reliable for estimating whether an individual is an adult or a juvenile. Schmeling et al.4 suggested the use of combined investigative methods involving a physical examination, an assessment of sexual maturation and a radiographic examination of the hands and medial clavicular epiphysis to improve overall accuracy and minimize the risk of misclassification.

However, this study has some limitations. First, the retrospective cross-sectional design prevents the evaluation of individual growth patterns and long-term secular trends, which may affect the stability of population-specific cut-off values. A prospective cohort study would provide a clearer understanding of third molar developmental trajectories and help refine the cut-off points. Second, all radiographic assessments were conducted by trained undergraduate dental students. Although comprehensive training, supervised calibration, and high intra- and inter-observer agreement support measurement consistency, observer expertise remains a critical factor in forensic age estimation. The absence of experienced forensic examiners may limit external validity and practical applicability in medico-legal settings. Consequently, while the study demonstrates methodological reliability under controlled academic conditions, implementation in real-world forensic contexts should involve evaluation or confirmation by qualified forensic experts. Third, the use of radiographs from a single institution in Northern Thailand may limit generalizability to other Thai regions or broader Southeast Asian populations, underscoring the need for multi-center studies across the region. Lastly, this study focused solely on mandibular third molar development without incorporating other dental or skeletal maturity indicators, such as clinical emergence and root pulp visibility of third molars, hand–wrist radiographs, cervical vertebral maturation or medial clavicular epiphyseal stages, which could enhance age estimation accuracy.

Accordingly, future research should prioritize longitudinal or prospective cohort designs, involve multi-center data collection across different regions of Thailand, and include experienced clinicians or forensic odontologists as observers to confirm the reproducibility and applicability of these findings under real-world forensic conditions. In addition, integrating multiple biological maturity markers and exploring advanced analytical approaches, including artificial intelligence–based models58, may further improve the accuracy and forensic relevance of age estimation at the legal adulthood threshold.

Conclusions

In this Northern Thai population, Demirjian’s stage H and Cameriere’s I3M < 0.02 demonstrated high specificity for suggesting adulthood, supporting their use as part of a probabilistic forensic age assessment framework. However, due to inherent variability and low sensitivity, these markers should be used in conjunction with additional age indicators in forensic practice.
