Endoscopic Ultrasonography‐Guided Gastroenterostomy Is More Cost Effective Than Surgical Gastrojejunostomy: A Health‐Economic Evaluation Alongside a Randomized Trial
Authors: Yorick L van de Pavert, Kirsten Opmeer, Marc G Besselink, Rina Bijlsma, Dieke C Booij, Jurjen J Boonstra, Judith Boot, Marco J Bruno, Olivier R Busch, Freek Daams, Wouter J M Derksen, Paul Fockens, Janine B Kastelijn, Bas Groot Koerkamp, Jeroen Hagendoorn, Jeanin E van Hooft, Akin Inderson, Willem J Lammers, Daan J Lips, J Sven D Mieog, I Quintus Molenaar, Alexander A F A Veenhof, Niels G Venneman, Robert C Verdonk, Rogier P Voermans, Roy L J van Wanrooij, Paco M J Welsing, Thomas R de Wijkerslooth, Hjalmar C van Santvoort, Leon M G Moons, Miriam P van der Meulen, Frank P Vleggaar, for the Dutch Pancreatic Cancer Group
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundThere is a paucity of data on the cost‐utility of endoscopic ultrasonography‐guided gastroenterostomy (EUS‐GE) compared with surgical gastrojejunostomy (SGJ) as palliative treatment of malignant gastric outlet obstruction.MethodsIn the ENDURO trial, 98 patients with malignant gastric outlet obstruction from 12 Dutch hospitals were randomized between EUS‐GE (N = 48) and SGJ (N = 50). Cost utility was evaluated from a healthcare perspective with a time horizon of 1 month. A bottom‐up approach was used to calculate the costs of each intervention, including the per‐minute price of the endoscopy and operating room (OR), considering personnel, construction, inventory and overhead costs. Other in‐hospital costs were calculated using insurance claim data. Effectiveness was displayed as quality‐adjusted life years (QALY). Bootstrapping was performed to estimate uncertainty.ResultsThe calculated per‐minute price for the endoscopy room was €8.64 (US $9.34). The per‐minute price for an OR was €11.78 (US $12.74). The mean intervention costs were estimated at €4405.40 (US $4762.68) for EUS‐GE and €3393.71 (US $3668.93) for SGJ. Mean total in‐hospital costs were lower following EUS‐GE (€5172 [US $5591]) than after SGJ (€10,458 [US $11,306]), mainly due to a shorter length of hospital stay following EUS‐GE. Bootstrapping showed that 99.9% of iterations resulted in lower costs for EUS‐GE. Mean difference in 1‐month QALY contribution was 0.004 in favor of EUS‐GE. Most iterations fall within the bottom right quadrant (95.6%) of the cost‐effectiveness plane, indicating lower costs and improved QALY contribution for EUS‐GE.DiscussionIn this study, EUS‐GE was more cost effective than SGJ. The additional intervention costs were clearly outweighed by a decrease in in‐hospital costs after the procedure.Keywords: cost‐utility analysis, endoscopic ultrasonography‐guided gastroenterostomy, malignant gastric outlet obstruction, surgical gastrojejunostomy

Introduction

Malignant gastric outlet obstruction is a frequently occurring manifestation of advanced cancers in the foregut and complicates late stages of pancreatic and ampullary cancer in up to 25% of patients [1, 2]. Symptoms of nausea and vomiting and an inability to tolerate oral intake have a considerable negative impact on patients' quality of life, warranting adequate therapy [3].

Traditionally, enteral stent placement (ES) is recommended in patients with a poor performance status and short life expectancy. Despite its rapid treatment success, ES is associated with high reintervention rates of up to 30% [4, 5]. For patients with an adequate performance status and longer life expectancy laparoscopic surgical gastrojejunostomy (SGJ) is usually considered. SGJ has a low reintervention rate but high postoperative morbidity [6, 7]. Endoscopic ultrasonography‐guided gastroenterostomy (EUS‐GE) with a lumen‐apposing metal stent (LAMS) has emerged as a promising minimally invasive alternative to these treatment modalities [6]. Recently, the ENDURO trial, in which patients were randomized between EUS‐GE and SGJ, demonstrated that EUS‐GE reduced the time to tolerate solid oral intake while maintaining a non‐inferior rate of persistent or recurrent obstructive symptoms requiring reintervention [8]. Moreover, the length of hospital stay was shorter after EUS‐GE. Based on these favorable results, it was hypothesized that EUS‐GE would reduce post‐procedural costs. However, the costs of the LAMS are substantial, which might offset this potential advantage.

There is a paucity of data regarding the potential cost and effect differences between EUS‐GE and SGJ.

The present study reports the results of a cost‐utility analysis that was performed alongside the ENDURO randomized clinical trial.

Materials and Methods

ENDURO

This trial‐based economic evaluation compared the costs and quality‐adjusted life‐years (QALYs), that is cost‐utility, of SGJ and EUS‐GE for the treatment of malignant gastric outlet obstruction and was conducted alongside the ENDURO trial. ENDURO was a multicenter, randomized controlled trial comparing SGJ and EUS‐GE in 12 Dutch hospitals. The trial protocol was approved by the institutional review boards of the University Medical Center Utrecht and participating centers and registered at the International Clinical Trials Registry Platform (NL9592). The trial protocol was published previously [9]. All authors had access to the study data and reviewed and approved the final manuscript.

Between February 2022 and February 2024, 98 patients with malignant gastric outlet obstruction were randomized (48 to EUS‐GE and 50 to SGJ [Supporting Information S1: Appendix D, Figure D1, Table D1]) [8]. EUS‐GE was performed using a Hot AXIOS Stent & electrocautery‐enhanced delivery system (Boston Scientific, Marlborough, MA, USA). During laparoscopic SGJ, an antecolic side‐to‐side anastomosis was created. Patients in the EUS‐GE group resumed oral intake after a median of 1 day versus 3 days in the SGJ group (hazard ratio 2.21 [p = 0.0003]). The rate of persistent or recurrent obstructive symptoms requiring reintervention was non‐inferior, 10% and 12%, respectively. Median length of hospital stay was shorter in the EUS‐GE group: one day versus 4 days.

Unit Costs of Intervention

As standardized unit costs for both EUS‐GE and SGJ were unavailable in the literature, a bottom‐up approach was used to calculate unit costs of both interventions. In this context, the bottom‐up approach entails identifying and calculating the costs of all cost components. For each cost component, the individual resource used during the procedure was measured and then summed to determine the per‐minute price. Total costs of the procedures were then calculated by multiplying the per‐minute price with the individual duration of the two interventions and summing all other cost components. This included costs related to the operating room (OR) and endoscopy room, endoscopic and laparoscopic equipment, periprocedural medication, and disposable materials. OR and endoscopy room costs were calculated by multiplying the time a patient spent in the specific room by the per‐minute price of the OR or endoscopy room.

Costs of endoscopic equipment and disposables were based on hospital cost overviews supplied by the endoscopy department. Costs of laparoscopic equipment were based on a previously published cost‐utility analysis of laparoscopic and open gastrectomy for gastric cancer [10]. Additional costs for surgical instruments and disposables were based on hospital invoices obtained from the department of surgery.

Costs of medication were based on the average volume of administered medication of 6 EUS‐GE patients and 13 SGJ patients and were multiplied by the average unit price [11]. For these patients, the exact administered volumes in the two largest participating hospitals were known.

Per‐Minute Price Operating and Endoscopy Room

The per‐minute price of an OR was mostly based on a study by Patel et al., in which the costs per minute of a conventional OR were compared with a hybrid OR [12]. The per‐minute price of endoscopy room use was determined bottom‐up with information obtained from two participating hospitals (Supporting Information S1: Appendix A, Table A1). The Dutch guideline for costing analyses and the bottom‐up method of Patel et al. were followed for this analysis [12, 13]. A bottom‐up approach with costs based on hospital‐specific data was chosen as it provides more insight into the cost drivers of the endoscopy room price compared to a top‐down approach [12, 14].

Costs were divided into personnel, construction, inventory, and overhead costs. Personnel costs were calculated based on the number of staff present per procedure, working hours per year, the Dutch costing guidelines, and the hourly wage derived from collective labor agreements of both academic and general hospitals (Table 1) [13, 15, 16]. Total personnel costs per procedure were calculated by taking the sum of costs of all personnel present (Table 2).

Construction costs were calculated based on the Dutch price of constructing a hospital square meter (m2), as presented by the Dutch advisory board on healthcare housing (AcvZ) [17]. Different functionalities within the department were considered by differentiating m2 price according to the specific functionality with differentiation rates from the AcvZ, supplemented with expert elicitation (Table A2) [18]. Based on the floor plans of the endoscopy department in the two hospitals, the total m2 per functionality was determined and the m2 price of the endoscopy room was calculated (Supporting Information S1: Appendix A, Formula 1 and 2), including interest, amortization and maintenance costs (Table 2).

The actual acquisition costs of the inventory and equipment in the endoscopy room were determined based on invoices of the participating hospitals that were considered in this analysis, including tax and discounts. Yearly costs were calculated with a depreciation period of 10 years for general inventory and 8 years for imaging equipment, maintenance costs and interest rate (Table 1) [12]. Inventory costs of the conventional OR were taken from Patel et al. [12].

Overhead costs (e.g., water, electricity, cleaning service) were calculated over construction and personnel. All unit costs prior to 2024 were converted to 2024 EUR values based on the Dutch Consumer Price Index [19]. The costs in this calculation, except personnel costs, are yearly costs. To calculate the per‐minute costs, the yearly costs were combined with the utilization rate. The available hours and occupied hours of the OR and endoscopy room were identified for 2024 or were based on the analysis from Patel et al. [12] The yearly costs were divided by the occupied hours and converted to costs per minute.

In‐Hospital Healthcare Resource Use and Costs

Only in‐hospital costs with a time horizon of 1 month were considered. Therefore, discounting was redundant in this analysis. In‐hospital resource use was obtained from claim data in hospital registries of all participating and referring hospitals. Per‐patient costs were calculated by multiplying resource use by unit costs of each procedure. Unit costs were based on prices from the Dutch Healthcare Authority (NZa), NZa‐tariffs, and internal price lists [13, 20]. Claim data were missing for one patient and were imputed. All prices were converted from EUR to USD using the average 2024 exchange rate, as reported by the European Central Bank (€1 EUR = US $1.0811).

Quality‐Adjusted Life Years

QALYs were calculated using the EQ‐5D‐3L, with the Dutch EQ‐5D‐3L tariff applied [21]. A QALY of zero indicates death and one indicates perfect health [22]. Patients completed surveys at baseline, and 2, 4, 12, and 24 weeks after the procedure. As QALY impact was expected to be most pronounced in the first weeks following treatment, the analysis adopted a 1‐month time horizon to include QALY up to 4 weeks. This decision was supported by a high rate of missing values at 12 and 24 weeks, primarily attributable to patient mortality (Supporting Information S1: Table C2, Table C3). QALY contribution per patient was calculated using the area under the curve with linear interpolation between time points. Patients who died were assigned a score of zero from the date of death onward. QALY contributions were corrected for baseline scores. The EQ‐5D‐3L values were available for alive patients in 73% of the EUS‐GE group and 50% of the SGJ group at 1 month (Supporting Information S1: Table C2).

Missing EQ‐5D‐3L values and missing in‐hospital costs were imputed using multiple imputation by chained equations with the 'mice' package in R (version 4.4.0; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), using baseline characteristics, treatment outcomes, hospital costs, and available questionnaires at other time points. Twenty imputed data sets were generated [23].

Cost‐Utility Analysis

Cost‐utility of EUS‐GE compared with SGJ up to 1 month following treatment was analyzed with standard trial‐based health economic statistics and followed a healthcare perspective [24]. To estimate the uncertainty, bootstrapping was used with 2000 iterations for each of the 20 imputed datasets [23]. Analyses were performed according to a modified intention‐to‐treat principle, excluding one patient in the EUS‐GE group and six patients in the surgical group who did not receive the study intervention. The base case analysis included costs and QALY contribution up to 1 month after intervention. In addition, a scenario analysis assessed the outcomes at 6 months. The outcomes of the bootstrap iteration are displayed in cost‐effectiveness planes, representing the uncertainty in the costs and QALY differences between the treatment groups.

Results

Per‐Minute Price Endoscopy and Operating Room

The per‐minute price for the endoscopy room was estimated to be €8.64 (US $9.34). Personnel costs accounted for 54% and inventory for 27% of the costs. For the OR, we found a minute‐price of €12.27 (US $13.27), for which personnel costs accounted for 67% (Table A3).

Per‐minute costs of personnel in the endoscopy room were €4.67 (US $5.05) in an academic institution and €4.70 (US $5.08) in a general hospital. Per‐minute construction costs were estimated at €0.35 (US $0.38) and annual inventory costs at €2.32 (US $2.51). Total overhead costs were €1.27 (US $1.37) per minute.

Based on Patel et al., personnel costs per minute of the OR were €7.81 (US $8.44) in an academic hospital and €8.01 (US $8.66) in a general hospital, construction costs were €0.81 (US $0.88), and annual inventory costs were €1.27 (US $1.37) per minute [12]. Overhead costs per minute were €2.28 (US $2.46).

The mean utilization rate for the endoscopy room was 81%, based on hospital data, and 92% for the OR [12].

Cost‐Utility Analysis

For EUS‐GE (n = 47), mean total costs and adjusted QALY contributions during the first month were €9507 (US $10,278) and 0.0536 compared with €13,812 (US $14,932) and 0.0493 in the SGJ group (n = 45), respectively. This yielded an incremental difference of €4305 (US $4654) in mean costs and 0.004 in mean QALY contribution in favor of EUS‐GE. In the scenario analysis with a 6‐month time horizon, we found an incremental difference of €3254 (US $3518) in mean costs and 0.0244 in mean QALY contribution for 6 months in favor of EUS‐GE (Figure 2B).

Discussion

This cost‐utility analysis alongside the ENDURO trial showed that the procedural costs were higher for EUS‐GE compared to SGJ. The mean postprocedural in‐hospital costs within month following EUS‐GE were considerably lower than those following SGJ. The majority of iterations fall within the bottom right quadrant (95.6%) of the cost‐effectiveness plane, indicating lower costs at improved QALY contribution for EUS‐GE.

Although the mean procedural time of EUS‐GE was shorter and the minute price was lower, total procedural costs of EUS‐GE were higher than those of SGJ. This can be explained mainly by the costs of the LAMS. However, the higher procedural costs of EUS‐GE were clearly outweighed by savings in the post‐procedural costs. These lower postprocedural in‐hospital costs are primarily caused by the shorter length of hospital stay. Furthermore, EQ‐5D‐3L imputed utility scores were stable in the EUS‐GE group between baseline and 4 weeks after treatment, with a higher score at 4 weeks compared with SGJ. The mean QALY contribution at one month was higher in EUS‐GE patients than in SGJ patients, although this difference was modest. EUS‐GE presumably is less burdensome than SGJ, given the rapid resumption of oral intake and shorter hospital stay [8]. A study by Garcia‐Alonso also observed an increase in quality of life after EUS‐GE, primarily on the domains of nausea, pain, appetite loss, constipation, and global health [25]. Approximately 75% of patients in the ENDURO trial died during the follow‐up period. Therefore, quality of life might be more affected by disease progression than by the effect of EUS‐GE or SGJ, which could explain the limited QALY gain of EUS‐GE.

As the primary objective of EUS‐GE and SGJ is to alleviate symptoms of gastric outlet obstruction, the reduced time to solid oral intake observed after EUS‐GE in the ENDURO trial likely explains the decreased length of hospital stay, which is an important driver of costs. Moreover, although the trial showed non‐inferiority of persistent or recurrent obstruction for which reintervention was required, the randomized GOOSE trial showed a higher rate of reinterventions or need for supplemental nutrition after SGJ, which could also contribute to lower costs following EUS‐GE [8, 26].

Even though the length of follow‐up was 6 months in the ENDURO trial, a time horizon of one month was chosen in this health economic analysis. We expected that the impact of both procedures on healthcare resource use and quality of life would lie within the first month following treatment, since the median time to solid oral intake was one and 3 days in the endoscopic and surgical groups, respectively. After this period, it was assumed that healthcare resource use and quality of life would mainly be affected by disease progression and not by symptoms of gastric outlet obstruction. Furthermore, only one patient in the EUS‐GE group experienced LAMS dysfunction after one month, caused by migration of the LAMS. Due to the limited survival of patients with malignant gastric outlet obstruction, we are not concerned that the one‐month time horizon obscures costs associated with LAMS dysfunction. Costs associated with adverse events, such as reinterventions and other complications, are reflected within the mean in‐hospital costs within this time frame. Nevertheless, the scenario analysis with a 6‐month time horizon also found a difference in costs and QALY contribution in favor of EUS‐GE.

To our knowledge, no previous cost‐utility analyses on EUS‐GE and SGJ have been conducted, but some studies have focused on the costs of EUS‐GE and SGJ. First, the randomized GOOSE trial, conducted in the USA, Germany and India, also showed superiority of EUS‐GE over SGJ with regard to oral intake tolerability, hospital stay, reinterventions, and quality of life [26]. Furthermore, a cost‐analysis yielded a mean difference of US $17,503 in favor of EUS‐GE based on lower admission, professional, pharmacy, and anesthesia costs. However, only costs made during hospital admission and not after discharge were included in the analysis. Second, a Dutch study calculated the costs of ES and SGJ, observing higher initial costs after SGJ but no difference in costs during follow‐up [27]. Third, a retrospective study conducted in the United States reported lower total costs of EUS‐GE compared to SGJ via laparotomy (US $49,387 vs. US $124,192, p < 0.001) [28]. Factors contributing to these differences were a shorter length of hospital stay and lower procedural costs for EUS‐GE. However, this study only included patients who received an open gastrojejunostomy, in contrast to the laparoscopic approach that was standard practice in the ENDURO trial.

The results of this health economic analysis support clinicians and policymakers in choosing the optimal approach for palliating malignant gastric outlet obstruction. Even though life expectancy of these patients is limited, the modest QALY gain of EUS‐GE, corresponding to 2 days in perfect health in favor of EUS‐GE, and cost savings, suggest that intervention in this phase might be appropriate. Furthermore, the conclusions of this study are likely generalizable to other countries owing to the inherent differences of SGJ and EUS‐GE. Nevertheless, cost differences between the two procedures depend on disposable use, LAMS costs, and protocols for post‐procedural care. For example, the shorter median length of stay following EUS‐GE and SGJ in the ENDURO trial (one day and 3 days, respectively) compared to the GOOSE‐trial (3 and 9 days) suggests that length of hospital stay differs between countries [8, 26].

This study has several limitations that should be addressed. First, the analysis was conducted from a healthcare perspective instead of a societal perspective. In addition, only in‐hospital costs were included. Because of the palliative phase of the enrolled patients, it was not feasible to distribute medical consumption questionnaires. Therefore, we might have missed information regarding the effects of EUS‐GE and SGJ on medical costs made at home, in an assisted living facility, or through informal care. However, we expect that cost savings of EUS‐GE would be more pronounced when these healthcare and societal costs are considered, since short hospital stay, as seen after EUS‐GE, is associated with favorable post‐discharge outcomes [29]. Second, missing data regarding EQ‐5D‐3L questionnaires was extensive at all time points. We found that utilities were lower after multiple imputation, indicating that patients who completed the questionnaires had higher EQ‐5D‐3L scores. Therefore, even after multiple imputation, QALYs might be overestimated. Missing data is a frequently encountered problem in research on palliative care [25, 30]. For this reason and because of the assumption that maximum treatment effect would be achieved in the first weeks after treatment, our primary analysis focused on quality of life at one month. Lastly, no questionnaires were distributed in the first days after treatment. We speculate that differences in quality of life might be even more evident in this period, given the reduced time to oral intake and length of hospital stay in favor of EUS‐GE.

The bottom‐up approach used to calculate the OR and endoscopy room per‐minute price, and costs of disposables, medication, and medical instruments is an important strength of this study. To our knowledge, calculation of the per‐minute price of an endoscopy room equipped with fluoroscopy has not previously been performed, and this information may be valuable in other cost‐utility analyses on gastrointestinal endoscopic interventions. Furthermore, we extracted claim data from registries from both participating and referring hospitals. This provided a clear overview of the direct treatment effects, post‐procedural hospital stay, and reinterventions on total costs. Finally, the randomized design permitted a solid comparison between both interventions.

In conclusion, this cost‐utility analysis alongside the ENDURO randomized trial demonstrated that EUS‐GE was more cost‐effective than SGJ as a palliative treatment for malignant gastric outlet obstruction. This is predominantly explained by the shorter length of hospital stay and higher QALYs associated with EUS‐GE. These results support the use of EUS‐GE as the primary treatment approach for this condition.
