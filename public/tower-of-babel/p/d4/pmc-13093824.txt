Enhancing Accuracy and Safety in the Removal of Multiple Impacted Supernumerary Teeth With A Computer‐Designed Localization Guide—A Clinical Case Report
Authors: Paul Galvez, Lélia Ménager, Jean‐Christophe Fricain, Sylvain Catros, Mathilde Fénelon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTComputer‐guided surgical templates provide a safe and minimally invasive approach for extracting deeply impacted lingual supernumerary teeth, especially in pediatric patients. This technique enhances precision, reduces surgical time, and minimizes risks to adjacent anatomical structures, making it a valuable tool in complex oral surgery cases.

Introduction

Impacted supernumerary teeth are often responsible for delayed eruption, root resorption of adjacent teeth, or may interfere with orthodontic treatment, thus often requiring surgical removal. These teeth are frequently located on the lingual aspect, making access particularly difficult—especially in pediatric patients. In cases involving impacted supernumerary teeth in pediatric patients, surgical intervention must be both rapid, considering the pediatric patient's ability to tolerate the procedure, and highly precise to avoid damage to adjacent anatomical structures, including the roots of neighboring teeth. Guided surgery, increasingly adopted across all oral surgery disciplines, not only streamlines implant placement and impacted tooth removal, but also enhances the precision and safety of excising benign and malignant jaw lesions, as well as performing extensive or complex surgical procedures [1]. Computer‐designed localization surgical guides improve intraoperative accuracy and reduce surgical time, offering a valuable aid in managing these challenging cases.

To the best of our knowledge, this appears to be the first reported pediatric case of multiple deeply impacted supernumerary teeth being removed using a lingual mandibular multisite localization guide.

Case History/Examination

A 14‐year‐old patient with no medical history was referred to the Oral Surgery Department of Bordeaux University Hospital for the removal of four mandibular supernumerary teeth before orthodontic treatment. These supernumerary teeth were first incidentally discovered through a panoramic radiographic examination. Then, cone‐beam CT (CBCT) revealed four clearly outlined and hyperdense images located lingually between the roots of teeth 35 and 36, 36 and 37, 45 and 46, and 46 and 47 (Figure 1).

Methods (Differential Diagnosis, Investigations, and Treatment)

The initial radiographic findings suggested four impacted supernumerary teeth germs, which were differentiated from odontomas or other odontogenic lesions based on morphology and localization. Indeed, subsequent CBCT confirmed the presence of multiple supernumerary teeth by revealing their complete crown–root structure. These teeth were distinct from the adjacent teeth and were not connected to the dental lamina of permanent teeth. This radiological aspect was typical of supernumerary teeth and allowed odontomas to be excluded. The orthodontist recommended their removal prior to orthodontic treatment to avoid root resorption of adjacent teeth or interference with the treatment.

Given the patient's young age, the proximity to adjacent roots, and the limited visibility of a lingual approach, it was decided to create a localization guide. First, a digital mandibular impression was taken using an intraoral scanner (3Shape Trios) and the data were exported in STL format. The DICOM and STL files were imported and superimposed using a point‐based registration method within coDiagnostiX software for 3D reconstruction. The DICOM data were used to visualize bony structures and supernumerary teeth, while STL files were used to reconstruct the dental crowns and gingival contour, achieving registration with a deviation below 0.3 mm.

Four virtual implants were positioned facing each supernumerary tooth with an oblique and mesial axis to facilitate surgical access (Figure 2). The aim was to enable the precise localization of the lesion through dental implant positioning with an initial ostectomy using a pilot drill through the localization guide. The trajectory was thus centered on the supernumerary teeth and a minimum 2–3 mm bone buffer was systematically maintained between the planned drill trajectory and the adjacent root surfaces. The CBCT analysis confirmed that a lingual approach also avoided the emergence of the mental foramen, thereby reducing the risk of nerve injury. The angulation of the drilling axis was also intentionally planned to increase the distance to the mandibular canal and to remain confined within the lingual compartment. Regarding the lingual nerve, as it is not visualized on CBCT and does not course within bone, preoperative measurements cannot be used to determine its exact location. Consequently, preservation relied on raising a full‐thickness lingual flap and maintaining constant protection of the flap with a lingual retractor during drilling. In collaboration with the laboratory, a tooth‐supported localization guide was designed with minimal thickness and control windows to ensure stable repositioning during surgery. The localization guide was printed in resin (NextDent SG, 3DSYSTEMS) which is a biocompatible CE certified Class I material, using the Rapid Shape P40 system (Straumann) (Figure 3). After printing, the localization guide was cleaned with isopropyl alcohol (10 min) and then underwent UV curing (Form Cure, 20 min at 60°C). The integration of 5‐mm‐inner‐diameter titanium drill sleeve (Straumann) was performed to prevent any direct contact between the rotating instruments and the resin in the localization guide. Finally, the localization guide was immersed in povidone iodine prior to use, thereby ensuring that it would not deform.

The procedure was performed under local anesthesia, with a lingual intra‐sulcular incision from 32 to 37 and from 42 to 47. A lingual subperiosteal flap was raised to allow placement of the localization guide and thus protect the lingual nerve. A 4‐mm‐internal diameter trephine bur that perfectly matched the sleeve was used. It was inserted through the sleeve at a speed of 1200 rpm with maximum irrigation to create a bone window that marked the exact position of each tooth. Trephining continued until the lingual cortex was fully penetrated. After removing the localization guide, the bone window was removed then enlarged using a round bur to facilitate tooth removal. This was followed by curettage and rinsing of the socket before closure with interrupted sutures (Vicryl 4/0). The procedure lasted 45 min.

Conclusion and Results (Outcome and Follow‐Up)

No immediate complications were observed, such as active bleeding, paresthesia, or soft tissue swelling. The patient was thus discharged with instructions to maintain oral hygiene, take analgesic medication and follow a soft, warm diet for three days. At the two‐week postoperative checkup, the patient expressed high satisfaction with his recovery of oral function. He only experienced slight soreness within the first 24 h, which was alleviated by taking 500 mg of paracetamol every six hours. No postoperative pain, bleeding, swelling, or paresthesia occurred during the follow‐up period and adjacent teeth remained vital after 10 months (Figure 4). The patient expressed satisfaction with the overall management (i.e., the clarity of the explanations given, the care and follow‐up provided as well as the clinical outcome). Discussion

Firstly, regarding the diagnostic and planification explorations, CBCT was justified in this adolescent patient: (1) to characterize the radiopaque mass and confirm the diagnostic hypothesis before surgical management and (2) because of the impacted position of the supernumerary teeth, their proximity to the roots of permanent teeth, and the need for precise 3D planning prior to minimally invasive guided surgery. These factors meet accepted ALARA‐based indications in pediatric patients.

Secondly, regarding the advantages of surgical guides, these localization guides facilitate surgery in areas with limited visibility, such as extracting lingually positioned teeth, which often pose access challenges, especially in pediatric patients. Other studies have reported the usefulness of these guides in similar situations, such as the extraction of mesiodens [2] in the anterior maxilla or the management of impacted odontomas. Moreover, impacted supernumerary teeth are often closely associated with the roots of adjacent teeth. In this case, the precision allowed by the localization guide made the procedure safer regarding the surrounding anatomical structures [3]. Finally, incorporating drill sleeve prevents direct contact between the rotating instruments and the resin of the guide. In addition to enhancing accuracy and stability, it also prevents resin debris from contaminating the surgical site. Recent evidence also highlights the accuracy and dimensional reliability of computer‐guided navigation, with mean deviations around 1–1.4 mm depending on coronal/apical measurement and angulation parameters, supporting the clinical relevance of guide‐based precision in oral surgery [4]. Furthermore, methodological analyses have shown that accuracy is influenced by cumulative error sources—including imaging superimposition, guide fabrication tolerance, and drill‐sleeve dimensions—emphasizing the need for careful planning and controlled surgical execution [5].

Some studies already demonstrated that digitally designed surgical localization guides improve surgical precision and make it safer to access impacted teeth, especially when visibility is reduced and risky anatomical structures are close by [1, 6].

A subset of limitations should be acknowledged regarding methods and follow‐up of the present case, including the absence of postoperative imaging and missing details regarding CBCT acquisition parameters. Furthermore, while objective measures for accuracy assessment have been previously reported, the present case is rather supported by qualitative clinical observations indicating successful clinical outcomes.

Our work continues in this vein by confirming the feasibility and precision of this approach, while extending it to a more anatomically complex situation involving four posterior lingual and deeply impacted teeth in a young patient. This case demonstrates that a localization guide can secure lingual access, reduce the extent of bone fenestration, and maintain a safety margin with respect to neurovascular structures, thus reinforcing the place of these devices in the operative arsenal of oral surgery.
