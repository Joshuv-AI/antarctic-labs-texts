Selenium nanoparticles with bioorthogonal targeting to boost CAR-cell therapy for colorectal cancer
Authors: Jinfan Zhou, Gaohua Li, Yunze Xu, Wenhui Ma, Xuehua Zhang, Haitao Yuan, Zilin Tan, Zhenghao Li, Hongwei Yu, Zhenzhao Xu, Zhiqiang Yu, Jiafei Xi, Chaoping Fu, Yanan Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The treatment of solid tumors with chimeric antigen receptor natural killer (CAR-NK) cell therapy confronts significant barriers, notably poor cellular infiltration and a highly immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME). To overcome these challenges, we developed selenium-containing polymer nanoparticles (ManNAl-SeNPs) loaded with dibenzocyclooctyne (DBCO)-modified mannose. Metabolic glycoengineering metabolic glycoengineering (MGE) enabled efficient labeling of exogenous DBCO on azide (N3) groups on CAR-NK cells, establishing a bioorthogonal click chemistry targeting strategy, significantly improving the anti-tumor activity of azide-CAR-NK (N3-CAR-NK) cells, including recognition specificity, migration efficiency, and cytotoxic activity. Herein, ManNAl-SeNPs with ultra-sensitive responsiveness of diselenide bonds, in the acidic TME, diselenide bond releases seleninic acid, acting as an immune checkpoint inhibitor while augmenting CAR-NK cells cytotoxicity. In vivo, the combination of ManNAl-SeNPs with N3-CAR-NK cells significantly increased targeting capacity and invasiveness. This study presents a TME-responsive nanoplatform with artificial bio-orthogonal combination strategy that effectively enhance the migratory ability and accumulation of CAR-NK cells for potent antitumor therapy.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12951-026-04174-y.

Introduction

Adoptive cell therapy (ACT) is an emerging immunotherapeutic approach that involves isolating immunoreactive cells from cancer patients and reinfusing the engineered immune cells into the body for direct elimination of tumors or activation of the immune system [1, 2]. CAR-NK cell therapy represents a promising immunotherapeutic approach, characterized by Human Leukocyte Antigen (HLA) unrestricted tumor-killing capability. The therapeutic potential is evaluated, with clinical trials already confirming encouraging efficacy and a favorable safety spectrum in patients suffering from hematologic malignancies [3–5], their applicability for solid tumors remains unclear. The translation is particularly hindered by limitations in tumor trafficking and infiltration, which are critical barriers to achieving clinical efficacy [6–8], and TME impaired function of CAR-NK cells [9, 10]. Aberrant expression of adhesion molecules and downregulated chemokines can inhibit the trafficking, infiltration, and vascular extravasation of CAR-NK cells into solid tumor sites. The TME including acidosis, tissue hypoxia and altered redox conditions, can significantly damage the anticancer effect of immune cells [11]. Tumor expression of inhibitory immune checkpoint ligands can directly impair CAR-NK cells and negatively regulate immune responses [12]. To address these challenges, we developed a new nanocarrier to enhance the tumor-targeting capacity of CAR-NK cells. Overall, this advancement is of great significance to clinical CAR-NK therapy for solid tumors, particularly in modulating the TME.

TME exhibits distinct physiological and biochemical features such as lower pH, localized elevation in temperature, and heightened concentrations of reducing agents like glutathione, plays a critical role in promoting tumorigenesis and proliferation. Leveraging these unique pathological characteristics to design smart drug delivery systems that respond specifically to the aberrant TME holds significant promise for enhancing tumor-specific accumulation [13]. Smart targeting is increasingly regarded as a promising strategy beyond conventional passive and active targeting methods. This strategy is distinguished by its activation mechanism, which is initiated by the unique TME characterized by higher concentrations of glutathione (GSH) and reactive oxygen species (ROS) compared to healthy cells, as well as specific enzymes and the extracellular condition [14]. The extensive research on disulfide bonds over recent decades, which are utilized in redox-responsive drug delivery systems (DDS) to achieve tumor-specific drug release while significantly reducing premature release to the other tissues [13, 15, 16]. Under oxidation conditions, the diselenide bond is cleaved and oxidized to form hydrophilic selenite, which can be reduced to selenol under reducing conditions [17].

Our previous study designed polymeric nanoparticles incorporating diselenide bonds for the delivery of indocyanine green (ICG) employing supercritical carbon dioxide (SC-CO2) technology, thereby facilitating the photodynamic therapy efficacy in breast cancer [18]. Furthermore, oxidation-triggered cleavage of these diselenide bonds generates selenite compounds. Both inorganic and organic selenites could be engineered to as immune checkpoint inhibitors by downregulating the expression of HLA-E in tumor cells, prompt CAR-NK cells achieve an effective anti-tumor response [19–21]. Studies have shown that targeting immune checkpoint genes can promote a favorable remodeling of the tumor immune microenvironment [22].

A novel engineering strategy offers the potential to improve the target activity without necessitating genetic modification of CAR-NK cells, thereby circumventing challenges, including insertion mutations, a laborious process and high costs [12]. Metabolic engineering presents an effective approach to introduce various chemical groups into cell surface glycans by the intrinsic cellular metabolic pathway. Based on this principle, the metabolism of non-natural sugars can be utilized to covalently bind chemical reporters to markers carrying a paired group [23, 24]. The adoption of N3 groups in bioorthogonal click chemistry is driven by their favorable physicochemical profiles. These include straightforward synthesis, a compact structure that minimizes steric hindrance, remarkable inertness under physiological conditions, and their virtual absence in living systems, which ensures high specificity [25–28]. Bioorthogonal reactions proceed with high speed and specificity under mild physiological conditions, effectively minimizing background noise caused by cross reactions in living organisms due to their exceptional selectivity. By employing bioorthogonal click chemistry, specifically the alkyne azide cycloaddition, researchers have developed a powerful tool for precise immune recognition. Cai et al. [29, 30] developed a strategy to promote specific CAR-T cells recognition of tumors through a bioorthogonal recognition system. This was achieved by metabolically labeling the CAR-T cells with an N3 group and the tumor cells with a bicyclononyne (BCN) group, with their selective binding facilitated by bioorthogonal chemistry. The efficient reaction between these engineered cells leads to their firm adhesion, which significantly improves CAR-T cells recognize tumor cells and potently activates the CAR-T cells for anti-tumor activity. More recently, In an immunosuppressed TME, Cai et al. constructed interleukin-21 (IL-21) nanoparticles (ILNPs) conjugated with anti-CD45 on glycoengineered NK cells. The subsequent bioorthogonal click reaction between these complementary groups significantly strengthened the mutual recognition, promoting deeper penetration, greater accumulation, and improved homing of NK cells within tumor tissues. This nanoparticles strategy fostered an immunologically permissive TME, which promoted the accumulation and recruitment of NK cells and ultimately enhanced the efficacy of immunotherapy [31]. Therefore, we designed a TME-responsive nanoparticle delivery system for orthogonal targeted immunotherapy. The introduction of exogenous artificial target receptors onto CAR-NK cells has significantly enhanced their specific recognition and infiltration of solid tumors, which were paired with complementary bioorthogonal moieties to label the cells.

We designed a selenium-based polymer nanoplatform functionalized with DBCO-mannose as a bioorthogonal ligand. These nanocomposites preferentially accumulated in tumor tissue through the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect, thereby enabling targeted delivery and potent antitumor activity. Within the TME, the diselenide-containing DDS demonstrated ultrasensitive responsiveness, leading to the controlled release of synthetic sugar molecules from the ManNAl-SeNPs. These sugar precursors were metabolically incorporated into tumor cell membranes via the intrinsic sialic acid biosynthetic pathway, thereby enabling cell-surface display of DBCO groups. Simultaneously, the diselenide bonds underwent cleavage and oxidation, generating selenoic acid derivatives, which can function as an immune checkpoint inhibitor to further strengthen immune cytotoxicity against tumor cells. Following metabolic labeling with N₃ and DBCO groups on the cell surfaces strengthened the recognition interactions between CAR-NK cells and tumor cells. This artificial interaction promoted the immune cytotoxicity against tumor cells, increased accumulation, and improved homing of CAR-T cells into tumor tissues, collectively leading to a superior therapeutic outcome against solid tumors (Scheme 1).

Synthesis of PSe

PSe was synthesized according to our previous reports [18]. The diselenide-based initiator was synthesized directly from 11-bromo-1-undecanol and elemental selenium. In a standardized procedure, the reactants were combined in a suitable solvent system, and the mixture was stirred under optimized conditions. Subsequently, this initiator was used to initiate the ring-opening polymerization of ε-CL catalyzed by Tin (II) 2-ethylhexanoate (Sn(Oct)₂), affording a diselenide-containing polycaprolactone (PCL-SeSe-PCL). Finally, the terminal hydroxyl groups of PCL-SeSe-PCL were esterified with polyethylene glycol (PEG)-COOH, yielding the target linear triblock copolymer, poly(ethylene glycol)-b-poly(ε-caprolactone)-b-poly(ethylene glycol) (PSe).

Synthesis of DBCO-intermediate 3

The synthesis of DBCO-COOH (N-azabicyclo[2.2.2]octyne-8-carboxylic acid) was conducted as described by Stuart A. McNelles et al. [32]. First, DBCO-COOH was reacted with N-hydroxysuccinimide (HOSu) in dichloromethane to yield DBCO-NHS. This intermediate was then reacted with D-mannosamine hydrochloride and N, N-diisopropylethylamine to synthesize DBCO-Mannose. Finally, the product underwent acetylation with acetic anhydride and pyridine, producing DBCO-tetraacetyl-mannosamine (DBCO-Intermediate 3).

Synthesis of ManNAl-SeNPs

Using the SC-CO2 antisolvent process, DBCO-Intermediate 3 and PSe were dissolved in a mixed solvent system consisting of dichloromethane (2 mL) and ethanol (18 mL) in a 1:1 mass ratio. The homogeneous solution was sprayed into a SC-CO2 environment. SC-CO2 effectively extracted the organic solvents, leading to the co-precipitation of DBCO-Intermediate 3 and PSe, as shown in Fig. 2a. The liquefied CO₂ was transferred to a 500 mL high-pressure crystallizer at a constant rate of 30 g·min⁻¹ using a syringe pump. The drug-polymer solution was subsequently delivered into the crystallizer using a high-pressure feed pump. Upon completion of the injection, the system was maintained under constant CO₂ flow rate, temperature, and gas purge conditions for 15 min. This dwell phase was critical to facilitate the complete diffusion and removal of any residual organic solvents, thereby ensuring the purity of the final product. The high-pressure vessel was then gradually depressurized at a controlled rate to collect the final product.

Culture of CAR-CEA-NK cells

A serum-free culture system was established to support the undifferentiated growth of human embryonic stem cells (hESCs). These hESCs were initially maintained on a feeder layer of Mouse Embryonic Fibroblasts (MEFs) to ensure that the stem cells maintained their differentiation potential and expanded stably in culture. Next, we constructed a specific CAR vector targeting carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) and screened the CAR-hESC cell line by lentiviral transfection of hESCs with the CAR gene. We also utilized the human embryonic stem cell (H9) cell line for coculture with MEFs to ensure that the CAR-hESCs expanded stably. Then, the CAR-hESCs were cultured by the spin embryoid body (Spin EB), and the CAR-hESCs were cultured in a serum-free culture system. CAR-hESCs were subsequently induced into CAR-NK cells by the Spin EB method. Flow cytometric analysis revealed that CAR-NK cells lines stably expressing the CAR gene and exhibiting stable stemness could be obtained, with a representative percentage of 76.9%.

Cell viability evaluation

Tumor cells were first inoculated into flat-bottom 96-well plates at a density of 5000 cells per well, with 3 replicate wells set up for each group. After the tumor cells were inoculated for 12 h and had completely attached to the wells, cell proliferation was detected following the addition of ManNAl-SeNPs at different concentrations (50, 100, 150, and 200 µM), with continued monitoring for 24 and 48 h. The culture medium was then replaced with medium containing 10% CCK-8. After 1 h, the absorbance of the cell culture solution in each well was recorded at 450 nm.

Evaluation of the optimal coculture time of ManNAl-SeNPs with CAR-NK cells

To evaluate the time-dependent effect of metabolic labeling on CAR-NK cell-mediated cytotoxicity, SW480 tumor cells were seeded in flat-bottom 96-well plates at a density of 5,000 cells per well. Following cells attachment, were pretreated with 150 µM ManNAl-SeNPs for varying durations (0, 6, 12, 24, and 36 h). Subsequently, CAR-NK or N3-CAR-NK cells were respectively cocultured with target cells at an predetermined effector target (E/T) ratio of 5:1, and the co-culture was continued for 12 h. Finally, the absorbance of SW480 cells, indicative of cell viability, was measured at a wavelength of 450 nm. CAR-NK cells killing rate (%) = (OD experimental group − OD effector cell group)/(OD target cell group) × 100%.

Targeted labeling of the cell membrane

To assess the incorporation efficiency of the metabolic label, three distinct colorectal cancer cell lines were seeded in confocal dishes and allowed to adhere for 12 h. Upon confirmation of cell attachment, the cultures were supplemented with 150 µM ManNAl-SeNPs for 48 h. The labeled cells were then incubated with a N3-modified Cy5.5 fluorescent dye (N3-Cy5.5) for 30 min, exploiting the specific bioorthogonal reaction for detection. Following washes with PBS to remove unbound dye cells, cells were treated with Hoechst 33,342 for 10 min. Cellular localization of the labeling were visualized using a laser scanning confocal microscope (LSCM). For quantitative assessment, the percentage of fluorescence-positive cells were analyzed by flow cytometry. To evaluate the capability of N-Azidoacetylgalactosamine-tetraacylated (Ac4GalNAz) to introduce N3 groups onto the surface of CAR-NK cells, which were pretreated with 50 µM Ac4GalNAz for 1 h. Subsequently, the cells were incubated with an AZDye 488 DBCO probe for 30 min, allowing for a highly specific reaction. After staining with Hoechst 33342, the efficiency of the surface modification was analyzed by LSCM and quantitatively determined by flow cytometry to calculate the labeling efficiency.

Bioorthogonal effects for target identification

To evaluate the cellular interactions, SW480 cells were seeded into 8-well chambers and allowed to adhere for 12 h. The cells were then randomly assigned to three experimental groups: PBS; SeNPs/CAR-NK cells, and ManNAl-SeNPs/CAR-NK cells. With or without metabolic glycan labeling CAR-NK cells, were stained with an anti-CD56-PI antibody for 1 h, followed by three washes with PBS. Simultaneously, the SW480 cells in the different groups were labeled with CellMask™ Plasma Membrane Stains to visualize their membranes. The effector cells were subsequently co-cultured with the prepared SW480 cells for 1 h at 1:1 E/T. The pictures of specific aggregation were documented using LSCM.

LDH cytotoxicity assay

CAR-NK cells cytotoxicity is evaluated after 48 h of pretreatment with or without Ac4GalNAz. After a day coculture period at varying E/T ratios, the cell culture plates were centrifuged. From each well, 50 µL of supernatant was then sampled and combined with an equal volume of prepared LDH substrate in a fresh microplate for subsequent spectrophotometric measurement. For NKG2A blockade studies, CAR-NK cells were first incubated with anti-NKG2A monoclonal antibody (10 µg·mL⁻¹) at 4 °C for 30 min, with absorbance read at 490 nm. The cell lysis rate was calculated using the following formula: cell lysis rate (%) = (OD Experimental-OD Effector Spontaneous-OD Target Spontaneous)/(OD Target Maximal-Target spontaneous) × 100%.

Cellular uptake efficiency

To quantify cellular selenium uptake, SW480 cells were first seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 5 × 10⁴ cells per well and allowed to adhere for 12 h. Following treatment with ManNAl-SeNPs 8 h, the cells were washed, counted, and lysed using 1 mL of lysis buffer. The lysates then underwent digestion with HNO₃, after which each sample was diluted to a final volume of 5 mL for analysis. The total selenium content was ultimately determined by inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS).

Quantification analysis of cytokines

SW480 cells were inoculated at a density of 5 × 104 cells overnight and pretreated with different treatments for the indicated times. Modified or unmodified CAR-NK cells were then added (E/T 5:1) and coincubated for 12 h, consistent with the previously described LDH cytotoxicity assay. After centrifugation, 100 µL of supernatant was collected from each sample into a new 96-well plate for cytokine detection. The concentrations of IFN-γ, GZB, and TNF-α were determined by ELISA according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Based on the standard curve, the released cytokine levels in both CAR-NK cells and control groups were calculated and statistically compared.

Western blotting

SW480 cells were cultured and treated in 6-well plates for 24 h. Total protein was subsequently extracted by ice-cold lysis followed by boiling. After quantification with a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay, equal protein aliquots were separated by 12% polyacrylamide sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS‒PAGE) and transferred to a polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane. The membrane was blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA), incubated with primary antibodies against HLA-E at 4 °C overnight, and probing with a secondary antibody at room temperature for 1 h. Following another series washes, the protein bands were finally visualized by incubation with an electrochemiluminescence (ECL) substrate. The detection was performed using the GelView 6000 Pro system.

Xenograft mouse model

Male NOD-SCID mice (6–8 weeks old) were utilized as xenograft models to evaluate the in vivo efficacy of the therapy. The in vivo distribution of Cy7-labeled Ac4ManNAz was assessed in SW480 tumor-bearing mice. When the tumor volumes reaching approximately 100 mm³, the mice received an intravenous injection of either Cy7-labeled ManNAl-SeNPs (8 mg·kg⁻¹) or free Cy7. Whole-body fluorescence imaging was performed at predetermined time intervals (0, 0.5, 3, 6, 12, 24, and 48 h) post-injection using an in vivo imaging system (IVIS). To quantify organ accumulation, a subset of mice was sacrificed, and key organs (heart, liver, spleen, kidneys, lungs) were collected for in vitro imaging to quantitatively analyze the final organ accumulation and support the in vivo data. The SW480-Luc cells were inoculated subcutaneously into the right dorsolateral buttock of each mouse and randomly arranged into PBS, CAR-NK, CAR-NK/SeNPs, and N3-CAR-NK/ManNAl-SeNPs groups. When tumor volume growth reached approximately 150 mm3, the SeNPs and ManNAl-SeNPs (2 mg·kg−1 Se) were intravenously administered every two days for a total of two doses. Next, a dose of CAR-NK cells (1.5⋅107 cells/100µL) with or without N3 modification was injected intravenously every 8 days for 3 weeks. Mice were anesthetized with isoflurane, and imaging was performed approximately 3 min after intraperitoneal administration of D-luciferin (150 mg·kg⁻¹) using an IVIS. During the 25 day experimental period, body weight and tumor dimensions were recorded every 5 days. Tumor volumes were calculated using the formula: Volume (mm³) = (long diameter × short diameter²)/2. Mice bearing tumors larger than 1500 mm3 in volume were sacrificed. At the end of the experiment, blood was sampled from rat eyes and the serum indices in peripheral blood were detected. Following the final in vivo imaging, the primary organs and tumor tissues were systematically harvested. Serial sections were prepared from these blocks and subjected to Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) staining. Following the preparation of single-cell suspensions, the cells were subjected to staining using fluorescently tagged anti-CD56 and anti-CD3 antibodies by analysis on a flow cytometer. The infiltration of CAR-NK cells and HLA-E expression was evaluated by immunohistochemistry and immunofluorescence, respectively.

Statistical analysis

All data were expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) from at least four independent experiments. To determine the significance of differences between experimental groups, a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was employed,, with significance levels set at *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001 and ****p < 0.0001.

Results and discussion

Preparation of CAR-hESC-NK

Our group successfully developed a serum-free culture system for hESCs, constructed with MEFs as the feeder layer, which enables stem cells to maintain their differentiation potential and exhibit a steady rate of expansion (Fig. 1a). A specifically targeted CAR vector for CEA was designed and introduced via lentiviral transfection. The induction of NK cells is divided into two stages (Fig. 1b). The first stage involved SpinEB culture, an indicator of changes driving the expansion of a stable CAR-hESC cell line incubated with SCF, VEGF, TPO, EPO, IL-3, and IL-6 (Fig. 1c). Round-bottom 96-well plates were utilized to simulate embryoid bodies (EBs). This initial induction stage lasted for six days, during which the expression of green fluorescent protein (GFP) was continuously monitored. On the sixth day, stable expression of stem cells indicated successful transfection of the CAR. The second stage involved the transition from CAR-CEA-hESC to NK cells. On the third day, stromal cells adhering to the surface of the culture vessel were observed at the bottom of the EB spheres, while semi-suspended hematopoietic stem cells emerged from their periphery. As induction progressed, peripheral hematopoietic stem cells gradually proliferated in suspension above the stromal layer, forming dense suspensions around the EB spheres. Next, microscopic observations revealed an increased number of NK cell clusters with reduced volume, exhibiting a rounded and translucent morphology with distinct nuclei. During the induction stage II, a greater number of hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells emerged, accompanied by an obvious decrease in expression of surface antigens CD34, CD43, and CD45 to 37.8% and 58.6% (Fig. 1d), respectively. We intravenously administered CAR-NK cells to NOD/SCID mice bearing SW1116 xenografts in the right dorsogluteal region. Terminal tumor weight quantification revealed that the CAR-NK cells demonstrated significantly enhanced tumor growth suppression (Fig. 1e-f). Parallel survival analysis over 32 days post-infusion showed that CAR-NK cell therapy not only delayed tumor progression but also conferred superior survival benefits compared to control groups (Fig. 1g).

Preparation and characterizations of ManNAl-SeNPs

Se was prepared by Steglich esterification with PEG-COOH and PCL-SeSe-PCL, using DCC as a coupling reagent and dimethyl aminopyridine (DMAP) as a catalyst (Fig. 2a, b). Dynamic light scattering (DLS) measurements of the ManNAl-SeNPs revealed an average hydrodynamic diameter of 86.5 ± 0.8 nm (Fig. 2d), which is slightly larger than the size determined by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) (Fig. 2c), likely due to a minor degree of aggregation during the measurement. The FTIR spectra (Fig. 2e) revealed characteristic peaks, confirming the successful synthesis of the block copolymers. Characterisation of ManNAl-SeNPs via SEM imaging combined with EDS revealed uniform distribution of selenium elements both on the microsphere surface and within the interior (Fig. 2f). Furthermore, the 1H NMR spectrum of DBCO-Intermediate 3 and GPC curves of PEG-COOH, PCL-SeSe-PCL and PSe are shown in Fig. S1a, b. As shown in the Fig. S2, the release of selenium from ManNAl-SeNPs under different conditions indicates that the selenium content released increases with rising concentrations of H2O2 and GSH. This demonstrates that the selenium release efficiency of ManNAl-SeNPs is higher in simulated tumor environments than in physiological conditions.

Efficient glycometabolic engineering of CAR-NK cells and tumor cells for enhanced target recognition

To explore whether mannose-loaded ManNAl-SeNPs and paired groups could successfully modify the surface of tumor cells and CAR-NK cells via metabolism pathways, a CCK-8 assay was conducted to assess tumor growth after culture with ManNAl-SeNPs (Fig. 3a). SW480 cells were incubated with either prepared DBCO-Intermediate 3 or ManNAl-SeNPs for 48 h to introduce exogenous DBCO-orthogonal groups through metabolic engineering. The incorporation of N3 on the cell surface is crucial for achieving CAR-NK cells bioorthogonal targeting of tumors. The cytotoxicity of commercial Ac4GalNAz to CAR-NK cells was detected by a CCK-8 assay after 48 h of cocultivation, which revealed a significant decrease in viability at 100 µM (Fig. 3b). Flow cytometry revealed a concentration-dependent increase in fluorescence intensity with ManNAl-SeNPs treatment, indicative of higher alkynyl group density on the cell surface. The maximum fluorescence signal was observed at a concentration of 150 µM. (Fig. 3c).

ManNAl-SeNPs demonstrated their universal metabolism-labeling capability upon incubation with Caco2 cells and LS-174T cells, two distinct colorectal tumor cell lines. The results showed that under the same conditions (200 µM), the nanoparticles were non-toxic to cancer cells (Fig. S3a, b). Meanwhile, the ability of DBCO to modify tumor cell membranes through metabolic processes was assessed using confocal microscopy and flow cytometry (Fig. S3c-f), revealing no restriction across different colorectal cancer cell lines.

After N3-CAR-NK cells were treated with 50 µM AZDye 488 DBCO for 30 min, flow cytometry analysis revealed a dose-dependent effect of concentration on fluorescence intensity (Fig. 3d). When the concentration was 50 µM, peak fluorescence emission was observed. After incubation with N3-Cy5.5 for 30 min, red fluorescence was observed on the tumor cell membranes using laser confocal microscopy (Fig. 3e), demonstrating the successful expression of the DBCO group. Furthermore, confocal imaging showed strong green fluorescence intensity on the cell surface (Fig. 3f).

To establish the optimal pretreatment duration, tumor cells were incubated with two selenium-based compounds at an equivalent selenium concentration for varying periods before being co-cultured with CAR-NK cells. Our results indicate that although SeNPs alone exhibited no direct antitumor activity in vitro, a significantly stronger cytotoxic effect was observed when CAR-NK cells were introduced to tumor cells pre-conditioned with ManNAl-SeNPs. This marked augmentation of tumor cell killing suggests that pretreatment with ManNAl-SeNPs effectively sensitizes tumor cells to subsequent CAR-NK cell-mediated cytotoxicity. Earlier research indicated that a disturbance in cancerous cells redox balance inhibits NK cell-mediated antitumor immune responses through the downregulation of HLA-E. We propose that the cleavage of the diselenide bond within the TME generates selenite/seleninic acid species, which downregulate HLA-E expression. The disruption of this inhibitory checkpoint enhances the cytotoxicity mediated by CAR-NK cells. To establish a direct causal link between the observed HLA-E downregulation and the augmentation of NK cell function, we employed antibodies to block the interaction between HLA-E and the NKG2A receptor complex. As shown in Fig. S4, specific blockade of NKG2A significantly boost the cytotoxicity of CAR-NK cells. Notably, when both anti-NKG2A and ManNAl-SeNPs were applied, the cytotoxicity of CAR-NK cells did not differ substantially from that observed with ManNAl-SeNPs alone. This suggests that the enhanced NK cell activity resulting from the combined treatment is primarily attributable to the effective disruption of the HLA-E/NKG2A inhibitory axis. We next pre-incubated SW480 cells with ManNAl-SeNPs for different time (0, 6, 12, 24, and 36 h) prior to co-culture with N3-CAR-NK cells (E/T = 5:1). Quantitative cytotoxicity assays revealed maximal enhancement (47.63% and 48.47%) with 24 h and 36 h pre-treatment, respectively (Fig. 3g). Compared to CAR-NK cells monotherapy, shorter pre-incubation periods (0, 6, and 12 h) with nanoparticles demonstrated a moderate enhancement in N3-CAR-NK cells cytotoxic activity. Comparative analysis with SeNPs showed more modest cytotoxicity enhancement, ranging from 10.53 to 20.04%, with maximal sensitization observed at 12 h pre-treatment. Notably, prolonged co-culture duration failed to amplify this effect. Compared with the control group, the cytotoxicity against LS-174T and Caco2 cells pretreated for 24 h was validated to be significantly greater, by factors of 3.64 and 3.84, respectively. Targeting recognition under bioorthogonal effects improves the antitumor activity of N3-CAR-NK cells

To directly visualize the targeting effects based on biological orthogonal reactions, preincubated SW480 cells were cocultured with N3-CAR-NK cells (E/T = 5:1) for 4 h. The results showed substantial accumulation of N3-CAR-NK cells on alkynylated tumor cells, indicating an enhanced capability for tumor recognition and migration (Fig. 4a). We quantified the cytotoxicity of N3-CAR-NK cells using a LDH release assay. N3-CAR-NK and target cells were cocultured for 24 h across a range of E/T ratios (1:1, 5:1, and 10:1). The control and SeNPs group showed significantly declines in cytotoxicity compared with the ManNAl-SeNPs group at the same ratios (Fig. 4b). Overall, the N3-CAR-NK group displayed 2.84 and 1.72-fold greater killing activity than the control and SeNPs group at E/T ratio of 5:1, with cytotoxicity being less than twice that of the SeNPs group. The cytotoxicity was 2.53 and 1.63 times greater than that of the other groups at E/T ratio of 1:1, respectively, indicating positive association between cytotoxicity and the E/T ratio. Besides, the cytotoxicity of N3-CAR-NK cells was compared in two other colorectal cell lines (Fig. S5c, d). At an E/T ratio of 10:1, the cytotoxicity of N3-CAR-NK cells against Caco2 cells increased by 1.51–2.46 times. The LDH release from LS-174T cells was 2.7-fold greater than the control group. These findings establish that the bioorthogonal strategy significantly enhances the cytotoxic potency of CAR-NK cells.

Besides, we performed a direct assessment of cells aggregates was further confirmed by calcein-AM/PI staining (Fig. 4c), which revealed a broader area of red fluorescence in the ManNAl-SeNPs group compared with the SeNPs group, demonstrating the potent antitumor therapeutic effect of the combination. The study found that bioorthogonal targeting could enhance both recognition and cytotoxicity to tumor cells. Selenium content in the tumor cells was quantified using ICP-MS after coculture (Fig. 4d). The uptake of both SeNPs and ManNAl-SeNPs treatment increased 3-fold compared with the control group after coculture for 8 h. In previous studies, we engineered CAR-NK cells with CEA as a target antigen, which exhibited superior killing efficacy against CEA-positive colorectal cancer cells, including LS-174T cells and SW480 cells, compared to the CEA-negative Caco2 cell line in vitro. Our preliminary results suggest that pretreatment with ManNAl-SeNPs and SeNPs increased the susceptibility of CEA-negative tumor cells to N3-CAR-NK cells. Western blot analysis revealed that HLA-E expression decreased in all three colorectal cancer tumor cell lines (Fig. 4e), indicating decreased checkpoint recognition between NKG2A and HLA-E.

Inhibitory effect of CAR-NK cell therapy on tumor growth in mice

To assess the tumor clearance capacity of CAR-NK cells in combination in NOD-SCID mice, we constructed a xenograft mouse model with SW480-luc cells to validate the in vivo therapeutic effect of immunotherapy combined with targeted enhancement of CAR-NK cytotoxicity (Fig. 5a). To evaluate the killing efficiency of selenium-containing orthogonal biological nanoparticle treatment on solid tumors in vivo, we used bioluminescence imaging to monitor and quantitatively analyze tumor proliferation during the whole treatment (Fig. 5b, e). By day 10, a marked increase in tumor burden was observed in the control group. In contrast, mice treated with the combination of CAR-NK cells and selenium nanoparticles exhibited a markedly weaker bioluminescence signal, demonstrating a significant enhancement in the antitumor efficacy of the CAR-NK cells. At 21 days, only weak luminescent signals were detected in the N3-CAR-NK/ManNAl-SeNPs. As confirmed by dissected tumor photographs and tumor volume measurements, only the N3-CAR-NK group exhibited tumor growth inhibition (Fig. 5c, h). We next assessed the in vivo tumor-targeting efficacy of ManNAl-SeNPs in a xenograft model. Following intravenous injection of the Cy7-labeled nanoparticles, IVIS imaging revealed clear tumor delineation within 3 h, with the fluorescence signal reaching its peak intensity at 12 h (Fig. S6a), demonstrating efficient tumor accumulation. In contrast, free Cy7 was rapidly cleared from the circulation. In vitro imaging of tissues at the 48 h time point further confirmed significantly greater retention of the nanoparticles in tumor tissue compared to major organs (Fig. S6b, c). While our in vivo therapeutic data demonstrate a potent synergistic effect upon the combination of ManNAl-SeNPs and N₃-CAR-NK cells, a direct visualization of the accumulation of N₃-functionalized imaging agents at the tumor site was not undertaken. Future work employing N₃-modified imaging probes, will be a critical next step to visually corroborate this mechanism and precisely quantify the dynamics of cell recruitment. The therapeutic effect was further verified by immunohistochemistry analysis, which revealed significantly decreased expression of HLA-E in both SeNPs and ManNAl-SeNPs treatment (Fig. 5d). The body weights were monitored periodically. There were no significant weight differences observed between the control and treatment groups at any time point, arguing against overt toxicity induced by the experimental procedures. (Fig. 5f). SeNPs also mitigated tumor growth to a certain extent. Nevertheless, when mice were pretreated with ManNAl-SeNPs and then injected intravenously with N3-CA-NK cells, tumor progression was significantly mitigated. Furthermore, as indicated by CD56 upregulation, the N3-CAR-NK/ManNAl-SeNPs-treated mice exhibited greater accumulation of CD56 + tagged CAR-NK cells in the tumor tissue than the N3-CAR-NK/SeNPs groups (Fig. 5g), which may be due to enhanced immune activity of CAR-NK cells under bioorthogonal targeting, leading to increased recruitment into the solid tumors. Besides, the improvement of CAR-NK immune function was associated with the inhibition of HLA-E protein expression on the membranes of cancer cells. Flow cytometric analysis at the experimental endpoint demonstrated a striking enhancement in the tumor infiltration of N3-CAR-NK cells within the ManNAl-SeNPs treatment group. In contrast, only minimal levels of accumulated effector cells were readily detectable in tumors from the groups treated with CAR-NK cells alone or SeNPs. (Fig. S7a). Analysis revealed that the combination treatment led to a significant reduction in tumor growth. Histological analyses of the liver, spleen, lung, and kidney revealed no obvious histopathological features after the different treatments, indicating that the engineered selenium-containing nanoparticle offered greater safety based on a biorthogonal reaction combined with CAR-NK cell immunotherapy. Assessment of several serum biochemical indices, including ALT, AST, ALP, LDH, UA, and Crea, found no significant difference between untreated tumor-bearing mice and different experimental groups (Fig. S8a, b).

Conclusion

In this study, we successfully constructed a selenium-containing bioorthogonal delivery system. To address the limitations of CAR-NK cell therapy in solid tumors, particularly poor tumor homing and infiltration. To overcome these barriers, we developed a multifaceted strategy centered on ManNAl-SeNPs. Our approach initiates with metabolic glycoengineering, which introduces bioorthogonal chemical reporters. This engineering establishes a specific, artificial recognition system between the effector and target cells. Consequently, under the guidance of bioorthogonal click chemistry, the targeting precision and tumor tissue penetration capability of N3-CAR-NK cells are significantly augmented, leading to enhanced cytotoxic efficacy against solid tumors. Such a system can mitigate off-target toxicity at nontargeted sites, thereby minimizing potential impairment of the physiological function of normal cells. The stimuli-responsive TME delivery system can accumulate passively at the tumor site in vivo through the EPR effect. Following cellular uptake, the encapsulated synthetic sugar precursors ManNAl are metabolically incorporated into the glycocalyx of tumor cells through intrinsic glycometabolic pathways that is a ubiquitous cellular function. This metabolic engineering artificially introduces DBCO groups specifically onto the tumor cell surface. The resulting DBCO groups enable highly specific and N₃ groups engineered on CAR-NK cells via a bio-orthogonal click reaction, thereby promoting targeted immune recognition and cytotoxicity. Moreover, the cleavage of the diselenide bond in the strong redox environment of the TME generates selenite, which can downregulate the expression of HLA-E at the tumor site and act as an immune checkpoint inhibitor, thereby synergizing with the antitumor immune response. Therefore, bioorthogonal chemistry combined with a selenium nanocomposite delivery system is a safer, more efficient and nontoxic strategy. Building upon natural immune recognition, a targeting receptor was introduced through metabolically glycoengineered CAR-NK cells, an attractive non-genetic engineering approach that enhances target recognition and therapeutic efficacy in vivo. This combined strategy holds broad application prospects in the clinic and provides a novel approach to improve the efficacy of CAR-NK cells in solid tumors.
