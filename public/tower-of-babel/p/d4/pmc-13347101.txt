Prevalence, causes, management strategies, and outcomes of secondary interventions during long-term follow-up after endovascular aneurysm repair: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Hyo Jun Kim, Hyung Sub Park, Jinsol Jung, Ji Young Lee, Myung Yup Hyun, Jonghyuk Park, Taeseung Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThis study was conducted to evaluate the prevalence, causes, management strategies, and outcomes of secondary interventions after endovascular aneurysm repair (EVAR) in a Korean cohort.MethodsWe retrospectively reviewed 242 patients who underwent standard EVAR for infrarenal abdominal aortic aneurysm at Seoul National University Bundang Hospital between 2008 and 2023. Clinical and anatomical parameters were analyzed. Secondary intervention indications, management, and outcomes were documented. Kaplan-Meier analysis estimated freedom from intervention and survival, and Cox regression identified predictors of mortality.ResultsDuring a mean follow-up of 61.4 ± 45.8 months, 54 patients (22.3%) underwent 70 secondary interventions. The most common indications were Type II endoleaks with sac growth (24.1%), graft limb occlusion/stenosis (20.4%), Type IB endoleaks (11.1%), and Type III endoleaks (9.3%). Type II endoleaks were primarily treated with transarterial embolization, but many required repeated interventions or open conversion. Type I and III endoleaks were managed with stent graft extensions, endoanchors, or revision EVAR. Freedom from secondary intervention was 73.3% at 5 years and 57.5% at 10 years. Aneurysm-related survival was high (95.3% at 10 years), but overall survival declined to 42.5%, mainly due to comorbidities like malignancy, pneumonia, and cardiovascular disease. Multivariate analysis identified age, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, hospital stay, and aneurysm diameter as independent mortality predictors, with dyslipidemia being protective.ConclusionSecondary interventions after EVAR were required in over 20% of patients, primarily for Type II endoleaks. While aneurysm-related survival remained high, overall survival was limited by comorbidities. Lifelong imaging surveillance and management of comorbidities are essential for optimizing EVAR outcomes.

INTRODUCTION

Endovascular aneurysm repair (EVAR) is now widely accepted as a standard treatment for abdominal aortic aneurysm (AAA). In the United States, EVAR accounts for approximately 80% of AAA repairs, and in Korea, it comprises more than half of all AAA treatments [12]. Although early outcomes of EVAR are favorable, with lower perioperative morbidity and mortality compared to open surgical repair, concerns remain regarding its long-term durability. A substantial proportion of patients require secondary interventions, most commonly due to stent graft-related complications such as endoleaks, graft migration, or aneurysm sac enlargement. Randomized trials and observational studies have reported reintervention rates in the range of 20%–30% over intermediate to long-term follow-up after EVAR [34].

These elevated rates demand lifelong surveillance, driving up healthcare costs and placing a significant burden on patients’ quality of life. Beyond device durability, patient anatomy significantly influences long-term EVAR outcomes. An additional challenge arises from anatomical features common in Korean patients, such as angulated proximal necks, tortuous iliac arteries, and frequent involvement of the common iliac arteries (CIA) [5]. These factors often require procedures performed outside the device manufacturers’ instructions for use (IFU). Studies in Korea have shown that a significant proportion of patients do not meet IFU criteria, particularly due to neck angulation [6].

Data specifically examining long-term outcomes of EVAR in Korean populations, particularly with respect to secondary interventions, remain limited. This study aimed to evaluate the prevalence, causes, management strategies, and outcomes of secondary interventions after EVAR in Korean patients over a long-term follow-up.

METHODS

Ethics statement

The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Seoul National University Bundang Hospital (No. B-2507-987-115). The requirement for informed consent was waived.

Study population

We conducted a retrospective cohort study at Seoul National University Bundang Hospital, including all patients who underwent standard EVAR for infrarenal AAA between January 2008 and December 2023. Both elective and emergency cases were eligible for inclusion. Fenestrated or chimney techniques were not used for the primary repair. Patients who underwent non-standard index procedures (e.g., physician-modified endografts [PMEGs]) were excluded, as were those with isolated iliac aneurysms or insufficient postoperative imaging. Among 353 patients treated during the study period, 242 met the criteria for final analysis. Patients were excluded if they underwent non-standard techniques (e.g., PMEGs), had isolated iliac aneurysms, or lacked postoperative imaging follow-up.

Endovascular aneurysm repair procedure

Procedures were performed in a dedicated operating room or hybrid suite under general anesthesia, local anesthesia, or monitored anesthesia care, depending on patient status and the anesthesiologist’s discretion. Device selection and implantation techniques were individualized according to the aortic and iliac morphology and lesion location. Adjunctive procedures, including internal iliac artery (IIA) embolization and use of iliac branch devices, were selectively performed in patients with aneurysm extension to the iliac bifurcation or when IIA preservation was required.

Completion angiography was performed in all cases to confirm accurate stent graft deployment, seal integrity, branch patency, and the presence or absence of endoleak.

Surveillance protocol

Post-EVAR surveillance included contrast-enhanced CT angiography (CTA) or duplex ultrasonography at 1 month, 6 months, and 12 months postoperatively, and annually thereafter. If abnormal findings such as sac growth or the presence of endoleak were identified, the follow-up interval was shortened.

Clinical and imaging data collection

Demographic variables included age, sex, body mass index (BMI), smoking history, and comorbidities: hypertension, diabetes mellitus, dyslipidemia, coronary artery disease, cerebrovascular disease, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), chronic kidney disease, dialysis dependence, and peripheral arterial disease. Preoperative anesthetic risk was classified using the American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status classification. Medication history (antiplatelet use) and surgical history (prior aortic or abdominal operations) were also documented.

Morphological data were extracted from preoperative CTA: maximum aneurysm diameter, proximal neck diameter and length, infrarenal and suprarenal neck angulation, involvement of the CIAs, and CIA diameters and lengths.

Secondary intervention data included the indication (endoleak type, sac enlargement, limb occlusion, or stenosis), management strategy (both endovascular and open), and outcomes. For patients undergoing multiple reinterventions, each event was recorded separately.

Mortality outcomes were obtained from electronic medical records, national death registry, and follow-up visits. Deaths were categorized as aneurysm-related or non-aneurysm-related.

Definitions

Secondary intervention was defined by any secondary procedure performed after the index EVAR that was directly attributable to the aneurysm or the implanted endograft, including treatment of complications that may compromise aneurysm exclusion, or sac stability. Non-aneurysm-related interventions, such as access site complications or procedures unrelated to the endograft, were not considered secondary interventions. Aneurysm-related mortality was defined by death due to aneurysm rupture, procedure-related complications, or sequelae of secondary intervention. Sac growth was defined as an increase of ≥5 mm in maximal aneurysm diameter compared with the postoperative baseline measurement, confirmed on serial follow-up imaging over a 6- to 12-month interval. Intervention for Type II endoleak was performed only when this criterion was met. All the other parameters were defined according to previously published reporting standards [78].

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were summarized as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range), as appropriate, based on data distribution. Categorical variables were expressed as counts and percentages. Between-group comparisons for categorical variables were performed using the Pearson chi-square test or Fisher exact test, and comparisons of continuous variables were conducted with the Student t-test or the Mann-Whitney U-test.

Survival curves for all-cause mortality, aneurysm-related mortality, and freedom from aneurysm-related secondary intervention were estimated by the Kaplan-Meier method and compared using the log-rank test. Univariate Cox proportional-hazards regression was used to identify predictors of secondary intervention and mortality. Variables with a univariate P < 0.25 were entered into the multivariate Cox model. Hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported.

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics ver. 27.0 (IBM Corp.). A P-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Baseline demographics and aneurysmal morphology

A total of 242 patients underwent standard EVAR for infrarenal AAA during the study period. Secondary interventions were required in 54 patients (22.3%). Of these, 12 patients required an additional reintervention, and 3 patients underwent a third reintervention, yielding a total of 70 secondary interventions (Fig. 1).

The cohort was predominantly male (88.0%) with a mean age of 73.5 ± 7.9 years. The most prevalent comorbidity was hypertension (72.3%), followed by diabetes mellitus (24.0%), dyslipidemia (24.4%), and coronary artery disease (24.4%). Cerebrovascular disease (18.6%), chronic kidney disease (11.2%), and peripheral arterial disease (7.9%) were less frequent. COPD was present in 7.0% of patients, and 2.5% were on dialysis at baseline.

Comparison between the reintervention group (n = 54) and the non-reintervention group (n = 188) showed no significant differences in age, sex, BMI, smoking history, or prevalence of comorbidities (Table 1). ASA classification was also similar between groups, with the majority classified as ASA II–III. Antiplatelet use was more common in the reintervention group (66.7% vs. 55.3%), but this did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.137).

Preoperative aneurysm morphology is shown in Table 2. The mean maximum aneurysm diameter was 57.7 ± 14.7 mm and did not differ significantly between groups. Likewise, proximal neck diameter (23.9 ± 3.5 mm) and neck length (34.5 ± 15.4 mm) were not significantly different.

Common iliac involvement was significantly more frequent in the reintervention group (63.0% vs. 32.4%, P < 0.001). Furthermore, the right CIA length was significantly shorter in the reintervention group (39.6 ± 9.9 mm vs. 43.9 ± 12.6 mm, P = 0.008).

Device distribution included Cook Zenith Flex (84.7%), Gore Excluder (7.4%), and Medtronic Endurant (5.4%), with no significant differences between groups. Due to this imbalance, device-specific comparisons were not feasible. Notably, procedures performed outside the IFU were common, occurring in 45.0% of the total cohort. Half of the reintervention group (50.0%) and 43.6% of the non-reintervention group were outside the IFU, but this difference was not statistically significant (P = 0.406).

Causes of secondary intervention

A total of 70 secondary interventions were performed in 54 patients. The mean time to first secondary intervention was 30.5 ± 38.7 months.

The most common indication was Type II endoleak with sac growth (n = 13, 24.1%), followed by graft limb occlusion or stenosis (n = 11, 20.4%). Proximal or distal sealing zone failures also contributed substantially, including Type IB endoleaks (n = 6, 11.1%), Type III endoleaks (n = 5, 9.3%), and Type IA endoleaks (n = 4, 7.4%).

Less frequent causes included endotension, graft infection, aortoenteric fistula, and abdominal compartment syndrome, each occurring in isolated cases (Table 3).

Management strategies and outcomes

Management and outcomes by indication are summarized in Table 4.

Type IA endoleak

Four patients developed proximal Type IA endoleak. Management included proximal extension graft insertion, PMEGs using a fenestrated graft with renal scallop, fixation with endoanchors, and open repair. One patient developed thrombotic occlusion after proximal extension and required open conversion. The patient treated with PMEG later developed a sac growth, also leading to open repair. Both patients who underwent open conversion subsequently remained free of further events.

Type IB endoleak

Six patients required reintervention for distal Type IB endoleak. Four underwent iliac limb extension with or without IIA embolization. While 3 remained stable, one developed sac growth and was lost to follow-up. Another patient treated with left IIA embolization and limb extension also showed sac growth but did not return for further management.

Type II endoleak with sac growth

This was the most frequent cause of secondary intervention (n = 13). Initial management was usually transarterial coil embolization of branch vessels, such as the inferior mesenteric artery (IMA) or the iliolumbar arteries. However, durability was limited: more than half of the patients had recurrent sac growth despite embolization.

Several required repeated procedures, including histoacryl embolization, transarterial embolization, or right IIA embolization with stent extension. Two patients required a third reintervention (Fig. 2). One patient ultimately presented with rupture after progression to Type IB endoleak, necessitating open repair. Another underwent laparoscopic IMA ligation but later required open conversion due to persistent sac growth.

Type III endoleak

Five patients developed Type III endoleak due to device component separation or limb disjunction (Fig. 3). Two were treated with redo-EVAR, and 2 underwent open conversion. All remained stable after reintervention.

Limb occlusion/stenosis

Eleven patients developed graft limb occlusion or stenosis, accounting for 20.4% of all reinterventions. Management strategies included femoral–femoral bypass (n = 5), balloon angioplasty with stent insertion (n = 5), and thrombectomy (n = 1). All patients had satisfactory outcomes without recurrence.

Survival outcomes and predictors of mortality

Kaplan-Meier analysis revealed a freedom from secondary intervention of 73.3% at 5 years and 57.5% at 10 years (Fig. 4). Aneurysm-related survival rates were 97.6% at 5 years and 95.3% at 10 years (Fig. 5A). Patients without secondary intervention had significantly higher aneurysm-related survival compared with those requiring reintervention (5-year, 97.6% vs. 90.2% and 10-year, 95.3% vs. 87.3%; P = 0.015). Overall survival was substantially lower. At 5 years, survival was 92.6% in the non-reintervention group compared with 88.0% in the reintervention group. At 10 years, survival declined sharply to 66.2% and 42.5%, respectively (P = 0.021) (Fig. 5B).

The most common causes of death were malignancies (lung, bile duct, bladder cancer), followed by pneumonia and myocardial infarction. Notably, aneurysm rupture accounted for only a small fraction of deaths.

Univariate and multivariate Cox regression identified several predictors (Table 5). Independent risk factors were age (HR, 1.07; 95% CI, 1.04–1.11; P < 0.001), lower BMI (HR, 0.90; 95% CI, 0.84–0.96; P = 0.002), COPD (HR, 3.04; 95% CI, 1.55–5.97; P = 0.001), longer hospital stay (HR, 1.02; 95% CI, 1.01–1.04; P < 0.001), and larger maximum aneurysm diameter (HR, 1.02; 95% CI, 1.00–1.03; P = 0.026). Dyslipidemia (HR, 0.46; 95% CI, 0.24–0.88; P = 0.019) was identified as a protective factor.

DISCUSSION

In this single-center, 15-year retrospective study, secondary intervention after EVAR was relatively common, occurring in 22.3% of 242 patients. A subset required multiple reinterventions, underscoring the ongoing challenges of long-term EVAR durability.

Our findings are consistent with previous long-term prospective trials. In the EVAR-1 trial, approximately 20% of patients required reintervention during extended follow-up [9]. The DREAM and OVER trials similarly demonstrated that while EVAR offered early survival benefits compared with open repair, these advantages diminished over time, and reintervention rates were significantly higher in the EVAR groups [1011]. More recent meta-analyses and large retrospective cohorts have reinforced these results, reporting that 15%–19% of patients undergo reintervention by 5 years and 30%–33% by 10 years after EVAR [1213].

The most frequent indications for secondary intervention were Type II endoleak with sac growth, followed by limb occlusion/stenosis, Type I endoleaks, and Type III endoleaks.

Type II endoleaks were initially managed with transarterial embolization. However, success rates were limited, with more than half of patients experiencing recurrent sac growth. This often led to repeat procedures and, in several cases, eventually open surgical conversion. These findings are consistent with prior studies showing that transarterial embolization achieves limited long-term success, with high recurrence rates and minimal sac regression [14]. In our previous analysis of chronological sac changes after EVAR, we likewise observed recurrent sac enlargement following an initial stable or regressed phase after transarterial embolization [15]. Guo et al. [16], among others, have suggested that translumbar embolization provides superior durability, though even with this approach, definitive sac stabilization may not be guaranteed. More recently, laser-assisted transgraft embolization has shown encouraging results for managing persistent Type II endoleaks [1718]. Based on these observations, earlier consideration of translumbar or transgraft embolization may provide a more effective alternative when sac growth persists.

In contrast, Type I and III endoleaks were generally managed successfully with stent-graft extensions, endoanchors, or EVAR revision, with fewer recurrences. This supports current guidelines recommending prompt reintervention for Type I and III endoleaks, as these sealing zone failures, though less frequent, are more definitively correctable [19].

A frequent challenge when deciding on secondary intervention is persistent sac enlargement without a demonstrable endoleak, known as “endotension.” Current guidelines recommend stent-graft relining or open conversion when sac growth is clinically significant (≥10 mm) despite negative imaging. In our cohort, one patient underwent elective open repair for endotension and died in the immediate postoperative period from multiorgan failure. Given the physiologic burden of open conversion, often a prolonged operation under general anesthesia, the appropriateness of aggressive intervention for sac enlargement alone is uncertain. Based on our experience, we suggest reserving elective open repair for cases with symptoms or imaging features concerning impending rupture, rather than relying solely on sac diameter increase or growth rate in asymptomatic endotension.

Approximately half of our procedures were performed outside the IFU. Prior studies have consistently demonstrated that EVAR outside the IFU is associated with increased risk of sac enlargement, endoleak, and secondary intervention [20]. In our cohort, this finding must also be interpreted in the context of regional anatomical characteristics. Korean and East Asian patients frequently exhibit smaller neck diameters, shorter iliac landing zones, and more severe angulation compared with Western populations. Hwang et al. [6] found that only one-third of Korean patients met IFU neck criteria, with women particularly disadvantaged by smaller diameters and greater angulation. Hyun et al. [21] similarly reported complex neck anatomy in 51.2% of patients, with just one-third of juxtarenal aneurysms suitable for fenestrated grafts. More recently, Korean outcome data confirmed that EVAR outside the IFU is linked to increased graft-related adverse events [22]. These findings suggest that IFU noncompliance in Korean practice often reflects intrinsic anatomical constraints rather than aggressive case selection.

The difference between aneurysm-related and overall survival in our series is notable. While aneurysm-related survival exceeded 95% at 10 years—consistent with EVAR-1, DREAM, and OVER trials [91011]—overall survival was only 42.5%. Most late deaths were due to comorbidities, particularly malignancy, pneumonia, and cardiovascular disease. Similar findings have been reported previously: Goodney et al. [23] demonstrated that late deaths after both EVAR and open repair were predominantly non-aneurysm-related, while Oliveira et al. reported that cardiovascular causes accounted for nearly 40% of late deaths after EVAR [24]. These results emphasize that while EVAR effectively prevents aneurysm rupture, long-term mortality is determined largely by systemic comorbidities.

Our multivariate analysis further supports this observation, identifying COPD as a strong predictor of mortality. In contrast, dyslipidemia appeared to be protective, which may reflect the beneficial effects of statin therapy, as nearly all patients in our cohort with dyslipidemia were receiving statins [25]. These findings highlight that EVAR follow-up must extend beyond aneurysm surveillance. Comprehensive long-term care should integrate management of cardiovascular risk factors, pulmonary disease, and cancer screening in order to improve overall survival in this high-risk population.

This study has several limitations, since it is a retrospective, single-center analysis, which may limit generalizability. Because 84.7% of implants were Cook Zenith devices, the cohort was not sufficiently balanced in evaluating device-specific risks such as limb occlusion or stenosis. In addition, some patients with recurrent endoleaks were lost to follow-up, potentially underestimating adverse events. Management strategies also evolved over the 15-year study period. Future multicenter registries and prospective trials are needed to validate our findings and to refine strategies for secondary intervention, surveillance, and comorbidity management in diverse patient populations.

In conclusion, in this 15-year single-center study, secondary interventions after EVAR were required in 22.3% of patients, most commonly for Type II endoleak with sac enlargement. While aneurysm-related survival remained excellent, overall survival was substantially limited by comorbidities. These findings highlight that, in addition to lifelong imaging surveillance, comprehensive management of medical comorbidities is essential to optimize long-term outcomes after EVAR.
