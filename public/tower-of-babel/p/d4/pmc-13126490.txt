Distinct infectivity, pathogenicity, and adaptive evolution of clade 2.3.4.4 H5N2 avian influenza viruses in chickens in Taiwan
Authors: Li-Hsuan Chen, Yu-Ju Lin, Yu-Pin Liu, Wan-Chen Li, Yang-Chang Tu, Shu-Chia Hu, Wei-Cheng Hsu, Yen-Wen Chen, Fan Lee, Shan-Chia Ou, Ming-Chung Deng, Wei-Li Hsu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
High pathogenicity avian influenza virus (HPAIV) drives fulminant poultry epizootics with high mortality and significant production losses. In Taiwan, HPAIV clade 2.3.4.4c H5N2 has circulated since 2015 across different farms, providing an opportunity to assess strain-specific virulence over time. Four representative strains were examined: the index strain GS-2015 (January 2015) and three subsequent isolates—CK-2015 (December 2015), CK-2017 (2017), and CK-2018 (2018). Virulence was evaluated in the chicken model alongside genomic characterization. Overall, strains isolated after the index case exhibited higher mortality rates, shorter mean death times (MDTs), and lower minimal lethal doses compared with GS-2015. Marked variation in the 50 % bird lethal dose (BLD50) was observed across isolates: the index case showed the lethality only at the highest infection dose of 106 EID50, whereas progressively lower BLD50 values were determined for CK-2015 (104), CK-2017 (103.77), and CK-2018 (103.5). Noticeably, CK-2018 was most infectious (lowest BID50/BLD50) yet produced the longest survival and most prolonged shedding. CK-2017 was intermediate, with higher splenic loads than CK-2018. Across all strains, oropharyngeal shedding preceded and exceeded cloacal shedding. On 3 dpi, the index strain consistently yielded the lowest viral loads across most organs, except the pancreas. Phylogenetic analysis indicated these four strains exhibited divergence toward different evolutionary directions. Comparative genomic analysis revealed progressive accumulation of non-synonymous substitutions across all gene segments, particularly in the viral polymerase complex, NS1, and surface glycoproteins. Several substitutions occurred in functional domains of NS1, suggesting fine-tuning of polymerase activity, antigenicity, and immune evasion. In conclusion, even within the same genotype, clade 2.3.4.4c H5N2 displayed marked heterogeneity, with strains circulating for longer periods showing virulence, survival rates, and extended viral shedding changes in chickens. This virulence–infectivity trade-off not only poses challenges for outbreak control in poultry but also reflects a broader evolutionary trend in which pathogenicity is balanced with transmissibility.

Introduction

High pathogenicity avian influenza viruses (HPAIVs), a leading cause of epizootics in poultry, drive rapid mortality and substantial production losses worldwide, while sporadic zoonotic infections maintain public health risks. Over the past two decades, several H5 avian influenza virus (AIV) subtypes have emerged in Taiwan. The first outbreak of H5N2, identified as a Mexican-like low pathogenicity avian influenza (LPAI), was reported in 2003 (Lee et al., 2006), followed by a second wave in 2008 (Cheng et al., 2010). In early 2015, Taiwan experienced a simultaneous incursion of three Gs/Gd 2.3.4.4 H5 HPAIV subtypes—H5N2, H5N3, and H5N8 (hereafter referred to as Taiwan HPAI H5Nx viruses)—which caused severe losses in the poultry industry (Lee et al., 2016). The epidemic affected not only chickens but also waterfowl, resulting in the death of millions of geese, chickens, domestic ducks, and turkeys (Huang et al., 2016). Notably, domestic geese were disproportionately affected, with mortality exceeding 90 % during the initial outbreaks (Chang et al., 2016).

Phylogenetically, these viruses belong to clade 2.3.4.4c, with the initial H5N2 strain designated as genotype G0 (Group WOFHNEW, 2008; Huang et al., 2021). These G0-H5N2 viruses were subsequently detected in both domestic and wild birds in Taiwan. Molecular epidemiology suggests the G0 viruses likely arose from a single introduction, followed by reassortment with local LPAI viruses and persistence in poultry production systems (Li et al., 2020). After the explosive outbreaks of 2015-2016, transmission became localized (Li et al., 2024). Molecular evolution studies further indicate limited positive selection in the HA and NA genes, with most adaptive changes not reaching fixation (Huang et al., 2021), and constrained diversification compared with clade 2.3.4.4b viruses, consistent with ongoing but incomplete host adaptation (Li et al., 2020; Li et al., 2024). Importantly, the G0-H5N2 virus appears to be restricted to Taiwan, with no reports from other countries, highlighting unique ecological and agricultural drivers of its maintenance. Based on hemagglutinin (HA) genetics, the clade 2.3.4.4 H5Nx has evolved into several subgroups (namely a-h), caused infections in multiple species, and spread worldwide ((WHO) WHO 2022), and the pathobiological features of these viral subgroups have been comparatively analyzed.

Experimental infection studies have demonstrated that clade 2.3.4.4 H5Nx HPAIVs are uniformly lethal in chickens, but their transmissibility varies by subgroup. Previous work on clade 2.3.4.4 HPAIVs has shown that different subgroups can vary in pathogenicity in domestic poultry. For instance, H5N8 viruses isolated from chickens in Korea in 2014 were highly pathogenic but exhibited lower transmissibility and longer mean death times, highlighting how reassortment and genetic divergence can alter virulence characteristics (Song et al., 2015). Moreover, in one comparative study, viruses from clades 2.3.4.4a and 2.3.4.4c caused 80-90 % mortality with approximately 67 % transmissibility, whereas clade 2.3.4.4e viruses caused 100 % mortality and efficient transmission, and clade 2.3.4.4b viruses caused 100 % mortality but limited transmission to co-housed birds (Kwon et al., 2023). Regardless of subgroup, all infected chickens developed systemic infection, highlighting the high pathogenic potential of these viruses. On the other hand, studies in the United States during the 2015 outbreak examined the adaptation of clade 2.3.4.4 H5N2 viruses in chickens and mallards. Poultry isolates showed increased adaptation to chickens, with lower mean bird infectious doses than the wild-duck index virus but limited onward transmission, while maintaining high infectivity and transmissibility in mallards (DeJesus et al., 2016). These findings demonstrated that even within clade 2.3.4.4 HPAIVs, different subgroups and strains can display substantial variation in pathogenicity, transmission, and host adaptation.

Because previous studies have demonstrated that different strains of the clade 2.3.4.4 HPAIV differ markedly in pathogenicity, transmissibility, and host adaptation, it is essential to characterize the strain-specific biology of Taiwan’s endemic viruses, such as Taiwanese G0-H5N2 strains.

In Taiwan, clade 2.3.4.4c H5N2 (genotype G0) has circulated in the field for years. However, the pathogenicity of viruses that evolved during the peak prevalence period (2015-2018) remains poorly defined. To address this gap, the present study evaluated the pathogenicity and transmission dynamics of four representative G0-H5N2 strains isolated between 2015 and 2018 in a chicken model, and further analyzed genetic differences between the original and subsequent strains to identify signatures of viral adaptation.

Materials and methods

Preparation of virus stocks

All viral strains used in this study share the same gene constellation, with no evidence of reassortment among the analyzed segments; accordingly, all strains were classified as G0 genotype, as described by Huang et al. in 2021 (Huang et al., 2021). They were A/goose/TW/15010004/2015(abbreviated as GS-2015), A/chicken/TW/15120008/2015 (CK-2015), A/chicken/TW/17070010/2017 (CK-2017), and A/chicken/TW/18060001/2018 (CK-2018), which were collected in January 2015, December 2015, July 2017, and June 2018, respectively. Stock virus strains were obtained from clinical cases submitted to the Veterinary Research Institute and were passaged once in 9- to 11-day-old embryonated chicken eggs (CEE), except for CK-2015, which was passaged twice due to its lower initial yield. Each virus was identified, confirmed to have a hemagglutination titer greater than 25, and verified to be free of bacterial contamination. To determine the 50 % egg infectious dose (EID50), each stock virus was serially diluted 10-fold, and 0.1 mL of each dilution was inoculated into four 9-to 11-day-old CEE. The EID50 was calculated according to the method of Reed and Muench. Virus stocks were aliquoted into single-use volumes and stored at −80 °C to avoid repeated freeze–thaw cycles and preserve infectivity consistency across experiments.

Animals

The 4-to 6-week-old specific-pathogen-free (SPF) chickens were obtained from the Animal Drug Inspection Branch of the Veterinary Research Institute, Ministry of Agriculture. The chickens were divided into 17 groups and 4 birds in each group. The animals were housed in isolators in an ABSL-3 animal house of the Veterinary Research Institute. All the animal experiments were approved by the Institute Animal Care and Use Committee of the Veterinary Research Institute (Approval numbers: A06106 and A08001).

Pathogenesis and infection dose experiments

To determine the 50 % bird lethal dose (BLD50) and 50 % bird infectious dose (BID50) of each G0-H5N2 viral strain (GS-2015, CK-2015, CK-2017, and CK-2018), the titrated stock virus was diluted in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to final concentrations of 106, 105 104, and 103 EID50/0.1 mL immediately before inoculation. Each dilution was intranasally administered to four 4-to 6-week-old SPF chickens, which were monitored for the development of clinical signs or mortality. An additional group of four SPF chickens was inoculated with 106 EID50/0.1 mL and euthanized on 3 days post-inoculation (dpi) for tissue collection.

Post-inoculation sampling and observation

Oropharyngeal and cloacal swabs were collected from surviving chickens on days 1, 3, 4, 7, 10, and 14 dpi. Swabs were placed in PBS supplemented with gelatin and antibiotics and then stored at −80 °C until processing. The chickens were observed for 14 days post-inoculation; survivors were euthanized at the end of the observation period, and serum samples were collected for serological analysis. The presence of antibodies was assessed by hemagglutination inhibition (HI) assay, as described previously (Ou et al., 2013). HI titers were reported as reciprocal log2 titers, with a 3 log2 titer or below considered negative.

Tissue collection

While performing the necropsy, tissues including brain, trachea, lung, heart, liver, kidney, pancreas, spleen, intestine, and bursa of Fabricius were harvested from the additional group of SPF chickens intranasally infected with 106 EID50/0.1 mL and euthanized on 3 dpi. Each tissue sample was homogenized in PBS to yield a 10 % (w/v) suspension for subsequent analyses.

Quantification of viral RNA

RNA was extracted from serially diluted virus stocks to generate strain-specific standard curves for quantitative reverse transcription PCR (qRT-PCR), according to the protocol described by Spackman et al. (2002) with modifications (Spackman et al., 2002). Reactions targeting the matrix protein (M) gene were performed using the SuperScript® III Platinum® One-Step Quantitative RT-PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA) on a LightCycler 480 Instrument II (Roche, Basel, Switzerland). Viral RNA levels in swab and tissue samples were quantified using the established standard curves, and results were expressed as EID50 /mL (swabs) or EID50/g (tissues).

Sequence manipulation, alignment, and phylogenic tree reconstruction

Complete viral genome sequences for the viruses analyzed in this study were obtained as described by Huang et al. (2021). To assess the phylogenetic relationships of the four H5N2 strains used herein, the HA and matrix protein (M) genes, which were previously shown to be non-reassorted among Taiwan HPAI H5Nx viruses circulating between 2015 and 2018 (Huang et al., 2021) were selected. Complete HA and matrix protein (M) gene sequences of 289 isolates collected in Taiwan, along with 24 additional genetically related reference strains (Supplementary Table S1), were retrieved from the GISAID database. The complete open reading frames (ORFs) of HA and M genes were extracted via the NCBI Influenza Virus Sequence Annotation Tool (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/genomes/FLU/annotation/). Multiple sequence alignments were performed using the MAFFT online server (https://mafft.cbrc.jp/alignment/software/). Phylogenetic analyses of the HA and M genes were conducted with IQ-TREE (http://iqtree.cibiv.univie.ac.at/) under the maximum likelihood (ML) framework. The best-fit substitution models were selected using ModelFinder, and branch supports were evaluated using ultrafast bootstrap analysis (1,000 replicates). The resulting phylogenetic trees were visualized and annotated using Molecular Evolutionary Genetic Analysis (MEGA) software (version 7.0.26).

Sequence analysis for virulence and host adaptation markers

Full-length viral genome sequences (GS-2015: EPI2584832, and EPI750192-EPI750198; CK-2015: EPI1890978-EPI1890985; CK-2017: EPI1891767-EPI1891774; CK-2018: EPI1891871-EPI1891878) were analyzed using the FluMut database (https://flumut.org) and the NCBI FluSurver tool (https://flusurver.bii.a-star.edu.sg) to identify amino acid substitutions associated with virulence, receptor-binding specificity, and host adaptation. All detected markers were annotated according to established reference datasets.

Survival monitoring by Kaplan-Meier survival curves analysis

SPF chickens were intranasally inoculated with the indicated four strains of H5N2 viruses. Animals were observed at least once daily for 14 days post-inoculation (dpi). Survival data were plotted as Kaplan-Meier (K-M) curves stratified by group, and median survival time (MST) with 95 % CI was reported when estimable. Group differences were assessed by the log-rank (Mantel–Cox) test. Mean death time (MDT) was calculated from uncensored deaths within each group and presented alongside K–M metrics for comparability. Analyses were performed in GraphPad Prism version 9.4.1. (GraphPad Software, San Diego, California, USA).

Statistical analysis

One-way ANOVA with Tukey’s multiple comparison tests was used to evaluate viral loads in swab and tissue samples across virus-inoculated groups, as well as MDT comparisons. Survival curves were generated for each virus strain and inoculation dose, and statistical significance was evaluated using the log-rank (Mantel-Cox) test. Statistical tests for all analyses were selected according to data distribution, and significance thresholds are specified in the corresponding figure legends.

Results

Virulence characterization of four H5N2 strains in a chicken model

We first evaluated the virulence of four clade 2.3.4.4c H5N2 HPAIV strains (genotype G0) isolated from different poultry farms between 2015 and 2018 (Huang et al., 2021), based on mortality, 50 % bird infectious dose (BID50), 50 % bird lethal dose (BLD50), and MDT.

Remarkable differences in mortality rates were observed among the four strains. For GS-2015, chicken deaths were only observed at the highest inoculation dose (106 EID50), which translates to 75 % mortality with an MDT of 5 days. No mortality occurred in birds infected with this virus at lower doses (103-105 EID50). Molecular detection of the virus in oropharyngeal and cloacal swabs and serological testing revealed no evidence of viral shedding or seroconversion in birds infected with, indicating that infection was not established. In general, infection with strains emerging after the index case caused higher mortality rates and shorter MDTs compared with GS-2015. For example, CK-2015, isolated 12 months after the index case, exhibited markedly greater virulence. At 106 EID50, all chickens succumbed by 3 dpi (MDT: 3 days). At 105 and 104 EID50, once infection was established, deaths occurred within 3-4 dpi (MDTs of 3.3 and 3 days, respectively), representing the shortest survival times among the four strains. In the 10⁴ EID50 group, 75 % infected chickens survived, with one seroconverting (HI titer of 32). This strain was unique in that its BLD₅₀ (10⁴ EID50) was lower than its BID50 (104.5 EID50). In the CK-2017 infection group, MDT, similar to the mortality rate, followed a dose-dependent pattern, ranging from 4 to 5 days across inoculation doses of 106 to 103 EID50. Finally, CK-2018 had the lowest BID50 and BLD50 values among the four strains (103.5 EID50). Despite its high infectivity and lethality at doses ≥103 EID50, this strain was associated with the longest survival times; for instance, chickens inoculated with 10⁴ EID₅₀ had an MDT of 7.5 days (Table 1).

Survival profile analysis of four H5N2 strains in a chicken model

Next, Kaplan-Meier survival curves of the chickens infected with a single dose of each of the four viruses were generated. Survival analysis demonstrated that CK-2015 was the most virulent strain, inducing 100 % mortality by 3 dpi. The onset of mortality of CK-2015 occurred significantly earlier than the other three strains, as determined by the log-rank (Mantel-Cox) test (p < 0.05). In contrast, GS-2015 (index case) exhibited delayed lethality, with approximately 50 % of chickens surviving until the end of the 10-day observation period. CK-2017 and CK-2018 displayed intermediate virulence, with deaths occurring between 3 and 7 dpi, resulting in significantly longer mean survival times than CK-2015. Viral shedding in oropharyngeal and cloacal routes

We next measured shedding of these viruses in the surviving chickens. The viral RNA loads from oropharyngeal and cloacal swabs, collected on 1, 3, 4, 7, 10, and 14 dpi, was estimated by qRT-PCR. In oropharyngeal swabs (Fig. 3), viral shedding displayed clear dose-dependent dynamics across strains. Noticeably, viral shedding of GS-2015 could only be detected at the106 EID50 group, the highest infection dose until 7 dpi (Fig. 3A). Interestingly, due to the high mortality rate, viral shedding of CK-2015 infected chicken at 106 EID50 only can be detected on 1 dpi with low yield. Similarly, at 105 EID50 infection, low virus shedding was observed during the first 2-3 dpi, but the short survival time limited detection beyond 4 dpi; at 104 EID50, shedding was sporadic and at lower titers (Fig. 3B). Of note. CK-2017 produced robust oropharyngeal shedding at all tested doses, with high titers at 106-105 EID50 and detectable virus even at 10⁴ EID50 on survived chickens, despite greater variability in titers and duration (Fig. 3C). Distinct phylogenetic relations of the four G0-H5N2 strains

To assess the evolutionary relationships of our isolates, phylogenetic trees were constructed using concatenated coding sequences of the HA and M genes from Taiwanese HPAI H5Nx viruses collected between 2015 and 2018. Interestingly, the four G0 H5N2 strains segregated into distinct subclades within the Taiwanese clade 2.3.4.4c lineage, each clustering with contemporaneously isolated strains. Specifically, the initial GS-2015 strain was clustered together with the H5N2 viruses isolated in 2015-2016. Moreover, CK-2015, isolated in December 2015, belonged to a subclade distinct from GS-2015, suggesting that the G0 viruses introduced in early 2015 evolved rapidly as they spread among domestic poultry. The CK-2017 and CK-2018 strains tested in this study showed that they were in the subclade of chicken isolates isolated between 2017 and 2018. Sequence variations in viral genomes

Subsequently, complete genomes (all eight segments) of the four G0 strains were selected for further in-depth analysis of the nucleotide and deduced amino acid levels. Comparison of the three successor strains (CK-2015, CK-2017, and CK-2018) with the index strain GS-2015 revealed a broad distribution of non-synonymous substitutions across all gene segments (Supplementary Table S2).

As summarized in Table 3 (also in Supplementary Table S2), the genes encoding the polymerase complex carried a large number of changes: PB2 (19 substitutions), PB1 (9 substitutions), PB1-F2 (8 substitutions), and PA (13 substitutions). Additional substitutions were detected in HA (19), NA (17), PA-X (9), M1 (1), M2 (5), NS1 (9), NS2 (6), and NP (2) (Table S2). Specifically, the 9 substitutions found in PB2 were: 13Q/H, 21T/A, 79S/I, 80K/R, 102N/K, 106I/T, 191E/K, 249E/K, 338V/I, 339K/N, 355R/K, 368R/Q, 456N/D, 460M/V, 521T/A, 532S/A, 590G/S, 659N/D, and 660K/R, of which 590G/S, a residue adjacent to the known 590/591 compensatory motif. PB1 substitutions (75E/D, 152S/L, 181I/V, 182I/T, 372M/V, 453A/S, 581E/D, 622G/A, 739E/D) were scattered across the polymerase domains but not at known adaptation hotspots. Notably, a change at residue 66N/S in PB1-F2 was observed, which has been associated with enhanced virulence and apoptosis in mammalian systems (Conenello et al., 2007; Varga et al., 2011). PA protein had substitutions at 5V/M, 49S/L, 62I/L, 63V/I, 85T/A, 141G/E, 214L/I, 254N/S, 354I/V, 396D/N, 497N/K, 531R/K, 532S/L, and 675N/S residues. Of note, multiple substitutions were found on the two surface glycoproteins; HA (5V/L, 6L/I, 9A/V, 10V/M, 11K/E, 30K/E, 87I/T, 99A/T, 102V/A, 139P/T, 143T/S, 152P/S, 157S/P, 167V/A, 233S/V, 243G/D, 253D/E, 531I/V, and 543A/V). Nine out of 17 were clustered within the receptor-binding and antigenic sites (e.g., 99, 102, 139, 143, 152, 157, 167, 233, and 243) of HA. Another enveloped protein NA accumulated seventeen substitutions (40N/T, 312Q/H, 135G/S, 143K/N or R, 188R/A, 236D/N, 306S/G, 336A/V, 334R/K, 344S/T, 358A/V, 385T/K, 444N/E, 460T/K, 467M/V, and 468P/S), including at positions 143 and in the 300-400 region, which may affect HA-NA balance and facilitate efficient shedding (Gaymard et al., 2016; Gao et al., 2021). NP contained two substitutions (417S/N and 482S/N), despite not having known adaptation markers. Mutations in M segment led to amino acid substitutions in M1 (207K/N), and M2 (10P/L, 26L/I, 27V/I, 43L/F, and 51I/V). The two substitutions identified in M2 at positions 26 and 27 were located within the transmembrane domain that could influence ion channel activity (Wang et al., 2011). In NS1, several substitutions (79M/I, 123T/I, 125D/G, 127N/I, 139D/G, 140L/I, 170T/A, 209D/G, and 213P/L) clustered within the RNA-binding domain (Kim et al., 2021), potentially altering dsRNA binding and interferon antagonism. NS2, the other gene transcribed from NS segment, had 6 substitutions (12I/M, 14M/T, 36E/G, 52M/V, 56H/Y, and 85H/N). While none of these are canonical adaptation markers, 5 out of 6 of the variations in the N-terminal domain (at region 12-56) could subtly modulate nuclear export efficiency, potentially contributing to differences in replication kinetics (Long et al., 2019).

Beyond these gene-level observations, substitution patterns differed across isolates. CK-2015 (2015, 11 months after index) accumulated substitutions across PB2, PB1-F2, and HA but fewer changes overall compared with subsequent isolates. Of those, the presence of PB1-F2 66S is noteworthy as a known virulence-associated marker. CK-2017 displayed broader variation across polymerase genes (PB2, PB1, and PA) and HA, including multiple substitutions in the receptor-binding and antigenic sites of HA. CK-2018 accumulated the highest number of substitutions across nearly all segments, including in PB2 (G590S), HA (multiple antigenic/RBD positions), NA (head domain and stalk), NS1 (RNA-binding domain), and NS2 (N-terminal). Functionally, these changes are consistent with high infectivity, prolonged shedding, and extended survival, despite reduced lethality.

Discussion

In late 2014, clade 2.3.4.4c H5N2, H5N3, and H5N8 viruses infected poultry farms in Taiwan, resulting in devastating outbreaks across the industry. Phylogenetic analyses in the current study showed that the HA and M genes of four G0-H5N2 strains clustered closely with spatiotemporally related Taiwanese H5Nx strains (Fig. 1). Moreover, these Taiwanese H5Nx viruses were closely related to strains isolated in Japan and South Korea during 2014-2015, suggesting a common origin (Lee et al., 2016; Huang et al., 2021). Following this initial wave, Taiwan’s poultry industry experienced multiple subsequent incursions of HPAIVs, including clade 2.3.4.4 H5N6 in 2017 (Chen et al., 2018), clade 2.3.4.4b H5N2 in 2021 (https://www.aphia.gov.tw/theme_data.php?theme=NewInfoListWS&id=20117), and clade 2.3.4.4b H5N1 since 2022 (https://www.aphia.gov.tw/theme_data.php?theme=NewInfoListWS&id=20553), yielding at least 12 circulating H5Nx genotypes. Because infectivity, transmissibility, and pathogenicity vary across clade 2.3.4.4 viruses and hosts, systematic, genotype-matched evaluations are needed.

The pathogenicity of diverse clade 2.3.4.4 HPAIV subgroups has been studied in various avian species (Song et al., 2015; Kwon et al., 2023; DeJesus et al., 2016). However, these studies involved viruses H5 paired with different NA subtypes and varied internal gene constellations, and were performed under heterogeneous laboratory conditions, complicating direct comparisons within the same genotype across time. To address this gap and better understand local viral evolution, we examined four sequential H5N2 genotype G0 isolates collected between 2015 and 2018. This within-genotype approach enabled direct comparison of virulence and genetic changes over time. As evidenced in the chicken model of this study, the later isolates exhibited lower minimal lethal doses, shorter mean death times, and extended shedding duration relative to the index strain (GS-2015). Specifically, the survival analyses demonstrate distinct virulence patterns among the four H5N2 strains (Fig. 1, Fig. 2). CK-2015 was the most virulent in chickens, inducing rapid and complete mortality at high titer of inoculum, though survival improved at lower doses due to reduced infectivity. GS-2015 showed the lowest virulence in chickens, requiring the highest titer of inoculum to cause lethality, and even then, partial survival was observed on 14 dpi. CK-2017 displayed a unique profile, with intermediate lethality at high doses (106 and 105 EID50) but enhanced efficiency in establishing infection at low doses (104-103 EID50), resulting in reduced survival compared with CK-2015 under those conditions. CK-2018 combined high infectivity with delayed mortality, producing the longest survival times despite a low BID50 and BLD50. Overall, these findings highlight that all four strains belong to the same genotype, but the waterfowl isolate (GS-2015) showed lower mortality and shedding titers in chickens than the three chicken isolates (Table 1, Table 2), indicating that the dose-dependent virulence dynamics might reflect distinct pathogenicity between chickens and waterfowls.

Comparable observations have been reported for clade 2.3.4.4 HPAIVs isolated outside Taiwan. Pantin-Jackwood et al. (2016) demonstrated that different clades of Gs/Gd lineage H5Nx isolates showed different infection and shedding dynamic characteristics in ducks (Pantin-Jackwood et al., 2016). Another report compared the infectivity, pathogenicity and transmission in chickens and ducks by different clade 2.3.4.4 H5N2 viruses isolated in the U.S. in 2014 and 2015. The results showed that the isolates from 2015 had lower BID50 values than the 2014 wild-duck index virus, indicating enhanced infectivity in chickens despite inefficient chicken-to-chicken transmission (Pantin-Jackwood et al., 2016). Moreover, the waterfowl-origin clade 2.3.4.4 H5N2 virus, without prior adaptation, required a high infectious dose, exhibited poor transmission, and failed to induce seroconversion in chickens or turkeys; however, H5N2 viruses isolated from chickens or turkeys showed the opposite effects (Spackman et al., 2016; Bertran et al., 2016). Consistently, in our data, the chicken-origin H5N2 from 2015 had shorter MDT and earlier shedding than the 2017–2018 chicken isolates, suggesting that, with field circulation, virus-host interactions may shift toward higher infectivity and persistence with moderated virulence. This evolutionary trajectory facilitates long-term maintenance in domestic flocks and wild bird reservoirs.

Genomic analysis revealed a progressive accumulation of substitutions across the polymerase complex, HA, NA, and NS1, consistent with adaptive diversification that may enhance viral persistence and transmission in poultry populations (Table 3). Substitutions in the viral RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (RdRp composed of PB2, PB1, and PA) are well recognized as key determinants of host adaptation, replication efficiency, and pathogenicity in influenza A viruses (Gilbertson et al., 2023; Te Velthuis and Fodor, 2016). Previous study investigated gene mutations between a later outbreak stage of chicken clade 2.3.4.4 H5N2 isolate and an early stage wild bird index strain. The results showed that amino acid substitutes in HA, NA, NP, PB1 and entire polymerase complex involved in viral infectivity, pathogenicity, and transmission dynamics in different hosts (Youk et al., 2021). In the current study, some of the non-synonymous changes in viral RdRp that we observed in later H5N2 isolates overlap functionally with known virulence or host-adaptation markers (Table 3 and Table S2). For example, the PB1-F2 N66S substitution is well documented to enhance pathogenicity and suppress type I interferon responses in mammalian models (Conenello et al., 2007; Varga et al., 2011). Although these isolates do not possess the canonical mammalian‐adaptation PB2 markers such as E627K or D701N, the presence of PB2 G590S is of interest. Mutations in or around the PB2 590/591 region have been implicated in compensatory adaptation in the absence of E627K or D701N in several avian influenza viruses adapting to mammalian or avian hosts (Sun et al., 2020; Lee et al., 2020). Similarly, Mänz et al. (2016) observed that PB2-G590S polymorphisms in avian influenza viruses correlate with increased replication fitness even without E627K (Manz et al., 2016). In the presence of G590S and others in this region may contribute to enhanced polymerase activity or altered host interaction, consistent with the phenotype of increased infectivity and prolonged viral shedding in the later isolates.

The two major surface glycoproteins, HA and NA, drive viral evolution and host adaptation, with coordinated changes in receptor binding and enzymatic activity shaping immune escape and transmission (Long et al., 2019; Youk et al., 2021). As shown in Table S2, many of the HA substitutions were detected in these G0 H5N2 viruses, including 99A/T, 102V/A, 139P/T, 143T/S, 152P/S, 157S/P, 167V/A, 233S/V, and 243G/D, within or near antigenic loops and the receptor-binding domain. Previous studies have shown that substitutions in these regions in H5 viruses (e.g. at positions ∼131-156) can affect antibody binding, fusion pH, and virus replication capacity (Luczo and Spackman, 2024; Rudneva et al., 2013; Zhang et al., 2023). These parallels suggest our observed changes may modulate antigenicity and help explain the prolonged viral shedding and persistent circulation of later isolates. Moreover, multiple substitutions in the NA head domain—particularly around position 143 and within the 300-400 region—could shift the HA-NA functional balance, facilitating more efficient shedding without conferring drug resistance. Such shifts have been documented in work showing that natural variation in NA activity influences how HA evolves under selection pressure (Liu et al., 2024) and that changes in NA catalytic or receptor-binding regions can compensate for HA variation or adjust virion release dynamics (Youk et al., 2021; McAuley et al., 2019).

Another noteworthy finding is the NS1 125D/G substitution, which is associated with the emergence of an alternative splice variant, NS3. Prior work showed that an H5N2 virus (A/goose/Taiwan/01031/2015; hereafter NS1031) encoding NS3, a novel NS1 variant, more efficiently suppresses protein kinase R activation, enhancing replication and virulence in mammalian hosts (Chung et al., 2022). Although the avian impact of NS3 in the HPAIV H5N2 background remains undefined, its presence in our isolates underscores additional functional diversity within clade 2.3.4.4c H5N2. Notably, the genotype G0 viruses analyzed here were contemporaneous with NS1031 in 2015, yet NS1031 was rapidly displaced in the field, suggesting that the virus expressing NS3 did not confer a sufficient fitness advantage in poultry.

It is notable that the index isolate was recovered from a goose, whereas the three subsequent isolates were chicken-derived. This host shift possibly imposed poultry-specific pressures favoring traits that increase fitness in chickens—e.g., adjustments in HA receptor avidity/glycan engagement, NA stalk changes that rebalance HA–NA activity in terrestrial poultry (Liu et al., 2024), and NS1 substitutions that more effectively antagonize chicken innate immunity. These mechanisms plausibly underlie the observed pattern of higher infectivity, prolonged shedding, and—in some lineages—slower time-to-death, consistent with selection for transmission efficiency rather than maximal acute lethality. However, links between sequence change and phenotype here are inferential, not causal: we did not perform reverse-genetics or targeted functional assays, and formal codon-level selection analyses were not undertaken. A broader phenotypic panel (e.g., HA receptor binding/activation pH, NA activity, NS1-mediated innate-immune antagonism, polymerase kinetics) would better resolve mechanisms but is beyond the scope of this study. Finally, several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the limited number of lineages analyzed (four isolates), together with potential survivor/endpoint bias—where viral shedding was primarily measured in surviving birds—may have overlooked alternative evolutionary trajectories and underestimated late-stage shedding in highly virulent strains. Second, the qRT-PCR assay used in this study may have limited sensitivity for detecting low levels of viral RNA, and the HI assay may underestimate low-titer antibody responses. Therefore, low-level infections, particularly at lower inoculation doses, cannot be completely excluded.

In summary, clade 2.3.4.4c H5N2 genotype G0 viruses circulating in Taiwanese poultry (2015-2018) exhibited marked within-genotype heterogeneity, as later isolates trended toward higher infectivity with longer shedding and survival despite attenuated lethality. This pattern suggests a virulence–infectivity trade-off that may favor flock persistence and increase the risk of subclinical transmission. Although derived from Taiwanese H5N2 viruses, these findings have broader relevance to poultry production worldwide, as they highlight that surveillance and control programs should not rely solely on mortality or severe clinical signs, but also consider transmission-related traits such as infectivity and shedding duration. Therefore, early detection, routine surveillance, timely reporting, and strong on-farm biosecurity remain essential for limiting the long-term circulation of emerging HPAIV variants.
