Plasma‐Assisted and Solvent‐Free Ultra‐Fast Synthesis of Gold and Gold Oxide Ultra‐Small Nanoparticles and its use as Multi‐Sensing Platforms
Authors: Jordi Sans, Samuele Colombi, Marc Arnau, Adrián Fontana‐Escartín, Leonor Resina, José Garcia‐Torres, Carlos Alemán
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn this work, a plasma‐assisted and solvent‐free synthesis of gold (Au) and gold oxide (Au2O3) nanoparticles (NPs) is explored. Accordingly, only the precursor (i.e., HAuCl4 powder) is exposed to the N2:O2 plasma gas mixtures, selected to promote its reduction to Au (HAuCl4‐to‐Au0) and/or favor its further oxidation to Au2O3. As a result, the production of highly crystalline spherical Au ultra‐small NPs (USNPs) of 2.7 ± 0.7 nm and Au2O3 edged pyramidal‐like NPs of 18 ± 5 nm is obtained in very short times (i.e., 10 s−1 min), showing excellent stability without the need to add any ligands. To investigate the potential features of the synthesized particles, they are loaded into alginate hydrogels and tested as multi‐detection sensor platforms of H2O2 and NADH in front of several interferents. Although both samples present excellent electrochemical sensing activity (as compared to Au NPs synthesized through the Turkevich method), Au2O3 NPs stand out for its high analytical sensibility toward H2O2 (153.1 and 16.8 µA mm −1 cm−2 for single and multiplexing detection, simultaneously); and capacity to measure NADH in real human urine and low detection limits produced by Escherichia coli (7.5 × 103 CFU mL−1). Herein, a green, easy, and fast method is proposed to produce stable Au USNPs and Au2O3 NPs, showing great potential for the catalytic and biomedical fields.Keywords: Au USNPs, Au2O3 NPs, multiplexing detection, plasma technologies, sensing biological environments

Introduction

Due to their biological compatibility, high chemical stability, and unique electrochemical and optical properties, gold nanoparticles (Au NPs) have been intensively used for developing cutting‐edge new technologies ranging from environmental catalysis to electronics.[ 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 ] More specifically, Au NPs are of particular interest in the biomedical field as they have been successfully used for sensing,[ 6 , 7 ] cancer therapy,[ 8 , 9 ] smart drug release[ 10 , 11 ] and bio‐imaging,[ 12 , 13 ] among others.[ 14 , 15 ]

Within this context, developing appropriate synthesis routes to refine the structure of such Au NPs (reaching in some cases an atomic precision)[ 16 ] has become essential to maintain the high rate of development.[ 17 , 18 ] Some representative examples can be found in the control of the morphology of the Au NPs,[ 19 ] including the production of edged Au NPs to enhance the number of catalytic binding sites,[ 20 ] or the possibility to produce Au clusters, the so‐called ultra‐small nanoparticles (USNPs: 2–100 atoms, <2 nm) showing luminescence properties.[ 21 , 22 ] However, most of the syntheses reported raise several concerns when applied in the biomedical field due to their complexity, environmental impact, and potential toxicity associated with the precursors and techniques used.

In this sense, the so‐called Turkevich method merits special attention, as it only uses chloroauric acid (HAuCl4) citrate and ascorbic acid (low toxicity) triggered by temperature (100 °C) or UV irradiation. Although it cannot reach the USNPs domain, the size can be controlled over a wide range (9–120 nm).[ 23 ] Nonetheless, research toward greener bio‐inspired routes has attracted increasing attention considering non‐toxicity and environmental friendliness aspects.[ 24 ] Thus, plant extracts,[ 25 ] algae,[ 26 ] fungi[ 27 ] and biomolecules[ 28 ] have been successfully used to obtain and stabilize Au NPs. Despite of the promising results obtained, such studies remain highly specific, requiring further optimization for settling down and/or scaling up the processes. Moreover, most of the aforementioned routes tend to prioritize their green nature rather than the final properties arising from their refined structure, hindering their resulting capabilities in the biomedical field.[ 29 ]

This statement gains special relevance when dealing with the design of electrochemical sensors based on Au NPs. Usually, such sensors can be seen as electrochemical catalysts whose properties (e.g., sensitivity, selectivity, and limit of detection) will ultimately depend on the design of specific binding sites and electrochemical activity (i.e., charge transfer rate and electrical conductivity). Herein, the proper balance between the controlled generation of vacancies (increased binding site density) and the crystallinity (reduction of undesired charge loss phenomena) is crucial. Accordingly, the approach presented in this work relies on developing a green synthesis technique capable of achieving such a degree of structural control to revisit the current challenges of electrochemical sensors, showing multi‐sensing capabilities and avoiding false‐positives due to the presence of interferents.

Motivated by the maturation of the technology, non‐thermal plasma‐assisted synthesis techniques have also gained remarkable popularity in the scientific community due to their green nature and accurate size control (i.e., 2–10 nm, as the plasma induces a negative charge on the NPs, avoiding particle agglomeration), while high crystallinity and versatility are achieved.[ 30 ] Besides, non‐thermal plasmas are inherently ligand and solvent free, which allows a wide variety of experimental operating conditions and different plasma set‐ups (i.e., atmospheric/non‐atmospheric, gas composition, plasma jets, and pulsed/continuous operation, among others).[ 31 , 32 , 33 , 34 ] Such versatility and accuracy at the nanoscale has promoted the synthesis of new polymeric systems like ultra‐thin films in conjunction with the development of new associated characterisation techniques.[ 35 ] Accordingly, for the most conventional atmospheric‐plasma synthesis of Au NPs, the precursor is directly injected into the plasma gas or dissolved in liquid solutions.[ 36 , 37 ] Anyhow, as compared to the Turkevich method (i.e., mainly restricted to aqueous solutions), the solvent can be chosen depending on the final application, favoring superior control on the NPs shape and size and, even, promoting the one‐step synthesis of complex systems (e.g., Au NPs on carbon nanotubes and specific drug loading and functionalization).[ 38 ] On the other hand, the application of O2 plasma gas on Au surfaces can produce a thin layer and small NPs of gold oxides (Au2O3 and Au2O).[ 39 , 40 ] Although they have been scarcely explored due to their poor stability, gold oxides show higher catalytic activity toward carbon monoxide oxidation, as compared to metallic Au.[ 40 , 41 ]

With the aim to control the chemical composition and structure of Au‐based NPs, in this work, we study the synthesis of metallic Au and gold oxide NPs by means of a non‐thermal and low‐pressure plasma system developed for surface functionalization. Herein and as a differentiating feature, we expose only the solid precursor (HAuCl4 powder) to the plasma treatment. The effect of the experimental conditions of the treatment (i.e., time, gas composition, and plasma power) has been elucidated by characterizing the samples through high‐resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) and X‐ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS). The Turkevich method has been used as a reference for the synthesis of Au NPs. Finally, the most representative Au‐based NPs have been loaded into alginate (Alg) hydrogels to test the resulting electrochemical sensing properties over nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NADH), which is a biomarker used to detect bacterial infection,[ 42 ] and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), which participates in many oxidative biochemical reactions,[ 43 ] as multi‐sensing platforms capable of operating under real biological environments.

Results and Discussion

Plasma‐Assisted Reduction of HAuCl4‐to‐Au0: Effect of the Plasma Power, Pressure and Time

Plasma‐assisted synthesis approaches are governed by the stochastic interactions between plasma and the precursors (i.e., capture of electrons, ions, and their subsequent recombination). Therefore, the plasma conditions might not show a proportional response on the resulting products due to the generation of diverse reactive species and cascade reactions that lead to different reaction pathways. Accordingly, the effect of the plasma conditions (i.e., power (33%, 66% and 100%), pressure (0.4, 0.6, and 0.8 mbar), and time (1s and 1 min)) was initially explored for the reduction of HAuCl4‐to‐Au0. Within this context, N2 was chosen as the plasma gas to favor the reduction of Au+3‐to‐Au0 through electron capture while reducing secondary reactions involving ion interactions (i.e., nitrogen plasma ions and Cl– ions). To do so, HAuCl4 attached to the carbon tape (see Experimental Section and Scheme 1 for the complete synthesis set up) was analyzed by X‐ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS). The most representative spectra obtained are presented in Figure 1a–d, corresponding to the XPS high‐resolution regions of Au 4f, Cl 2p, C 1s, and N 1s, respectively. To support the XPS assignation and provide further details on the reaction mechanism, Figure S1 (Supporting Information) shows the XPS high‐resolution region of O 1s (sample treated at 33% power, 0.8 mbar and 1 min), while Table 1 lists the atomic ratios of the most relevant samples studied.

As it can be seen in Figure 1a, all the conditions explored result in the reduction of Au3+‐to‐Au0. This is confirmed by the peaks at 84.1 eV (Au0 4f7/2) and 87.8 eV (Au0 4f5/2), as compared with the peaks at 85.3 eV (HAuCl4 4f7/2) and 89.0 eV (HAuCl4 4f5/2).44 Other much less intense peaks (Au organic and AuCl) are also observed in some samples, coming from secondary reactions between the precursor and the plasma gas. The efficiency of the reduction process (ηR) as a function of plasma conditions is presented in terms of Au distribution (in atomic percentage; Figure 1e,f) among the different Au‐related species (Au0, Au3+, Au organic, and AuCl). Interestingly, increasing the plasma power does not initially result in an increment of the ηR but promotes undesired ionic interactions both from the precursor and the plasma gas. More specifically, instead of the Cl2(g) (Table 1: ∑Cl/∑Au <4 while in HAuCl4 the Cl/Au ratio ∼ 4) produced at low plasma power (33%), Cl– ions interact with the carbon substrate and gold (Figure 1b: Cl 2p3/2 peaks at 199.6 and 198.6 eV, respectively).[ 44 ] Accordingly, ∑Cl/∑Au is ∼ 4 for such samples (Table 1). Figure 1c, which corresponds to the C 1s XPS high‐resolution region, supports the conclusions presented (i.e., C─Cl specie at 286.1 eV). On the other hand, the standard NH3 + radicals (14% at with respect to gold) found at 402.3 eV (33% power, 0.8 mbar; Figure 1d) are replaced by the incorporation of the nitrogen into the substrate (peak at 401.7 eV attributed to N in an organic matrix)[ 45 ] when 100% power at 0.6 mbar is used. Such energetic interaction results in the complete degradation of the carbon tape when exposed to 100% plasma power and 0.8 mbar of N2, as seen by both the peak at ≈101 eV attributed to the Co/Fe 3s (the complete survey with the characteristic XPS lines is provided in Figure S2, Supporting Information) of the XPS holder (inset of Figure 1a) and the lack of N 1s measured signal.

Overall, such ionic interaction properly explains the behavior obtained in Figure 1e,f, suggesting that the mildest experimental conditions (i.e., plasma pressure and plasma power) are the optimum to achieve the higher ηR. The trends obtained have been further corroborated by exploring more experimental plasma conditions (Figure S3, Supporting Information). Nonetheless, to reduce undesired ionic interactions but favor a high density of electrons, the same experiment was carried out at 1/6 of the time (i.e., 10 s) and maximum plasma power and pressure conditions. Surprisingly, the sample treated with only 10 s (see dashed line) shows the highest ηR with a production of 72.2% of Au0 and no secondary compounds. Therefore, herein we experimentally demonstrate an ultra‐fast treatment for the reduction of Au+3‐to‐Au0, and thus (as seen in the following section), for the synthesis of Au NPs. Overall, we hypothesize that the oxidation process is governed by electrons as a very few content of nitrogen plasma‐derived species (i.e., N, N+, and N2 +) are found. Moreover, the experimental results obtained suggest that such species might be generated on the carbon tape, rather than the gold surfaces. Nonetheless, it is worth highlighting the fact that ion bombardment is still necessary to produce the nanoparticles from the microscopic grains of the powder precursor.

Plasma‐Assisted Synthesis of Gold Oxide: Effect of O2 Concentration

To produce gold oxides, N2: O2 mixtures (ranging from 0 to 100%) were considered as the plasma gas because of the fact that both electron and ion (i.e., oxygen) capture were needed. Therefore, and according to the conclusions reached in the section above, the plasma power and pressure were set at 100% and 0.8 mbar for 1 min. The high‐resolution XPS regions of Au 4f and O 1s are presented in Figure 2a,b. As can be seen, a new Au3+ specie at 86.2 eV (Au 4f7/2) and 89.8 eV (Au 4f5/2), which shifted 5.7 eV from Au0 and 0.9 eV from HAuCl4, can be clearly observed, confirming the unequivocal formation of Au2O3.[ 39 , 40 ] The presence of Au2O3 is further confirmed by the O 1s peak at 529.5 eV (Figure 2b).

XPS studies of the samples treated with N2 + O2 plasma gas. a) High‐resolution spectra of Au 4f (peaks for 4f7/2 in eV): 84.1 (Au), 85.3 (HAuCl4), 86.2 (Au2O3), 88.5 (AuOx). b) High‐resolution spectra of O 1s (in eV): 531.5 (C═O), 533.0 (C─O), 534.5 (H2O/O2 diffusion), 529.5 (gold oxide). c) Au atomic distribution depending on the O2 plasma gas concentration. d) O atomic distribution depending on the O2 plasma gas concentration.

Careful analysis of the evolution of the atomic distribution of Au and O species when increasing the O2 content in the N2:O2 plasma gas, which is presented in Figure 2c,d, allows elucidating part of the oxidation mechanism. At low O2 concentrations (i.e., ≤ 33%), the oxygen tends to diffuse (O 1s: 534.5 eV) or adsorb as H2O (in case of the pure N2 plasma due to the high hydrophilicity of HAuCl4, the O 1s peak being shifted 0.3 eV in that case). However, the abrupt decrease in the O2 diffusion observed in Figure 2d (O2 concentration of 67%) suggests that the diffused O2 is capable of oxidizing Au0 (see also the abrupt increase in the Au2O3 production in Figure 2c), which is in agreement with the results reported in the literature.[ 46 ] Accordingly, the measured experimental Au / O atomic ratio of Au2O3 obtained for such O2 concentration (0.65 in Table 1) is extremely close to the theoretical value (i.e., 0.67). Nonetheless, at higher O2 concentrations, the O2 diffusion dominates again over the oxidation kinetics, resulting in a less stoichiometric Au‐rich Au2O3 (i.e., Au / O atomic ratio of Au2O3 of 0.85).

Therefore, herein it can be observed that the crucial role of O2 plasma species (i.e., O, O2, O3, O+, O2 +, O4 +, O‐,O2 ‐, O3 ‐, and O4 ‐) in such diffusion and oxidation of gold. Unluckily, we have not been able to determine the most relevant species in the mechanism. Nonetheless, we can discard a priori the bombardment of NO species (produced in N2:O2 plasma mixtures)[ 47 ] as very few nitrogen species are observed (<10% at with respect to gold; not shown). Furthermore, we can also discard a priori an oxidation mechanism mediated by ozone (O3). This is because Klyushin and co‐workers,[ 48 ] by means of atmospheric pressure XPS, reported the appearance of bands at 83.3 eV attributed to low‐coordinated atoms that appear on the Au surface as a result of the restructuring during oxidation mediated by O3. In our study, we do not observe such bands (see the absence of shoulders below 84 eV in the inset of Figure 2a), further supporting the oxidation mechanism proposed.

An interesting feature is the presence of the extremely rare +4 oxidation state of Au in AuOx (peaks at 88.5 and 92.1 eV, which correspond to the Au 4f7/2 and Au 4f5/2, respectively).[ 40 , 49 , 50 ] This has been attributed to crystal lattice distortions arising from the O2 diffusion and the subsequent oxidation reaction. The presence of “exotic” Au oxidation states has been reported in the literature using synthesis techniques based on radio frequency and/or reactive sputtering using O2(g).[ 40 , 51 , 52 ] Certainly, the Au4+ state might be an excellent binding site promoting enhanced sensing activity. Thus, plasma technology is presented as a fast and green technique to produce gold and gold oxide (i.e., 1 min and solvent‐free).

Structural Characterization of Nanoparticles

As sketched in Scheme 1, NPs were detached from the carbon substrate by ultrasonication and suspended in ethanol for their morphological and structural analysis. Dynamic light scattering (DLS) measurements revealed the presence of two particle distributions at 98 ± 15 nm (attributed to aggregates) and 6 ± 2 nm (original NPs). Furthermore, the synthesis of NPs was completely confirmed by high‐resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) exhaustive analyses. Figure 3 compares the HRTEM study of the Au NPs synthesized using the conventional Turkevich method with those obtained using 0.8 mbar of N2 at 33% of plasma power for 1 min and 0.8 mbar of N2 at 100% of plasma power for 10 s. While the typical sizes of the Au NPs obtained with the Turkevich method range from ≈9 to 30 nm, the plasma‐assisted Au NPs are much smaller, falling in the range of ultra‐small nanoparticles (USNPs). Hence, samples treated for 1 min (inset in Figure 3b) show an average diameter of 3.0 ± 1.2 nm, while the average size of the samples treated for 10 s (inset in Figure 3c) is 2.7 ± 0.7 nm. This is a relevant finding because USNPs typically exhibit a set of new and unique properties, such as fluorescence (noble metal USNPs) or quantized spin (magnetic USNPs).[ 22 , 53 ] It is also important to highlight the superior stability of the suspensions in front of other methods, as no ligands, functionalization procedures, or specific liquids are used. This is a well‐established behavior in non‐thermal plasma synthesis approaches, as NPs are primarily negatively charged, preventing particle agglomeration.[ 30 ] Furthermore, it is worth highlighting the fact that, as the NPs are produced using only a solid precursor (i.e., the route is solvent‐free) storing them in a liquid suspension is not a requisite. That is, samples can be saved adhered in the carbon tape, avoiding further long‐term agglomeration problems.

HRTEM analyses of Au NPs prepared by means of the Turkevich method a,d,g) and of Au USNPs synthesized applying a N2 plasma (33%, 0.8 mbar) plasma for 1 min b,e,h) and 100%, 0.8 mbar of N2 for 10 s c,f,i). Different high magnification areas are presented to show that the analyses carried out are representative.

On the other hand, plasma‐assisted USNPs also present a superior crystalline structure as compared to Turkevich NPs. Figure 3a,d,g shows that Turkevich Au NPs exhibit a less ordered crystal growth with multiple characteristic crystallographic planes (002) and (111) at 2.08 and 2.35 Å (among other reflections, as seen by the numerous spots in the Fourier‐Transformed insets). Contrarily, USNPs are mostly single nanocrystals with a well‐defined growth toward specific directions such as (200) at 2.06 Å (Figure 3e) or (111) at 2.33 Å (Figure 3h,f,i). Apart from the size differences, the time of the plasma‐treatment does not affect the crystalline structure of the resulting USNPs, stressing out the possibility to produce them using ultra‐short treatment time. Finally, another distinctive structural feature is the presence of the characteristic superficial amorphous layer (d ∼ 1.6 nm, inset Figure 3a) associated to the citrate functionalization of NPs inherent in the Turkevich synthesis route. Although such a layer is desired to stabilize the NPs and avoid agglomeration, it might also be responsible for decreasing the electrochemical properties of NPs (i.e., less electrical conductivity and less exposed binding sites).[ 54 , 55 ] Certainly, the aforementioned superior stability of the particles synthesized by plasma allows neglecting such functionalization step, thus increasing their electrochemical response. Overall, the plasma‐assisted synthesis of Au USNPs presents several advantages regarding both the low‐impact, green nature and simplicity of the technique, but also the possibility to obtain USNPs with superior crystallinity.

Figure 4 shows the HRTEM studies carried out for samples prepared at 100% plasma power, 0.8 mbar of N2: O2 (33: 67%) for 1 min. As suggested by the XPS studies (Figure 2), the low magnification image in Figure 4a reveals the presence of both Au USNPs (apparently not affected by the presence of O2 plasma gas, Figure 4b) and highly‐crystalline stable Au2O3 NPs (average size: 18.21 ± 4.61 nm). To further confirm the generation of Au2O3, a rigorous analysis of the Fourier‐Transformed spots (Figure 4c,d, insets) has been carried out considering both the spacing of the equivalent reciprocal lattice points with their stereographic projections (details in the Experimental Section). Overall, the presence of Au2O3 is demonstrated by the characteristic crystallographic planes at (262), (620), and (511) at 1.20, 1.98, and 2.09 Å (among all the others). Interestingly, some of the observed reflections show a small divergence in the lattice spacing (e.g., (331) and (131) at 2.34 and 2.46 Å, respectively, deviate 0.13 and 0.07 Å, respectively), supporting the presence of the specific Au4+ (AuOx) detected by XPS. On the other hand, Au2O3 NPs exhibit a clear truncated orthorhombic bipyramidal‐like morphology with well‐defined edges (Figure 4d, inset).

Au and Au2O3 Loading into Alg Hydrogels for NADH and H2O2 Sensing

Aqueous Au USNPs and Au2O3 NPs suspensions were prepared and loaded into Alg hydrogels at (≈0.5% at), as is described in the Experimental Section. Zeta potential measurements (i.e., −15.7 ± 3.2 and −21.5 ± 4.5 mV for Au USNPs and Au2O3 NPs; respectively) allowed ensuring that the suspensions were stable at least during the preparation time (always below 30 min). It is worth noticing that although such values are not above ± 30 mV, the zeta potentials measured are quite high considering that no additives nor special solvents are used. The successful particle loading in the resulting systems, hereafter denoted Alg/Au‐USNPs and Alg/Au2O3‐NPs, respectively; was corroborated by XPS analyses (not shown) and SEM inspection (Figure S4, Supporting Information), further confirming the stability of the suspensions (see detailed discussion in the caption of the figure). The performance of both Alg/Au‐USNPs and Alg/Au2O3‐NPs hydrogel composites as electrochemical sensors of NADH and H2O2 has been evaluated by progressively increasing the concentrations of such analytes from 0 to 5 mm and 0–10 mm, respectively. Unloaded Alg hydrogels have been used as the control.

Cyclic voltammograms recorded for the Alg, Alg/Au‐USNPs, and Alg/Au2O3‐NPs hydrogels of the solutions with NADH concentrations ranging from 0 to 5 mm are shown in Figure S5a–c (Supporting Information). As it can be seen, the area of the recorded voltammograms is significantly higher for Alg/Au‐USNPs and Alg/Au2O3‐NPs than for Alg, the incorporation of USNPs and NPs significantly enhances the electrochemical activity of the bare hydrogel, increasing the signal area by 736% and 1148% for Alg/Au‐USNPs and Alg/Au2O3‐NPs, respectively. Furthermore, the current density at the reversal potential (j max) increases with the concentration of NADH, which, according to previous observations, has been ascribed to the electrocatalyzed oxidation of NADH to NAD+.[ 56 , 57 ] However, this variation is more evident for Alg/Au2O3‐NPs than for Alg/Au‐USNPs and, especially, Alg. These results indicate that the electrocatalytic activity toward NADH oxidation is higher for Au2O3 NPs than for Au USNPs. This feature has been confirmed by the calibration plots obtained by representing j max against the NADH concentration (Figure 5a). Thus, the linear regime is better defined for Alg/Au2O3‐NPs than for Alg/Au‐USNPs (R2 = 0.9701 and 0.7289, respectively). The analytical sensitivity (AS), which corresponds to the slope of the linear regime, and the limit of detection (LOD), which has been calculated assuming a signal‐to‐noise ratio of 3, of the NADH detection are displayed in Table 2 . Discarding Alg/Au‐USNPs due to the poor linearity of the calibration plot, the LOD obtained for Alg/Au2O3‐NPs (0.40 mm) is comparable to the values reported for poly(3,4‐ethylenedioxythiophene) integrated in surgical sutures and meshes (0.49 and 0.18 mm, respectively).[ 57 , 58 ]

The voltammograms of solutions without and with 1, 2, 5, and 10 mm H2O2 for Alg, Alg/Au‐USNPs, and Alg/Au2O3‐NPs hydrogels are displayed in Figure S6a–c (Supporting Information). As occurred for NADH solutions, for each H2O2 concentration, the highest current response is exhibited by Alg/Au2O3‐NPs. The calibration plots (Figure 5b), which were obtained using three independent repetitions for each H2O2 concentration, indicate that the current density (in absolute value) at the final potential (j min), –0.80 V, linearly increases with increasing H2O2 concentration for both Alg/Au‐USNPs and Alg/Au2O3‐NPs. Instead, the linearity is lost for Alg hydrogel, indicating that the reduction of H2O2 is not occurring without Au USNPs and Au2O3 NPs. The analytical parameters (Table 2) indicate that the LOD of Alg/Au2O3‐NPs is almost six times lower than that of Alg/Au‐USNPs, while the AS is more than two times greater for the former than for the latter. Overall, results evidence that the oxygen ions of Au2O3 NPs in conjunction with the higher oxidation states of gold play a key role as sites in the electrocatalytic oxidation of NADH and, especially, the electrocatalytic reduction of H2O2.

Another important aspect is the comparison of the electrocatalytic properties of Au USNPs obtained using the plasma‐assisted synthesis and Au NPs prepared by means of the Turkevich method. It is worth mentioning that in a recent study, Alg hydrogels loaded with Au NPs derived from the Turkevich method were prepared using the same gelation protocol as in this work and subsequently used for H2O2 detection.[ 6 ] It is worth noting that the AS performance is considerably better for Alg/Au‐USNPs than for such hydrogels, as is reflected by the obtained calibration plot (included in Figure 5b), while the LODs are comparable. Thus, the AS and LOD for the hydrogel with Turkevich‐derived NPs were 22.61 µA mm −1 cm−2 and 0.91 mm, respectively (Figure 5b: pink).[ 6 ] Such a difference can be directly attributed to the higher crystallinity of USNPS and the lack of the amorphous layer at the interface, resulting in a higher electrochemical activity due to both enhanced charge conductivity and higher binding site accessibility.

In order to examine the influence of the presence of several bioanalytes on the electrocatalytic performance of the prepared particles, the multiplexing detection of NADH and H2O2 in Alg, Alg/Au‐USNPs, and Alg/Au2O3‐NPs has been carried out. For this, solutions containing simultaneously the two analytes (with concentrations ranging from 0.5 to 10 mm) have been considered. The cyclic voltammograms and the calibration plots obtained using Alg/Au‐USNPs and Alg/Au2O3‐NPs are displayed in Figure 5c–f, while those recorded for the Alg hydrogel control are shown in Figure S7 (Supporting Information).

Table 2 shows that the analytical parameters obtained for the simultaneous detection of NADH and H2O2 at Alg hydrogel electrodes are even worse than those reached for solutions with a single analyte, suggesting a direct interference between the two analytes. This has been further confirmed by the results obtained for Alg/Au‐USNPs (Table 2), even though in this case the increase of j max and the decrease of j min with the increasing concentration of NADH and, especially, and H2O2, respectively (Figure 5c), is much clearer than for Alg. Besides, Alg/Au2O3‐NPs preserve the highest electrocatalytic activity, showing the highest sensitivity and the lowest LOD for the two analytes. Thus, the properties of Au2O3 NPs prevail over the interference of the two analytes (Table 2).

Exhaustive examination of the experimental results allows elucidation of the interference sources. First, the possibility of interaction between reaction products has been assessed. The NADH oxidation‐reduction reaction is not completely reversible in most cases.[ 59 ] In this sense, there are different pathways for the oxidation reaction, which, among other products, might result in the temporary production of H2O2. Accordingly, the experiments carried out discard the presence of intermediates (arising from the incomplete reversibility of the NADH reaction) as we do not observe any additional oxidation‐reduction peaks at the CV spectra (Figure 5c,e). Moreover, for the specific case of the hypothetical production of H2O2 it is worth highlighting that in the multiplexing experiments we have tested the conditions of 0.5 mm of NADH and 0 mm of H2O2. As it can be seen in the insets of the Figure 5c,e there is a small increment in the oxidation peak of H2O2 which might be attributed to the production of H2O2. However, such a difference is very small, falling inside the experimental error and suggesting that their contribution is minimal, at least in the concentration range of the study. Interestingly, the increment in oxidation current is slightly lower for Au2O3‐NPs, suggesting that the electrochemical activity and the multiple oxidation states of the particles promote a higher reversibility of the NADH reaction. On the other hand, the competitive adsorption represents a more probable source of interference. That is because NADH (C21H29N7O14P2) is a relatively large molecule with an average mass of 664.5 g mol−1 (oxidized), while H2O2 is a small molecule (34.01 g mol−1). Therefore and although both bio‐analytes compete for the same number of binding sites, the geometrical conformation and size of NADH require more space around, affecting more the binding of H2O2. Accordingly, the diminution of the AS is lower, in percentage, for NADH (from 0.81 to 0.31 µA mm −1 cm−2) than for H2O2 (from 153.1 to 16.8 µA mm −1 cm−2); see Figure 5 and Table 2.

Such a result is further corroborated by the electron configuration of the gold atom. Because of relativistic considerations, the gold closed‐shell 5d10 orbitals are no longer inert and can interact with other elements, including gold atoms in molecules (including the coordination of several gold atoms with carbon) or clusters. Such a phenomenon was named as “aurophilicity” by Schmidbaur in 1988.[ 60 ] Furthermore, such destabilization of the 5d orbitals might promote the oxidation of gold mainly to Au3+, or in the case of our study, a further oxidized Au4+. Back to the sensing studies presented, Au4+ might take special advantage of such enhanced coordination with Au4+···C····Au4+ resulting in an especially effective site for the NADH oxidation (similarly, the same mechanism might be used to justify the +3 oxidation state). Overall, exploiting such oxidation states represents a promising strategy for the development of specific and robust sensors in front of interferents.

Finally, in order to ascertain the stability of the nanoparticles in the hydrogels, after electrochemical measurements, Alg/Au‐USNPs and Alg/Au2O3‐NPs samples were freeze‐dried and analyzed by XPS. As presented in Figure S8 (Supporting Information), both samples show the Au 4f characteristic peaks attributed to the Au0 and Au2O3. Interestingly, the number of Au4+ sites attributed to the gold AuOx has increased for the latter, suggesting a possible stabilization mechanism of the Au2O3 due to the presence of the aqueous media and the voltage applied during the sensing tests. Hence, not only is the good adhesion of the NPs confirmed, but also the stability of the Au2O3 NPs, which explains the differences found in the electrochemical detection assays.

Proof of Concept: Au2O3 NPs for the Detection of NADH in Urine Samples and Bacteria Cultures

As a proof of concept, the detection of NADH by Alg/Au2O3‐NPs has been evaluated considering real fluids with the corresponding interferents. It should be noted that bacteria growth can be identified by detecting the level of NADH, which is a coenzyme present in the environment of all living cells. Accordingly, real‐time monitoring of NADH by electrochemical detection serves to anticipate bacterial infections, as it is shown by in vitro and ex vivo assays.[ 61 , 62 , 63 ] More specifically, Molina et al. distinguished[ 56 , 62 ] the formation of bacterial biofilms from the growth of normal eukaryotic cells by detecting the oxidation of NADH to NAD+ through CV. Eukaryotic cells present two major NADH pools, the cytosolic and the mitochondrial pools,[ 64 ] while aerobic respiration reactions in such cells take place in mitochondria. However, the mitochondrial double‐membrane is impermeable to NADH or NAD+ and, therefore, mitochondrial NADH levels are maintained even upon massive depletion of cytosolic NADH.[ 65 , 66 ] Instead, prokaryote cells, such as bacteria, do not have mitochondria and their respiration occurs in the cytosol or on the inner surfaces of the cell membrane. Therefore, as prokaryote cellular membranes are permeable to NADH and NAD+, a gradient is expected, and the extracellular detection of such species can be taken as an appropriated biomarker for monitoring growing bacterial infections.[ 56 , 57 , 58 , 63 ] Furthermore, it is well known that the level of NADH increases considerably when the urinary tract suffers from infection.[ 67 , 68 , 69 ]

The first tested fluid has been healthy (non‐infected) human urine, which contains a significant amount of interferents. Thus, in addition to water (95%), human urine includes urea, creatinine, uric acid, enzymes and co‐enzymes, carbohydrates, hormones, pigment, mucins, and inorganic ions (e.g., Na+, K+, Cl–, Mg2+, Ca2+, NH4 +, SO4 2–, and PO4 3–). To this urine sample, an increasing amount of NADH has been incorporated progressively (1, 2, 5, and 10 mm). The cyclic voltammograms recorded using Alg/Au2O3‐NPs are displayed in Figure 6a, which show how the current density at the reversal potential (j max) increases with the NADH concentration. The calibration plot, which is shown in Figure 6b, presents two linear regimes above and below 2 mm NADH. The AS and LOD, which are defined by the first regime, are 0.36 µA mm −1 cm−2 and 0.15 mm, respectively (Table 2), evidencing the potential of Alg/Au2O3‐NPs to detect infections in the urinary tract.

a) Cyclic voltammograms recorded using Alg/Au2O3‐NPs and b) calibration plot obtained from human urine samples with increasing concentrations of NADH. c) Cyclic voltammograms recorded using Alg/Au2O3‐NPs in a culture medium without and with bacteria.

As a second proof of concept, the concentration of NADH in extracellular media produced by the aerobic respiration reactions of bacteria has been determined using Alg/Au2O3‐NPs. For this purpose, Escherichia coli (E. coli) colony‐forming units (CFU) were cultured in Bacto Brain and Heart Infusion medium. The bacteria used for this purpose were ATCC 25922, a biofilm‐positive strain (B+). Colonies were incubated at 37 °C and 80 rpm for 24 h, maintaining conditions. Voltammograms were recorded considering media without bacteria (blank) and with 15 × 103 and 7.5 × 103 CFU mL−1 (Figure 6c). It is worth noting that the latter values are much lower than those typically observed in infected fluids (>1 × 108 CFU mL−1). As it was expected, the current density at the reversal potential (zoom image) increases with the number of bacteria, evidencing the release of NADH to the culture medium from bacteria respiration reactions. Results were performed in three series of triplicate showing an error <10% for all three points, thus ensuring the reproducibility of the proof of concept. These results, which are fully consistent with those observed in previous studies,[ 57 , 61 , 62 ] shows the potential of Alg/Au2O3‐NPs to detect not only bacteria infections but also to monitor the growth of bacteria in real‐time.

Advantages and Challenges of the Synthesis Route Proposed

Overall, the plasma‐assisted synthesis approach reported presents several technical advantages as compared to other plasma technologies. As presented in the introduction section, in most of the plasma‐assisted synthesis routes studied, the precursor is either dissolved or applied directly (spraying; implying that the precursor needs to be atomized) to the final material. Although it has some advantages, this kind of application presents some challenges, including the proper preparation of the precursor, avoiding plasma damage at the surface of application, stabilization issues (in case of the suspension approach) and/or increase of the complexity of the atmospheric plasma set ups for their direct application. In this sense, the use of solid precursor and production of particles adhered in the carbon tape avoids most of the aforementioned problems. More specifically, stabilization for particle storage is completely avoided. Furthermore, avoiding solvents and/or direct application of the precursor allows working with non‐atmospheric plasmas. Accordingly, the plasma produced in non‐atmospheric conditions is less energetic and is more controllable, thus achieving (as seen in this work) particles with higher crystallinity and more accurate morphologies. Interestingly, the unprecedented possibility to produce crystalline gold oxide particles, which requires a controlled interaction of oxygen ions with the precursor, supports such conclusions. Finally, it is worth highlighting that the commercial plasma set up used in this work is typically more accessible than other atmospheric plasma prototypes.

Although the technology is completely scalable, we have identified some challenges that need to be further assessed. First, the efficiency is still low compared to other approaches. Thus, the application of several plasma cycles needs to be studied. In this sense, we have explored the possibility of increasing the plasma exposition time. However and although we are using a non‐thermal plasma, some melting is observed (30 min exposition, not shown) attributed to resistive heating. Indeed, such melting avoids the formation of nanoparticles. Hence, we propose the use of pulsed plasmas as a solution to increase the yields. On the other hand, a further challenge to be assessed for scaling up the technology is achieving an accurate protocol to detach the particles from the carbon tape to prepare controlled suspensions. This is important when specific loads of particles want to be applied. Herein, the problem can be solved by calibrating the suspensions and diluting them if necessary. Anyhow, such methods might not be accurate enough requiring the characterization of the loading after the preparation. Both processes have been done in this work. Although this might not be a problem at a laboratory level, adding an extra step might reduce the viability of the synthesis route in large scale production. Therefore, the determination of an accurate protocol for detaching the particles would be extremely advantageous.

Conclusion

In summary, in this study, we propose a new synthesis route based on a non‐thermal and low‐pressure plasma set‐up capable of producing Au‐based nanoparticles from a solid precursor. More specifically, while under optimized conditions, N2 plasma gas produces pure and almost mono‐crystalline Au ultra‐small nanoparticles (Au USNP; ∼ 3 nm), the introduction of N2:O2 plasma gas mixtures results in the synthesis of crystalline and stable Au2O3 NPs (∼18 nm). Furthermore, such particles present some specific lattice distortions (i.e., AuOx regions) where an Au4+ oxidation state is measured. Based on the mechanistic studies carried out, the oxidation of gold is attributed to the diffusion of the plasma oxygen atoms throughout the Au crystal lattice.

As the inherent biocompatibility and green nature of the process (i.e., only the solid precursor is needed) does not restrict the lattice refinement of the produced particles (i.e., high crystallinity and stability, presence of exotic binding sites and/or accurate size control below 5 nm), Au‐based particles show a promising potential as multi‐sensing platforms for biological applications. To illustrate such feature and test their electrochemical activity, Au‐based particles loaded into Alginate hydrogels are capable of sensing NADH or H2O2 in presence of several interferents. Although both Au USNPs and Au2O3 NPs present a higher performance than Au NPs synthesized via the Turkevich method, Au2O3 NPs particularly stand out for their high sensibility and selectivity, being able to properly measure in real urine media or low concentrations of NADH directly produced by Escherichia coli (7.5 × 103 CFU mL−1). The presence of +3 and +4 gold oxidation states favors the stability and robust sensing of NADH in front of interferents; showing great potential for the design of further selective sensors.

Overall, the possibility to produce such stable Au2O3 NPs showing a remarkable electrochemical activity opens the door to their study, characterization, and use in a wide range of fields. As demonstrated and considering that the synthesis route proposed is accessible, green, and low time‐demanding (i.e., 10 s – 1 min), the use of Au2O3 NPs can be of potential interest specifically for the catalytic and biomedical fields.

Experimental Section

Plasma‐Assisted Synthesis of Gold and Gold Oxide Particles

≈2 mg of chloroauric acid (HAuCl4; Sigma–Aldrich) powder was fixated onto a circular carbon tape attached to a quartz microscope slide (Knittel Glass). Then, the dry sample was exposed to low‐pressure and non‐thermal plasma corona discharge Zepto equipment from Diener electronic. After purging and vacuuming the chamber, the plasma was generated at the desired plasma power (33, 67, 100%; total power 100 W), plasma gas composition (N2:O2 mixtures), pressures (0.4, 0.6, and 0.8 mbar, total pressure). The duration of the treatment was set to 10 s and 1 min. Finally, the carbon tapes were detached from the quartz slices and immersed in ethanol / aqueous media (1 mL). After the application of ultrasonication for 20 min, the carbon tapes were removed, obtaining the NPs suspensions. If needed, samples were re‐suspended to fulfill the desired concentrations. The complete synthesis route is graphically summarized in Scheme 1.

Synthesis of Gold NPs following the Turkevig Method

In this synthesis method, the size of the gold nanoparticles was regulated by the citrate / gold ratio. A solution of HAuCl4 (10 mg) in deionized water (100 mL) was prepared and heated to 100 °C. Once reached the temperature, the capping agent was added (3 mL of Na3Ct at 1%) in order to stabilize the nanoparticles. The sodium citrate acts as a reducing agent as well. The suspension obtained was left at 100 °C for 30 min under vigorous stirring. During this time, color changes were appreciated (i.e., from yellowish to purple and in the end to red wine color, typical of gold nanoparticles). Finally, the suspension was cooled to room temperature.

Characterization Techniques

Samples attached to the carbon tape were analyzed using X‐ray Photoelectron Spectroscopy (XPS) technique (SPECS system). The spectrometer was equipped with a high‐intensity twin‐anode X‐ray source XR50 of Mg/Al (1253 eV/ 1487 eV) operating with the Al anode at 150 W, placed perpendicular to the analyser axis, and using a Phoibos 150 MCD‐9 XP detector. The position of the stage was digitally controlled to ensure that the spot was in the same exact place during the whole treatment. The pass energy of the hemispherical analyzer was set at 25 eV, and the energy step of high resolution spectra was set at 0.1 eV. The pressure in the analysis chamber was always below 10−7 Pa, and binding energy (BE) values were referred to the C 1s π–π * peak (carbon tape) at 284.1 eV.

Morphological and structural studies were performed by dynamic light scattering (DLS) and high‐resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) techniques. To do so, samples were NPs were detached from the carbon tape (i.e., were ultrasonicated for 20 min) and suspended in ethanol (Shcarlab, purity >99.5%). DLS measurements were performed at room temperature on a NanoBrook 90Plus Zeta equipment. Particle size (DLS) was set to collect and average data every 3 min. Zeta potential measurements were carried out using the same equipment, using an AQ‐1321 cell. Accordingly, the samples were suspended and ultrasonicated (resembling the first step in the preparation of the hydrogels) in ultrapure milli‐Q water (Millipore) and 1 mm KCl water solution, respectively. Zeta potential (ELS) was obtained from averaging two series of 10 measurements to ensure representative data. All measurements were performed at room temperature. When needed, samples were re‐suspended and diluted to ensure proper data acquisition. High‐resolution transmission electron experiments were carried out in a JEOL JEM J2100 microscope, equipped with a LaB6 thermionic electron gun and operated at an accelerating voltage of 200 keV. Images were recorded using a Gatan Orius CCD camera. TEM sample preparation was achieved by placing droplets of ultrasonicated water (Milli‐Q) solution suspensions onto carbon‐coated grids. All particle size distributions were calculated by measuring 100 NPs for all cases, ensuring sample representability. Crystallographic plane assignation was performed by comparing FFT reciprocal lattice points with their stereographic projections through the ReciPro software.[ 70 ]

Particle Loading into Alginate Hydrogels

For the synthesis of the Alg hydrogel (control), a 3.9 wt.% alginic acid solution was prepared by dissolving the biopolymer in deionized water at 50 °C with vigorous stirring for 1 h. Then, silicone molds with dimensions 200 × 50 × 2 mm3 were fabricated with Ecoflex 00–10 (a platinum cure silicon rubber compound) and filled with 200 µL of the prepared solution. The hydrogel was obtained by immersing the molds filled with the alginic acid solution into a CaCl2 3 wt.% solution for 24 h. Then, the excess of CaCl2 was removed by washing the samples with milli‐Q water. Alg/Au‐USNPs and Alg/Au2O3‐NPs hydrogel composites were prepared using the same procedure but dissolving the alginate in the suspension of Au USNPs and Au2O3 NPs, respectively. Such suspensions were stable without showing sedimentation during the whole preparation.

The amount of Au USNPs and Au2O3 NPs added to the alginic acid solution corresponded to those obtained by applying the plasma treatment described above to an adhesive carbon tape with 2.0 ± 0.5 mg of HAuCl4 powder (10 s and 1 min for Au USNPs and Au2O3 NPs, respectively). After the plasma treatment, the adhesive carbon tapes were introduced in Eppendorfs containing 1 mL of Milli‐Q water and exposed to ultrasound treatment for 20 to detach the produced nanoparticles. After ultrasonication, the carbon adhesive was removed from the suspensions before adding the samples to the alginic acid solution. Furthermore, suspensions were also filtered in order to remove eventual residues of the carbon adhesive before using them as solvents for the alginic solutions.

Electrochemical Detection of NADH and H2O2

All electrochemical experiments were run in triplicate using the Autolab VIONIC equipment. Experiments were performed in a three‐electrode cell using as reference and counter electrodes an Ag|AgCl electrode and a platinum wire, respectively. Cyclic voltammetry (CV) were performed in the potential range from –0.80 to 0.80 V at a scan rate of 50 mV s−1. As a working electrode, a hydrogel with or without nanoparticles deposited onto an ITO (Indium Tin Oxide)‐coated PET (polyethylene terephthalate) sheet were used. To perform NADH and H2O2 single and multiplexed detections as supporting electrolyte a 0.1 m CaCl2 solution (5 mL) were used, and the experiment were carried out at room temperature. The detection of NADH in human urine samples and bacteria culture media was performed using the same set‐up. As CV and sensing measurements were especially sensible to experimental error, 4–6 repetitions were usually performed on separate days and separate synthesis batches to ensure the reproducibility of the results reported.

Biologic Essays

First, a frozen stock of Escherichia coli (biofilm positive) was thawed, resuspended in 10 mL of the Bacto Brain and Heart Infusion medium, and cultured overnight at 37 °C and 80 rpm. Then, 1 mL of the bacterial suspension was added to 45 mL of the Bacto Brain and Heart Infusion medium and cultured at 37 °C and 80 rpm for 24 h. The bacterial concentrations of the grown bacterial cultures were determined by the McFarland standards method, and absorbance was read at 600 nm in a SpectraMax Mini (Molecular Devices LLC.) microplate reader, using the calibration curve displayed in Figure S9 (Supporting Information).
