Diagnosis and risk factors for invasive mould disease—a round table discussion on directing antifungal therapy according to the available evidence
Authors: Ana Alastruey-Izqueirdo, Johan Maertens, Monica A Slavin, Paul E Verweij, P Lewis White
Published: 2026

Introduction

When choosing a preferred antifungal therapy strategy various factors need to be considered (Table 1 and Figures 1a and b). Understanding the current and evolving risk predisposing patients to invasive fungal disease (IFD) is critical to underpinning the overall need for antifungals, and how these are best applied (i.e. prophylactically, empirically, pre-emptively and targeted) requires an understanding of the benefits/limitations or each approach.1 Despite an increasing range of options to aid in the diagnosis of IFD, there remains a heavy reliance on therapeutic antifungal strategies lacking mycological confirmation of IFD (e.g. empirical therapy). Such strategies were introduced when diagnostic options were limited, but this is no longer the case, although access to mycological tests with timely reporting remains limited in many countries. Nevertheless, the diagnosis of IFD is typically a multicomponent process involving assessment of clinical, radiological and mycological evidence. A keen understanding of the many factors influencing test performance is required, along with the capacity to identify potential antifungal resistance.2

The management of IFD refractory to antifungals (not necessarily caused by a resistant fungus) can be difficult given the limited number of these drug classes. Moreover, a good grasp of antifungal pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic profiles is important (Table 1). Given the complexity of managing patients both at risk of IFD or already with IFD, a multidisciplinary team involvement is critical. At present, our understanding of the clinical impact of IFD is difficult to determine; the global burden of IFD is based on estimates, with surveillance schemes limited and variable in content.3,4 With significant numbers of patients with IFD residing in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), it is critical that both diagnostic capacity and surveillance is improved in these settings.5 A round table involving renowned experts in the field of medical mycology was convened to discuss the evidence on how these factors influence antifungal therapy. This article describes the main points derived from these discussions and while much of the content is based on expert opinion/consensus, most is underpinned by referenced knowledge of the field of medical mycology. It is designed to highlight and discuss all aspects that influence decisions on administering antifungal therapy and managing patients at risk of, or with IFD and is intended (but not limited) to benefit healthcare professionals who are not working or are commencing in the field of medical mycology. In response to a brief on improving the understanding of IFD, a proposal containing structured content for discussion and the selected experts was submitted by the chair (P.L.W.) and approved by the publisher and the sponsor (Pfizer Inc.). Experts involved were invited on the basis of their reputation across the field of medical mycology with the aim to cover clinical, laboratory and epidemiological aspects.

The changing epidemiology of IFD

Historically, IFD has been typically associated with established underlying host and clinical risk factors (e.g. haematological/stem cell transplantation) that predispose these patients to IFD through prolonged periods of immunosuppression (e.g. neutropenia). Through prophylactic strategies, the epidemiology of IFD has changed from yeast to primarily invasive mould disease (IMD). While in Europe, IMD is typically caused by Aspergillus fumigatus, other moulds are prevalent in other regions (e.g. A. flavus in Middle East and increasing rates of Fusarium spp. in South America).5 Previous antifungal use can also select for breakthrough infections with difficult-to-treat resistant species (e.g. azole resistant A. fumigatus, Scedosporium apiospermum and Lomentospora prolificans) and dual mould infections (e.g. Aspergillus and Mucorales spp.). Knowing your local mycological epidemiology (prevalent species and patterns of antifungal resistance) is critical to selecting the most appropriate antifungal therapy.

IFD is now of considerable concern beyond established cohorts (e.g. haematology), including solid organ transplant recipients, patients receiving corticosteroids or biological immunotherapy and those with existing respiratory conditions [e.g. invasive aspergillosis (IA) in COPD]. IFD is also seen after severe trauma/burns and increasingly in critical-care patients, lacking established host factors but requiring mechanical ventilation or diagnosed with a primary respiratory virus infection (e.g. Influenza or SARS-COV-2). Approximately 20% of patients with influenza requiring critical-care intervention will develop secondary IA (influenza associated pulmonary aspergillosis) early on or even on admission to critical care.6 While the rates of secondary IFD in COVID-19 critical-care patients are probably lower (5%–10%), the range of fungal pathogens is broader (Aspergillus, Candida and Mucorales spp.).7 Awareness of these expanding at-risk groups and potential pathogens is critical for developing clinical management strategies using both appropriate diagnostics and antifungal therapy.

Real world diagnosis of IFD

IMD is seldom proven. In routine clinical practice, probable IMD, possible IMD, suspicion and even simply concern of IMD are regularly managed on the basis of clinical experience rather than the strict application of international consensus definitions aligned with clinical trial enrolment.8 With a lack of perfect mycological tests, false positivity, false negativity and conflicting results arise with the use of multiple biomarkers and treatment of patients with radiology typical of IMD but lacking mycology confirmation regularly occurs due to the risk, poor prognosis and diverse aetiology of IMD. Combination testing increases the chance of finding infection, and probable cases with radiology and multiple sources of mycological evidence could be considered approximately equivalent to proven IFD, which obviates the need for a biopsy. In the absence of timely diagnostic results a reliance on empirical therapy remains. However, access to radiology is ubiquitous in high-income countries and it is very unusual to have a pulmonary IMD with a normal chest computed tomography, so possible IMD is a reasonable starting point for antifungal therapy. It is important to remember that with typically imperfect tests, the patient is being treated not the test result.

Identifying antifungal resistance in the clinic can be limited by the lack of positive cultures and access to molecular testing, but is critical to efficacious treatment. Resistance can be driven by non-clinical pressures (agricultural fungicides and azole resistance in A. fumigatus) affecting the antifungal naïve patient.5 Clinicians are regularly faced with managing patients with IMD refractory to antifungal therapy (e.g. worsening clinical or radiological symptoms despite treatment, persistent or worsening mycology), without knowing whether the fungus is resistant.9 If resistance is suspected or identified and antifungal levels confirmed, then a switch to another class of antifungal is warranted. Accepting options are limited by antifungal class, the route of administration and the reduced efficacy of second line therapies, and so combination or novel therapies are often used.9 In clinical practice, stable disease in an immunosuppressed patient should not always be considered failure, particularly with a difficult-to-treat mould infection.9 In the near future, improving the host’s immunity may improve the management of IFD. Identifying individual host predisposition can also be used to stratify patients according to perceived risk.

Antifungal management

Therapeutic management of IFD is dependent on the risk of developing disease, where the incidence dictates the number needed to treat to prevent or treat a case of IFD. If there is a high risk of IFD (e.g. >10%) within a particular cohort then antifungal prophylaxis can be initiated for the prevention of IFD. While prophylaxis will reduce rates of IFD, it will also result in unnecessary exposure to antifungals in some cases, with the potential to induce adverse effects and influence epidemiology, potentially affecting further antifungal therapy. Breakthrough infections can also result from inappropriate antifungal blood levels, so the requirement, frequency and availability of therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM) should be considered, particularly when using mould-active azoles. Empirical antifungal therapy is widely used in the absence of, or preferably while awaiting diagnostic results, to treat fever refractory to broad spectrum antibacterials in high-risk hosts (e.g. neutropenic fever despite 48–72 hours of antibacterials).1 Typically, empirical strategies, will undoubtedly treat some patients with undiagnosed IFD but will also result in over-treatment by administering an antifungal in the absence of an obvious pathogen. When considering empirical antifungal therapy for refractory fever in patients on mould-active prophylaxis, a change in antifungal class should be considered in case of breakthrough/resistant infection.9 Nowadays, pre-emptive or diagnostic driven antifungal strategies are being employed to safely withhold antifungal therapy in certain patients (e.g. acute myeloid leukaemia, haematopoietic stem cell transplant recipients) unless there is an early indication of infection.10 However, the use of these strategies may be undermined by the impact of mould-active drugs on diagnostic test performances. When a diagnosis has been made, targeting therapy to treat the identified fungus should be administered in line with international guidelines.11 Beyond targeted therapy, the evidence base for the other therapeutic strategies is limited outside the haematology/SCT populations, where both risk of IFD, and diagnostic test performance can vary.

Multidisciplinary teams

Given the complexity of patients suffering from IFD, there is a need for multidisciplinary management to understand all aspects from the clinical presentation and risk, the diagnostic test result through to treatment. Stewardship schemes ensure the correct diagnostic tests are performed, the results are appropriately interpreted, thus achieving the correct diagnosis with appropriate therapy being administered and supported by TDM and local clinical protocols. Antifungal stewardship programmes need not be formalized in structure but must include dedicated healthcare professionals across the spectrum of patient management and can form part of larger antimicrobial stewardship schemes.

Future directions

To achieve a better understanding of burden of IFD, improvements in the surveillance of both IFD and antifungal resistance are required and many countries lack a mycology reference laboratory.4 Standardization of surveillance protocols, public health involvement and financial underpinning are required to build on the increased awareness of IFD achieved through the publication of WHO Fungal Priority Pathogen List (FPPL), diagnostic and therapeutic landscape documents and upcoming blueprint for the implementation of the FPPL. Improved accessibility to diagnostics is critical to improve surveillance, particularly in, but not restricted to, LMICs. Given the likely burden of IFD in LMICs, infrastructure independent diagnostics need to be developed and access to antifungals needs improving to enhance patient prognosis and global equity.

With the emergence of antifungal resistant fungal species with the capacity to colonize patients and persist in the clinical environment (e.g. Candidozyma auris) and increasing rates of transmissible fungal diseases outside the established cohorts (e.g. Pneumocystis pneumonia in the HIV negative cohorts) infection control is becoming increasingly relevant in medical mycology. Education about the risk of IFD and how to limit the inevitable exposure to fungi is also required for those at risk.
