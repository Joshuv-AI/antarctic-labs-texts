Yeast species resource exploration and application in cell factories
Authors: Meiling Zhang, Lin Xiang, Lei Qin, Haitao Yue, Chun Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Yeasts serve as important industrial hosts, contributing significantly to sustainable and green biomanufacturing efforts in the pursuit of global carbon neutrality, alongside other vital microorganisms. This review systematically summarizes recent advances in yeast cell factories, focusing on the exploration of yeast species resources, innovation in enabling technologies, and efficient biosynthesis strategies. The characteristics and industrial applications of commonly used industrial yeast chassis were reviewed. The advent of synthetic and systems biology tools has resulted in a significant enhancement of the efficiency of yeast manipulation and regulation. Key strategies for enhancing the titter, yield, and productivity (TRY) of yeast cell factories are elaborated, including dynamic regulation of metabolic pathways, transcription factor engineering for global metabolic regulation, GEM-driven optimization, dynamic trade-off between growth and production, and cofactor engineering for redox and energy balance. In the future, yeast cell factories will advance green biomanufacturing by leveraging AI-guided design and dynamic metabolic control to utilize C1 and recalcitrant carbon sources with high efficiency.

Introduction

Amidst escalating global fossil fuel consumption and the imperative of carbon neutrality, green biomanufacturing is emerging as a pivotal pathway to replace traditional chemical industries for the sustainable production of high-value bio-based chemicals. Microbial cell factories, particularly yeast-based systems, have become central to this transition due to their efficient metabolic networks, mature genetic manipulation platforms, and industrial scalability [1]. As unicellular eukaryotic model organisms, yeasts not only possess sophisticated post-translational modification capabilities but also enable precise metabolic pathway reconstruction through synthetic biology tools, demonstrating unique advantages in synthesizing natural products (e.g., vindoline) and carbon capture [2]. However, as industrial demands shift from single-molecule production to complex system optimization, the development of yeast cell factories continues to face critical challenges, including limited diversity of yeast species resources, constraints in dynamic regulation technologies, and poor scalability in industrial settings.

In recent years, the deep integration of synthetic biology and systems biology has driven transformative innovations in yeast chassis cells. In the exploration of yeast species resources, classical industrial strains (e.g., Saccharomyces cerevisiae and Komagataella phaffii) have been successfully applied to enable the high-efficiency synthesis of high-value products through genome-scale resolution and metabolic network reconstruction [[3], [4], [5]]. Novel yeast species, such as the thermotolerant Kluyveromyces marxianus and the oleaginous Yarrowia lipolytica, have emerged as promising chassis due to their environmental adaptability and specialized metabolic pathways [6,7]. At the level of enabling technologies innovation for advanced yeast cell factories, CRISPR/Cas-based editing has advanced from single-gene modification to multiplex genetic circuit regulation, while optogenetic tools and AI-driven metabolic modeling have significantly enhanced the precision of dynamic control [[8], [9], [10], [11], [12]]. The design of synthetic promoters also offers novel strategies to achieve cross-species metabolic pathway compatibility [13,14]. Regarding productivity enhancement, modular pathway design, cofactor engineering, and dynamic metabolic burden balancing strategies have markedly improved product titer, yield, and productivity (TRY).

Nevertheless, significant bottlenecks persist in the industrial application of yeast cell factories. Regarding yeast species resources, the limited diversity of cultivable industrial strains (with only 20–30 species applied at scale) and the lagging development of genetic tools for extremophilic yeasts collectively constrain the exploration of their metabolic potential [[15], [16], [17]]. Technically, insufficient compatibility of cross-species regulatory elements, signal attenuation in dynamic systems, and the paucity of multi-omics data for non-model yeasts hinder the universality of rational design. In industrial translation, metabolic rigidity in high-density fermentation, low co-utilization efficiency of mixed substrates, and product toxicity accumulation collectively result in a significant performance gap between laboratory strains and industrial requirements [[18], [19], [20]].

This review systematically synthesizes recent advancements in yeast cell factories across three dimensions—expansion of yeast species resources, enabling technologies innovations, and high-efficiency biosynthesis strategies—while exploring their synergistic optimization mechanisms. Major breakthroughs, particularly in the development of CRISPR technology, have been achieved. Through this framework, we seek to establish a theoretical foundation for the deep integration of synthetic biology and green biomanufacturing, thereby accelerating the dynamic design and industrial deployment of next-generation yeast platforms.

Yeast species resources

As a unicellular eukaryotic model organism, yeast has emerged as a fundamental chassis cell system in synthetic biology and metabolic engineering due to its well-established genetic manipulation platform, highly reconfigurable metabolic network, and remarkable robustness in industrial environments. A globally established repository of diverse yeast species resources—spanning multiple species and featuring distinctive metabolic traits and biosynthetic capabilities—provides a foundational platform for synthetic biology applications. The systematic elucidation of metabolic regulatory mechanisms in these chassis cells, coupled with the development of precision engineering strategies, provides critical theoretical frameworks and practical solutions for enhancing biomanufacturing efficiency. Furthermore, such approaches offer viable pathways to address the inherent limitations of conventional chemical processes and advance sustainability objectives.

Saccharomyces cerevisiae

As the first model eukaryote to have its entire genome sequenced, Saccharomyces cerevisiae, with its core strengths in whole genome resolution (Saccharomyces Genome Database), modular gene editing tools (CRISPR-Cas, SCRaMbLE) and standardized libraries of synthetic biology components, is one of the most popular chassis cells for metabolic engineering [[21], [22], [23], [24], [25]]. In S. cerevisiae, a comprehensive “plug-and-tune” combinatorial gene regulation system has been established [22]. Furthermore, an iterative SCRaMbLE (Synthetic Chromosome Recombination and Modification by LoxPsym-mediated Evolution)-based debugging and optimization toolchain has been successfully developed for enhancing the functionality of synthetic genomic modules and chromosomes [26]. Under conditions of low sugar or non-fermentable carbon sources (e.g., glycerol and lactate), S. cerevisiae achieves efficient ATP synthesis by activating the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle and mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation, and the TOR (target of rapamycin) kinase pathway works synergistically to regulate both carbon and nitrogen sources, thereby coordinating growth and metabolism [[27], [28], [29]]. S. cerevisiae can dynamically adapt to environmental stress through multidimensional molecular mechanisms. Regulation of the heat shock proteins Hsp70, Hsp90, and Hsp104 enables short-term tolerance to 40 °C [[30], [31], [32]]. The HOG pathway regulates glycerol synthesis to resist hypertonic environments [33]. The proton pump Pma1 maintains intracellular pH homeostasis [34,35]. Superoxide dismutase (SOD), catalase (CAT), and the glutathione system synergistically maintain intracellular redox homeostasis [36,37]. The ABC transporter Pdr5 mediates efflux pumps and cell membrane composition adjustment to cope with ethanol stress [38].

In S. cerevisiae, the Crabtree effect is observed under high-glucose conditions, characterized by a preferential shift towards glycolysis and the subsequent conversion of pyruvate into ethanol, even in the presence of fully functional mitochondrial respiration and abundant oxygen [39] (Fig. 1). This Crabtree effect is fundamentally driven by a phosphate tug-of-war between fermentation and respiration, where glycolytic flux outcompetes respiratory processes for crucial phosphate resources [40]. Consequently, manipulation of sugar phosphorylation presents a strategic approach to alter the mode of yeast metabolism [41]. Knocking out the UBP3 gene, which encodes a deubiquitinase enzyme, mitigates the Crabtree effect by increasing mitochondrial phosphate levels, thereby tipping the balance in favor of respiration [42]. Alternatively, introducing a sucrose phosphorolysis pathway coupled with deletion of the phosphoglucose isomerase gene PGI1 can effectively decouple glycolysis from respiration. This metabolic rewiring facilitates the transition of yeast to a Crabtree-negative state by channeling carbon flux away from fermentative metabolism [43].Fig. 1The metabolic basis of the crabtree effect in yeast.Fig. 1

Komagataella phaffii

Komagataella phaffii (previously named Pichia pastoris), a methylotrophic yeast originally isolated from the oak tree, exhibits the unique ability to utilize methanol as a sole carbon source while maintaining facultative metabolic versatility for alternative substrates [44,45]. The widely used industrial strains GS115 and CBS7435 were fully genome-sequenced in 2009 and 2011, respectively [46,47]. The metabolism of methanol by the subject is dependent on the regulation of the alcohol oxidase AOX1 [48]. The gene expression of AOX1 has been shown to efficiently drive exogenous gene expression under conditions of methanol induction [49,50]. Conversely, carbon sources such as glycerol or glucose achieve strict temporal control of expression by inhibiting Aox1 enzyme activity [51,52]. The exceptional protein secretion capacity of K. phaffii is fundamentally underpinned by the highly efficient and faithful protein folding quality control system within its endoplasmic reticulum (ER) [53]. This system ensures the precise processing and maturation of complex heterologous proteins, leveraging a sophisticated network of molecular chaperones, folding enzymes, and quality control mechanisms.

The genome lacks natural plasmids, and exogenous genes need to be integrated into the chromosome by homologous recombination (e.g., AOX1/HIS4 locus) to ensure genetic stability [54]. However, the limited availability of selectable markers and the low efficiency of homologous recombination have historically constrained the development of K. phaffii as a chassis organism. Recent advances in CRISPR-Cas9-mediated methods have enabled highly efficient and marker-free multigene knock-in, significantly accelerating its development as a synthetic biology chassis [55,56]. SynPic-X is a programmable yeast expression platform that provides a novel “plug-and-tune” synthetic biology chassis, featuring integrated synthetic transcriptional amplifiers (iTSAD) coupled with orthogonal CRISPR interference/activation (CRISPRi/a) systems [57] (Fig. 2). Furthermore, the expansion of synthetic biology toolkits has also contributed to the accelerated advancement of this platform [6,58,59].Fig. 2Overview of the SynPic-X platform.Fig. 2

Overview of the SynPic-X platform.

The SynPic-X platform is engineered to respond to user-defined signals, operating as a genetic NOT gate. In this circuit, the presence of an inducer signal turns the system OFF, while the absence of the inducer leads to system activation and high-level expression. The CRISPRiD (CRISPR interference device) is the primary component responsible for this NOT gate function, as it actively represses the output in response to the input signal. The CRISPRaD (CRISPR activation device), constitutively expressed, provides a potent transcriptional activation potential. It ensures that the output reaches a high maximum level when the repression by CRISPRiD is relieved. The synergistic yet distinct actions of CRISPRiD and CRISPRaD together enable the precise and high-amplitude regulation of the SynPic-X platform.

Kluyveromyces marxianus

Kluyveromyces marxianus, initially isolated from grapes, is characterized by its thermotolerance and broad substrate spectrum, metabolizing sugars such as glucose, lactose, and inulin [60]. Notably, it has been employed to achieve high-efficiency bioconversion of starch to inositol [61]. With a maximum growth temperature of 52 °C and a specific growth rate nearly twice that of S. cerevisiae, this yeast can attain high-density fermentation within 48 h [62]. Its thermotolerance significantly reduces cooling requirements and minimizes contamination risks. Under thermal stress, K. marxianus modulates membrane fluidity by increasing the average fatty acid chain length to maintain cell membrane integrity [63]. Moreover, anaerobic fermentation at 45 °C has been achieved through heterologous expression of the tetrahydroprimidine synthase TtSTC1 [64].

The development of K. marxianus as a robust microbial cell factory has been hampered by the slow progress in molecular genetic tools and synthetic biology strategies. Although both CRISPR-Cas9 and Cre-loxP systems have been successfully adapted for use in K. marxianus, their editing efficiencies remain constrained by multiple factors [[65], [66], [67], [68]]. Notably, the application of ALLEGRO—an efficient, scalable, and cross-species CRISPR sgRNA design tool—has enabled rapid genetic domestication and engineering of K. marxianus, promoting its broader use in biomanufacturing [69]. Nonetheless, due to limitations in metabolic understanding, current strategies for enhancing product synthesis still predominantly rely on overexpression of key genes and knockout of competing pathway genes [[70], [71], [72]].

Yarrowia lipolytica

Yarrowia lipolytica, a strictly aerobic dimorphic yeast, was originally isolated from lipid-rich spoilage materials in sausage-processing facilities. The colony morphology of this yeast strain is modulated by diverse cultivation parameters, encompassing carbon and nitrogen sources as well as ambient pH levels [73]. As a Crabtree-negative yeast, Y. lipolytica exhibits negligible ethanol production during cultivation in comparison with S. cerevisiae. This characteristic eliminates the occurrence of ethanol-induced product inhibition [74]. The substance's broad substrate spectrum, encompassing glycerol and alkanes, is indicative of its significant industrial potential [75,76]. In the presence of glucose as the substrate, the metabolic flow is focused on the glycolytic pathway. In contrast, when fatty acids or alkanes serve as the carbon source, the metabolic flux shifts primarily toward the β-oxidation pathway of fatty acids, accompanied by the activation of the glyoxylate cycle to synthesize the necessary phosphosugars for cellular growth [[77], [78], [79]]. The foundational metabolism underlying the lipid accumulation phenotype of this yeast, coupled with the high metabolic flux through acyl-CoA precursors and the TCA cycle, creates a favorable metabolic environment for the expression of relevant heterologous pathways [80,81]. By introducing orthogonal precursor pathways to enhance the supply of acetyl-CoA and NAD(P)H and rewiring central metabolism, high-level production of β-farnesene from glucose was achieved [80,82].

The development of transcriptional elements, homologous recombination (HR)-proficient systems, and CRISPR-mediated genome editing tools has accelerated the establishment of its genetic manipulation framework, thereby laying a solid foundation for biosynthetic applications [[83], [84], [85], [86], [87]]. The EXPRESSYALI toolkit, which employs a combinatorial and iterative metabolic engineering strategy tailored for Y. lipolytica, enables rapid and high-throughput strain engineering [88]. Leveraging genome-wide functional screens, a deep learning algorithm named DeepGuide was developed, which enables accurate prediction of guide RNA activity for both CRISPR-Cas9 and CRISPR-Cas12a in Y. lipolytica [89] (Fig. 3). An optimized, genome-wide CRISPR-Cas9 knockout screening platform was established to enable rapid engineering of Y. lipolytica [85]. A genome-wide CRISPRi library was constructed in Y. lipolytica to systematically mine for novel tolerance genes [90].Fig. 3DeepGuide algorithm architecture and workflow.Fig. 3

DeepGuide algorithm architecture and workflow.

This flowchart illustrates the DeepGuide algorithm, custom-developed for Yarrowia lipolytica. The process consists of two main stages: (1) Unsupervised Pre-training: A convolutional autoencoder (CAE) is trained on k-mer fragments extracted from the whole genome to learn a generic feature representation of the organism's genomic background. (2) Supervised Learning: The weights from the pre-trained encoder are used to initialize a convolutional neural network, which is then trained with experimentally derived sgRNA sequences and their corresponding genome-wide cutting scores (CS). For the Cas9 system, a separate branch is incorporated, where nucleosome occupancy data (from MNase-Seq data) at the target site is processed by a small fully connected network and fused with the sequence features via an element-wise multiplication layer to output the predicted CS. This architecture, optimized through ablation analysis, ensures accurate prediction of species-specific, high-activity sgRNAs.

Application of yeast platforms in engineered cell factories

Against the backdrop of accelerating global transition towards a green economy, bio-manufacturing is revolutionizing the production paradigm of high-value-added compounds through its precise regulatory capacity in complex molecular synthesis. In this context, the aforementioned yeast cell factories exhibit distinct advantages over prokaryotic chassis cells in reconstructing metabolic pathways from higher eukaryotes, owing to their high degree of similarity in subcellular organization and metabolic networks. As exemplified by the artemisinin acid biosynthesis, the endoplasmic reticulum of yeast enables the seamless integration and functional support of heterologous, plant-derived cytochrome P450 enzyme systems, a feat notoriously challenging to accomplish in prokaryotic cells due to the absence of a compatible membrane-bound compartment [91]. Similarly, in the synthesis of taxadiene, the key precursor to paclitaxel, S. cerevisiae not only leverages its robust native mevalonate pathway to supply abundant terpenoid precursors but also exploits its endoplasmic reticulum system to efficiently catalyze the critical P450-mediated hydroxylation steps [92]. This intricate process, by contrast, encounters formidable challenges in Escherichia coli due to the absence of a native, compatible support system for these complex eukaryotic enzymes [93]. Enabling technologies for yeast cell factories

The construction of efficient yeast cell factories is akin to a precision engineering problem, requiring manipulation at both the “Element” and “System” levels. At the “Element” level, a diverse array of genetic regulatory elements serves as the cornerstone for fine-tuning the expression levels of individual genes. At the “System” level, CRISPR technology provides a powerful platform for global and high-throughput intervention in the complex genome and transcriptome. Together, they constitute the core technological toolbox for yeast metabolic engineering.

Genetic regulatory elements: the foundational design framework for metabolic engineering

Genetic regulatory elements—including promoters and terminators—serve as modular building blocks for metabolic pathway assembly, enabling precise spatiotemporal control of gene expression intensity and context-dependent responsiveness. The success of yeast metabolic engineering critically depends on systematic mining and engineering of these elements to achieve rational allocation of metabolic flux.

Promoters, functioning as upstream regulatory elements, orchestrate transcriptional initiation through recruitment of RNA polymerase and transcription factors (TFs), serving as critical components in metabolic engineering to balance gene expression with cellular resource allocation. Based on their expression patterns and design strategies, promoters can be categorized into three types (Table 4) [13]. Constitutive promoters drive sustained high-level gene expression and are suitable for metabolic housekeeping enzymes that require stable production [179]. Inducible promoters are regulated by specific chemical or physical signals, enabling transcription activation under inducing conditions; this facilitates the avoidance of metabolic burden or product toxicity associated with constitutive expression [180]. Through diverse synergistic strategies, significant progress has been engineered in yeast promoter development. Synthetic promoters exhibiting high inducibility and minimal leaky expression have been successfully constructed in yeast [180]. PromoDGD enables the de novo design and optimization of promoters with predetermined expression strengths [182]. Furthermore, DREAM-CNN has revealed that the coordinated regulatory function of natural bidirectional promoters is governed by a shared central hotspot for activator binding [183]. Simultaneously, PromoterAI facilitates the systematic identification of non-coding promoter variants that significantly alter transcriptional activity [184].

Terminators, functioning as downstream regulatory elements of transcriptional units, coordinate transcription termination and mRNA homeostasis regulation in yeast metabolic engineering. Their functional mechanisms rely on two key molecular events: Terminators recruit RNA polymerase II termination complexes (e.g., Nrd1-Nab3-Sen1) via conserved sequence motifs, mediating transcription termination and suppressing readthrough to ensure independent gene expression; Polyadenylation signals (AAUAAA) and AU-rich elements (AREs) in the 3′ untranslated region (3′UTR) regulate poly-A tail formation and interactions with RNA-binding proteins (e.g., CCR4-NOT complex), dynamically balancing mRNA nucleocytoplasmic transport, translational activity, and degradation rates [208,209].

By analyzing the microhomology-mediated end joining (MMEJ) mechanism, Pythia can predict and optimize the outcomes of CRISPR-Cas9-mediated genome editing [220]. The Evolutionary Scale Model (ESM-2) identified a Cas12a subtype, AmCas12a, which exhibits a broader PAM recognition range and enhanced gene-editing efficiency [221]. The engineered circular guide RNAs substantially enhance both gene activation and base editing efficacy of the miniature CRISPR/Cas12f system [222].

CRISPR interference (CRISPRi) and CRISPR activation (CRISPRa) constitute a versatile transcriptional modulation toolkit. CRISPRi employs a catalytically dead Cas9 (dCas9) fused to repressor domains, which, upon targeting gene promoters, sterically blocks transcription initiation [223]. Conversely, CRISPRa utilizes dCas9 fused to activator domains to recruit transcriptional machinery and upregulate gene expression from promoters or enhancers [224]. Crucially, both platforms function without inducing DNA double-strand breaks, enabling reversible epigenetic regulation while minimizing genotoxic risks [225]. This has established them as core technologies for functional genomics screening and the construction of precise genetic circuits.

Efficient biosynthesis strategies for yeast cell factories

The construction of high-performance yeast cell factories constitutes a systems biology endeavor, centered on the precision design and reconstruction of cellular metabolic networks. Predominant optimization strategies can be categorized into three key dimensions. In terms of regulatory strategies, dynamic regulation of metabolic pathways offers a sophisticated means for temporal and spatial control, whereas transcription factor engineering enables global mastery over multiplex metabolic pathways. At the level of design and prediction, Genome-scale Metabolic Model (GEM)-driven optimization provides a powerful framework for rational design and a holistic perspective. Regarding the resolution of core bottlenecks, research on the strategic trade-off between growth and production is dedicated to optimizing cellular resource allocation, while cofactor engineering specifically addresses supply bottlenecks of energy and reducing equivalents within synthetic pathways.

Dynamic regulation of metabolic pathways

Conventional static control strategies, often limited by their inability to respond to the dynamically shifting physiological states within the cell, frequently lead to issues such as metabolic imbalance, resource competition, and intermediate metabolite toxicity. To address these challenges, dynamic regulation of metabolic pathways has emerged as a promising alternative. This approach ingeniously links gene expression to specific intra- or extracellular signals, thereby achieving precise spatiotemporal control of metabolic fluxes.

The development of self-responsive dynamic regulation strategies for glycolytic metabolism enables the synthesis of high-value-added compounds in non-fermentative S. cerevisiae [27]. The Gal4M9-mediated temperature-sensitive regulatory system enables temporal control of the key genes in the astaxanthin biosynthetic pathway [232]. The high-performance, blue-light-inducible gene expression system pYLBI, based on optogenetics, enables precise, rapid, and reversible control over gene expression in time, space, and dosage through the modulation of blue light illumination [233]. The quorum sensing (QS)-mediated dynamic protein degradation strategy enables autonomous switching of metabolic modes based on population developmental stages [234].

Transcription factor engineering for global metabolic regulation

Transcription factors (TFs) play a central role in yeast metabolic engineering by orchestrating the temporal regulation of gene clusters and enabling dynamic pathway coordination. Transcription factor engineering extends beyond the direct modification of known regulatory elements. It also encompasses the discovery of novel regulatory networks through systems biology approaches, thereby providing new targets for cellular reprogramming.

The newly identified and engineerable Oca5–InsP7–Gcr1/Mig1–Hap4 transcriptional regulatory circuit provides a powerful tool for dynamically balancing yeast metabolism by fine-tuning the glycolysis–respiration equilibrium [235]. The galactose-inducible system in S. cerevisiae was ingeniously repurposed into a glucose-dependent switch by disrupting the repressor protein Gal80p within the canonical Gal4p–Gal80p–Gal3p transcriptional regulatory system [236]. Engineering the transcription factor Adr1 to mimic the oleate-induced state of peroxisomal proliferation, thereby enhancing the β-oxidation capacity [237].

GEM-driven optimization

Genome-scale metabolic models (GEMs) serve as a core computational biology tool for the rational design and global optimization of yeast cell factories. By integrating genome annotation, biochemical reaction networks, and enzyme kinetic constraints, GEMs construct mathematical models capable of simulating the global metabolic state of the cell.

As the most current community-curated consensus genome-scale metabolic model for Saccharomyces cerevisiae, Yeast9 integrates vast biological data and algorithmic refinements to simulate yeast metabolic behaviors under diverse conditions, establishing itself as a central reference resource in the field [238].

Dynamic trade-off between growth and production

In the construction of yeast cell factories, a fundamental bottleneck lies in the inherent limitations of intracellular resources. Both rapid cell growth and the efficient synthesis of the target product are highly energy-demanding biological processes, which inevitably create a competitive dynamic within this shared resource pool. This inherent growth-production trade-off often triggers the classic phenomenon of overflow metabolism, wherein the cell diverts excess carbon flux toward the synthesis of by-products rather than the target chemical, ultimately constraining the final titer and yield.

The sterol-responsive promoter was combined with an N-terminal degron to construct a ‘degradation module’, which dynamically modulated the stability of the squalene synthase (ERG9) protein based on intracellular sterol levels, thereby balancing cell growth and lycopene synthesis [239]. The splicing intein-mediated temperature-responsive gene expression system (SIMTeGES) enables the precise decoupling of cell growth from sanguinarine biosynthesis via a temperature shift strategy (30 °C for growth → 25 °C for production) [240]. The galactose induction system was re-engineered by placing the expression of the activator GAL4 and the repressor GAL80 under the control of diauxic shift promoters, thereby achieving a decoupling of the growth and production phases [241].

Cofactor engineering for redox and energy balance

The central objective of cofactor engineering is to dynamically and efficiently balance the intracellular supply of energy and reducing equivalents with the substantial demand imposed by heterologous pathways.

The integration of three modules—the pentose phosphate cycle, the transhydrogenase cycle, and the external respiratory chain—decouples the supply of reducing power from the inherent rigid coupling of central carbon metabolism [242]. The decompartmentalization of mitochondrial metabolism alters the spatial architecture of cofactor metabolism, thereby enhancing the overall efficiency of reductive power utilization [243]. The application of customized approaches—including downstream pulling, compartmentalization control, and recycling restoration—to address the insufficient supply of the three cofactors NADPH, FADH2, and SAM [244].

Conclusions and prospects

This review systematically summarizes recent advances in yeast cell factories, covering the exploration of yeast species resources, innovation in enabling technologies, and efficient biosynthesis strategies. The deep integration of synthetic biology and systems biology is driving a paradigm shift in yeast cell factories from fundamental research to industrial translation. Significant progress has been achieved in green manufacturing of terpenoids, flavonoids, and biofuels through the utilization of yeast species resources, enabling technological innovations, and advanced synthesis strategies in yeast chassis cells.

Nevertheless, critical challenges remain in bridging existing technical frameworks with complex industrial demands. Conventional static regulation approaches often fail to resolve the trade-off between cell growth and product synthesis. Heterologous expression of complex pathways frequently triggers metabolic conflicts, while issues of strain instability and poor adaptability under large-scale fermentation persist. Transcending these limitations requires a fundamental shift from local optimization to system-level redesign to facilitate multidimensional and dynamically regulated engineering of yeast cell factories.

Future advances will rely on the convergence of multiple cutting-edge domains. Intelligent design frameworks will play a central role, with AI and machine learning becoming deeply embedded throughout the DBTL (Design-Build-Test-Learn) cycle. The integration of multi-omics data will empower predictive digital models, automate design processes, and drastically shorten development timelines. Moreover, engineering strategies will expand beyond metabolic pathways to include global cellular restructuring—such as reprogramming organelles (e.g., mitochondria and peroxisomes), exploiting non-classical secretion mechanisms to enhance protein trafficking, and constructing synthetic microbial consortia for distributed and efficient pathway execution.

Equally important is the development of carbon-neutral biomanufacturing systems. Future efforts will focus on transitioning from sugar-based feedstocks to novel carbon sources such as CO2, C1 compounds (methanol, formate), plastic-derived monomers, and lignocellulosic hydrolysates. Unlocking the functional potential of genomic “dark matter” and epigenetic regulatory networks will further enhance cellular efficiency and robustness. Through coupling energy-saving fermentation processes, next-generation yeast platforms are expected to combine high productivity, stability, and sustainability, ultimately establishing themselves as the cornerstone of a future bioeconomy.
