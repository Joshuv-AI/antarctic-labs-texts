Hospitalization for Malignant Arrhythmias Following Initiation of Donepezil: A Retrospective Cohort Study
Authors: Yichang Huang, John‐Michael Gamble, David N Juurlink, Mhd Wasem Alsabbagh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPurposeLimited evidence suggests donepezil might pose an increased risk of malignant arrhythmias. We examined whether donepezil initiation was associated with a higher risk of hospitalization for malignant arrhythmia compared to other acetylcholinesterase inhibitors.MethodsPopulation‐based cohort study using linked administrative databases from seven Canadian provinces (BC, AB, SK, MB, ON, PEI, NL). We included individuals aged ≥ 66 who were new users of donepezil, galantamine, or rivastigmine from April 1st, 2011, to January 31st, 2019. We used multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression to estimate the risk of hospitalization for malignant arrhythmia among new donepezil users compared to new users of galantamine or rivastigmine.ResultsWe identified 162 616 subjects (mean age 82, 40% male). Most (127 103; 78%) were treated with donepezil, while 25 597 (15.7%) received galantamine, (n = 6541; 4.0%) received oral rivastigmine, and (n = 3375; 2.1%) received transdermal rivastigmine. The median age was 82 years, and 59.8% were women. Baseline characteristics were generally well balanced. During a median follow‐up of 386 days (maximum 2616 days), we identified 90 hospitalizations for malignant arrhythmias, including 58 in the donepezil group (23 per 100 000 person‐years) and 32 for other AChEIs (44 per 100 000 person‐years). Cumulative incidence curves showed a lower risk among donepezil initiators (Log‐Rank p = 0.0026; Wilcoxon p = 0.0008). Crude regression suggested a reduced hazard (HR 0.52; 95% CI 0.34–0.80), which persisted after adjustment (aHR 0.55; 95% CI 0.36–0.85). In drug‐specific analyses, donepezil initiators had a lower hazard relative to galantamine (aHR 0.49; 95% CI 0.31–0.77) but not rivastigmine (aHR 0.90; 95% CI 0.36–2.27). Sensitivity analyses—including alternative diagnosis field definitions, restricted follow‐up, modified exclusion criteria, and propensity score adjustment—yielded consistent findings. No clear dose–response pattern was observed for any AChEI.ConclusionIn this population‐based cohort study, initiation of donepezil was not associated with an increased risk of malignant arrhythmias compared to initiation of galantamine or rivastigmine.Keywords: acetylcholinesterase inhibitor, Alzheimer's disease, cardiac arrhythmia, dementia, donepezil

Introduction

With the aging population, it is estimated that 12.7 million American and 152.8 million worldwide aged 65 and older will have Alzheimer's disease (AD) by 2050 [1]. Acetylcholinesterase inhibitors (AChEIs) are the most widely used medications for AD and include donepezil (initially marketed in Canada in 1997), rivastigmine (2000), and galantamine (2001). Of these, donepezil is the most widely used AChEI in Canada, likely reflecting earlier marketing, lower cost, and familiarity to clinicians.

Common adverse effects of all AChEIs include nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, and dizziness, as well as vagal potentiation [2]. More serious adverse events, acquired long‐QT syndrome (aLQTS) and its associated life‐threatening arrythmia torsades de pointes (TdP), are also associated with certain medications; available pharmacological literature and case reports indicate that donepezil may be associated with a greater risk of aLQTS and malignant arrhythmias in comparison to the other two AChEIs [3]. CredibleMeds, a resource used to assist clinicians with regards to QT‐prolonging medications [4], listed donepezil (but not other AChEIs) on their known‐risk list—which states a medication may be associated with risk even during routine clinical use [3]. One proposed mechanism by which donepezil might be associated with an increased risk of ALQTS or TdP is that it inhibits the cardiac delayed rectifier potassium current (IKr) at clinically relevant concentrations [5]. In addition, donepezil inhibits expression of the channel protein expression through which IKr flows, a property not shared by other AChEIs [5]. With malignant arrhythmias and AD also having common risk factors such as advanced age and female sex, given donepezil's widespread use, this adverse event may be of concern [5].

Although some small studies hint that donepezil may carry a higher risk of dangerous heart arrhythmia than other AChEIs, no large studies have looked into this in real‐world settings. We investigated whether donepezil is associated with a greater risk of hospitalization for malignant arrhythmias, in comparison to other AChEIs.

Methods

Study Design and Data Sources

We conducted a population‐based retrospective cohort study of individuals aged 66 and over in seven Canadian provinces (British Columbia, Alberta, Saskatchewan, Manitoba, Ontario, Prince Edward Island, Newfoundland and Labrador) using linked healthcare databases provided by the Canadian Institute for Health Information (CIHI). We identified prescription claims using the National Prescription Drug Utilization Information System (NPDUIS), which captures all publicly funded prescriptions, and the Discharge Abstract Database (DAD), which captures hospital encounters in participating Canadian jurisdictions. Linkage of DAD and NPDUIS was performed by CIHI, based upon the province or territory of the individual's health insurance identification number, combined with the year of birth [3]. A previous report by CIHI [6] found that demographic variables were coded extremely well in DAD (> 99.8% agreement between original coder and re‐abstractor on data such as gender, birthdate, health card number, admission date, and discharge date). We utilized DAD data between FY2006‐07 to FY2018‐19 and NPDUIS between FY2010‐11 to FY2018‐19 [7].

Canadians have universal access to hospital care. Data from the DAD are received directly from either active care facilities, or from provincial health/regional authorities or ministries/departments of health [3], and contain up to 25 fields of diagnostic information, including the main diagnosis field (most responsible for hospital stay) as well as 24 diagnosis fields that can correspond to other diagnosis types, including pre‐admit comorbidity or subsequent diagnoses due to hospital intervention. DAD also contains detailed patient information including age, sex, hospital admission date, and other variables. Because only month and year of hospitalization were provided in DAD, we considered the date of hospitalization as occurring on the 15th of the month. NPDUIS collects claims data from individual provincial/territorial public drug programs through the ministries of health and it contains information about each dispensing including prescription medication dispensed, medication class, and number of days supplied [3]. Most prescription drugs for people aged 65 and older are funded by public drug plans at the provincial level and thus captured within NPDUIS; importantly, all AChEIs are publicly funded, with no between‐province differences in coverage for all oral formulations available during the study period [8].

Study Cohort

We obtained a base cohort from CIHI of individuals aged 66 and over with a newly dispensed AChEI between April 1st, 2011, and January 31st, 2019 (Figure 1). We defined the cohort entry date (index date) as the date of the first prescription for an AChEI, defining new use as no prescription for any drug from the class in 365 days prior to cohort entry. Patients entered the cohort at their first AChEI dispensing, and each control group was formed independently using the same new‐user design; donepezil users were not selected first. We excluded patients who received prescriptions for more than one AChEI on the index date, those whose sex was unknown, and those who received neostigmine or pyridostigmine (AChEIs prescribed for other indications) before the index date. Patients who switched to neostigmine or pyridostigmine after the index date were censored at the switch date. Lastly, patients with a malignant arrhythmia hospitalization in one year prior to cohort entry using any of the 25 DAD diagnosis fields were excluded.

Study designs.

Exposure Definition and Follow‐Up Time

The primary exposure of interest was initiation of donepezil. As a comparator, we considered patients who initiated oral rivastigmine, transdermal rivastigmine, or galantamine as one group in the primary analysis and as three separate groups in the secondary analysis. We considered a patient to be exposed to either donepezil or another anticholinesterase inhibitor from the date of their first prescription record to the date of their last prescription record, plus a grace period of 150% of the last prescription days' supply in the event of filling a prescription prior to the completion of the prior prescription and the potential for missed doses at any given time during the course of medication usage. Therefore, gaps between prescriptions were considered periods of exposure. We censored patients if they switched from their initial AChEI (in which case they were censored at the date of initiation of the new agent) or if they discontinued their initial AChEI (in which case they were censored at 150% of last prescription days' supply).

Primary Outcome

The primary outcome was hospitalization for a malignant arrhythmia, defined as a hospital admission for either ventricular tachycardia (ICD‐10‐CA diagnostic code of I47.2) or ventricular fibrillation (ICD‐10‐CA I49.00) [3] in the most responsible DAD diagnosis field. These ICD‐10‐CA codes have been used in the assessment of the outcome in other studies, and were found to have a high positive predictive value (92%, 95% CI 87%–95%), albeit at a low sensitivity of 39%, which indicates most instances are not identified [3].

Statistical Analysis

We used descriptive statistics to compare characteristics of donepezil users and users of other AChEIs. Baseline differences between the Donepezil and other AChEI groups were evaluated using standardized mean differences (SMD). We interpreted standardized mean differences under 0.1 as indicating negligible differences between groups [9]. We calculated crude incidence rates of malignant arrhythmia for each exposure group, expressed per 100 000 person‐years, using the total number of events divided by accumulated person‐time. Rate ratios comparing donepezil to other AChEIs were estimated using a Poisson rate‐ratio test [10]. We assessed time from the index date to outcome by generating cumulative incidence curves and univariate proportional hazards models. We compared cumulative incidence curves by both Log‐Rank and Wilcoxon tests, with the rationale for the Wilcoxon test (which weighs earlier outcomes heavier) being based upon the possibility of earlier outcomes being more strongly associated with medication initiation. We further examined the association between donepezil use and malignant arrhythmia through Cox proportional hazard modeling. We considered 42 variables for confounding adjustment in the model, with medication use covariates being within one year prior to cohort entry, and hospitalization covariates being within five years prior to cohort entry. A list of covariates considered, with definitions, is included in Appendix A (Supporting Information). Covariate definitions were drawn from prior Canadian pharmacoepidemiologic studies of medication safety [11, 12], and from validated ICD‐10‐CA algorithms for chronic conditions [13].

All combinations of confounding variables were assessed through the Phi coefficient (for any 2 × 2 comparison) or Cramer's V (for any comparison bigger than a 2 × 2 comparison) statistic to determine correlations among predictor variables. The cut‐off we used for a significant correlation among predictor variables was the absolute value of the Phi coefficient or Cramer's V greater than 0.15 [14]. The Cox regression model was built through a forward building methodology, in which variables that were significant in the univariate analysis (and not correlated with covariates already in the model) were retained if they were significant in the Log Ratio Test (LRT) and reduced Akaike's Information Criterion (AIC). In addition to exposure (AChEI medication group), we forced sex, patient age quintile, occurrence of previous malignant arrhythmia between 2 and 5 years pre‐index, and loop diuretic use into the model prior to forward building addition of covariates. We plotted Martingale residuals to assess the proportional hazards assumption, assessed interactions using a multiplicative model, and we considered a p‐value of less than 0.05 to be significant with regards to variables in the Cox model. We used SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC) to perform statistical analysis.

Secondary and Sensitivity Analyses

We performed several sensitivity analyses to test the robustness of our conclusions. First, we used all 25 DAD diagnosis fields (with no separation of diagnosis type) in the outcome in a secondary analysis to see if conclusions drawn from the most responsible diagnosis will differ when using all diagnosis fields with the same ICD‐10‐CA codes. It has been argued that only the most responsible DAD diagnosis field be used to conduct analyses [6]. Second, we limited follow‐up to 365 days. A study showed that the onset of TdP following administration of a QTC‐prolonging drug was within 72 h for 18% of people; between 3 and 30 days, onset occurred for 42% of people; although the remaining 40% of people had onset after 30 days [15]; however, keeping follow‐up to only 30 days resulted in too few outcomes to analyze. Although it is not a certainty that the development of malignant arrhythmia occurs soon after medication initiation, it was expected one year of follow‐up may better capture true occurrences rather than a non‐time‐bound follow‐up duration, which would instead dilute the signal.

We also performed an additional sensitivity analysis on hospitalization date. Since the exact date of hospitalization was not available in our data, we defined the hospitalization date (in separate analyses) as either the first or the end of the month. This will affect follow‐up time (e.g., hospitalization definition of first of month would shorten follow‐up time by 14 days in comparison to hospitalization definition of 15th of month), or it may affect cohort size, as a patient who had an AChEI prescription in the same month as an ICD‐10‐CA hospitalization of I47.2 or I49.00 may go from an outcome of interest (hospitalization occurs after prescription date) to an exclusion (hospitalization occurs immediately before prescription date). A sensitivity analysis was to modify the previous malignant arrhythmia exclusion to only exclude based on primary DAD diagnosis field. Given differing diagnosis methodologies between the primary diagnosis field and diagnosis fields 2 to 25 as described previously, modifying the previous malignant arrhythmia exclusion as a sensitivity analysis is warranted to see if this exclusion criteria definition also affects results. Finally, a propensity score sensitivity analysis was conducted to adjust for confounding. We first estimated the propensity score (PS) for each individual by fitting a logistic regression model that included all prespecified baseline covariates with the outcome as the treatment. Then, we estimated the stabilized inverse probability of treatment weights (IPTW) by dividing the marginal probability of treatment the PS among patients who received donepezil (i.e., treated) and by 1‐PS for other subjects (i.e., controls). These weights were applied to create a weighted‐population. Covariate balance after weighting was also assessed using SMDs, with values below 0.1 regarded as negligible imbalance [9]. Subsequently, we fit a weighted Cox proportional hazards model, incorporating the IPTW weights to obtain marginal (population‐average) treatment effect estimates [16].

Dose–Response Analyses

Dose–response analyses were performed using categorized AChEI doses (low vs. medium/high), based on the last dispensed dose. Low‐dose categories were defined as 5 mg/day for donepezil, ≤ 8 mg/day for galantamine, ≤ 6 mg/day for oral rivastigmine, and 4.6 mg/24 h for the rivastigmine patch, corresponding to the recommended starting doses for these medications [17]. Medium/high doses were defined as donepezil 10 mg/day, galantamine 16–24 mg/day, rivastigmine oral 9–12 mg/day, and rivastigmine patch 9.5 mg/24 h, reflecting the standard maintenance dose ranges [17]. We then fitted stratified Cox models within each AChEI medication group to evaluate within‐drug dose–response patterns. We also examined dose category as the main exposure in a multivariable Cox model, independent of the specific AChEI. Both approaches used the same covariate adjustment set as the primary analysis.

Results

From a base cohort of 165 604, we identified 162 616 patients for analysis. The process of cohort selection is presented in Figure 2. Most patients in the cohort had a new prescription for donepezil (n = 127 103, 78.2%), while the remaining received galantamine (n = 25 597, 15.7%), oral rivastigmine (n = 6541, 4.0%), or transdermal rivastigmine (n = 3375, 2.1%). The baseline characteristics of the cohort are presented in Table 1 (donepezil vs. all other AChEIs) and Appendix B (Supporting Information) (donepezil vs. individual AChEIs). The median age was 82, and more than half (59.8%) were women. Transdermal rivastigmine use was overwhelmingly limited to BC (> 99%), given that it was the only province in the cohort that publicly funded the product during the time period [8, 18]. The donepezil group appeared to have a lower comorbidity burden, with lower percentages of certain comorbidities in comparison to the other AChEIs. However, across most characteristics, with the exception of index year, SMDs were small (< 0.1), indicating minimal baseline imbalance between groups.

A breakdown of incidence rates is presented in Table 2 (most responsible diagnosis) and Table 3 (all diagnosis fields) for all AChEI groups, follow‐up durations. The rate‐ratio test indicated a lower malignant arrhythmia incidence among donepezil users; however, this association was not significant when all diagnosis fields were included (Table 3). The results of the primary outcome are presented in Table 4. Follow‐up time was heavily right skewed, with a median of 386 days and a maximum of 2616 days. The cumulative incidence curve showed that donepezil was associated with a lower risk of hospitalization for malignant arrhythmia relative to the other AChEIs combined (Log‐Rank, p = 0.0026; Wilcoxon, p = 0.0008). This was supported by crude regression (unadjusted HR 0.52, 95% CI 0.34, 0.80). Adjustment for covariates (Table 4) generated similar results (adjusted HR 0.55, 95% CI 0.36, 0.85). Galantamine initiators drove the decreased hazard in donepezil; when we assessed AChEI initiators separately, donepezil initiators had a significantly decreased hazard ratio (aHR 0.49, 95% CI 0.31, 0.77) relative to galantamine, but not compared to rivastigmine (aHR 0.90, 95% CI 0.36, 2.27). Assessment of two‐way interaction terms found no significant interaction terms among any covariates in the Cox model.

In the secondary analyses, utilizing all 25 DAD diagnosis fields removed any association between AChEI initiator group and hazard for hospitalization for malignant arrhythmia (aHR 1.01, 95% CI 0.79, 1.30). Reducing follow‐up to 365 days increased the strength of association for the primary outcome (donepezil vs. other AChEIs, most responsible diagnosis field only, aHR 0.43, 95% CI 0.24, 0.79) and did not change the secondary outcome (donepezil vs. other AChEIs, all diagnosis fields, aHR 0.77, 95% CI 0.53, 1.14). Analyses on differing hospitalization date changed no conclusions; hazard ratio breakdowns for hospitalization date are found in Appendix C (Supporting Information). Slightly modifying exclusion criteria by excluding occurrences of previous malignant arrhythmia in the one‐year prior to index date to only use instances in the primary DAD diagnoses field also changed no conclusions. The number of subjects excluded due to previous malignant arrhythmia decreased by 22 (from 156 to 134), leading to a slight increase in sample size to 162 638. Adjusting the model by propensity score yielded similar results to the main model (aHR 0.52, 95% CI 0.317 to 0.858) (Appendix D (Supporting Information)).

Overall, no consistent dose–response pattern was observed across AChEI medications. In the interaction term models, the association between donepezil and malignant arrhythmia did not differ meaningfully by dose category (Appendix E (Supporting Information)). Among patients receiving low doses, donepezil was associated with a lower hazard compared with other AChEIs (aHR 0.49, 95% CI 0.30–0.79), whereas the estimate for medium/high doses was imprecise and not statistically significant (aHR 0.75, 95% CI 0.26–2.14). Within‐drug comparisons in the stratified analysis also showed no evidence of a dose–response relationship for any medication where medium/high versus low doses were not associated with increased risk among users of donepezil or galantamine, and estimates for rivastigmine oral and patch users could not be obtained due to sparse data (Appendix E).

Discussion

We assessed the association between donepezil and hospitalization for malignant arrhythmia in a large cohort over 8 years. We found that donepezil was associated with a 45%–57% lower hazard for hospitalization for malignant arrhythmia in comparison to other AChEIs; however, this should be interpreted with caution for several reasons. First, the association dissipated when all hospitalization diagnosis fields were examined. Second, the donepezil group had a lower comorbidity burden which, along with having limited information on covariates, suggests residual confounding is likely contributing to the observed decreased risk. Third, because a non‐fatal event was used as the main outcome, death was a potential competing risk. Nonetheless, this study provides additional empirical data regarding the cardiac safety of cholinesterase inhibitors.

Overall, these results were not expected given our working hypothesis [5]. There was a 2007 report from the Australian Adverse Drug Reaction Advisory Committee which found galantamine to have the highest rate of reporting for arrhythmia, although this was not a comparative study and only reporting rates were noted [19]. The results are also not consistent with the hypothesis based upon the CredibleMeds warning and case reports for donepezil. CredibleMeds uses available evidence assessment method in the listing of medications—including (but not limited to) case reports, FDA label changes, and pharmacological literature [4]. As a whole, this study offers a measure of reassurance regarding the cardiac safety of AChEIs; previous studies have found that for other known‐risk medications (citalopram, escitalopram, macrolide antibiotics), when extrapolated for incidence rate (with hospitalization outcomes determined using the same ICD‐10‐CA codes), there was an incidence rate of 256 per 100 000 person‐years for citalopram users and 324 per 100 000 person‐years for macrolide users [12, 20]. These are considerably higher than any of the incidence rates calculated for our cohort. As such, although comparative estimates between AChEIs must be interpreted cautiously, the consistently low absolute incidence of malignant arrhythmia across all agents is clinically reassuring and aligns with the broader safety profile observed in other population‐based studies [21, 22].

A recent systematic review assessing major cardiac events with donepezil in 60 randomized‐controlled trials found that major adverse cardiac events are associated with donepezil. However, none of these events were arrhythmia events, and no increased risk of cardiac events is seen with donepezil in comparison to placebo [23]. Notably, a 2023 study in the Japanese adverse drug event database found that the adjusted reported odds ratio for donepezil use and combined TdP/ventricular fibrillation/ventricular tachycardia occurrence or combined QT prolongation/TdP was 9.1 for the former and 9.3 for the latter, with neither galantamine nor rivastigmine noted anywhere in the study [24]. It is important to recognize that the Japanese study was non‐comparative and dependent on pharmacovigilance reports, while our study uses comparative population‐based retrospective analysis and provides additional data using comprehensive databases, enriching the knowledge on AChEI use and arrhythmias in a real‐world setting.

Prescribing trends may have also influenced our findings. Although no specific AChEI is recommended [8, 18], donepezil is typically given to be the first‐line treatment for AD, with switching to galantamine or rivastigmine requiring reassessment of the patient's condition [18]. The high proportion of donepezil use in our cohort indicates that this practice is consistent among all Canadian provinces. This may indicate channeling bias [25] if the factors related to exposure selection were correlated with malignant arrythmias. Given trends in our cohort (i.e., non‐donepezil group having higher comorbidity burden) and previous literature [11, 26], residual confounding pertaining to higher comorbidities among galantamine and rivastigmine users could perhaps explain the decreased hazard of hospitalization in donepezil initiators in our cohort, even with adjustment for available confounders. In addition, formulary policies during the study period likely contributed to differential prescribing patterns across AChEIs. For example, in Ontario—where most cohort members reside—donepezil has been available without restriction, whereas both galantamine and rivastigmine have been subject to Limited Use criteria requiring specific diagnostic documentation and evidence of clinical improvement for continued coverage [27]. These policy differences may have resulted in healthier or less clinically complex patients being preferentially initiated on donepezil, while galantamine and rivastigmine were more often prescribed in situations of diagnostic uncertainty, treatment refractoriness, or greater cognitive or functional impairment. Such structural differences in access reinforce the likelihood of residual confounding and channeling bias, even after adjustment for available covariates, and may partially explain the lower observed hazard among donepezil users. As such, the observed association is therefore more plausibly explained by unmeasured differences in baseline health status, disease severity, and clinical decision‐making processes rather than a protective pharmacologic effect of donepezil.

Several reasons may account for why the use of all 25 DAD diagnosis fields dissipated association between hospitalization and medication use. Field 2 to 25 diagnoses may be from outside of the hospital admission—for example, one diagnosis type for any diagnosis from field 2 to 25 is “pre‐admit comorbidity diagnosis”. If an ECG (the gold standard for diagnosing cardiac conditions) [28] was not used to make a pre‐admit diagnosis (as may be the case outside a hospital environment), it cannot be said for certain of its accuracy. It may also be that a malignant arrhythmia in fields 2 to 25 was associated with something else (such as the most responsible diagnosis that was not a malignant arrhythmia or an intervention such as a medication causing a malignant arrhythmia after admission—which are diagnoses that may be identified through diagnosis type and/or prefix). Altogether, non‐primary diagnosis fields may just contribute noise.

Key strengths of this study are that the cohort covers an extensive Canadian population (the provinces included make up 2/3 of the Canadian population) over an extended period while using real‐world data. However, some limitations of our study merit emphasis. First, this cohort only captures individuals covered by provincial public drug plans. Although this reduces sample size, individuals covered by private insurance may be systematically different than those only covered by public drug plans (which may be rather pertinent for Saskatchewan given the low rate of publicly funded users of cognitive enhancers). Furthermore, although AChEIs are all publicly funded in participating provinces, the criteria for initial and continued coverage are not uniform. Use restrictions for galantamine and rivastigmine may have selected for patients with more advanced disease or greater comorbidity burden. In addition, we did not have access to the date loss of follow‐up (such as when a person was no longer covered under provincial health care plan). Subgroup analysis by province was also not possible given limited event occurrence in smaller provinces. Secondly, the rationale for excluding patients who were dispensed donepezil, galantamine, or rivastigmine in the 365 days prior to cohort entry date (index date) is to capture “new” users of AChEIs (as initiator design is advantageous in comparative studies—since it eliminates prevalent user bias and bias due to covariates being affected by medication use itself/reduces the confounding for intermediate characteristics in the causal path) [29, 30]. However, it is still possible that some patients may have received AChEIs prior to the earliest fiscal year captured by our particular NPDUIS dataset (which may further impact the residual confounding present). Thirdly, mortality could not be assessed through this study. All‐cause mortality differences among AChEI groups could provide an overall safety endpoint. Fourthly, although the definition of malignant arrhythmia has been validated by previous studies for the use of comparative studies between medications [12, 20], any relationship with AChEI medication use (or any medication use for that matter) would be purely speculative. Fourthly, transdermal rivastigmine could not be assessed separately in this study given that it only makes up a tiny proportion of the total cohort (2.1%) with at most 8 events of malignant arrhythmia observed dependent upon follow‐up definition. This is due to not all Canadian provinces providing public funding for transdermal rivastigmine, as clearly evidenced in the provincial breakdown of transdermal rivastigmine initiators in Appendix B (Supporting Information). Assessing whether a different medication administration method may affect results when it comes to adverse events associated with medication use is always of interest. Finally, not all confounders can be measured, as we did not have access to patient records pertaining to diagnoses by their primary care physician, cognitive test scores, functional assessments, or the clinical rationale for selecting a specific AChEI. Although usage of certain medications can be a proxy measure of certain health conditions, the presence of exact diagnoses would be relevant to adjust for.

In conclusion, our results do not support the hypothesis that patients who use donepezil are at a higher risk of malignant arrhythmia in comparison to those who use galantamine or rivastigmine. Further research may be warranted to further assess the impact of comorbidities on association with malignant arrhythmia related mortality.
