An efficient and interpretable intrusion detection framework for software-defined networks with multi-class imbalanced data using genetic and GAN-based optimization
Authors: Md. Tamim Hasan Saykat, Md. Ehsanul Haque, Fahmid Al Farid, Rakib Hossen, Jia Uddin, Hezerul Bin Abdul Karim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Software-Defined Networks (SDNs) are already susceptible to cyber-attacks because of the weak security mechanisms and limited resources. This is predicted to be intensified in the Internet of Things era with a forecast of more than 29 billion connected devices in 2030, thereby broadening the threat surface significantly and making it increasingly difficult for cybersecurity solutions to address these threats. Conventional intrusion detection systems(IDSs) on SDNs are challenged by imbalanced multi-class data, high-dimensional and noisy features, and a lack of interpretability. To address these challenges, this study proposes a hybrid SDN-based IDS framework that integrates Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) to handle imbalanced datasets, one-way ANOVA and Genetic Algorithm (GA) for feature selection, baseline classifier optimization using Grid Search and Explainable AI techniques such as SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP), Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations (LIME), Morris sensitivity analysis and permutation combination to achieve robust, accurate, and interpretable intrusion detection. The grid search-optimized XGBoost model, denoted as OptiXGB-IDS, demonstrates high performance on the InSDN dataset, with a test accuracy of 99.87%, and macro-averaged precision, recall, and F1-score of 0.9618, 0.9951 and 0.9775 respectively. In addition, the model achieves a Cohen’s kappa coefficient of 0.9982 and a Brier score of 0.0024, with an inference time of 0.026312 ms per flow. In order to further test cross-dataset robustness, the proposed framework was also validated on the CIC-IDS2017 dataset, where OptiXGB-IDS achieved the highest test accuracy of 98.70% and outperformed all other competing models. The proposed hybrid GA-GAN-XAI framework significantly improves the IDS performance by providing key traffic flow features and improving spatial and temporal feature learning, making it highly robust and providing a very precise and reliable solution for detecting complex and previously unseen cyber threats in SDN-based IoT environments.

Introduction

The rapid digitalization of society, industry, and critical infrastructure has turned computer networks into the backbone of modern society. Cloud, edge, and Internet of Things (IoT) computing have dramatically expanded network scale and heterogeneity of the network extensively, creating massive volumes of dynamic traffic1. SDN has become a key technology due to its ability to decouple the control plane from the data plane and offer programmable interfaces for flexible traffic engineering and security policies across traditional vendor-locked networks. At the same time, the centralized controller becomes a high-value single point of failure, where the southbound or northbound APIs can inject malicious flow rules,hijack paths or depleted controller resources, and the global visibility of the controller makes it a favorable target for reconnaissance, denial-of-service (DoS), and data-exfiltration attacks2. These threats are even more magnified in SDN-enabled IoT and Industrial Internet of Things (IIoT), where billions of resource-constrained devices produce bursty and protocol-diverse traffic, and system failures can directly affect safety or lead to economic consequences3.

According to recent macro-level statistics, the total damage caused by cybercrime worldwide may go up to 10.5 trillion USD per year by 2025, with the average cost of a single data breach estimated at approximately 4.45 million USD globally, and over 5 million USD in the United States4. Small and medium-sized businesses are especially susceptible, experiencing an estimated 43–46% of all cyberattacks, and an average direct loss of approximately 120,000 USD per attack, as well as a significantly increased likelihood of business shutdown in the wake of major attacks5. These findings show that efficient and timely intrusion detection is no longer a matter of purely technical value, but a precondition for business continuity. Over the last several years, the use of Machine Learning (ML) and Deep Learning (DL)-based IDS has received intense interest due to its ability to identify nonlinear patterns and detect even the most subtle anomalies that cannot be identified by handcrafted signatures6. Several surveys demonstrate that DL based IDS can outperform classical methods in a diverse range of new technologies, cloud, edge, IoT, and SDN by training high-dimensional representations of traffic data. Most ML and DL IDS-based models ensure that the controller and the data plane are safe against volumetric DoS/DDoS attacks, scanning, botnets, and multi-stage attacks in the SDN domain specifically2,3. This has been greatly expedited by the introduction of the InSDN dataset, which is one of the famous publicly available flow-level benchmarks, and is SDN-specific. It offers realistic traces of traffic that contain benign traffic and a variety of attacks on various SDN components. It also records a multi-class environment with very skewed label distributions, including large volumes of Probe and DDoS traffic, and other minor categories which consist of brute-force, web attacks, botnets, and the occasional user-to-root (U2R) intrusions7.

Even with this latest progress, some detailed surveys of IDS in emerging technologies and SDN suggest a number of gaps that remain unaddressed and that prevent many state-of-the-art systems from being safely deployed in production. First, class imbalance, as well as the lack of data realism remain major limitations. Most IDS models continue to use outdated datasets (e.g., KDD99, NSL-KDD, CICIDS-2017) that don’t fully represent modern SDN/IoT traffic, also they are biased toward more of a majority behaviour. Because of this, the model becomes overfit on the majority classes and the ability to detect rare but high-impact attacks is significantly reduced8,9. Secondly, high-dimensional noisy feature spaces and lack of accurate strategies to select principal components often result in overly complex models that are hard to train, overfit, and expensive to implement in real-time deployment. At the same time, the overall performance of IDS is highly sensitive to preprocessing and feature engineering; yet, many studies still stick to the generic or ad-hoc feature selection without a rigorous statistical validation10. Third, only a few studies explicitly use Explainable AI (XAI) Techniques (e.g., LIME, SHAP) for IDS. These studies are mostly restricted to classical enterprise data, instead of SDN-based IoT or complex multi-class scenarios. Therefore, network operators can’t clearly understand which features influenced the model’s decisions the most, how different types of attacks are being distinguished, and how robust the IDS is against new threats11. Fourth, there are still incomplete evaluation practices. Many SDN based IDS studies report very high accuracy on small datasets without k-fold cross-validation, formal statistical testing, external validation, or ablation analysis. Also, computational complexity, memory footprint and per-flow inference time are rarely analysed,although these factors are essential to deployment on resource-constrained controllers and SDN based IoT gateways12. Many recent studies on deep learning (DL) for IDS in emerging technologies have shown that there is still a large gap between the results of controlled lab tests and the requirements for working in real production networks13. Finally, while some recent SDN and IoT-based IDS frameworks have started to use resampling techniques or generative models like GANs to address class imbalance problems, most of these are usually focused on a limited number of attack types (such as only DDoS), or cannot integrate class imbalance handling, feature optimization, and interpretability within the same architecture14.

Consequently, it remains an open research problem to create an SDN-based IDS that can handle severely imbalanced multi-class traffic, at the same time, it uses a concise but informative feature set suitable for real-time inference, able to provide transparent explanations of its decisions, verified through rigorous and statistically sound evaluation on realistic SDN benchmarks. In this research, we proposed an optimized, SDN-centric intrusion detection architecture called OptiXGB-IDS to overcome the aforementioned limitations. In OptiXGB-IDS, GAN techniques were first used to solve the data imbalance challenge, so that realistic network traffic can be generated for minority attack classes.

Moreover, for the feature selection and optimization, we first applied One-way ANOVA followed by a genetic algorithm. Through this process, we were able to select a small subset of the most effective and discriminative features from the original high-dimensional traffic-flow features. Finally, at the model level, the XGBoost classifier is optimized through grid search along with other baseline models. We also ensure a model decision-making process by using Explainable AI techniques such as SHAP and LIME. Finally, to identify the most sensitive and influential features, we apply Morris sensitivity analysis along with permutation-based feature importance methods.

So we can summarize the key contributions of this work as follows: We develop a leakage-free SDN intrusion detection framework that integrates class-specific WGAN-GP hybrid resampling for distribution-preserving balancing of minority attack classes, and dual-stage ANOVA–genetic algorithm feature selection to yield a compact, discriminative, and methodologically rigorous detection pipeline.We propose OptiXGB-IDS, a grid-search-optimized XGBoost-based intrusion detection framework, and validate it through dataset-aligned comparative evaluation, highlighting its high efficiency, interpretability, and methodological rigor across benchmark settings.We validate the proposed framework through a broad and rigorous experimental protocol involving two public imbalanced multiclass datasets, cross-validation, ablation analysis, statistical testing, robustness evaluation, and explainable AI analysis.We establish the cross-dataset robustness of OptiXGB-IDS through comprehensive validation on an additional benchmark, confirming its strong generalizability and consistent effectiveness beyond the primary dataset.Overall, these contributions provide a robust, interpretable, and high-performing framework for Intrusion Detection in software-defined networks.

Literature review

Machine learning (ML) methods have been widely used in cybersecurity over the last few decades because they can find hidden patterns that help distinguish malicious from legitimate activity15,16. In particular, ML is an effective approach for detecting anomalies in network traffic. Consequently, numerous studies have explored ML, Deep Learning (DL), and hybrid models for the development of Intrusion Detection Systems (IDS).

ML approaches

ML-based intrusion detection has been extensively investigated because of its ability to model complex traffic behavior, reduce manual feature dependency, and support data-driven decision-making in cybersecurity environments. Within this direction, Lin et al.17 proposed a cloud-based IoT intrusion detection framework combining a multi-layer security architecture with Multi-Feature Extraction Extreme Learning Machine (MFE-ELM). The model achieved 96.53% accuracy and required 4.64 s of training time; it was better than the standard ELM, SVM, and BP baselines. Nevertheless, the work is limited to a single, relatively small dataset, lacks Explainable AI (XAI),fails to validate the results via cross-validation techniques or external validation, and doesn’t report confidence intervals or formal statistical analysis. In a related effort to improve interpretability, Mohale et al.18suggested an XAI-enhanced ML-based intrusion detection system,which fused Decision Trees, XGBoost, CatBoost, MLP and Gaussian Naive Bayes on the UNSW-NB15 dataset. CatBoost, Decision Tree, and XGBoost achieved 87% of test accuracy with FPR 0.07 and FNR 0.11–0.12, while GNB reached 73% (AUC 0.83). However, it utilizes only one dataset, does not offer cross-validation, statistical significance analysis, and external validation, and provides no discussion and experimentation on low-resource. Similarly, Patil et al.6 developed an explainable intrusion detection framework with an ensemble of decision tree, random forest and SVM using feature reduction, SMOTE/undersampling, and local explanations based on LIME. The individual base models achieve 88.66% and F1-score of 0.77, which is lower than the proposed voting classifier of 96.25% and F1-score 0.89. Although these are almost perfect metrics, but it is evaluated using only one dataset and lack cross-validation, statistical significance analysis, external validation, or real edge deployment situations. Beyond ensemble-based classification, Ahmad et al.19 built a machine learning based system using feature clustering on the UNSW-NB15 dataset, where clusters were applied for Flow, MQTT, and TCP protocols. They minimized high dimensionality overfitting and imbalance in the dataset by clustering. The clustering process terminates the overfitting that was the curse of dimensionality and data-set inequity. The Proposed model achieved an accuracy of 98.67% binary and 97.37% multiclass classification. Still, the research doesn’t provide XAI, is limited to a specific small dataset, has no analysis of statistical significance, or edge-deployment validation, and resource profiling. To further address feature dimensionality and computational efficiency, Seth et al.20 proposed a time-efficient smart intrusion detection model, using a hybrid of the two feature selection methods, specifically, the Random Forest and the PCA, a LightGBM classifier to reduce 80 features to 24 principal components on CIC-IDS-2018 traffic flows. It achieves 97.7% test accuracy, 99.3% precision, and 96% recall. Nevertheless, the experimental validation is only highlighted on a single dataset, lacks cross-validation, explainable AI techniques, statistical significance tests, external validation, computational/edge-deployment profiling, and does not examine the learning-curve overfitting. In the context of critical infrastructure security, Kumar et al21 investigated ML-based intrusion detection for critical infrastructure using the SWaT water-treatment testbed. They relatively evaluated supervised (Random Forest, 1D-CNN, LSTM) and unsupervised (Isolation Forest, one-class SVM) models, and focused on the F1-score under severe class imbalance. The Random Forest achieved 99.89% of test accuracy, 99.30% precision, 99.90% recall, and 99.60% F1-score. However, the assessment is confined to only one publicly available small dataset, lacks XAI, has no cross-validation or external validation, does not incorporate comprehensive statistical analyses, and discusses the Computational cost. More recently, Munna et al.11 introduced a two-tier IDS architecture for multi-class classification of KRACK attack, and legitimate Wi-Fi traffic on AWID3 dataset using Gaussian-noise augmentation,Principal Component Analysis (PCA), heterogeneous base learners such as SVM, RF, KNN, MLP, XGBoost models. Their pipeline achieved 98% test accuracy with macro F1 0.98. Nevertheless, the study is restricted to a small dataset, lacks XAI, no cross-validation or external validation, and provides no discussion of the Computational cost, statistical analysis, and edge deployment. Overall, the current IDS-based studies using ML show high potential in detecting malicious network behavior, especially by using ensemble learning, feature selection, imbalance-aware preprocessing, and explainability-oriented modeling. However, most approaches remain constrained by single-dataset evaluation, limited external benchmarking, insufficient statistical validation, incomplete interpretability analysis, and inadequate deployment-oriented assessment.

Deep learning approaches

Deep learning has also been widely explored for IDS because of its ability to learn hierarchical and nonlinear representations from high-dimensional traffic data. Aleesa et al.22 suggested a deep learning IDS system using a modified UNSW-NB15 dataset formed by combining three CSV files with traffic categorized into ten classes, ten training ANN, DNN, and RNN-LSTM models. The Proposed DNN model achieved 99.59% test accuracy for multiclass and 99.22% test accuracy for binary class, outperforming previous machine learning and deep learning approaches. Even so, there is no detailed complexity or latency analysis, lack of XAI, potential overfitting, limited to a single dataset, while cross-validation, formal statistical analysis, and external validation aren’t reported. Kesswani et al.23 proposed a Deep Neural Network-based intrusion detection mechanism using eight flow features identified from KDD-Cup’99, NSL-KDD, & UNSW-NB15 datasets, with a 20-layer DNN classifier integrated at the 6BR router. Their model achieved 96.3% accuracy on KDD-Cup99, 91.5% on NSL-KDD, and 99.2% on UNSW-NB15. However, this study does not include any explanations regarding the use of XAI, fails to validate via cross-validation techniques, provides no confidence intervals, and Computational cost and latency for large-scale IoT deployments aren’t analyzed. CNN-based IDS models have also been investigated by transforming tabular traffic features into image-like representations. For instance, Kim et al.24 developed a CNN-based intrusion detection framework of the DoS attacks, which transforms KDD and CSE-CIC-IDS2018 tabular features into small RGB or grayscale images and systematically evaluates 18 CNNs against an RNN baseline. The CNN attained 99% on the KDD dataset and 91.5% on CSE-CIC-IDS2018, obviously much higher than the RNN (65% accuracy), with precision, recall, and F1 also reported. Nevertheless, they do not report any computational cost, attack scope limited to DoS, lack XAI, no k-fold cross-validation across multiple random splits, statistical and external validation, and provide no discussion on real-time deployment. In addition to centralized DL architectures, federated and distributed learning strategies have been explored for IoT monitoring. Prabhugaonkar et al.25 proposed an IoT monitoring system based on deep learning that filters MQTT traffic using supervised DNNs and unsupervised autoencoders, each trained in a centralized and federated setting with FedSGD, FedAvg, and FedProx, based on the MQTT set dataset. Their proposed federated DNN achieved 95% of test accuracy with AUC 0.9535, while the test accuracy of federated and centralized autoencoders was approximately 80%. Still, the study is limited specific dataset,no non-IID client experiments, lacks XAI, No real-world deployment or external network validation, and computational cost and formal statistical analysis not reported. In general, DL-based IDS methods have shown promising representational power and high prediction accuracy on multiple benchmark datasets. However, many studies still suffer from limited interpretability, weak deployment-oriented evaluation, insufficient robustness analysis, and validation protocols. In SDN and IoT, these limitations are especially pertinent, as detection models need to be accurate, efficient, explainable, reliable, and generalizable.

Hybrid approaches

To integrate the advantages of traditional ML, deep representation learning, optimization algorithms, and ensemble decision- making, hybrid IDS frameworks have appeared. Bountzis et al.26 introduced a deep hybrid one-class NIDS, OCSVM-AE, in which an OC-SVM trained only on the benign NetFlow traffic is used to give decision scores that augment the feature space of a deep autoencoder to detect anomalies on the UNSW-NB15v2 dataset. The model attains an AUC of 0.974 and a FPR of 0.046 with competitive F1, GM, BA, and DR against the baseline model. However, the research is based on a single dataset, has a moderate computational cost, lacks XAI, has no adversarial/drift robustness, and has no statistical significance analysis. Khan et al.27 suggested an adaptive hybrid IIoT intrusion detection framework that was based on an ANN classifier, along with a combination of Genetic Algorithms and Particle Swarm Optimization to optimize features and weights on the merged IoTID20 and IoT Botnet traffic. The proposed GA-PSO-tuned ANN achieved a test accuracy of 99.5%,with a precision of 0.97, a recall of 0.98, F1-score 0.975. Even so, the research doesn’t provide any Interoperability, statistical analysis, external validation, or latency/throughput benchmarks. Alhayan et al.28 built the IDCS-ELIBWO system that integrates min-max normalization, Remora Optimization-based feature selection, an ensemble of DBN-GRU-LSTM classifiers, and Improved Beluga Whale Optimization of hyperparameter tuning on the UNSW-NB15 dataset. The model achieves 99.77% accuracy and average precision, recall, and F1 of 98.8. But, it is assessed on a specific dataset, lacks XAI, cross-validation, and external validation, so there is still a risk of dataset-specific overfitting. Sensitivity to data quality, statistical test, or real-time deployment isn’t reported. Argud et al.29 proposed a determinantal leakage-free evaluation of three classical IDS classifiers (NB, LR, LDA) and a hybrid Autoencoder-embedding plus Logistic Regression pipeline. The hybrid model (AE+LR) scored AUC 0.904 on the NSL-KDD dataset and 0.996 with F1 0.9715 on the CICIDS2017 dataset. Nevertheless, the research concentrates on binary detection, does not consider the integrated XAI, cross-validation, cross-edge or external deployment analysis, and the learning-curve diagnostics. More recently, Shahin et al.30 introduced a two-stage hybrid federated learning IDS combining a benign-only Variational Autoencoder(VAE) filter, and a Histogram-based Gradient Boosting (HGB) classifier over N-BaIoT traffic from nine IoT devices. The proposed VAE-HGB pipeline achieved 99.14% test accuracy with macro precision 98.9%, recall and F1 score around 98.5%, and 98.7% , respectively. However, the study is restricted to a single dataset, lacks XAI, has no analysis of statistical significance, or edge-deployment validation, and resource profiling. Overall, Hybrid IDS methods offer considerable scope for advancing representation learning, optimization, feature selection, and ensemble classification. However, the current hybrid systems are still constrained by partial interpretability, dataset-specific assessment, weak statistical validation, insufficient robustness testing, and lack of deployment-oriented analysis. These gaps motivate the development of a unified IDS framework that integrates imbalance-aware data generation, compact feature optimization, strong classification performance, explainable decision support, and efficiency-aware evaluation for SDN-based intrusion detection. Data collection

The dataset used in this study is the InSDN Intrusion Detection Dataset, which is a publicly available SDN-based network intrusion database on Kaggle. It contains a total number of 343,889 records of network flows with 85 features in each. Among these, 78 are numeric features, that is, they contain detailed flow statistics, timing behavior, and protocol-level features, and the other 7 are categorical (object) features, which constitute protocol identifiers and other qualitative network features. The dataset includes nine different types of attacks, which are a wide range of harmful activities that can be identified in Software-Defined Networks. This variety, together with the large size of the dataset and the number of features, renders InSDN a strong and demanding benchmark when it comes to the assessment of modern intrusion detection systems. Along with InSDN, a secondary experiment was carried out to assess the effectiveness of our proposed framework on the CICIDS2017 dataset. The dataset is a standard in intrusion detection studies and consists of 2,830,743 network flow records (benign traffic and 14 attacks). The attacks cover a broad range of network risks, such as distributed denial of service, port scanning, brute force, web attacks, and privilege escalation, against which intrusion detection systems can be evaluated in a comprehensive manner. Dataset splitting

Correct dataset partitioning scheme is required to ensure objective model evaluation and avoid information leakage. The dataset was split into 80% training and 20% testing after data collection. This split was prior to any preprocessing steps such as normalization, feature selection or balancing of classes to ensure that no data of the testing set is utilized during training. The dataset consists of 343,889 samples, 275,111 of which are utilized in the training process, and 68,778 of which are utilized for testing. This partition allows the model to learn patterns based on the training data and is evaluated on completely unknown data thereby providing a valid indication of its generalization ability. Data preprocessing seperatley on training and testing

The training and testing datasets were stored in two separate data frames namely train_df and test_df, to ensure a leakage-free preprocessing pipeline. All preprocessing operations were conducted in the same order on both datasets; however, preprocessing parameters were estimated using only the training data and then applied to the testing data. In this approach, there are no issues of global statistical bias that could be introduced due to combined processing. The preprocessing steps include feature encoding, missing value imputation, outlier handling where the IQR method is used, and feature standardization. Specifically, numeric feature selection was initially determined using the training data, and the identical feature space was imposed on the testing data. The bounds for IQR-based outlier capping were calculated only from the training distribution and applied uniformly to both datasets. Likewise, median-based imputation and standardization using StandardScaler were also fitted on the training data and reapplied to the testing data. This method guarantees methodological consistency of the evaluation by keeping the same transformation logic and restricting all statistical learning to the training set, therefore preventing information leakage and keeping the validity and generalizability of the evaluation intact.

Hybrid sampling strategy with class-specific WGAN-GP

After partitioning the dataset, the problem of class imbalance was addressed exclusively within the training subset to maintain the integrity of the evaluation process. The testing set was not processed at any stage to ensure unbiased model evaluation and to eliminate the possibility of information leakage. The original training data were quite imbalanced, with majority classes (Probe, DDoS, Normal, and DoS) having significantly more samples, whereas minority classes (U2R, BOTNET, and Web-Attack) had significantly fewer samples. To address this concern, a hybrid sampling method was adopted, which combines controlled undersampling of majority classes and synthetic oversampling of minority classes by a class-specific Wasserstein Generative Adversarial Network with Gradient Penalty (WGAN-GP). The resampling method was developed to minimize extreme skewness, but maintain the natural distribution properties of the data, instead of using aggressive balancing. The number of majority classes was reduced to a predetermined value, and the number of minority classes was increased by creating synthetic samples to a moderate target size without achieving strict uniformity between classes. The different classes were independently modeled with a specific generator-critic pair to conserve the distribution of features in the classes, as well as to avoid mode collapse across different types of attacks. This modeling by class is to guarantee that, even the rare attack patterns do not lose their unique statistical properties in augmentation.

Baseline classifiers

In order to assess the work of the suggested intrusion detection model, a number of classical and ensemble-based machine learning models have been used as baseline models. Decision Tree (DT) is a non-parametric model, which splits the feature space by recursive partitions to build a model in uniformly labeled regions and provide a comprehensible model structure. Random Forest (RF) is a combination of several decision trees that uses bootstrapped data samples and random feature selection in order to come up with a system that reduces model variance and prevents overfitting. Extremely Randomized Trees (Extra Trees, ET) algorithm that is abbreviated as Extremely Randomized Trees introduces additional randomness to split threshold selection, which results in superior generalization results and higher processing speeds in high-dimensional data. K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN) is an instance-based, non-parametric classifier, which uses the majority vote of the nearest neighbors to label the data, which works well when the boundaries between classes are well represented. Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) is a feedforward neural network that is capable of learning the complicated nonlinear patterns by using multiple hidden layers and nonlinear activation functions to process the high-dimensional network traffic characteristics. Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) builds decision tree ensembles by building a sequence of models, which is optimized by a differentiable loss function with regularization to provide superior predictive performance and to be able to work with large datasets. Lastly, CatBoost is a gradient boosting model that is optimized to work with tabular data, which is highly efficient with categorical variables and reduces overfitting by ordered boosting and more complex regularization methods. Collectively, these base models offer a complete reference point on tree-based, instance-based, and neural network models on the InSDN intrusion detection dataset.

Benchmark data preprocessing

The CICIDS2017 dataset was used as an additional benchmark dataset in order to benchmark the proposed framework. The preprocessing pipeline that was used in the main framework was used identically with no alterations made to maintain methodological consistency. These involve partitioning of the dataset into training and testing, encoding of features, alignment of numeric features, treatment of outliers with the IQR, imputation of missing values, and standardization of features. All preprocessing parameters were trained only on the training subset and then applied to the testing subset, hence a strictly leakage-free evaluation protocol was followed. The training and testing data sets were processed separately and also with the same logic of transformation so that there is consistency in each phase. It is noteworthy that this benchmarking procedure is not a cross-dataset validation. Rather, it is aimed at testing whether the proposed methodology is stable and effective when used on a different benchmark dataset under identical preprocessing conditions.

Interpretability, sensitivity, and feature importance analysis

Based on the output of all candidate models, the most stable and high-performing model was chosen for detailed investigation. We used a combination of interpretability and sensitivity methods to determine the contribution of each feature and how strong the predictions of the model are. In particular, SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlainations) and LIME (Local Interpretable Model-Agnostic Explanations) were applied to provide both the global and local explanations of model predictions. Also, Morris sensitivity analysis and permutation importance were used to measure the impact of each feature on model output and to determine whether feature rankings are consistent. It is a comprehensive method that allows interpretability and validation of the relevance of features in the proposed framework.

5-fold cross validation across all models

In order to measure the strength and the generalization of all models, a 5-fold cross-validation was conducted. The dataset were separated into five equal subsets; in each iteration four subsets used in training and one in validation. Evaluation method

Three complementary measures are used to assess the models and select the most effective model: F1 score, cross-validation (CV) score, and Brier score. These metrics give different information regarding model performance and they are used jointly to guarantee strong selection.

The rationale behind the choice of F1 score is that it maintains a balance between precision and recall, and is thus especially useful with class-imbalanced datasets where accuracy alone may give incorrect information. In the case of multi-class problems, we apply the macro averaged F1 score which is computed as:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text {F1}_{\text {macro}} = \frac{1}{C} \sum _{i=1}^{C} \frac{2 \cdot \text {Precision}_i \cdot \text {Recall}_i}{\text {Precision}_i + \text {Recall}_i}$$\end{document}where C is the number of classes, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text {Precision}_i$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text {Recall}_i$$\end{document} are the precision and recall for class i. This ensures that each class contributes equally to the overall metric, preventing the majority classes from dominating the evaluation.

The stability and generalizability of the model on other subsets of the data is assessed using cross-validation (CV) score. The mean of the F1 scores across CV folds will decrease the chances of being overfitted to a particular train-test split, and will give us a more accurate prediction of performance once unseen data is seen.

The Brier score is incorporated to estimate the calibration of predicted probabilities, rather than the final predicted class labels. It is defined as:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text {Brier Score} = \frac{1}{N} \sum _{t=1}^{N} \sum _{i=1}^{C} (f_{t,i} - o_{t,i})^2$$\end{document}where N is the number of samples, C is the number of classes, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$f_{t,i}$$\end{document} is the predicted probability of sample t belonging to class i, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$o_{t,i}$$\end{document} is 1 if sample t belongs to class i and 0 otherwise. A lower Brier score indicates that the model’s probability estimates are closer to the true outcomes, which is crucial for applications requiring well-calibrated probabilities.

Using a combination of F1 score, CV score and Brier score, we can be certain that the fitted model is not only accurate when predicting classes, but is also stable when considering various data splits, and that its probability estimates are sound.

Statistical analysis of misclassification asymmetry using the bowker symmetry test

To further examine the reliability of model predictions in terms of other than conventional performance measures, Bowker symmetry test was performed. The Bowker test also studies the misclassification errors in the confusion matrix unlike the accuracy-based evaluation. Precisely, it assesses the similarity of predictive errors between classes of each pair. This gives further understanding of the existence of systematic bias of a model on specific classes. The null hypothesis of Bowker test states that the misclassification patterns are symmetric, i.e. the frequency of class i being predicted as class j and vice versa are the same. A p-value of less than 0.05 is statistically significant, showing evidence that the errors are not symmetrical. Table 19 below shows that all models have statistically significant p-values (p < 0.05), which implies that the null hypothesis is rejected in all instances. This establishes the fact that the misclassification trends are asymmetric in all the models. Notably, this finding can not be seen as synonymous with ground truth agreement; rather, it reflects the inherent distribution of the classification errors. Compared to a number of baseline models, including MLP and Decision Tree, OptiXGB-IDS is characterized by a relatively smaller value of chi-square (64.87) and Cramer V (0.0116), which indicates a relatively lower asymmetry. The predictive performance and the control of error distribution are more balanced in OptiXGB-IDS compared to Extra Trees, which has the least asymmetry, on the whole. Conversely, MLP has the greatest asymmetry, implying a greater disparate misclassification across classes. In general, the findings show that all models have good predictive performance, but none of them shows the error patterns that are fully symmetric. Nonetheless, other models like OptiXGB-IDS and CatBoost exhibit a more stable and balanced error behavior, which is more reliable when used in intrusion detection tasks.Table 19Bowker symmetry test results for the evaluated classifiers. Final optiXGB-IDS configuration and model characteristics

Based on the final evaluation, OptiXGB-IDS was retained as the selected intrusion detection model. Table 20 provides a summary of the model, which had a training accuracy of 99.96% and a test accuracy of 99.87%, along with a test AUC of 1.0000, Cohen Kappa of 0.9982 and a Brier score of 0.0024. The overall findings suggest a high level of discriminative performance, a near-optimal performance with ground truth other than chance, and a high level of probabilistic reliability. From a computational perspective, the model required 4.0694 s for training and 1.809655 s for inference, while the per-sample inference cost remained low at 0.026312 ms on the test set. This performance-efficiency balance is particularly important for SDN intrusion detection, where both predictive accuracy and timely decision-making are essential. Table 20 demonstrates the optimized hyperparameters, which indicate that the ultimate OptiXGB-IDS setup is a combination of a moderate depth control and explicit regularization and subsampling. Even though the trained ensemble has 5600 trees, the learned structure is relatively small: the average realized maximum depth is only 2.0789 and the average number of leaves is 4.5127, despite upper limits of 8 and 44, respectively. This suggests that the model is not based on deep or highly branched trees, but rather it is the combination of a set of relatively simple decision structures that gives it strong performance. For interpretability, Fig. 12 presents a surrogate decision tree that approximates part of the learned decision logic. The visualization indicates that Dst Port is the major split, followed by Init Bwd, which divides the data into sections that predominantly consist of DDoS, Web-Attack, Probe, and BOTNET traffic. Specifically, the left branch separates a DDoS-dominant area, with later splits distinguishing Web-Attack and DDoS on the right side of the tree, and Probe and BOTNET on the right side of the tree. Even though this surrogate does not represent the entire ensemble, it offers a clear approximation of the contribution by a small number of semantically meaningful flow features to class separation. Hierarchical explainability analysis of optiXGB-IDS

To examine the interpretability of OptiXGB-IDS beyond aggregate performance measures, we jointly analyzed its behavior using global SHAP feature importance and class-wise local LIME explanations. Taken together, the two analyses indicate that the model relies on a compact and semantically coherent subset of traffic descriptors rather than on diffuse or unstable decision cues. As shown in Fig. 13, Init Bwd Win Byts, Src Port, and Dst Port are the dominant global drivers of prediction, followed by Fwd Header Len, Flow Pkts/s, and Protocol. This global ranking suggests that the model primarily distinguishes attack classes through backward-window behavior, port-level communication patterns, header structure, and flow-rate characteristics. The local LIME results in Fig. 14 refine this picture by showing how the same globally important variables are combined at the instance level through class-specific threshold-based explanations. In particular, Init Bwd Win Byts recurs as a major local determinant for BFA, BOTNET, Web-Attack, U2R, and DoS, while Src Port and Dst Port provide strong local evidence for classes such as DDoS, DoS, Probe, and Web-Attack. Likewise, Pkt Len Max, Bwd Header Len, and Bwd Pkts/s repeatedly appear in the local explanations, indicating that packet-length characteristics, backward-header structure, and packet-rate dynamics are central to class discrimination. This consistency between SHAP and LIME is particularly important, because it shows that the variables identified as globally influential are also the ones driving individual predictions. An additional insight from the combined analysis is that lower-ranked global features can still become locally decisive in specific traffic contexts. For example, Bwd IAT Min contributes to the explanation of Normal traffic, whereas TotLen Bwd Pkts and Down/Up Ratio contribute to Probe, DDoS, and U2R decisions. Thus, the explainability results do not suggest a rigid one-feature model; rather, they show that OptiXGB-IDS is governed by a stable global hierarchy of features that is adaptively specialized through local decision rules. Conclusion

This study addressed the problem of reliable intrusion detection in software-defined networks under severe multi-class imbalance, high-dimensional flow representations, and limited interpretability. We proposed a leakage-free hybrid model, integrating class-specific WGAN-GP balancing, ANOVA-genetic feature selection, grid-search optimization, and explainability analysis, with OptiXGB-IDS serving as the final classifier. Using 13 discriminative features, the proposed framework achieved 99.87% test accuracy and a macro-F1 of 0.9775 on InSDN, together with strong calibration (Brier score 0.0024), near-perfect agreement (kappa 0.9982), stable five-fold validation, and low per-flow inference latency. Additional evaluation on CIC-IDS2017 dataset yielded a test accuracy of 98.70%, the highest among the compared models, while the ablation study results confirmed the advantage of GAN-based balancing over SMOTE-based balancing. SHAP, LIME, Morris and permutation importance analysis further showed that the model predictions were informed by a compact and operationally significant set of traffic features. However, real-world SDN deployment was not evaluated and extremely rare classes such as U2R contained too few samples to estimate per-class reliability with high confidence. Overall, the results support the proposed framework as a strong foundation for accurate and interpretable SDN intrusion detection. Future work should examine its performance in live SDN/IoT settings and under tighter deployment constraints.
