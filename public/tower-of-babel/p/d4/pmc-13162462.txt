Functional implications of lesion morphology in untreated osteochondral lesions of the talus
Authors: Mehmet Orçun Akkurt, Ali Said Nazlıgül, Nihat Yiğit, Sadık Emre Erginoğlu, Nuri Koray Ülgen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOsteochondral lesions of the talus (OLTs) are a significant cause of chronic ankle pain and functional impairment. However, the relationship between lesion morphology and functional scores remains insufficiently studied, particularly in untreated patients who did not undergo surgical intervention. This cross-sectional study investigates the relationship between MRI-based lesion morphology and functional scores in patients with osteochondral lesions of the talus who did not undergo surgery.MethodsThis retrospective study included 182 patients diagnosed with OLT based on ankle MRI between January 2023 and March 2025. Lesion surface area and depth were measured using standardized imaging protocols. Functional scores were evaluated using the American Orthopaedic Foot and Ankle Society (AOFAS) score and the Foot and Ankle Disability Index (FADI). Lesions were categorized into six anatomic regions. Demographic variables and lesion characteristics were analyzed for their associations with lower functional scores.ResultsMedial lesions accounted for 76.3% of cases, with the centromedial region being the most frequently affected (55%). Lesion location did not significantly influence functional scores. In contrast, lesion surface area demonstrated a strong negative correlation with both AOFAS (ρ = − 0.81) and FADI scores (ρ = − 0.81), while lesion depth showed a moderate negative correlation. Age was negatively associated with functional scores. Body mass index and sex did not demonstrate consistent associations.ConclusionThese findings indicate that lesion surface area is a stronger predictor of functional impairment than lesion location or depth in the untreated state of non-surgically treated OLT. The results underscore the value of quantitative lesion assessment in the nonsurgical management of talus osteochondral defects and suggest that MRI-based lesion area measurements may help to characterize functional impairment in patients with untreated OLT.Keywords: Osteochondral lesion, Talus, Ankle MRI, Lesion area, Non-surgical management

Introduction

Osteochondral lesions of the talus (OLTs) involve damage to the articular cartilage and subchondral bone [1]. Although the etiology remains unclear, recent studies suggest that trauma-induced shearing forces—particularly high-impact forces on the cartilage—may lead to subchondral damage through bone crushing, contributing to lesion development [2, 3]. The most common causes of OLTs are ankle instability and prior trauma [4]. Traumatic injury is implicated in nearly all lateral defects and in most medial defects [5]. Ankle sprain or strain is the most prevalent musculoskeletal injury of the lower extremity and is often underestimated in clinical practice because it is commonly treated conservatively [6]. However, accumulating evidence suggests that a substantial proportion of patients may develop chronic pain and instability [7]. OLTs may occur in approximately 50% of ankle sprains and ankle fractures and are a major contributor to chronic ankle pain [8, 9].

Although plain radiographs are commonly used in diagnosing OLTs, they may miss nearly half of the lesions [10]. Even when a lesion is visible, radiographs provide limited information due to their one-dimensional nature. Modern imaging modalities such as computed tomography (CT), arthro-CT, single-photon emission computed tomography (SPECT–CT), and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) offer superior capabilities in detecting lesion size and location [11–14]. MRI, in particular, demonstrates excellent sensitivity and specificity for assessing cartilage and subchondral bone without radiation exposure [15, 16]. A widely used method for lesion localization is presented by Raikin et al. The grid system divides the talar dome into nine regions [17].

Studies have shown that OLTs adversely affect quality of life [18–21]. Prior research has examined the relationships among lesion location, depth, patient demographics, and pain severity. The American Orthopaedic Foot and Ankle Society (AOFAS) Ankle–Hindfoot score is the most commonly applied metric in evaluating ankle function [22]. Various functional assessments are employed, with the Foot and Ankle Disability Index (FADI) also recognized for its reliability in functional scoring [23, 24].

Osteochondral lesions of the talus are managed using a wide spectrum of treatment strategies depending on lesion size, morphology, symptom severity, and patient-related factors [25]. Surgical treatment options have been reported to yield favorable outcomes, including bone marrow stimulation techniques such as microfracture and drilling, osteochondral autograft transfer procedures (mosaicplasty), autologous matrix-induced chondrogenesis, and osteochondral allograft transplantation, particularly in larger or more complex lesions [26, 27]. Recent studies have also emphasized the role of biologic and scaffold-based approaches using autologous or allogenic matrices to enhance cartilage repair and functional recovery [28, 29]. In addition to surgical interventions, nonoperative treatments such as intra-articular hyaluronic acid and platelet-rich plasma injections have demonstrated symptomatic and functional improvement in selected patient populations [30].

Against this broad therapeutic background, we investigated the relationship between lesion area and depth—determined via MRI—and ankle functional scores in patients with OLTs managed conservatively without surgical or intra-articular procedures. The AOFAS and FADI scores were used to evaluate ankle function. Additionally, associations between these scores and patients’ sex, age, body mass index (BMI), and lesion location were analyzed.

Methods

Ethical approval was obtained from the institutional ethics committee, and all patients provided written informed consent before participation. The study included patients presenting to our Orthopedics and Traumatology outpatient clinic with ankle complaints between January 1, 2023, and March 31, 2025, who were diagnosed with OLT based on MRI performed during initial evaluation. During this period, a total of 784 patients who underwent ankle MRI were evaluated, and 182 patients meeting the inclusion criteria were finally included in the analysis (Fig. 1).

STROBE flow diagram of patient selection in the study cohort

Inclusion criteria were as follows: completion of skeletal maturation, a history of pain or trauma ≥ 6 months prior, and diagnosis confirmed by clinical examination and MRI. Exclusion criteria were a history of surgery or fracture involving the foot or ankle, any prior intra-articular injection (while patients receiving only oral medical therapy were not excluded), presence of multiple osteochondral lesions within the joint, radiographic or clinical evidence of ankle osteoarthritis, concomitant tibial osteochondral lesions, and rheumatologic joint disease.

Standard ankle MRI protocols were used for all patients, with imaging acquired in axial, coronal, and sagittal planes using 3-mm slices. Following MRI-based diagnosis, patients with confirmed OLTs provided informed consent for study participation and completed the AOFAS and FADI functional scoring questionnaires. Weight and height were measured at the time of evaluation, and demographic data including sex and age, as well as history of trauma, were recorded.

Radiological measurements were conducted using institutional Picture Archiving and Communication System (PACS) tools. Lesion surface area was measured in mm² on axial images using an automatic elliptical tool (Fig. 2). Lesion depth was measured as the average of the maximum depth recorded in coronal and sagittal planes, expressed in mm (Fig. 2). Lesion depth was defined as the maximum vertical extent of the osteochondral defect measured from the articular surface of the talar dome to the deepest point of subchondral bone involvement on sagittal or coronal MRI images. In addition, MRI examinations were reviewed for concomitant ligamentous and soft-tissue injuries such as anterior talofibular ligament (ATFL) sprain, deltoid ligament injury, or peroneal tendon pathology, and their presence was recorded. Lesions were classified into six anatomical regions: anteromedial, centromedial, posteromedial, anterolateral, centrolateral, and posterolateral (Fig. 3). If a lesion extended across multiple regions, the region with the largest involvement was designated as the primary site. To assess measurement reliability, lesion surface area and depth were independently measured by two researchers on a random sample of 40 ankles (approximately 22% of the cohort). Inter-observer agreement was good for both surface area (ICC = 0.87) and depth measurements (ICC = 0.83).

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and categorical variables as frequency and percentage (%). The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to assess normality. For non-normally distributed data, the Mann–Whitney U test was used for comparisons between two groups, and the Kruskal–Wallis test for multiple-group comparisons. Chi-square tests were applied for categorical variables. Spearman correlation analysis was conducted to examine associations between lesion morphology (depth and area) and functional scores (FADI and AOFAS). Correlation strength was interpreted based on the absolute value of the correlation coefficient, with values of 0.30–0.49 indicating a moderate correlation and values ≥ 0.50 indicating a strong correlation. Multiple linear regression analysis was used to identify independent predictors of functional scores. Multicollinearity was assessed using the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF). A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The resulting p-values from correlation and subgroup comparisons were adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure, with a false discovery rate (FDR) set at q = 0.05. All regression models were evaluated as a whole; therefore, p-values were not adjusted for multiple comparisons in regression analyses. For exploratory threshold analysis, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were constructed using an AOFAS score of 80 to define worse functional status. The optimal cut-off value for lesion area was determined using the Youden index, which maximizes the sum of sensitivity and specificity.

Results

A total of 182 patients and 182 ankles diagnosed with talar osteochondral lesions on ankle MRI were included. The mean age was 49.0 years, and 63.2% of the patients were female. The affected side was nearly evenly distributed between right (52.7%) and left (47.3%) ankles. The mean BMI was 28.4 kg/m². Patients were categorized into medial and lateral lesion groups. Concomitant ligament or soft-tissue abnormalities were present in 75 patients (41.2%). Of these, 41 patients (54.7%) had isolated ligament injuries—most commonly ATFL sprain—19 patients (25.3%) had isolated peroneal tendon pathology, and 15 patients (20.0%) demonstrated combined ligament–tendon involvement. No statistically significant differences were observed in age (p = 0.422), lesion depth (p = 0.265), lesion area (p = 0.363), FADI score (p = 0.562), or AOFAS score (p = 0.318) based on lesion laterality. However, the medial lesion group had a significantly higher proportion of female patients compared to the lateral group (p = 0.040) (Table 1). Among the lesion location groups, no significant differences were noted in age (p = 0.897), sex distribution (p = 0.243), lesion depth (p = 0.738), lesion area (p = 0.662), FADI scores (p = 0.937), or AOFAS scores (p = 0.774). These findings suggest that lesion location does not significantly affect functional scores (Table 2). Spearman correlation analysis was conducted to assess the relationship between lesion morphology (depth and area) and functional scores. Lesion depth showed a moderate negative correlation with FADI (ρ = − 0.47, p < 0.001) and a strong negative correlation with AOFAS scores (ρ = − 0.51, p < 0.001). A strong negative correlation was observed between lesion area and both FADI (ρ = − 0.81, p < 0.001) and AOFAS scores (ρ = − 0.81, p < 0.001), indicating that larger lesion size is associated with lower functional scores (Table 3).

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was performed using an AOFAS score of 80 to define worse functional status., consistent with prior studies evaluating functional outcomes in osteochondral lesions of the talus [18, 20, 21]. Lesion area demonstrated excellent discriminatory ability for identifying patients with AOFAS scores < 80 (AUC ≈ 0.91, p < 0.001). Lesion area (mm²) and lesion depth (mm) were both negatively correlated with AOFAS (top row) and FADI scores (bottom row). Red lines represent linear regression trend lines. Stronger associations were observed between lesion area and functional scores, consistent with Spearman’s ρ values (Fig. 4). A multiple linear regression model was used to identify factors associated with FADI score. Age (β = − 0.30, p < 0.001), lesion depth (β = − 0.61, p = 0.045), and lesion area (β = − 0.14, p < 0.001) were statistically significant predictors of lower FADI scores. In the multivariate model, age, lesion depth, and lesion area were statistically significant predictors of AOFAS score (all p < 0.001), with increasing values corresponding to worse functional scores. Additionally, male sex was weakly but significantly associated with higher AOFAS scores compared to females (β = 2.30, p = 0.048). No significant associations were observed for lesion location or BMI (Table 4). Collinearity diagnostics confirmed the absence of multicollinearity between lesion area and depth (VIF = 1.39 for both variables).

Discussion

OLTs are a chronic condition that may result in persistent ankle pain, impaired joint function, and long-term morbidity, particularly among active individuals [31]. Although imaging and clinical assessment techniques have advanced, the relationship between lesion morphology and functional scores remains inadequately understood. Previous studies have largely emphasized treatment approaches or lesion localization, while the prognostic value of lesion dimensions—such as surface area and depth—has not been definitively established [19, 21]. In this context, the present study contributes meaningful data by evaluating the association between functional scores, assessed using both AOFAS and FADI scoring systems, and MRI-based lesion measurements in a well-characterized patient population. By limiting the cohort to patients who had not undergone surgical or intra-articular intervention, the study reduces confounding treatment effects and more accurately reflects the intrinsic functional impact of lesion morphology. Although this study was cross-sectional and reflects associations observed at a single time point, it provides valuable insight into how lesion morphology relates to functional scores in untreated OLT. Nevertheless, causal inferences or long-term prognostic implications cannot be directly established from the current design.

Examination of the demographic characteristics revealed a predominance of female patients (63.2%) and a mean age of 49 years. Although lesion laterality was relatively balanced, a slight predominance was noted on the right side (52.7%). In a meta-analysis conducted by van Diepen et al., the mean age was reported as 37 (range: 13–55) years, with a male predominance of 59% [4]. Other studies with smaller cohorts have also noted higher proportions of male patients [19–21]. The comparatively higher mean age and predominance of middle-aged female patients in the current study distinguish it from prior investigations. This discrepancy may be attributable to differences in patient selection; unlike many earlier studies, which focused on surgically treated patients, our cohort consisted exclusively of individuals who had not received surgical treatment. As such, a higher average age is not unexpected.

In the classification system proposed by Raikin et al., which divides the talar dome into nine zones, only 3.7% of lesions were reported in the central region [17]. Their study identified the medial central zone (Zone 4) as the most common lesion site (53%), followed by the lateral central zone (Zone 7) at 25.7%. Although the nine-zone model has been widely accepted for lesion localization, several studies have documented a very low incidence of centrally located talar lesions [17–20]. To enhance clinical relevance and ensure adequate statistical power, we adopted a simplified 6-region classification comprising anteromedial, centromedial, posteromedial, anterolateral, centrolateral, and posterolateral regions. This approach aligns with methodologies used in previous studies, particularly those involving smaller sample sizes or nonoperative patient populations.

In the present study, 76.3% of osteochondral lesions were located on the medial side of the talar dome, while 23.7% were on the lateral side. Among the medial lesions, 55% were localized to the centromedial region, making it the most frequently affected site. On the lateral side, 15.9% of all lesions were found in the centrolateral region, which was the most common location for lateral lesions. Overall, the lesions demonstrated a non-central distribution pattern, favoring peripheral regions in the horizontal plane. These findings are consistent with those of Raikin et al., who reported the medial central zone (Zone 4) as the most frequently affected site (53%), followed by the lateral central zone (Zone 7) at 25.7% [17]. Similarly, van Diepen et al. identified 31% of lesions in the centromedial region (Zone 4) and 28% in the posteromedial region (Zone 7) [4]. Nakasa et al. also observed that 87.9% of osteochondral lesions were located medially [15]. Collectively, the evidence from our study and the existing literature consistently identifies the centromedial region as the most prevalent site for talar osteochondral lesions.

The mean lesion depth in the medial region was 6.40 ± 2.48 mm, with a lesion area of 115.4 ± 68.5 mm². In the lateral region, the mean lesion depth was 5.88 ± 2.00 mm, and the lesion area was 102.7 ± 56.4 mm². While female sex accounted for 67.6% of cases in the medial region, sex distribution in the lateral region was nearly equal. When lesion characteristics were compared by laterality—specifically age, lesion area, and depth—no statistically significant differences were observed, except for the higher proportion of females in the medial region (p = 0.040). Similarly, comparison across the six subregions revealed no significant differences in age, sex, lesion depth, or area.

Raikin et al. reported that medial lesions were significantly deeper and larger than lateral lesions (p < 0.001) [17]. In contrast, van Diepen et al. found that lateral lesions tended to be larger [4]. Körner et al. calculated average lesion areas of 126 mm² for medial lesions and 100 mm² for lateral lesions, concluding that medial lesions were generally larger [19]. Choi et al., in their evaluation of pre- and postoperative functional scores in patients with OLT, identified lesion area as the most critical predictor of surgical failure (p < 0.001) [21]. Lesions measuring ≥ 150 mm² were associated with poorer surgical outcomes and lower AOFAS scores. Chuckpaiwong et al. reported that patients with lesion diameters ≥ 15 mm had significantly lower AOFAS scores and worse functional outcomes following microfracture surgery [32]. Li et al., in a finite element analysis of lesion size during gait phases, found that tensile stress increased in lesions ≥ 12 mm² [33]. Collectively, these findings support the conclusion that lesion area is a key determinant of both functional scores and treatment response in patients with OLT. Unlike prior studies using a fixed cutoff, our study evaluates the relationship between lesion area and depth-without predefined thresholds-in non-operatively managed patients. This approach may improve prognostic understanding in conservatively managed patients.

When AOFAS and FADI scores were evaluated separately based on lesion location, no significant association was found between lesion area and scores. These findings indicate that lesion location does not significantly influence functional scores. This finding may be partly influenced by patient selection, as individuals with more symptomatic or extensive lateral lesions are often referred for surgical treatment and thus were not represented in our cohort. In Spearman correlation analysis, a moderate negative correlation was identified between lesion depth and both FADI (ρ = − 0.47, p < 0.001) and AOFAS scores (ρ = − 0.51, p < 0.001). A strong negative correlation was observed between lesion area and both FADI (ρ = − 0.81, p < 0.001) and AOFAS scores (ρ = − 0.81, p < 0.001). These results suggest that lesion surface area is more closely related to the functional scores of patients than lesion depth. An increasing lesion area is associated with poorer clinical outcomes. AOFAS and FADI scores declined significantly with increasing age (p < 0.001). No significant relationship was found between BMI and AOFAS or FADI scores. While no relationship was identified between sex and FADI scores, AOFAS scores were slightly but significantly higher in males. Choi et al. reported that patients with a lesion area ≥ 150 mm² had lower AOFAS scores following surgery [21]. D’Ambrosi et al. found an association between centrally located lesions and lower AOFAS scores with increasing age [18]. The same study also reported a positive correlation between larger lesion areas and BMI [22]. Chuckpaiwong et al. observed lower AOFAS scores with increasing BMI values [32]. In contrast, Woelfle et al. found that patients with BMI > 25 had higher AOFAS scores in their evaluation of outcomes after osteochondral autologous transplantation treatment for OLT [34]. Additional research is needed to clarify the impact of BMI on functional scores in patients with OLT.

Although osteochondral lesions are inherently three-dimensional defects, two-dimensional MRI measurements remain widely used in clinical research because standardized volumetric MRI protocols and validated 3D reconstruction tools are not routinely available in many centers. Several studies evaluating OLT morphology have similarly relied on 2D surface area and depth measurements [15, 18, 19]. While practical and reproducible, these 2D methods cannot fully capture the true volumetric geometry of the lesion and may underestimate its extent. Therefore, the use of 2D measurements should be interpreted as a methodological limitation, and future studies utilizing 3D or volumetric MRI techniques may provide a more comprehensive assessment of lesion morphology [35].

In conclusion, this cross-sectional study demonstrates that lesion area is more strongly associated with functional scores than lesion depth in patients with osteochondral lesions of the talus. Lesion location did not significantly influence functional scores. The combined evaluation of MRI-based lesion morphology and functional scores provides valuable insight into the relationship between lesion characteristics and ankle function in patients who did not undergo surgery. However, as the study reflects a single time-point assessment, it cannot determine prognosis or the natural course of OLT. Future longitudinal research is needed to clarify how lesion morphology affects outcomes over time.

This study has several limitations. First, most patients reported long-standing ankle symptoms, which may have affected the accuracy of recalling the initial trauma and its characteristics. Consequently, the etiological factors could not be clearly defined. Second, minor discrepancies may also exist between MRI-based lesion measurements and arthroscopic findings due to differences in spatial resolution and contour delineation, although MRI remains a highly reliable and noninvasive modality for evaluating osteochondral defects. Another limitation is the relatively short accrual period (January 2023–March 2025) and the absence of longitudinal follow-up data. Therefore, the reported functional scores reflect only the patients’ baseline status at presentation, and long-term changes in untreated OLTs could not be evaluated. Although concomitant ligamentous and soft-tissue injuries were present in 41.2% of patients, these variables were not included as covariates in the multivariable regression analyses. This may have introduced residual confounding, as such injuries can independently affect AOFAS and FADI scores. Additionally, the use of the AOFAS score has inherent limitations. The AOFAS score has been criticized for limited measurement precision and inclusion of physician-reported components, low construct validity when correlated with validated health measures, and suboptimal internal consistency and test–retest reliability compared with modern patient-reported outcome measures [36–38]. These limitations should be considered when interpreting the functional outcomes, although the concurrent use of the patient-reported FADI score provides additional validation. Finally, because only patients who underwent MRI were included, and those who proceeded directly to surgical treatment were excluded, selection bias favoring patients with milder lesions may have occurred, which should be considered when interpreting the findings.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that lesion area has a stronger association with functional scores than lesion depth in patients with osteochondral lesions of the talus. Lesion location did not significantly influence functional scores. The findings suggest that lesion morphology, particularly lesion area, may be a key factor reflecting functional impairment. As all participants were managed without surgery, the results provide valuable cross-sectional insight into the functional characteristics of untreated OLTs. Future longitudinal and multicenter studies are needed to confirm these findings and clarify their prognostic relevance.
