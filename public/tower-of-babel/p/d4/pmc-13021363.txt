Apoptotic periodontal ligament stem cells combined with developmental endothelial locus-1 counteract experimental periodontitis with type 2 diabetes mellitus
Authors: Qian Ma, Houxuan Li, Xiaoyu Yang, He Wang, Yiyao Hu, Han Li, Deping Zeng, Jie Li, Jinlin Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractStem cell therapy has been utilized in the treatment of periodontitis. Recent studies have demonstrated that treatment with apoptotic mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) exhibits immunomodulatory effects comparable to those of living MSCs. However, the effect of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) on the efficacy of apoptotic MSCs therapy for periodontitis remains poorly understood. In this study, a ligature-induced experimental periodontitis model was established in wild-type (WT) and db/db mice, followed by the injection of exogenous apoptotic periodontal ligament stem cells (PDLSCs). The results revealed suboptimal therapeutic outcomes with apoptotic PDLSCs in db/db mice. It was observed that the progression of periodontitis was associated with a reduction in the expression of developmental endothelial locus-1 (DEL-1) in experimental periodontitis model. Additionally, the expression of DEL-1 was partially restored during the resolution phase of inflammation. T2DM mice exhibited exacerbated alveolar bone loss and suppressed regeneration, accompanied by the inhibition of DEL-1 expression. In co-culture experiments, impaired macrophage efferocytosis of apoptotic PDLSCs was ameliorated by the addition of exogenous DEL-1 under lipopolysaccharide and high glucose conditions. Moreover, the co-administration of exogenous DEL-1 enhanced the therapeutic efficacy of exogenous apoptotic PDLSCs in db/db mice. In conclusion, diminished DEL-1 expression and impaired macrophage efferocytosis constrain the therapeutic potential of exogenous apoptotic PDLSCs in periodontitis with T2DM. The diminished therapeutic efficacy may be alleviated by the combination of exogenous DEL-1 and apoptotic PDLSCs, offering novel insights into potential therapeutic strategies for periodontitis with T2DM.Keywords: periodontitis, periodontal ligament stem cells, efferocytosis, macrophages

Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs)-mediated immunomodulation has been used to treat periodontal diseases, and numerous studies have demonstrated that MSCs apoptosis is required for its therapeutic effect. This study pioneers a synergistic strategy of combining developmental endothelial locus-1 with stem cell therapy, directly responding to the major clinical demand for treating periodontitis with type 2 diabetes mellitus. This work not only enriches the understanding of the role of stem cells in dentistry but also offers a new therapeutic paradigm for a clinical challenge.

Introduction

Recently, mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) have emerged as a promising therapeutic approach for periodontal regeneration.1–3 In addition to their multi–lineage differentiation potential, MSCs ameliorate periodontitis by promoting macrophage polarization toward the M2 anti-inflammatory phenotype.4–6 Numerous studies have demonstrated that MSCs undergo apoptosis following administration, and this apoptosis is essential for their therapeutic efficacy.7–9 This treatment modality effectively elicits an anti-inflammatory reaction in macrophages, facilitates the clearance of apoptotic cells via efferocytosis, and induces significant immunosuppression.10–12 Apoptotic MSCs therapy showed therapeutic effects equivalent to those of live MSCs in acute tissue injury.11 Compared with living cells, apoptotic MSCs offer advantages such as enhanced durability, ease of preservation, and reduced immune rejection. In contrast to apoptotic vesicles, apoptotic MSCs act as integrated functional units that restore efferocytosis and orchestrate a sustained tissue-repair program.13 Periodontal ligament stem cells (PDLSCs) are an easily accessible subtype of dental MSCs that exhibit immunomodulatory capabilities comparable to those of bone marrow-derived MSCs (BMSCs) and thus hold significant potential as therapeutic agents for addressing periodontal defects in individuals with periodontitis.14,15

However, epidemiologic studies have consistently identified type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) as a crucial risk factor for periodontitis.16 Both the prevalence and severity of periodontitis are higher in T2DM patients compared to non–T2DM individuals.17 Substantial evidence has revealed that alterations in immune system cells exacerbate periodontitis in T2DM animal models and patients.17 Hyperglycemia or a high glucose (HG) environment may exacerbate inflammation-induced cell apoptosis.18–20 Under normal physiological conditions, apoptotic cells are rapidly removed by phagocytes such as macrophages in a multistep process known as efferocytosis, which promotes polarization of macrophages toward the M2 anti-inflammatory phenotype and results in resolution of inflammation to maintain internal tissue homeostasis.21–23 In mouse experimental periodontitis models, macrophages with impaired efferocytosis fail to clear apoptotic neutrophils in a timely manner, perpetuating inflammation and leading to periodontitis.24 Macrophage efferocytosis has been shown to be impaired in periodontitis with T2DM.25 However, it remains unclear whether this impairment reduces the therapeutic potential of apoptotic PDLSCs in periodontitis with T2DM.

In addition, macrophage efferocytosis of apoptotic cells is mediated by bridging molecules.26 As a bridging molecule, developmental endothelial locus-1 (DEL-1) promotes efferocytosis by binding simultaneously to phosphatidylserine on apoptotic cells and to αvβ3 integrin on macrophages.27–29 Accumulating evidence indicates that the resolution of inflammation correlates with the resurgence of DEL-1 expression, and that DEL-1 facilitates the resolution of periodontal inflammation through enhancing efferocytosis in human and mouse periodontitis.24 Lower DEL-1 expression levels have been observed in obese and T2DM patients.30,31 Numerous clinical studies have demonstrated that chronic periodontitis patients with T2DM have reduced levels of salivary DEL-1 and elevated levels of IL-17 compared to healthy individuals.32,33 Reduced DEL-1 levels may be associated with impaired macrophage efferocytosis of apoptotic cells. It remains uncertain whether the decreased DEL-1 expression inhibits efferocytosis, resulting in a diminished therapeutic response of apoptotic PDLSCs in periodontitis with T2DM.

This study compared the effects of exogenous apoptotic PDLSCs on periodontal tissue regeneration in wild-type (WT) mice and db/db mice with periodontitis and explored the underlying factors contributing to the differential therapeutic efficacy of apoptotic PDLSCs. It also investigated the synergistic effects of exogenous DEL-1 and apoptotic PDLSCs in enhancing the resolution of inflammation and regeneration of periodontal tissue in periodontitis with T2DM. These findings may provide new insights into potential therapeutic targets for the chronic periodontitis in T2DM.

Results

The therapeutic effect of exogenous apoptotic PDLSCs was diminished in periodontitis with T2DM

The resolution of ligature-induced experimental periodontitis model was constructed in WT and db/db mice. These mice were administered exogenous apoptotic human PDLSCs (hPDLSCs) or phosphate buffer solution (PBS) (Figure 1A-C). After the removal of ligation and local injection of apoptotic hPDLSCs, both WT and db/db mice demonstrated restored alveolar bone height, an increased bone volume fraction (BV/TV), and reduced trabecular separation (Tb.Sp) compared to PBS injection (Figure 1D-H). However, in db/db mice, the recovery of alveolar bone was reduced compared to the WT groups (Figure 1I-L). H&E, Masson’s trichrome staining, and immunohistochemical staining offered complementary information about the inflammation resolution and periodontal tissue regeneration (Figure 2A). H&E and Masson’s trichrome staining revealed denser collagen fibers and more new bone formation on the treated side than on the control side. IL-17A immunohistochemical staining revealed that apoptotic hPDLSCs therapy reduced inflammatory infiltration (Figure 2B). Meanwhile, positive staining of CD206 was stronger on the treatment side than on the control side. Multiplex immunohistochemistry (mIHC) staining showed that the ratio of M1 (F4/80+INOS+) to M2 (F4/80+ARG1+) macrophages was different. The treatment sides had more M2 or fewer M1 macrophages than the control side (Figure 2D, E). Moreover, the injection of apoptotic hPDLSCs in WT mice resulted in a more significant impact compared to db/db mice (Figure 2C, F).

Alveolar bone loss in the progression and resolution of ligature-induced experimental periodontitis with T2DM

Following this, we established a model of experimental periodontitis and inflammation resolution in WT and db/db mice. (Figure 3A). During periodontitis, alveolar bone persistently lost height and volume density (Figure 3B-F). The height and density of the alveolar bone partially recovered on the fifth day after the removal of ligatures but did not return to the initial baseline level. When the ligatures were not removed until day 15, the mice experienced progressive alveolar bone loss. Moreover, in db/db experimental periodontitis mice, alveolar bone loss increased, and recovery was significantly reduced compared to the WT groups. Furthermore, non–ligated db/db mice exhibited minor spontaneous alveolar bone resorption. Consistent with the micro-CT findings, stereomicroscopy revealed a decrease in alveolar bone height on the buccal side of mice maxillae (Figure 3G). The mice developed progressive alveolar bone loss as the periodontitis progressed, and the height and density of alveolar bone partially recovered in inflammation resolution. However, the severity of alveolar bone loss was increased and the restoration was suppressed in T2DM.

Apoptotic periodontal cells increased and DEL-1 expression decreased in the progression and resolution of experimental periodontitis with T2DM

H&E staining revealed morphological and structural changes in periodontal tissue (Figure 4A first column). With the periodontitis progression, alveolar bone height and collagen fiber deposition decreased, whereas inflammatory cell infiltration increased between the first and second molars, which was exacerbated in db/db mice. Inflammatory cells decreased, and collagen fibers regenerated during the resolution of inflammation. The collagen fibers of WT mice were denser and more abundant than those of db/db mice. The results of immunohistochemical staining demonstrated that the expression of CD206 decreased as periodontitis progressed (Figure 4A second column, B). Positive staining of IL-17A progressively increased with the progression of periodontitis and gradually decreased as inflammation resolved (Figure 4A third column). Additionally, the expression of IL-17A was significantly higher in db/db mice compared to WT mice. In addition, the expression pattern of DEL-1 was inversely correlated with that of IL-17A and positively correlated with that of CD206, and both inflammation and diabetes mellitus were found to suppress the expression of DEL-1 (Figure 4A fourth column). mIHC staining results demonstrated a decrease in F4/80+ARG1+macrophages with the progression of periodontitis, indicating a decrease in M2 macrophage infiltration (Figure 5A, B). With higher infiltration of M2 macrophages, inflammation gradually subsided, and tissue recovered in the resolution of experimental periodontitis.

The alterations in IL-17A and DEL-1 mRNA expression levels were consistent with the immunohistochemistry results. Moreover, TGF-β1, TGF-β2, and IL-10 gene expression followed the same trend as DEL-1 (Figure 5D-G), and while of TNF-α and IL-6 gene expression followed the same trend as IL-17A (Figure 5H-J). TUNEL (Figure S1—see online supplementary material for a color version of this figure) and cleaved-caspase3+ (Figure 5A, C) staining demonstrated that cell apoptosis increased with the progression of periodontitis, which was exacerbated in db/db mice. Interestingly, an increase in inflammatory markers, a decrease in DEL-1 expression, and an increase in apoptosis were observed in the non–ligated db/db mice compared to non–ligated WT mice.

Exogenous DEL-1 enhances periodontitis resolution by promoting efferocytosis of macrophages on apoptotic PDLSCs in inflammation and HG environment

Primary hPDLSCs displayed spindle fibroblast-like morphology under an optical microscope (Figure S2A—see online supplementary material for a color version of this figure). Alizarin Red staining showed intracellular calcium nodule formation after induction for 21 days, indicating that hPDLSCs can differentiate into osteoblasts (Figure S2B—see online supplementary material for a color version of this figure). Oil Red O staining showed the lipid droplet formation after induction for 21 days, indicating that hPDLSCs can differentiate into adipocytes (Figure S2C—see online supplementary material for a color version of this figure). The flow cytometry results indicated that hPDLSCs expressed stem cell-specific surface markers CD90 (99.97%), CD73 (99.88%), and CD105 (96.82%), while they were negative for hematopoietic cell surface markers CD45 (0.10%), CD34 (0.10%), and HLA-DR (0.03%) (Figure S2D, E—see online supplementary material for a color version of this figure).

Hoechst 33342/PI double staining revealed that the normal glucose (NG) environment produced few apoptotic hPDLSCs (Figure S3A—see online supplementary material for a color version of this figure). More apoptotic hPDLSCs were observed after the lipopolysaccharide (LPS) or HG treatment, particularly when combined treatments. Similarly, Annexin V-FITC/PI double staining in a flow cytometric assay produced comparable results (Figure S3B, C—see online supplementary material for a color version of this figure).

To investigate the fate of apoptotic hPDLSCs, staurosporine (STS) was used to induce apoptosis in hPDLSCs, which resulted in significant morphological changes under the optical microscope. Moreover, fluorescence microscopy of Hoechst 33342/PI double staining (Figure S3D—see online supplementary material for a color version of this figure) and flow cytometric assay of Annexin V-FITC/PI double staining (Figure S3E, F—see online supplementary material for a color version of this figure) revealed that the apoptosis percentage of hPDLSCs was > 95% after 24 h of treatment with STS.

To further explore the interaction between macrophages and apoptotic hPDLSC, macrophages were co–cultured with apoptotic hPDLSCs (Figure 6A). Figure 6E depicts representative fluorescence images of macrophages (CFSE+, green) co-cultured with apoptotic hPDLSCs (CM-Dil+, red) in NG or HG media with LPS. The results demonstrated that DEL-1 significantly increased efferocytosis (Figure 6B). Efferocytosis was significantly suppressed in the HG medium containing LPS compared to the NG medium, but it was still partially enhanced by DEL-1, albeit to a lesser extent than in the NG groups. The flow cytometric assay results followed the same pattern (Figure 6C, F).

Macrophages exhibited polarization toward the M2 phenotype following co–culture with apoptotic hPDLSCs, and DEL-1 further promoted their polarization toward M2 (Figure 6D, G). However, this effect was attenuated in HG medium supplemented with LPS. Notably, DEL-1 still enhanced M2 polarization under these conditions, albeit to a lesser degree compared to the NG group.

The co-administration of exogenous DEL-1 enhanced the therapeutic effect of exogenous apoptotic PDLSCs

The role of exogenous apoptotic PDLSCs and DEL-1 in the inflammation resolution and periodontal tissue recovery in periodontitis with T2DM was then investigated (Figure 7A). Local injection of apoptotic hPDLSCs, either alone or in combination with DEL-1, significantly enhanced alveolar bone regeneration in db/db mice compared to control group injected with PBS (Figure 7B-F). Compared with the treatment using apoptotic hPDLSCs alone, the combination of apoptotic hPDLSCs and DEL-1 demonstrated greater recovery of the cemento-enamel junction to the top of the alveolar crest (CEJ-ABC), a significant increase in BV/TV and trabecular number (Tb.N) as well as a notable decrease in Tb.Sp. H&E and Masson’s trichrome staining revealed denser collagen fibers and more new bone formation in the treated group than in the control side (Figure 7G first and second column). IL-17A immunohistochemical staining revealed that apoptotic hPDLSCs and DEL-1 therapy reduced inflammatory infiltration (Figure 7G third column, H). Meanwhile, positive staining of CD206 (Figure 7G fourth column, I) and αvβ3 integrin (Figure S4A, B—see online supplementary material for a color version of this figure) was stronger in the treatment group than in the control group. Moreover, the combination of apoptotic hPDLSCs and DEL-1 had a more significant effect than apoptotic hPDLSCs treatment alone (Figure 7I and Figure S4B—see online supplementary material for a color version of this figure).

On tissue sections that have been locally injected with apoptotic hPDLSCs and DEL-1, co-localization of M2 macrophages (F4/80+ARG1+) and apoptotic hPDLSCs (cleaved-caspase3+) can be detected in mIHC staining (Figure S5A—see online supplementary material for a color version of this figure). The results demonstrate an upregulation in the ratio of (F4/80+ARG1+cleaved-caspase3+) to (F4/80+ARG+) in the treated group relative to the control (Figure S5B—see online supplementary material for a color version of this figure). Moreover, the concomitant administration of apoptotic hPDLSCs and DEL-1 produced a more significant effect compared to the administration of apoptotic hPDLSCs alone. The ratio of M1 (F4/80+INOS+) to M2 (F4/80+ARG1+) macrophages was different. The treatment group had more M2 or fewer M1 macrophages than the control group (Figure S5C, D—see online supplementary material for a color version of this figure). Moreover, the combination of apoptotic hPDLSCs and DEL-1 had a significantly greater effect than apoptotic hPDLSCs treatment alone.

Discussion

Periodontitis ranks as the sixth most frequent complication associated with diabetes mellitus, with accelerated periodontal bone loss in T2DM patients.34 Numerous studies are actively investigating the effective treatment options for periodontitis with T2DM.35,36 PDLSCs have been isolated, characterized, and have been extensively studied as a potential therapeutic tool for improving periodontitis.37,38 In this study, we determined that apoptotic hPDLSCs exhibited a greater capacity to promote periodontal regeneration in WT compared to db/db mice. We further explored the underlying factors contributing to the differential therapeutic efficacy of apoptotic hPDLSCs. Meanwhile, our findings revealed the combined effects of exogenous DEL-1 and apoptotic hPDLSCs in reprograming macrophages toward the M2 anti-inflammatory phenotype by enhancing efferocytosis during the resolution phase of periodontitis with T2DM.

The present study suggested that exogenous apoptotic hPDLSCs could restore alveolar bone regeneration in both WT and db/db mice. However, the regeneration of alveolar bone was significantly reduced in db/db mice. Alveolar bone regeneration in periodontitis is closely linked to the polarization of macrophages.39 Macrophages are crucial in immune responses and inflammation.40,41 Several studies and our previous work have revealed that following stimulation with LPS, U937 cells differentiated into macrophage-like cells and exhibit polarization toward the M1 phenotype. This polarization was enhanced by pre-exposure of U937 cells to HG for at least 2 weeks.42–45 M1 macrophages impair efferocytosis, while efficient efferocytosis facilitates macrophage transition to the M2 phenotype.46 M2 macrophages are the primary effector cells that exert anti-inflammatory functions during the resolution of inflammation.47 The addition of exogenous apoptotic hPDLSCs enhanced both efferocytosis and M2 polarization. However, under the influence of HG and LPS, the ability of exogenous apoptotic hPDLSCs to modulate macrophage efferocytosis and polarization toward the M2 phenotype was attenuated, which was consistent with the results of the animal experiment.

The efferocytosis of macrophages is mediated by the bridging molecule DEL-1.48 Our results demonstrated that the expression of DEL-1 was significantly reduced during the progression of periodontitis in mice, consistent with findings from another study conducted in both mice and humans.24 This suggests that the resolution of inflammation may be associated with the up-regulation of DEL-1. The present study demonstrated that DEL-1 was significantly down-regulated in the gingival tissue of db/db mice compared with WT mice during the periodontitis progression. db/db mice showed attenuated DEL-1 resurgence during inflammatory resolution. Meanwhile, the gene expression of anti-inflammatory factors followed the same pattern as DEL-1, and the expression of pro-inflammatory factors appeared to be the opposite. Collectively, these findings indicate that apoptosis and the expression of pro-inflammatory factors increased, and the expression of DEL-1 and anti-inflammatory factors decreased in periodontitis with T2DM which is consistent with findings reported in the literature.25 The inhibition of efferocytosis due to the down-regulation of DEL-1 during the progression and resolution of periodontitis with T2DM may account for the reduced therapeutic efficacy of exogenous apoptotic hPDLSCs.

Further research found that combining exogenous DEL-1 with apoptotic hPDLSCs significantly improved the therapeutic effect compared to apoptotic hPDLSCs administered alone. It has been established that exogenous DEL-1 facilitates macrophage phagocytosis of apoptotic neutrophils.24 In this study, impaired macrophage efferocytosis of apoptotic hPDLSCs was ameliorated by the addition of exogenous DEL-1 in LPS and HG environments. Flow cytometry results revealed that macrophages engaged in efferocytosis were polarized to the M2 anti-inflammatory phenotype. Local injection of DEL-1 combined with exogenous apoptotic hPDLSCs into the periodontal tissues of db/db mice led to a significantly enhanced regeneration effect and an increased proportion of M2 macrophages compared to exogenous apoptotic hPDLSCs alone. Indeed, the injection of exogenous DEL-1 can promote efferocytosis, while macrophages that engulf apoptotic cells can secrete more DEL-1, generating a positive feedback loop.27 Exogenous DEL-1 and apoptotic hPDLSCs as efferocytosis materials might repair the impaired macrophage efferocytosis of apoptotic periodontal cells in tissues, thereby contributing to bone regeneration. The efferocytosis process itself, not merely the apoptotic products, plays an instructive role in promoting M2 polarization. In this study, during efferocytosis which mediated by DEL-1 between apoptotic hPDLSCs and the receptor αvβ3, the engagement of efferocytic receptors αvβ3 triggers intracellular signaling cascades that actively suppress pro-inflammatory cytokine production such as IL-17A and induce the expression of M2-associated protein such as Arg-1 and CD206.23,26

Meanwhile, our earlier research showed that DEL-1 can enhance hPDLSCs osteogenic differentiation in both inflammatory and HG conditions.49 These actions may reveal how DEL-1 facilitates the regeneration of periodontal tissue in periodontitis associated with T2DM. However, the therapeutic approaches and their stability must be studied further in large animals and with more samples.

Conclusion

On the whole, in T2DM individuals, the reduced expression of DEL-1 and impaired macrophage efferocytosis of apoptotic periodontal cells limited the therapeutic efficacy of exogenous apoptotic PDLSCs in periodontitis with T2DM. This limitation may be alleviated by the combination of exogenous DEL-1 and apoptotic PDLSCs. It offered novel insights into potential therapeutic strategies for chronic periodontitis with T2DM.
