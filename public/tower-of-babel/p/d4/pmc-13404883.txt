Timing of TEVAR in grade III blunt traumatic aortic injury: a 12-year single-center retrospective study
Authors: Qi Qin, Lun-Chang Wang, Quan-Ming Li, Ming Li, Hao He, Xin Li, Chang Shu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study aimed to summarize a 12-year single-center experience with Grade III blunt thoracic aortic injury (BTAI) and to evaluate the outcomes of early versus delayed thoracic endovascular aortic repair (TEVAR) in the context of real-world clinical decision-making, with the goal of providing further insight into how optimal surgical timing may be individualized based on patient presentation and clinical stability.MethodsA retrospective analysis was conducted on 53 patients with Grade III BTAI treated between August 2011 and January 2024. Based on clinical condition, patients were categorized into early TEVAR (< 24 h) and delayed TEVAR (> 24 h) groups. Perioperative and follow-up outcomes were assessed.ResultsAmong the 53 patients, 16 underwent early TEVAR and 37 underwent delayed TEVAR. The median age was 54.00 (IQR 37.00-59.50) years, and 77.4% were male. Motor vehicle collisions were the leading cause of injury (60.4%). Common associated injuries included fractures (94.3%), pulmonary injuries (62.3%), and cranial injuries (41.5%). The early TEVAR group had significantly higher Injury Severity Score (ISS), a higher proportion of shock index > 1, and higher rates of emergency surgery (all p < 0.01). There were no significant differences in perioperative mortality or endoleak rates between the two groups. No patients experienced paraplegia, cardiovascular events, or renal impairment. Aortic-related length of stay was shorter in the early TEVAR group (p < 0.001), which also had a higher rate of post-TEVAR surgery for associated injuries (p = 0.006). During follow-up, no significant differences were observed in all-cause mortality or aortic-related reintervention rates between groups. No aortic-related deaths occurred in either group.ConclusionDelayed TEVAR may be safe and feasible in selected patients with Grade III BTAI, but retrospective design limits direct comparison with early intervention. Treatment decisions and timing of TEVAR should be individualized.

Background

Blunt traumatic aortic injury (BTAI) is a rare but life-threatening condition, most commonly tearing at the aortic isthmus. It is primarily caused by high-impact mechanisms, such as motor vehicle collisions or falls from height. As the second leading cause of death in trauma patients, BTAI is frequently associated with multiple concomitant injuries, which complicates both clinical diagnosis and management [1–3]. The most widely adopted grading system for BTAI is based on imaging findings and stratifies injuries by severity: Grade I manifests as intimal tears, intimal flaps, or both; Grade II as intramural hematoma; Grade III as aortic wall disruption with pseudoaneurysm; and Grade IV as aortic wall disruption with free rupture [4, 5]. Current guidelines recommend conservative management with serial imaging for Grade I-II injuries. Surgical intervention is advised for selected Grade II injuries with high-risk features (e.g., a posterior mediastinal hematoma > 10 mm or compressive hematoma) and is considered mandatory for Grades III-IV unless contraindicated [3].

Thoracic endovascular aortic repair (TEVAR) has become the first-line surgical choice, but controversy persists regarding the optimal timing of TEVAR. Earlier guidelines suggested surgical repair within 24 h of injury (early TEVAR) to reduce the risk of aortic rupture-related mortality [5]. More recent evidence shows that delayed or elective TEVAR (performed more than 24 h after injury) significantly reduces perioperative mortality compared to early TEVAR [6–9]. Current guidelines have not yet established a definitive consensus regarding the ideal timing for TEVAR, indicating the need for further clinical investigation—particularly in cases of Grade III BTAI involving pseudoaneurysm.

This study aimed to summarize a 12-year single-center experience with Grade III BTAI and to evaluate the outcomes of early versus delayed TEVAR in the context of real-world clinical decision-making, with the goal of providing further insight into individualized timing strategies based on patient presentation and clinical stability.

Methods

This retrospective study included patients with Grade III BTAI admitted to our center between August 2011 and January 2024. Grade III BTAI was defined as aortic wall disruption with pseudoaneurysm formation by the Society for Vascular Surgery (SVS) clinical practice guideline [5]. Baseline characteristics of patients, such as age, gender, trauma types, medical history, associated injuries, complications were obtained from medical records. Injury severity was assessed using the Injury severity score (ISS) Hemodynamic parameters including systolic and diastolic blood pressure (BP), heart rate, and shock index (SI) were recorded. Hospitalization metrics included total hospital length of stay, aortic-related length of stay, and intensive care unit (ICU) stay. Post-discharge follow-up was conducted through outpatient clinic visits and telephone interviews. Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS 26.0 software. Quantitative variables were described as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median (interquartile range) and compared using an independent sample t-test or non-parametric rank-sum test. Qualitative variables were described as frequencies (n) and proportions (%), and comparisons were made using the chi-squared (χ²) test or non-parametric rank-sum test. A p -value < 0.05 indicated statistical significance.

Results

General characteristics of patients

A retrospective analysis was conducted on these 53 patients, the majority of whom were male (77.4%). The median age of all patients was 54.0 years (IQR 37.0-59.5), ranging from 21 to 80 years, with 26.4% of patients aged < 40 years. The main cause of trauma was motor vehicle collisions (60.4%), followed by falls (24.5%) and other types of blunt trauma (15.1%). A history of cardiovascular disease was relatively uncommon in this population. Hypertension was present in 19 patients (35.8%), while only 1 patient (1.9%) had a history of coronary artery disease, and none had a history of cerebral infarction. All 53 patients underwent TEVAR after admission. Based on the time interval from injury to surgery, 16 patients were assigned to the early TEVAR group (< 24 h), and 37 patients to the delayed TEVAR group (> 24 h). There were no statistically significant differences in the baseline demographic and clinical characteristics between the early and delayed TEVAR groups. (Table 1)

Clinical presentations and imaging characteristics of patients

All 53 patients sustained multiple traumatic injuries, with fractures (94.3%), pulmonary injuries (62.3%), and cranial injuries (41.5%) being the most common. Renal (15.1%) and hepatic (9.4%) injuries were less frequent. Pleural effusion was observed in 43 patients (81.1%), including 6 patients (11.3%) with large-volume effusion, and pericardial effusion in 6 patients (11.3%). Three patients (5.7%) were admitted in a comatose state, all of whom were in the delayed TEVAR group. No patients of preoperative paraplegia were recorded. Overall, there were no statistically significant differences in the types of associated injuries or clinical presentations between the early and delayed TEVAR groups. However, when injury severity at admission was assessed, a significant difference emerged. The median ISS at admission was 29.00 (IQR 26.00-34.50) across all patients, with significantly higher scores in the early TEVAR group compared to the delayed TEVAR group (33.00 [IQR 30.00–35.00] vs. 27.00 [IQR 25.50–32.00], p = 0.004) (Table 2). Although patients in the delayed group appeared to have more concomitant injuries across multiple organ systems, their overall ISS was lower. This may be explained by the fact that ISS is primarily driven by the severity of the most critical injuries rather than the number of injuries, and patients in the early group more frequently presented with high-severity conditions, such as severe traumatic cranial injury or hemodynamic instability. This distinction between comparable injury patterns and differing injury severity should be considered when interpreting outcome differences between the two groups.

Following initial stabilization at the trauma center, the mean systolic BP on admission was 138.04 ± 23.18 mmHg, the mean diastolic BP was 81.34 ± 11.86 mmHg, and the mean heart rate was 88.83 ± 15.44 beats per minute. These findings suggest that while hypertension is not the primary cause of BTAI, most patients present with hypertensive crises upon arrival. The shock index (SI), calculated as heart rate divided by systolic pressure, exceeded 1.0 in 5 patients (9.4%). All of the 5 patients (31.3%) were in the early TEVAR group. The proportion of SI > 1 was significantly higher in the early TEVAR group than in the delayed group (p = 0.002), suggesting that the timing of TEVAR should consider the patient’s hemodynamic status, with early TEVAR recommended when hemodynamically unstable.

CTA imaging revealed the aortic isthmus as the rupture site in 45 patients (84.9%), consistent with typical BTAI patterns, with no significant difference in distribution between groups. No statistically significant differences were found in rupture site distribution between the early and delayed TEVAR groups (Table 2).

Treatment strategies and perioperative outcomes

According to earlier international guidelines, TEVAR is recommended within 24 h for patients with Grade III or higher BTAI. To better characterize treatment strategies, we analyzed the clinical factors influencing the timing of TEVAR in our cohort. In total,16 patients received emergency early TEVAR (< 24 h) after admission, primarily due to hemodynamic instability, progression of aortic injury on CTA, or the need for urgent surgery for associated injuries. Delayed TEVAR (> 24 h) was performed in 37 patients. The reasons for deferring intervention were categorized as follows: (1) delayed presentation to our center (> 24 h after trauma); (2) hemodynamic stability without evidence of imminent aortic rupture; (3) the need for prioritized management of severe concomitant injuries, including traumatic cranial injury, intra-abdominal bleeding, or unstable fractures; and (4) physiological optimization prior to TEVAR. Some patients had overlapping reasons. All patients in the delayed group underwent close monitoring with serial imaging and strict blood pressure control.

All 53 patients underwent TEVAR under general anesthesia with systemic heparinization, achieving a technical success rate of 100%. Emergency surgeries were performed in all patients in the early TEVAR group (100%) while none in the delayed TEVAR group underwent emergency procedures, showing a statistically significant difference (p < 0.001). The median operative time for all 53 patients was 1.16 (IQR 0.83–1.33) hours, with no significant difference between the early (1.16 [IQR 0.77–1.49] hours) and delayed TEVAR groups (1.00 [IQR 0.83–1.33] hours), suggesting comparable surgical complexity between the two groups. Arch branch reconstruction was performed in 12 patients (22.6%), with similar rates in both groups, reflecting a higher rate of arch branch reconstruction related to the location of the injury predominantly at the aortic arch. Perioperative mortality was numerically higher in the early TEVAR group (6.3% vs. 0%), although the difference was not statistically significant (p > 0.05). The single death in the early TEVAR group was attributed to refractory acidosis following shock. No patients experienced myocardial infarction, stroke, paraplegia, or unplanned reintervention in either group. Postoperative CTA follow-up revealed no endoleaks in the early TEVAR group, while two endoleaks (type II and IV) occurred in the delayed group (0% vs. 5.4%, p > 0.05), both resolved spontaneously. Among patients who underwent arch branch reconstruction in both groups, follow-up results indicated patency of the branch stents without ischemic complications.

Aortic-related length of stay was significantly shorter in the early TEVAR group (9.13 ± 4.63 days vs. 15.95 ± 5.85 days, p < 0.001), possibly reflecting the greater need for secondary non-vascular surgeries in the early TEVAR group. However, the total hospital length of stay and ICU stay showed no significant difference (Table 3).

Management of associated injuries and follow-up results

Management of associated injuries represents an important component of care in patients with BTAI. In our cohort, the overall rate of surgical intervention for associated injuries was 34.0%. In the delayed TEVAR group, 6 patients underwent emergency surgeries before TEVAR, including cranial, abdominal, and orthopedic procedures. After TEVAR, 12 patients required additional surgeries, with a higher rate in the early TEVAR group (50.0% vs. 10.8%, p = 0.006). This finding suggests a higher incidence of secondary surgical interventions for associated injuries post-TEVAR in the early TEVAR group.

Imaging follow-up with CTA was available in 48 patients (90.6%), with a median duration of 12.25 (IQR 1.50–26.50) months. Telephone follow-up was completed in 40 patients (75.5%), with a median duration of 37.00 (IQR 15.50–64.00) months. The median follow-up duration was longer in the delayed TEVAR group than in the early group. This difference likely reflected the retrospective design and the temporal distribution of cases, particularly the inclusion of patients undergoing late intervention in the delayed cohort. Overall survival was 100% in the early TEVAR group and 92.3% in the delayed group based on telephone follow-up, with no aortic-related deaths. Two endoleaks in the delayed group resolved spontaneously during follow-up, and one patient required open surgery for retrograde type A dissection. No reinterventions were required in the early TEVAR group. None of these differences reached statistical significance (Table 5).

Discussion

BTAI is a rare but highly lethal condition. With advances in imaging technology, the diagnostic rate of BTAI increased by 196.8% from 2003 to 2013 [12]. Although BTAI and acute aortic syndrome are both categorized as acute aortic injuries, they differ significantly in etiology and management. Previous studies have identified motor vehicle collisions as the leading cause of BTAI, followed by falls from height, pedestrian-vehicle collisions, and crush injuries [13]. In our study, motor vehicle collisions accounted for 60.4% of patients. The prevalence of pre-existing comorbidities was relatively low, with hypertension present in 35.8% and coronary artery disease in 1.9% of patients, while no patients had a history of cerebral infarction, which may explain the low perioperative cardiovascular complication rate.

Another characteristic of BTAI patients is the high incidence of concomitant injuries to other organs. In our study, fractures (94.3%), pulmonary injuries (62.3%), and cranial injuries (41.5%) were most commonly observed, often accompanied by pleural effusions or pericardial effusions. In severe cases, patients may present with coma. These findings are consistent with previous literature [14, 15].

The management of associated injuries should be individualized. In our cohort, approximately one-third of patients required surgical management for these associated injuries. Additionally, BTAI patients frequently presented with elevated admission blood pressure, resembling the hemodynamic profile observed in acute aortic syndrome. This highlights the importance of strict hemodynamic control. Previous studies have suggested target values of systolic BP of 100–120 mmHg, mean arterial pressure around 80 mmHg, and heart rate of 60–80 beats per minute [5, 16–18]. Importantly, patients with elevated ISS, hypotension, or a SI > 1 generally represent a more severe condition and may require more urgent intervention. This observation is consistent with our findings that patients undergoing early TEVAR and significantly higher injury severity at presentation.

TEVAR has largely replaced open repair as the preferred treatment for anatomically suitable Grade III BTAI patients due to its minimal invasiveness and favorable outcomes [19]. However, the optimal timing of TEVAR remains controversial. Historically, early TEVAR within 24 h was the standard recommendation [5], but recent evidence supports delayed TEVAR in BTAI patients with severe concomitant injuries. The 2015 Eastern Association for the Surgery of Trauma guidelines recommend delayed TEVAR for such patients, while early TEVAR remains an option for stable Grade III BTAI patients [6]. Over time, statistical analysis by Romijn [9] of 1,339 patients showed a higher mortality rate with early TEVAR (p = 0.028), and meta-analysis by Marcaccio [8] of 507 patients found a statistically significant difference in mortality rates between early (11.9%) and delayed TEVAR (5.4%) (p = 0.047). Increasing clinical evidence also suggests lower mortality rates with delayed TEVAR [7, 20–22]. Despite this accumulating evidence, the optimal timing remains uncertain, particularly in hemodynamically stable patients with Grade III BTAI. Prior to the 2026 SVS update, major international guidelines [3, 10, 23] had not clearly defined the optimal timing for surgery.

A legitimate concern with delayed TEVAR in Grade III BTAI is the potential for aortic rupture, even in patients who are initially hemodynamically stable. We acknowledge this risk and emphasize that, in our study, delayed TEVAR was reserved only for patients who met strict clinical criteria. This selective approach is supported by evolving evidence and updated guidelines. The 2026 SVS clinical practice guideline [24] offers a Grade 2 C recommendation for delayed TEVAR (> 24 h) in hemodynamically stable patients with Grade III BTAI. The 2025 guideline update by Wagner et al. [25] similarly notes that “if possible, endovascular treatment should be delayed beyond 24 h after injury.” Furthermore, a recent meta-analysis by Liu et al. (2025) [26], encompassing 13 studies and 5,690 patients, reported that delayed TEVAR was associated with significantly lower in-hospital mortality compared with early TEVAR (OR = 1.99, 95% CI 1.53–2.58), while stroke risk was comparable between the two strategies.

In our cohort, among the 37 patients who underwent delayed TEVAR, no aortic-related deaths or preoperative ruptures were observed. One patient in the early TEVAR group died from refractory acidosis secondary to shock, but this death was not aortic-related. Taken together, these findings suggest that, in carefully selected and hemodynamically stable patients with Grade III BTAI, deferring TEVAR beyond 24 h may be a reasonable approach, provided that close monitoring and strict hemodynamic control are maintained. Nevertheless, we reiterate that any evidence of disease progression should prompt immediate intervention, and delayed TEVAR is not recommended for all patients with Grade III BTAI.

Baseline characteristics were comparable between the early and delayed TEVAR groups. However, as demonstrated in the Results, patients in the early TEVAR group presented with significantly greater injury severity at admission, as reflected by higher ISS and SI. This disparity likely reflected real-world clinical decision-making, in which patients with more severe injuries or hemodynamic instability are more likely to undergo early intervention, whereas relative stable patients are considered for delayed repair following initial optimization. Despite this difference in initial severity, mortality, complication rates, and aortic-related outcomes did not differ significantly, although one death occurred in the early group due to shock—not aortic pathology. These findings suggest that both delayed and early TEVAR for Grade III BTAI patients in our cohort had satisfactory results. However, delayed TEVAR was associated with a longer aortic-related length of stay, while total hospital length of stay and ICU stay remained comparable between the two groups. The prolonged aortic-related hospitalization in the delayed TEVAR group likely reflected the staged management strategy, in which patients undergo a period of medical optimization and imaging surveillance prior to intervention, rather than representing an adverse outcome per se. Notably, despite this extended aortic-related timeline, no increase in overall hospitalization burden or ICU utilization was observed. Taken together, these findings support the safety and feasibility of delayed TEVAR in carefully selected stable patients.

A higher proportion of patients in the early TEVAR group required additional surgeries post-TEVAR (50.0% vs. 10.8%, p = 0.006), likely due to concurrent injuries requiring urgent treatment. Conversely, delayed TEVAR was successfully performed in selected patients with complex clinical presentations, including those with coma or requiring emergency surgery for associated injuries, without an apparent increase perioperative complication. This supports the use of delayed TEVAR in complex trauma scenarios when the aorta is not the primary concern. Importantly, we found that 92.3% (24/26) of patients in the delayed TEVAR group were stable and achieved good outcomes, supporting the safety and efficiency of delayed TEVAR in this population. Currently, there are no recommendations for the specific timing of delayed TEVAR, and there is limited clinical evidence. In our center, 94.6% (35/37) of delayed patients were treated during the acute (< 14 days) or subacute (14–90 days) phase, with no significant differences in perioperative clinical outcomes between the two subgroups. The specific timing of delayed TEVAR, the effectiveness and safety of arch branch reconstruction in Grade III BTAI patients require further exploration.

It should be noted that all patients included in the present study underwent TEVAR, and the following discussion on conservative management is based solely on previously published literature. While the current study focuses on endovascular treatment, the role of nonoperative management in Grade III BTAI remains a topic of ongoing debate. Further extending this perspective, research by Fortuna [27] indicates that Grade III and IV BTAI are associated with a high risk of aortic rupture, and nonoperative management of Grade III BTAI as an independent risk factor for mortality. Nonetheless, some studies have demonstrated the safety of conservative management in Grade III BTAI [28]. The therapeutic efficacy of conservative treatment in Grade III BTAI patients warrants further clinical investigation and validation.

Anatomically, in our study, the aortic isthmus was the most common site of BTAI (84.9%, 45/53). Clinically, this is significant as many patients may involve the left subclavian artery (LSA) or the left common carotid artery (LCCA), or may require reconstruction of the supra-aortic branches to extend the landing zone. At our center, the reconstruction was performed in 24.5% of patients (13/53), with no observed ischemic complications or stent occlusions. These findings are consistent with previous reports demonstrating acceptable safety profiles for such techniques. A study by DuBose reported stent migration in 3% and endoleak in 2% of patients undergoing TEVAR [29]. In our study, endoleak incidence was low (3.8%, 2/53), with both patients resolving spontaneously. Concerns remain regarding the use of systemic anticoagulation in trauma patients. However, in line with previous study [30], we observed no bleeding complications with intraoperative full-dose heparinization, suggesting that its use may be safe in carefully selected patients, even in the setting of polytrauma.

Based on our 12 years’ experience, we developed a management algorithm (Fig. 2). It involves immediate hemodynamic assessment and whole-body CTA upon admission, followed by individualized treatment based on the primary life-threatening injury. For patients in whom the aortic injury is the predominant life-threatening condition, the timing of TEVAR is guided by hemodynamic stability: unstable patients undergo early TEVAR (< 24 h), whereas for stable patients, the decision between early and delayed TEVAR depends on additional risk stratification. Early TEVAR is considered in stable patients with high-risk imaging features (rapidly enlarging pseudoaneurysm, progressive periaortic hematoma, or large pseudoaneurysm with irregular morphology) or when urgent concurrent surgery for other injuries is required. Delayed TEVAR (> 24 h) is reserved for stable patients without such high-risk features, particularly when other severe injuries (traumatic cranial injury, intra-abdominal solid organ injury) need prioritized management, or when the patient presents beyond 24 h after trauma. For patients whose primary life-threatening injury is non-aortic (severe traumatic cranial injury, ongoing intra-abdominal hemorrhage), stabilization and urgent surgery for those injuries take precedence, and TEVAR is performed subsequently once the patient is physiologically optimized.

In this management strategy, we define the “primary injury” as the injury that poses the most immediate threat to life at a given time point and determines treatment priority. This prioritization is based on a comprehensive assessment of hemodynamic status, risk of imminent deterioration (such as aortic rupture, progression of traumatic cranial injury, or ongoing hemorrhage), and overall physiological condition. Importantly, this is a dynamic process that may evolve over time and requires multidisciplinary coordination. Under this framework, TEVAR may be prioritized in patients where aortic injury represents the leading life-threatening condition, whereas delayed intervention may be appropriate when other injuries require more urgent management.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the retrospective, single-center design introduces inherent selection bias. Patients in the early TEVAR group were significantly more hemodynamically unstable and had higher ISS scores, reflecting a more severe injury profile, whereas the delayed group comprised predominantly stable patients. Therefore, the favorable outcomes in the delayed group should not be interpreted as evidence of superiority, but rather support the safety of delayed intervention in carefully selected stable patients. Second, the sample size is relatively small, which may limit the generalizability of our findings. Third, follow-up durations varied between groups, partly due to the temporal distribution of cases. Larger prospective studies are needed to refine indications for delayed TEVAR, and assess supra-aortic branch reconstruction in selected patients.

Conclusion

Delayed TEVAR may represent a safe and feasible treatment option in carefully selected patients with Grade III BTAI. However, given the retrospective design and inherent selection bias, these findings do not allow direct comparison of efficacy between the two strategies. Treatment decisions should be individualized, and further prospective studies are required to better define the optimal timing of intervention.
