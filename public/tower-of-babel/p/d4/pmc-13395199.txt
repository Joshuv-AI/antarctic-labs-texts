Cognitive assessment of risk-taking behavior in adults with substance use disorders and internet addiction
Authors: Hrutul Chhag, Chaithanya Leon, Simran Kaur, Yatan Pal Singh Balhara, Ravneet Kaur, Ratna Sharma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Individuals with substance use and internet addiction disorders have a greater likelihood to indulge in risky behavior and show signs of impairment in self-regulation. Risk-taking behavior depends on reward seeking, impulsiveness, and cognitive control systems such as executive functioning, cognitive flexibility, and working memory. Assessment using cognitive tasks and validated questionnaires can provide an insight into the risk-taking behavior of these individuals.Aim:To assess the risk-taking behavior in adults with substance use disorders (SUDs) and internet addiction, by both subjective and objective assessment methods compared to matched healthy controls.Methods:This cross-sectional observational study included 120 participants comprising 60 adults with SUDs (30 subjects each with opioid use disorder, alcohol use disorder (AUD)), 30 with internet addiction (IA), and 30 matched healthy volunteers. Each participant performed an objective cognitive function task (Balloon Analogue Risk Task (BART)) and subjective questionnaires were administered (risk-taking questionnaire, brief self-control scale, and Eysenck impulsivity subscale).Results:Subjects with opioid use disorder were found to have poor self-control (37 ± 9.052 vs 44.4 ± 7.686, P < 0.01) and higher risk-taking score (12.13 ± 2.32 vs 9 ± 1.576, P < 0.0001) as compared to controls. Further, they also exhibited higher median reaction time (all pumps, safe bets) during BART compared to controls (18.57 (12.49–24.34) vs 9.81 (6.23–11.34), P < 0.0001 and 21.64 (13.66–36.59) vs 10.64 (6.8–12.59), P < 0. 0001, respectively), AUD (18.57 (12.49–24.34) vs 10.75 (8.84–17.65), P < 0.05 and 21.64 (13.66–36.59) vs 11.83 (9.28–21.52), P < 0.05, respectively), and IA groups (18.57 (12.49–24.34) vs 9.92 (7.92–11.90), P < 0.0001 and 21.64 (13.66–36.59) vs 11.34 (8.95–13.71), P < 0.001, respectively).Conclusion:Among all participants with SUDs and IA, those with opioid use disorder exhibited higher risk taking, as assessed by subjective and objective measures.

INTRODUCTION

Addiction is characterized by the compulsive use of a substance or engagement in behaviors despite its negative consequences.[1] In India, substance use represents a significant public health concern. The National Drug Dependence Treatment Centre (NDDTC), AIIMS, survey estimated that 14.6% of Indian population (10–75 years)—an estimated 16 Crore people—consumes alcohol, with 5.2% exhibiting harmful or dependent alcohol use. Additionally, 2.06% of the population uses opioids, and 0.55% suffer from opioid dependence.[2] Beyond substances, behavioral addictions, such as gambling, gaming, and internet addiction (IA), activate similar reward pathways.[3] A recent meta-analysis reported a 58.34% prevalence of IA in India, with varying severity levels based on Young’s IA test.[4]

Risk taking is defined as the “intentional or unintentional exposure to the possibility of injury or loss.”[5] Moore and Gullone characterized risk taking as behaviors that involve moderate-to-high short-term gain, followed by potential for a greater long-term loss.[6] Several studies have demonstrated an association between the substance use disorders (SUDs) and an increased engagement in risky behaviors and criminal acts.[7] Similarly, Bezinović et al.[8] found a positive correlation between substance exposure and adolescent risk taking, with males showing a higher propensity regardless of exposure.

Among the available decision-making tasks, the Balloon Analogue Risk Task (BART) has shown a strong correlation with substance use.[910] For instance, Fernie et al.[11] reported risk taking as the only behavioral predictor of alcohol use, while Ashenhurst et al.[12] observed its stronger role in early alcohol use that diminishes with greater symptomatology.

Late adolescence and adulthood are critical periods marked by increased experimentation and heightened engagement in risky behaviors, including substance use.[13] The dual system model explains this by faster maturation of the socioemotional system, driving reward seeking, alongside slower development of cognitive control, limiting inhibition.[14] Furthermore, risk-taking behavior often exhibits poor self-control and heightened impulsivity.[1516]

SUD and IA share overlapping neurobiological mechanisms and neurocognitive deficits. Given their frequent co-occurrence, comparing these groups allows an initial examination of shared and potentially additive effects on risk taking, impulsivity, and self-control.[1718] While many studies have explored SUD, few have directly compared opioid use disorder (OUD), alcohol use disorder (AUD), and IA, which may provide insights into both shared and disorder-specific patterns of risk taking across substance and behavioral addictions. Opioids and alcohol differ in their neuropharmacological mechanisms and clinical course, while IA represents a nonsubstance behavioral addiction that engages similar reward circuitry without pharmacological exposure. Including a healthy control group allows for the identification of deviations from normative risk-taking behavior. Additionally, early identification of risk taking can guide preventive interventions and improve therapeutic strategies.

Therefore, the present study aimed to evaluate risk taking in young adults with SUDs and IA as they might demonstrate greater risk-taking behavior compared to healthy controls on both subjective and behavioral measures.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design

This was a cross-sectional observational study conducted between August 2023 and April 2024. Data were collected from an inpatient setting at a tertiary care facility for addictive disorders and a community-based center located in an urban settlement in India.

Subjects

A total of 120 males aged between 18 and 40 years were enrolled and divided into four groups: OUD (n = 30), AUD (n = 30), IA (n = 30), age and gender matched healthy controls (n = 30), and a minimum educational attainment of 10th grade.

Sample size was calculated based on effect size estimates derived from the study by Arash Khodadadi et al.,[19] for opioid and the study by Claus et al. (2018) for alcohol group, using an alpha of 0.05 and power of 0.8.[20] This calculation indicated a required sample size of approximately 30 participants per group.

Diagnoses of OUD and AUD were made according to DSM-5 criteria, while Young’s criteria were used to identify IA.[212223] The subjects with SUD were in the detoxification phase at the time of assessment. The subjects were excluded if they had a history of neuropsychiatric/neurodegenerative disorder other than addiction or were in a state of complicated withdrawal. The study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee and written informed consent was obtained from all the subjects in accordance with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki of 1975.

The following performance and questionnaires were administered to the participants. All questionnaires were administered in English. When necessary, items were explained in the participant’s preferred language (Hindi) by the investigator to ensure accurate comprehension. All participants had at least basic literacy and were able to understand the questionnaire format.

Clinicodemographic proforma

This section collected demographic details (name, age, sex, address, contact number, and email) and addiction-related information, including substance type, duration, and quantity. The patients of behavioral addiction had no other comorbid SUD.

Risk-taking questionnaire-18 (RT-18)

This is a brief, 18-item self-report scale derived from a longer version containing 65 items, maintaining 91% predictive accuracy making it an easy and rapid yet highly reliable test. Each item is answered with “yes” (scored as 1) or “no” (scored as 0), with total scores ranging from 0 to 18. It has a high reliability, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.886.[24]

Brief Self-Control Scale (BSCS)

It evaluates trait self-control, which reflects the individual’s ability to inhibit risk-taking behavior and resist temptation. Responses are recorded on a five-point Likert scale (1 = not at all like me, 5 = very much like me). This scale also exhibits strong reliability (Cronbach’s α = 0.914).[2526]

The Eysenck Impulsiveness Scale (EIS)

It is a 54-item questionnaire and measures three dimensions: impulsiveness (which means the tendency of a person to work suddenly without considering the consequences), venturesomeness (how much a person is inclined to take risk), and empathy (emotional responsiveness). Responses are binary (“yes” = 1 score, “no” = 0 score).[27]

Following the administration of the questionnaire, participants were subjected to a cognitive function task (BART) to assess risk-taking behavior.

Balloon Analogue Risk Task (BART)

It is a validated computerized task that simulates real-world decision-making process and is widely used as laboratory-based behavioral measure of risk-taking behavior.[928] During the task, subjects were presented with 60 red balloons, one at a time. On each trial, subjects were asked to either pump the balloon and accumulate the money or to bank the accumulated money for that trial and move on to the next balloon. They were informed that each balloon could burst at any moment, leading to a loss of unbanked earnings, though the explosion probability was undisclosed. The maximum number of allowable pumps (116) was also hidden from subjects.[29] The instructions for the task were standardized and were shown to the subjects on screen at the start of the task and were explained too by the investigator.

At the end of the task, the following outcome variables were logged and used to compare the performance among each group:

Average adjusted number of pumps, i.e., the average number of pumps excluding trials where the balloon exploded. Higher values indicate a greater willingness to take risks before securing a reward, whereas lower values suggest more cautious behavior.

Average reaction time: time between the appearance of the balloon and till it is either banked or busted for all the trials. It represents overall decision-making speed during the task. Higher average reaction time suggests slower decision-making and may indicate cognitive inefficiency or impairment, whereas shorter latencies reflect faster processing.

Average reaction time of safe bets (balloons for which the subject presses the bank button) time between the appearance of the balloon and till it is banked. It captures the speed of decisions, specifically leading to risk-avoidance. Increased reaction time for safe-bet trials indicates hesitation or slowed cognitive processing even during low-risk choices, while shorter reaction times reflect more efficient decision-making during banking responses.

Statistical analysis

The outcome variables from each of the subjects for each of the groups were collected and analyzed using GraphPad Prism V10. The data were tested for normality distribution using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Parametric (Brown–Forsythe test–analysis of variance or ANOVA) or nonparametric tests (Kruskal–Wallis test) were applied as appropriate for each of the parameters (BART (average adjusted pumps, average reaction time of each pump, average reaction time of safe bets); risk-taking questionnaire-18 (RT-18) questionnaire, brief self-control scale (BSCS) questionnaire, and Eysenck impulsiveness scale (EIS) questionnaire). Normally distributed values are represented by mean and standard deviation, while nonparametric data are represented in median and interquartile range. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed among the groups (AUD, OUD, IA, and matched healthy controls). Further, correlational analysis was performed between parameters of the BART test with each of the questionnaire scores using Pearson’s r formula for each group. The level of statistical significance was kept at P < 0.05 for all the tests.

Statistical analysis of the administered questionnaire revealed significant results as follows: Subjects in OUD and IA groups had significantly higher RT-18 scores (P < 0.0001 and P < 0.01, respectively) and significantly lower BSCS scores (P < 0.01 and P < 0.01, respectively) than the control group while subjects in the AUD group did not show significant difference as compared to controls for both the questionnaires. Additionally, subjects with OUD had significantly higher RT-18 scores than the AUD subjects (P < 0.05). On the EIS, subjects in the AUD group demonstrated significantly higher impulsiveness compared to both healthy controls and the IA group (P < 0.05 for both). The OUD and IA groups did not show significant differences from controls, but OUD subjects had higher impulsiveness scores than the IA group (P < 0.05). In contrast, for the venturesomeness subscale, subjects in the OUD group showed significantly higher venturesomeness than the control group and AUD group (P < 0.01 and P < 0.01) while the AUD and IA groups did not show any significant difference as compared to control group. Further, no significant difference was observed among the groups on the empathy subscale scale.

Analysis of the BART revealed that there was no significant difference in average adjusted pumps across all groups. However, subjects in the OUD group exhibited significantly higher reaction time for each pump as compared to control group (P < 0.0001), IA groups (P < 0.0001), and AUD group (P < 0.05). Similarly, the OUD group exhibited significantly higher reaction time for safe bets as compared to control group (P < 0.0001), IA group (P < 0.001), and AUD group (P < 0.05).

Group-wise correlational analysis revealed several significant relationships. In the AUD group, RT-18 score was positively correlated with venturesomeness (P = 0.0015, r = 0.5461), while BSCS score was negatively correlated with impulsiveness (P = 0.044, r = -0.363). In the OUD group, BSCS score was negatively correlated to impulsiveness (P = 0.025, r = -0.408), reaction time of each bet (P = 0.03, r = -0.396), and reaction time of safe bets (P = 0.049, r = -0.362). Additionally, RT-18 score was positively correlated to venturesomeness (P = 0.0153, r = 0.4389) in OUD. In the IA group, RT-18 score was positively correlated to impulsiveness (P = 0.0027, r = 0.5289). adjusted average pump was positively correlated to venturesomeness (P = 0.018, r = 0.430) and empathy (P = 0.02, r = 0.423). Furthermore, the BSCS score was negatively correlated to impulsiveness (P = 0.005, r = -0.500) but positively correlated to reaction time on safe bets (P = 0.026, r = 0.407). In the control group, impulsiveness score was positively correlated to reaction time on all pumps (P = 0.029, r = -0.398) and negatively correlated to BSCS scores (P = 0.001, r = -0.591).

DISCUSSION

The present study examined risk-taking behavior in young adults with OUD, AUD, and IA using both subjective and behavioral measures.

We observed that subjects with OUD exhibited higher risk taking, as indicated by RT-18, and higher reaction time during BART performance, despite showing comparable average adjusted pumps when compared to other subjects’ groups and healthy control. Given that participants were assessed during the detoxification phase, these findings are unlikely to reflect acute state effects of intoxication or withdrawal. Instead, higher reaction times may reflect trait-dependent cognitive impairments associated with chronic opioid use, particularly in processing speed and executive functioning, as supported by Baldacchino et al.[30] They also demonstrated higher self-reported risk-taking on the RT-18, indicating elevated trait-level risk preference. However, despite this increased dispositional risk propensity, behavioral risk taking on the BART—as indexed by adjusted average pumps—did not differ from other groups or healthy controls. This dissociation suggests that although individuals with OUD endorse greater risk preference, its behavioral expression may be constrained by cognitive factors.[31] In support of the clinical relevance of trait-level risk propensity in OUD, Aklin et al.[32] demonstrated that greater score on the BART indicating higher risk-taking was negatively associated with successful naltrexone induction, suggesting that heightened dispositional risk is linked to greater opioid consumption severity and poorer treatment engagement rather than acute substance effects.

Our findings indicate discordance between the subjective and objective indicators of risk taking, suggesting a differential appraisal and behavioral expression of risk. This dissociation may be partly attributable to reduced self-control, as observed in our study, wherein elevated trait-level risk propensity and venturesomeness influence subjective appraisal of risk without necessarily translating into riskier task-based behavior.[33] Supporting this, Malouf et al. reported that impaired self-control acts as a vulnerability factor in OUD and predict engagement in risky behaviors rather than arising solely as a consequence of substance use.[34]

Subjects with AUD were comparable to controls on both subjective and objective measures of risk taking. Nevertheless, they showed greater impulsivity than control and had lower RT-18 scores and reaction time during BART performance as compared to OUDs. Prior studies suggest that impulsive decision-making, rather than risk-taking per se, may play a more central role in alcohol-related pathology. Courtney et al. evaluated risk taking and impulsiveness in AUD and reported impulsive decision-making to be the robust predictor of both alcohol use severity and alcohol related pathology.[35] Risky decision-making showed a weak or negative association with alcohol misuse severity and dependence-related harm (as measured by AUDIT and ADS). These findings suggest that impulsivity, rather than risk-taking behavior per se, may play a more prominent role in adverse alcohol-related outcomes.

The finding that the AUD group exhibited higher trait impulsivity without corresponding behavioral risk taking on the BART may be attributable to assessment during the detoxification phase, as alcohol-related risk-taking is often state dependent and more pronounced during intoxication. In abstinent individuals, risk-taking behavior may normalize or reflect greater caution. Additionally, participants may have adopted more conservative decision-making strategies in structured, computerized laboratory tasks. Prior literature has reported inconsistent findings regarding BART performance in AUD, suggesting that this task may have limited sensitivity in capturing alcohol-specific risk-taking behavior. In contrast to our findings, Campbell et al. (2013) reported lower adjusted average pumps in individuals with AUD, which they attributed to neuropsychological impairments in immediate memory and executive functioning.[36] Conversely, some studies have reported a positive association between alcohol use and risk-taking behavior, highlighting the heterogeneity of findings and the context-dependent nature of alcohol-related risk-taking.[37]

Further, IA group demonstrated high risk-taking behavior, as assessed by RT-18, in comparison with controls. This aligns with a study conducted by Soni et al., wherein they found that individuals with more internet usage (>1 hr) were associated with a higher risk taking.[38] However, this group did not differ significantly from controls on BART performance. Similar findings were reported by Chih Hung Ko et al., who also found no differences in BART performance among individuals with IA. They concluded that these individuals may not exhibit overt behavioral risk tendencies on objective tasks.[39] We hypothesize that either subjects with IA do not have any cognitive impairment, as assessed by BART, or that frequent engagement in IA may have neutralized or masked these impairments, resulting in normal performance metrics and comparable reaction time and adjusted average pumps. This neutralization effect could be due to rapid decision-making and visuomotor coordination in the case of gaming, and continuous stimulation, familiarity with screen-based tasks, and switching between content in the case of nongaming forms of IA.[40]

Our study attempted to investigate risk-taking behavior within an Indian population, focusing specifically on individuals with addictive disorders. By employing both objective and subjective parameters, the study provides a comprehensive assessment of risk-taking behavior among subjects with SUDs and addictive behaviors. Notably, the research targets adults, an age group characterized by a higher prevalence of addictive disorders and risk-taking behavior.

Despite these strengths, the study has few limitations. We included male subjects only, owing to the low rate of female admissions at the deaddiction center. Additionally, recruitment from inpatient settings may introduce a severity bias, as admitted patients often exhibit more pronounced symptomatology and functional impairment than outpatient or community samples; therefore, caution is needed when extrapolating these findings to broader populations. However, participants had minimum educational attainment of 10th grade and were recruited from an urban resettlement colony largely representing lower-middle and lower socioeconomic strata, as inferred indirectly through indicators such as absence of a BPL card. This relative socioeconomic and educational homogeneity may have reduced variability within the sample; since sociodemographic variables, such as education and income level, have been associated with type of substance use and known to influence cognitive performance and recovery patterns, it restricts the generalizability and interpretability of findings.[414243] Participants were assessed during the detoxification phase, and the potential influence of withdrawal symptoms, as well as concomitant medications, on cognitive performance cannot be excluded. The duration of substance use was not systematically considered, which may affect the severity of cognitive deficits. The BART has inherent limitations: Performance may reflect not only risk taking but also learning effects, familiarity with probabilistic or screen-based tasks, impulsivity, and visuomotor coordination, and its correlation with real-world risk behaviors and other cognitive measures is variable. The direct pharmacological effects of the substances themselves on cognitive function were not controlled for and may have influenced task performance. Finally, the study utilized three questionnaires, which inherently carry subjective biases; responses may vary based on subjects’ understanding of the questions and their comfort level in providing honest answers, leading to social desirability bias.

Identifying individuals at risk for negative outcomes associated with risk-taking behavior will be crucial for developing early intervention strategies aimed at mitigating these behaviors and their potential consequences. Although the current study accounted for impulsivity, self-control, cognitive function, and risk-taking behavior, a longitudinal study would be required to derive precise cause–effect relations between these variables and SUD. Additionally, future research could delve deeper into the neural correlates of risk-taking behavior by employing neuroimaging techniques such as MRI and EEG, offering a more comprehensive understanding of the underlying mechanisms.

CONCLUSION

Our findings underscore the heterogeneous nature of risk-related traits across addiction types, with implications for tailoring interventions to specific cognitive and behavioral profiles. Integrating self-report and behavioral assessments can enhance early detection and treatment personalization. OUD and IA groups showed higher risk-taking and lower self-control, though only the OUD group had longer reaction times on the BART reflecting deficits in cognitive processing speed and greater impulsivity. The IA group performed similarly to controls on tasks. AUD subjects differed only in self-reported impulsivity.
