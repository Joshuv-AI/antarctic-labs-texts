A Qualitative Study of Medical and Rehabilitation needs of Primary Caregivers of Children with Developmental Disabilities in India: Implications for Health Service Interventions
Authors: Charuta Gokhale, Anita Kar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Children with developmental disabilities require medical care, rehabilitation therapies, and social welfare support. While district early intervention centers offer some services, the private sector remains unorganized. The study aimed to qualitatively assess caregivers’ needs, identify gaps in referral linkages, and suggest health systems interventions to assure smooth transition from medical to rehabilitation services. In-depth interviews of 28 caregivers of children with cerebral palsy, intellectual disability and autism spectrum disorder were conducted. Interviews were analyzed from the perspective of health systems interventions, using content analysis. Study revealed three themes. The first theme related to inadequate information at diagnosis including insufficient information about complications among high-risk babies, and inadequate guidance when delayed milestones were observed. The second theme related to interaction with the doctor, highlighting perceived lack of knowledge among doctors about developmental disabilities, ineffective communication, and limited knowledge about rehabilitation services. The third theme was regarding challenges with rehabilitation such as caregivers’ inadequate knowledge about therapies benefits and need for coordination between doctors and therapists. Findings indicate the need for strengthening knowledge about early childhood development, the benefits of early intervention, and strengthening referral pathways to assure coordination between available services and continuity of care.

INTRODUCTION

Cerebral palsy, intellectual disability, and autism spectrum disorder comprise the most prevalent conditions contributing to childhood disability. In India, the Rashtriya Bal Swasthya Karyakram (RBSK) program conducts community-based screening for children with birth defects and disabilities, and refers them to District Early Intervention Centres (DEICs) for further diagnosis, medical care, and therapies. From DEIC, caregivers are referred to the District Disability Rehabilitation Centre (DDRC) and receive counselling for disability registration under the Universal Disability Identity card (UDID) and other welfare benefits.[1]

However, referral between private medical care to rehabilitation services remains unregulated, leaving caregivers to navigate through a range of services for care seeking. This study used a qualitative methodology to assess caregiver needs in the private sector to suggest a coordinated care model linking medical and rehabilitation services for affected children.

MATERIAL AND METHODS

Study design, setting, and participants

The study used a qualitative descriptive design[2] and COREQ guidelines for reporting. In-depth interviews were conducted with 28 caregivers of children with intellectual disability (n = 7), autism spectrum disorder (n = 8), and cerebral palsy (n = 13) recruited from special schools (n = 20), therapy centers (n = 7), and public recreational center (n = 1) located in Pune city, Maharashtra, India. Recruiting centers were selected purposively due to a lack of a comprehensive list of rehabilitation facilities.

Data collection and analysis

In-depth interviews were conducted in the Marathi language by a trained female researcher (CG) using a guide developed from literature[3] and were audio-recorded with written consent. Transcripts were translated into English, anonymized, and analyzed manually. CG and AK independently coded the data using a deductive approach,[4] and disagreements were resolved by an independent researcher.

Ethical considerations

The study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of the Savitribai Phule Pune University.

RESULTS

Characteristics of children and caregivers

The study included 28 caregivers of 13 children with cerebral palsy, eight children with autism spectrum disorder and seven with intellectual disability. Mean age of caregivers was 37 (±6.0) years. Three were (10%) single caregivers living with extended families. Socioeconomic status of primary caregivers was calculated using the latest version of Kuppuswamy’s scale.[5] Majority (61%) belonged to upper or upper-middle socioeconomic strata. Mean age of the 16 boys and 12 girls was 12 (±3.0) years. Additional conditions included attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (n = 7), low vision/hearing impairment (n = 2), and history of frequent convulsions (n = 6). Twenty-four children (85%) were partially verbal or mute. Eighteen children (64%) were incontinent/not toilet trained [Table 1].

Medical and rehabilitation care usage

Mean duration between onset of symptoms and confirmed diagnosis was ten months. Fourteen children (50%) were undergoing some form of rehabilitative therapy. All children with cerebral palsy were using assistive aids. None were aware of the government services, but three caregivers (10%) had accessed the Niramaya public health insurance scheme[6] while one family (3%) received a monthly disability allowance by the local municipality.

Themes and subthemes

Analysis of the in-depth interviews identified three themes and six subthemes.

Theme 1: Inadequate information at the time of diagnosis

Subtheme 1.1: Inadequate information about complications among high-risk babies

Twenty-seven children were high-risk babies (preterm births, low birth weight, neonatal jaundice, prolonged labor, and no cry at birth). Caregivers reported not being alerted about the elevated risk of disabling conditions and guidance on when to seek help.

Subtheme 1.2: Inadequate knowledge on action to be taken in case of delayed milestones

A gap existed between initial health concerns of caregivers and diagnosis of the condition, as caregivers were uncertain about next steps, or were advised by relatives or doctors to wait and watch.

“her father started walking late. Mother-in-law said the same thing must be happening with her.” (mother of a child with cerebral palsy)

“He did not crawl till 8 months. I used to tell the doctor; he is different from other babies. Doctors, and even my relatives used to ridicule me.” (mother of a child with intellectual disability)

Inadequate knowledge of next steps led to missed opportunities for early intervention.

Theme 2: Interaction with medical practitioners around diagnosis

Subtheme 2.1: Perception of inadequate knowledge among doctors about developmental disabilities

Caregivers were dissatisfied with the doctor’s advice and felt their concerns were not addressed empathetically.

“Big doctors don’t guide us thoroughly. When I used to go for his vaccinations, I realized that doctors don’t know much about special children.” (mother of a child with cerebral palsy)

“Doctors don’t reveal the diagnosis. During one of the consultations, autism spectrum disorder was suspected but doctors were not sure. At that time if someone would have guided me on the presumptive diagnosis and in terms of how to train him, things could have been different.” (mother of a child with autism spectrum disorder)

Subtheme 2.2: Need for sensitive communication

Caregivers articulated the need for appropriate counselling to prepare them for the diagnosis

“If we suddenly come to know about the diagnosis, it becomes very shocking.” (Mother of a child with autism spectrum disorder)

“Dr.xxx told us. He straightway told me that he will never go to a normal school. He will not even reach up to second standard. I got very angry at him. (Mother of a child with intellectual disability)

“We had completely collapsed. We had not expected this. We had gone to consult for constipation and ended up having all together a different diagnosis.” (Mother of a child with autism spectrum disorder)

Subtheme 2.3: Doctors’ knowledge about rehabilitation services

There was an inadequate private sector referral from medical to rehabilitation services, highlighted in the third subtheme.

“Doctors tell us what is needed. But they don’t tell us where to get (the services) from.” (mother of a child with intellectual disability)

Often, medical practitioners did not provide any referral for rehabilitation therapies, and caregivers sought help from their own social groups to identify a rehabilitation therapist

“We had a neighbour who used to work at the social welfare department. From her we came to know about therapies. But still what therapies I don’t know.” (mother of a child with intellectual disability)

Theme 3: Challenges with rehabilitation services

Subtheme 3.1 Inadequate counselling to caregivers regarding therapies and their benefits

It was observed that caregivers were not adequately informed about the benefits and duration of therapies.

“She told us to do this (physiotherapy). We thought he will be immediately recovered. Subsequently we realized that this is going to be a long-term treatment. It has been 15 years; he is undergoing physiotherapy.” (mother of a child with cerebral palsy)

“We are giving him therapies as per our knowledge. We are not sure about them. Since his fine motor skills are impaired, we are giving occupational therapy; he cannot speak, so we are giving him speech therapy. This is all going directionless” (mother of a child with intellectual disability)

“We did not know anything about therapies and course of treatment in general. Everything was on trial-and-error basis. Children are just labelled. But nobody gives advice in terms of early intervention.” (mother of a child with autism spectrum disorder)

“Caregivers get cheated by therapists. Children who are treated for sensory issues, caregivers don’t even know whether it is really a sensory or behavioural issue. There should be some governing body to monitor these therapists.” (mother of a child with autism spectrum disorder)

Subtheme 3.2: Need for coordination between doctors and rehabilitation therapists

Caregivers cited the need for a multidisciplinary team to guide them about the duration and benefits of each rehabilitation therapy

“There must be coordination between paediatrician and rehabilitation specialist. Medicines cannot be the way to treat these children. By the time a child reaches 7, communication with the paediatrician is lost. There must be a standard protocol (mother of a child with autism spectrum disorder)

Every child has different needs. Specialists must come together to guide about treatment and rehabilitative care, and schools” (mother of a child with cerebral palsy)

Without such integrated care, caregivers were left to make their own decisions, without any review of the child’s development

Caregivers’ suggestions on addressing challenges expressed during interviews

Caregivers were asked about the solutions to address these challenges, which are summarized in Table 2. Caregivers suggested monitoring of developmental milestones by a physician during routine check-ups or immunization visits. For emotional support, parental counselling by trained personnel, parent support groups, and a telephonic helpline were suggested. There was also a suggestion to have a list of therapy centers, hospitals, and schools, and a common kiosk to display information on rehabilitation and welfare services.

Solutions prescribed by caregivers for the service challenges

The study identified a framework of caregiver, health service, and health systems-related interventions for developmental difficulties [Figure 1]. Caregiver interventions include improving awareness of early childhood development and knowledge of responses to developmental delays. Child health services should incorporate monitoring of developmental milestones during immunization and provide counselling for high-risk children on complications, prognosis, and early intervention services. Psychosocial support through NGOs can help parents cope emotionally during diagnosis. For both public and private care providers, there is a need for CMEs on sensitive communication and training on developmental disabilities. Health system interventions include strengthening of DEICs and regulation of private rehabilitation centers, and creating a dashboard of available therapists to help referrals and access affordable care providers. The key finding of the study is the need for developing guidelines for integrated care involving caregivers, physicians, and therapists.

DISCUSSION

Challenges arising due to inadequate knowledge about developmental milestones, poor referral from medical to rehabilitation care and unregulated private services reported in the study are corroborated by available global literature. Delay in diagnosis was primarily due to absence of neonatal screening for disabilities and monitoring of developmental milestones as a routine procedure in Indian public health system. Similar observation was made by Saetermoe et al.[7] in the United States where caregivers demanded access to services for timely diagnosis of locomotor disabilities. Need for counselling to overcome the shock of diagnosis was reported by Ludlow et al. and Derguy et al. in the UK and France, respectively.[89] A study by Chittem et al.[10] conducted in Andhra Pradesh, India, reported caregiver concerns about lack of knowledgeable specialists for neurological conditions. Caregivers stated the need of empathetic and sensitive approach by doctors. Similar observations were reported by Lindsay et al.[11] in one of the Canadian studies where ineffective communication with physicians was perceived as a cause for misinformation of the condition. Desai et al. and others noted that children with autism often face challenges, such as visiting multiple providers for diagnosis and traveling long distances for care.[1213]

These findings have key policy implications. Currently, the RBSK screens and refers children from community settings to DEICs for further evaluation. However, a similar model is unavailable in the private sector. Caregivers emphasized the role of pediatricians in increasing awareness about developmental milestones, parental counselling for high-risk babies, and leveraging immunization visits for monitoring development. The key recommendation was the formulation of comprehensive teams, including the caregiver, pediatrician, and therapist, to avoid delay in seeking care. While such teams exist in DEICs, studies highlight their suboptimal performance[1415]

Study highlights the need for private practitioners to refer low-income families to free services at DEICs. Only three out of 2 children sought care from DEIC. Regular CMEs of private practitioners on treatment protocol and social welfare schemes can improve linkages between private and public health services.

Study limitations include enrolment of caregivers predominantly from upper and upper middle socioeconomic strata. Severity of disability was ascertained by disability certificates issued by the government and the motor functions of children with cerebral palsy could not be measured by the researcher. Some findings related to access to therapies or school enrolment may be attributed to selection bias as participants were recruited from these settings.

CONCLUSION

The study identified that low awareness about developmental disabilities and unregulated private rehabilitation services imposes significant challenges for caregivers. Caregiver recommended strengthening early childhood development services, tracking the development of children during immunization, and developing guidelines for private practitioners for integrated care.
