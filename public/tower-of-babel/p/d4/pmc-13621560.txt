Complications of varicella and associated factors among hospitalized patients at a tertiary infectious disease hospital in Southern Vietnam
Authors: Chau Buu Le, Nhut Minh Vuong, Thuy Thi Hoang, Khai Viet Nguyen, Vi Lan Lu, Vien Truong Nguyen, Araba O. Gyan, Giao Huynh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundVaricella remains common in countries without widespread vaccination and may lead to serious complications, particularly in adults and immunocompromised individuals. Data on hospitalized patients in Vietnam are limited. This study aimed to describe the clinical characteristics and complications of varicella and explore associated factors with complications among hospitalized patients in Vietnam.MethodsA mixed retrospective–prospective observational cohort study was conducted at a tertiary infectious diseases hospital in Ho Chi Minh City, Vietnam. Consecutive patients with suspected or confirmed varicella requiring hospitalization were enrolled. Clinical data were collected from medical records and patient interviews. Complications were defined based on clinical features supported by laboratory and imaging findings. Factors associated with complications were analyzed using Poisson regression.ResultsA total of 329 patients were included, of whom 45.6% developed at least one complication. Secondary skin infection was the most common complication (35.3%), followed by pneumonia (13.1%) and sepsis (8.2%). In the multivariable analysis, gender, immunodeficiency and prior acyclovir using were independently associated with complications. Participants with immunodeficiency had a higher risk than those without immunodeficiency, with an aRR of 2.23 (95% CI: 1.51–3.28) in males and 2.25 (95% CI: 1.45–3.49) in females (both p < 0.001). Among participants without immunodeficiency, males had a higher risk than females, with an aRR of 1.51 (95% CI: 1.15–1.97; p = 0.003). Underweight/thin participants had a lower risk than those with normal BMI, with an aRR of 0.56 (95% CI: 0.33–0.93; p = 0.026).ConclusionsComplications of varicella were common among hospitalized patients in this tertiary care setting. Male sex and immunocompromised status were associated with an increased risk of complication. Early identification and close monitoring of high-risk patients may help reduce severe outcomes.

Background

Varicella, commonly known as chickenpox, is a contagious disease caused by the varicella-zoster virus (VZV). This disease is characterized by high transmissibility and a strong potential for outbreaks, particularly in countries where widespread vaccination has not yet been implemented. According to data from the Global Burden of Disease Study, approximately 84 million new cases and more than 14,000 deaths from varicella are recorded globally each year [1]. The clinical presentation of varicella includes fever, aching muscles, nausea, decreased appetite, and headache followed by pruritic vesicular lesions at different stages distributed across the body during the eruptive phase [2, 3]. Although varicella is generally considered a self-limited disease it can lead to a wide spectrum of complications such as skin infections, neurological complications, and pneumonia—especially in adults, neonates, and immunocompromised individuals [2, 4]. In severe cases, disseminated varicella-zoster virus infection involving multiple organ systems carries a mortality rate as high as 25% [5]. Complications of varicella are more common in patients with underlying conditions, particularly patients with autoimmune disorders on immunosuppressive therapy, individuals with malignancies undergoing chemotherapy, and transplant recipients. In these cases, the organs most frequently affected include the lungs, liver, heart, kidneys, and pancreas [5, 6]. The presence of underlying conditions have been identified as an independent prognostic factor associated with mortality, septic shock, multiple organ failure, and acute liver failure [5]. Complications may also occur in healthy individuals [7], as supported by findings from a study conducted in India; reporting a substantial proportion of cases of patients without comorbidities [8], highlighting the unpredictable nature of varicella.

In Vietnam, varicella remains prevalent and retains significant outbreak potential. This is partly due to the absence of the varicella vaccine in the national Expanded Program on Immunization [9]. Studies in Vietnam indicate that varicella can cause various complications, most commonly skin and soft tissue infections and respiratory complications such as pneumonia [10, 11]. Findings from studies in Vietnam show that severe complications predominantly affects immunocompromised patients particularly those with systemic lupus erythematosus, malignancy, long-term corticosteroid therapy, or individuals with varicella during pregnancy [10, 11]. When varicella occurs in these patients, it often follows a more severe course [12].

In Vietnam, existing studies on varicella have largely focused on pediatric populations [10, 11, 13], despite evidence that the disease tends to be more severe in adults. Data on adult patients remain limited. To date, only a small number of studies have included adults; for example, a study conducted at Bach Mai Hospital was constraint by a small sample size and the grouping of varicella and herpes zoster cases, which obscured to distinguish and characterize varicella-specific clinical patterns [10]. There is a lack of data specifically describing varicella in hospitalized adult patients – a population that is more likely to present with complications or severe disease. This gap limits the understanding of the disease burden and risk factors in clinically relevant, high-risk settings. Therefore, this study aims to (1) describe the clinical characteristics and complications of varicella among hospitalized patients and (2) explore their associated factors in this specific population.

Methods

Study design and setting

A mixed retrospective–prospective observational cohort study was conducted at the Hospital for Tropical Diseases, a 550-bed tertiary infectious diseases hospital in southern Vietnam, from January 2023 to August 2025. Data were collected retrospectively from January 2023 to November 2024 and prospectively from November 2024 to August 2025. The dual-phase design was adopted to maximize sample size and capture a comprehensive spectrum of hospitalized varicella cases over time. The retrospective phase allowed inclusion of patients treated before study initiation, while the prospective phase enabled more standardized and complete data collection, particularly for clinical variables and outcomes.

Study population and case definition

At Hospital for Tropical Diseases, patients with suspected or confirmed varicella were admitted upon suspicion of complications or the presence of risk factors for severe disease or unable to isolate at home were also considered for admission. High-risk criteria included malignancy on chemotherapy, autoimmune diseases on immunosuppressive therapy, recent transplantation (within 12 months), type 2 diabetes mellitus, prolonged corticosteroid use, HIV infection/AIDS, other immunodeficiency conditions, or pregnancy (Fig. 1). All hospitalized patients during the study period were consecutively included in the study.

Admission criteria for patients with confirmed/suspected varicella

In clinical practice, varicella was primarily diagnosed clinically, based on characteristic rash with lesions at different stages of evolution, history of recent exposure, and exclusion of alternative diagnoses (e.g., herpes simplex infection, hand-foot-and-mouth disease, and drug-related eruptions). The laboratory confirmation was not routinely required, consistent with standard clinical practice [3, 14, 15]. PCR testing for varicella-zoster virus was performed in cases with diagnostic uncertainty, as determined by the treating physician.

Sample size

Minimum sample size was calculated for estimating the proportion of complications. The parameters included type I error α = 0.05, margin of error at 5%, the expected proportion of complications was 73.1% [10]. The study required at least 302 patients.

Sampling methods

During the study period, all eligible patients were consecutively enrolled in the study. A total of 329 participants were included in the analysis.

Data collection

Data was collected using both retrospective and prospective approaches. In the retrospective phase, data was extracted from medical records using a standardized data collection form, that included demographic characteristics, comorbidities, acyclovir use prior to admission, clinical features, laboratory findings at admission, disease progression, and treatment outcomes. In the prospective phase, data collection was conducted through direct patient interviews and extraction of clinical and laboratory information from medical records. To ensure data consistency across the retrospective and prospective phases, the same standardized case report form and variable definitions were applied throughout the study. For retrospective participants, data were extracted and cross-checked using multiple hospital sources, including medical records, laboratory databases, and imaging archives. Data completeness was assessed during data abstraction. No missing values were identified for the primary outcome or variables included in the multivariable analyses. Consecutive enrollment based on predefined eligibility criteria was used in both phases to minimize selection bias.

Patients were followed throughout hospitalization with regular clinical and laboratory assessments to identify complications, such as pneumonia, secondary skin infection, encephalitis, meningitis, sepsis, septic shock, and the need for respiratory support.

Variable definitions

Immunodeficiency was defined as the presence of one or more of the following conditions documented in the medical records: AIDS, malignancy, solid organ transplantation, current treatment with chemotherapy, radiotherapy, or immunosuppressive agents (cytotoxic drugs, antimetabolites, cyclosporine, or tacrolimus) or prolonged corticosteroid use [3].

Nutritional status was assessed using BMI-based criteria. For individuals aged < 18 years, BMI-for-age z-scores were classified according to WHO standards, with underweight defined as < − 2 SD, overweight as > + 1 SD, and obesity as > + 2 SD [16]. For adults (≥ 18 years), underweight was defined as BMI < 18.5 kg/m², overweight as BMI ≥ 23.5 kg/m², and obesity as BMI ≥ 25 kg/m² [17].

All patients were monitored throughout their hospital stay, and complications present at admission or occurring during the hospital stay were recorded. Complications were defined as organ involvement identified based on clinical manifestations and supported by imaging and laboratory findings. Secondary skin infections were defined as bacterial infections presenting as cellulitis, impetigo, or erysipelas [3, 18]. Pneumonia was determined based on clinical manifestations supported by radiology [8, 19]. Neurological complications, such as encephalitis and meningitis were diagnosed based on clinical presentation, characteristic neuroimaging findings, cerebrospinal fluid abnormalities, and/or a positive varicella PCR result [20]. Sepsis and septic shock were defined according to the Sepsis-3 criteria [21].

Data analysis

The data was analyzed using Stata version 17. Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages. The factors associated with complications were assessed using univariable and multivariable Poisson regression models with robust standard errors. All variables in the univariable analysis were included in the multivariable Poisson regression model. Backward stepwise selection was performed to eliminate non-significant variables until only statistically significant variables remained in the final model. Adjusted risk ratios (aRR) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported, with a statistical significance set at p < 0.05.

Ethics approval

The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Hospital for Tropical Diseases, Ho Chi Minh City (No 1134/QĐ–BVBNĐ, dated 05 November 2024). For the retrospective phase, the requirement for informed consent was waived by the ethics committee. For the prospective phase, written informed consent was obtained from all adult participants. For participants younger than 16 years of age, written informed consent was obtained from their parents or legal guardians. Where appropriate, assent from minor participants was also considered according to their age and capacity to understand the study information. For participants who were unable to provide informed consent, consent was obtained from their legal guardians. All study procedures were conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Majority of patients were aged between 18 and 40, accounting for 69.3%; approximately 11% were below age 18. Male and female patients were roughly equal at 53.8% and 46.2% respectively. 18.54% of patients were pregnant. Overweight/Obesity accounted for 35.87%, while underweight/thinness was 11.55%. The most common comorbidities in this group were hypertension, type 2 diabetes, and cancer, with rates of 6.69%, 4.86%, and 3.95% respectively. Immunodeficiency accounted for 6.08% and 23.1% had received acyclovir treatment before admission (Table 1).

Overall mortality was low (2/329, 0.61%). Both deaths occurred in patients with severe disseminated varicella complicated by multi-organ failure. Five patients (1.52%) were referred to other healthcare facilities, including three clinically stable patients transferred for ongoing management of underlying malignancies and two patients requiring renal replacement therapy.

Immunodeficiency was associated with pneumonia, sepsis, and mortality. Mortality or referral to a higher-level hospital rate was 15% in the immunodeficiency group and 1.3% in the non-immunodeficiency group. The rates of pneumonia and sepsis were both 35% in the immunodeficiency group, compared to 11.7% and 6.5% in the non-immunodeficiency group, respectively (Table 2).

Complications were associated with gender, immunodeficiency, pregnancy, underweight/thinness, and prior acyclovir treatment. Males had a higher risk of complications with a cRR = 1.53 (1.18–1.97), p = 0.001. Immunodeficiency was associated with a higher risk of complications with a cRR = 1.59 (1.16–2.18), p = 0.004. Pregnant and underweight/thinness patients had a lower risk with cRR = 0.56 (0.37–0.86), p = 0.007 and cRR = 0.57 (0.34–0.96), p = 0.034. Patients treated with acyclovir had a higher risk of complications with an cRR = 1.53 (1.21–1.91), p < 0.001. (Table 3).

Discussion

In this study, nearly half of the hospitalized patients developed at least one complication. The overall complication rate was comparable to that reported in previous studies from Vietnam and other hospitalized cohorts internationally [4, 10, 11], suggesting that severe manifestations remain frequent among patients requiring inpatient care. Consistent with previous reports [4, 5, 8], immunocompromised status were independently associated with an increased risk of complications. Overall, most patients experienced favorable outcomes, with only a small minority requiring transfer for advanced, multidisciplinary care; likely reflecting timely recognition and management in a specialized referral center.

The patients in this study were aged 18–40 years, accounting for nearly 70% of all hospitalized cases. Historically, varicella has been primarily reported in children [2, 22]; however, several recent studies have suggested a shift toward older age groups, particularly in tropical countries where universal varicella vaccination has not been widely implemented [15]. Although our findings may partly reflect the study setting—a tertiary infectious diseases hospital that mainly treats adult patients, they nevertheless suggest that adults may represent a substantial proportion of hospitalized varicella cases, highlighting the need for targeted prevention strategies in this population.

Nearly half (45.59%) of the patients in our cohort experienced at least one complication. This proportion is comparable to findings reported by Le Thi Hoa (54.7%) [10] and Nguyen An Nghia (41%) [11], but lower than that reported in the study by Mohindra (68%) [8]. Overall, these results are consistent with a recent systematic review reporting complication rate ranging from 30 to 50% among hospitalized varicella cases [4]. In our study, most patients were older than 18 years, which may partly explain the relatively high complication rate. Varicella in adults tends to have a more severe clinical course as other studies have previously shown [8, 23]. These differences may be partly attributable to the fact that varicella in adults is more likely to occur in individuals with underlying or special clinical conditions that predispose them to more severe disease [23]. These findings underscore the importance of early recognition and close monitoring of varicella cases in adults to reduce the risk of severe complications.

Secondary skin and soft tissue infections were the most common complication. This complication has also been consistently reported as the leading complication in several studies conducted both in Vietnam and internationally [4, 10, 11]. This is likely due to bacterial superinfection through disrupted skin barriers [2, 3]. Preventive strategies for these infections mainly rely on proper hygiene and timely antiviral therapy, as evidence for topical antipruritic agents remains limited [24].

The prevalence of pneumonia was 13%, consistent with previous reports (10–20%) in hospitalized adults with varicella [4, 8]. This may be partly explained by the high proportion of adult patients, as pneumonia is more common in adults and immunocompromised individuals [2, 14]. Varicella pneumonia, resulting from viral or secondary bacterial infection, is a severe complication with a mortality rate as high as 24% [25]. However, in our cohort, overall mortality was low (0.61%, 2/329). Notably, both deaths occurred in patients with severe disseminated varicella complicated by multi-organ failure, suggesting that mortality may be associated with the overall severity of the disease and multiple complications rather than pneumonia alone. Although complications were more frequent among immunocompromised patients, severe outcomes were not exclusively limited to this group [26]. Early recognition and close monitoring of respiratory symptoms are therefore essential to reduce severe outcomes.

Immunodeficiency patients had longer hospital stays, higher complication rates, and worse outcomes than immunocompetent individuals (Table 2). This reflects the role of cell-mediated immunity in controlling varicella-zoster virus replication. Impaired immunity may lead to viral dissemination and multi-organ failure [4, 5, 8]. Preventive strategies are therefore essential in addition to early diagnosis and antiviral therapy [3, 24]. Studies have shown that the recombinant zoster vaccine provides over 70% protection against the disease, which includes immunodeficiency individuals [27]. However, it is worth noting this study had a relatively small number of immunodeficient patients.

The association between prior acyclovir use and complications likely reflects confounding by indication, with more severe cases more likely to receive treatment before admission [3, 24]. The inverse association with underweight/thinness was unexpected and contrasts with previous studies [4, 28]. This finding may reflect limitations in using BMI-based measures as a proxy for nutritional status, particularly in children and adolescents, where misclassification may occur despite age adjustment. Therefore, this result should not be interpreted as evidence of a protective effect. In contrast, overweight and obesity were not significantly associated with complications in this study. While obesity has been implicated in increased severity of several infectious diseases, its role in varicella remains unclear. The absence of a significant association in our cohort may reflect differences in patient characteristics or disease presentation.

Pregnancy was not associated with complications in this study. This contrasts with previous reports showing increased risk of severe disease and adverse fetal outcomes [2, 24, 29]. This discrepancy may be partly explained by differences in hospitalization patterns, as pregnant women may be admitted for closer monitoring even in the absence of severe disease, potentially increasing the proportion of milder cases within this subgroup and influencing the observed association. Male sex was associated with a higher risk of complications. This finding may reflect underlying biological differences in immune response [30] or differences in the distribution of disease severity at admission, although residual confounding cannot be excluded.

This study has several strengths, including its conduct at a tertiary infectious disease hospital with a relatively large sample size. However, it also has limitations. First, this study was conducted in a tertiary infectious diseases hospital and included a selected population of hospitalized patients, many of whom had suspected complications or risk factors for severe disease. In addition, because hospitalization decisions were made according to routine clinical practice, factors other than disease severity, including social circumstances such as the inability to isolate at home, may also have contributed to admission in some cases. As these factors were not systematically recorded, their potential influence on patient selection and the observed associations between risk factors and complications could not be formally assessed. Therefore, the findings may not be generalizable to patients with milder disease in community settings. Second, the study incorporated both retrospective and prospective data collection. Although standardized definitions, case report forms, and cross-checking across multiple hospital sources were used to enhance data quality, some information bias due to incomplete or inaccurately documented clinical information in the original medical records cannot be entirely excluded. No missing values were identified for the primary outcome or variables included in the multivariable analyses; however, under-documentation of certain clinical information in routine medical records may still have occurred. In addition, a sensitivity analysis restricted to the prospective adult cohort yielded findings consistent with the primary analysis, supporting the robustness of the results. Third, molecular confirmation using PCR was not routinely performed in all cases and diagnosis was primarily based on clinical features, reflecting real-world practice. This may have led to a small degree of misclassification. Lastly, although the study included both pediatric and adult patients, the majority were adults, with individuals aged < 18 years accounting for a relatively small proportion (~ 11%). As such, the findings may primarily reflect the clinical characteristics of adult hospitalized patients.

Overall, our findings suggest that varicella imposes a significant burden of complications on hospitalized patients with suspected severity or high-risk factors. These results – particularly among immunocompromised individuals underscores the necessity of early identification of high-risk patients, close clinical monitoring and vaccination to mitigate risk of severe complications in this population.

Conclusions

Varicella complications were prevalent among the hospitalized cohort, with secondary skin infections and pneumonia emerging as the most frequent clinical manifestations. Notably, male sex and immunocompromised status were associated with complications among hospitalized patients with varicella. While early recognition and vigilant monitoring of high-risk individuals may improve clinical outcomes, further research across more diverse populations is warranted to validate these findings.
