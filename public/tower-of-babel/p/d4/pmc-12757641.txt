The scaffold protein IQGAP1 regulates the epithelial Na+ channel
Authors: Louise Thines, Shujie Shi, Samar Sayedyahossein, Zhigang Li, Francis J. Roushar, Thomas R. Kleyman, David B. Sacks
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The epithelial Na+ channel (ENaC), by mediating the flow of Na+ ions across biological membranes, plays a key role in the regulation of Na+ and water absorption in several human epithelia. IQGAP1 is a scaffold protein that mediates diverse cellular processes, including intracellular signaling and trafficking. In this study, we establish that IQGAP1 binds to ENaC and regulates its functions. We observed that IQGAP1 coresides with aquaporin 2 in ENaC-expressing principal cells of the distal nephron of mouse kidney. We showed that purified IQGAP1 pulls down the α-, β-, and γ-subunits of ENaC. We demonstrated that endogenous IQGAP1 coimmunoprecipitates with ENaC subunits in Madin–Darby canine kidney cell lysates. Functionally, coexpression of IQGAP1 with ENaC in Xenopus oocytes reduces ENaC surface abundance and amiloride-sensitive Na+ transport activity. Together, our data indicate that IQGAP1 binds to ENaC, which likely influences channel trafficking to regulate its cell surface abundance and Na+ transport function. More broadly, these observations suggest new physiological roles for IQGAP1 in Na+ and water absorption in epithelium.

The epithelial Na+ channel (ENaC) mediates Na+ reabsorption at the apical membrane of epithelial cells in various organs, including the kidney, lung, and colon (1, 2). For example, in principal cells of the kidney aldosterone-sensitive distal nephron (ASDN), ENaC mediates Na+ reabsorption from the ultrafiltrate, thereby controlling net total body Na+ content and extracellular fluid volume, influencing blood pressure (2). As another example, the flow of Na+ ions through ENaC into lung epithelial cells generates an osmotic gradient that drives water absorption and is required to maintain an appropriate fluid layer on airway and alveolar surfaces (3). Structurally, ENaC in renal, lung, and colonic epithelia is a heterotrimer composed of homologous α-, β-, and γ-subunits. Each subunit contains two transmembrane spans connected by a large, highly folded extracellular loop, as well as short cytosolic N and C termini (1, 4). The abundance of ENaC at the apical membrane of epithelial cells is balanced by the folding and assembly of the newly synthesized units in the endoplasmic reticulum (ER), intracellular trafficking to the cell surface, and endocytic removal followed by recycling back to the plasma membrane or lysosomal degradation (5, 6). Once at the cell surface, the Na+ transport activity of ENaC is determined by its channel open probability (PO), which is modulated by extracellular Na+, Cl−, and H+ concentrations, post-translational proteolytic cleavage, shear stress, anionic phospholipids, and palmitoylation, among other factors (2, 7, 8, 9).

IQGAP1 is a ubiquitously expressed scaffold protein that facilitates the formation of intracellular protein complexes. Structurally, IQGAP1 contains five domains, each binding selected proteins. By assembling signalosomes, IQGAP1 regulates multiple cellular activities, including intracellular signaling and actin dynamics (10). In addition, there is growing evidence that IQGAP1 influences protein trafficking (11), through binding to trafficking proteins (12, 13) and by modulating cytoskeleton dynamics (14).

We demonstrate here that IQGAP1 colocalizes with aquaporin 2 (AQP2) in ENaC-expressing principal cells of the distal nephron and binds to the α-, β-, and γ-ENaC subunits. Importantly, we show that IQGAP1 significantly reduces ENaC surface density and amiloride-sensitive Na+ transport activity in Xenopus oocytes. Together, our data strongly suggest that IQGAP1 regulates ENaC trafficking and activity, which implies physiological functions for IQGAP1 in regulating Na+ reabsorption.

Results

IQGAP1 is expressed in principal cells of the distal nephron

One of the primary functions of ENaC is to mediate Na+ reabsorption at the apical membrane of principal cells in the ASDN (15). We first assessed whether IQGAP1 is expressed in ASDN, where ENaC resides. Analysis of published RNA-Seq data reveals that IQGAP1 is expressed in multiple mouse nephron segments, including both the descending limb of the loop of Henle and collecting ducts within the cortex and medulla (16). Among the three major cell types of collecting ducts, principal cells have the highest abundance of IQGAP1 transcripts, followed by type A and type B intercalated cells (17).

To validate these observations at the protein level, we immunostained IQGAP1 in paraformaldehyde-fixed mouse kidney sections. IQGAP1 was specifically detected in mouse renal tubular cells. Moreover, IQGAP1 colocalizes with AQP2, a marker for principal cells in the ASDN (18) (Fig. 1). Note that IQGAP1 was detected both at the basolateral and luminal sides of the AQP2-positive tubules. In contrast, IQGAP1 staining was nearly undetectable in kidneys of IQGAP1-null mice (Fig. 1). In parallel, we expressed glutathione-S-transferase (GST)-tagged IQGAP1 in Escherichia coli and purified it on glutathione-Sepharose beads. The purity and integrity of GST-IQGAP1 were verified by Coomassie blue–stained gels (Fig. 2). We then incubated each ENaC subunit with GST-IQGAP1. We evaluated whether endogenous ENaC subunits in cell lysates bind to pure IQGAP1. We used human epithelial lung cancer A549 cells that endogenously express all three ENaC subunits (19). We incubated A549 cell lysates with GST-IQGAP1 and performed pulldowns. Endogenous α-ENaC and β-ENaC bind to pure GST-IQGAP1 (Fig. 2, D and E). Note that the endogenous ENaC subunits in the cell lysates migrate at a higher apparent molecular weight than the TNT fragments, likely because of endogenous glycosylation (20). These results confirm that endogenous α-ENaC and β-ENaC in cell lysates bind to pure IQGAP1.

IQGAP1 forms a complex with ENaC channels in cells

Analysis was performed with Madin–Darby canine kidney (MDCK) cells that stably express mouse hemagglutinin (HA)-tagged α-ENaC, V5-tagged β-ENaC, and Myc-tagged γ-ENaC (MDCK-ENaC) (21). HA immunoprecipitation from cell lysates, followed by Western blotting, showed that IQGAP1, β-ENaC, and γ-ENaC coimmunoprecipitate with α-ENaC (Fig. 3A). No V5, Myc, or IQGAP1 signal was observed in control precipitation, validating specificity of binding. α-ENaC binds to the IQ domain of IQGAP1

IQGAP1 comprises five domains separated by linkers (Fig. 4, A and B). We investigated the region of IQGAP1 to which α-ENaC binds. To do so, we produced TNT Myc-tagged full-length IQGAP1 as well as IQGAP1 fragments encompassing its N-terminal half (amino acids 2–863), IQ region (717–916), and C-terminal half (864–1657) (Fig. 4C). We were unable to purify pure full-length tagged α-ENaC protein. Therefore, in vitro binding studies were performed with the GST-tagged C-terminal portion of α-ENaC (amino acids 336–669), which we expressed in E. coli. Western blotting validated the expression of the GST-tagged α-ENaC C-terminal and IQGAP1 fragments (Fig. 4, D and E). After verifying that α-ENaC-C binds to full-length IQGAP1 (Fig. 4D), we observed that it pulls down both the N-terminal and IQ regions of IQGAP1 (Fig. 4E). In contrast, neither the C-terminal half of IQGAP1 nor GST alone bound. To validate this observation, we used an IQGAP1 construct lacking the IQ domain (amino acids 746–860), termed IQGAP1ΔIQ (22) (Fig. 4C). We incubated GST-tagged IQGAP1ΔIQ and full-length IQGAP1 with α-ENaC produced by TNT. By contrast to full-length IQGAP1, minimal α-ENaC binds to GST-IQGAP1ΔIQ (Fig. 4F). Together, these results validate that the IQ domain of IQGAP1 is necessary and sufficient to bind to α-ENaC.

IQGAP1 reduces the Na+ transport activity and surface abundance of ENaC in oocytes

To determine if IQGAP1 modulates ENaC function, we examined the effect of mouse IQGAP1 on the Na+ transport activity of mouse αβγ-ENaC in Xenopus oocytes by two-electrode voltage clamp. Oocytes expressing ENaC display inward Na+ currents, as expected. The ENaC inhibitor amiloride markedly reduces the currents, indicating that most of the signal reflects ENaC activity (Fig. 5, A and C). To further challenge whether IQGAP1 directly regulates ENaC transport activity, we coexpressed ENaC with IQGAP1ΔIQ, which lacks the ENaC binding site. Surprisingly, and in contrast to wildtype IQGAP1, IQGAP1ΔIQ significantly increases ENaC-mediated Na+ currents in Xenopus oocytes (Fig. 5, C and D). These observations suggest that IQGAP1ΔIQ may be a dominant negative inhibitor of endogenous IQGAP1, thereby preventing IQGAP1-mediated reduction of Na+ import by ENaC.

To further investigate the mechanism by which IQGAP1 reduces ENaC activity, we assessed the abundance of ENaC at the oocyte surface with and without IQGAP1 coexpression. To do so, we detected the surface expression of β-ENaC with a FLAG tag in its central loop using a chemiluminescence assay we previously described (23). Importantly, IQGAP1 reduces the oocyte surface expression of ENaC by 79% (Fig. 5E). These results strongly suggest that IQGAP1 reduces ENaC-mediated Na+ transport by altering its trafficking to the cell surface.

Discussion

Maintenance of Na+ homeostasis is a fundamental physiological process that influences not only extracellular volume but also blood pressure (24). Defective regulation of Na+ transporters in the ASDN leads to pathological conditions like hypertension, hypotension, or edema (25). In this article, we showed that the scaffold protein IQGAP1 physically interacts with ENaC, the channel protein that mediates the rate-limiting step of Na+ reabsorption in various epithelial tissues (1). Importantly, IQGAP1 regulates ENaC cell surface abundance and, consequently, modulates Na+ transport.

We initially validated that IQGAP1 is expressed in AQP2-expressing principal cells of the ASDN in mouse kidneys, where ENaC resides. This observation is in agreement with RNA-Seq databases identifying IQGAP1 expression in multiple segments of mouse nephrons (16). We also showed, using purified polypeptides and ENaC-overexpressing cells, that IQGAP1 binds to the α-, β-, and γ-subunits of ENaC. We further validated that endogenous IQGAP1 forms a complex with endogenous ENaC in lung epithelial cells. ENaC subunits have C-terminal cytosolic Pro-Tyr motifs that typically bind to WW domain–containing proteins (26). Our initial hypothesis was that the WW domain of IQGAP1 would mediate its interaction with ENaC. Unexpectedly, our binding assays with selected α-ENaC and IQGAP1 constructs revealed that α-ENaC binds to the IQ domain of IQGAP1. Other cases of unexpected binding sites on IQGAP1 have been reported, suggesting unique domain properties. For example, the IQGAP1 WW domain binds to proteins lacking proline-rich regions like ERK1/2 (27). As another example, while Ca2+ associations are not common for calponin homology domains, the IQGAP1 calponin homology domain interacts with calmodulin and with Ca2+ (28). These findings emphasize the importance of future structural investigation of IQGAP in complex with its binding partners to understand its scaffolding mechanisms.

Functionally, our chemiluminescence-based surface expression assay shows that IQGAP1 reduces ENaC cell surface abundance when expressed in Xenopus oocytes. This observation indicates that IQGAP1 regulates ENaC trafficking. IQGAP1 modulates trafficking of diverse plasma membrane proteins, including the CXCR4 and DOR1 G protein–coupled receptors (29), glutamate-gated N-methyl-D-aspartate receptors (30), the epidermal growth factor receptor (31), E-cadherin (32), nephrin (33) adhesion molecules, and TRPA1 ion channels (34). IQGAP1 also regulates trafficking of insulin-secreting membranes (35) as well as of Epstein–Barr virus and HIV (36) viral particles. The exact mechanisms by which IQGAP1 regulates trafficking of selected proteins remain to be elucidated. IQGAP1 binds to several trafficking proteins, including endosomal sorting proteins TSG101 and ALIX (37), exocyst subunits Sec3 and Sec8 (13), the SNARE protein Syntaxin-A1 (12), the caveolar protein caveolin-1 (38), the adaptor complex AP2 (39), and the trafficking GTPases Arf1 (40), Arf6 (41), Rab5, and Rab10 (42). IQGAP1 also coordinates actin dynamics (43), with possible implications for membrane trafficking (14).

The data presented here, combined with current understanding of ENaC trafficking (5, 44), suggest several hypotheses that could explain how IQGAP1 reduces ENaC surface density. In vivo studies support the concept that ENaC is in an intracellular pool under normal, salt-replete conditions and traffics to the apical membrane of epithelial cells for Na+ absorption under conditions of a low Na+ diet (44, 45). IQGAP1 could reduce trafficking of this ENaC pool to the apical membrane, for example, by blocking its movement to the ER, Golgi apparatus, or trafficking vesicles. In addition, individual unassembled ENaC subunits are not stable and are targeted to proteasomal degradation (46, 47, 48, 49). IQGAP1 could interfere with the assembly of newly synthesized ENaC subunits into a functional channel in the ER, leading to reduced surface targeting. Alternatively, IQGAP1 could stimulate endocytic removal of the ENaC channel from the cell surface. This process is initiated by Nedd4-2-mediated ubiquitination of ENaC (50). Ubiquitinated ENaC then undergoes clathrin-mediated endocytosis, during which it interacts with the AP2 complex (50). Interestingly, IQGAP1 also binds AP2 and promotes apical protein endocytosis in kidney epithelial cells (39). Moreover, the endocytic protein caveolin-1 binds to ENaC, which reduces its cell surface abundance (51). IQGAP1 also interacts with caveolin-1, suggesting that it could also promote caveolae-mediated ENaC endocytosis (38). Post-endocytosis, IQGAP1 may impair ENaC recycling to the apical membrane, a process that requires ENaC deubiquitination. Altered deubiquitination may also lead to increased lysosomal degradation (52). Such functions for IQGAP1 in regulating plasma membrane protein turnover are supported by a recent report of IQGAP1 increasing epidermal growth factor receptor plasma membrane abundance via reduced receptor lysosomal degradation (31). More generally, IQGAP1 was also reported to modulate protein recycling by regulating early endosome interactions with microtubules (29). IQGAP1 may also indirectly reduce ENaC trafficking through its influence on actin remodeling. For example, IQGAP1-mediated cytoskeletal rearrangements inhibit E-cadherin endocytosis (32). IQGAP1 binding to several other trafficking proteins may further influence ENaC trafficking. For instance, both ENaC (53) and IQGAP1 (12) bind to the t-SNARE Syntaxin-A1, suggesting that these interactions could regulate SNARE-mediated ENaC trafficking. While additional studies are required to delineate the mechanism(s) by which IQGAP1 modulates ENaC surface density, our results reinforce the role of IQGAP1 in intracellular trafficking.

Importantly, we showed that reduced ENaC surface levels correlate with decreased amiloride-sensitive Na+ currents in oocytes. This observation strongly suggests that IQGAP1 impairs ENaC-mediated Na+ absorption by decreasing channel expression at the cell surface. While ENaC PO is influenced by various extracellular and intracellular factors that modulate Na+ transport (2, 7, 8, 9), we have no evidence that IQGAP1 affects ENaC PO. Nevertheless, the different magnitudes of IQGAP1-induced reduction of ENaC Na+ currents and surface expression may result from the trafficking of low PO, low-conducting ENaC channels to the oocyte surface.

In contrast to wildtype IQGAP1, IQGAP1ΔIQ (lacking the ENaC binding site) increases ENaC-mediated Na+ currents. This suggests that IQGAP1ΔIQ may inhibit endogenous IQGAP1 in oocytes by functioning as a dominant negative construct. We previously observed that deletion of the GTPase-activating protein–related domain (GRD) from IQGAP1 (IQGAP1ΔGRD) similarly produces a dominant negative protein (54). The mechanism by which IQGAP1ΔIQ produces its dominant negative effect is unknown, but there are several possibilities. For example, IQGAP1 was previously demonstrated to dimerize (55). IQGAP1ΔIQ may trap endogenous IQGAP1 in nonfunctional heterodimers, thereby abrogating the inhibition of ENaC Na+ currents by the endogenous IQGAP1. Another possibility is that IQGAP1ΔIQ may inhibit ENaC regulators by forming inactive complexes. Importantly, several other proteins, including growth factor receptors, Ca2+-binding proteins, and signaling kinases, bind to the IQ domain of IQGAP1 (10). Therefore, the opposite effect of wildtype IQGAP1 to IQGAP1ΔIQ on ENaC Na+ transport may result from disrupted interactions of IQGAP1 with other IQ domain–binding partners that indirectly influence ENaC functions. Regardless of the molecular mechanism by which full-length IQGAP1 and IQGAP1ΔIQ influence ENaC-mediated Na+ currents, our combined data strongly suggest that IQGAP1 is a regulator of ENaC channels.

IQGAP1 binds to and regulates other ion channels and transporters, including the Cu2+ ATPase ATP7A (56), the Cl−/HCO3− exchanger pendrin (57), and the cation channel TRPA1 (34). Interestingly, the IQGAP1–ENaC interaction that we observed in lung epithelial cells may have physiological implications in the regulation of airway surface liquid volume, which is maintained by the balanced transport of sodium across the airway epithelium. ENaC trafficking also constitutes an important mechanism to regulate salt reabsorption in distal nephrons of the kidney. Since we showed that IQGAP1 and ENaC are both expressed in principal cells of the mouse ASDN, their interaction could potentially be physiologically relevant for balanced kidney Na+ reabsorption across apical epithelial membranes. These hypotheses need to be evaluated in future in vivo studies.

In summary, we documented here that IQGAP1 is a previously unidentified binding partner of ENaC that regulates its activity by reducing the number of channels at the cell surface. These results reinforce the notion that IQGAP1 coordinates intracellular trafficking. Our data also suggest important new physiological functions for IQGAP1 in Na+ absorption across apical epithelial membranes.

Mouse kidney immunofluorescence staining

Wildtype and IQGAP1-null (littermate control) (60) mice were bred in the animal facility at the National Institutes of Health (NIH). Mice were maintained according to the NIH guidelines, with approval of the NIH Animal Care and Use Committee. Mice were euthanized at 4 to 6 months of age. The kidneys were harvested and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde at 4 °C for 16 h. Samples were embedded in paraffin wax and cut into 8 μm-thick serial sections. After deparaffinization and rehydration, the tissues were blocked in 10% goat serum. Kidney sections were then incubated for 16 h at 4 °C with rabbit anti-IQGAP1 and rat anti-AQP2 antibodies. After washing with PBS, the sections were incubated with donkey anti-rabbit Alexa488 (2 μg/ml) and donkey anti-rat Cy3 (0.5 μg/ml) for 2 h at 20 °C. A Leica SP8 confocal microscope was used to image the sections with a 40X oil, 1.3 numerical aperture objective.

Cell culture and treatments

A549 cells were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection. The MDCK cell line expressing HA-α, V5-β, and Myc-γ ENaC has been previously described (21). Cells were grown at 37 °C with 5% CO2 in Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (MDCK-ENaC) or RPMI1640 (A549) medium, supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum. Where indicated, cells were transfected using Lipofectamine 2000 according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Plasmid constructs

GST-tagged human IQGAP1 and IQGAP1ΔIQ (lacking residues 746–860) cloned in the pGEX-2T vector, as well as Myc-tagged constructs of full-length IQGAP1, IQGAP1-N (amino acids 2–863), IQGAP1-C (864–1657), and IQGAP1-IQ (717–916) in the pcDNA3.1 vector, have been previously described (22, 61). pcDNA3.1 plasmids coding for FLAG-tagged human α-ENaC (catalog no.: 83430), V5-tagged human β-ENaC (catalog no.: 83429), and Myc-tagged human γ-ENaC (catalog no.: 83428) were obtained from Addgene. The GST-tagged ⍺-ENaC-C construct (amino acids 336–669) was generated by PCR using the plasmid coding for FLAG-tagged human α-ENaC as a template and the primers 5′-CGGGATCCCAGAATGACTTCATTCCCCTCCTG-3′ (forward) and 5′-CGGAATTCTCTAGACTACTTGTCATCGTCGTCCTTGTAGTCGG-3′ (reverse). The construct was then inserted into the pGEX-2T plasmid into the BamHI and EcoRI restriction sites. All plasmids were validated by sequencing, and the constructs migrated to the expected positions on SDS-PAGE.

Expression and purification of fusion proteins

GST-IQGAP1, GST-IQGAP1ΔIQ, and GST-α-ENaC-C were expressed in E. coli and isolated using glutathione-Sepharose, essentially as previously described (28). The IQGAP1-N, -IQ, and -C peptides, as well as the α-, β-, and γ-subunits of ENaC, were synthesized with the TNT quick-coupled Transcription/Translation system (Promega). The kit was used according to the manufacturer’s instructions, using 1 μg of plasmid as a template. Note that the ENaC subunits produced by TNT migrated on SDS-PAGE to a lower molecular weight (∼75 kDa) than their endogenous counterparts in cells (∼90 kDa), likely because of the absence of glycosylation (20) or other post-translational modifications of the endogenous proteins.

Pulldowns with GST-tagged constructs

For pulldowns of TNT products, samples were precleared using glutathione-Sepharose beads for 1 h at 4 °C. Samples were then incubated with glutathione-Sepharose beads coated with the indicated protein for 3 h at 4 °C in 1 ml buffer A (150 mM NaCl, 50 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.4, 1% Triton X-100, and 1 mM EGTA). Beads were washed five times with buffer A. Proteins bound to the beads were eluted by resuspension in Laemmli sample buffer and incubation at 95 °C for 5 min. Samples were separated by SDS-PAGE and analyzed by Coomassie blue staining or Western blotting, as described in the figure legends.

For pulldowns from cell lysates, cells were washed with ice-cold PBS and lysed by sonication in buffer A supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Insoluble fractions were pelleted at 15,000 g for 10 min at 4 °C. Equal amounts of protein from cell lysates were precleared and subjected to pulldown as described above.

Immunoprecipitations

For immunoprecipitation of α- and β-ENaC, cells were washed twice with ice-cold PBS and lysed by sonication in buffer A supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Insoluble fractions were pelleted by centrifugation at 15,000 g for 10 min at 4 °C. Cell lysates were precleared with protein A-Sepharose for 1 h at 4 °C and then incubated with anti-α- or anti-β-ENaC antibodies for 3 h. Samples processed in parallel with IgG were the negative controls. Immune complexes were isolated using protein A-Sepharose for 2 h at 4 °C. After five washes with buffer A, proteins bound to the beads were eluted by resuspension in Laemmli sample buffer and heated at 95 °C for 5 min. Samples were analyzed by SDS-PAGE and Western blotting.

For immunoprecipitation of HA-α-ENaC, cells were lysed as described above, followed by preclearing of the cell lysate with agarose beads for 1 h at 4 °C. Samples were then incubated with anti-HA affinity gel or control agarose beads for 3 h at 4 °C. Bead washing and protein elution were done as described above.

Xenopus oocyte expression and two-electrode voltage clamp

Circular RNAs (cRNAs) of the α-, β-, and γ-subunits of mouse ENaC and of mouse wildtype IQGAP1 and IQGAP1ΔIQ were synthesized using the mMESSAGE mMACHINE transcription kit (Invitrogen) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Oocytes were harvested from Xenopus laevis following a protocol approved by the University of Pittsburgh Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee. Stage V–VI oocytes were injected with 1 ng cRNA of each ENaC subunit, with or without coinjection of 1 ng IQGAP1 cRNA. Oocytes were then incubated for 24 h at 18 °C in a modified Barth’s saline (88 mM NaCl, 1 mM KCl, 2.4 mM NaHCO3, 15 mM Hepes, 0.3 mM Ca(NO3)2, 0.41 mM CaCl2, 0.82 mM MgSO4, 10 μg/ml sodium penicillin, 10 μg/ml streptomycin sulfate, 100 μg/ml gentamycin sulfate, pH 7.4). Whole-cell Na+ currents were measured by clamping oocytes at −100 mV, with continuous perfusion with a solution containing 110 mM NaCl, 2 mM KCl, 1.6 mM CaCl2, and 10 mM Hepes, pH adjusted to 7.4. At the end of each recording, 10 μM amiloride was applied to block ENaC activity. Voltage clamp was performed using a GeneClamp 500B amplifier and DigiData 1440A interface (Molecular Devices). Electrophysiological data were analyzed with Clampfit 10.5 (Molecular Devices).

Xenopus oocyte surface abundance assay

ENaC surface abundance in oocytes was measured using a chemiluminescence assay as previously described (23). Briefly, oocytes were injected with 1 ng mouse cRNA of wildtype α- and γ-ENaC and 1 ng mouse cRNA of β-ENaC with an extracellular FLAG tag, with or without coinjection of 1 ng mouse IQGAP1 cRNA. ENaC surface levels were assessed 2 days after cRNA injection. Oocytes were first blocked in modified Barth’s saline supplemented with 1% bovine serum albumin (30 min, room temperature), labeled with 1 μg/ml anti-FLAG antibodies (2 h, 4 °C), then incubated with 1 μg/ml anti-mouse horseradish peroxidase antibodies (1 h, 4 °C). Non-injected oocytes from the same batch were included as a negative control. Intact oocytes were individually transferred into a 96-well plate. Chemiluminescence was developed with Super Signal ELISA Femto Substrates, and relative light units were quantified using a GloMax-Multi Detection System.
