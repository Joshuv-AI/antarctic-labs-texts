Exploring gender-based challenges after the earthquake: a phenomenological study of women in Turkey
Authors: Gizem ÇITAK, Serpil TOKER
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEarthquakes often exacerbate existing gender inequalities, particularly in regions where women’s social status is already vulnerable. This study investigates how the February 2023 earthquake in Türkiye affected the lived experiences of women, with a specific focus on gender-based challenges.MethodsThis phenomenological qualitative study, grounded in a Heideggerian approach, was conducted with 45 women residing in the earthquake-affected region between March 1 and April 1, 2024. Participants were selected using purposive and snowball sampling techniques. Data were collected through semi-structured interviews, transcribed verbatim, and analyzed using inductive content analysis with MAXQDA 24. Throughout the research process, researchers practiced bracketing (epoché) to minimize bias and remain focused on the participants’ lived experiences. The study adhered to the COREQ guidelines.ResultsFive main themes and eleven sub-themes were identified: (1) Necessities—unmet basic needs including hygiene and shelter; (2) Role of Masculinity—increased financial burdens and paternal responsibility among men; (3) Role of Femininity—comprising five sub-themes such as habitual constraints, vulnerability, maternal, sexual, and psychological burdens; (4) Women’s Power—expressions of resilience and empowerment; and (5) Gender Discrimination—experiences of oppression, invisibility, and unequal treatment. Women reported significant challenges in meeting their own and their children’s basic and emotional needs, with amplified inequality and psychological distress.ConclusionsThe findings reveal that the earthquake intensified existing gender-based disparities, impacting women’s physical, emotional, and social well-being. Addressing these disparities requires more than integrating gender equality into disaster policies—it demands systemic changes, inclusive planning, and context-specific interventions that prioritize women’s voices, safety, and well-being in all phases of disaster response.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-23907-6.

Introduction

Gender is a socially constructed and dynamic concept that can vary across societies, cultures, and time periods. Social roles and expectations based on gender significantly shape how individuals react to disasters and how they cope with their consequences. International research reveals that women and men are affected differently by disaster risks, and their coping mechanisms vary across regions and cultural contexts [1].

Studies have shown that women tend to mobilize more rapidly than men in post-disaster survival and recovery processes [2]. However, they also face increased exposure to violence during and after disasters and may experience significant security concerns within their living environments. Additionally, the trafficking of women and children tends to rise during such emergencies [3, 4].

In disaster situations, women’s safety is often compromised due to overcrowded shelters, rising levels of domestic violence, and broader gender inequalities [5]. This increase in violence has been associated with men’s prolonged unemployment, fear of death, and difficulties in controlling anger during crisis situations [6, 7]. At the same time, women are burdened with additional responsibilities such as caring for the household, preparing food, and maintaining hygiene under harsh conditions [6]. Emotional trauma, stress, anxiety, shock, and sleep disturbances have also been commonly reported by women following disasters [8, 9].

This study aims to address the problems experienced by women exposed to gender inequality in the aftermath of a disaster. By identifying these issues, it seeks to contribute to the development of appropriate health policies and the implementation of targeted awareness and training programs. Ultimately, the study aims to support efforts toward reducing gender-based disparities and improving disaster preparedness and recovery strategies for women.

Material method

Purpose of the study

This phenomenological study aimed to determine women’s exposure to gender inequality following the earthquake.

Type of study

This phenomenological qualitative study, grounded in a Heideggerian approach, was conducted with 45 women residing in the earthquake-affected region between March 1 and April 1, 2024. Participants were selected using purposive and snowball sampling techniques. Data were collected through semi-structured interviews, transcribed verbatim, and analyzed using inductive content analysis with MAXQDA 24. To enhance the trustworthiness of the findings, the researchers engaged in bracketing (epoché) throughout the research process, actively reflecting on and setting aside their own assumptions and preconceptions to focus on the participants’ lived experiences. The study adhered to the COREQ guidelines [10].

Population and sample of the study

The population of this study was selected from women residing in the earthquake-affected area between March 1, 2024 and April 1, 2024. The sample consisted of 45 women who visited the obstetrics outpatient clinics of a university hospital. In qualitative studies, a specific sample size is not predetermined; thus, data collection was concluded when saturation was reached, which means that the concepts and responses that could potentially answer the research questions began to repeat during the data collection process [11].

Data collection tools

Data were collected by the researcher through a questionnaire that included sociodemographic characteristics of the participants (age, education level, occupation, social security status, employment status, etc.), 24 questions regarding their knowledge about the earthquake [12, 13], and 7 semi-structured open-ended questions about gender inequality.

Data evaluation

Data were collected through semi-structured face-to-face interviews. The interviews were conducted in a quiet and private setting to ensure participants’ comfort and confidentiality. Each interview lasted approximately 30 to 45 min, depending on the participant’s willingness and the depth of the answers provided.

The interview process was initiated by obtaining informed consent from each participant. The researcher explained the purpose of the study, the confidentiality of the responses, and the voluntary nature of participation. Once consent was granted, the interviews proceeded with the help of a designed questionnaire.

The questionnaire was divided into three sections:

Sociodemographic Information (age, education level, occupation, etc.).

Knowledge about the Earthquake (24 questions related to earthquake awareness).

Gender Inequality (7 semi-structured open-ended questions aimed at exploring perceptions and experiences related to gender inequality post-earthquake).

The semi-structured format allowed for flexible responses, encouraging participants to share their personal experiences and thoughts freely, while ensuring the consistency of the questions across all participants. Additionally, follow-up questions were posed as needed to clarify or expand on certain responses.

All interviews were audio-recorded with the consent of the participants to ensure accuracy and completeness of the data. The recordings were then transcribed verbatim and analyzed using MAXQDA 24 software.

Findings

The sociodemographic characteristics of the participants in this study revealed that 37.8% were primary school graduates, 71.1% had an income that was equal to their expenses, 77.8% resided in the district, and 93.3% had a nuclear family structure. Among the participants, 73.3% were married, and 66.7% had been married for 11 years or more (Table 1).

Regarding the obstetric characteristics, 57.8% of the women had experienced three or more pregnancies, and 44.4% had three or more children. After the earthquake, 77.8% reported that they were able to rebuild their homes. In terms of personal needs, 64.4% of the women stated that their most urgent needs included such as freedom, love, money, work, and psychological needs, and 71.1% indicated that they had partially met these needs (Table 1).

Theme 3. the role of femininity

In the study, women stated that they were involved in many roles and responsibilities due to their social position. The Role of Femininity was defined under five sub-themes: a (habitualities), b (vulnerability), c (the burden of motherhood), d (sexual burden), and e (psychological burden).

Habitualities

Women have stated that they are subject to male pressure in society and that they do not have the freedom to make decisions. They stated that the reason for this is the cultural order that has persisted from the past to the present.

“That inequality affects our mental health in every way; it affects our brain, our thoughts, and it makes us not speak our thoughts openly. In other words, we cannot say everything openly; sometimes we are not given the right to speak. We are told that you cannot do this; you cannot do that. They do not think that women can achieve “something”.(P4).

“That inequality affects our mental health in every way; it affects our brain, our thoughts, and it makes us not speak our thoughts openly. In other words, we cannot say everything openly; sometimes we are not given the right to speak. We are told that you cannot do this; you cannot do that. They do not think that women can achieve “something”.(P4).

“As a result, men have always been in charge here. Before we got married, at home, we did whatever my father requested, because he would yell at us if we didn’t comply, and we would follow his instructions as soon as he said it. After we got married, we became habitual for us to do whatever our husband said as soon as he instructed, and we became accustomed to it, so we always followed his directives. We already live in a place where the male is dominant; it cannot be expected to be different.’’ (P13).

Discussion

The findings of this study reveal that, in the aftermath of the earthquake, women were confronted with severe challenges related to hygiene, safety, shelter, and economic insecurity. Participants frequently reported being unable to bathe safely, lacking access to sanitary products during menstruation, and having to share overcrowded living spaces under unsanitary conditions. These unmet needs created not only physical discomfort but also deep emotional distress, often articulated as feelings of shame, invisibility, and a loss of control over their own bodies.

From a phenomenological perspective, these experiences illustrate how women’s bodily presence in the disaster context was disrupted. The inability to perform basic hygiene routines—such as menstruation management or private bathing—was not merely an inconvenience but a lived rupture of bodily dignity and personhood. Women described a heightened bodily awareness, where the skin, odors, and physical proximity to others were no longer neutral but charged with anxiety and embarrassment. Temporally, this loss of bodily autonomy extended across days and weeks, making each passing moment a sustained reminder of displacement and dehumanization.

Our findings underscore that women’s specific bodily and psychological needs—such as menstrual hygiene, spatial privacy, and safety—must be treated as central in humanitarian response, rather than auxiliary concerns. Similar conclusions have been drawn in previous research [14–19], which further supports the call for gender-sensitive, embodied approaches to post-disaster intervention.

The findings of this study reveal that many women experienced a profound sense of sexual reluctance following the earthquake, often feeling unable to refuse their husbands’ sexual demands. These encounters, situated within emotionally exhausting states and overcrowded, non-private spaces, were described as not only physically unwanted but also as deeply psychologically intrusive. For many women, sexual intimacy ceased to be an act of connection and instead became a source of anxiety, loss of agency, and emotional dissonance.

Phenomenologically, these accounts reflect a disruption of bodily integrity and relational autonomy. The lack of privacy rendered their bodies publicly exposed yet personally inaccessible—a paradox of visibility without control. Women spoke of their bodies as no longer their own, responding mechanically while internally withdrawing. This inward retreat marks a breakdown in embodied intimacy, where the act of sex is lived not as a mutual experience but as an emotional evacuation. Temporally, this shift persisted beyond the immediate disaster, with some women fearing future pregnancies or completely withdrawing from sexual contact, highlighting how trauma can become sedimented in the lived body.

Similar dynamics have been reported after the Nepal and Haiti earthquakes, where coerced sexual interactions and the collapse of emotional safety contributed to severe psychosocial strain among women [17, 18, 20]. Likewise, studies from post-disaster contexts in China indicate that emotional disconnect and coercion within marriages often translate into long-term psychological distress [20]. These findings emphasize the urgent need for psychosocial interventions that engage with intimate, embodied vulnerabilities—not merely generalized trauma. Furthermore, the absence of private, safe living spaces stands as a structural violence against women’s right to consent, safety, and embodied autonomy. Emergency housing responses must prioritize these aspects to reduce the emotional and sexual burden experienced by women in post-disaster contexts.

Our study highlights that women experienced an intense psychological burden in the aftermath of the earthquake, often describing themselves as more emotionally sensitive, vulnerable, and overwhelmed. Participants conveyed not just a cognitive recognition of stress but a lived sense of helplessness and emotional exhaustion that permeated their everyday existence. The psychological fatigue was not abstract—it was felt in the body as heaviness, insomnia, chest tightness, and a paralyzing sense of internal collapse.

This burden was deeply entangled with women’s perceived and internalized responsibilities for family survival. Phenomenologically, their subjectivity became structured around caregiving, even when they themselves were unraveling. Participants described the dissonance between their emotional depletion and the social expectation to “hold everything together.” This gap—between internal fragility and external performance—was a central axis of distress. Temporally, the emotional weight was not limited to the immediate post-disaster phase but extended into enduring sense of anticipatory anxiety: fear of aftershocks, economic precarity, and family breakdown.

These findings resonate with prior research showing that women are more likely to report severe psychological distress following disasters [21–24]. For instance, Tearne et al. [17] found significantly higher distress levels in women compared to men following earthquakes. In this context, disaster preparedness and recovery must go beyond clinical diagnosis and address embodied psychological vulnerability. Sexual and reproductive health (SRH) services and gender-responsive psychosocial support, should be embedded into both the Local Level Health Working Group Operation Plans (LLHWGOP) and the national Turkey Disaster Response Plan (TAMP). Rather than framing women as inherently weaker, their psychological distress should be understood as a consequence of disproportionate responsibility and embodied caregiving—making them a priority in equitable recovery planning.

Our findings indicate that women perceived themselves as living in a male-dominated society shaped by deep-rooted cultural norms that limit their autonomy. Participants frequently reported lacking the freedom to make decisions, both in their personal lives and within broader disaster-related processes. While men often migrated in search of economic opportunity, women remained behind to carry out domestic labor and manage the consequences of the earthquake. This double burden—disaster-induced instability layered on top of entrenched gender roles—was experienced not just cognitively but affectively, and corporeally.

Phenomenologically, many women described their roles as “obligated survivors,” expected to endure, serve, and sustain others without recognition. The repetition of domestic tasks under stress was experienced as a form of bodily erasure; their efforts were physically exhausting and emotionally invisible. Several women voiced a profound weariness—not just from the labor itself, but from being overlooked, unheard, and unvalued. This invisibility was not metaphorical: it was lived through dismissive interactions, the absence of consultation in decision-making, and a persistent silencing of their needs.

These accounts align with Tearne et al. [17], who describe post-disaster stressors as layered upon pre-existing patriarchal structures and cultural beliefs about women’s rights and capabilities. Similarly, Gündüz [25] found that women’s needs were systematically ignored following disasters. Our findings reaffirm that disempowerment is not a passive condition but an embodied experience—marked by fatigue, resignation, and emotional depletion.

Importantly, participants highlighted the need for women to have a voice in both pre-disaster preparation and post-disaster response. In the eastern regions of Türkiye most affected by the 6 February Kahramanmaraş earthquake—where women’s educational attainment and social status are often low—this call becomes especially urgent. Empowering women in these contexts must be more than a policy directive; it must respond to the lived realities of exclusion and build structures that recognize women’s capacities, voices, and emotional labor, considering them as central to resilience.

Our research reveals that women were subjected to gender-based discrimination by both their husbands and extended family members in the aftermath of the earthquake. These discriminatory dynamics were not merely interpersonal tensions, but deeply embodied experiences that shaped women’s sense of self-worth, agency, and visibility. Participants described being excluded from decision-making, having their opinions dismissed, or being reduced to passive caretakers within their own families. This marginalization was lived as a persistent invalidation of their personhood—women felt “present but unheard,” “useful but unrecognized.”

Phenomenologically, these experiences reflect a rupture in intersubjectivity and social belonging. Women were not only physically displaced by the earthquake, but also emotionally displaced from relational networks that should have offered support. The repeated denial of voice and value led many to internalize a diminished sense of agency. Their bodies were conceived of as sites of both labor and silence—serving others while being denied participation. This duality produced feelings of resentment, fatigue, and invisibility.

Such findings reflect existing literature showing that disasters often exacerbate pre-existing inequalities, particularly for marginalized groups such as women [26, 27]. Effective disaster response must therefore recognize how systemic gender norms manifest not only in policy gaps but in women’s lived experiences of exclusion, silence, and constraint. Empowering women to take on leadership roles in disaster response is not just about representation, but about restoring agency and validating the emotional and practical labor they already contribute.

Our data also echo previous earthquake studies, which found that entrenched sociocultural barriers—especially in less privileged regions—limit women’s ability to act as agents of change [17, 28]. These attitudes may prevent the recognition of women’s capacities in disaster contexts, rendering their contributions invisible. For true gender equity in crisis response, these lived obstacles must be addressed alongside institutional reforms, so that women’s roles in rescue, care, and recovery are seen, valued, and structurally supported.

Conclusion

Our research results show that the persistence of gender inequality has resulted in worse outcomes for women during and after the earthquake, such as inequalities in access to livelihoods, high rates of violence against women, unclear legal frameworks regarding women’s rights, and persistent discriminatory attitudes towards women. Implementing measures to address these problems can help to reduce adverse impacts on women. In more general terms, the article’s conclusions are supported by a large body of literature suggesting that mainstreaming gender equality in disaster preparedness policy alone is not sufficient to address the negative impacts of disasters on marginalised groups such as women and girls. Instead, systemic and socio-cultural factors related to the gender context should also be addressed [29].

The United Nations International Strategy for Disaster Reduction [30] shows that mainstreaming gender equality is a parallel but interlinked process to sustainable development policies and activities in disaster reduction, and recommends integrating gender, development, environmental management, and disaster risk reduction into both research and practice.
