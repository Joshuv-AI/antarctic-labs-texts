Longevity as a quality control metric for male Aedes aegypti (Diptera: Culicidae): does test design matter?
Authors: Hanano Yamada, Claudia Martina, Patindé Didier Alexandre Kaboré, Yiquan Cai, Wadaka Mamai, Hamidou Maiga, Jérémy Bouyer, Chantel Janet de Beer, Muhammad Chaudhury
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn mosquito Sterile Insect Technique (SIT) programs, adult male longevity is widely used as a quality control (QC) indicator for process monitoring and post-treatment assessment. However, longevity assays are frequently implemented using non-standardized protocols, limiting their comparability and operational interpretation. In this study, we evaluated whether and how key assay design parameters influence measured longevity and the ability of longevity-based tests to detect high dose irradiation related quality effects in male Aedes aegypti (Linnaeus, 1762). Using a factorial cage-based design, we assessed the effects of adult holding density, female presence, and observation period on male survival under standard holding conditions. Increasing density reduced male survival, primarily reflecting an assay-related stress effect. Irradiation consistently reduced longevity across all experimental conditions, while female presence imposed additional survival costs under some conditions. Combined high-density and mixed-sex environments produced the lowest overall survival but did not disproportionately amplify irradiation related effects. We also evaluated a rapid acute-stress assay conducted without access to sugar or water. Treatment-related differences were detectable during the early stages of the assay but diminished as mortality approached saturation, demonstrating that rapid stress assays are highly dependent on the timing of assessment. Together, these findings demonstrate that longevity-based QC indicators can be strongly influenced by experimental design and must be applied under standardized and purpose-specific conditions. Rapid stress assays can support routine SIT QC as targeted screening tools, but they require prior calibration and should not be considered universal proxies of overall male quality.Keywords: Sterile Insect Technique, survival assay, mosquito male quality, rapid acute-stress assay

Introduction

The Sterile Insect Technique (SIT) as a tool to reduce targeted mosquito populations is based on mass rearing, female elimination, male reproductive sterilization, and the repeated release of large numbers of sterile males in a treatment area (Dyck et al. 2005, WHO and IAEA 2020). The development and improvements of the various components of the SIT have advanced substantially in recent years for Aedes and Anopheles mosquitoes (Diptera: Culicidae) (Vreysen et al. 2021). One of the core requirements for the SIT is the consistent release of high-quality sterile males capable of surviving, dispersing, and mating under field conditions. Thus SIT programs emphasize continuous quality control (QC) throughout the production chain and post-treatment handling steps to ensure that released insects meet minimum standards and that operational changes do not inadvertently impair performance (Dyck 2010, FAO/IAEA/USDA 2019, Culbert et al. 2020, Dor et al. 2020). In mosquito SIT, product (sterile male) QC typically includes measures of wing length (as a proxy of size), flight ability, longevity, and mating propensity (insemination rates, and mating competitiveness). Among these indicators, adult male longevity is frequently used because it is simple, biologically meaningful (longer-lived males have greater opportunity to mate) and can serve as a proxy to overall male quality. Longevity is therefore often used as both a baseline quality endpoint and as an output that can be impacted by mass rearing, sex sorting, irradiation, chilling, compaction, or transport procedures (Mamai et al. 2016, 2018, Maiga et al. 2017, Culbert et al. 2018, Li et al. 2021, Somda et al. 2022, Yamada et al. 2022, 2023, Balestrino et al. 2024, Eisa et al. 2025). However, longevity is also a trait that might be strongly influenced by laboratory experiment designs. Variation in cage size, holding density, access to sugar and water, handling procedures and frequency, the factor of mating (eg, female presence), and environmental conditions can alter survival outcomes. As a result, longevity outcomes from different studies, facilities, or time periods may not be directly comparable unless methodologies are harmonized and reported with sufficient detail.

The need for standardized QC tests has been widely recognized in mosquito SIT development, including for rapid and scalable assays that can be applied routinely at small or operational scale. Work on standardized flight ability tests highlights a broader lesson for mosquito QC: seemingly minor methodological choices (eg, test density, handling, device configuration, device color, operator) can substantially affect measured outcomes and the capacity to discriminate treatment effects (Dor et al. 2020, Maïga et al. 2022, Eisa et al. 2025). In parallel, guidance documents for mosquito SIT testing and irradiation protocols emphasize the importance of controlled and well-documented procedures, because QC results inform operational decisions and promote confidence in field performance (FAO/IAEA 2019).

While longevity is widely used as a quality indicator in mosquito SIT programs, routine operational QC assays ideally possess several practical characteristics: they should be rapid, simple to perform, require minimal labor and infrastructure, and provide reproducible results that can support operational decision-making. These principles have been key to the development of other standardized QC assays, such as the FAO/IAEA flight ability test for mosquitoes and stress-survival tests used in genetic sexing strains of the Mediterranean fruit fly, which provide rapid indicators of insect quality under standardized conditions. Whether longevity assays can be adapted to fulfil a similar role remains unclear.

Despite its frequent use, the experimental designs reported for longevity assays in mosquitoes remain heterogeneous. Across the mosquito SIT literature, adult longevity is assessed using a wide range of experimental protocols that differ substantially in both biological and logistical parameters (Mamai et al. 2016, 2018, Maiga et al. 2017, Culbert et al. 2018, Li et al. 2021, Somda et al. 2022, Yamada et al. 2022, 2023, Balestrino et al. 2024, Eisa et al. 2025). Common sources of variation include cage size, adult holding density, sex composition, nutritional regime (eg, water only, sucrose solution (5% or 10%), intermittent feeding), environmental conditions, and handling frequency. In many studies, males are held at relatively low densities under ad libitum sugar feeding to estimate baseline longevity, whereas others use higher densities or mixed-sex cages that allow mating and associated energetic expenditure. In some cases, longevity is measured under acute stress conditions, such as deprivation of sugar or water, with the aim of producing a rapid proxy for robustness or treatment-induced damage.

These methodological differences are rarely standardized across studies and are not always reported in sufficient detail to allow direct comparison. Furthermore, each of these design elements can possibly influence survival outcomes. Increased density may elevate mortality through physical contact, competition for resting sites or food access, and altered cage microclimates. Female presence can increase male activity levels and energetic expenditure through courtship and mating, thereby reducing longevity independently of crowding. Nutritional regime and deprivation-based stress assays may exacerbate physiological differences between treated and untreated males, but at the cost of measuring stress tolerance rather than baseline lifespan.

As a consequence, apparent differences in longevity reported across mosquito SIT studies may reflect assay-dependent stressors rather than intrinsic differences in male quality or treatment effects. This heterogeneity complicates the interpretation of irradiation or other stress factor impacts and limits the comparability of QC data across laboratories and operational programs. Without explicit consideration of experimental design, longevity assays risk producing results that are difficult to generalize or translate into operational decision-making.

In this study, we begin to address this issue by testing how basic design choices influence the ability of longevity assays to detect treatment effects relevant to mosquito QC. Using a factorial cage-based design, we evaluate the independent and combined effects of adult holding density and female presence on male longevity, and we quantify how these experimental stressors modify apparent differences between treated (irradiated at high dose) and untreated males. In addition, we assess a rapid “longevity under acute stress” test (no sugar, no water) to determine whether a short-duration test can provide an operationally useful cut-off time for QC screening. Together, these experiments aim to support better standardization and interpretation of longevity-based QC results in mosquito SIT programs.

Materials and Methods

Mosquito Strain and Rearing

Aedes aegypti (L.) (Diptera: Culicidae) originating from the island of La Reunion, France, were reared and maintained at the Insect Pest Control Section of the Joint FAO/IAEA Centre of Nuclear Techniques in Food and Agriculture, Seibersdorf, Austria for the duration of this study, following routine rearing procedures (FAO/IAEA 2017). Males and females (from the same cohort) were sorted based on pupal size with a glass pupal sorter (Focks 1980), counted, and placed in 15 cm3 insect cages (Megaview Science Education Services Co., Ldt., Taiwan) until emergence.

The treatment (Tx) consisted of exposure of male mosquitoes to a high irradiation dose of 90 Gy. This dose was deliberately selected because the objective of the study was not to evaluate an operational SIT dose, but rather to assess the ability of longevity assays to detect treatment-related quality effects. Previous studies have shown that doses commonly used for SIT in Ae. aegypti (approximately 60 Gy), which achieve near-complete sterility, have little or no measurable effect on adult longevity, whereas higher doses can induce detectable reductions in male quality without causing excessive early stage mortality (Somda et al. 2022, Yamada et al. 2022). A dose of 90 Gy has therefore been used in previous studies as a standardized “high-stress” treatment when evaluating the sensitivity of mosquito quality control assays. Control males were handled identically but were not irradiated.

Irradiation was performed at pupal stage (>30 h old) in a Co60 gamma irradiator, Gammacell 220 (Nordion Ltd., Kanata, Ontario, Canada) which had a dose rate of 46.5 Gy/min—44 Gy/min from start to end of the experiments, due to the natural decay of the source. To confirm the dose received by the samples during irradiation, a dosimetry system based on Gafchromic HD-V2 film (Ashland Advanced Materials, Bridgewater, NJ, United States) was used. The films were read 24 h post-exposure using an optical density reader DoseReader4 (RadGen Inc, Budapest, Hungary).

Experiment 1: Longevity Under Standard Holding Conditions

Experimental Design

Longevity tests were conducted in small cages 15 cm3 (volume = 3,375 cm³). Males were held at 3 predefined densities: 0.04, 0.13, and 0.27 mosquitoes/cm2 of resting surface (the smooth plastic side of the cage was not counted as resting surface) achieved by varying the total number of mosquitoes per cage, corresponding to low (30 individuals), medium (90 individuals) and high (180 individuals) densities with reference to the recommended maximum adult stocking density (Density Resting Surface) of 0.9 adult/cm2 in mass rearing conditions (FAO/IAEA 2020). For each density, 2 social environments were established: (i) male-only cages, and (ii) mixed-sex cages with a 1:1 male-to-female ratio.

When females were added, the total number of mosquitoes per cage was maintained, such that overall density remained constant and only sex composition differed. This design allowed the independent evaluation of density effects and social (mating-related) stress. Each density × social environment combination was tested using both irradiated (Tx) and non-irradiated control males, resulting in a fully factorial design. The experiment was conducted using 2 independent biological cohorts derived from different generations of the same laboratory strain. Within each cohort, each treatment combination was replicated 3 times using mosquitoes from the same generation and rearing batch, with each cage treated as an independent experimental unit. The biological repetitions were included to assess repeatability across independently reared cohorts rather than to evaluate generation-to-generation variation. All cages were provided continuous access to a 10% sugar solution. Cages were maintained under identical controlled environmental conditions throughout the experiments. Dead male mosquitoes were removed and counted on weekdays (Monday counts were divided equally to account for Saturdays and Sundays for up to 70 d after emergence, or until all males in a cage were dead). Dead females were removed from the cage but were not counted. Longevity was defined as time to death (days) for individual males. Survival data were used to evaluate the effects of density, social environment (presence of females), high-dose irradiation treatment, and their interactions.

Experiment 2: Longevity Under Acute Stress (Rapid QC Assay)

Experimental Design

To assess a rapid and operationally simple QC assay, a longevity under stress experiment was conducted using adult males from treatment (Tx) and control groups (same as described in Experiment 1). Thirty treated (irradiated) males or untreated males were placed in 15 cm3 cages with no access to sugar or water throughout the assay. Mortality was recorded at 24-h intervals. Observations continued until mortality approached 100% across groups. Based on preliminary inspection of survival patterns, particular attention was given to crucial timepoints (eg, days 2 to 4), during which treatment-related differences were expected to be most pronounced. The experiment was repeated 3 times (3 different cohorts) with 5 technical replicates.

The primary objective of this experiment was not to measure baseline longevity, but to determine whether acute stress exposure could rapidly discriminate between irradiated and control males and to identify a practical time-based cut-off suitable for routine QC.

Statistical Analysis

To evaluate how assay design parameters affected male survival while accounting for the factorial design and replicate structure, Cox mixed-effects proportional hazards models (frailty models) were fitted using the coxme package. Cage identity was included as a random effect to account for non-independence of individuals within cages, and replicate (experimental run) was included as a fixed effect to control for between-run variability.

A series of models was constructed to assess the effects of irradiation (Tx: yes/no), holding density (low, medium, high), and social environment (female presence: yes/no) on male survival. First, a model including all 3 fixed effects was fitted to the full dataset to evaluate their overall contributions to mortality risk.

To further disentangle intrinsic assay effects from treatment-specific responses, additional models were fitted on stratified datasets. The data were subset by social environment, and separate models were fitted for irradiated and control males within male-only cages and mixed-sex cages. This approach allowed the effect of density to be evaluated within each treatment and social context, directly addressing the a priori biological questions motivating the study.

Density was treated as a categorical variable based on observed values (low, medium, high), reflecting slight deviations from target densities across cages. Effect sizes are reported as hazard ratios (HR), derived from model coefficients. Statistical significance was assessed using Wald tests (z statistics).

Model assumptions, including proportional hazards, were evaluated using Schoenfeld residuals. Model robustness was further assessed using bootstrap resampling of log-likelihood estimates, confirming stability and absence of overfitting.

For the acute deprivation assay (no sugar and no water), survival dynamics were similarly analyzed using Kaplan–Meier survival curves (Kaplan and Meier 1958) and Cox proportional hazards models to compare Tx and control males. Because mortality accumulated rapidly and approached saturation within a few days, analyses focused on early mortality dynamics relevant for quality control decision-making.

In addition to survival models, cumulative mortality proportions at predefined observation timepoints (eg, days 2 to 4) were analyzed at the cage level using a Wilcoxon rank-sum test. A priori emphasis was placed on mortality at approximately 72 h (day 3), corresponding to the time window in which treatment-related differences were maximal and before mortality saturation reduced discriminatory power.

To ensure consistency between experimental design, analysis, and interpretation, survival outcomes were evaluated using both full-lifespan monitoring under standard holding conditions and a rapid acute-stress test. Results are presented as survival curves and cumulative mortality plots to illustrate how irradiation effects interact with density and social environment. Figures were structured to directly reflect the factorial design and the a priori questions motivating the study, allowing visual comparison of Treatment (Tx) and control males across densities, cage compositions, and assay types.

Results

Experiment 1: Longevity Under Standard Holding Conditions

Effects of Cage Density on Male Longevity in Male-Only Cages

Observed cage densities closely matched the predefined low-, medium-, and high-density treatments, with minimal variation among replicate cages (<15% of the mean), confirming the stability and reproducibility of the density manipulation across all 36 experimental cages.

Increasing cage density reduced survival in control males, but no significant density effect was detected in irradiated males under male-only conditions. (Fig. 1A–C). Across all densities, irradiated (Tx = 90 Gy) males exhibited significantly shorter survival than control males. The magnitude of the irradiation effect varied modestly across densities, but was statistically significant at all density levels. At low density (0.04 mosquitoes/cm2), irradiated males showed a 2.31-fold higher mortality risk compared with controls (hazard ratio [HR] = 2.31, z = 6.88; 95% CI: 1.82 to 2.93, P < 0.001). At intermediate density (0.13 mosquitoes/cm2), the treatment effect was strongest (HR = 3.15, 95% CI: 2.51 to 3.95, P < 0.001), while at high density (0.27 mosquitoes/cm2) irradiated males again showed significantly reduced survival relative to controls (HR = 2.41, 95% CI: 1.91 to 3.05, P < 0.001).

Although the treatment effect appeared largest at intermediate density based on hazard ratios, formal interaction tests detected no statistically significant treatment-by-density interaction (Tx × density: P = 0.73), indicating that the relative impact of irradiation did not significantly increase or decrease with density.

Effects of Female Presence on Male Longevity

The presence of females (mixed-sex cages, 1:1 male: female ratio) did not significantly affect survival in control males but was associated with increased mortality in irradiated males. (Fig. 1D–F). Within mixed-sex cages, irradiated males consistently exhibited significantly higher mortality than controls at low (HR = 2.45, 95% CI: 1.47 to 4.07, P < 0.001), intermediate (HR = 2.90, 95% CI: 2.22 to 3.81, P < 0.001), and high densities (HR = 3.34, 95% CI: 2.46 to 4.52, P < 0.001).

Despite the visually greater separation between treatment and control curves in mixed-sex cages, statistical analysis detected no significant interaction between irradiation treatment and female presence (Tx × females: P = 0.88). This indicates that female presence does not significantly modify the relative effect of irradiation.

Density and Female Effects Within Treatment Groups

When treatment groups were analyzed separately, no significant effect of density was detected within irradiated males, indicating that irradiation-induced mortality dominated over any potential density-dependent effects under these conditions. In contrast, density effects were evident in control males, confirming that crowding acts as a general assay-related stressor rather than a treatment-specific response.

Similarly, the addition of females was associated with increased mortality in irradiated males, whereas no significant effect of female presence was detected in control males. However, no statistically significant interaction between irradiation and female presence was detected, indicating that female presence does not disproportionately modify the relative effect of irradiation.

Timing of Treatment Effects and Necessity of Full-Lifespan Monitoring

Inspection of Kaplan–Meier survival curves indicated that survival differences between irradiated and control males emerged early in the experiment, with clear divergence evident within the first 2 wk post-emergence across most conditions. These early differences were maintained throughout the lifespan rather than converging over time.

Although survival differences between irradiated and control males were detectable early in the experiment, the survival curves continued to separate beyond the first 2 wk, and late mortality contributed substantially to cumulative differences in longevity. As a result, full-lifespan monitoring provided the most comprehensive assessment of treatment effects, whereas restricting analyses to early time points would underestimate total survival costs and reduce statistical power for detecting treatment-related differences. However, the time and labor required for full-lifespan monitoring limit its practicality for routine operational QC. Consequently, full-lifespan assays may be most appropriate for research, method development, and validation studies, whereas routine QC programs may benefit from standardized rapid screening assays that provide timely operational feedback.

Experiment 2: Longevity Under Acute Stress (Rapid QC Assay)

In the acute stress assay, irradiated males exhibited significantly higher mortality than control males at 72 h post-stress (Wilcoxon rank-sum test: U = 30.5, P < 0.001; Fig. 2). By 96 h, mortality was high in both groups, and no significant difference between treatments was detected (U = 78.5, P = 0.16).

Discussion

In this study, we aimed to address an important gap in knowledge regarding the standardization and implementation of SIT protocols and QC parameters. To show the wide variability in longevity test designs within the broader SIT literature, we compiled key methodological features from selected mosquito studies incorporating a male longevity component (Table 1). This comparison highlights substantial heterogeneity in holding density, cage dimensions, carbohydrate provision, and social environment during longevity assessments. Male numbers per cage range from fewer than 30 individuals in small laboratory containers to several hundred individuals in field-release contexts, with cage volumes varying by more than 2 orders of magnitude. Carbohydrate sources include 5% to 20% sucrose solutions, honey pads, and mixed carbohydrate diets, while some studies assess male-only survival and others include virgin or wild females under mating conditions. In most cases, adult stocking density is not standardized relative to resting surface area, further complicating direct comparison across studies. These differences demonstrate that “longevity” is not a single standardized endpoint but rather an assay outcome shaped by experimental design. Without explicit reporting and harmonization of core parameters such as density and social environment, cross-study comparisons of sterile male survival remain difficult to interpret operationally.

Experimental design characteristics of selected mosquito SIT studies incorporating a male longevity component

Our results demonstrate that mosquito longevity, widely used as a quality control (QC) indicator, is not an intrinsic property of the insect alone but an emergent outcome affected by experimental design. Cage density, social environment (ie, female presence), and monitoring duration each showed clear effects on survival outcomes, while social environment (ie, female presence) imposed conditional effects primarily in irradiated males. These findings highlight the need for careful interpretation of longevity-based QC data and for strict standardization of test conditions.

Density effects reflect baseline stress rather than treatment-specific responses. Across experimental conditions, cage density significantly influenced male longevity, with increased mortality particularly evident in control groups, confirming that crowding constitutes a baseline stressor inherent to cage-based assays, corroborating several historical reports (Chung et al. 2018, Mamai et al. 2018, Hammond et al. 2021, Pudar et al. 2021). In contrast, no statistically significant effect of density was detected within irradiated males, indicating that irradiation-induced mortality can mask density-dependent effects. Critically, no irradiation × density interaction was detected, demonstrating that density alters absolute survival values but does not modify the relative magnitude of irradiation-associated mortality. From a QC perspective, this supports the interpretation that density affects baseline assay conditions without fundamentally altering treatment detectability.

Female presence causes conditional survival costs. By maintaining constant overall density while manipulating sex composition, the study separated crowding from social stress. Female presence was associated with increased mortality within irradiated males, whereas no statistically detectable effect was observed within controls. This suggests that social or mating-related interactions may impose additional survival costs primarily in males already affected by irradiation. However, the absence of a significant irradiation × female interaction indicates that female presence did not disproportionately amplify irradiation-associated mortality relative to controls. Thus, mixed-sex conditions alter absolute survival outcomes but do not fundamentally modify the relative treatment effect.

Combined stress conditions represent extreme but not treatment-specific scenarios. The lowest absolute survival values were observed under combined high-density and mixed-sex conditions, reflecting the additive effects of crowding and social stress. Under these conditions, irradiated males consistently showed reduced survival relative to controls, as observed across all experimental settings. However, because interaction effects were not statistically significant, these combined stress conditions should be interpreted as extreme assay environments rather than as scenarios that uniquely amplify or cloud irradiation effects. While such conditions may be useful for stress-testing overall male robustness, they are unlikely to reflect routine operational or field-relevant environments and may underestimate baseline male performance.

Timing and monitoring duration. Differences between irradiated and control males were detectable early in the lifespan and persisted over time. Although early detection is feasible, full-lifespan monitoring provides a more comprehensive estimate of cumulative mortality differences and improves precision in estimating overall survival impacts. For QC applications, this distinction is important: early divergence supports screening use, whereas full monitoring supports magnitude estimation.

Although no significant differences were observed between the 2 biological repetitions, the present study was not designed to formally assess variation across consecutive generations. Longevity is known to be influenced by numerous biological and operational factors, including larval nutrition, rearing density, environmental conditions, handling procedures, and treatment protocols. Consequently, the long-term robustness of longevity-based QC assays will depend not only on assay standardization, but also on maintaining consistency in the underlying rearing and production processes. Future studies evaluating assay performance across multiple generations and production cycles would provide additional information on the reproducibility and operational stability of longevity-based QC measurements.

Rapid Stress Assays Are Quick But Have Limited Resolution

The acute stress test (no sugar, no water) produced rapid mortality in both irradiated and control males, with most deaths occurring within 3 to 4 d. Under these conditions, treatment-related differences were detectable at an early time point (72 h), when variability among replicates remained high and mortality had not yet saturated. By contrast, no significant difference between treatments was detectable at 96 h, when mortality approached ceiling levels in both groups.

These results demonstrate that rapid stress tests primarily measure acute stress tolerance rather than baseline longevity. Their discriminatory power is highly dependent on the timing of assessment and diminishes once mortality becomes widespread. Consequently, while such tests offer logistical advantages and rapid turnaround, they cannot substitute for full-lifespan assays when cumulative or delayed treatment effects are of interest.

Within the context of SIT quality control, the rapid stress assay applied here was purposefully designed as an operational screening tool for a single, predefined stressor (ie, exposure to a high irradiation dose) and the 72-h assessment window proved appropriate for detecting irradiation-related survival impairment. However, SIT QC guidelines emphasize that quality indicators must be validated for their intended use, and the present findings indicate that the optimal cut-off for rapid assays cannot be assumed to be transferable across different sources of stress. Factors such as handling, chilling, transport, nutritional status, or rearing conditions are likely to manifest mortality effects at different rates and magnitudes, meaning that a fixed observation window may under- or overestimate their impact. As a result, the routine application of rapid stress assays in SIT programs requires prior calibration and validation for each factor to be monitored, including confirmation that the selected observation period is sufficiently sensitive and operationally relevant. When appropriately calibrated, rapid assays can provide robust, cost-effective, and time-efficient tools for routine monitoring of specific process steps, but they should not be interpreted as universal proxies of overall insect quality.

Overall, these results indicate that acute stress assays are sensitive to early differences in stress tolerance but are strongly dependent on the timing of assessment and can lose discriminatory power once mortality becomes high. Full-lifespan assays, while more time- and labor-intensive, provide a more comprehensive and stable assessment of treatment effects on male survival. Reliance on rapid stress assays alone may therefore underestimate or miss longer-term survival costs, whereas their use alongside full-lifespan monitoring can help identify early vulnerability to stress. However, rapid stress-based assays can serve as a practical screening tool for routine QC, provided that observation time points are carefully standardized.

Collectively, our results demonstrate that longevity-based QC assays are highly sensitive to experimental design and therefore require explicit standardization to ensure meaningful interpretation and comparability. Based on the findings of this study, we recommend that QC protocols: (i) explicitly define and report cage density and cage volume; (ii) standardize sex composition and avoid pooling male-only and mixed-sex longevity data. For routine operational QC, male-only cages are recommended because they minimize biological variability and simplify assay implementation (Zhang et al. 2024); (iii) match assay design to purpose, distinguishing baseline longevity measurements from stress-tolerance testing; and (iv) use rapid stress tests as standardized screening tools with predefined assessment time points rather than as substitutes for full-lifespan monitoring or overall quality assessments.

Longevity remains a valuable tool for evaluating mosquito production processes and treatment effects, but its interpretation depends critically on experimental design. Unlike flight ability tests, which provide a rapid assessment of physical integrity and immediate functional performance, longevity assays evaluate the cumulative physiological consequences of rearing, handling, and treatment processes over time. The 2 approaches therefore assess different components of insect quality and should be viewed as complementary rather than interchangeable. Full-lifespan assays provide comprehensive information on survival costs and remain valuable for research, method development, validation of new procedures, and identification of delayed treatment effects. However, their duration and labor requirements limit their suitability for routine operational quality control.

In contrast, standardized rapid stress assays may offer greater practical utility for routine QC in mass-rearing facilities, similar to the flight ability test used in mosquito SIT programs and stress-based quality assessments used in other SIT systems. When appropriately calibrated for a specific process or treatment, such assays can provide rapid, reproducible, and operationally relevant indicators of insect quality while minimizing time and resource requirements. Importantly, the present study was not designed to establish operational pass/fail criteria analogous to those used in the Mediterranean fruit fly VIENNA genetic sexing strain, where predefined survival thresholds support routine production monitoring. Only a single treatment factor (irradiation) was evaluated, and survival outcomes were not linked to downstream performance indicators.

Nevertheless, our results demonstrate that rapid stress assays can discriminate treatment-related quality differences when assessed within an appropriately calibrated observation window. Future studies should therefore evaluate a broader range of stressors and quality levels and determine whether survival-based thresholds can be linked to established quality indicators such as flight ability, mating competitiveness, survival after handling and transport, or field persistence. Such work would help establish whether rapid longevity-based assays can become standardized operational QC tools for mosquito SIT programmes.
