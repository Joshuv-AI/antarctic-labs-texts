Effects of a TUG-based graded multi-component exercise program for reversing frailty in community-dwelling older adults: a multi-center randomized controlled trial
Authors: Jin Yan, Bingjie Lu, Cheng Qin, Zhihui Zhu, Ning Di, Cunmei Yang, Qiuli Xu, Xiao Bai, Jian Liu, Rongxia Chen, Chaobo Wang, Zheng Liu, Li Fan, Yixin Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFrailty is a prevalent yet reversible condition in older adults. While exercise is a cornerstone intervention, the optimal protocols tailored to varying functional levels remain underdefined. This study aimed to evaluate the feasibility and effectiveness of a Timed Up and Go (TUG)-based graded multi-component exercise (GME) program in reversing frailty among community-dwelling older adults.MethodsThis study was a multi-center, parallel-group, superiority randomized controlled trial conducted in community settings in Beijing, China. Participants were aged ≥ 60 years and diagnosed with pre-frailty or frailty according to the Fried phenotype. Participants were randomly assigned (1:1) via a centralized computer-generated randomization tool (random.org) to either the GME group or the Medical Health Education (MHE) group. The GME group received a 5-month, twice-weekly exercise program (aerobic, resistance, balance, flexibility) graded by TUG performance (Levels A, B, C). The MHE comparator group received weekly health education. The primary outcome was the Fried frailty score. Data collectors and statistical analysts were blinded to group assignment, while participants and exercise instructors were not.ResultsAmong 111 participants (GME: n = 66, MHE: n = 45), the GME group showed superior reduction in Fried frailty scores (P = 0.008), with 21.2% reversing to a Robust state. Improvements were significant for pre-frail individuals (P = 0.016) and TUG Levels B/C, but not for the frail subgroup or Level A. GME participants also achieved better walking speed (P_adj = 0.006) and PHQ-9 scores (P_adj < 0.001). Significant within-group gains occurred in grip strength, TUG, and blood pressure (P_adj < 0.05). However, no significant inter-group differences were found after FDR adjustment for FTSTT, SPPB, BMI, SMI, muscle mass, calf circumference, WHR, VFA, BADL, IADL, MNA-SF, GAD-7, PFS, and MoCA. No adverse events were reported.ConclusionThe TUG-based graded multi-component exercise program is a safe and effective strategy for reversing frailty in community-dwelling older adults. It significantly enhances physical function, optimizes body composition and improves mood.Trial registrationChinese Clinical Trial Registry, ChiCTR2400081175. Registered 26 February 2024. Significance statement

As a systematic frailty interventions in Asian older adults, this study delivers translatable evidence to combat population aging and informs precision geriatrics and public health policy.

Introduction

Rapid ageing across global societies has given rise to an escalating focus on frailty. Frailty is a clinical syndrome characterized by increased vulnerability to adverse health outcome such as poor physical health, falls, hospitalization, disability, and mortality [1], due to the functional decline and diminished reserve capacity that accompany the ageing process [2]. The global prevalence of frailty varies significantly, ranging from 4.9% to 65.2%, and is heavily influenced by geographic, clinical, and socioeconomic factors [3]. In Chinese community-dwelling adults aged 60 and above, the prevalence of frailty is approximately 7.0%, while the prevalence of pre-frailty ranges from 49.3% to 53.3% [4].

Nevertheless, evidence suggests that frailty is a dynamic process and can potentially be delayed or reversed [5], the main tools to improve it are augmented exercise training, optimal nutritional structure, and psycho-social support [6].Exercise interventions are highly prevalent for their wide applicability, cost-effectiveness, and minimal side effects.The latest guideline, International Clinical Practice Guidelines: Recognition and Management of Physical Frailty, developed by the International Conference on Frailty and Sarcopenia Research (ICFSR) in 2019, also points to physical activity as the most viable method of preventing and treating frailty.

Multi-component exercises are combinations of two or more forms of exercise that include resistance, aerobic, balance and flexibility, and is now recognized as the best option for enhancing functional capacity in older patients [7].However, frail older adults always exhibit extreme heterogeneity in their baseline functional capacities and physical resilience. Therefore, exercise interventions need to be adapted and customized to the individual. Graded exercise interventions, which stratify training into diverse levels based on an individual’s physical circumstances and incrementally escalate the intensity and complexity of the regimen, offer an optimal solution. This approach yields greater clinical benefits while mitigating the risk of adverse events associated with inappropriate training loads, facilitating a safer and more sustainable functional recovery.

Consensus statement of Chinese experts on exercise (2023) encourages older adults to engage in a comprehensive exercise regimen that includes aerobic exercise, resistance training, balance exercises, and flexibility training, with at least two sessions per week [8]. The ACSM/AHA guidelines on physical activity and exercise for older adults recommend that older adults engage in at least two sessions per week of aerobic training, resistance training, and flexibility exercises, though specific recommendations for the frequency and intensity of balance training are not provided [9]. However, these recommendations are principally directed at the general elderly population rather than specifically catering to frail individuals. Moreover, they do not encompass graded physical function evaluations. The International Conference on Frailty and Sarcopenia Research (ICFSR) has declared that there is a lack of adequate evidence to ascertain the most suitable exercise frequency, intensity, duration, and type for the treatment and management of frailty. [10] While programs like VIVIFRAIL provide a robust framework, the complexity of SPPB-based assessments can limit efficiency for large-scale screening in busy community settings [11]. The TUG test is a highly practical and comprehensive alternative widely used in primary care; it uniquely integrates multiple functional domains—including dynamic balance, gait speed, and functional mobility—into a single, time-efficient task. Yet to our knowledge, no prior intervention studies have specifically utilized TUG performance as a stratification tool for exercise prescriptions [12].

Therefore, this study represents an initial attempt and exploration to design and evaluate a TUG-based graded multi-component exercise intervention for pre-frail and frail older adults By systematically evaluating its feasibility and clinical efficacy, our findings will provide robust evidence for developing safe, scientific, and precise exercise prescriptions, thereby advancing the health management of frailty.

Methods

Study design and trial registration

This study was a multi-center, randomized controlled trial (RCT) conducted across several communities in Beijing from March 1, 2023, to December 31, 2023. The study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Chinese PLA General Hospital (Ethical Approval Number: S2022-560-02). Trial registration was retrospectively completed with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2400081175, Registered: 26 February 2024). The retrospective registration was primarily due to COVID-19-related administrative delays in the issuance of the prerequisite ethical approval documents.

This study followed the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) reporting guideline. Informed consent was obtained from each participant after they fully understood the trial procedures.

Participants and grouping

Participants were recruited from fixed communities in Beijing, selected based on pre-existing collaborative long-term partnerships with local community health centers, which facilitated efficient cohort management, streamlined access to annual health check-up records, and enhanced participant adherence throughout the intervention.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion and exclusion criteria are as follows: Inclusion Criteria: (1)Aged 60 years or older; (2)Diagnosed with pre-frailty or frailty according to the Fried criteria; (3)Voluntarily consented to participate in the study and signed the informed consent form while fully alert and aware. Exclusion Criteria: (1) Presence of severe acute or chronic medical conditions that, in the clinical judgment of the investigators, would preclude safe participation in the exercise intervention or severely compromise the validity of the functional assessments (e.g., long-term bedridden status, profound stroke sequelae, advanced Parkinson’s disease with significant motor impairment, or severe osteoarthritis). (2)Inability to communicate effectively with others due to disease or poor mental conditions, including severe cognitive decline, dementia, profound hearing or speaking loss, psychiatric disorders, etc.; (3)End-stage disease with a life expectancy of less than one year; (4)Contraindications for participation in exercise interventions identified during exercise risk assessment; (5)Participation in other clinical trials that may interfere with the results of this study: (6) refusal by the participant to take part in the trial. .

Blinding and randomization

Randomization and Allocation Concealment An independent researcher who was not involved in participant recruitment or clinical assessment generated the random allocation sequence using a computerized random number generator (random.org). Participants were randomly assigned at a 1:1 ratio to either the GME group or the MHE group using simple randomization. To ensure allocation concealment, the allocation sequence was placed in sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes and was not accessible to the personnel enrolling participants until the moment of group assignment.

Blinding Due to the nature of the exercise intervention, it was not possible to blind the participants or the exercise instructors to the group assignments. However, to minimize detection bias, outcome assessors and statistical analysts were strictly blinded to group allocation. Blinding of outcome assessors was maintained by ensuring they were not present during intervention sessions and by instructing participants not to reveal their group assignment during follow-up assessments.

Sample size calculation

The sample size was calculated based on the between-group difference in frailty improvement rates. Based on a previous pilot study, the anticipated improvement rate in the exercise group was 64% (P1 = 0.64) [13]. Considering the natural regression of frailty (23.3% to 35.2%) and the active health education provided, the control group’s improvement rate was conservatively estimated at 30% (P2 = 0.30) [14]. PASS software was used for a two-independent-proportions test (two-sided α = 0.05, power = 1-β = 0.90, 1:1 allocation ratio), 44 participants per group were required. Accounting for a 10% attrition rate, the final target sample size was 98 (49 per group). Intra-group comparisons were treated as secondary analyses and excluded from this estimation.

Data collection and assessments

Baseline assessments

The face-to-face survey interviews conducted with the research participants, with the interviewers unaware of the groupings, mainly involve the following content: (1) Sociodemographic characteristics: Age, gender, ethnicity, marital status, educational level, caregiving status, monthly income, etc. (2) General condition: Height, weight, Blood Pressure: Systolic Blood Pressure (SBP), Diastolic Blood Pressure (DBP), Resting Heart Rate (HR). (3) Lifestyle habits : Smoking and alcohol consumption in the past year, exercise habits. (4) Comorbid chronic diseases and medication: Comorbid chronic diseases such as hypertension, coronary heart disease, type 2 diabetes, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, Parkinson’s disease, chronic liver disease, chronic kidney disease, cancer, osteoporosis, osteoarthritis, etc., and hospitalization status in the past year. Polypharmacy was defined as using ≥ 5 medications [15]. (5) Exercise Risk Assessment: Reconfirm current activity level and medical history, apply the Exercise and Screening for You (EASY) [16]questionnaire for risk screening. A qualified physician will inquire about symptoms or signs of cardiovascular, metabolic, or kidney diseases and perform physical examination. (6)Blood examination: Complete the assessment and conduct the blood test on the same day, then send the samples to a unified testing center for examination.

Primary outcome: frailty status and adverse events

Frailty Status was evaluated using the five-item Fried Frailty Phenotype (unintentional weight loss, self-reported exhaustion, low grip strength, slow gait speed, and low physical activity). Participants were classified as robust (0), pre-frail (1–2), or frail (3–5) [17].

Adverse events included falls, hospitalizations, and all-cause mortality.

Secondary outcomes: physical function and body composition

Physical function assessments included the Timed Up and Go (TUG) test, where participants rose from a chair, walked 3 m, and returned; to ensure accuracy, this was performed twice, and the fastest time was recorded for analysis. Grip strength was measured using a calibrated digital hand dynamometer (JAMAR Plus+, USA) with the participant seated and the elbow flexed at 90 degrees; three consecutive trials were performed with the dominant hand (incorporating a 1-minute rest interval), and the maximum value was recorded. Additional physical tests included the Short Physical Performance Battery (SPPB)—a composite evaluation of balance, a 4-meter gait speed, and the Five Times Sit-To-Stand Test (FTSTT)—as well as the Back-scratch tests for body flexibility, respectively.

Body composition was quantitatively evaluated using a multi-frequency bioelectrical impedance analyzer (InBody 770; InBody Co., Ltd., Seoul, Korea), which underwent calibration according to standardized procedures before use to ensure measurement accuracy. All participants were tested in the morning under fasting conditions and after voiding their bladders. Participants removed all metallic accessories and stood barefoot on the device’s tactile electrode platform, remained stationary with arms extended and abducted at approximately 15 degrees from the torso. A set of parameters was extracted for analysis, including indicators of adiposity and metabolic load (Body Mass Index [BMI], body fat percentage, visceral fat area [VFA], and waist-to-hip ratio), as well as skeletal muscle distribution (Skeletal Muscle Index [SMI], upper limb muscle mass, lower limb muscle mass, and trunk muscle mass). Besides, flexible tape was used to measure the calf circumference.

Other outcomes

To comprehensively evaluate the participants’ overall health and functional status, a standardized Comprehensive Geriatric Assessment (CGA) was conducted, encompassing the following common geriatric domains: Basic and instrumental activities of daily living (BADL and IADL) were evaluated using the Barthel Index and the Lawton IADL Scale, respectively. For both scales, higher scores reflect greater functional independence [18]. Nutritional Status: Evaluated using the Mini Nutritional Assessment Short Form (MNA-SF), with higher scores denoting better nutritional health [19]. Anxiety and depressive symptoms were measured using the Generalized Anxiety Disorder 7-Item Scale (GAD-7) [20] and the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) [21], respectively. For both instruments, higher scores indicate greater symptom severity. Subjective physical and mental fatigue were quantified utilizing the Pittsburgh Fatigue Scale (PFS-Physical and PFS-Mental), where higher scores signify more severe fatigue [22]. Cognitive Function was assessed using the Beijing version of the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA), with higher scores corresponding to better cognitive performance [23].

Interventions

GME ( Graded Multi-component Exercise )group

The intervention lasted for five months, with training sessions held twice a week in the community activity centers of each unit. The GME program consisted of 40-minute supervised sessions including six phases: warm-up, aerobic training, resistance training, balance training, flexibility, and relaxation. Exercise intensity and progression were tailored to each participant’s functional tier, stratified by baseline TUG performance: Level A (unable to stand), Level B (TUG > 15s), and Level C (TUG ≤ 15s). Aerobic training targeted 50–75% HRmax, while resistance training ranged from 20% to 60% of one-repetition maximum (1RM) across levels. Progression was systematically built into the protocol, escalating difficulty and load as functional capacity improved. The exhaustive itemization of the exercise protocol, including construction, specific modalities, precise grading loads, and progression criteria for each level, is provided in Supplementary Material 1 ( including Tables S1–S3).

Blood pressure, heart rate, and oxygen saturation were monitored before, during, and after the exercise. During the intervention process, a team consisting of at least one clinical medicine professional, one rehabilitation therapy professional, and one physical education teaching professional was formed to jointly guide the intervention process. The ratio of clinical medical staff with first aid qualifications to the trainees on site throughout the training was not lower than 1:3. Participants also received medical health education once a week the same as MHE group.

MHE (Medical Health Education )group

Participants received the online medical health education once a week. Each 40-minute session was structured into a 30-minute lecture followed by a 10-minute interactive Q&A period. The curriculum was delivered by a multidisciplinary team comprising licensed physicians, exercise physiologists, registered dietitians, and rehabilitation therapists. The educational content covered nine essential themes: (1) definitions and adverse consequences of frailty; (2) the association between frailty and geriatric chronic diseases such as hypertension and osteoporosis; (3) etiologies of geriatric knee pain; (4) physiological benefits of exercise training; (5) the importance of preserving muscle mass; (6) recommended exercises and safety precautions for seniors; (7) respiratory regulation techniques during activity; (8) post-exercise relaxation and recovery; and (9) evidence-based dietary guidelines and nutritional considerations for older adults.

Quality control and adherence management

(1)The exercise program was reviewed by more than five experts. After incorporating feedback and making revisions, the program was reassessed until it was confirmed; (2)All research staff involved in the study underwent uniform and rigorous training and assessments. Researchers responsible for questionnaire surveys and physical function assessments were required to thoroughly understand the questionnaire content, familiarize themselves with the proper administration of scales, and demonstrate effective communication skills and attention to detail during assessments; (3)The researchers responsible for data collection were blinded to the group assignments; (4)Personnel supervising and guiding the exercise interventions underwent standardized training to ensure consistency in exercise instruction and guidance, thereby maintaining the homogeneity of the interventions across different units; (5)Once the classification of participants was determined, it was not altered, and participants were strictly assigned to exercise targets within their designated group ranges; (6)Data were recorded in a timely, efficient, and accurate manner. Prior to data processing and analysis, repeated checks and corrections were performed to verify the accuracy and authenticity of the data, and random spot checks were conducted multiple times; (7)This ensures that the study maintains high standards of quality control throughout its design and implementation.

The graded management allowed the elderly with different physical functions to have more suitable exercise regimens and goals. Telephone inquiries and reminders were carried out prior to each intervention session, and attendance is recorded during the intervention. Moreover, appropriate incentives were provided for the participants of the clinical trial, such as the freely formation of groups, honor roll establishment, and photo wall creation. They were encouraged to actively engage and adhere to the research protocol. Good communication is maintained to offer clear information and guidance, thereby enhancing their understanding and Adherence.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed by SPSS (version 27.0) and R (version 4.2.3) software. Data normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Continuous variables were expressed as mean±standard deviation (X ± SD) or medians with interquartile ranges[M (P25, P75)], as appropriate. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages (n, %) and compared using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test.

Primary analyses were conducted on participants who completed the intervention (completer analysis). For continuous secondary outcomes, Analysis of Covariance (ANCOVA) was employed for inter-group comparisons, using baseline values as covariates to calculate adjusted mean differences and effect sizes. Within-group longitudinal changes were assessed via paired t-tests or Wilcoxon signed-rank tests. To control for Type I error, the Benjamini-Hochberg (FDR) procedure was applied to the exploratory comparisons. Pre-defined analyses were performed using linear regression. All tests were two-sided with significance set at P < 0.05.

To further evaluate the clinical significance of the intervention, a qualitative analysis of frailty status (Robust, Pre-frail, and Frail) was performed. At baseline, there were no significant differences in the distribution of frailty categories between the GME and MHE groups (P = 0.553). Following the 5-month intervention, the GME group exhibited a significant improvement in frailty status (P < 0.001). Notably, 21.2% (n = 14) of participants in the GME group transitioned to the “Robust” state, while the proportion of pre-frailty decreased from 80.3% to 56.1%. In contrast, no significant longitudinal change was observed in the MHE group (P = 0.114), where the proportion of frail individuals actually increased from 24.4% to 37.8%. Inter-group comparison at post-intervention revealed a statistically significant difference in the distribution of frailty status (P = 0.013) (Table S4, Fig. 3).

In Grade B, the score of the GME group was 0.83 points lower than that of the MHE group (P = 0.028), and in Grade C, the score of the GME group was 0.70 points lower than that of the MHE group (P = 0.017). However, the difference among the three subgroups was not statistically significant (P = 0.136). When stratified by 90 years old (a value close to the average age in this study) and gender, the differences between subgroups were not significant.

Secondary outcomes: physical function and body composition

At baseline, no significant differences were observed between the MHE and GME groups across most physical function and body composition indicators, confirming successful randomization. Following the 5-month intervention, ANCOVA adjusting for baseline values (Table S5) revealed that the GME group achieved a significantly higher walking speed compared to the MHE group (FDR-adjusted P = 0.006, #). While nominal inter-group differences were initially observed in grip strength (P = 0.020) and VFA (P = 0.030), these did not retain statistical significance after Benjamini-Hochberg (FDR) adjustment within their respective domains. No other significant differences were found for the remaining functional tests (TUG, FTSTT, SPPB) or body composition parameters (BMI, SMI, and muscle mass) after multiple testing correction.

For physical function and body composition changes (Table S6), the GME group showed significant within-group improvements in walking speed (P < 0.001), grip strength (P < 0.001), TUG test performance (0.93 ± 2.32, P = 0.005), back scratch test results (P < 0.001), and VFA (19.81 ± 33.18, P = 0.001). After Benjamini-Hochberg (FDR) adjustment within their respective domain, these indicators remained statistically significant (P_adj < 0.05, #). Conversely, nominal improvements in the GME group’s waist-to-hip ratio (P = 0.030) and calf circumference (P = 0.032) did not maintain significance after FDR correction (P_adj > 0.05), and no significant longitudinal changes were observed in BMI, SMI, or limb muscle mass.

Other secondary outcomes: geriatric assessment scales

Baseline comparisons revealed no significant differences in physiological parameters or CGA scales between the MHE and GME groups, indicating well-balanced initial status. Following the 5-month intervention, ANCOVA results (Table S7) showed that the GME group achieved a significantly lower PHQ-9 score compared to the MHE group (FDR-adjusted P = 0.006, #). Although a nominal inter-group difference was observed in MoCA scores (P = 0.037), it did not remain statistically significant after Benjamini-Hochberg (FDR) adjustment across the 11 indicators (P_adj > 0.05). No other significant inter-group differences were found for blood pressure, heart rate, or other CGA dimensions—including functional independence (BADL, IADL), nutritional status (MNA-SF), anxiety (GAD-7), and fatigue levels—after multiple testing correction.

Regarding the 5-month changes in CGA outcomes (Table S8), the GME group demonstrated significant within-group improvements in PHQ-9 scores (1.88 ± 3.41, P < 0.001), SBP (6.18 ± 17.07, P = 0.010), and DBP (P = 0.010), all of which remained statistically significant after Benjamini-Hochberg (FDR) adjustment across 11 indicators (P_adj < 0.05, #) .

In contrast, while the MHE group showed a nominal decline in MoCA scores (P = 0.010) and the inter-group difference for MoCA reached nominal significance (P = 0.037), neither remained significant after FDR adjustment (P_adj > 0.05). No other significant differences were observed in functional status, nutrition, anxiety, or fatigue levels after multiple testing correction.

Discussion

This study developed a graded multi-component exercise program for pre-frail and frail elderly individuals and validated its effectiveness in community-dwelling older adults. The results showed that the program not only improved frailty status but also had positive effects to varying degrees on physical function, body composition, blood pressure and mood. This study developed the program based on the physical functional status of the population and implemented it, confirming its effectiveness and value from multiple assessment dimensions, providing a reference for health management of frail populations.

At the end of the 5-month intervention, the Fried frailty score of the GME group was 0.49 points lower than that of the MHE group, and it decreased by 0.35 points compared to the baseline frailty score, which directly confirming the effectiveness of this program in improving frailty. This result is consistent with the conclusions of other similar studies [24].Exercise is a significant challenge to overall homeostasis but also a major opportunity to reshape pathways and improve bodily functions. In addition, we conducted a subgroup analysis of frailty scores after the intervention. Subgroup analysis showed that the GME group had a 0.53-point lower frailty score than the MHE group in the pre-frailty subgroup However, no such difference was found in the frailty subgroup. Pre-frailty is a transitional state between robust and frail older adults, with overall health levels better than the frailty group, and a better response to exercise in terms of physical function. The potential for improvement and remodeling of physical function is greater, and the possibility of frailty reversal is higher.Patients already in a frail state may face more physical limitations and challenges, which could reduce their adherence to exercise interventions.Other studies abroad have also conducted exercise interventions specifically targeting pre-frail older adults, which improved their frailty levels, consistent with the conclusions of this study [25]. A subgroup analysis based on TUG levels showed that the GME group had higher frailty scores than the MHE group: 0.83 points higher in level B and 0.70 points higher in level C, however, there was no significant difference in frailty scores between the GME and MHE groups in level A. On one hand, the exercise intensity in level A is relatively low, and most exercises are performed while seated. The program itself is designed for individuals who cannot engage in lower limb movements, resulting in lower completion rates. On the other hand, due to their weaker physical function, the body’s adaptability decreases, and the response to exercise diminishes, leading to less effective results compared to the B and C groups. This finding is consistent with the conclusions drawn from the frailty subgroup analysis.

Decline in physical function is an important manifestation of frailty. A comparative analysis of physical function shows that the GME group had improvements in walking speed and grip strength after intervention. Walking speed is easy to measure and serves as a sensitive early indicator for identifying physical decline in elderly individuals. The increase in grip strength indicates improved muscle function. Both of these are important items in the Fried frailty diagnostic criteria. Many studies have confirmed the beneficial effects of exercise intervention on walking speed improvement [26]. Resistance training promotes muscle adaptation, helping the body adjust to increased loads, making muscles stronger and more endurance-capable.TUG test was used as a grading standard for exercise intensity in this study and is also an important indicator of intervention effectiveness. It can reflect various aspects of elderly physical function, including lower limb strength, walking speed, balance, and neuro-muscular response. According to the grading scheme, our multi-component exercise program targeted training across these various dimensions. The GME group showed a reduction in TUG time, which is consistent with the findings of other similar studies [27]. Flexibility is an important aspect of physical function in the elderly. Flexibility training can reduce muscle and joint stiffness, improve joint range of motion, and lower the risk of injuries such as strains and sprains during exercise. The GME group showed a 5 cm improvement in the back stretch test, and the seated forward bend test was better than that of the MHE group. Studies have shown that flexibility training in older adults can improve gait stability and balance [28], reduce the risk of falls, and alleviate muscle soreness caused by resistance training. Four months of exercise training can increase knee flexor/extensor strength and flexibility [29], which is consistent with the findings of this study. SPPB is a more comprehensive indicator of physical function. It is also an important screening tool for elderly health [30].This study showed that the GME group had better SPPB results than the MHE group, which aligns with conclusions from other international studies [26].

The GME group showed a reduction of 19.81 cm² in VFA after the intervention. VFA refers to the total area of fat surrounding the internal organs, and high VFA is associated with various adverse conditions and events [31].Exercise increases energy expenditure, promotes fat burning, and helps regulate the secretion of hormones closely related to fat metabolism, such as insulin and cortisol. While reducing fat, it is important to maintain skeletal muscle mass, especially in individuals with sarcopenic obesity. Resistance training is a primary method to preserve skeletal muscle mass and muscle function [32].Some studies suggest that a higher BMI may have a protective effect for older adults [33], however, since BMI calculation is based only on weight and height, it cannot distinguish between lean muscle mass and fat content. Therefore, it is often necessary to consider other body composition data, such as disease status and muscle mass, when evaluating it. Other body composition data, such as muscle mass and skeletal muscle index, did not show significant improvement, which may be related to the short duration of the intervention.

Post-intervention, the GME group exhibited significant reductions in SBP and DBP. These improvements likely stem from enhanced endothelial function (e.g., increased nitric oxide production), reduced sympathetic activity, and improved cardiac efficiency, which collectively lower systemic vascular resistance and cardiac burden. These findings are consistent with previous literature, notably occurring without significant changes in resting heart rate. [34].

Besides, the GME group exhibited a significant reduction in PHQ-9 scores, markedly outperforming the MHE group. This improvement likely stems from the synergistic effects of increased social interaction, enhanced self-efficacy, and the modulation of mood-regulating neurotransmitters. These findings align with evidence suggesting that exercise can be as effective as pharmacotherapy for alleviating depression in older adults [35],further supporting its recommendation for individuals with depressive symptoms [36]. Ultimately, this multi-component intervention provides robust evidence for utilizing exercise prescriptions to manage both frailty and depression in the elderly.

We analyzed the proportion of patients in both groups who experienced falls, hospitalizations, and deaths after the intervention, and found no statistical differences. This may be related to the short follow-up period [37].We plan to continue managing and following up with this cohort in the future to explore the long-term impact of this intervention on the incidence of adverse events.

Our study has several strengths including its multi-center randomized design. First, We developed and implemented a multi-component exercise intervention program for pre-frailty and frail older adults, providing a practical reference for clinical management. Besides, frailty is a systemic condition. In addition to frailty indicators, we conducted a comprehensive assessment that included physical function, body composition, blood pressure and resting heart rate, ability to perform daily activities, nutrition, emotional status (anxiety, depression), subjective fatigue (physical and mental), cognitive function, and adverse events. We explored the effects of exercise intervention on various frailty-related conditions, which may offer new insights and considerations for frailty management and its potential underlying mechanisms.

This study also has several limitations. First, the study participants were all from urban communities that maintain long-term collaborative partnerships with our research centers, characterized by potentially higher socioeconomic status and better health resource accessibility compared to rural settings, and the sample size was relatively small. Therefore, the conclusions may not be generalizable to all pre-frail and frail older adults. However, the study still provides valuable evidence and new directions, and future research will include populations from different economic backgrounds and regions, with an expanded sample size for further exploration and validation. Second, the study had a relatively high dropout rate. Despite measures to improve compliance, such as telephone follow-ups, reminders, reward lists, and photo walls, both groups still experienced considerable attrition. To accurately evaluate the true physiological and clinical benefits of the intervention when adhered to, we adopted a completer analysis. The elderly individuals who completed the intervention and follow-up showed significant improvements, demonstrating that this program still holds substantial reference value and clinical significance. In the future, we will actively explore more effective methods to enhance compliance by focusing on psychological factors, physical function, and social support, thereby improving the reliability and generalizability of the study outcomes. Third, the study did not include long-term follow-up. Due to time constraints, only post-intervention assessments were conducted, and no statistically significant differences in the incidence of falls, hospitalizations, or mortality were observed. Future research will continue with long-term follow-up to further explore and validate the long-term effects of this intervention on pre-frail and frail older adults.

Older adults living with pre-frailty and frailty is a special group that is often accompanied by varying degrees of chronic diseases, as well as potential cognitive decline, depression, and other conditions. Early health screening and assessment of intrinsic capacity (especially in the domain of physical function), along with tailored interventions for mobility limitations in older adults, are essential [38]. Exercise is an effective means to improve frailty. The graded multi-component exercise program developed and implemented in this study represents a successful exploration for older adults living with pre-frailty and frailty in communities, providing research evidence for frailty management.

Conclusion

The five-month graded multi-component exercise program effectively ameliorated the frailty status of older adults, particularly those in the pre-frail stage. The intervention yielded beneficial effects on physical function, body composition, blood pressure, and mood.Early frailty screening and exercise management for older adults are essential. Such measures not only help enhance their physical fitness and quality of life but also contribute to healthy aging and reduce the burden on healthcare systems.
