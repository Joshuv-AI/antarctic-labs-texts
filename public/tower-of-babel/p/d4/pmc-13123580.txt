Adjunctive Eptifibatide Administration in ACS Patients Undergoing Percutaneous Coronary Intervention: A Randomized Comparison of Bolus-only Versus Standard Therapy
Authors: Fatemeh Baharvand, Maedeh Dadras, Arsalan Salari, Shiva Parvaneh, Asieh Ashouri, Fatemeh Mirrazeghi, Amir Aris, Yaser Jenab
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGuidelines do not recommend routine glycoprotein IIb/IIIa inhibitors (GPIs), reserving them for high thrombotic-risk or bailout cases. Most acute coronary syndrome (ACS) patients undergoing percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) are already on dual antiplatelet therapy (aspirin plus clopidogrel). GPIs thus act as adjunctive therapy, providing short-term platelet inhibition. The optimal strategy, bolus-only versus bolus-plus-infusion, remains uncertain, especially in patients at higher bleeding risk.ObjectiveTo evaluate whether bolus-only eptifibatide provides ischemic protection comparable to standard bolus-plus-infusion while reducing bleeding in ACS patients undergoing PCI.MethodsIn this open-label, randomized trial, 183 ACS patients on aspirin and clopidogrel undergoing PCI received bolus-only eptifibatide (Group A, n = 102) or bolus plus continuous infusion (Group B, n = 81) and were followed for 90 days. The primary outcome, major adverse cardiac events (MACE), included cardiac death, recurrent myocardial infarction, stent thrombosis, or repeat target-vessel revascularization. Secondary endpoints included left ventricular function, mechanical/electrical complications, and bleeding. Major bleeding was defined by TIMI criteria; minor bleeding included clinically apparent non-life-threatening events.ResultsMACE rates were similar (2.0% vs. 2.5%; p = 0.87). No major bleeding occurred. Left ventricular ejection fraction improved in both groups without significant difference (p = 0.52). Minor bleeding was lower in the bolus-only group (1.0% vs. 8.6%; p = 0.02). Electrical complications were infrequent and comparable.ConclusionsIn ACS patients on dual antiplatelet therapy, bolus-only eptifibatide provides ischemic protection comparable to bolus-plus-infusion while reducing minor bleeding, supporting a simplified adjunctive strategy in high thrombotic-risk patients undergoing PCI.

Introduction

Acute coronary syndromes (ACS), which include ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI), non-ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI), and unstable angina, result from abrupt reductions in coronary blood flow and remain a leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide. In the United States alone, ACS accounts for over one million hospitalizations each year [1], [2], [3], [4].

Platelet activation and aggregation are fundamental to the pathophysiology of acute coronary syndromes (ACS), particularly during percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI), a setting associated with an increased risk of thrombotic complications. To mitigate this risk, potent antiplatelet therapies, including glycoprotein (GPI) IIb/IIIa inhibitors, may be employed in selected high-risk patients undergoing PCI to reduce ischemic events (5).

Current guidelines explicitly advise against the routine use of glycoprotein (GPI) IIb/IIIa inhibitors in ACS, reserving their administration for bailout situations or patients with a high thrombotic burden during percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) [6], [7]. This reflects the need to balance potent antiplatelet effects with the heightened risk of bleeding, emphasizing selective rather than indiscriminate use (5).

The clinical application of GPIs depends on prior antiplatelet therapy. In patients not previously treated with oral P2Y₁₂ inhibitors, GPIs provide rapid platelet inhibition until the oral agent reaches therapeutic levels, effectively serving as a temporary bridging strategy. In contrast, for patients already receiving P2Y₁₂ inhibitors and aspirin, GPIs may be administered as an adjunct to enhance platelet inhibition, particularly in patients with high thrombotic burden or during bailout interventions (4).

The 2021 ACC/AHA/SCAI guidelines recommend eptifibatide administration in patients undergoing PCI as two intravenous boluses of 180 μg/kg given 10 min apart, followed by a continuous infusion at 2 μg/kg/min for up to 18 h (8). In contrast, the 2023 ESC guidelines recommend a single bolus of 180 μg/kg followed by infusion, without mention of a second bolus (9).

While this regimen is designed to mitigate thrombotic complications in high-risk patients with ACS, prolonged infusion has been associated with increased bleeding risk, particularly at vascular access sites after sheath removal, as well as extended hospital stays and logistical challenges related to post-procedural care (10).

These considerations have prompted interest in simplified eptifibatide strategies. The 2025 ACC/AHA update does not provide clear guidance on the necessity of both bolus and infusion phases or the optimal duration of therapy, highlighting a gap in current clinical recommendations (7). Since most ACS patients undergoing PCI are already receiving aspirin and a P2Y₁₂ inhibitor, and the optimal administration strategy, remains unclear, particularly in this population, which is simultaneously at an elevated risk of bleeding, this randomized trial evaluates bolus-only versus bolus-plus-infusion eptifibatide in DAPT-pretreated ACS patients to assess efficacy and safety while minimizing bleeding.

Methods

Study design and setting

This open-label, randomized clinical trial was conducted at Dr. Heshmat Educational and Medical Center, a tertiary referral hospital in Rasht, Iran, between January and December 2023. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Guilan University of Medical Sciences (IR.GUMS.REC.1402.247) and conducted following the Declaration of Helsinki (11). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The trial was registered with the Iranian Clinical Trials Registry (IRCT20230805059045N1).

Sample size calculation

The sample size was informed by previously published data in ACS patients undergoing PCI (10), Based on these data, a minimum of 71 patients per group was initially estimated to provide adequate numbers for exploratory analyses. To account for a potential 10% loss to follow-up, the target enrollment was adjusted to at least 78 patients per group. Ultimately, 102 patients were assigned to the bolus-only group and 81 to the bolus-plus-infusion group. The slight imbalance reflects operational challenges during enrollment and post-randomization reassessment of eligibility. Given the preliminary nature of this study and the small expected event rates, the trial should be considered hypothesis-generating, with findings intended to provide initial insights into the comparative safety and efficacy of bolus-only versus bolus-plus-infusion eptifibatide in ACS patients pretreated with dual antiplatelet therapy. Both groups exceeded the minimum target for exploratory analyses, supporting the interpretability of the primary outcome.

Study population

Adult patients (≥18 years) presenting with acute coronary syndrome (ACS), including ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) and non–ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI), who received dual antiplatelet therapy with aspirin and clopidogrel prior to percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) were eligible for enrollment.

In accordance with our local guidelines and current ESC/ACC/AHA recommendations [7], [12], all patients received aspirin and a P2Y₁₂ inhibitor prior to PCI. NSTEMI patients were typically pretreated with dual antiplatelet therapy several hours before the procedure, whereas STEMI patients received a loading dose of aspirin and a P2Y₁₂ inhibitor immediately prior to primary PCI.

Patients requiring immediate life-saving intervention before antiplatelet administration, or those with contraindications to aspirin or P2Y₁₂ inhibitors, were excluded. The study protocol did not introduce any delay in reperfusion therapy or PCI.

Among these dual antiplatelet–pretreated ACS patients, only those who developed intraprocedural thrombotic complications warranting adjunctive glycoprotein IIb/IIIa inhibitor (GPI) therapy were included.

Indications for adjunctive GPI use were restricted to angiographically documented high thrombus burden, persistent or large intracoronary thrombus, distal embolization, slow-flow or no-reflow phenomena, abrupt vessel closure, or other thrombotic complications compromising coronary flow, consistent with contemporary guideline-recommended selective or bailout use (13). These indications include bailout situations as well as other higher thrombotic-risk procedural features.

Randomization and allocation

Randomization was performed using a computer-generated permuted block sequence (block size of 4), generated via the online platform (https://www.sealedenvelope.com). Allocation concealment was maintained using sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes, opened at the time of enrollment by a study coordinator not involved in outcome assessment. Although a 1:1 allocation ratio was intended, the final group distribution consisted of 102 patients in the bolus-only group and 81 patients in the bolus-plus-infusion group. This deviation from equal group sizes was due to operational challenges encountered during patient enrollment, including post-randomization exclusions secondary to intra-procedural reassessment of eligibility criteria, technical errors in allocation execution at certain time points, and uneven accrual of eligible patients within pre-specified enrollment blocks. Importantly, both groups met the predefined minimum sample size requirement (n ≥ 78) based on power calculations.

Intervention protocol

All patients received standard guideline-directed therapy including aspirin (325 mg loading, then 80 mg daily), clopidogrel (300 mg loading, then 75 mg daily), and atorvastatin (80 mg daily). During PCI, unfractionated heparin (50–70 IU/kg) was administered to maintain an activated clotting time (ACT) of 200–250 s.

Follow-up and data collection

Patients were followed for 90 days post-discharge through structured telephone interviews and in-person clinic visits. Transthoracic echocardiography was performed at baseline and at 90 days. Clinical events were confirmed via hospital records and imaging reports and adjudicated by assessors blinded to group allocation.

TIMI flow grading post-PCI was not systematically recorded in the study database. Procedural success was assessed according to standard angiographic and clinical criteria, but formal TIMI flow data are not available for analysis, representing a study limitation.

Primary efficacy outcomes

Major adverse cardiac events (MACE) were defined as a composite of recurrent myocardial infarction, stent thrombosis, repeat revascularization, or cardiac death, based on the standardized definitions of the Academic Research Consortium (ARC) (14). For this study, MACE was assessed within 90 days post-discharge. Recurrent myocardial infarction was defined according to the Third Universal Definition of MI (15). Stent thrombosis included any documented stent thrombosis. Repeat revascularization was defined as any unplanned PCI or coronary artery bypass grafting of the target vessel. Cardiac death was defined as any death due to cardiovascular causes during follow-up.

Primary safety outcomes

Major bleeding was defined according to the TIMI (Thrombolysis In Myocardial Infarction) criteria (16) as including fatal bleeding, retroperitoneal hemorrhage, hemoglobin drop >4 g/dL, bleeding requiring blood transfusion or surgery, or any bleeding-related death.

Secondary safety outcomes

Minor bleeding included clinically apparent but non-life-threatening bleeding, such as hematuria, hemoptysis, localized hematoma, and puncture site bleeding not requiring transfusion or surgical intervention.

Statistical analysis

Baseline continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and were compared between groups using Student's t-test. Categorical variables are expressed as frequencies and percentages and were analyzed using the chi-square test or Fisher's exact test, as appropriate, based on expected cell counts. Changes in left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) from baseline to 90 days were assessed using paired t-tests within each group and independent t-tests for between-group comparisons. The incidence of clinical outcomes, including major adverse cardiac events (MACE), minor bleeding, and mortality, was compared using Fisher's exact test due to low event frequencies. Risk differences and 95% confidence intervals were calculated for binary outcomes when appropriate. No statistical comparisons were performed for outcomes with no events in either group. Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided P-value <0.05. All analyses were conducted using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

Efficacy outcomes

At 90 days, major adverse cardiac events (MACE) were limited to all-cause mortality. Two patients (2.0%; 95% CI, 0.2%–6.9%) in Group A and two patients (2.5%; 95% CI, 0.3%–8.7%) in Group B died, with a risk ratio of 0.79 (95% CI, 0.11–6.35; P = 0.87). No other MACE components, including recurrent myocardial infarction, stent thrombosis, or repeat target-vessel revascularization, were observed in either group.

Discussion

In this randomized trial of ACS patients pretreated with dual antiplatelet therapy undergoing PCI, bolus-only administration of eptifibatide provided ischemic protection comparable to the standard bolus-plus-infusion regimen, with significantly fewer minor bleeding events. Over 90 days, rates of major adverse cardiac events (MACE) were similarly low between groups, and no major bleeding occurred, supporting bolus-only as a potentially safer adjunctive strategy in high thrombotic-risk patients on aspirin and clopidogrel.

These results are consistent with emerging evidence questioning the necessity of prolonged infusion following bolus eptifibatide in selected PCI populations. Historically, glycoprotein IIb/IIIa inhibitors demonstrated reduced ischemic events when added to PCI, as seen in landmark trials such as IMPACT-II (17) and ESPRIT (18), where bolus plus infusion reduced death, myocardial infarction, or urgent revascularization compared with placebo or control regimens in mixed elective and ACS settings. Despite these benefits, prolonged infusion has been associated with increased bleeding risk, which has limited routine use in contemporary practice, particularly with widespread adoption of potent P2Y₁₂ inhibitors (19).

In addition to placebo-controlled and adjunctive GPI studies, the INFUSE-AMI trial represents an important randomized evaluation of an intracoronary bolus-only GPI strategy in the setting of primary PCI for ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI). In INFUSE-AMI, patients undergoing primary PCI were randomized to receive intracoronary bolus eptifibatide directed at the infarct-related artery versus control, with the aim of reducing infarct size and microvascular obstruction. Although the trial did not demonstrate a significant reduction in the primary endpoint of infarct size by magnetic resonance imaging, subgroup and mechanistic analyses suggested potential benefits in certain high-risk anatomical subsets, and importantly, did not show an excess of major bleeding with bolus-only administration compared with standard therapy (20). This randomized evidence supports the conceptual rationale for short, targeted GPI bolus strategies and complements data from adjunctive studies in ACS and PCI populations.

Observational and comparative studies have shown that bolus-only or truncated infusion strategies can maintain similar ischemic outcomes with lower bleeding and resource use. In a large single-center cohort, bolus-only GPI therapy was associated with reduced vascular and bleeding complications without compromising 30-day MACE or long-term mortality when compared with standard bolus-plus-infusion (21). Similarly, high-dose single bolus eptifibatide without prolonged infusion proved a safe and cost-effective alternative in elective PCI, with low MACE and major bleeding rates, suggesting that intense initial platelet blockade may suffice in many patients (22).

More recent randomized data further corroborate these findings in primary PCI settings. A double-blinded randomized controlled trial in STEMI patients showed no significant differences in major bleeding, access site complications, or ischemic events between bolus-only and bolus-plus-infusion approaches, supporting the safety of bolus-only eptifibatide even in acute settings (23). Other mechanistic studies, such as the ICE trial (24), have demonstrated that higher local platelet receptor occupancy can be achieved with intracoronary administration of eptifibatide, which may enhance microvascular perfusion without requiring extended infusion.

Platelet function studies also suggest that adjunctive antiplatelet strategies combining potent oral agents (e.g., ticagrelor) with a bolus of eptifibatide may already achieve maximal platelet inhibition early after PCI, which may diminish the incremental value of continued infusion and concomitantly reduce bleeding (25).

The reduced minor bleeding in the bolus-only group in our study aligns with prior work indicating that shortened GPI exposure decreases procedural bleeding and sheath-site complications, which are common contributors to morbidity after PCI (21). The absence of major bleeding in both groups further highlights that with contemporary DAPT, the bleeding risk attributable directly to GPIs may be modest, but still modifiable.

Our results also align with real-world clinical practice, where clinicians increasingly employ bolus-only strategies in high thrombotic-risk patients. In particular, a recent large registry of 297 STEMI patients undergoing primary PCI demonstrated that intracoronary bolus-only GPI administration, whether used as adjunctive therapy or bridging therapy before P2Y₁₂ inhibitor loading, was associated with low rates of both major and minor bleeding, while maintaining effective ischemic protection (4). This real-world evidence complements our findings, suggesting that bolus-only administration is a safe and effective option in routine clinical practice, especially in patients already on dual antiplatelet therapy.

While our study focused exclusively on adjunctive GPI use, the pharmacologic properties of bolus-only GPIs, including prolonged platelet inhibition relative to cangrelor, suggest potential conceptual relevance for bridging in patients not fully pretreated with oral P2Y₁₂ inhibitors. Future studies could explore this application in bridging scenarios. Strengths of this study include its randomized design, focus on DAPT pretreated ACS patients, a population understudied in prior GPI research, and comprehensive clinical and echocardiographic follow up.

Strengths of this study

Strengths of this study include its randomized design, focus on DAPT-pretreated ACS patients, a population understudied in prior GPI research, and comprehensive clinical and echocardiographic follow-up. These features strengthen the relevance of our results to real-world PCI care.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the single-center design and modest sample size may limit generalizability to broader patient populations and diverse clinical settings. Second, the follow-up period was limited to 90 days, precluding evaluation of longer-term outcomes such as late stent thrombosis, recurrent ischemic events, or long-term mortality. Third, only intravenous adjunctive administration of eptifibatide was investigated; intracoronary administration was not evaluated, and future studies comparing administration routes are warranted. Fourth, mechanistic insight into myocardial recovery and thrombotic or bleeding complications was limited by the absence of serial imaging and biomarker assessments during follow-up. Fifth, although the study was powered for its primary outcomes, low event rates for major endpoints such as major bleeding and mortality may have limited the ability to detect statistically significant differences, and the small number of events precluded multivariable adjustment for potential confounders. Additionally, several procedural details were not systematically collected, limiting further analyses. Specifically, post-PCI TIMI flow grades, detailed procedural characteristics, vascular access site, and timing of P2Y₁₂ inhibitor administration relative to PCI were not recorded. Importantly, this study focused exclusively on adjunctive GPI use in patients already on dual antiplatelet therapy, and no bridging data were collected or analyzed. Finally, the study was open-label, which may introduce bias; however, all clinical outcomes were adjudicated by assessors blinded to group allocation. Taken together, these limitations indicate that the findings should be considered hypothesis-generating, providing preliminary insights to inform the design of future, larger, multi-center studies with comprehensive procedural and mechanistic data collection.

Conclusion

Bolus-only eptifibatide provided comparable ischemic outcomes to bolus-plus-infusion in ACS patients on modern DAPT undergoing PCI, while significantly reducing minor bleeding. This supports a simplified adjunctive strategy that balances potent platelet inhibition with safety and efficiency. Larger multicenter trials are needed to confirm these findings and refine patient-specific indications for bolus-only GPI therapy, particularly in the contemporary era of potent P2Y₁₂ inhibitors and radial access PCI.
