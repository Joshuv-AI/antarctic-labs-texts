All-cause mortality risk following diabetic avoidable hospitalization among older patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus: a nationwide retrospective cohort study in South Korea
Authors: Woo-Ri Lee, Sungyoun Chun, Noorhee Son, Yehrhee Son, Ki-Bong Yoo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn South Korea, the prognosis of avoidable hospitalization in diabetes has not been evaluated, even though the incidence rate is considerably high compared to the Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development average. This study investigated the association between diabetic avoidable hospitalization after the diagnosis of type 2 diabetes mellitus and short- and long-term all-cause mortality.MethodsParticipants were type 2 diabetes mellitus patients aged 60 years and older and gathered through the National Health Insurance Services Senior cohort data from 2008 to 2019. The outcome measures included five-year and overall period all-cause mortality. The main independent variable was the first-year experience of diabetic avoidable hospitalization after type 2 diabetes mellitus diagnosis. Regression analysis was performed using the Cox proportional hazard model. To enhance the robustness of the study results, a sensitivity analysis and inverse probability of treatment weighting method were conducted.ResultsAmong 27 005 participants, 2099 (7.8%) died within five-year of type 2 diabetes mellitus diagnosis and 4693 (17.4%) died in the overall study period after type 2 diabetes mellitus diagnosis. Participants who experienced diabetic avoidable hospitalization one year after being diagnosed with type 2 diabetes mellitus had a higher risk of all-cause mortality compared to those who did not (inverse probability of treatment weighted, Five-year: hazard ratio 1.24, 95% confidence interval 1.14–1.36; Overall period: hazard ratio 1.41, 95% confidence interval 1.33–1.50).ConclusionsEnhancing accessibility and quality of primary care to prevent avoidable hospitalization in older patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus is necessary.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24273-z.Keywords: Type 2 diabetes mellitus, Diabetic avoidable hospitalization, All-cause mortality, Older patients, Inverse probability of treatment weighting

Background

As the older population continues to grow, patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) [1, 2] and burden associated with T2DM are also increasing, becoming a major concern in the global healthcare field [1]. As of 2017, an estimated 462 million individuals worldwide were afflicted with T2DM, and approximately 1 million patients succumbed to diabetes, making it the ninth leading cause of death [1]. In South Korea, around 6 million individuals had diabetes as of 2020 [3], and diabetes ranked sixth among all causes of death [4]. Older patients with diabetes face higher risks of both death and complications [5, 6]. Furthermore, more than 30% of individuals aged 65 years or older are affected by diabetes [3], escalating health-related burden of diabetes on older individuals.

Diabetes-related hospitalizations are typically driven by both acute metabolic crises and chronic diabetes complications. According to the American Diabetes Association, hospital admission may be required for life-threatening acute complications (e.g., diabetic ketoacidosis, hyperosmolar hyperglycemic state, or severe hypoglycemia), chronic metabolic dysregulation requiring intensive monitoring, and diabetes-related end-organ complications such as nephropathy, neuropathy, or cardiovascular disease [7]. In particular, older adults are more likely to be hospitalized for chronic complications rather than acute metabolic events, due to delayed diagnosis and the cumulative burden of long-standing hyperglycemia [8–10].

Diabetes is a typical ambulatory care-sensitive condition. If managed effectively at the appropriate time, patients can prevent hospitalization. Therefore, it is classified as avoidable hospitalization [11, 12]. Previous studies have confirmed the association between continuity of care (COC) and avoidable hospitalization [12–14]. In South Korea, through the continued improvement of healthcare accessibility and enhancement of health insurance coverage, the sex-age standardized diabetes hospital admission rate per 100,000 persons decreased from approximately 340 in 2011 to 196 in 2021 [15]. However, the average Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) sex-age standardized diabetes hospital admission rate per 100,000 persons in 2021 is approximately 114 people, ranking South Korea high in diabetes hospital admission rate, which necessitates concern [15].

Previous studies have confirmed close association between avoidable hospitalizations and mortality risk [16, 17]. However, in the case of diabetic avoidable hospitalization, the effect of such occurrences on prognosis has not been investigated. Therefore, this study explores the association between diabetic avoidable hospitalization and all-cause mortality.

Methods

Data and study population

Analysis used available data from the National Health Insurance Services (NHIS) Senior cohort between 2008 and 2019. The NHIS serves as South Korea’s singular insurer, managing and storing medical service information utilized by all citizens [18]. The NHIS Senior cohort data represents a nationwide random sample of participants, with approximately 8% of the elderly population aged 60 years or older as of 2008. The random sampling was made after distinguishing individuals based on gender, age, healthcare insurance premium, and regional area [19]. This study included patients with T2DM based on the International Classification of Disease, with Tenth revision (ICD-10) criteria “E11” as the primary diagnosis (N = 326,789). Excluded from this study were individuals diagnosed with T2DM before 2008 or after 2014 (N = 250,830), and those hospitalized for T2DM prior to T2DM diagnosis (N = 1,497). To enhance diagnostic validity using claims data, we included only individuals with at least four outpatient visits listing T2DM as the principal diagnosis (N = 43,106) and who had been prescribed antidiabetic medication within one year of diagnosis (N = 4,232). These criteria were applied to ensure that the study population comprised patients who were actively being treated for diabetes and to reduce potential misclassification of cases. In addition, those who died within one year after the T2DM diagnosis (N = 119) were also excluded. After applying all criteria, the study proceeded with a final sample of 27,005 participants (Fig. 1).

Variables

Outcome measures

The outcome measure for this study is all-cause mortality. As the NHIS cohort contains data on deaths from Statistics Korea, it can be ascertained whether a patient has died [18]. We conducted an analysis of all-cause mortality throughout the first five-year of post-T2DM diagnosis and within the overall study period following T2DM diagnosis to assess both the short- and long-term effects.

Independent variable

The independent variable in this study is diabetic avoidable hospitalization. Avoidable hospitalization serves as an indicator to assess accessibility and quality of primary care [20]. This suggests a decline in accessibility or quality of primary care for participants who have experienced avoidable hospitalization [20]. Diabetes‑related avoidable hospitalization refers to admissions that occur because of diabetes complications. As part of its 2014–2015 Health Care Quality Indicators (HCQI) project, the OECD designated this measure as a key marker of primary‑care quality [21, 22]. The HCQI indicator is constructed by combining the metrics most commonly applied to people with three types of diabetes-related avoidable hospitalizations for short‑term complications, long‑term complications, and uncontrolled diabetes (Supplementary Table 1) [21–23]. The OECD defines diabetes hospital admission as an individual who is hospitalized with the ICD-10 criteria “E11.0–E11.9” [24]. Following the OECD indicators, diabetic avoidable hospitalization was considered to have occurred if participants were admitted with T2DM as the primary diagnosis within one year after their T2DM diagnosis.

Covariates

The covariates in this study were analyzed by categorizing them into socioeconomic, health-related, and diabetes-related factors. The socioeconomic factors included sex (male, female), age (60s, 70 s, over 80 s), income (quartile), region (capital city, other cities), and type of healthcare insurance (Medical Aid, NHI Self-employed, NHI Employee). Health-related factors included disability (non-disabled, disabled) and Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) score. The CCI score was categorized into 17 diseases according to Quan’s classification, weighted [25], and further divided into 4 groups (0, 1, and ≥ 2) based on the score. Diabetes-related factors included COC, Diabetes Complications Severity Index (DCSI), Types of antidiabetic treatment, and the diagnosed year of T2DM. COC for chronic diseases have values ranging from 0 to 1. The closer the value is to 1, the better the COC [12]. We assessed COC within one year after T2DM diagnosis and categorized participants into two groups: good COC (score = 1) and poor COC (score < 1). The DCSI evaluates the severity of diabetes-related complications and primarily divided into seven diseases (nephropathy, neuropathy, retinopathy, cerebrovascular disease, cardiovascular disease, peripheral vascular disease, and acute metabolic complication) [26], and further classified into four groups (0, 1, and ≥ 2) based on the score. The type of antidiabetic treatment was assessed based on prescriptions received within one year after the diagnosis of T2DM. We categorized treatment into two groups: oral antidiabetic medication only and insulin-based treatment. The oral medication group included patients prescribed one or more of the following drug classes: sulfonylureas, biguanides, thiazolidinediones, α-glucosidase inhibitors, meglitinides, DPP-4 inhibitors, sodium-glucose co-transporter 2 inhibitors, and medications for diabetic neuropathy. The insulin-based treatment group included all patients who received insulin therapy, regardless of whether they also received oral medications.

Statistical analysis

We differentiated and analyzed the short- and long-term effects of diabetic avoidable hospitalization. The analysis was conducted in five stages. First, a chi-squared analysis was performed to validate the relationship between each variable and all-cause mortality. Statistical significance between variables was determined based on a P-value < 0.05. Second, we calculated the incidence rate of diabetic avoidable hospitalization over the overall study period. The incidence rate was calculated as the number of events per 100,000 person-months, stratified by the type of diabetic avoidable hospitalization. Third, a survival analysis was performed. Survival analyses were conducted for both short-term and long-term periods. The short-term analysis evaluated mortality within five-year of the initial T2DM diagnosis, while the long-term analysis extended follow-up from diagnosis until the data cutoff in December 2019. Accordingly, the long-term observation period varied by participant, ranging from a minimum of five-year to a maximum of twelve-year. The proportional hazard assumption was assessed through the Kaplan–Meier survival curve [27], while the difference in survival time between avoidable hospitalization and all-cause mortality was examined using a log-rank test, and statistical significance was determined based on a P-value < 0.05. Regression analysis to proceed with the association between diabetic avoidable hospitalization and all-cause mortality was conducted using a Cox proportional hazard model. Furthermore, the statistical significance was considered when the lower limit of the confidence interval (CI) exceeded 1. Fourth, a sensitivity analysis was conducted to enhance the robustness of the study results. The sensitivity analysis comprised three components: (a) the types of antidiabetic treatment were considered in the analysis of diabetic avoidable hospitalization, (b) subgroup analysis, and (c) change in the definition of study participants. Participants who experienced diabetic avoidable hospitalization were classified according to the type of antidiabetic treatment received, and then categorised into three groups: no avoidable hospitalization, avoidable hospitalization with oral antidiabetic medication only, and avoidable hospitalization with insulin-based treatment. Subgroup analysis was conducted, stratified by non-disabled, DCSI scored 0, and good COC, to analyze only participants with a relatively good baseline health status. The T2DM definition for participants was modified to over two outpatient visits, and the analysis proceeded in a manner similar to Steps 1 to 3. Fifth, to enhance the robustness of the analysis results, we used inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW), a methodology utilizing propensity score (PS) [28]. IPTW is a causal inference method, and its value is derived by taking the reciprocal of the PS. It is also a doubly robust method entered by weighting the model during regression analysis and incorporating all covariates used to calculate IPTW in the regression analysis [28, 29]. This application of IPTW allows the derivation of a doubly robust estimation value. Homogeneity between treatment groups was confirmed by assessing the absolute value of the standardized difference, with a criterion set at 0.1. All regression analyses were adjusted for all covariates included in the study (sex, age, income, region, type of healthcare insurance, disability, CCI, COC, DCSI, types of antidiabetic treatment and diagnosed year of T2DM). The independent variable in this study is time-dependent, which may occur repeatedly during the study period. To consider the effects of time-dependent variables and immortal time bias, the analysis utilized the landmark method [30, 31]. It was performed after setting the landmark period to one year, which is the observation period for diabetic avoidable hospitalization. All analyses were performed for 2023 using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA) and R software (version 3.6.3; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Among the participants, 737 (2.7%) experienced diabetic avoidable hospitalization within one year after the diagnosis of T2DM (Supplementary Table 2). When classified by the type of diabetic avoidable hospitalization, long-term complications were the most common (389 cases, 1.4%), followed by uncontrolled diabetes (332 cases, 1.2%) and short-term complications (16 cases, 0.1%). Results of examining the relationship between the participants’ general characteristics and all-cause mortality are presented in Table 1. The five-year all-cause mortality rate was 16.6% and 7.5% for participants who experienced and did not experience diabetic avoidable hospitalization, respectively, indicating a difference (P < 0.01). The overall period of all-cause mortality also demonstrated a difference, with rates of 33.6% and 16.9% for participants who experienced and did not experience diabetic avoidable hospitalization, respectively (P < 0.01).

Discussion

Key findings

This study aims to explore the short- and long-term relationship between diabetic avoidable hospitalization after T2DM diagnosis and all-cause mortality. The analysis revealed increases in five-year and overall period all-cause mortality rates among participants who experienced diabetic avoidable hospitalization after T2DM diagnosis compared to those who did not. These effects increased with more pronounced with the insulin-based treatment on diabetic avoidable hospitalization, and the results remained consistent even when the analysis was restricted to participants with a good baseline health status.

Interpretation

The results are close with those of previous studies indicating that patients with avoidable hospitalization had a higher risk of mortality [16, 17]. Avoidable hospitalization serves as an indicator to assess the accessibility and quality of primary care [20]. Individuals experiencing diabetic avoidable hospitalization are likely to have poor accessibility to or low-quality primary care. To conclude, there is a clear imperative to enhance the accessibility and quality of primary healthcare to prevent diabetic avoidable hospitalization. Development of avoidable hospitalization can be prevented through sustained management [11, 12].

However, preventing avoidable hospitalization also depends on social support. Family involvement is cognitively and emotionally beneficial for older patients managing chronic disease [32], yet 19.8% of Koreans aged ≥ 65 live alone [33, 34], limiting informal care. According to the Diabetes Fact Sheet in Korea, as of 2021, among diabetic patients aged 65 and over in Korea, only about 73.3% of the patients were prescribed antidiabetic medication and undergoing treatment, and approximately 28.3% of the patients had well-regulated blood sugar with an HbA1c level < 6.5% [3]. Moreover, older adults are at heightened risk of hypoglycemia—both from insulin-based regimens and age-related physiological changes—making it essential to ensure they do not skip meals or inadvertently take extra doses [35]. Considering these factors, community-based initiatives should include regular meal-and-medication checks and social support services to promote sustained treatment and safe self-management. Therefore, alongside strengthening primary-care quality, targeted community care programs are needed to reduce avoidable hospitalizations among Korea’s older diabetic population.

In the older ages, diabetes was frequently detected only after a prolonged asymptomatic period. Multiple studies show that 12–16% of newly diagnosed older patients already present with micro- or macro-vascular lesions. Consistent with these reports, more than half of the first-year diabetes-related admissions in our cohort were coded as chronic complications. In addition, our study’s results show that long-term complications predominated regardless of the observation window: 1,379 of 2,714 diabetic avoidable hospitalizations (52.8%) involved long-term complications, whereas only 79 admissions (2.2%) were due to short-term metabolic crises and 1,256 (45.0%) to uncontrolled diabetes. Coupled with the finding that 11.4% of participants already had a DCSI score ≥ 1 at baseline, these results indicate that many older adults entered the study with pre-existing chronic end-organ damage. Although short-term complications were infrequent in our study, they remain clinically important; diabetic ketoacidosis, in particular, is a life-threatening condition in older patients. In the United States, a retrospective analysis of diabetic ketoacidosis in patients aged ≥ 65 years reported an in-hospital mortality rate of 16.7% among older patients [36]. In England, hospital admissions for diabetic ketoacidosis in adults with T2DM rose by 4.24% per year between 1998 and 2013 [37]. Furthermore, a UK tertiary-care cohort of 786 consecutive DKA episodes found that 24.1% occurred in T2DM patients, who experienced significantly longer hospital stays than those with T1DM (median 11.0 vs. 3.0 days) [38]. Accordingly, our findings underscore the need for community-based initiatives to facilitate the timely identification and management of diabetes among older adults before chronic or acute complications arise.

The sensitivity analysis, stratified by diabetic avoidable hospitalization by type of treatment, revealed a clear gradient in all-cause mortality risk: participants admitted on insulin-based therapy had the highest hazard, followed by those admitted on oral medication, compared to non-admitted individuals. These results indicate that both the occurrence of an avoidable hospitalization and the intensity of antidiabetic treatment serve as important prognostic markers, especially in those requiring insulin. Accordingly, preventing avoidable admissions and optimizing post-hospitalization treatment plans—including careful review of insulin regimens—are critical components of ongoing diabetes management in older adults. A prior randomized clinical trial study in the United States demonstrated improvement in hemoglobin levels in the telehealth group compared to the general treatment group [39]. Considering the older age of participants in this study, proposing telemedicine could enhance medical accessibility and strengthen the management of diabetic-related health conditions. Although this study does not specifically address telehealth, future research should explore how telehealth interventions impact patients with diabetic avoidable hospitalization.

Even in a subgroup analysis of participants who were in relatively good health at baseline, it was confirmed that participants who experienced diabetic avoidable hospitalization had a poor prognosis. A previous study has indicated that typically healthy individuals tend to be overconfident about their health, leading to less frequent visits to healthcare providers [40]. We underscore the importance of preventing avoidable hospitalization through the effective management of T2DM, irrespective of the underlying health status.

Although sufficient studies have not yet been conducted on the association between avoidable hospitalization and mortality risk, the consistent findings between previous studies and the present study suggest that avoidable hospitalization is a reliable predictor of mortality risk.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, health behavioral factors (smoking, alcohol consumption, and exercise) and blood test results (HbA1c and fasting blood sugar) were not included. These factors are necessary to accurately reflect the basic health status of diabetic patients; however, due to the constraints of claim data, it was impossible to include these variables. To address these limitations, the analysis proceeded by incorporating the health status of diabetic patients through disability and CCI, and reflecting diabetes-related health status through the DCSI and COC variables. Second, in the case of claim data, as the data are billed to receive healthcare costs from the insurer, there may be a discrepancy in the diagnosis code between the claim data and the actual hospital data. However, in South Korea, the claim data requested by the NHIS undergo evaluation by the Health Insurance Review and Assessment Service to determine the appropriateness of the request. Therefore, the discrepancy in diagnosis codes is low [41]. Third, as avoidable hospitalization is a time-dependent variable, some instances may occur even one year after T2DM diagnosis, resulting in repeated events. To address this limitation, the analysis uses the landmark method, an analytical approach that effectively controls time-dependent variables, where efforts minimized bias in the analysis results. Despite these limitations, the present study is of significance as it confirms an association between diabetic avoidable hospitalization and all-cause mortality in elderly T2DM patients. Additionally, the analysis using IPTW demonstrates robust results.

In summary, older patients with T2DM who experienced avoidable hospitalization exhibited higher all-cause mortality rates in both the short and long terms. Enhancing the accessibility and quality of primary care to prevent avoidable hospitalization in older patients with T2DM is necessary.
