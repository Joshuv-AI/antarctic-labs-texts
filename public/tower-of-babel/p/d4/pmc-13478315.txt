Optimizing Traps and Dihedral Angles to Modulate Charge Transport Behavior for High-Temperature Dielectric Energy Storage
Authors: Yang Zhao, Shuo Zhao, Shiyi Wu, Mufeng Zhang, Minzheng Yang, Lei Yang, Weibin Ren, Ce-Wen Nan, Yang Shen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
High-temperature dielectric films hold broad prospects in next-generation microelectronics and power systems. However, commercially available dielectric materials for electrostatic energy storage, specifically biaxially oriented polypropylene, suffer from drawbacks such as low energy density, low efficiency, and poor reliability, which limit the application of film capacitors in harsh environments. Herein, a series of naphthalene tetracarboxylic acid dianhydrides were incorporated into polyetherimide (PEI) backbone, thereby modulating the charge transport behavior. Specifically, synergistic regulation of traps and dihedral angles has effectively enhanced the breakdown strength of PEI. At 200 °C, the optimized dielectric film delivers a discharged energy density of 6.00 J cm−3 at an efficiency of 90%, and maintains stable performance over 105 charge-discharge cycles at 300 MV m−1. Multilayer stacked film capacitors prepared from the optimized films exhibit remarkable thermal stability and capacitive performance. This work proposes a feasible strategy for developing dielectric films with remarkable energy storage performance, superior thermal stability, and scalable fabrication capability.

Introduction

Dielectric materials are extensively employed owing to their superior insulating properties, tunable dielectric constants, and low dielectric losses. Dielectric film capacitors possess extensive application in aerospace, gas/oil exploration, hybrid electric vehicles, clean energy, electric power transmission and other high-voltage environments1–3. Dielectric polymer film capacitors, which account for approximately 50% of the global market, outperform their ceramic counterparts and possess inherent advantages such as lightweight properties4. However, the heat tolerance of most polymers remains constrained, hampering the further progress of dielectric films in high-temperature environments. Biaxially oriented polypropylene (BOPP), a typical dielectric material for commercial film capacitors, delivers ~4 J cm−3 discharged energy density (Ud) at room temperature but degrades sharply above 105 °C3. The P(MMA‑VN) copolymer5 exhibits a discharged energy density of 19.3 J cm−3 with an efficiency exceeding 89% at room temperature, while PVDF‑based bilayer films deliver 13.87 J cm−3, 97.2% efficiency6 and a breakdown strength (Eb) of 767.1 MV m−1. Nevertheless, these impressive performances are mainly achieved at moderate temperatures, leaving the critical issue of high‑temperature energy storage still unresolved. Thus, their dielectric performance deteriorates significantly at elevated temperatures (150-200 °C), failing to satisfy the high-temperature energy storage demands of emerging technologies, which highlights the urgency of developing high-temperature polymer dielectrics7–12.

Among dielectric materials with high thermal resistance, polyimide has advantageous properties, including chemical stability, mechanical strength, high radiation resistance, structural designability, salt-free polymerization behavior, and compatibility with processing techniques13–17. However, as the applied electric field increases from 50 to 250 MV m−1 at 150 °C, the conductivity of PI increases significantly, which leads to a pronounced reduction in its energy storage efficiency and breakdown strength. This has driven researchers to employ a range of strategies for enhancing the performance of polymer dielectric materials. For example, inorganic nanofillers with wide bandgaps (e.g., boron nitride nanosheets [BNNS], Al2O3, MgO, CaF2, HfO2, TiO2, SiO2)18–23 have been integrated into dielectric materials to engender deep traps, thereby enhancing discharged energy density. However, achieving a high dielectric constant requires increasing the content of inorganic nanofillers, which in turn reduces the processability of the polymer. Meanwhile, at elevated temperatures, the interfacial loss generated at the organic-inorganic interface can easily induce electrical breakdown24. Therefore, the improvement in high-temperature energy storage density of polymers achieved by such approaches is relatively limited.

As one of the most promising polyimide-based dielectric energy storage materials, polyetherimide (PEI) suffers from relatively low discharged energy density due to the previously mentioned constraints. To this end, this work proposes an approach of incorporating a series of naphthalene tetracarboxylic acid dianhydride derivatives (NTCDAs) into PEI backbone to modulate charge transport behavior. Naphthalene tetracarboxylic acid dianhydride derivatives, functioning as an n-type semiconductor with higher electron affinities (EA, e.g., 4.06 eV) than PEI dielectric films, facilitates the trapping and localization of charge carriers. The incorporation of naphthalene-based semiconducting dianhydrides into the PEI backbone results in a near-perpendicular dihedral angle (~91°), which is conducive to the suppression of charge transfer. In addition, this proposed approach addresses the issue of poor dispersion uniformity of organic semiconductors in PEI. This approach effectively reduces the conduction loss of the material, while achieving enhancements in both breakdown strength and energy storage performance. Consequently, the PEI incorporated with 5 mol% NTCDA (PEI-co-NTCDA 5%) film exhibits significantly reduced conduction loss and enhanced breakdown strength, attaining an exceptional Ud of 6.00 J cm−3 at 200 °C with a charge-discharge efficiency (η) of 90%. This represents a 240.9% enhancement compare to the pristine PEI, which showed a Ud of 1.76 J cm−3 at 200 °C (η = 90%). Furthermore, PEI-co-NTCDA 5% showed a remarkable charge-discharge cycling stability, enduring more than 105 fatigue tests under the conditions of 200 °C and 300 MV m−1. The approach proposed in this work is compatible with the conventional two-step process in PI manufacturing, thereby holding significant practical potential for industrialization. A metallized stacked films capacitor based on PEI-co-NTCDA designed for high-temperature capacitive energy storage was also investigated, which maintains a capacitance consistently above 40 nF within the wide temperature range from 25 to 200 °C. Moreover, in the temperature range of 25 to 200 °C, the capacitance varied by only 1.5%, demonstrating superior performance stability of the capacitor. This work proposes a feasible strategy to enhance the thermal stability and dielectric performance of PEI-based dielectric films, and facilitates the practical application of these films in high-temperature film capacitors.

Results

Design and synthesis of dielectric polymers

Naphthalene based dianhydrides exist in three isomeric forms, which include three derivatives with n-type semiconductor properties (Fig. 1a), namely naphthalene-1,4,5,8-tetracarboxylic dianhydride (NTCDA), 1,2,5,6-naphthalenetetracarboxylic dianhydride (m-NTCDA), and 2,3,6,7-naphthalenetetracarboxylic dianhydride (p-NTCDA). NTCDAs were widely used as raw materials for functional materials such as polyimides and organic semiconductors. Density functional theory (DFT) calculation results show that the lowest unoccupied molecular orbitals (LUMO) of NTCDA, m-NTCDA, p-NTCDA and PEI were −4.06, −3.89, −3.49 and −2.36 eV, respectively (Fig. 1b and Supplementary Fig. 1). The molecular semiconductors possessed significantly higher EA (e.g., 4.06 eV) than PEI (2.36 eV, Fig. 1c), which were capable of trapping injected and excited electrons through strong electrostatic attraction. This would generate a deep trap (Φe, e.g., 1.70 eV for NTCDA) that captured electrons must overcome to escape from trap sites (Fig. 1c). Herein, NTCDAs were incorporated into PEI backbones (Supplementary Fig. 2), and the homogeneous distribution of NTCDAs (PEI-co-NTCDAs) within PEI was achieved (Fig. 1d). In the reaction of 1,3-phenylenediamine (MPD) with 4,4’-(4,4’-isocyanatodiphenyl oxide) phthalic anhydride (BPADA), NTCDA, m-NTCDA, or p-NTCDA were added at ice-water bath, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 2). Notably, among the three types of NTCDAs, the regions with strong electron-withdrawing ability27 in NTCDA was the largest (Supplementary Fig. 3), which suggested NTCDA need the shortest reaction time. The chemical composition and molecular structure of polyamic acid (PAA) were characterized by proton nuclear magnetic resonance (1H-NMR) spectroscopy (Supplementary Fig. 4). The proton peaks in the range of 6.98-8.86 ppm were attributed to the aromatic protons. The signals around 10.50 and 13.00 ppm corresponded to the -CONH- and -COOH units, respectively28. The influence of dianhydride monomer molar ratios in the polymers were systematically examined in subsequent sections, with the aim of attaining high energy storage performance.

The samples underwent testing via Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy with attenuated total reflection (FTIR-ATR, Supplementary Fig. 5). The characteristic absorption peaks at 1784, 1717 and 1350 cm−1 corresponded to the asymmetrical C = O stretching, symmetrical C = O stretching and C-N stretching vibrations, respectively15. By comparing the peak area ratios at 1380 and 1510 cm−1 of PEI-co-NTCDAs, all films exhibited an imidization degree above 98%, indicating that the imidization process were nearly complete for all dielectric films29. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and atomic force microscopy (AFM) results indicate that NTCDAs have a negligible effect on the surface morphology and structural integrity of the PEI films, and all the as-fabricated films are dense with smooth surfaces (Supplementary Figs. 6 and 7). In addition, the film cross-sections are smooth and devoid of voids (Supplementary Fig. 8). Moreover, transmission electron microscopy (TEM) characterization reveals that the sample exhibits uniform and homogeneous morphology throughout the entire field of view, with no observable phase separation or aggregation, confirming the homogeneous distribution of the components in the polymer matrix (Supplementary Fig. 9). AFM-IR spectroscopy was performed to examine the response of C = O groups to IR radiation in PEI and PEI-co-NTCDAs (Supplementary Fig. 10). Under IR radiation at a wavenumber of 1717 cm−1 (ascribed to C = O symmetric stretching), the absorbance intensity of C = O groups in PEI-co-NTCDAs is comparable to that in pure PEI (Supplementary Fig. 11). AFM-IR characterization further demonstrates that the characteristic absorption signal exhibits uniform intensity across the scanned region, without significant fluctuations or localized enhancement, confirming the homogeneous distribution of the components.

Mechanical/thermal properties and charge transport behavior

In most cases, a monomer structure with higher planar character yielded wider overlaps of carbon π orbitals and stronger π-electron delocalization. Crucially, the electronic coupling between conjugated monomer planes was at its peak when their dihedral angle equals zero. Potential energy surface scanning simulations were conducted to monitor the variation in molecular energy as a function of the dihedral angle (from 0° to 360°) between the phenyl ring and the naphthalene diimide moiety linked by an imide bridge. At the minimum energy configuration, the dihedral angles of PEI, PEI-co-p-NTCDA, PEI-co-m-NTCDA, and PEI-co-NTCDA are 33°, 35°, 38°, and 91°, respectively (Fig. 2a and Supplementary Fig. 12). Among all samples, PEI-co-NTCDA exhibits the highest rotational energy barrier and a nearly perpendicular preferred conformation, indicating the most significant steric hindrance and conformational rigidity (Supplementary Fig. 13). This structural feature effectively suppresses planar conjugation within the PEI backbone and thus hinders charge transfer13,30–32. The fluorescence results reveal that, relative to pristine PEI, PEI-co-p-NTCDA, PEI-co-m-NTCDA, and PEI-co-NTCDA all display a distinct blueshift in their fluorescence characteristic peaks accompanied by a pronounced enhancement in fluorescence intensity, suggesting that NTCDAs can efficiently suppress charge transfer in PEI (Supplementary Fig. 14)30,31. Furthermore, above 150 °C, the fluorescence intensity of the three NTCDA-containing samples decays more slowly with increasing temperature in comparison to pure PEI (Supplementary Fig. 15). X-ray diffraction (XRD) measurements (Supplementary Fig. 16) demonstrated the increased interchain spacing of PEI films, which were attributed to the increased dihedral angle caused by the introduction of NTCDAs and in turn led to the enlargement of the molecular stereoscopic configuration. In addition, the variation in dihedral angles also affects the free volume of the polymer. Molecular dynamics simulations reveal that the fractional free volumes (FFV) of PEI, PEI-co-p-NTCDA, PEI-co-m-NTCDA, and PEI-co-NTCDA are 15.3%, 16.8%, 17.4%, and 18.7%, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 17). with corresponding densities of 1.19, 1.12, 1.07, and 1.05 g cm−3. According to the UV-Vis absorption spectra (Supplementary Fig. 18), the optical bandgap (Eg) of this series of films ranges from 3.1 to 3.2 eV. Moreover, the thermal stability of PEI and PEI-co-NTCDAs films were evaluated using differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) and thermogravimetric analysis (TGA), as illustrated in Supplementary Figs. 19 and 20, respectively. In addition, these films exhibited relatively high Tg (218-230 °C) and could effectively enhance the heat resistance of PEI films, which resulted from the introduction of NTCDAs (Fig. 2c). A series of TGA measurements were carried out to assess the thermal degradation behavior of the PEI and PEI-co-NTCDAs. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 20, due to the incorporation of a portion of NTCDAs, those PEI films possess Td5% values ranging from 419 to 459 °C for PEI and PEI-co-NTCDA 5%, respectively. Thermal test results indicated that these PEI films exhibited remarkable thermal stability (Fig. 2c). To this end, thermal performance tests demonstrated that such polymer structures enable PEI films to achieve high energy storage density under high temperature conditions. Data on Young’s modulus (Y) determined via the nano-indenter are shown in Fig. 2c. The Young’s modulus of PEI films ranging from 4.6 to 4.9 GPa for PEI and PEI-co-NTCDA, respectively. According to Eb ≈ 0.6(Y/εrε0)1/2, breakdown strength of the film takes place when mechanical stress fails to counterbalance electrostatic stress3. The Eb exhibited a positive correlation with Young’s modulus, which indicated that PEI-co-NTCDA possess a higher Eb.

To investigate the effect of traps on charge transport in PEI, Kelvin Probe Force Microscopy (KPFM) was employed herein to characterize surface charge injection and decay behaviors in PEI and PEI-co-NTCDA 5% (Supplementary Fig. 21). In experiments, an 8 V voltage was applied for 240 s to 5×5×11 μm films. After voltage removal, the surface potential of films was tracked continuously via a probe. Those results revealed that relative to pristine PEI (Fig. 2d), the PEI-co-NTCDA 5% (Fig. 2e) accumulates more injected charge carriers on its surface, with slower decay kinetics. Moreover, after 240 s of charging, the PEI-co-NTCDA 5% film showed an initial surface potential of −1119 mV (Fig. 2f), the absolute value of which was markedly higher than that of the pristine PEI film (−835 mV). The pristine PEI film exhibited faster potential decay than the PEI-co-NTCDA 5% film; the time taken for their surface potential to drop to 50% of the initial value was 85 s (pristine PEI) and 138 s (PEI-co-NTCDA 5%), respectively. These results indicate that compared with pristine PEI, the PEI-co-NTCDA possesses deeper traps, which allow it to trap more charge carriers33–35.

Dielectric and energy storage performance

To evaluate the impacts of NTCDAs on the electrical properties of PEI films, we initially examined the dielectric constant and dissipation factor (tanδ) of these polymer films at high temperatures. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 22a, the dielectric constant increases slightly with the gradual rise in the molar ratio of NTCDA. This arises from the inherent polarity of NTCDA, and the dielectric constant increases accordingly. Furthermore, across a wide temperature range, the εr and tanδ of such polymers were both found to remain stable (Supplementary Fig. 22b). The dielectric constants of PEI-co-m-NTCDA 5% and PEI-co-p-NTCDA 5% were also slightly increased compared to pure PEI, and this increase was not as effective as that of PEI-co-NTCDA 5% (Supplementary Fig. 23). The variations of dielectric constant and dielectric loss of PEI and PEI incorporating NTCDAs were minimal with temperature (Fig. 3a), being conducive to high temperature applications. Owing to the variability of breakdown mechanisms, Eb values are influenced by multiple factors, including, though not limited to, Young’s modulus. Therefore, NTCDAs could enhance the Young’s modulus of PEI while also increasing its Eb. Herein, we first investigated the influence of NTCDA content on the Eb of PEI. As the NTCDA increased, the Eb of PEI-co-NTCDA at high temperatures increased initially and then decreased, among which the PEI incorporating 5 mol% NTCDA showed the maximum value. For example, at 150 °C, incorporating 1 mol% NTCDA (PEI-co-NTCDA 1%) increased the Eb from 533.07 to 545.70 MV m‒1. Subsequently, when 3% and 5% NTCDA were incorporated, the Eb increased to 574.85 and 674.49 MV m-1, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 24). This indicates that when the NTCDA content was 5 mol%, the charge trapping capability of such organic semiconductors reached the maximum level. However, the Eb decreased to 595.37 and 572.05 MV m‒1 with the NTCDA increased to 7 mol% and 10 mol%, respectively. This was because the probability of enabling electronic conduction via excited charge carriers in the NTCDA component increased significantly25. Notably, the influence trend of NTCDA on the Eb of PEI at 200 °C was consistent with that mentioned above, where the PEI-co-NTCDA 5% exhibited an optimal value of 604.18 MV m‒1 (Supplementary Fig. 24). At a low molar ratio of NTCDA, the molecular semiconductor with high electron affinity can trap most free electrons in the PEI backbone. As the molar ratio of the molecular semiconductor increases, the trap density rises25. However, since NTCDA is a narrow-bandgap semiconductor, a further increase in its content reduces the average spacing between adjacent molecular semiconductor units. This significantly enhances the probability of electronic conduction via excited charge carriers, which gradually dominates the electrical properties of the material. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 25, the leakage current measurements reveal that the leakage current density of PEI-co-NTCDA exhibited a slight decrease with the increase in NTCDA content from 0 mol% to 5 mol%. Conversely, when the NTCDA content was further raised from 5 mol% to 10 mol%, the leakage current density of PEI-co-NTCDA increased. This indicates that 5 mol% of NTCDA shows the most pronounced inhibitory effect on charge transport in PEI. Accordingly, the breakdown strength of PEI is maximized at an NTCDA copolymerization content of 5 mol%.

Moreover, m-NTCDA and p-NTCDA could also enhance the Eb of PEI. For instance, PEI incorporated with 5 mol% m-NTCDA yielded an Eb of 587.69 MV m‒1 at 150 °C and 553.29 MV m‒1 at 200 °C. Similarly, when PEI incorporating 5 mol% p-NTCDA, the Eb values at these temperatures were 569.87 and 498.12 MV m‒1, respectively. Because the traps and dihedral angles of PEI-co-m-NTCDA and PEI-co-p-NTCDA were smaller than those of PEI-co-NTCDA, m-NTCDA and p-NTCDA enhanced the Eb of PEI less effectively than NTCDA did. For physical blended with PEI and 5 mol% NTCDA (PEI-BW-NTCDA 5%), the Eb enhanced to 583.23 and 539.03 MV m‒1 at 150 and 200 °C, respectively, which was not as effective as that of PEI-co-NTCDA 5%. This is mainly because compared with the copolymerization strategy, NTCDA introduced via the blending method only serve to construct trap energy levels and have almost no impact on the molecular structure of PEI, and in particular, they exert no significant regulatory effect on the dihedral angles of the molecular chains of PEI.

Deep traps can be further corroborated via thermally stimulated depolarization current (TSDC) measurements, as TSDC peaks at high temperatures stem from trapped charges36. As shown in Fig. 3c and Supplementary Fig. 26, the TSDC curves were fitted into two peaks, which are attributed to the charge traps generated by NTCDAs (denoted as Peak 1) and electrode charge injection (denoted as Peak 2), respectively24. As for peak 1, higher trap energy level and larger trapped charge quantity are advantageous for trapping charge carriers. The trap energy levels of peak 1 for PEI-co-p-NTCDA, PEI-co-m-NTCDA, and PEI-co-NTCDA increase sequentially, with values of 0.83, 0.96 and 1.02 eV, respectively (Supplementary Table 1). The trapped charge quantity is significantly increased from 19.70 nC to 23.80 nC and 45.80 nC for PEI-co-p-NTCDA, PEI-co-m-NTCDA and PEI-co-NTCDA, respectively (Supplementary Table 1). The increase in trapped charge quantity indicates that NTCDAs with deeper traps can capture more charge carriers, thus ensuring superior energy storage performance at high temperatures. This is consistent with the results obtained from DFT simulations and further elucidates the role of NTCDAs in improving the breakdown strength. All samples exhibit a peak 2 associated with electrode charge injection. For PEI, PEI-co-p-NTCDA, PEI-co-m-NTCDA, and PEI-co-NTCDA, the trap energy levels of peak 2 are 0.70, 1.97, 2.59, and 3.60 eV, respectively, with the corresponding trapped charge quantities being 6.52, 6.40, 5.71, and 5.06 nC. Furthermore, the introduction of comonomer units influences the dihedral angle of the PEI backbone. Larger dihedral angles result in weaker intermolecular interactions, thereby suppressing charge transfer complex (CTC) formation35. In other words, the incorporation of NTCDAs modulates the dihedral angles of the PEI backbone and suppresses the charge transfer in PEI. These results demonstrate that the synergistic effect between deep traps and large dihedral angles suppresses charge transport and consequently lowers charge carrier mobility18,36,37.

To further clarify the enhancement in insulating properties of dielectric films, the leakage current density of PEI and PEI incorporating NTCDAs were measured, with results shown in Supplementary Fig. 27. NTCDAs could significantly reduce the leakage current density of PEI, and especially compared with m-NTCDA and p-NTCDA the reduction effect of NTCDA was more prominent. Herein, the leakage current densities for PEI, PEI-co-NTCDA 5%, PEI-co-m-NTCDA 5%, PEI-co-p-NTCDA 5% are 3.41×10‒8, 8.15×10‒9, 1.83×10‒8, 2.27×10‒8 A cm‒2 at 200 °C and 200 MV m‒1, respectively. This indicated that NTCDAs could effectively inhibit charge transport in PEI. Diminished conduction loss and enhanced breakdown strength will significantly contribute to the higher discharged energy density in PEI. After incorporated NTCDA into the PEI backbone, this modification not only enhanced the Young’s modulus of PEI, increased the dihedral angle and reduced its leakage current, but also promoted PEI to trap more charges and extended its potential decay time. These performance optimization results jointly confirmed that NTCDA could effectively enhance the breakdown strength of PEI, which further indicated that it was expected to enhance the dielectric energy storage performance of PEI.

The high-temperature energy storage properties of PEI and PEI incorporating NTCDAs were assessed via the unipolar electric displacement-electric field (D-E) loop (Supplementary Figs. 28-32). PEI incorporating 5 mol% NTCDA showed the optical Ud of 8.44 and 6.00 J cm‒3 at 150 and 200 °C (η = 90%), respectively. However, the Ud for PEI at 150 and 200 °C (η = 90%) were only 2.99 and 1.76 J cm‒3, respectively (Fig. 3d and Supplementary Fig. 28). The Ud of PEI-co-NTCDA 5% were over 2.82 and 3.41 times higher than those of PEI, respectively. The discharged energy densities (η = 90%) of PEI-co-m-NTCDA 5% at 150 and 200 °C were 4.51 and 2.89 J cm‒3, respectively. However, those of PEI-co-p-NTCDA 5% at 150 and 200 °C were 4.25 and 2.09 J cm‒3, respectively. The discharged energy densities of PEI-co-m-NTCDA and PEI-co-p-NTCDA, although showing a significant enhancement compared to PEI, were not as effective as those of PEI-co-NTCDA. Moreover, the discharged energy densities of PEI-BW-NTCDA 5% also showed a slight increase compared to pure PEI at 150 and 200 °C, while it was still lower than that of PEI-co-NTCDA 5%. To determine the optimal content for physical blending, we investigated the discharged energy density and breakdown strength of PEI composites with varying NTCDA blend ratios. At 150 °C, the breakdown strengths for 0, 0.1, 0.3, 0.5, 0.7, 1, 3, 5, 7 mol% NTCDA are 533.07, 534.38, 539.48, 545.54, 565.51, 589.65, 586.30, 583.23, 542.51 MV m‒1 (Supplementary Fig. 33), with corresponding discharged energy densities (η = 90%) of 2.99, 3.11, 4.02, 4.17, 4.98, 4.85, 4.56, 3.71 J cm‒3 (Supplementary Figs. 34–35). The optimal blend ratio of NTCDA is found to be 1 mol%. At 200 °C, the optimal blend ratio remains 1 mol%, which exhibits a breakdown strength of 548.02 MV m‒1 (Supplementary Fig. 36) and a discharged energy density of 4.04 J cm‒3 (Supplementary Figs. 37–38). Thus, 1 mol% NTCDA is optimal for this physical blending system. This clearly demonstrated the significant role of incorporating NTCDAs into the PEI backbone in enhancing energy storage density. In addition, PEI modified via incorporating NTCDA exhibits the largest dihedral angle and the deepest trap; the former achieves this through a strong charge trapping effect, while the latter does so by impeding intra-chain charge migration. Together, these two factors can effectively inhibit charge transport in the PEI, ultimately endowing PEI-co-NTCDA with the optimal energy storage performance.

Furthermore, based on a comprehensive performance summary in Fig. 3e, the PEI-co-NTCDA film showed marked advantages compared to PEI, PEI-co-m-NTCDA and PEI-co-p-NTCDA across several parameters, encompassing high ɛr, Eb as well as low tanδ. More importantly, as shown in Fig. 3f, the PEI-co-NTCDA film delivered an Ud90% (i.e., Ud with a η ≥ 90%) at 200 °C that significantly surpassed that of most widely employed dielectric films, including PEI/UV-Al2O3 (≈3.59 J cm‒3)38, PEI/TE ( ≈ 4.01 J cm‒3)39, PI-NH2-POSS ( ≈ 3.45 J cm‒3)36, PEI/t-BPB-8 ( ≈ 3.81 J cm‒3)40, PEI/ZIF8-67 ( ≈ 1.84 J cm‒3)41, PEI/HAP ( ≈ 3.10 J cm‒3)42, PEI-g-TOC ( ≈ 4.55 J cm‒3)24, PEI/PCBM ( ≈ 3.00 J cm‒3)25, PEI/PWNS ( ≈ 4.54 J cm‒3)43, PEI/E-c-SiO2 NPs (≈4.26 J cm‒3)44, PEI-co-10PAB/90MPD ( ≈ 4.32 J cm‒3)45. Furthermore, the Ud90% value of the PEI-co-NTCDA film at 150 °C is also considerably higher than that of most conventional dielectric films, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 39.

Reliability and capacitors

A large-sized PEI-co-NTCDA 5% dielectric film measuring 15×9 cm was fabricated via the solution-casting method to verify its potential for large-scale preparation (Fig. 4a). The film was observed to display favorable transparency, flexibility, and surface smoothness (inset of Fig. 4a). Different regions of the film were tested for their energy storage performances, with the results summarized in Fig. 4a. Notably, D-E loop shapes and Ud values were highly consistent across nine regions of the PEI-co-NTCDA 5%, confirming the uniformity and high quality of the film. The high-temperature breakdown strength of these large-sized samples was also comparable to that of the smaller samples mentioned above, which indicated that the PEI prepared via incorporating the NTCDA in this study had the potential for scalable production. Flexible device prototypes produced via stacking, pressing, and lead-out validate the potential of PEI-co-NTCDA 5% films for use in metallized stacked film capacitors (Supplementary Fig. 47). These film capacitors with five active dielectric layers demonstrate good flexibility, featuring an effective dielectric area of approximately 5.0×5.0 cm and a 1.0 cm edge margin. Multilayer stacked films featuring alternating electrodes and dielectric layers were produced via the identical hot-pressing technique (Supplementary Fig. 48). As shown in Fig. 4d and e, cross-sectional scanning electron microscopy and energy dispersive spectrometry (EDS) were used to reveal the structure of the multilayer films, which showed that the electrodes and dielectric materials were tightly stacked. Within the EDS element mappings, the Al (Fig. 4e) and C (Supplementary Fig. 49) elements were clearly distinguishable at their respective positions. Fig. 4f and Supplementary Fig. 50 presents the frequency-dependent capacitance and dissipation factor of the film capacitor at different temperatures. At 25 °C and 103 Hz, the film capacitor exhibited a capacitance of 40.70 nF, with its dissipation factor below 0.002. Furthermore, at 200 °C and 103 Hz, this capacitor still maintained a capacitance of approximately 40 nF, and its dissipation factor remained under 0.003. Within the temperature range of 25–200 °C, the capacitance variation was only around 1.5%, which demonstrated that the capacitor possesses remarkable performance stability. The above results indicated that PEI-co-NTCDA based dielectric films possess the potential for application in multi-layer capacitors.

Discussion

In this work, a series of naphthalene-based dianhydrides were incorporated into PEI backbone, which enlarged the dihedral angle and engineered traps. Thus, the charge transport behavior of PEI was effectively modulated. These unique structural and functional characteristics of PEI-co-NTCDA endow it with remarkable high-temperature capacitive energy storage performance and operational reliability, with key performance metrics including Eb, Ud, η, cycling stability, and stable capacitance. Finally, an exceptionally high Ud of 8.44 J cm‒3 at 150 °C as well as 6.00 J cm‒3 at 200 °C, alongside an η of 90%, were achieved by the PEI-co-NTCDA 5% dielectric film. Furthermore, the PEI-co-NTCDA 5% demonstrated superior long-term stability under the conditions of 200 °C and 300 MV m‒1, with such stability remaining intact even after up to 105 cycles. In addition, the heat-resistant stacked film capacitors fabricated using PEI-co-NTCDA 5% dielectric film exhibit remarkable capacitance stability over a wide temperature range from 25-200 °C. This work provides a rational material strategy for tailoring the high-temperature capacitive performance of dielectric films. Those results hold great potential for the large-scale preparation of high-performance dielectric films and further advance miniaturized, high-temperature resistant capacitors.

Methods

Materials

N-methylpyrrolidone (NMP, purity > 99%), 4,4’-(4,4’-isocyanatodiphenyl oxide) phthalic anhydride (BPADA, purity > 98%), naphthalene−1,4,5,8-tetracarboxylic dianhydride (NTCDA, purity > 97%), 1,2,5,6-naphthalenetetracarboxylic dianhydride (m-NTCDA, purity > 95%), and 2,3,6,7-naphthalenetetracarboxylic dianhydride (p-NTCDA, purity > 97%) were purchased from Tokyo Chemical Industry (TCI). 1,3-Phenylenediamine (MPD, purity = 99%) was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (Shanghai) Trading Co., Ltd. Polyetherimide (PEI, melt index 18 g 10 min−1) was purchased from Shanghai Titan Science & Technology Co., Ltd. Deionized water was purchased from Macklin Inc. Deuterated dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO-d6) was purchased from Cambridge Isotope Laboratories, Inc. Dimethylsiloxane was purchased from ZHONGKESHANGDE. Biaxially oriented polypropylene (BOPP) films were purchased from Polyk Technologies LLC. All the chemicals were used as received without further purification.

Synthesis of the PEI precursor PAA

The synthetic routes of PEI and PEI-co-NTCDAs were shown in Supplementary Fig. 2. In the synthesis of polyamic acid (PAA), the reaction system was prepared with 1 mmol of monomers (e.g., diamine and dianhydride) with NMP as the solvent, and the mass fraction of PAA in the total system was controlled at 18 wt%.

1 mmol of MPD was weighed and placed in 20 mL glass vial. NMP was added to dissolve the MPD, and the mixture was stirred for 1 h. Then, 1 mmol of BPADA was added to the glass vial, and additional NMP was added to maintain a consistent mass fraction of PAA. The reaction was stirred for 12 h at room temperature, allowing the full reaction of MPD with BPADA to form PAA (PEI-PAA).

Preparation of the PEI dielectric films

PEI polymer films were prepared using the classic solution casting method. The PAA solution obtained from the reaction was cast onto a cleaned glass plate. The cast films were initially dried in a vacuum drying oven at 50 °C for 4 h to remove the organic solvent and then transferred to a convection oven. Imidization was completed via a thermal process using the following temperature program under N2 atmosphere: 70 °C for 2 h, 150 °C for 2 h, 200 °C for 2 h, and 250 °C for 2 h. The films were then allowed to cool to room temperature. The PEI films were then peeled off the glass plate in deionized water, followed by vacuum drying at 90 °C overnight to eliminate residual moisture, thus completing the film preparation process.

Preparation of the PEI-co-NTCDAs dielectric films

Dielectric films with molar ratios of NTCDA to BPADA (1:99, 3:97, 5:95, 7:93, 10:90) were synthesized (PEI-co-NTCDA-PAA). The samples were named PEI-co-NTCDA 1%, PEI-co-NTCDA 3%, PEI-co-NTCDA 5%, PEI-co-NTCDA 7% and PEI-co-NTCDA 10%, respectively. The reaction route is shown in Supplementary Fig. 2. MPD was first dissolved in NMP, followed by the addition of NTCDA and BPADA according to the molar ratios. The mixture was stirred 12 h at room temperature to obtain PAA containing both NTCDA and BPADA dianhydride. Replacing NTCDA with m-NTCDA or p-NTCDA (with the ratio of m-NTCDA or p-NTCDA to BPADA being 5:95), these reaction mixtures were stirred for 18 h or 14 h, so as to obtain PAA containing BPADA and m-NTCDA or p-NTCDA dianhydride (PEI-co-m-NTCDA-PAA or PEI-co-p-NTCDA-PAA). Subsequently, following the aforementioned PEI film preparation process, these PAA solutions were subjected to thermal imidization treatment, ultimately yielding the corresponding PEI-co-NTCDAs films.

Preparation of stacked film capacitors

The electrode layers employed in the film capacitors were aluminium foils with a thickness of approximately 15 μm. As depicted in Supplementary Fig. 47, dielectric and electrode layers were arranged alternately, with the topmost layer being a dielectric film. The dielectric polymer layer was positioned at the center, while each electrode layer was alternately offset to the left and right sides. A 1 cm margin was retained between the dielectric layer and electrode edges to electrically insulate the anode and cathode. The assembled multilayer stacks were hot-pressed at 230 °C under a pressure of roughly 5 tons for 5 h, followed by natural cooling to room temperature, yielding dense laminated films. Copper wires were attached as external electrodes to both sides of the multilayer structures using conductive silver paste, thus completing the fabrication of stacked film capacitors. For the characterization of electrode-dielectric interfacial structures, multilayer films were fabricated using an Al foil as the substrate, followed by the sequential deposition of Al foil and dielectric layers, and final capping with an additional Al foil layer. The remaining preparation procedures were identical to those described above.

Copper electrodes with a diameter of 2.5 mm were sputtered onto both sides of the films to acquire displacement-electric (D-E) field hysteresis loops and leakage current data. Copper electrodes with a diameter of 11.5 mm were sputtered onto both sides of the films for measuring dielectric spectra and thermally stimulated depolarization currents. Dielectric spectra were acquired using a Novocontrol broadband dielectric/impedance spectrometer (Novocontrol Technologies GmbH & Co. KG) equipped with a liquid nitrogen cooling system. The spectra were recorded over a frequency range of 10−106 Hz and a temperature range of 20-200 °C. Thermally stimulated depolarization currents (TSDC) were measured using the Novocontrol broadband dielectric/impedance spectrometer. The samples were polarized under a DC electric field of 40 MV m−1 at 180 °C for 10 min, then rapidly cooled to −100 °C at a rate of 10 °C min‒1 while maintaining the electric field. After the polarization procedure, the samples were short-circuited and heated to 210 °C at a heating rate of 2 °C min‒1, and the corresponding currents were recorded simultaneously. Unipolar displacement-electric field (D-E) loop measurements (at 100 Hz) and charge-discharge cycling tests were conducted using a polarization loop & dielectric breakdown test system (PolyK Technologies, LLC). Breakdown strength was recorded using a Trek 20/20B instrument with a voltage ramp of 200 V s‒1, where 9 data points were collected and analyzed via the two-parameter Weibull statistics for each sample. In the D-E loop, charge-discharge cycling, and breakdown strength tests, the samples were immersed in temperature-resistant silicone oil to control the temperature. Leakage currents were measured using a multiferroic ferroelectric test system (Radiant Technologies, Inc., Premier II). Fast charge-discharge tests were conducted using a PK-DIS20012 system (PolyK), which includes a 20 kΩ load resistor and an internal resistor of 110 Ω integrated into the circuit. The surface potential of the composite dielectric materials was characterized using a Kelvin Probe Force Microscopy (KPFM) equipped with an Oxford Instruments CYPHER instrument.

Simulation

Density functional theory (DFT) calculations were performed using Gaussian 16 software, with GaussView 6.0 employed for visualization. Geometric optimizations of the organic semiconductor and polymer structures were carried out at the B3LYP/6-311 G (d, p) level. Building upon these optimized structures, more accurate single-point energies and potential energy surface scanning (PES) were computed utilizing the B3LYP-D3BJ functional46 in conjunction with the larger 6-31 G(d) basis set47. Electrostatic potential (ESP) distributions, molecular orbitals and dihedral angle were analyzed and visualized. The electron-hole distributions were further analyzed using Multiwfn software (Version 3.8)48,49. Molecular dynamics simulations were performed using GROMACS 202350. Geometries of the polymer monomers were optimized with the XTB package, and their RESP charges were subsequently calculated via Multiwfn48,49. Parameters for the molecular simulations were constructed for the polymers using the GAFF force field51. The simulation box was set to 4.93 ×4.93×4.93 nm3, and each system contained 14 randomly placed polymer chains. After energy minimization, all systems were subjected to 100 ns NPT molecular dynamics simulations. The system temperature was maintained at 298.15 K using the V-rescale thermostat, and the pressure was controlled at 1 bar with the C-rescale barostat. An annealing algorithm was employed during the compression process to ensure reliable polymer configurations. Long-range van der Waals interactions were truncated at 12 Å, and electrostatic interactions were treated using the particle-mesh Ewald (PME) method. The production simulation time step was 2 fs, with trajectories saved every 2 ps. Trajectory snapshots were generated using VMD52.
