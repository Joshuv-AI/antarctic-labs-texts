Health responsibility among marginalized women in Mashhad, northeastern Iran: a critical ethnographic study
Authors: Faezeh Rastgoo, HamidReza Zendehtalab, Mehrdad Arabestani, Nayyereh Davoudi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHealth responsibility is a cornerstone of self-care and health promotion. However, for marginalized women, this concept is intricately shaped by intersecting cultural, social, economic, and political forces that often impede their access to care and limit their decision-making autonomy. This study, therefore, utilizes critical ethnography to explore how these structural and cultural conditions shape marginalized women’s understanding and practice of health responsibility in their daily lives.MethodsThis critical ethnographic study, based on Carspecken’s (1996) methodology, was conducted between September 2023 and October 2024 in a marginalized neighborhood on the periphery of Mashhad, one of Iran’s largest metropolises. Data collection involved extensive fieldwork, encompassing participatory observations, in-depth interviews, analysis of documentary materials and cultural artifacts, and data gathered through virtual interactions with women in the community via social media platforms. All collected data were meticulously analyzed using MAXQDA software.ResultsOne of the main categories identified in this study was “Maintaining Women’s Health Amidst Inequality and Cultural Norms.” This overarching category is further explained by three intermediate categories that collectively illustrate how women’s health responsibility is socially constructed rather than a matter of individual choice. The first intermediate category, “Self-Regulated Health Practices beyond Formal Medicine,” highlights women’s reliance on accessible and culturally familiar healing methods, often stemming from a distrust of formal biomedical services. The second, “Health within the Spiritual Sphere,” demonstrates how faith, divine providence, and ritualistic practices offer moral fortitude and meaning when confronting illness. The third, “Health Constrained by Inequalities,” encapsulates the pervasive economic hardship, gender-specific restrictions, and institutional neglect that narrow women’s choices and perpetuate their vulnerability.ConclusionsThis critical ethnographic study found that women’s health responsibility in the marginalized neighborhoods of Mashhad is socially constructed rather than individually chosen. Everyday practices such as herbal healing, faith-based rituals, and self-medication serve as adaptive strategies within structural inequality. Interpreting the findings through Bourdieu’s logic of practice shows a habitus shaped by necessity, while Foucault’s concept of biopower reveals how moral and spiritual discourses transform deprivation into self-discipline. Promoting health equity, therefore, requires shifting responsibility from individuals to social and institutional structures.Keywords: Health responsibility, marginalization, Women’s health, Critical ethnography, Carspecken’s method

Background

In health promotion literature, health responsibility is traditionally conceptualized as an individual capacity and obligation to maintain well-being. This perspective emphasizes health awareness, self-care practices, and the timely utilization of professional health services [1, 2]. Over recent decades, this individual-centered view has increasingly been institutionalized, aligning with public health discourses that promote self-care and empowerment [3]. However, such approaches frequently overlook the profound structural and socioeconomic constraints that significantly shape health-related behaviors, particularly within marginalized contexts.

The term “marginalized settlements” refers to informal or semi-formal urban areas that arise from rapid urbanization and population growth. These areas are often characterized by a lack of essential municipal facilities and services, leading to the formation of impoverished communities deprived of basic amenities [4]. Common features of these settlements include high unemployment, low income, limited educational attainment and literacy, large household sizes, substantial migration rates, and inadequate access to health infrastructure [5]. Women constitute approximately half of the population in these areas. In recent years, marginalized women have been recognized as a crucial target group for health promotion and empowerment programs initiated by international organizations [6].

Among marginalized women, health responsibility transcends being a mere individual attribute; it emerges as a socially produced and multifaceted phenomenon, intricately shaped by the confluence of cultural, social, economic, and political forces. While public health and biomedical discourses champion ideals of self-care and personal accountability, marginalized women do not passively internalize these expectations. Instead, they actively reinterpret and negotiate health responsibility in response to material deprivations, demanding caregiving roles, and experiences of institutional exclusion. Consequently, they often prioritize survival-oriented practices over preventive health behaviors [7–10]. A theoretical examination of these practices is crucial for understanding the specific forms that health responsibility assumes within this context.

Drawing upon Bourdieu’s theoretical framework, the concepts of field, habitus, and various forms of capital (economic, cultural, social, and symbolic) offer valuable insights into how health-related practices are shaped by individuals’ structural positions and the unequal distribution of resources, rather than by autonomous choice [10–12]. From a Foucauldian perspective, health responsibility is further influenced by biopower and governmental discourses that discipline bodies, normalize self-care, and assign responsibility for health outcomes to individuals. Simultaneously, these discourses often obscure the structural and institutional constraints that significantly limit women’s capacity for health-oriented agency [13, 14]. These dynamics have been extensively documented in empirical research across diverse global contexts.

Empirical evidence from low- and middle-income countries strongly supports this structural interpretation. Numerous studies demonstrate that disparities in women’s health are profoundly influenced by interconnected socioeconomic, cultural, and institutional determinants; including poverty; limited education; patriarchal norms; and inadequate health infrastructure; rather than being solely a consequence of individual behavior [15, 16]. Cultural norms also play a mediating role. For instance, Opara et al. (2025) found that among Igala women in Nigeria, traditional beliefs, such as a preference for home births and the stoicism regarding labor pain, actively discouraged engagement with formal healthcare services [17]. Similarly, studies on cultural safety in Europe have reported that Indigenous and ethnic minority women frequently encounter mistrust, discrimination, and emotional exclusion during clinical encounters, leading to reduced healthcare utilization [18].

Despite this substantial body of evidence, limited research has critically investigated how health responsibility is socially produced, negotiated, and constrained by power relations, structural inequality, and institutional practices within the everyday lives of marginalized women. Addressing this research gap necessitates an approach that moves beyond simplistic, individual-level explanations and foregrounds the critical roles of power, culture, and lived experience.

Critical ethnography, as a qualitative and analytical research approach, provides a robust framework for examining these complex dynamics. It not only amplifies the voices of marginalized women but also actively challenges and exposes the underlying structures of inequality and power [19, 20]. Employing Carspecken’s critical qualitative framework, this study investigates how marginalized women in Mashhad, Iran, construct and negotiate health responsibility within a matrix of intersecting structural constraints. By foregrounding power relations and structural inequalities, the analysis illuminates the practical ways in which women’s health agency and their daily decision-making processes are shaped. The findings offer valuable insights for developing more equitable approaches in health planning and policy, with significant implications for public health and nursing practice.

Methods

This section outlines the critical ethnographic framework employed in this study, the specific context of the research, the criteria for participant selection, the procedures for data collection, and the rigorous, multi-stage analytical process utilized to investigate the complex dimensions of health responsibility among women residing in a marginalized district of Mashhad, Iran.

The critical paradigm was adopted for this study due to its capacity to enable researchers to examine how power relations and structural inequalities influence marginalized women’s conceptualization of health responsibility. This paradigm was particularly suitable for the research aim, as it facilitates not only the uncovering of oppressive mechanisms but also the identification of opportunities for potential transformation within women’s daily health practices [19]. The study was fundamentally grounded in Carspecken’s methodology of critical ethnography [20]. Carspecken’s method was selected over other critical ethnographic approaches owing to its distinct, structured analytical stages and its emphasis on reconstructing meaning systems through dialogical engagement, rather than through researcher-imposed critique. This methodology comprises three preliminary stages and five main stages [21] (Table 1).

Stages of Carspecken’s critical ethnographic method

Research field and participants

In ethnographic research, the “research field” is defined as the naturalistic setting where the phenomenon of interest unfolds, encompassing the environment where participants live and their experiences transpire [22]. The judicious selection of both the research field and its participants is fundamentally guided by the study’s core research questions [23]. This critical ethnographic investigation was purposefully conducted in a neighborhood situated on the periphery of Mashhad, a major metropolis in northeastern Iran, spanning from September 2023 to October 2024. Mashhad’s unique position as a primary referral hub for diverse populations from the Khorasan provinces and adjacent countries rendered it a geographically, culturally, and economically rich and varied field for exploration.

The primary locus for this study was a suburban neighborhood within Mashhad, located in the city’s northeastern quadrant. Geographically, this area extends beyond the established urban agglomeration, integrating a mosaic of residential zones with agricultural lands. It is contiguous with several older urban districts, and its character is significantly shaped by high population density coupled with persistent migration from rural areas and neighboring countries, thereby establishing it as one of Mashhad’s most socially and spatially heterogeneous environments. To thoroughly address the research inquiries and achieve a nuanced understanding of women’s health responsibilities within their daily lives, the fieldwork extended across a wide array of urban and semi-formal settings. These included, but were not limited to, a comprehensive health service center, private residences of participants, educational institutions, beauty salons, food vendors (bakeries, butcher shops), herbal medicine stores, shopping centers, bustling weekly markets, public transportation (buses), recreational areas (parks), various social and religious gatherings, community events (charity and women’s parties), and informal group meetings.

Within the ethnographic tradition, participants function not merely as conduits of information but as crucial “teachers,” whose unique linguistic expressions and cultural meanings are instrumental in shaping the researcher’s comprehension of the local culture [23]. Consequently, the participants in this investigation comprised all individuals who directly or indirectly influenced women’s health outcomes within the selected district and who directly experienced the phenomenon under scrutiny. This cohort included healthcare professionals such as physicians, midwives, nurses, environmental health officers, nutritionists, and psychologists, alongside other allied health workers. Critically, however, women experiencing marginalization within this neighborhood were identified as the key informants for this research.

The established criteria for participant selection were meticulously designed to ensure both the relevance of the sampled individuals to the study phenomenon and the overall quality of the collected data. The inclusion criteria stipulated that participants must have been current residents of the study neighborhood for a minimum of one year, exhibit a genuine and demonstrable willingness to cooperate throughout the research process, possess substantial and pertinent experience related to the phenomenon under investigation, and demonstrate adequate articulatory capabilities to clearly and comprehensively convey their experiences, beliefs, and perspectives. Conversely, exclusion criteria were strictly limited to instances where a participant expressed an unwillingness to continue their cooperation at any phase of the research, or where an individual was demonstrably unable to provide valid, usable, and detailed information pertinent to the study’s defined scope.

Subsequently, a complementary checklist was developed to delineate the precise categories of information essential for addressing the study’s research questions. This included specifying relevant social routines, identifying critical documents, and pinpointing key individuals for observation or interview during the fieldwork.

Prior to commencing fieldwork, the principal investigator underwent a qualitative interview administered by a seasoned qualitative methodologist. This pre-field interview was designed to rigorously examine the researcher’s personal values, underlying assumptions, and potential biases concerning the research subject matter; the ensuing audio-recorded conversation was meticulously transcribed. Salient insights derived from this discussion were consistently revisited throughout the entire research process—from data collection and analysis to manuscript preparation. This sustained process of critical reflection ultimately served to uphold transparency, foster reflexivity, and ensure methodological rigor across all phases of the study.

Main stages

Compilation of primary records: fieldwork engagement

Following the acquisition of ethics committee approval and a formal introduction to the health center director, the researcher commenced fieldwork, assuming a dual role as a practicing nurse and dedicated researcher. The study’s purpose was clearly articulated to the community, fostering rapport through open and respectful communication that underscored the principles of voluntary participation, trust, and confidentiality. Data collection was approached through a synergistic combination of in-person and digital methodologies.

Participant observation and field engagement: Over a twelve-month period, the researcher conducted immersive participant observation across a diverse spectrum of community settings. This engagement spanned participants’ private residences, educational institutions, social hubs such as hair salons, and various commercial and public spaces including bakeries, butcher shops, herbalist stalls, shopping centers, weekly markets, public transportation, parks, and venues for social, religious, and women-specific gatherings. This sustained presence and intimate interaction within these varied environments facilitated close observation of quotidian health behaviors, nuanced communication styles, and the intricate dynamics of informal care networks. Meticulous field notes were diligently recorded, capturing not only environmental details and sensory impressions but also the contextual nuances of each site. These notes encompassed observations regarding ambient temperature, spatial configurations, color palettes, auditory landscapes, non-verbal communication cues, and the prevailing emotional atmosphere. To ensure the preservation of accuracy and contextual richness, these field notes were systematically expanded and typed within a 24-hour window following each site visit.

Dialogical data generation (interviews, virtual chats, and recordings): Continuous dialogue with participants formed a cornerstone of the data generation process, focusing on observed events and delving into the underlying beliefs, norms, and values that shaped their health-related behaviors. Dialogical engagement was further achieved through in-depth, privately conducted interviews, secured with explicit written consent and meticulously audio-recorded. Complementing these face-to-face interactions, the researcher maintained ongoing contact with participants via social media platforms. These sustained virtual exchanges yielded invaluable insights into women’s personal reflections on health, their familial roles, and prevailing community expectations. The corpus of primary ethnographic data thus comprised transcribed exchanges from these online interactions, alongside approximately 200 photographs, numerous short video clips, and voice recordings capturing social and spatial interactions.

Documents and cultural artifacts: Documentary evidence was systematically collected and included forms from patient health records, registration logs, official notices and posted warnings within clinic or institutional settings, and signage in public areas. Furthermore, everyday cultural symbols and artifacts; encompassing modes of dress, prevailing customs and traditions, leisure pursuits and hobbies, and culinary practices, were meticulously documented. These diverse sources provided critical contextual clues, illuminating the meanings participants ascribed to health, responsibility, and womanhood within the fabric of their daily lives.

Reflective diary: A reflexive journal was maintained throughout the duration of the study. This journal served as a critical space for documenting the researcher’s own positionality relative to the participants, including candid reflections on intersecting identities of gender and class, as well as emotional responses to observed systemic inequalities. This diary proved instrumental in tracking the evolution of the researcher’s assumptions regarding “marginalized women’s health responsibility,” thereby ensuring transparency and minimizing interpretive bias in alignment with critical ethnographic standards. This reflexive orientation consciously positioned the researcher not as a detached, neutral observer, but as an engaged participant within the power dynamics under investigation, echoing Carspecken’s assertion that knowledge is fundamentally co-constructed within asymmetrical social contexts.

All field notes, following their transcription into Word documents, alongside other meticulously collected materials, were systematically imported into MAXQDA for rigorous coding and in-depth analysis, consistent with the principles of critical ethnography.

Data trustworthiness

This study rigorously pursued scientific integrity and accuracy by adhering to Lincoln and Guba’s [24, 25].

Seminal five criteria for trustworthiness: credibility, dependability, confirmability, transferability, and authenticity. Enhanced credibility and contextual depth were achieved through prolonged ethnographic engagement, sustained participant interaction, and robust data triangulation across multiple sources; including women participants, healthcare providers, and other community members, within diverse settings such as health centers, private homes, and religious sites. To uphold dependability and confirmability, all field notes, transcripts, and coded data underwent thorough review by the supervisory team. Comprehensive documentation was securely archived in a password-protected folder, granting access solely to the principal researcher. Member checking, conducted meticulously after each interview, served to validate interpretations and ensure the accurate representation of participants’ lived experiences. Finally, the conveyance of rich, contextual descriptions, interwoven with direct participant quotations, powerfully reinforced transferability and authenticity, thereby ensuring that the research faithfully encapsulated the lived realities of marginalized women in Mashhad while upholding the highest standards of scholarly integrity.

Preliminary reconstructive analysis

The analysis of the collected data was guided by Carspecken’s (1996) seminal inductive reconstructive approach. This methodology employs an iterative and reflexive analytical sequence, progressing methodically from descriptive primary records towards a deeper interpretive reconstruction of meaning. Approximately 800 pages of field notes were subjected to this Preliminary Reconstructive Analysis, executed through three distinct yet interrelated and sequential phases: low-level coding, meaning field reconstruction, and horizon analysis.

The initial phase involved applying low-level coding to the primary records. This process was characterized by minimal abstraction, yielding an initial set of 871 distinct codes. The analytical imperative at this stage was to maintain maximal proximity to the participants’ lived experiences and observable practices, consciously deferring premature theoretical interpretation. In strict adherence to Carspecken’s methodological directives, meaningful segments of data were identified concurrently with the coding process. These segments were purposefully selected for their salience in revealing recurring behavioral patterns, instances of deviation from established norms, or the implicit rules underpinning routine practices.

In the second phase, the focus shifted to constructing “meaning fields” for each identified segment. This analytical procedure aimed to reconstruct the latent meanings embedded within participants’ actions and interactions. Essentially, this involved an analytical substitution: observed conduct was rendered interpretable by articulating the precise words participants might have employed had they explicitly verbalized the meanings conveyed through their bodily movements, temporal coordination, vocal intonation, gestures, or the execution of routine practices. This crucial reconstructive step served to render analytically visible those implicit and often taken-for-granted cultural assumptions that informed participants’ behaviors.

In the third phase, horizon analysis was applied to each meaning field to identify patterned meaning structures across cases. Actions were interpreted across horizontal levels (objective, subjective, normative, value-based, and identity-related claims) and vertical levels (foreground, intermediate, and background horizons) [20]. This intricate interpretive process culminated in the generation of 16 distinct high-level codes.

These 16 high-level codes were subsequently synthesized and merged into three intermediate categories. This categorization was based on discernible shared practice logics, underlying structural conditions, and the negotiated forms of agency exhibited by the participants. Through further interpretive abstraction and synthesis, a primary overarching category was identified. This main category captured the fundamental logic or framework through which participants navigated the complexities of health responsibility, particularly within the constraints imposed by conditions of inequality.

Consistent with the tenets of Carspecken’s critical ethnographic framework, particular emphasis was placed throughout the horizon analysis on interactive power relations. This included a detailed examination of various forms of power, such as normative power (i.e., the acceptance of authority grounded in culturally embedded norms), coercive power (i.e., compliance elicited through pressure or sanctions), contractual power (i.e., consent to authority in exchange for perceived benefits), and charismatic power (i.e., influence stemming from an individual’s inspirational or compelling personal qualities) [20, 26].

The fourth and fifth stages of Carspecken’s critical ethnographic methodology, which involve the discussion and interpretive synthesis of findings, correspond to the Discussion section of this manuscript. These stages will be elaborated upon in greater detail therein, where the reconstructed cultural meanings will be explicitly connected to broader social structures and established theoretical frameworks.

Main category: maintaining women’s health amid inequality and cultural patterns

The findings reveal that women navigate their health through a spectrum of everyday strategies, profoundly shaped by pervasive socioeconomic inequalities and prevailing cultural norms. This overarching theme is distinctly manifested across three intermediate categories:

First Intermediate category: Self‑Regulated Health Practices outside Formal Medicine

First Intermediate category: Self‑Regulated Health Practices outside Formal Medicine

This intermediate category encompasses women’s adaptive strategies for health preservation, employing personal, traditional, and non-institutional means. Six high-level codes underpin this category, each explained in detail:

Distrust in medical services

This code captures a significant lack of confidence among women in formal healthcare services. This distrust stems from prior negative experiences, perceptions of inefficiency within the system, and a perceived misalignment between biomedical approaches and women’s lived realities. Consequently, this leads women to seek alternative health management pathways, diverging from institutional care. For instance, one participant shared:

I went to the clinic several times, but the doctor didn’t really listen to me. They kept prescribing the same medications over and over again. I spent a lot of money, but instead of getting better, I felt worse.

(Woman, 42 years old)

Reliance on the healing power of plants for disease treatment: This code highlights women’s confidence in and application of medicinal plants for managing illnesses and alleviating physical discomfort. These practices are often informed by inherited knowledge and accumulated practical wisdom. An example of employing herbal remedies for health concerns is illustrated below:

“For four years, I struggled to conceive and tried different treatments without success. Eventually, based on others’ advice, I turned to herbal remedies. I followed the prescribed plants carefully, and after one course, the treatment worked. Since then, I have trusted herbal medicine and recommended it to others”. (37‑year‑old pregnant woman)

Resort to traditional healing methods: This code refers to women’s engagement with established, traditional therapeutic practices deeply rooted in local and cultural knowledge systems for addressing physical ailments. Participants mentioned various methods, including hijāmat (wet cupping), bādkesh (dry cupping), zālū-therapy (leech therapy), shekasteh-bandi (traditional bone-setting), and tol-giri or bandi (a traditional technique for dislodging perceived obstructions, often in the throat, typically involving manual manipulation or directed exhalation). The following quote demonstrates the recourse to these traditional healing modalities:

My feet always burn; they get so hot at night. Everyone says it’s because of my liver. I’m planning to get Hijama done soon. Every time I have gotten cupping, I’ve felt much better, and that burning sensation goes away for a while. It’s the best way to clean the blood. (68-year-old woman)

Dependence on informal sources of health information: This code reflects women’s reliance on non-formal, everyday channels for acquiring health-related knowledge. This includes information obtained from family, friends, neighbors, and community networks, which then informs their health decisions and practices. The subsequent statement exemplifies this reliance on informal information streams:

While shopping, I spoke with another woman who had diabetes. She told me about a well‑known apothecary who prescribed herbal medicine for lowering blood sugar. I followed her advice and went there, and since then I have continued using that treatment. (63‑year‑old woman with diabetes)

Seeking relief through tobacco use: This code reflects women’s use of tobacco or other substances, including cigarettes, hookah, and opium, as immediate and accessible means to alleviate physical discomfort in situations where other coping or treatment options are perceived as unavailable. The following quote illustrates the use of opium for temporary pain management:

When the pain becomes unbearable, and there is nothing else I can do, I sometimes use a little opium or smoke a hookah. It helps my body relax and makes the pain easier to tolerate, at least for a while. (43-year-old woman)

Choosing treatment based on personal beliefs: This code highlights how women’s health-related decisions are often guided by their personal beliefs and their individual interpretations of health and illness. The following statement illustrates how personal convictions can influence their engagement with formal medical care:

I don’t believe in routine checkups. These things only make you start looking for illness. I prefer not to know. I’ve never had a checkup, and unless symptoms show themselves, I won’t do anything. (Woman, 45 years old)

Second Intermediate category: Health within the sphere of spirituality

Second Intermediate category: Health within the sphere of spirituality

This intermediate category illuminates participants’ profound conviction that health, illness, and overall well-being are inextricably linked to spiritual forces, divine providence, and deeply ingrained religious belief systems. When confronted with acute or chronic illness, and also proactively for protection and prevention, women frequently integrate spiritual practices into their health-seeking behaviors. These spiritual approaches are often employed concurrently with, or sometimes in lieu of, conventional medical care. This category is manifested through four high-level codes:

Turning to vows as a means of healing: This code describes women’s practice of making vows (nazr) as a spiritual endeavor aimed at seeking healing or relief from illness. This practice is grounded in the belief that fulfilling a vow can elicit divine intervention and lead to improved health outcomes. The following quotation exemplifies participants’ use of vows for healing:

When my illness did not improve, I made a vow and asked God for healing. I promised that if I got better, I would prepare bread and salt and distribute it among people. (Woman, 50 years old)

Submitting to treatment under the divine will: This code refers to women’s acceptance of illness, treatment outcomes, and recovery as being ultimately determined by divine will. In this perspective, medical interventions are seen as complementary to, or at times secondary to, an overarching trust in God’s decisions. Within this framework, relinquishing control to a higher power provided reassurance and helped participants endure uncertainty and suffering. The subsequent quotation illustrates how participants conceptualize illness and healing as being subject to divine will:

In the end, everything depends on God. If He wills it, the medicine will work, and if not, there is nothing more we can do. I leave it in God’s hands.

(Woman, 48 years old)

Seeking healing at sacred thresholds: This code describes women’s practice of seeking healing by visiting sacred sites or thresholds, such as shrines, holy places, or spiritually significant locations. Within these spaces, they believe proximity to the sacred can facilitate relief, protection, or recovery from illness. The following quotation illustrates participants’ experiences of seeking healing at such sacred thresholds:

We went to the Holy Shrine1many times and sometimes stayed there until morning. I made vows and prayed, asking God and Imam Reza to heal my son, and I truly believed that his healing was in their hands. (48-year-old woman, mother of a sick child)

Spiritual restoration through ritual practices: This code pertains to women’s engagement in religious or spiritual rituals; including prayer, recitation of scriptures, lighting candles, or participating in devotional ceremonies, as methods for restoring inner peace, emotional equilibrium, and a sense of personal strength when confronting illness or distress. The following quotation exemplifies this point:

“I attend religious ceremonies, listen to the Quran, and take part in Quran study sessions. These give me a lot of peace. I really love Imam Hussein’s ceremonies because I feel that whatever problem I have, it gets resolved there. For example, when my eldest son became ill, I went to Imam Hussein’s ceremonies, lit a candle, prayed, and after that, he got better”. (Woman, 40 years old)

Third Intermediate category: Health besieged by inequalities

Third Intermediate category: Health besieged by inequalities

This intermediate category reflects how women’s health is continuously constrained by economic hardship, structural limitations, and unequal access to resources. This category manifests through five high-level codes:

Forced trust in the state health system: This code captures situations where women are compelled to rely on the state health system due to financial constraints and the absence of viable alternatives. This reliance persists despite prior negative experiences and dissatisfaction with the quality of care. In this context, trust is not volitional but imposed by structural and economic limitations. The following field observation illustrates this experience:

An elderly woman anxiously entered the healthcare room at a government‑run Comprehensive Health Service Center and asked whether her neighbors were right that the center’s services were no longer free?! She explained, “I can’t afford the high fees. If I had that much money, I would rather go to a private clinic that offers better care.” (78-year-old woman)

Self-medication as a substitute for costly doctor visits: This code captures participants’ adaptive strategy of resorting to self-medication, using over-the-counter drugs, old prescriptions, or seeking informal advice, as a direct response to the prohibitive costs associated with formal medical consultations, diagnostic tests, and specialized treatment. The following quotation illustrates how financial constraints frequently compel participants to opt for self-medication instead of formal medical care:

A woman came in once, insisting I give her a pill for her daughter’s stomach ache. I told her we need to know the cause of the pain. She replied she couldn’t be bothered to go to the doctor, spend a lot of money, and pay for a sonogram and all that. She just wanted a pill from me. I refused. (Pharmacy staff member)

Dental health sacrificed for livelihood pressures: This code highlights the forced deprioritization of dental care due to severe economic constraints, positioning it as an optional expense rather than an essential health need. As comprehensive dental treatment is largely uninsured and highly costly, women often manage chronic pain and infection through temporary, inexpensive methods, such as over-the-counter painkillers or home remedies. This leads to prolonged suffering, delayed necessary procedures, and eventual tooth loss. The following quotation illustrates how dental pain, though severe, is often managed with temporary relief methods because the financial burden of treatment is simply unsustainable for the household:

My tooth hurts a lot. The dentist said I need to fix it, but it costs one and a half million tomans. I can’t afford it; I just take painkillers at night. (Woman, 44 years old)

Vulnerability of sexual health within the context of poverty: This code describes how poverty undermines women’s sexual and reproductive health by limiting access to basic preventive and treatment services. Financial hardship, a lack of affordable care, and restricted health literacy reduce women’s ability to undergo routine screenings, seek timely treatment, or utilize family planning services. The following quote provides evidence of this:

If I ever get sick, the first thing I do is search the Internet to see what remedies are effective. If it’s an herbal medicine, I use that, or if it’s another type of medicine, I go and buy it. For instance, recently I had a spotting problem; I searched online, and it suggested a suppository. I don’t recall the name, but I texted my husband, and he bought it from the pharmacy. (Woman, 42 years old)

Predominance of work and livelihood over health care: Among women living in marginalized urban areas, healthcare is frequently postponed or avoided because work, income generation, and household survival take precedence. The following quotation illustrates this point:

4.2: Description of system relations

In Stage Four, the findings are examined through four analytically interconnected steps:

Historical, Economic, Social, Cultural, and Political Contextual Analysis

Historical, Economic, Social, Cultural, and Political Contextual Analysis

Field findings reveal that women’s health responsibility in marginalized urban settings is deeply influenced by long-term processes of migration, poverty, and urban marginalization. This pattern mirrors broader trajectories in Iran, initiated by mid-1960s land reforms, rural economic decline, and intensified rural-urban migration. These factors were later compounded by post-revolutionary economic centralization, war-related disruptions, recurrent droughts, and persistent regional inequalities [29, 30]. In Mashhad, a confluence of strong migration pull factors, coupled with inadequate urban planning and structural deprivation in surrounding provinces, has led to the concentration of low-income populations in peripheral neighborhoods and the expansion of informal settlements [31]. Consistent with our findings, prior research indicates that these areas were integrated into the city without a commensurate development of health infrastructure, resulting in persistently limited access to comprehensive health centers and qualified healthcare professionals compared to central districts [32].

Historical analyses of Mashhad’s healthcare system further confirm that service expansion has lagged behind rapid population growth and the specific needs of peripheral areas, thereby constraining the quality and accessibility of care for women [33–35]. Our study findings highlight economic hardship as a central and structuring condition of everyday life in the studied neighborhood. Urban poverty, male unemployment, women’s engagement in informal and seasonal labor, and the absence of social insurance collectively compel women to constantly negotiate between income generation and self-care, often to the detriment of their own health. This pattern aligns with evidence showing that household economies in marginalized areas are characterized by instability, informality, and low-paid employment, undermining long-term planning and constraining health-related decision-making [4, 30]. Empirical studies similarly report that economic deprivation limits access to adequate housing, nutritious food, education, and healthcare, leading women to postpone formal care, resort to self-medication, or seek treatment only in advanced stages of illness [36–38]. Echoing our findings, Gorji and Madahi (2024) conceptualize poverty as a structural force perpetuating health inequalities, rather than merely an individual condition [39]. Furthermore, Rafii et al. (2013) demonstrate that women’s health-promotion practices are organized around the “remainder of resources” after essential household needs are met, a logic that closely mirrors the prioritization patterns observed in this study [40].

Social conditions in the studied neighborhood are marked by chronic insecurity, instability, and cumulative daily pressures. Structural poverty, unstable employment, low literacy, and inadequate urban services perpetuate cycles of social exclusion, psychological distress, and diminished social cohesion [4, 41]. Previous research documents heightened exposure to crime, violence, and the erosion of social bonds in marginalized urban areas, conditions that further restrict women’s mobility and participation in health-promoting activities [41–43].

Consistent with these findings, Mohammadi et al. (2022) emphasize that social determinants of health significantly contribute to disparities between marginalized and non-marginal populations. Within these contexts, intersecting factors such as early and polygamous marriages, frequent pregnancies, limited literacy, and restricted decision-making authority intensify women’s vulnerability. Patriarchal family relations sustain women’s subordination and impede the development of agency in health-related decisions [44], a dynamic empirically supported by Ahmadi and Aghaei (2017), who found that low educational attainment and entrenched patriarchal structures significantly erode women’s capacity for informed health choices [45].

Researchers posit that the culture of informal settlements arises from an intermediate position between conflicting value systems: on one hand, the persistence of social ties and norms rooted in rural traditions, and on the other, a lack of full acceptance and integration into the new urban order and culture. In this context, the presence of vulnerable peer networks, alongside educational constraints, a deficit of cultural facilities and spaces, and limited employment opportunities, contributes to the perpetuation of a culture characterized by poverty, deviance, and insecurity [46].

In this setting, social order is governed less by formal regulations and more by informal norms, collective judgments, and concepts such as “honor” and “what people will say.” These mechanisms disproportionately affect women, restricting their social participation, spatial mobility, and decision-making power. Consequently, the home often becomes the safest and most socially legitimate space for women.

The proximity of these neighborhoods to the Shrine of Imam Reza (AS) and the pilgrimage-centric nature of Mashhad significantly shape residents’ everyday lifeworlds. Religious beliefs, vows, rituals, and moral expectations are deeply interwoven into daily life. Concurrently, internal migration and diverse cultural backgrounds have fostered a heterogeneous and multicultural social fabric, exposing women to overlapping traditional, religious, and urban expectations, thereby intensifying moral and normative pressures on their bodies and behaviors.

Within this cultural context, poverty, illness, and suffering are frequently interpreted through notions of “destiny” and “God’s will.” Indigenous healthcare practices, including the use of herbal remedies, infusions, and home-based treatments, are widespread among women and function as experiential knowledge passed down through generations. These practices serve as strategies for pain management, anxiety reduction, and illness control amidst everyday insecurity; however, they may also contribute to the normalization of suffering and delays in seeking formal healthcare, particularly concerning women’s mental health.

From a gender-relations perspective, neighborhood culture is structured patriarchally. This reflects the internalization of the normative order and women’s unwitting participation in perpetuating gendered restrictions. Within this cultural order, women’s bodies are often shrouded in silence, shame, and taboo; sexual health education remains limited, and issues such as abortion, recurrent infections, unintended pregnancies, and psychological distress tend to remain hidden.

At the political level, macro-structural decisions critically influence the production and sustainment of health inequalities in urban peripheries. Resource allocation, zoning policies, and patterns of public service provision systematically disadvantage marginalized populations [31]. Empirical evidence indicates that weak gender‑sensitive policies, limited programs addressing women’s broader health needs, and poor cross‑sectoral coordination, particularly in noncommunicable disease management, reflect structural deficiencies in health governance [43].

Although national policies formally recognize health as a universal right, practical access hinges on administrative visibility, possession of national identification codes, legal tenancy, and formal employment records. Women lacking such documentation are excluded from insurance coverage, follow-up programs, and emergency support, leading to structural neglect in peripheral districts. In this regard, Azari and Bahrami (2020) highlight that health policies narrowly prioritize maternal and reproductive care while neglecting women’s broader well-being, thereby reducing the right to health to a limited and fragmented institutional framework [47].

Comparison of Cultural Forms

Comparison of Cultural Forms

Carspecken (2001) suggests that researchers should relate identified cultural forms to other forms present in different social sites, typically by examining areas repeatedly referenced by study participants [48]. Spatial stratification between Mashhad’s central and peripheral areas has engendered two distinct patterns of health responsibility. Women in central districts generally possess more stable access to education, income, and institutional resources, shaping their approach to personal health. In practice, this often translates into greater confidence in navigating medical systems and more consistent engagement with health-promoting activities. Preventive care, such as regular checkups, diagnostic tests, gynecological and dental visits, or participation in screening programs, is typically viewed as a normal aspect of daily life. This orientation toward prevention and follow-up is not merely a matter of personal motivation; it is supported by broader conditions that render medical services accessible, understandable, and trustworthy [49]. Overall, this pattern represents a form of health responsibility focused on prevention, continuity of care, and long-term well-being—an approach that remains structurally challenging for women in marginalized settlements due to persistent social, economic, and institutional constraints.

The Role of Media and Cultural Commodities

The Role of Media and Cultural Commodities

Following the comparison and analysis of similar cultural sites, Carspecken (1996) recommends careful attention to the direct and indirect roles of media and cultural commodities in shaping emergent concepts. Even a single cultural product can alter relationship structures and dominant discourses within a study site, influencing prevailing values and norms [20]. Media and other cultural goods play a dual and contradictory role in shaping women’s health discourses in marginalized urban areas. On one hand, media, particularly social networks, serve as accessible sources of health information, education, and peer support. Digital platforms enable women to learn about home remedies, traditional treatments, and medications, often relying on online recommendations and virtual herbal stores, fostering a sense of agency and self-reliance.

On the other hand, the circulation of misinformation and unscientific health advice poses significant risks to women’s physical and mental health. More critically, this media-driven form of health responsibility often reflects structural constraints rather than genuine empowerment, as it individualizes health management and shifts responsibility from institutions to women themselves. Consequently, media simultaneously expands access to information and shared experiences while reinforcing a self-control model of health that obscures underlying social, economic, and institutional inequalities.

Consequences of cultural forms for broader social networks

The subsequent step in systemic analysis, according to Carspecken’s approach (2001), involves examining how the consequences of identified cultural forms impact broader social networks and contexts [48]. Cultural practices in urban margins both reflect and reproduce structural economic, spatial, and gender inequalities. Structural poverty cultivates a durable habitus geared towards adaptive strategies, such as informal work, reliance on kin, and low-cost cultural consumption, which perpetuate inequality across generations [50]. Weak horizontal social capital and the dominance of vertical kin ties limit collective action and claims-making, thus reinforcing isolation [51]. Processes of spatial-social entrapment solidify negative representations of marginalized neighborhoods and deepen the symbolic and material distance from the urban core [52]. Within these constraints, women often engage in patriarchal bargains to secure minimal protection, thereby reproducing gendered power relations [53], while patterns of popular cultural consumption and political silence contribute to symbolic exclusion and the persistence of control-oriented urban governance [54].

5.2: Theoretical interpretation using macro social theories

This study employs the macro-theoretical perspectives of Pierre Bourdieu and Michel Foucault to analyze the findings. These frameworks, applied in conjunction, offer a comprehensive understanding of how everyday health practices are shaped by and embedded within unequal social structures and power relations [10, 13, 55].

Pierre Bourdieu’s theory provides an analytical lens for understanding the production of behaviors, lifestyles, and social inequalities through the interplay of objective social structures and individuals’ internalized dispositions [10]. Concepts such as habitus, capital, and field serve as theoretical tools to decipher how social inequalities, including those pertaining to health, become institutionalized and perpetuate over time [11, 55]. Bourdieu’s framework is particularly powerful for analyzing findings related to social reproduction.

Michel Foucault’s theoretical perspective complements this analysis by focusing on power, knowledge, and the governance of bodies. His concept of biopower illuminates how modern institutions, notably healthcare systems, regulate populations through norms, classifications, and expert knowledge [13]. From a Foucauldian viewpoint, daily health practices are molded by dominant “regimes of truth” that define what constitutes normal, healthy, or risky. Consequently, integrating Foucault’s theory enables this study to capture the operation of power through medical knowledge and health discourses, especially in marginalized contexts characterized by persistent inequality.

From a Bourdieusian standpoint, the findings reveal that women’s health practices are produced within a marginalized health field, structured by uneven power dynamics and disparities in access to economic, cultural, and symbolic capital. Within this field, health maintenance emerges as a necessity-driven social practice, shaped by chronic poverty, precarious livelihoods, institutional exclusion, and a cultural distance from biomedical systems. Women’s everyday health practices thus reflect a habitus oriented towards endurance, pragmatism, and survival, rather than prioritizing prevention and long-term medical planning.

Limited economic capital critically shapes women’s health trajectories. Repeated encounters with unaffordable medical costs, ineffective treatments, and a lack of tangible improvements discourage consistent engagement with formal healthcare. The reliance on the state health system, as exemplified by an elderly woman’s anxiety about service costs, illustrates a structurally imposed dependency rather than trust. Similarly, the sacrifice of dental health, as seen in a woman managing severe tooth pain with painkillers due to cost, demonstrates how economic scarcity prioritizes immediate survival over necessary care. Within this habitus of necessity, health decisions are governed by affordability and immediacy, superseding biomedical ideals of prevention.

Restricted cultural capital further influences women’s interaction with health systems. Many participants utilized embodied experience, inherited knowledge, informal advice, and internet searches instead of relying solely on biomedical explanations. Self-medication, observed among participants and noted by pharmacy staff, reflects a culturally patterned response to the perceived inaccessibility, cost, and symbolic distance of institutional medicine.

In settings with limited economic and cultural capital, alternative health practices gain significant symbolic capital and local legitimacy. Traditional healing methods, herbal remedies, informal consultations, and community-based knowledge are not regarded as inferior but as credible and effective forms of care. Likewise, spiritual practices, such as making vows, entrusting outcomes to divine will, seeking healing at sacred sites, and engaging in religious rituals, function as potent symbolic resources. These practices empower women to reclaim agency, restore emotional equilibrium, and find meaning amidst uncertainty. From a Bourdieusian perspective, spirituality represents a socially recognized form of symbolic and emotional capital, offering a compensatory mechanism for exclusion from the formal health field.

Collectively, the findings indicate that women’s health practices are socially constructed within a marginal health field marked by chronic inequality. Limited economic capital restricts access to formal care, restricted cultural capital creates a distance from biomedical logic, and symbolic capital legitimizes traditional, informal, and spiritual practices. These forms of capital interact to produce a habitus geared towards endurance, adaptability, and culturally meaningful coping strategies. Consequently, from a Bourdieusian viewpoint, maintaining women’s health amidst inequality is best understood as a collective, adaptive process, profoundly shaped by structural constraints and reproduced through daily practices.

A Foucauldian interpretation suggests that women’s health is organized within regimes of biopower and everyday governmentality, wherein the state and formal health systems subtly shift the burden of care from institutions to individuals. Practices such as compelled reliance on public health services, self-medication, and the deferral of essential dental and sexual healthcare can be interpreted as forms of self-regulation emerging under conditions of economic scarcity and institutional inadequacy. In this milieu, medical knowledge functions as an authoritative yet often inaccessible technology of power, rendering women’s bodies sites of individualized management and discipline. Women are thus compelled to compensate for deficiencies in formal care through avenues such as internet searches, over-the-counter medications, enduring pain, or recourse to spiritual resources.

Typology of power

Grounded in a critical ethnographic approach and drawing upon Carspecken’s (2012) typology of power relations, this study explicitly engages with the diverse forms of power operative in the field. Power is systematically examined at two distinct levels: micro-level, analyzing normative, coercive, contractual, and charismatic forms of power, and macro-level, investigating economic, political, and cultural dimensions of power. This layered examination elucidates how women’s health responsibility is structured, negotiated, and reproduced across both intimate, everyday interactions and broader institutional arrangements.

The study’s findings indicate that the health-oriented and health-avoiding behaviors of marginalized women are shaped within an intricate network of interacting micro-level powers. Macro-level power relations

Macro-level power, conceptualized by Carspecken (2012) into cultural, economic, and political forms, operates in this study through intertwined and mutually reinforcing dynamics within the lifeworld of marginalized women in Mashhad. Cultural Power: Manifests by normalizing and moralizing structural limitations. Limitations

This ethnographic inquiry, while deeply contextual, was confined to a single neighborhood within Mashhad, Iran. This geographic specificity inherently limits the direct transferability of our findings to diverse settings. However, our sustained engagement yielded profound, micro-level insights into the intricate ways inequality shapes women’s health behaviors. Future research extending to multiple marginalized districts across various Iranian cities is recommended to elucidate the influence of regional and cultural specificities on the development of health responsibility.

Conclusion

This critical ethnographic study elucidates that women’s health responsibility within Mashhad’s marginalized communities is not a matter of individual volition but a socially structured practice. Daily health behaviors, encompassing the use of herbal remedies, faith-based healing, and self-medication, emerge as adaptive strategies in the face of enduring inequalities. Framed by Carspecken’s critical lens, these actions underscore the confluence of structural deprivation, gendered hierarchies, and cultural beliefs in shaping the very notion of health responsibility for women. Consequently, health responsibility becomes an arena where dominance is internalized via culturally endorsed practices of endurance, piety, and resilience. By reconceptualizing health responsibility as a relational and structural phenomenon rather than an isolated individual attribute, this study underscores the imperative for public health systems to pivot from advocating self-care moralities towards addressing fundamental structural determinants. These include poverty, precarious housing, gender-based violence, and disparities in healthcare access. Ultimately, advancing health equity necessitates a systemic redistribution of responsibility, shifting the burden from vulnerable women to the social and institutional frameworks tasked with ensuring collective well-being.
