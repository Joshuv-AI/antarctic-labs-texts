Pancreatic metastasis from clear-cell renal cell carcinoma causing extrinsic left ventricular compression: A rare case report
Authors: Md. Deluwar Hussen, Sayema Begum Jahra, Fatematuj Johara, Rifat Ara, Farhana Zaman Kanon, Musfika Mostaque
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Renal cell carcinoma is the most common renal malignancy in elderly people. It is well known for its unpredictable metastatic potential in different sites of the body, even many years after curative nephrectomy. Metastatic spread to the pancreas, accompanied by extrinsic compression of the left ventricle, is extremely rare and a late presentation. We report the case of a 68-year-old man who presented with progressively worsening shortness of breath on exertion, early satiety, and occasional abdominal discomfort. He had a history of left-sided radical nephrectomy for clear cell RCC performed 5 years earlier. An initial transthoracic echocardiogram revealed normal cardiac chambers, but there was an extrinsic compression on the LV wall. A contrast-enhanced computed tomography of the chest and abdomen was conducted, which showed a hyperenhancing mass at the head of the pancreas, and the lesion extended superiorly, exerting pressure on the inferior wall of the LV. The diagnosis of metastatic clear cell RCC was confirmed using endoscopic ultrasound-guided fine needle aspiration of the pancreatic lesion. Metastatic RCC is still challenging due to its late recurrence and atypical metastatic site. Further research and extended surveillance should be considered in long-term survivors to optimize early detection of metastasis.

Introduction

RCC represents approximately 4% of all adult malignancies. RCC is more common in males than females, with the average age of diagnosis between 65 and 74 years [1]. RCC arises from the renal cortex and is the most common primary renal neoplasm (80%) [2]. There are different histological subtypes of RCC, in which clear cell carcinoma is the most predominant and has a high propensity for metastasis [3]. The most common sites for RCC metastasis are lungs, liver, lymph nodes, and bone, while unusual sites include the pancreas, thyroid, skin, and skeletal muscle [2]. Recurrence of RCC usually occurs in most patients within 5 years of curative nephrectomy, but a small subset of patients experiences late recurrence more than 5 years after initial treatment [4]. Most cases of RCC present with nonspecific symptoms, with 50% of renal masses found incidentally, 10% presenting with the classic triad, and 20% to 30% presenting with distant metastasis [5]. Pancreatic metastasis occurs in 4.5% of cases, and external compression of the LV is extremely rare. Histologically, metastatic carcinoma is considered fatal with a very poor outcome for most cancers. In some selected asymptomatic patients with metastatic RCC to the pancreas, surgery is likely to offer a sustainable long-term outcome. Modern treatment options like novel systemic and targeted therapy have resulted in excellent response with long-term survival in most patients [6]. Here we report a case of late recurrence of clear cell RCC with pancreatic metastasis and mass effect on the LV, emphasizing the significance of considering unusual presentations in long-term survival.

Case presentation

The 68-year-old man presented with a 2-month history of progressive worsening shortness of breath on exertion, early satiety, and occasional discomfort in the upper abdomen. His medical history was significant for a left-sided radical nephrectomy performed 5 years earlier for clear cell RCC. He was on regular follow-up. On general physical examination, the patient was mildly anemic, pulse 102 beats/min, BP 125/85 mmHg, respiratory rate 16 breaths/min, and other findings were within normal limits. On cardiovascular examination, there was no murmur or sign of heart failure. Abdominal examination revealed an old surgical scar mark at the left flank, and mild tenderness was present in the epigastric region on deep palpation.

The pancreatic lesion was located at the head of the pancreas with superior extension abutting the diaphragm and pericardial surface, resulting in extrinsic compression of the inferior wall of the left ventricle without evidence of myocardial invasion. The pancreatic mass is now indicated using blue arrowheads, while the area of left ventricular compression is separately marked using yellow arrowheads. Due to the hypervascular appearance of the lesion, pancreatic metastasis from RCC was suspected while pancreatic neuroendocrine tumor remained a differential consideration. To establish a definitive diagnosis, an endoscopic ultrasound-guided fine needle aspiration of the pancreatic lesion was performed, which confirmed metastatic clear-cell RCC. Endoscopic ultrasound-guided fine needle aspiration of the pancreatic lesion revealed clusters of atypical polygonal cells with abundant clear cytoplasm and prominent vascularity, consistent with metastatic clear-cell RCC. Immunohistochemical staining showed positivity for PAX8, CD10, and vimentin, while chromogranin A and synaptophysin were negative, excluding pancreatic neuroendocrine tumor. An MRI was advised for detailed anatomical assessment, and the patient was referred to a multidisciplinary team comprising cardiology, oncology, and surgical specialists for coordinated management planning. At 3-month follow-up, the patient remained clinically stable with mild improvement in dyspnea and early satiety. Follow-up imaging showed stable disease without progression of left ventricular compression, and the patient continued multidisciplinary follow-up for targeted systemic therapy.

Discussion

Pancreatic metastasis of renal cell carcinoma remains a rare tumor and accounts for only 2% to 5% of all pancreatic malignancies, and external compression of the left ventricle is exceedingly rare [7]. Multiple genetic and acquired risk factors, along with their pathophysiologic mechanisms, are associated with RCC. Acquired risk factors for RCC include smoking, hypertension, obesity, chronic analgesic use, and diabetes [8]. RCC is known for its diverse patterns of presentation and its tendency to metastasize to different parts of the body. Pancreatic metastases occur through a hematogenous route that involves parallel venous drainage from the primary RCC lesion or lymphatic spread via retroperitoneal lymph nodes. Direct seeding to the pancreas from RCC is extremely uncommon [9]. Pancreatic metastases exhibit slow growth. Patients typically remain asymptomatic if the metastatic lesion from RCC is small and isolated. In contrast, a large tumor may present with symptoms of abdominal pain, early satiety, and weight loss. It is possible for RCC to metachronously spread to the pancreas up to 30 years after the diagnosis of the original primary tumor. In our patient, pancreatic metastasis developed within 5 years of left radical nephrectomy, presenting with vague abdominal discomfort, early satiety, and shortness of breath due to mass effect on the left ventricle [9,10].

When a pancreatic tumor is detected, it is crucial to differentiate whether the tumor is primary pancreatic or metastatic. PET-CT, MRI, and endoscopic ultrasound are currently used as standard tests for early detection of metastatic disease. The most commonly observed solid pancreatic tumor is pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma and is usually visualized as a hypovascular tumor on contrast CT scan. On the other hand, pancreatic metastasis typically demonstrates a well-defined margin and greater enhancement. Due to the hypervascular appearance and overlapping imaging features of PmRCC and PNET, it is often difficult to differentiate on CT scan, and EUS-FNA is used for diagnostic purposes to identify the histologic type and origin of pancreatic metastasis [11].

In our case, the initial diagnosis was made by contrast CT scan of the chest and abdomen, which revealed a hyperenhancing tumor at the head of the pancreas and its superior extension to the LV wall. EUS-FNA confirmed the diagnosis of metastatic clear cell RCC.

Treatment options include surgical resection, molecular targeted therapy, and radiotherapy. Ma et al. reported that surgical resection might be an ideal option for PmRCC with oligometastatic disease, which showed a 5-year survival rate of 13% with untreated metastatic RCC compared to 65% after surgical resection. In the recent decade, molecular targeted therapy including tyrosine kinase inhibitors, mammalian target of rapamycin inhibitors, and immune checkpoint inhibitors such as programmed death-1 inhibitor has introduced a new therapeutic approach in the treatment of PmRCC [11,12]. Because the pancreas may still be the site of metastasis even many years after radical nephrectomy, patients need to be kept under close supervision. Therefore, the most suitable therapeutic approach for these patients is surgical removal of the metastatic tumor, along with the use of tyrosine kinase inhibitors. Our patient was referred to a multidisciplinary center for collaborative management planning.

Conclusion

Pancreatic metastasis of RCC with left ventricular compression is a rare clinical presentation that should be considered in RCC survivors who develop unexplained cardiac or gastrointestinal symptoms. Careful imaging evaluation and histologic confirmation are essential for accurate diagnosis and appropriate management.
