Changing prognostic significance of peritoneal transport status in contemporary incident peritoneal dialysis patients
Authors: Ho-Joong Yoon, Sion Lee, Ji-Hwan Kim, Sung-Gyun Kim, Jwa-Kyung Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundHigh peritoneal transport status has traditionally been linked to adverse outcomes in peritoneal dialysis (PD). However, its clinical relevance in contemporary, individualized PD practice remains unclear. We therefore evaluated its association with long-term outcomes across three successive treatment eras.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study of 473 incident PD patients treated between 2000 and 2024, grouped into three treatment eras reflecting major changes in PD practice. Baseline peritoneal transport status was defined by the 4-h dialysate-to-plasma creatinine ratio and categorized as high or non-high. The primary outcome was technique failure. Fine-Gray competing-risk models were used to assess technique failure and all-cause mortality, including transport-by-era interaction terms.ResultsOf these, 114 (24.1%) were classified as high transporters. In the earliest era, high transport status was associated with nearly two-fold higher crude technique failure rates than non-high status, but this difference narrowed over time and was minimal in the most recent era. In fully adjusted competing-risk models, high transport status was not independently associated with technique failure; instead, more recent treatment era and higher serum albumin were associated with a lower risk of technique failure. A significant transport-by-era interaction suggested attenuation of the adverse prognostic impact of high transport status in contemporary practice (P for interaction = .044). More recent treatment era was also associated with lower all-cause mortality, whereas the transport-by-era interaction was not significant for mortality.ConclusionThe prognostic significance of high peritoneal transport status appears to be era-dependent and less pronounced in contemporary PD practice. These findings suggest that PET-derived transport status may be better interpreted as a guide for individualized PD prescription rather than as a fixed predictor of adverse outcome.Keywords: competing-risk model, peritoneal dialysis, peritoneal equilibration test, peritoneal transport status, technique failure

INTRODUCTION

Over the past two decades, peritoneal dialysis (PD) practice has shifted from a clearance-centered model focused on fixed small-solute targets toward a physiology-guided, individualized treatment strategy [1]. Contemporary PD care increasingly prioritizes volume control, preservation of residual kidney function (RKF), use of biocompatible dialysis solutions, and patient-centered adequacy rather than strict adherence to fixed Kt/V thresholds. Alongside these conceptual changes, broader adoption of automated peritoneal dialysis (APD), increased use of icodextrin-based regimens, incremental PD strategies, and structured protocols for fluid and infection management have reshaped PD delivery and contributed to improved clinical outcomes [2, 3].

Within this modern paradigm, the role of peritoneal membrane assessment has also changed. The peritoneal equilibration test (PET), historically used to classify patients into transport categories associated with prognosis [4], is now increasingly regarded as a practical tool to individualize therapy [5]. Current International Society for Peritoneal Dialysis recommendations emphasize using PET to guide prescription decisions, such as dwell-time adjustment, modality selection, and osmotic agent choice, rather than to label patients as inherently high-risk or unsuitable for PD [5].

Much of the historical evidence linking high peritoneal transport status to adverse outcomes was generated in earlier treatment environments dominated by continuous ambulatory PD (CAPD), when strategies to mitigate rapid solute transport, such as shorter dwell cycles, nocturnal APD, and alternative osmotic agents, were less widely available. Under those conditions, high solute transport was associated with rapid dissipation of the osmotic gradient, impaired ultrafiltration, and higher risks of technique failure and mortality. Whether these associations retain the same clinical relevance in contemporary PD practice, where therapy can be adapted to membrane physiology, remains uncertain [6–10].

As PD management and therapeutic options have evolved, the prognostic meaning of PET-defined transport status may also have changed. Previous pivotal studies, including a large-scale cohort analysis and a comprehensive meta-analysis reported associations between higher peritoneal transport status and adverse clinical outcomes such as all-cause mortality, technique failure, and hospitalization rates [11, 12]. However, these studies primarily established the overall prognostic value of transport characteristics and did not specifically examine whether these associations varied across successive eras of PD care.

We therefore investigated a large single-center cohort of incident PD patients spanning more than two decades to determine whether the association between high peritoneal transport status and technique failure differed over time. By evaluating outcomes across treatment eras using competing-risk methods, we sought to clarify the contemporary clinical relevance of PET-derived transport status.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study subjects

We conducted a retrospective cohort study of incident PD patients who initiated PD between 1 January 2000, and 31 December 2024. All clinical and laboratory data were obtained from the hospital electronic medical record system. The date of PD initiation was defined as the index date for all analyses.

Eligible patients were adults aged ≥20 years who started long-term PD during the study period and remained on PD for at least 3 months, as the first PET was routinely performed at ∼16 ± 4 weeks after PD initiation. Patients were excluded if they received PD for acute kidney injury only, discontinued PD or died within 3 months of initiation (n = 5), had a history of kidney transplantation or maintenance hemodialysis, initiated long-term PD at another facility, did not undergo a baseline PET within the predefined time window, or had missing data for key baseline variables such as serum albumin. After applying these criteria, a total of 473 patients were included in the final analysis.

To capture secular changes in PD practice, patients were categorized into three treatment eras according to the calendar year of PD initiation: Era 1 (2000–07), Era 2 (2008–15), and Era 3 (2016–24). These cut points were chosen pragmatically to divide the long study period into three clinically interpretable calendar periods while maintaining adequate sample size within each era. Although the eras were not defined by the introduction of any single intervention, they broadly reflected the cumulative evolution of PD practice at our center over time, including greater use of APD, expanded icodextrin prescription, and more contemporary individualized PD care.

Baseline data at PD initiation included age, sex, underlying kidney disease, comorbidities including diabetes mellitus and coronary artery disease (CAD), blood pressure, body mass index (BMI), laboratory parameters [serum creatinine, phosphorus, albumin, and C-reactive protein (CRP), RKF (ml/day of urine output)], PD modality, and icodextrin use. RKF was estimated using 24-h urine volume collected at the time of baseline PET. Overall, 387 patients (∼82%) completed a 24-h urine collection. The study protocol was approved by the local Institutional Review Board, and all procedures were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. As this was a retrospective analysis of prospectively collected clinical data, the requirement for written informed consent was waived.

Assessment of peritoneal transport status

Peritoneal membrane transport characteristics were assessed using a modified PET performed with 4.25% dextrose dialysis solution. The first PET was scheduled at ∼16 ± 4 weeks after PD initiation. After an overnight drain, 2.0 lof 4.25% dextrose solution was infused into the peritoneal cavity, and dialysate samples were collected at 0 and 4 h, with a concomitant blood sample at 4 h. The D/P creatinine ratio at 4 h (4-h D/P creatinine) was calculated with a cutoff of ≥0.81 defining high transport status. Patients with high-average, low-average, or low transport status were grouped together as the non-high transport category. PET-derived transport status was treated as a baseline, time-fixed exposure for all subsequent analyses.

Outcome and follow-up

The primary outcome was technique failure, defined as permanent transfer to hemodialysis lasting at least 3 months. Temporary switches to hemodialysis of shorter duration were not considered technique failure. Death and kidney transplantation were treated as competing events. Patients were censored at transfer to another facility, loss to follow-up, or at the end of follow-up on 31 December 2024. All-cause mortality was additionally evaluated as a secondary outcome.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range), and categorical variables as counts and percentages. Baseline characteristics were compared between high and non–high transport groups, and across treatment eras, using Student’s t–test or one–way analysis of variance for continuous variables and the χ² test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables, as appropriate. Crude technique failure rates were calculated as the number of events per 100 patient–years of follow–up within each transport group and era.

Time–to–event analyses for technique failure accounted for the competing risks of death and kidney transplantation. We used Fine-Gray subdistribution hazard models to estimate subdistribution hazard ratios (sHRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for technique failure according to baseline peritoneal transport status and treatment era. Three sequential models were constructed. Model 1 included transport status (high vs. non–high) and treatment era only. Model 2 further adjusted for age, sex, and diabetes mellitus. Model 3 additionally adjusted for serum albumin, CRP, and incorporated interaction terms between high transport status and treatment era (High × Era 2 and High × Era 3) to assess potential effect modification by era. The overall P–value for interaction was derived from the joint significance of the interaction terms.

In a prespecified sensitivity analysis, treatment era was additionally modeled as a continuous variable to assess linear trends in technique failure risk and potential effect modification by high transport status. Additional sensitivity analyses further adjusted for baseline PD modality (APD vs. CAPD), icodextrin use, and 24-h urine volume. Because these additional covariates were not available in all patients, these sensitivity analyses were performed in a common complete-case cohort to allow comparison across sequentially adjusted models.

Death was analyzed using Fine-Gray competing-risk models with the same covariate structure as the primary models, treating technique failure and kidney transplantation as competing events. Follow-up for death analyses began at PD initiation and continued until death, technique failure, kidney transplantation, transfer to another facility, loss to follow-up, or the end of follow-up, whichever occurred first.

All Fine-Gray competing–risk analyses were performed using R (version 4.5.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) with the cmprsk package. Other statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS software (version 24.0; SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). All P–values were two–sided, and a P–value < .05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Baseline characteristics of incident PD patients

The study included 473 incident PD patients, of whom 114 (24.1%) were classified as high transporters at baseline (Table 1). The mean age was 53.2 ± 12.2 years and 64.1% were male. Compared with non-high transporters, high transporters were older and had a higher prevalence of diabetes mellitus and CAD. They also had lower serum blood urea nitrogen, creatinine, phosphate, and albumin levels and higher CRP concentrations. RKF was similar between groups. Icodextrin use and CCPD were more frequent among high transporters. The number of incident PD patients increased across treatment eras (Era 1: 101; Era 2: 155; Era 3: 217).

Secular trends in transport distribution and PD practice

The proportion of high transporters remained relatively stable across eras (22.8%, 23.9%, and 24.9% in Era 1, 2, and 3, respectively) (Table 2 and Supplementary Table S1). When the four conventional PET categories were examined, the proportion of high-average transporters became more common over time (25.7%, 29.0%, and 41.5%), whereas the proportion of low transporters decreased (24.8%, 18.1%, and 11.1%). Across all eras, high transporters were consistently older, more likely to have diabetes and CAD, and had lower albumin but higher CRP levels than non-high transporters (Table 2). PD practice patterns also changed over time (Supplemental Table S2). Icodextrin use increased substantially over time (10.8% in Era 1 to 35.5% in Era 3; P < .001), and in recent eras, its use was no longer confined to high transporters. When PD modality was reclassified as CAPD versus APD, CAPD remained the predominant modality throughout the study period. However, among high transporters, APD use increased numerically from 17.4% in Era 1 to 21.6% in Era 2 and 27.8% in Era 3. Baseline Kt/V data according to treatment era and baseline transport status are also presented in Supplementary Table S3 for the subgroup with available data.

Crude clinical outcomes across treatment eras

During a median follow-up of 36 months, 140 patients experienced technique failure, 82 died, and 87 underwent kidney transplantation (Table 3 ). Technique failure rates declined progressively across eras. In Era 1, high transporters had nearly double the technique failure rate compared with non-high transporters (18.6 vs. 9.1 per 100 patient-years). This gap narrowed in Era 2 (9.4 vs. 7.8 per 100 patient-years) and became minimal in Era 3 (6.7 vs. 5.4 per 100 patient-years), with similar crude cumulative incidences between groups (18.5% vs. 19.6%). In parallel, mortality rates decreased and transplantation rates increased in later eras, suggesting overall improvements in PD management and patient outcomes.

Technique failure across treatment eras

Figure 1 shows the cumulative incidence function curves for technique failure according to transport status within each treatment era. Clear separation between transport groups was observed in Era 1, modest differences in Era 2, and largely overlapping curves in Era 3, consistent with attenuation of the adverse prognostic impact of high transport status in more recent treatment eras.

In Fine-Gray competing-risk models accounting for death and kidney transplantation as competing events, high transport status showed a higher point estimate for technique failure in minimally adjusted analyses (Model 1 sHR 2.00, 95% CI 0.99–5.09), but this association attenuated after adjustment for age, sex, and diabetes mellitus (Model 2 sHR 2.05, 95% CI 0.80–5.20) and was no longer statistically significant in the fully adjusted Model 3 (sHR 1.86, 95% CI 0.60–5.79).

In contrast, the treatment era remained independently associated with technique failure risk. Compared with Era 1, initiation of PD in Era 3 was associated with a significantly lower risk of technique failure in the fully adjusted model (Model 3 sHR 0.24, 95% CI 0.06–0.80). Interaction analyses suggested that the association between high transport status and technique failure differed across eras. Specifically, the High × Era 3 interaction term was significant (sHR 0.39, 95% CI 0.10–0.86), and the overall transport-by-era interaction was significant (P for interaction = .044), indicating that the excess risk associated with high transport status in earlier eras was attenuated in the contemporary PD era. Older age remained associated with higher risk, whereas higher serum albumin was associated with lower risk.

Sensitivity analysis

When treatment era was modeled as a continuous variable, the overall attenuation pattern remained directionally consistent. In this analysis, the interaction between high transport status and treatment era remained significant (High transport × Era sHR 0.53, 95% CI 0.28–0.98; P for interaction = .045), supporting attenuation of the adverse prognostic impact of high transport status over time (Supplementary Table S4). Additional sensitivity analyses further adjusting for baseline APD, icodextrin use, and 24-hour urine volume showed similar overall patterns. After adjustment for baseline APD and icodextrin use, the transport-by-era interaction was modestly attenuated (P for interaction = .057). Further adjustment for 24-h urine volume yielded similar results (P for interaction = .061). Although the interaction became borderline after these additional adjustments, the overall pattern remained directionally consistent, indicating that the findings were not fully explained by baseline PD modality, icodextrin use, or baseline urine volume alone (Supplementary Table S5).

Causes of technique failure

The distribution of technique failure causes varied by treatment era and baseline transport status (Supplementary Table S6). Infection-related causes, including peritonitis and exit-site/tunnel infection, accounted for a progressively smaller proportion of technique failure events in more recent eras. Among high transporters, non-infectious causes were more common, whereas infectious and non-infectious causes were more evenly distributed in non-high transporters. Notably, fluid overload/ultrafiltration failure was the most common cause of technique failure among high transporters and accounted for a greater proportion of events than among non-high transporters (25.0% vs. 12.5%).

Cumulative incidence of death across treatment eras

The cumulative incidence of death declined across treatment eras. In competing-risk analyses treating technique failure and kidney transplantation as competing events, more recent treatment era was associated with a significantly lower risk of death (sHR 0.54, 95% CI 0.36–0.80, P < .001). However, unlike technique failure, no significant transport-by-era interaction was observed for death (P for interaction = .853), suggesting no clear era-specific difference in the association between transport status and death (Supplemental Fig. S1).

DISCUSSION

The present study suggests that the clinical significance of peritoneal transport status may differ across treatment eras in the setting of changing PD practice. In this large single-center cohort spanning more than two decades, the excess risk of technique failure historically associated with high peritoneal transport appeared less pronounced in more recent eras and was no longer independently associated with technique failure in the fully adjusted model. The significant interaction between transport category and treatment era suggests that the association may vary according to treatment context with eras. Overall survival improved over time; however, this improvement did not appear to differ according to baseline transport phenotype.

Importantly, high transporters in our cohort exhibited a greater burden of comorbidity at PD initiation, including older age, diabetes, and CAD, as well as biochemical features consistent with inflammation and protein–energy wasting, such as lower serum albumin and higher CRP levels. These characteristics likely contributed substantially to the crude excess risk observed in earlier eras, when PD prescriptions were less adaptable to membrane physiology. After adjustment to these factors, transport status itself was no longer independently associated with technique failure, whereas serum albumin and age remained independently associated with outcome. Taken together, these findings suggest that the risks historically attributed to high transport may have reflected underlying patient vulnerability in addition to intrinsic membrane characteristics.

Earlier observational studies, including CANUSA and analyses from the Australian–New Zealand registry, consistently reported worse ultrafiltration, greater protein losses, and higher mortality among high transporters in predominantly CAPD-based programs [8, 9, 12–14]. The HR for overall mortality was reported to increase by 1.11 (95% CI 1.05, 1.17) for every 0.1 higher D/P creatinine ratio [10]. However, subsequent studies conducted in programs with broader APD use and contemporary prescription strategies have reported weaker or absent associations with technique survival, suggesting that membrane transport characteristics may not have uniform prognostic implications across different treatment settings [11, 15]. Our findings add to this literature by suggesting that the prognostic implications of transport status may be context-dependent and less pronounced in more contemporary PD practice.

Importantly, the pattern observed in this study does not imply disappearance of the underlying biological phenotype of high transport. High peritoneal solute transport rate (PSTR) may be present at dialysis initiation or develop over time with continued PD exposure, reflecting both baseline interindividual variability and acquired alterations related to chronic glucose exposure or peritoneal injury [13, 16, 17]. Mechanistically, high transport reflects an increased effective peritoneal vascular surface area driven in part by inflammation-related neovascularization and accelerated glucose absorption, processes that can compromise ultrafiltration if unmanaged [4, 18]. Local peritoneal inflammation has been closely linked to faster transport characteristics and may contribute to membrane remodeling and functional alterations [19–25]. In our cohort, the combined proportion of high and high-average transporters increased across treatment eras, indicating that contemporary PD programs continue to care for patients with faster membrane characteristics.

Over time, PD management has increasingly incorporated strategies specifically designed to address the physiological challenges associated with high transport status. These include greater use of APD, more frequent icodextrin prescription, and increasing emphasis on individualized PD care. Contemporary evidence suggests potential benefits of these strategies. For example, Sanchez et al. demonstrated that the combination of APD with icodextrin provided significant patient survival benefits (HR 0.499, 95% CI 0.322–0.803) [26]. Additional studies have similarly reported improved outcomes with APD and icodextrin use, particularly in high transporters [15, 27–29].

Similarly in our cohort, both APD adoption and icodextrin prescription increased progressively across treatment eras, especially among high transporters (Supplementary Table S2), which might partly explain the observed attenuation of risk. However, given the observational design of the present study, treatment era should be interpreted as a contextual marker reflecting multiple concurrent changes in PD practice and patient case-mix rather than as a direct causal factor. In addition, temporal differences in patient case-mix, including age and comorbidity burden, should also be acknowledged as a potential source of residual confounding in the era-based comparisons. In particular, in our study, patients in later eras were older and tended to have a greater comorbidity burden; therefore, the observed attenuation in technique-failure risk is unlikely to be explained simply by a shift toward a lower-risk population. Accordingly, no single change in practice can be assumed to fully account for the observed improvement in prognosis across eras. In this context, our findings suggest that the prognostic implications of PET-derived transport status may be less fixed than previously assumed and may be better interpreted in relation to individualized prescription rather than as a standalone marker of poor prognosis [30, 31].

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this was a single-center observational study, which may limit generalizability to other PD populations with different patient characteristics or practice patterns. Second, peritoneal transport status was assessed only at baseline and treated as a time-fixed exposure. Because longitudinal changes in membrane characteristics and time-varying treatment adaptations were not captured, exposure misclassification may have occurred, and we could not assess whether changes in transport status during follow-up influenced technique failure risk. Third, our study was restricted to patients who survived and remained on PD for at least 3 months to allow baseline PET assessment. Although only five patients were excluded due to early discontinuation or death, this design may have introduced survivor bias by excluding patients with the most severe early complications. Fourth, although competing-risk models were applied, residual confounding from unmeasured factors including socioeconomic status, treatment adherence, or center-level practices, cannot be excluded. In particular, the treatment era likely reflects not only evolving PD practice but also temporal differences in patient case-mix. Fifth, RKF was approximated using baseline 24-h urine volume rather than clearance-based GFR measures, and such measurements were not available in all patients. Therefore, incomplete characterization of RKF may have resulted in residual confounding. Finally, the number of events within some era-specific subgroups was modest, which may have limited statistical power to detect smaller interaction effects. Nevertheless, the consistency of findings across categorical and continuous era analyses, together with concordant patterns in cumulative incidence curves, supports the robustness of the observed temporal attenuation in the prognostic impact of high transport status.

In conclusion, the prognostic significance of high peritoneal transport status appears to be context-dependent and less pronounced in the era of contemporary PD care. Rather than serving as a fixed risk marker, PET-derived transport characteristics now function primarily as a tool for individualized prescription and treatment optimization. Our findings suggest that technique outcomes in contemporary practice may be influenced not only by membrane transport characteristics but also by patient-level factors such as nutritional status and comorbidity. Further multicenter studies incorporating detailed treatment patterns are warranted to clarify how evolving PD practices continue to modify the clinical implications of peritoneal transport physiology.
