The great pretender: Torsion of a giant intra-abdominal seminoma presenting as acute appendicitis—A rare diagnostic trap
Authors: Daizha Naorbe Delos Reyes, Victor D. Espino
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Acute appendicitis may mask rare surgical emergencies. Torsion of an intra-abdominal undescended testis with malignancy is exceptionally uncommon. A 27-year-old man presented with 3 days of right lower quadrant pain, vomiting, and anorexia. Examination showed peritonitis and an absent right testis. Laparotomy for presumed appendicitis revealed a normal appendix and an 11 × 13 × 12 cm intra-abdominal mass with a 540° torsed spermatic cord. Right radical orchiectomy was performed. Histopathology confirmed classical seminoma. Final stage was pT2N0M0S0 Stage IB. Giant torsed seminoma can mimic appendicitis; genital examination is essential.

Introduction

Acute appendicitis remains the most common indication for emergency abdominal surgery; however, its diagnostic predominance predisposes clinicians to anchoring bias, whereby uncommon but life-threatening conditions may be overlooked because they closely mimic a common surgical disease. Torsion of an intra-abdominal undescended testis is an exceptionally rare but clinically significant cause of acute abdomen that frequently presents with nonspecific abdominal pain, resulting in delayed diagnosis and treatment.

Cryptorchidism is the strongest established congenital risk factor for testicular germ cell tumors. Compared with the general population, patients with cryptorchidism have an approximately 3.7- to 7.5-fold increased lifetime risk of developing testicular cancer, with intra-abdominal testes carrying the greatest malignant potential because of prolonged exposure to elevated intra-abdominal temperature, impaired germ-cell maturation, and delayed clinical recognition. Although the absolute lifetime risk remains relatively low (approximately 2–5%), seminoma is the predominant histologic subtype arising in untreated post-pubertal undescended testes. Early orchiopexy substantially reduces—but does not eliminate—the subsequent risk of malignancy.

The coexistence of malignancy and torsion within an intra-abdominal testis is exceedingly uncommon and represents simultaneous ischemic and oncologic emergencies. We report a giant torsed intra-abdominal seminoma presenting as acute appendicitis and emphasize the importance of meticulous physical examination, avoidance of cognitive bias, and multidisciplinary intraoperative collaboration in achieving definitive oncologic management.

Case presentation

A 27-year-old man presented to the emergency department with a 3-day history of progressively worsening right lower quadrant abdominal pain associated with nausea, vomiting, and anorexia. He denied previous abdominal surgery or trauma. Physical examination demonstrated localized peritoneal signs over the right lower quadrant and an absent right testis. Laboratory investigations revealed leukocytosis (11.7 × 109/L), neutrophilia (81%), an elevated C-reactive protein level (16.56 mg/L), and an Alvarado score of 7, raising a high clinical suspicion for acute appendicitis.

Emergency exploratory laparotomy was undertaken. Intraoperatively, the appendix appeared grossly normal. Continued exploration revealed a giant intra-abdominal mass measuring 11 × 13 × 12 cm, with approximately 540° torsion of the spermatic cord originating from the deep inguinal ring (Fig. 1). Serum tumor markers were obtained before excision of the mass, demonstrating an alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) level of 2.21 ng/mL, a beta-human chorionic gonadotropin (β-hCG) level of 3.11 mIU/mL, and an elevated lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) level of 573.73 U/L.

A right radical orchiectomy was subsequently performed. Gross examination demonstrated a markedly enlarged hemorrhagic testicular tumor with an attached torsed spermatic cord and epididymis (Fig. 2). Histopathological examination confirmed classical seminoma. Microscopic evaluation demonstrated sheets of uniform polygonal tumor cells with abundant clear glycogen-rich cytoplasm, distinct cell borders, and centrally located nuclei containing conspicuous nucleoli. The tumor was arranged in poorly demarcated lobules separated by delicate fibrous septa infiltrated by dense lymphocytic aggregates, characteristic of classical seminoma. Tumor invasion into the lymphovascular spaces, rete testis, epididymis, and tunica albuginea was identified. No tumor involvement of the spermatic cord or proximal surgical resection margin was observed, confirming negative surgical margins (Fig. 4).Fig. 4Histopathologic examination (hematoxylin and eosin stain) demonstrating classical seminoma composed of sheets of uniform polygonal tumor cells with abundant clear cytoplasm separated by delicate fibrous septa containing dense lymphocytic infiltrates. Following surgery, serum tumor markers normalized (AFP 2.09 ng/mL, β-hCG 0.43 mIU/mL, and LDH 159.58 U/L). Contrast-enhanced computed tomography of the chest and abdomen demonstrated no evidence of regional lymph node enlargement or distant metastatic disease. Based on the postoperative normalization of serum tumor markers (S0), negative staging imaging (N0M0), and histopathologic findings, the final pathologic stage was pT2N0M0S0 (AJCC Stage IB). The patient was subsequently referred for active surveillance in accordance with contemporary guideline recommendations.

Discussion

This case illustrates an uncommon but clinically significant diagnostic pitfall in emergency surgery. Although acute appendicitis remains the most frequent indication for emergency laparotomy, uncommon surgical diseases may present with identical clinical manifestations, predisposing clinicians to diagnostic anchoring bias and delayed recognition.1 In the present patient, the most important missed diagnostic clue was the absent ipsilateral testis, emphasizing the importance of routine genital examination in every male presenting with acute abdomen.

Cryptorchidism is associated with impaired germ-cell maturation and represents the strongest congenital risk factor for testicular germ cell tumors. Compared with normally descended testes, cryptorchid testes demonstrate a 3.7- to 7.5-fold increase in lifetime malignancy risk, with intra-abdominal testes carrying the highest malignant potential because of prolonged exposure to elevated intra-abdominal temperature.2˒3 Although the absolute lifetime risk remains approximately 2–5%, seminoma is the predominant histologic subtype observed in untreated post-pubertal patients.2˒3 Early orchiopexy significantly decreases—but does not eliminate—the subsequent risk of malignancy, underscoring the importance of early surgical correction.2

Torsion occurring in a malignant intra-abdominal testis represents the convergence of ischemic compromise and oncologic urgency. Review of previously reported cases (Table 1) demonstrates that most patients presented with acute abdominal pain requiring emergency surgery, highlighting both the rarity of this condition and the considerable diagnostic challenge it poses.4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10 Unlike previously reported cases, the present patient underwent complete perioperative oncologic evaluation, including preoperative serum tumor marker assessment, definitive histopathologic characterization, postoperative cross-sectional staging, and AJCC pathologic staging during a single emergency admission. Histopathologic examination demonstrated classical seminoma with lymphovascular invasion, rete testis, epididymal, and tunica albuginea involvement, while the spermatic cord and surgical margin remained free of tumor. Postoperative normalization of serum tumor markers together with unremarkable computed tomography of the chest and abdomen confirmed S0, N0, and M0 disease, supporting a final pathologic stage of pT2N0M0S0 (AJCC Stage IB) and appropriate selection of active surveillance.

This case reinforces several important clinical lessons. First, routine examination of the external genitalia should be considered mandatory in every male presenting with acute abdomen because failure to identify an ipsilateral absent testis may delay diagnosis of surgically correctable urologic emergencies. Second, discovery of a normal appendix during emergency laparotomy should prompt continued exploration for alternative pathology. Finally, prompt multidisciplinary collaboration enabled definitive oncologic treatment during the index operation, avoiding delayed diagnosis, repeat surgery, and potential disease progression.

Torsion of a malignant intra-abdominal seminoma is an exceptionally rare but clinically significant mimic of acute appendicitis. Routine examination of the external genitalia should be considered mandatory in every male presenting with acute abdomen because failure to recognize an ipsilateral absent testis may delay diagnosis of surgically correctable urologic emergencies. This case demonstrates that timely multidisciplinary collaboration, comprehensive perioperative oncologic evaluation—including serum tumor markers, postoperative cross-sectional staging, and detailed histopathologic assessment—and definitive radical orchiectomy can achieve accurate staging and optimal oncologic management during a single emergency admission.
