Role of vascular smooth muscle phenotypic transformation induced by histone modifications in the development of abdominal aortic aneurysms
Authors: Zihao Chen, Yunping Sun, Peng Zhu, Le Zhang, Jian Yang, Jing Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAbdominal aortic aneurysm (AAA) is a progressive vascular disorder closely associated with the phenotypic transformation of vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMCs). Histone modifications—including acetylation, methylation, phosphorylation, lactylation, succinylation, ubiquitination, and small ubiquitin-like modifier modification—play pivotal roles in regulating chromatin accessibility, gene expression, and cellular behavior during this transformation. These epigenetic marks influence VSMCs proliferation, migration, apoptosis, and inflammatory responses, thereby contributing to AAA development and progression. This review systematically summarizes the molecular mechanisms by which histone modifications regulate VSMCs phenotypes and explores emerging therapeutic strategies that target epigenetic regulators such as histone deacetylases, histone methyltransferases, and lactate dehydrogenase A. Furthermore, this study highlights the potential of spatial omics and CRISPR–dCas9 epigenome editing tools for deciphering and modulating VSMC states with spatial and cell type precision. Advancing our understanding of these regulatory networks may pave the way for novel, individualized interventions in AAA treatment.Keywords: Vascular smooth muscle cells, Histone modifications, Phenotypic transformation, Abdominal aortic aneurysm, Epigenetic therapy

Introduction

Aortic aneurysm (AA) is a significant cardiovascular pathology. On the basis of published data, it was estimated that in 2019, approximately 35.12 million individuals, aged between 30 and 79 years, were afflicted with aortic aneurysms. Furthermore, as of 2021, the prevalence of aortic aneurysms among the population aged 65 years and older in China was 0.11%[1]. AAA is a progressive and life-threatening vascular disorder influenced by multifactorial etiologies. Although typically asymptomatic in its early stages, AAA poses a high risk of fatal rupture, making it a critical concern in clinical practice. Owing to its insidious onset and the challenges associated with early detection, no pharmacological agents have been proven effective in halting aneurysm progression to date[2].

The formation and progression of AAA are strongly associated with structural remodeling of the vessel wall, particularly the phenotypic transformation of VSMCs[3]. Under physiological conditions, VSMCs predominantly exhibit a contractile phenotype, which is essential for maintaining vascular tone and regulating blood flow. However, in response to pathological stimuli such as inflammation, oxidative stress, or hemodynamic changes, VSMCs undergo a phenotypic switch to a synthetic state. This transformation leads to increased extracellular matrix (ECM) degradation, vessel wall thickening, and inflammatory infiltration, all of which contribute to aortic dilation and instability[4].

Emerging evidence suggests that epigenetic regulation, particularly histone posttranslational modifications (PTMs), plays a pivotal role in mediating VSMC phenotypic transformation and AAA pathogenesis. Histone acetylation has been implicated in promoting VSMC proliferation and migration, whereas methylation and phosphorylation govern gene transcription programs that influence the synthetic phenotype[5]. More recently, noncanonical histone modifications such as ubiquitination, lactylation, and succinylation have been identified as additional regulatory layers, potentially linking metabolic states to transcriptional reprogramming.

Notably, VSMCs in distinct aortic regions originate from different embryonic lineages, including the neural crest, somite-derived mesoderm, and splanchnic/lateral plate mesoderm. Such developmental heterogeneity may shape their innate responses to inflammatory and oxidative signals[6]. For example, lateral plate- or splanchnic mesoderm-derived VSMCs, which are predominant in the abdominal aorta, demonstrate greater sensitivity to cytokines such as IL-1β than do neural crest-derived VSMCs in the thoracic segment[7]. These lineage-dependent differences may partly account for the predilection of the abdominal aorta for histone modification-driven phenotypic transformation and AAA progression. Together, these findings underscore the phenotypic plasticity of VSMCs in AAA. Phenotypic transitions of VSMC and their roles in AAA development

Despite growing interest, the specific contributions of histone modifications to AAA pathophysiology remain poorly understood. Most current studies rely on generic vascular or tumor-derived cell lines and preclinical animal models, with limited translation to human disease. Moreover, how these distinct modifications interact or integrate to regulate chromatin accessibility and gene expression at different stages of AAA development is still unclear. Elucidating the specific molecular mechanisms underlying histone modifications in VSMCs may reveal novel epigenetic targets for therapeutic intervention in AAA.

In response to pathological stimuli during AAA progression, contractile VSMC undergoes phenotypic switching into multiple subtypes, including synthetic, inflammatory-like, osteogenic-like, macrophage-like, and stem cell-like VSMC. Each phenotype plays a distinct pathological role: Synthetic VSMC contributes to extracellular matrix degradation; inflammatory-like VSMC secretes cytokines and recruits immune cells; osteogenic-like VSMC promotes vascular calcification; macrophage-like VSMC enhances immune activity; and stem cell-like VSMC disrupts vascular homeostasis by impairing repair and remodeling.

Molecular mechanism of histone modification

Histones are structural proteins found in eukaryotic chromatin. The nucleosome is the fundamental structural unit of chromatin in eukaryotic cells and consists of approximately 147 base pairs of DNA wrapped around a histone octamer composed of two copies each of histones H2A, H2B, H3, and H4[8]. It not only enables efficient packaging of the genome but also plays a pivotal role in regulating gene expression, DNA repair, and chromatin remodeling through PTMs of histone tails. Key histone modifications include acetylation, methylation, phosphorylation, ubiquitination, SUMOylation, lactylation, and succinylation, each of which contributes to the epigenetic control of cellular processes and the development of various diseases.

Histone acetylation

Histone acetylation is one of the earliest and most extensively characterized histone modifications. This modification neutralizes the positive charge on lysine residues, thereby weakening histone–DNA interactions and relaxing chromatin to facilitate transcription[9]. Therefore, histone acetylation is generally considered an active histone marker. Among the most extensively studied histone acetyltransferases (HATs), p300 and CBP have been research hotspots since their discovery in 1996. As HATs, p300 and CBP play critical roles in regulating gene expression and cellular functions. They promote chromatin relaxation and enhance transcriptional activity by acetylating histones[10]. In addition, they interact with specific transcription factors to regulate various biological processes, including cell proliferation, differentiation, and immune responses.

In humans, 18 histone deacetylases (HDACs) are categorized into four major classes. Class I HDACs include HDAC1, 2, 3, and 8 and are primarily localized in the nucleus. Class II HDACs are divided into IIa (HDAC4, 5, 7, and 9) and IIb (HDAC6 and 10), which can shuttle between the nucleus and cytoplasm. Class III includes SIRT1–7, and class IV consists solely of HDAC11[11]. Numerous lysine residues on histones are subject to acetylation, including H3K4, H3K9, H3K27, H3K36, H3K79, H4K5, H4K12, and H4K20. Acetylation at specific lysine residues, particularly histone H3 lysine 9 acetylation (H3K9ac) and histone H3 lysine 27 acetylation (H3K27ac), is associated with transcriptional activation[9].

Histone methylation

Histone methylation involves the enzymatic addition of methyl groups to lysine or arginine residues on histone tails. This modification plays diverse roles in gene regulation by influencing chromatin accessibility and modulating the binding of transcription factors to DNA. Histone methyltransferases (HMTs) and demethylases regulate transcription by modifying specific residues. Specifically, the methylation of H3K4, H3K36, and H3K79 is associated with transcriptional activation, whereas the methylation of H3K9, H3K27, and H4K20 is linked to transcriptional repression[12]. Histone H3 lysine 4 methylation, especially in its trimethylated form (H3K4me3), is strongly linked to active gene transcription at promoters. It is distributed around transcription start sites and is correlated with cell type specificity and transcription consistency[13]. H3K4 methylation also influences enhancer activity, with H3K4me1 and H3K4me3 marks indicating active enhancers[14]. H3K79 methylation plays a crucial role in gene transcription, influencing elongation, the DNA damage response, and cell cycle checkpoints[15]. In eukaryotes, DOT1L is the only methyltransferase that adds mono-, di-, and trimethyl groups to H3K79, which is prevalent in active chromatin and essential for gene expression[16]. In contrast, the methylation of H3K9, H3K27, and H4K20 is linked mainly to transcriptional repression. H3K9 methylation is vital for heterochromatin formation and gene silencing[17], whereas H3K27 methylation, which is mediated by polycomb repressive complex 2 (PRC2), represses transcription at promoter regions[18]. H4K20 methylation affects chromatin accessibility and regulates housekeeping gene expression[19]. In addition to histones, lysine methylation also occurs on nonhistone proteins, where it regulates protein‒protein interactions, stability, and cross-talk with other PTMs, thereby extending its functional importance across various cellular pathways[20].

Histone phosphorylation

The phosphorylation of histones is closely linked to cell cycle control, the DNA damage response, and chromatin remodeling. This modification typically occurs on serine and threonine residues and is dynamically regulated by kinases and phosphatases. Histone H3 serine 57 phosphorylation (H3S57ph), for example, is involved in DNA repair across eukaryotic species. Checkpoint kinase 1 has been identified as a major kinase responsible for this modification, and its dysregulation affects cellular recovery following replication stress[21]. Furthermore, specific phosphatases regulate mitotic processes such as chromosomal condensation, spindle assembly, and mitotic exit, underscoring the importance of reversible phosphorylation in cell cycle fidelity[22].

Ubiquitination and SUMOylation

As dynamic and reversible PTMs, ubiquitination and SUMOylation not only regulate protein stability, localization, and function but also affect core histones—particularly H2AK119 and H2BK120—contributing to chromatin architecture and gene expression control[23]. Ubiquitination involves the covalent attachment of ubiquitin to lysine residues on target proteins, impacting gene transcription, protein degradation, DNA repair, and signal transduction[24, 25]. In contrast, SUMOylation involves the conjugation of small ubiquitin-like modifiers via a cascade of E1, E2, and E3 enzymes and modulates protein function, nuclear‒cytoplasmic trafficking, and stress responses. The reversible nature of SUMOylation makes it an essential regulator of cellular signaling and homeostasis in both physiological and pathological contexts [26, 27].

Lactylation and succinylation

Lactylation and succinylation are emerging histone modifications that integrate metabolic cues with epigenetic regulation. Lactylation is induced by elevated intracellular lactate levels, which are common under hypoxic conditions or in glycolytic tumors, such as those exhibiting the Warburg effect[28]. Succinylation introduces a bulky negative charge, altering the histone structure and weakening nucleosome stability[29]. Histone H2B succinylation at lysine 34 disrupts chromatin compaction and is correlated with transcriptional activation. Mutations in succinylation sites have been linked to chromatin structural defects in yeast[29]. Additionally, intracellular succinylation levels are tightly regulated by mitochondrial enzymes. For example, loss of succinate-CoA ligase GDP-forming subunit beta (SUCLG2) or Sirtuin 5 leads to excessive succinylation of mitochondrial proteins such as succinate-CoA ligase ADP-forming subunit beta (SUCLA2), impairing metabolic enzyme function and exacerbating mitochondrial dysfunction[30–32]. These findings highlight succinylation as a critical modulator of both chromatin dynamics and cellular energy homeostasis.

Phenotypic transformation of VSMC

VSMC plays a pivotal role in maintaining vascular homeostasis by regulating vascular tone, ECM composition, and inflammatory responses. However, under pathological conditions such as atherosclerosis, vascular calcification, and AAA, VSMC exhibits remarkable phenotypic plasticity and undergoes phenotypic transformation. This process is characterized by a transition from a contractile phenotype to multiple alternative phenotypes, including synthetic, osteogenic, macrophage-like, and stem-like states, which differentially contribute to AAA initiation and progression.

VSMC phenotypic diversity and plasticity

Under physiological conditions, VSMC primarily maintains a contractile phenotype characterized by low proliferative capacity and high contractility. This phenotype is sustained by the expression of contractile-specific genes such as Myh11 (SM-MHC), Acta2 (α-SMA), Cnn1, and Sm22α, which collectively ensure vascular tone and blood flow regulation [33]. However, under pathological conditions such as atherosclerosis, vascular calcification, and AAA, VSMC demonstrates remarkable phenotypic plasticity and heterogeneity. VSMC can transdifferentiate into multiple phenotypes:

Synthetic phenotype: Increased ECM production, proliferation, and migration are accompanied by the upregulation of Col1a1 and vimentin expression [34]. Inflammatory phenotype: Increased expression of proinflammatory mediators such as Csf1r and LGALS3, which promote immune cell recruitment. Macrophage-like phenotype: This phenotype is characterized by the expression of CD68 and Lyz2, enabling foam cell formation and lipid uptake [35]. Osteogenic phenotype: Marked by the upregulation of Runx2 and Sox9, leading to vascular calcification [36]. Mesenchymal-like and stem-cell-like phenotypes are associated with increased expression of pluripotency genes such as Octamer-Binding Transcription Factor 4 (Oct4), SRY-Box Transcription Factor 2 (Sox2), and Kruppel-like factor 4 (KLF4), which maintain high proliferative and self-renewal capacities[37]. These phenotypic transformations are orchestrated by a complex network of signaling pathways, including the TGF-β/SMAD, PDGF-BB, Wnt/β-catenin, NF-κB, Notch, and YAP/TAZ pathways, as well as regulatory molecules such as transcription factors (KLF4, Runx2) and noncoding RNAs (miR-143/145, miR-21, let-7g).

Overall, this phenotypic diversity and plasticity enable VSMC to adapt dynamically to microenvironmental cues, maintaining vascular homeostasis under normal conditions but also contributing to disease progression when dysregulated.

Mechanisms of phenotypic transformation in VSMC

To understand the regulatory landscape governing VSMC phenotypic transformation, the principal signaling pathways are systematically categorized into three functional clusters: (1) differentiation and stemness-related pathways, which regulate lineage plasticity and cellular identity; (2) inflammatory and proliferative pathways, which mediate immune activation and hyperplasia; and (3) osteogenic and synthetic phenotype-associated pathways, which drive calcification and ECM remodeling. In recent years, increasing evidence has shown that these signaling cascades not only directly modulate gene expression but also exert long-lasting effects by altering chromatin accessibility through histone modifications. This dual regulation is critical for sustaining VSMC phenotypic changes under chronic pathological stimuli. Differentiation and stemness-related signaling pathways

TGF-β/SMAD signaling pathway

Phenotypic transformation of VSMC represents a significant pathological process in arterial diseases. Numerous studies have substantiated the protective role of transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β) signaling in both AAA and TAA. Importantly, the underlying mechanisms of this protective effect vary by anatomical location: Extrinsic TGF-β signaling in VSMC is protective in the abdominal aorta, whereas intrinsic signaling serves a protective function in the thoracic aorta[38]. The TGF-β/SMAD signaling pathway critically regulates VSMC phenotypic transformation. Fosinopril suppresses the TGF-β1/SMAD axis, thereby mitigating Ang II-induced proliferation, phenotypic transformation, migration, and oxidative stress in VSMC[39]. Similarly, matrilin-2 inhibits the contractile-to-synthetic transition by upregulating the TGF-β/SMAD pathway. Moreover, TGF-β1 promotes phenotypic transformation via the PI3K/AKT/ID2 axis, which involves proliferation, migration, and the downregulation of α-SMA and SM22α with concurrent OPN upregulation[40]. Mechanical stimuli also influence TGF-β signaling. Increased ECM stiffness enhances SMAD2/3 phosphorylation and promotes a contractile phenotype, whereas a compliant matrix favors the expression of synthetic markers, including OPN and MMP-2[41]. The noncanonical RhoA/ROCK pathway also contributes to cytoskeletal remodeling.

Compared with normal aorta media, aneurysm samples have reduced TGF-β signaling, decreased p-Smad2 and TIMP3, and increased VCAM1 and MMP2 expression[42, 43]. PIASy deficiency attenuates aneurysm formation by modulating the TGF-β/Smad2/3 axis[44]. In Kawasaki disease-associated aneurysms, increased TGF-β signaling is a contributing factor to vascular remodeling[45]. TGF-β also cooperates with SOX9 to induce chondrogenic genes such as COL2A1 and ACAN, facilitating a chondrocyte-like phenotype[46]. In addition to canonical signaling, TGF-β activates the RhoA/ROCK and JNK pathways, modulating cytoskeletal dynamics and phenotypic remodeling[47]. Importantly, TGF-β interferes with epigenetic regulation by upregulating EZH2, which induces H3K27me3-mediated repression of contractile gene loci, and by modulating class I/II HDACs that control chromatin condensation during phenotype transition[48].

Notch signaling pathway

The Notch signaling pathway plays a pivotal role in regulating VSMC phenotypic transformation by modulating proliferation, differentiation, apoptosis, and inflammation[49, 50]. It regulates adenylyl cyclase 8 (AC8) expression, thereby influencing inflammatory responses and migratory capacity; its inhibition enhances IL-1β-induced AC8 expression and promotes an inflammatory phenotype[49]. Notch also controls PDGFR-β expression, as evidenced in Notch3-deficient mouse models, and affects VSMC proliferation and differentiation[51]. In addition to phenotypic modulation, Notch signaling is essential for vascular development, smooth muscle cell (SMC) differentiation, and vascular homeostasis[52]. It also regulates responses to mechanical and oxidative stress, with Notch1 enhancing antioxidant defenses in hyperglycemic environments[50, 53]. Moreover, Notch maintains the stem cell-like phenotype of VSMC by promoting self-renewal and preventing differentiation[54], and it contributes to angiogenesis and tissue regeneration through endothelial–mesenchymal stem cell interactions[55]. Epigenetically, Notch1 upregulates SIRT1, an NAD⁺-dependent deacetylase, which mediates chromatin remodeling at the promoters of genes involved in differentiation and the oxidative stress response[56].

YAP/TAZ signaling pathway

Yes-associated protein (YAP) and transcriptional coactivator with PDZ-binding motif (TAZ), the core effectors of the Hippo signaling pathway, function as mechanosensors that respond to ECM stiffness and mechanical stress[57]. Upon activation, unphosphorylated YAP/TAZ translocates to the nucleus, binds TEA domain transcription factor 1 (TEAD1), and promotes the expression of genes such as TAGLN and ACTA2, facilitating a myofibroblast-like transition in VSMC [58]. In stem cell-like phenotypes, YAP/TAZ supports self-renewal and maintains the undifferentiated state by interacting with the Notch and Wnt signaling pathways [37]. YAP also cooperates with Krüppel-like factor 4 (KLF4) to sustain stem-like traits in VSMC[58]. Additionally, YAP/TAZ synergizes with TGF-β/SMAD signaling to increase fibroblast activation and pathological remodeling during phenotypic transformation[59].

BMP/SMAD1/5/8 signaling pathway

The bone morphogenetic protein (BMP)/SMAD1/5/8 signaling pathway critically mediates the osteogenic and chondrogenic phenotypic transformation of VSMC. Upon activation, BMP2 induces SMAD1/5/8 phosphorylation, promoting osteogenic differentiation and vascular calcification[60, 61]. Inhibiting pyruvate dehydrogenase kinase 4 (PDK4) or applying mesenchymal stem cell-conditioned medium (MSC-CM) attenuates this signaling, thereby reducing calcification[60, 61]. This pathway also interacts with other regulators. Kruppel-like factor 2 (KLF2) impedes BMP/SMAD1/5 signaling in endothelial cells, highlighting the role of hemodynamic forces in modulating vascular calcification[62]. Additionally, cross-talk with the Notch pathway amplifies BMP2-induced Msx2 expression, further enhancing osteogenic differentiation in VSMC.

PPARγ signaling pathway

Peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma (PPARγ) is a ligand-activated transcription factor that preserves the contractile phenotype of VSMC and exerts anti-atherosclerotic and anti-calcification effects[63]. It suppresses PDGF-BB-induced proliferation and migration by upregulating mitochondrial uncoupling protein 2 (UCP2) and reducing reactive oxygen species (ROS) production[64]. PPARγ also promotes autophagy and delays H₂O₂-induced senescence via PTEN upregulation and mTORC2–FoxO3a pathway inhibition[65]. Under high glucose conditions, PPARγ activation by FGF21 attenuates VSMC aging by inhibiting the SYK–NLRP3 inflammasome pathway, reducing oxidative stress and senescence markers[66]. Additionally, PPARγ counteracts Wnt/β-catenin-driven osteo-/chondrogenic differentiation by inducing Wnt antagonists and inhibiting β-catenin signaling[67]. Conversely, its downregulation promotes synthetic transition; for example, Nesfatin-1 promotes VSMC dedifferentiation and neointimal hyperplasia by inhibiting PPARγ and activating MMP-2/9[68].

Inflammatory and proliferative signaling pathways

PDGF-BB signaling pathway

Platelet-derived growth factor-BB (PDGF-BB) promotes VSMC proliferation and migration via PDGFRβ-mediated activation of the PI3K/Akt, ERK1/2, and JAK2/STAT3 pathways, which converge on Krüppel-like factor 4 (KLF4), a key pro-dedifferentiation factor[35, 69, 70]. KLF4 suppresses the serum response factor (SRF)/myocardin-dependent transcription of contractile genes (MYH11, SM22α, and CNN1), promoting a synthetic phenotype[34]. PDGF-BB further enhances KLF4 via Sp1-mediated transcription, SUMOylation, and p21 downregulation[71, 72] and regulates CREB phosphorylation to control cyclin expression and proliferation[73].

In arterial injury models, sustained PDGF-BB/KLF4 activation promotes synthetic VSMC accumulation, aiding repair but potentially driving intimal hyperplasia and stenosis if prolonged[74]. Moreover, PI3K/Akt activation reduces SIRT1 and increases acetylation of inflammatory gene loci, linking PDGF signaling to epigenetic reprogramming [75].

MAPK signaling pathway

The MAPK pathway (ERK, JNK, p38) regulates VSMC phenotypic plasticity by controlling proliferation, migration, and apoptosis. ERK and p38 mediate HMGB1-induced RAGE expression, promoting proliferation[76]. Nucleolin enhances Ang II-induced phenotypic transformation by facilitating angiotensin II type 1 receptor (AT1R) internalization and activating PKC/MAPK signaling[77]. Similarly, Ang III promotes VSMC proliferation via p38 MAPK[78]. JNK and p38 are also key effectors in inflammation- or stress-induced transdifferentiation toward macrophage-like and osteogenic phenotypes[79]. Overall, MAPK acts as a central hub that integrates multiple cues to modulate VSMC behavior and fate.

JAK/STAT signaling pathway

Aberrant activation of the JAK/STAT pathway contributes to chronic inflammation and pathological VSMC proliferation, promoting vascular disease progression[80]. Cytokines such as IL-6 and IFN-γ activate JAK1/2 and STAT3, inducing genes such as CD68 and IL6 and driving macrophage-like or immune-activated phenotypes[81, 82]. Inhibition of MAP2K2 reduces inflammation and oxidative stress via suppression of JAK/STAT, preventing phenotypic transformation[83]. JAK2/STAT3 also mediates ox-LDL-induced transformation, where its inhibition limits lipid accumulation and inflammation[35]. Notably, phosphate-induced JAK/STAT activation sustains inflammation while suppressing calcification, underscoring its dual regulatory role in VSMC function[84].

NF-κB signaling pathway

The NF-κB pathway plays a central role in the phenotypic transformation of VSMC, particularly their shift toward a macrophage-like state, via IκB degradation, and NF-κB nuclear translocation. HOXA1 promotes this transition by modulating NF-κB p65 and KLF4 expression, accelerating atherosclerosis progression[85]. NF-κB activation is also associated with VSMC calcification under high-pH conditions through TLR4-mediated signaling[86]. Crosstalk with other pathways, such as NOX4/ROS and p38MAPK, further amplifies inflammation, as observed in SAA1-induced cytokine release[87] and Prevotella copri-induced gut‒liver axis activation[88].

Newman et al. demonstrated that CD68⁺ macrophage-like cells in plaques primarily originate from VSMC, not monocytes, underscoring the importance of this plasticity in disease[89]. Similarly, NaVO₃ induces IL-6 expression via ROS/p38/NF-κB, promoting proliferation and migration[90]. NF-κB also regulates macrophage polarization: Its activation favors M1 pro-inflammatory states[91], whereas its inhibition facilitates M2 anti-inflammatory phenotypes[92].

At the chromatin level, activated NF-κB recruits histone acetyltransferases (e.g., p300/CBP) to inflammatory gene promoters, increasing H3/H4 acetylation and sustaining pro-inflammatory transcriptional programs in VSMC.

Osteogenic and synthetic phenotype-associated pathways

Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway

The Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway plays a critical role in promoting the osteogenic transdifferentiation of VSMC, largely via the upregulation of Runx2, a key osteogenic transcription factor[36]. High-phosphate conditions inhibit GSK3β, leading to β-catenin accumulation and nuclear translocation, thereby activating Runx2 expression and driving arterial medial calcification (AMC)[36, 93]. BMP2, which acts downstream of Wnt signaling, is also elevated during this process[94].

Wnt/β-catenin interacts with other signaling pathways to amplify calcification. BMP9 enhances COX-2 expression and Wnt activity, whereas miR-195-5p promotes osteogenic differentiation by targeting Smad7[95, 96]. This pathway also engages in crosstalk with Notch and TGF-β signaling to regulate VSMC phenotype switching and vascular calcification.

Special role of Lgals3

Galectin-3 (Lgals3), a β-galactoside-binding lectin, is highly expressed in macrophage-like VSMC and participates in inflammation, fibrosis, and apoptosis under pathological conditions[97]. It facilitates oxLDL-induced phenotypic transformation of VSMC and accelerates atherosclerosis progression [98]. In addition, Lgals3, together with the receptor for advanced glycation end products (RAGE), differentially mediates vascular micro- and macro-calcification, acting via distinct inflammatory pathways. Notably, Lgals3 directly activates the Wnt/β-catenin signaling cascade, promoting the osteogenic transdifferentiation of VSMC[99].

In addition to classical pathways, such noncanonical regulators converge on the epigenetic machinery by modulating histone-modifying enzymes (e.g., HDACs, HATs, and methyltransferases), thereby altering chromatin accessibility and contributing to AAA progression through a signaling–epigenetic axis[48].

VSMC phenotypic transformation promotes the development of AAA.

Phenotypic transformation of VSMC is increasingly recognized as a central pathogenic mechanism in the initiation and progression of AAA. Under pathological stimuli, VSMC undergoes a transition from a contractile phenotype to synthetic, inflammatory, and osteogenic-like states. This transformation leads to the loss of contractile marker expression, increased secretion of matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), such as MMP-9, and increased production of proinflammatory cytokines. Collectively, these changes disrupt ECM homeostasis, reduce the tensile strength of the aortic wall, and predispose it to progressive dilation and rupture[100]. In parallel, ECM softening and mechanical stress reciprocally promote phenotypic transformation, creating a self-reinforcing loop that accelerates vascular wall degeneration[101].

Recent single-cell transcriptomic analyses have revealed substantial heterogeneity among VSMC-derived populations within AAA lesions. Rather than following a binary contractile-to-synthetic transition, VSMC exhibits a broad spectrum of pathological phenotypes, including immune-like, osteogenic, macrophage-like, mesenchymal, and fibroblast-like subtypes. These cells cooperate to degrade the ECM, propagate inflammatory signals, and weaken the medial layer of the aortic wall [102]. The immune-modulatory properties of these transformed VSMC underscore their dual structural and immunological contributions to AAA pathology.

In addition to phenotypic plasticity, imbalances in cell survival mechanisms further exacerbate AAA progression. Excessive apoptosis results in SMC loss and medial thinning, whereas defective autophagy enhances inflammatory signaling and destabilizes VSMC identity [103]. These survival pathways interact with epigenetic regulators and mechanotransductive cues to establish a permissive chromatin landscape that sustains aberrant VSMC phenotypes. Overall, the convergence of phenotypic transformation, inflammatory signaling, and survival dysregulation forms a pathological axis that drives aortic wall degradation and aneurysmal expansion in AAA.

Histone modifications: types and mechanisms of VSMC regulation

Histone acetylation

Histone acetylation is a dynamic and reversible epigenetic modification regulated by HATs and HDACs[104]. These enzymes play crucial roles in orchestrating gene expression programs and chromatin accessibility, thereby influencing the phenotypic plasticity of VSMC.

Among HDACs, HDAC9 is prominently implicated in vascular pathology. It promotes the phenotypic transformation of VSMC and is associated with vascular calcification and aortic aneurysm formation[105]. Mechanistically, HDAC9 forms a complex with MALAT1 and BRG1, which are recruited to the promoters of contractile genes (e.g., CNN1 and MYH11) to suppress their expression via histone deacetylation, thereby facilitating a synthetic or inflammatory phenotype[106]. Additionally, HDAC9 activates the IKK/NF-κB pathway and enhances RUNX2 expression, promoting osteogenic transdifferentiation and medial calcification[105, 107]. Other class I/II HDACs are also involved in inflammatory and ECM remodeling pathways, although their specific roles require further investigation.

Conversely, HATs such as CBP and p300 maintain VSMC homeostasis by promoting acetylation at H3K9 and H3K27, facilitating synthetic gene expression during phenotypic transformation[108]. Metabolic signals intersect with histone acetylation: Loss of the citrate transporter ANK increases intracellular citrate and acetyl-CoA levels, leading to hyperacetylation of H3K23, H3K27, and H4K5, which activates inflammatory genes and drives a proinflammatory phenotype in aortic aneurysm models[109]. Histone acetylation also modulates redox-sensitive transcription via the NF-κB pathway, influencing the inflammation, proliferation, and migration of VSMC[110].

Overall, histone acetylation integrates metabolic, mechanical, and inflammatory cues to orchestrate VSMC plasticity under both homeostatic and disease conditions.

Histone methylation

Histone methylation modulates the VSMC phenotype by regulating gene transcription and chromatin architecture. Key lysine residues—H3K4, H3K9, H3K27, and H3K36—mediate context-dependent effects on gene expression.

H3K4me3, which is generally associated with gene activation, is catalyzed by SET and MYND domain-containing protein 2 (SMYD2) and linked to proliferation and motility programs during phenotypic transformation[111, 112].

H3K27me3, a repressive mark mediated by EZH2 of the PRC2 complex, plays a central role in aortic aneurysm pathogenesis. EZH2 is upregulated in diseased vessels and silences contractile genes (e.g., MYOCD, ACTA2, and CNN1) while activating dedifferentiation and inflammation-related genes (e.g., KLF4, MMP9, and IL6)[113, 114]. Multi-omics analyses have shown that EZH2 enhances H3K27me3 deposition at the promoters of target genes (e.g., FOXO1, CDKN1A, and MMP2), contributing to DNA damage, inflammatory signaling, and phenotypic plasticity. Its inhibition (e.g., EPZ6438) alleviates vascular remodeling and attenuates aneurysm progression, highlighting its therapeutic relevance[115]. The dynamic balance between H3K27me3 and H3K27ac, modulated by EZH2 under oxidative stress and autophagy impairment, further governs cell fate transitions [116].

H3K36me supports transcriptional elongation and genomic integrity, whereas H3K36me dysregulation may facilitate aberrant transitions in the VSMC phenotype[117]. In contrast, H3K9me2 serves as a repressive mark that maintains transcriptional silencing; its loss in atherosclerotic lesions is correlated with increased inflammation and remodeling[118].

Additionally, sterile alpha motif domain containing 4A (SAMD4A), a post-transcriptional regulator, inhibits phenotypic transformation by suppressing the demethylase KDM2B, thereby attenuating AAA development[119].

Together, these findings underscore the essential roles of histone methylation and its regulatory enzymes—particularly EZH2—in shaping the transcriptional landscape of VSMC phenotypic transformation and vascular disease progression.

Histone phosphorylation

Histone phosphorylation is a dynamic and reversible epigenetic mechanism involved in VSMC proliferation, migration, and phenotypic transformation. Among the key sites, phosphorylation at histone H3 serine 10 (H3S10ph) and serine 28 (H3S28ph) links extracellular stimuli to transcriptional activation and chromatin remodeling[120, 121]. H3S28ph, in particular, couples MAPK signaling to early gene expression in cardiac and vascular contexts, contributing to plasticity and hypertrophic responses[121].

These phosphorylation events often cooperate with other modifications. H3S10ph promotes H3K14 acetylation, forming a transcriptionally permissive chromatin state for immediate-early genes[122]. Phosphorylated CREB further modulates mitogenic signaling and the phenotypic fate of VSMC.

Multiple kinases—including MSK1 downstream of ERK and p38 MAPK, as well as the JNK and NF-κB pathways—mediate histone phosphorylation in response to oxidative and inflammatory stimuli[123, 124]. In contrast, phosphatases such as PP1 and PP2A antagonize these signals, ensuring chromatin plasticity[125].

Modulating kinase/phosphatase activity represents a promising therapeutic avenue for controlling phenotypic transitions and vascular remodeling.

Histone lactylation

Histone lactylation is a recently discovered lysine modification driven by elevated intracellular lactate levels, particularly under hypoxic or glycolytic conditions. In VSMC, lactylation modulates phenotypic transformation by integrating metabolic and epigenetic signals. Specifically, the KLF4–eEF1A2/circCTDP1–PFKFB3 axis regulates glycolysis-dependent lactylation, promoting the transition from a contractile to an immune-like phenotype[126]. Moreover, lactylation may influence acid-sensing ion channel function, thereby altering cellular signaling cascades relevant to vascular remodeling[127]. These findings position histone lactylation as a critical metabolic–epigenetic interface linking environmental stress to transcriptional reprogramming in VSMC.

Histone succinylation

Histone succinylation, a recently identified type of lysine acylation, alters chromatin architecture by reducing histone–DNA affinity, thereby increasing transcription factor accessibility[128]. In VSMC, this modification contributes to phenotypic transformation by regulating mitochondrial metabolism and oxidative stress responses[129]. Succinylation may also modulate the expression of transcription factors that control cell proliferation and migration. The histone acyltransferase KAT2A (GCN5) catalyzes succinylation at H3K79, a mark linked to gene activation and proliferative signaling[130]. In addition to transcriptional regulation, histone succinylation has been implicated in DNA repair, nucleosome stability, and telomere maintenance[128], suggesting a broader role in VSMC plasticity under vascular stress conditions.

Ubiquitination and SUMOylation

Histone ubiquitination and SUMOylation contribute to VSMC phenotypic regulation by modulating transcription factor stability, chromatin accessibility, and signal transduction. These PTMs influence the switch from contractile to synthetic or inflammatory phenotypes in various pathophysiological contexts.

In the context of histone regulation, monoubiquitination at lysine residues such as H2AK119 and H2BK120 has been shown to modulate chromatin compaction and transcriptional activity, potentially impacting VSMC gene expression and phenotype. These modifications function as chromatin "flags" that recruit or repel specific transcriptional machinery, thus influencing the epigenetic landscape during vascular remodeling.

In addition to histones, ubiquitination also regulates key transcription factors involved in VSMC identity. The metabolic cytokine FAM3A modulates the ubiquitination of KLF4, a master regulator of VSMC dedifferentiation. In both human AAA tissues and murine models, FAM3A expression is markedly decreased in diseased vascular walls and plasma. FAM3A overexpression inhibits AAA progression by maintaining VSMC in a contractile state and suppressing their transdifferentiation into macrophage-, osteoblast-, mesenchymal-, or fibroblast-like subtypes[131].

SUMOylation, which is mediated by the covalent attachment of small ubiquitin-like modifiers (SUMOs) to lysine residues, also governs VSMC behavior by altering the activity, localization, and stability of key nuclear regulators. For example, SUMOylation can affect transcription factors such as myocardin, NF-κB, and p53, thereby modulating the expression of genes associated with contractility, inflammation, and cell survival[132]. Importantly, ubiquitination and SUMOylation are not isolated events but often act in a coordinated manner. These modifications may compete for the same lysine residues or form sequential regulatory cascades that fine-tune gene expression. The crosstalk between the ubiquitin and SUMO pathways adds another regulatory layer to VSMC plasticity, particularly under oxidative and inflammatory stress[133].

Collectively, histone ubiquitination and SUMOylation extend the epigenetic control of VSMC fate through both direct chromatin modifications and indirect regulation of transcriptional regulators. A better understanding of these mechanisms may reveal novel epigenetic targets for therapeutic intervention in AAA and related vascular diseases.

Role of PVAT in providing metabolic substrates for histone modifications

Recent studies suggest that perivascular adipose tissue (PVAT) acts as a critical metabolic niche that modulates the VSMC phenotype by supplying metabolites for histone modifications[134]. Metabolic by-products from PVAT—including lactate, free fatty acids, and β-hydroxybutyrate—serve as substrates for noncanonical histone modifications such as lactylation and β-hydroxybutyrylation [135, 136].

For example, PVAT-derived lactate enhances histone lactylation in VSMC, thereby promoting the expression of genes linked to the synthetic phenotype[135]. Similarly, β-hydroxybutyrate facilitates β-hydroxybutyrylation, altering chromatin accessibility and contributing to phenotypic transformation between contractile and synthetic states[137]. These metabolite-driven epigenetic alterations suggest a mechanistic link between PVAT metabolism and VSMC reprogramming.

Importantly, the epigenetic impact of these modifications is highly context dependent and varies with the phenotypic state of VSMC. Thus, PVAT not only influences histone-modifying enzyme activity but also dynamically shapes the chromatin landscape during vascular remodeling.

Histone modifications in phenotype-specific VSMC transdifferentiation

The phenotypic transformation of VSMC involves a spectrum of cell states, including synthetic, osteogenic, macrophage-like, inflammatory, and stem cell-like phenotypes. Recent studies have demonstrated that the dependency of these phenotypic switches on histone modifications varies significantly, with some transitions being tightly regulated by epigenetic remodeling, whereas others are predominantly driven by transcription factors or inflammatory pathways. While the previous chapter discussed the general mechanisms of histone modifications, this section focuses on their specific roles in directing VSMC phenotypic transdifferentiation. Different phenotypes exhibit varying dependencies on epigenetic remodeling, highlighting the selective influence of histone modifications on vascular pathology.

Synthetic phenotypes

The transition of VSMC from a contractile to a synthetic phenotype is critically dependent on histone modifications that reshape chromatin accessibility. Active histone acetylation marks, such as H3K27ac and H3K9ac, accumulate at the promoters of synthetic genes (Col1a1, VIM), promoting ECM production and proliferation[106].

Concurrently, repressive histone methylation plays a central role. EZH2, the catalytic subunit of PRC2, mediates H3K27me3 deposition at contractile gene promoters (MYH11, CNN1, MYOCD), which silences H3K27me3 expression while upregulating dedifferentiation markers such as KLF4, MMP9, and IL6[115]. This dual regulation accelerates phenotypic transformation toward a synthetic state and is amplified under oxidative stress or inflammatory stimuli.

Multi-omics profiling confirmed widespread epigenetic reprogramming in synthetic VSMC, characterized by reduced chromatin accessibility at contractile loci and increased activation of growth and migration genes[48]. Together, these findings underscore that the synthetic phenotype is strongly driven by coordinated histone acetylation and methylation dynamics.

Inflammatory phenotype

The inflammatory phenotype has a relatively weak association with histone remodeling. Activation of the NF-κB and STAT3 pathways directly drives the transcription of proinflammatory cytokines such as IL6, TNFα, and VCAM1, often independent of significant chromatin modifications[85]. Although histone acetylation can fine-tune inflammatory responses, nonepigenetic transcriptional activation dominates this phenotype.

Macrophage-like phenotype

The macrophage-like phenotype is moderately dependent on histone modifications. Lower levels of H3K27me3 release the suppression of KLF4, a transcription factor critical for activating macrophage-like gene expression[35]. Nevertheless, the NF-κB signaling pathway remains the dominant driver of macrophage-related gene programs, indicating that histone modifications act synergistically rather than as the primary regulatory mechanism.

Osteogenic phenotype

The osteogenic transdifferentiation of VSMC is tightly regulated by epigenetic modifications. The activation of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway synergizes with histone changes to upregulate Runx2 and Sox9, initiating osteogenic gene programs[36]. This process involves reduced H3K27me3 and increased H3K27ac at osteogenic loci, which collectively facilitate the transcription of calcification-associated genes. These chromatin-level changes highlight the central role of histone modifications in driving vascular calcification.

Stem cell-like phenotype

Stem cell-like or multipotent phenotypes exhibit the greatest dependency on histone modifications. The enrichment of H3K4me1/3 and H3K27ac at pluripotency-associated loci (KLF4, SOX2, OCT4) supports dedifferentiation and the acquisition of self-renewal capabilities. Additionally, the YAP/TAZ and Notch signaling pathways collaborate with chromatin remodeling to maintain stem cell-like traits and regulate lineage plasticity[37].

In summary, synthetic, osteogenic, and stem cell-like phenotypes are highly dependent on histone modifications, whereas inflammatory phenotypes are predominantly regulated by transcription factors and inflammatory pathways. The macrophage-like phenotype lies between these extremes, showing both epigenetic and nonepigenetic control. In conclusion, the phenotypic fate of VSMC is tightly governed by a phenotype-specific hierarchy of epigenetic modifications. These insights highlight the importance of employing integrated multi-omics strategies—such as ATAC-seq, ChIP-seq, and CUT&Tag—to delineate the chromatin architecture that underlies VSMC plasticity.

Epigenetic effects of oxidative stress on VSMC phenotypic transformation

Oxidative stress is a key upstream regulator of histone modifications in the phenotypic transformation of VSMC. Under pathological stimuli such as hypertension, hyperlipidemia, and chronic inflammation, intracellular ROS levels increase significantly, driving the transition of VSMC from contractile to synthetic, inflammatory, or osteogenic phenotypes—all of which contribute to AAA development[138].

ROS exert long-term regulatory effects via epigenetic reprogramming. It enhances EZH2 expression, leading to elevated H3K27me3 levels and the suppression of genes such as MYOCD and ACTA2[48]. Moreover, ROS suppress SIRT1, promoting histone hyperacetylation at proinflammatory loci and facilitating cytokine production[139].

Additionally, ROS activate the p38-MAPK/MSK1 pathway, which phosphorylates H3S10, loosening chromatin and enhancing the transcription of IL-6 and TNF-α[140, 141]. These histone modifications generate epigenetic memory that reinforces phenotypic reprogramming.

In human AAA tissues, ROS levels are elevated along with EZH2 and H3K27me3 levels, whereas SIRT1 expression is reduced. These changes are associated with matrix degradation and inflammatory remodeling of medial VSMC[48].

Together, oxidative stress links upstream pathological cues with chromatin remodeling via histone modifications. The ROS–epigenetic axis serves as a central node in AAA pathogenesis. Targeting this axis with antioxidants or epigenetic drugs may help limit VSMC plasticity and slow AAA progression[138].

Developmental origins of epigenetic plasticity in AAA pathogenesis

Abdominal and thoracic aortic aneurysms, although both involve aortic dilation and medial degeneration, are characterized by distinct molecular signatures and cellular responses. One underexplored explanation for their divergence lies in the embryonic heterogeneity of VSMC.

VSMC populating different aortic segments are derived from distinct embryonic lineages: The thoracic aorta harbors VSMC primarily from the neural crest and somite-derived mesoderm, whereas the abdominal aorta is populated by VSMC of splanchnic or lateral plate mesodermal origin[6]. This developmental divergence results in segment-specific functional properties, including differences in ECM production, oxidative stress responses, and cytokine sensitivity.

Notably, splanchnic mesoderm-derived VSMC exhibits significantly greater responsiveness to proinflammatory cytokines such as interleukin-1β (IL-1β) than do neural crest-derived VSMC[142, 143]. This heightened sensitivity predisposes abdominal aortic VSMC to oxidative activation, phenotypic modulation, and histone modification-mediated chromatin remodeling under pathological stimuli.

In addition to embryonic lineage-driven susceptibility, increasing evidence suggests that the epigenetic and oxidative stress environments differ substantially between AAA and TAA. For example, the levels of ROS, including NOX4 and 8-OHdG, are markedly elevated in AAA tissue but remain low in TAA lesions[144, 145]. These redox conditions correlate with increased expression of EZH2 and H3K27me3 and reduced SIRT1 in AAA medial VSMC, whereas TAA tissues often lack such coordinated epigenetic reprogramming [48]. Furthermore, inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β, which can activate chromatin-remodeling enzymes, are significantly more abundant in AAA than TAA[146].

Collectively, these findings suggest that the abdominal aorta provides a more permissive developmental and biochemical environment for VSMC phenotypic transformation than does the thoracic aorta, explaining the preferential link between histone modifications and AAA progression. Histone modifications as novel therapeutic targets for AAA

AAA is a life-threatening vascular disorder characterized by chronic inflammation, ECM degradation, and VSMC apoptosis, culminating in progressive aortic dilation. Mounting evidence shows that environmental risk factors such as smoking, aging, and hyperlipidemia induce persistent transcriptional alterations via epigenetic mechanisms, particularly histone methylation and acetylation[147]. Currently, there is no effective pharmacological treatment approved for AAA, and clinical management relies primarily on surgical interventions, including open surgical repair and endovascular aneurysm repair (EVAR). Given the pivotal role of histone modifications in VSMC phenotypic transformation and aneurysm progression, targeting these epigenetic regulators has emerged as a promising therapeutic avenue.

HDAC inhibitors

HDAC inhibitors have demonstrated efficacy in attenuating vascular remodeling and inflammation in preclinical AAA models. MGCD0103 reduces pressure overload-induced aortic remodeling by suppressing HDAC activity[148], whereas tributyrin inhibits HDACs and reduces Ang II-induced pathology by increasing histone H3 acetylation and suppressing AT1R expression[149].

Other HDAC inhibitors, such as trichostatin A (TSA), promote the expression of the contractile markers α-SMA and SM-MHC by increasing H3K9ac and H4ac levels, thereby inhibiting phenotypic switching[150]. Notably, FDA-approved HDAC inhibitors such as vorinostat and suberoylanilide hydroxamic acid (SAHA), which are used in cancer therapy, support their translational potential in treating vascular diseases[151].

HMT inhibitors

HMT inhibitors also show therapeutic potential in modulating the VSMC phenotype and vascular inflammation. Sphingosine-1-phosphate (S1P), a lipid component of HDL, exerts anti-inflammatory effects via the PTEN-dependent suppression of TNF-α-induced MMP9 and iNOS expression in VSMC[152]. Pharmacological inhibitors targeting EZH2 (e.g., tazemetostat), DOT1L (e.g., pinometostat), and PRMT5 have already entered clinical trials for cancer and hold potential in the treatment of AAA[153].

Metabolic-epigenetic targets: LDHA and PVAT

Strategies that target lactate metabolism by inhibiting LDHA may reduce histone lactylation and dampen the phenotypes of pro-synthetic VSMC. LDHA is known to promote M2 macrophage polarization and cardiomyocyte survival by modulating ROS levels[154]. Although the first phase III clinical trial for a cardiovascular HDAC/BET-targeting agent did not significantly reduce major adverse cardiovascular events (MACEs), subgroup analyses and study limitations indicate that such strategies still hold substantial future clinical potential[155].

Strategies aimed at regulating PVAT function may indirectly reshape VSMC epigenetic states[156, 157]. For example, regulating PVAT-derived lactate levels may attenuate histone lactylation and suppress the expression of synthetic phenotype-related genes [135], whereas controlling β hydroxybutyrate production could fine tune β hydroxybutyrylation, restoring the contractile VSMC phenotype[137]. Combining PVAT-targeted interventions with epigenetic modulators, including HDACis or HATa, offers a synergistic therapeutic approach that addresses both metabolic and epigenetic drivers of AAA progression[158].

CRISPR-based epigenome editing

Recent advances in CRISPR/dCas9 technology have enabled the precise regulation of epigenetic marks without altering the DNA sequence. By fusing dCas9 with functional effectors such as DNMT3A, TET1, p300, or KRAB, researchers can target histone modifications or DNA methylation to activate or silence gene expression at specific loci[159]. These tools effectively explore causal links between chromatin states and gene function, making them ideal for studying cell fate changes in complex pathological conditions such as AAA [160].

The dCas9-p300 system enhances histone acetylation and transcription at contractile gene promoters (e.g., SM22α and KLF4), whereas dCas9-KRAB recruits repressive complexes to suppress synthetic or inflammatory genes[161]. These strategies have been used to modulate VSMC phenotypic transformation, thereby limiting vascular remodeling.

Integration with single-cell omics (e.g., scATAC-seq, CUT&Tag) and AI-guided sgRNA design has further improved the specificity and resolution of CRISPR epigenome editing[162]. These technologies allow the mapping of chromatin accessibility and phenotypic plasticity across VSMC subpopulations, facilitating targeted epigenetic reprogramming in AAA. Compared with conventional pharmacological inhibitors (HDACs, HMTs), CRISPR offers reversible, locus-specific, and multiplex epigenetic control, potentially reducing off-target effects.

Histone modifications have emerged as central regulators of VSMC fate and AAA pathogenesis. Translating these mechanistic insights into therapeutic interventions—through HDAC or HMT inhibitors, metabolic reprogramming, or CRISPR-based epigenetic editing—offers a promising multidimensional strategy to delay aneurysm expansion and reduce the need for surgical repair. These approaches aim to modulate histone acetylation, methylation, phosphorylation, and lactylation pathways that govern VSMC phenotypic plasticity. Conclusion and foresight

Histone modifications have emerged as central regulators of VSMC phenotypic plasticity, shaping the transcriptional landscape that governs their transition across contractile, synthetic, osteogenic, inflammatory, and stem cell-like states. Classical modifications such as histone acetylation and methylation directly alter chromatin accessibility and transcriptional activity, whereas novel marks—including lactylation, phosphorylation, succinylation, ubiquitination, and SUMOylation—act as sensitive integrators of metabolic stress, oxidative signals, and environmental cues. Together, these epigenetic marks define a dynamic regulatory network that coordinates gene expression programs essential for vascular homeostasis and pathological remodeling.

From a therapeutic standpoint, histone-modifying enzymes—particularly HDACs, HMTs, and metabolic enzymes such as LDHA—have been investigated as potential intervention targets in AAA. Preclinical studies indicate that inhibitors of HDACs (e.g., TSA, SAHA, and tributyrin) can ameliorate pathological vascular remodeling and suppress VSMC phenotypic transformation. However, most available compounds lack isoform specificity, potentially affecting essential epigenetic programs in other tissues and raising concerns about off-target effects. Given the functional diversity among HDAC subtypes, the development of isoform-selective inhibitors or locally delivered therapeutics remains a crucial direction for future research.

Notably, current studies have focused primarily on VSMC in isolation. However, AAA represents a multicellular pathology. Immune cells, endothelial cells, and PVAT form a complex microenvironment that profoundly shapes epigenetic states through paracrine signaling, cytokine production, and metabolic flux. PVAT, in particular, has been recognized as an active source of metabolic intermediates—such as lactate and β-hydroxybutyrate—that can modulate histone modifications in adjacent VSMC. However, systematic studies addressing the intercellular crosstalk between PVAT and medial VSMC are limited.

Advances in high-resolution epigenomic technologies have provided new opportunities to dissect these regulatory networks. Spatial omics platforms, including mass spectrometry-based histone mapping and single-cell chromatin accessibility assays, enable precise localization of histone modifications within heterogeneous vascular tissues. Moreover, CRISPR–dCas9-based epigenetic editing has opened new avenues for locus-specific chromatin modulation. These tools provide the means to causally link histone modifications to gene expression and the cellular phenotype in vivo, but technical challenges regarding specificity, delivery, and long-term safety remain to be addressed.

In summary, histone modifications offer promising entry points for deciphering the epigenetic basis of AAA and developing novel therapeutic approaches. Future research should strive for a deeper mechanistic understanding, improved target selectivity, and an integrated view of multicellular interactions within the vascular wall. Only by bridging molecular insights with translational strategies can epigenetic therapy move toward clinical applicability in AAA and related vascular diseases.
