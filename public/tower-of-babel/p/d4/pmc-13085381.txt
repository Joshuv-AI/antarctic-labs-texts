Low Chimney Cutaneous Ureterostomy (LCCU): a novel technique for supra-vesical urinary diversion

Background

Supra-vesical urinary diversion is rarely needed in current pediatric urology practice. Developments in reconstructive surgery, pediatric endoscopy, and anesthesia have promoted early definitive management of the majority of congenital obstructive uropathies when indicated [1, 2]. The use of clean intermittent catheterization (CIC) and continuous antibiotic prophylaxis (CAP), together with the understanding of the natural history of these pathologies have resulted in the development of safe and successful conservative measures and a further decrease in urinary diversion procedures [3, 4].

Early ureteral reimplantation of a clinically significant refluxing or obstructed ureter is the ultimate management; however, it has technical limitations in the first year of life because of the small bladder capacity and the possible negative effect of surgery on bladder growth and maturation [5]. Therefore, newborns and infants with serious renal impairment, breakthrough pyelonephritis, and urinary sepsis with obstructed or refluxing megaureter are still candidates for temporary urinary diversion [1, 5–8].

In this uncommon situation, we are confronted with the fact that all the commonly used drainage methods have limitations. Nephrostomy tubes are difficult to handle by the family and present a poor long-term solution [4, 6, 9]. Endoscopic dilatation and stenting of the obstructed ureter is not always successful, an open procedure is needed in more than two thirds of the cases, it includes the involvement of a foreign body with its inherent drawbacks and not a suitable solution for the refluxing ureter [10]. Refluxing reimplantation is not a simple procedure; it exposes the upper urinary tract to the hazards of reflux and disturbs the anatomy of the lower ureteric segment, making final reconstruction more difficult in the recurrent surgical field [11].

Ureterostomy is an easy and effective alternative for decompressing the upper urinary tract [2, 4, 8]; however, none of the current techniques provide an optimal type of diversion. End ureterostomy (EU) and low-loop ureterostomy (LLU) do not allow urinary bladder cycling especially in bilateral cases and those with solitary kidneys. EU cannot drain the urinary bladder with a refluxing ureter. Sober’s ureterostomy allows immediate elimination of high intrarenal pressure together with urinary bladder cycling; however, it is inconvenient for the family to take care of, and closure requires a separate procedure during reconstruction with an extra scar [12, 13].

We present a novel low cutaneous ureterostomy technique based on the idea of uretero-uretero-cutaneoustomy en-Y (UUC en-Y) that allows ureteral drainage through an incision in the suprapubic skin crease in the diaper-covered area together with the simultaneous passage of urine through the lower ureteric segment to the urinary bladder for cycling or from it for drainage in cases of vesicoureteral reflux (VUR).

The ureterostomy technique

Figure 1 illustrates the general principle of this technique in cases of obstructed and refluxing ureters. Surgery was performed through a 2 cm incision at the lateral end of Pfannenstiel incision (Fig. 2-A). An oblique incision was used in the early cases, but was changed to a more cosmetic transverse incision. The sheath was incised using cutting monopolar diathermy, followed by splitting of the abdominal wall muscles. The peritoneum was deviated medially. The ureter was dissected, a loop of the ureter was exteriorized, its tortuosity was straightened, taking care to keep its adventitia intact to preserve the blood supply (Fig. 2-B), and the ureter was then divided (Fig. 2-C). In the obstructed cases, the ureter was divided between its lower and middle thirds, and the distal segment was anastomosed to the proximal segment, end to side, in a location permitting tension free anastomosis without kinking the ureter using 5/0 absorbable sutures (Fig. 2-D). The distal end of the proximal ureter was exteriorized through the same incision, 4–6 cm from the anastomosis site. The 4–6 cm segment will keep the anastomosis site away from the scar of the skin and muscle incision, facilitating the undiversion procedure, and will keep the ureter in its normal anatomical course without kinking. This segment can be longer if the procedure is used in older children. The extra ureteral length was excised to prevent tortuosity. The ureter was fixed to the sheath with four absorbable 4/0 stiches, the rest of the incision was closed in layers, and the stoma was fixed to the skin (Fig. 2-F). Both the proximal and distal segments of the ureter were stented using 6-Fr or 8-Fr feeding tubes for 2 days. This design allows for drainage of the kidney with free passage of urine from the proximal to the distal ureteral segments for urinary bladder cycling (Fig. 1). We used this design whenever the aim was to drain the kidney together with urinary bladder cycling.

In the refluxing ureters, the ureter was divided in a more proximal location, and the anastomosis was made between the distal end of the proximal ureteric segment and the side of the distal ureteric segment to facilitate the urinary flow from the urinary bladder through the ureterostomy (Fig. 2-E). Under these conditions, the diversion will drain the urine from the kidney together with the urine from the urinary bladder for bilateral urinary drainage.

Follow up evaluation was performed clinically, laboratory, and by US one-month post-operatively, every three months for one year and every six months after the first year until the time of undiversion.

Undiversion was performed at the time of ureteral reimplantation, when the child reached a suitable age for definitive reconstruction. Evaluation of the resolution of HN using US, assessment of the urinary bladder volume and its morphology by VCUG were performed before undiversion. Cystoscopy was performed at the time of reimplantation. Evaluation of both kidneys using DMSA or MAG 3 and urodynamic studies were performed as indicated.

The undiversion technique

Figure 3 illustrates the general principle of the undiversion technique. Dissection of the chimney was performed with limited dissection of the ureter at the site of the previous anastomosis through the same incision of the definitive reconstruction (Figs. 3-A and 4). The chimney was cut in flush with the ureter and excised (Fig. 3-B). The ureteral opening was closed with 5/0 absorbable sutures (Fig. 3-C). Ureteral reimplantation was performed using politano-lead-better reimplantation technique with psoas hitch. Tailoring was performed in 6 ureters.

Results

This study included 11 patients with 13 operated ureters for whom diversion was performed without any recorded perioperative complications. Bilateral diversion was done in two patients (case no. 2 and case no. 11). In all patients, there was an improvement in the dilatation of the upper urinary tract on the diverted side (Figs. 5-A & 5-B). In the cases of bilateral reflux and unilateral drainage, improvement was observed on both sides (Figs. 5-C to 5-F). All patients were maintained on suppressive antibiotic prophylaxis until the time of undiversion. No breakthrough pyelonephritis was reported during the diversion period. Meatal stenosis was detected in two cases without associated progressive hydronephrosis and without any indication for intervention. In the two cases of bilateral ureterostomy, urination was recorded by the family through the native urethra with variable amounts of urine.

Undiversion was performed in seven patients with nine ureters after a median time of 13 months (6–22). The patients’ criteria at the time of undiversion and follow up are demonstrated in Table 2. Four patients were not undiverted. Two patients (Cases no. 5 & 7) with neurogenic bladder and bilateral vesicoureteral reflux were presented with recurrent attacks of pyelonephritis with poor compliance to CIC managed with the same technique. There was a discrepancy in the renal function of both kidneys in DMSA scan. Unilateral ureterostomy was performed draining the more promising kidney through the ureterostomy, and the urinary bladder through the distal ureteral segment. Both cases are not yet undiverted; one of them has cerebral palsy, the family is satisfied with the ureterostomy, and it could be permanent (Case no. 7). Two cases were operated soon and were not yet ready for the undiversion at the time of data collection (Cases no. 5 & 9). No deterioration in the upper urinary tract was detected in the follow up period in any of the cases (Figs. 6-E & 6-F). One patient with PUV and a solitary dysplastic kidney (Case no. 3) died due to chest infections related to his pulmonary hypoplasia at the age of 15 months.

Discussion

An optimal ureterostomy should be feasible to be performed in newborns and infants with minimal morbidity, provide proper drainage of the upper urinary tract while maintaining urinary bladder cycling, preserve the blood supply of the lower ureteric segment intact for further reconstruction, allow for further reconstruction without extra scars, and be convenient for the family to take care of.

In our LCCU technique, ureterostomy was performed through the end of the pfannenstiel incision, which will be used for definitive reconstruction in the future. This avoids the main drawback of Sober’s ureterostomy and upper loupe ureterostomies (ULU) which drain the upper urinary tract properly at the cost of an additional scar at the time of the definitive reconstruction [14].

Unlike loop ureterostomies, LCCU allows straightening of the ureteral kinks and proper drainage of the upper urinary tract. We intend not to dissect the lower ureteral segment to maintain its blood supply and the surgical field of definitive reconstruction untouched. This made reimplantation feasible in the regular pattern, with any chosen technique, avoiding the classic main drawback of ureterostomy, which is the added difficulty in definitive reconstruction [1].

The location of the LCCU is contained in the diaper covered area. No extra care or appliances were needed, except for the regular care of the baby and the application of a soothing ointment to the stoma and its surrounding skin. Sober’s ureterostomy and ULU require special care and may need appliances that are inconvenient to the family. End ureterostomy (EU) is convenient for the family, it allows for reconstruction through the same incision without extra scars, but disturbs the anatomy of the lower ureteral segment and the surgical field of future ureteral reimplantation, adding to the difficulties of future definitive management. It does not allow for urinary bladder cycling and in order to achieve proper drainage of the upper urinary tract through it, the ureteral kinks should be properly straightened which may cause ureteral shortening and may compromise the future ureteral reimplantation. LLU has the same advantages as end ureterostomy in terms of convenience to the family and scar location. It maintains the blood supply of the lower ureter and the surgical field of the future reconstruction unaffected; however, it does not allow for proper urinary bladder cycling and does not provide proper drainage of the upper urinary tract in the presence of ureteral kinks [14].

Some reports show that early high diversion, which prevents urinary bladder cycling, may alter the phenotype of bladder wall cells, leading to more permanent infiltration of detrusor collagen affecting future urinary bladder function, especially in infancy and early childhood [15–17]. The chimney design allows for the passage of urine to the urinary bladder for cycling while draining the upper urinary tract. Our two cases with bilateral diversion (case no. 2 & 11) showed improvement in urinary bladder capacity and morphology, permitting bilateral ureteral reimplantation without bladder augmentation with maintained improvement of the upper urinary tract. Sober’s ureterostomy allows for urinary bladder cycling, but has drawbacks regarding location and care difficulties. To solve this issue in the solitary kidney using the LLU technique, Tekgul et-al reported a technique of urinary bladder wash through lower end ureteral catheterization and bladder wash which may increase the family difficulties and cause infections [18].

Conclusion

LCCU is a safe method for supra-vesical urinary diversion with satisfactory results. The location of the stoma makes care easy for families and enables the reversal of the stoma with ureteral reimplantation using the same incision. The technique keeps the blood supply and the anatomical field of the lower ureteral segment intact for the definitive procedure. We suggest using this technique whenever temporary or permanent supra-vesical urinary diversion is required.
