Robotic assisted reverse total shoulder arthroplasty: narrative review and surgical technique of humeral and glenoid preparation
Authors: Erick M. Marigi, Cristobal E. Lopez, Ian M. Marigi, Thomas R. Duquin, John W. Sperling
Published: 2026

Shoulder arthroplasty (SA) in the form of an anatomic total shoulder arthroplasty (aTSA) and reverse total shoulder arthroplasty (rTSA) remains a reliable treatment option for a number of glenohumeral joint conditions with successful outcomes.5,10,27,29 Over the years, the use of SA has continued to exponentially grow due to a better understanding of the indications, widespread teaching, and advancement in implant technology.3,6,21 Similar to any joint replacement surgery, the proper selection of components, correction of pathologic anatomy, and correct positioning remains crucial to implant longevity.4,12,26

During SA, accurate component placement is one of the most challenging aspects of this surgery, which is why continuous efforts have been made to develop tools that improve both pre-operative planning and the intraoperative execution of component placement, especially the glenoid component.1,25,27,32 Pre-operative planning software (PPS) enables surgical planning through three-dimensional (3D) reconstruction based on computed tomography (CT). This modern software has enhanced the ability to understand the presenting deformity, select the ideal component, and generate the desired position of the components. While PPS has great value in the pre-operative space, the intraoperative execution is limited to correlating the digital plan to physical landmarks to achieve the desired target.1,14,18,32

As such, there has been an evolution of technologies to assist with execution, which aims to achieve the pre-operative plan more accurately than with free-hand surgery. These options include patient-specific instrumentation (PSI), intraoperative navigation technology (NAV), virtual, augmented, or mixed reality technology, and robotic assistance.1,8,32 The use of PSI aims to leverage each individual patient's anatomy to design instruments that enable precise positioning of the central pin as preplanned.15 Many studies have shown greater concordance between the planned and achieved results in both aTSA and rTSA compared to conventional instrumentation.1,16 However, PSI has some drawbacks, such as the need for greater exposure to identify anatomical landmarks used for implant creation, as well as higher costs and manufacturing time.1,16,27,32

NAV allows for uploading of PPS data to a passive system that provides real-time feedback on the position and direction of the central pin and components via optical sensors.28 Although in some studies it has shown improved implant positioning and orientation similar to PSI, long-term studies are still needed to evaluate its outcomes.1,28,32 Augmented or mixed reality support allows for better understanding of the patient's scapular anatomy and the projection of the surgical plan onto the patient's anatomy using virtual landmarks to guide the positioning of the central pin and other components.8 However, this requires greater surgical exposure to align the anatomy with the augmented reality model, and consideration must be given to camera disturbances due to head movements or depth perception errors caused by interposed anatomical structures.11,24

Despite all the disadvantages and challenges of these new technologies, they undoubtedly present concrete opportunities to improve surgical outcomes. Even so, future studies are needed to further analyze real benefits and long-term patient outcomes. Nevertheless, there is still much room for improvement, for example adopting technologies that have already been successfully used in other joint replacement surgeries, such as robot-assisted surgery.1,19,23,27

Robotic assistance is not new to orthopedics. Robotic assistance is already widely used in total hip arthroplasty (THA) and total knee arthroplasty (TKA), with an expected increase in adoption amongst surgeons. In 2023 this technology crossed over to the shoulder with the first case report of a 3D image-guided robot-assisted rTSA was published by Han et al13 from China. Subsequently, in 2024, the first robotic-assisted TSA was performed in North America at the Mayo Clinic using the Zimmer Biomet Robotic Surgical Assistant (ROSA) system (Zimmer Biomet, Warsaw, IN, USA). Robotic assistance involves uploading the PPS created plan to the robot software, which then, through optical sensors, guides the robotic arm assisting in the surgery.27 The robotic arm, using planning data combined with real-time tracking from optical markers attached to the patient, functions as a guide to define the direction, depth, and orientation of cuts, reaming, and implant positioning.27 Robot-assisted surgery appears to be a promising tool for improving the outcomes of SA, especially given that the planning is performed with millimetric precision and in great detail.23 This enables a more accurate execution, potentially leading to better results. The aim of this article is to describe the ROSA Shoulder System and its surgical technique to perform a robotic-assisted reverse total shoulder replacement.

Robotic Surgical Assistant shoulder system

The ROSA system consists of 2 units: one corresponds to the robotic arm and the other to the optical unit, which includes an infrared camera mounted on a separate arm. This setup enables communication with the optical markers implanted in the patient's humerus and coracoid. Operating in a semiactive mode, the robot assists in both humeral and glenoid preparation through 3 modes: automatic, collaborative, and static. In automatic mode, the robot positions the arm in the surgical area. Once in place, it switches to collaborative mode, allowing the surgeon to move the arm within the limits established in the surgical plan. After the final position is determined, the robot transitions to static mode to perform precise cuts or reaming.

Humeral head resection is performed through robotic positioning of an extramedullary cutting guide, whose final position is adjusted in collaborative mode. The robotic arm controls the cut guide along the plane of the planned resection and the guide is then secured to the humerus with pins. The robot is then placed in static mode and the humeral cut is performed through this guide at the planned version, inclination, and height. The cut surface of the humerus is then validated to confirm appropriate resection of the humeral head. Following validation the remainder of the humeral preparation, including reaming and broaching, is performed manually.

Glenoid preparation is performed with a conventional reamer attached to the robotic arm, which is powered by a standard cordless reamer. This allows glenoid reaming, controlled by the robot with live tracking in collaborative mode, to the desired version, inclination, and depth. Bone preparation for aTSA involves 2 robotic controlled steps. The first reamer prepares the face of the glenoid to the correct version, inclination, and depth. The second prepares the central hole for the hybrid glenoid central post. The remaining glenoid preparation for the implant performed manually. In rTSA, the glenoid is prepared in a single-step ream for the glenoid face and central boss with the robot controlling the version, inclination, and depth in collaborative mode.

Surgical technique

Pre-operative planning

Pre-operative planning follows a routine approach for most commercially available software platforms. A shoulder CT scan obtained following a thin-slice protocol, with subsequent Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine files uploaded to the Zimmer Biomet Signature ONE Surgical Planning System (Zimmer Biomet, Warsaw, IN, USA). Notably, the field of view should include the entire scapula and proximal third of the humerus, and axial slice thickness should be 1 mm or less. Implants are then selected and positioned based on surgeon specific targets that incorporate patient size and bone loss (Fig. 1). The plan is then finalized and uploaded to the ROSA shoulder system, with selective landmarks generated for intraoperative registration (Fig. 2).Figure 1Pre-operative surgical plan displayed in the screens of the ROSA robotic system. Patient positioning and robot setup

After the induction of general anesthesia, the patient is placed in a low beach-chair position, with the back of the table elevated to 35°. The surgical arm is prepped and draped in a sterile manner, ensuring full access to the shoulder and extending the field medial to the coracoid process. A towel is wrapped around the head and neck region to avoid interference of the drapes with visualization of the robotic trackers. The robotic tracker is attached to the arm that extends above the surgical field, and the sterile drapes are applied to the robotic arm. The alignment tracker tool is applied to the end of the robotic arm. The robot is then positioned using the alignment tool positioned in line with the scapula and over the lateral edge of the acromion. The optical camera is then positioned on the opposite side of the table using the alignment tracker to confirm appropriate orientation of the robotic arm and the optical camera. Robotic registration is achieved by placing the arm in 6 orientations, allowing the robot to register its position relative to the optical camera.

Approach and initial exposure

A standard deltopectoral approach is performed, with an incision that allows complete exposure of the coracoid and deltoid mobilization (Supplementary Video S1). The subacromial and subdeltoid spaces are released, and a biceps tenodesis is performed to the pectoralis tendon. The biceps sheath is released through the intertubercular groove and rotator interval. The biceps tendon is released at the articular margin to expose the groove fully, which is essential for humeral registration. The subscapularis is released using tenotomy, peel, or lesser tuberosity osteotomy. If using an osteotomy, it is critical not to violate the biceps groove, which is used for registration. The inferior capsule is released, and the humeral head is dislocated anteriorly. After dislocation, any remaining cartilage is removed from the anterior and superior humeral head. Removal of the cartilage in the regions used for registration is critical for successful registration of the humeral anatomy, which is based on the subchondral bone on the CT scan.

Humeral tracker positioning

The humeral optical tracker is attached to the proximal humerus using the proximal humeral clamp. The clamp is designed to slide over the lateral aspect of the proximal humerus distal to the intertubercular groove (Fig. 3). The clamp is initial secured by tightening the screw that closes the main articulation of the clamp. Once the main clamp is secure on the bone, the stabilization screws are tightened to obtain rigid stability of the clamp. The robotic optical array is attached to the clamp and positioned to allow for visualization by the optical camera (Fig. 4). The optical array can be positioned in 3 locations to allow for optimal visualization. In most cases, the negative position provides the best visualization of the tracker. The height of the table or position of the arm may need to be adjusted to allow for visualization of the humeral array. The stability of the array fixation should be confirmed to ensure it will not move position during the humeral preparation.Figure 3Humeral clamp placement on the proximal humerus.Figure 4Humeral clamp and humeral optical array positioned on the humerus.

Humeral bone registration

With the tracker in place, registration of the humeral anatomy is then performed. Registration is the process of informing the optical navigation system of the location of the humeral anatomy. Registration allows the optical navigation system to track the location and movement of the humerus and relay this to the robotic arm. This is performed by using the pointer to digitize points on the medial and lateral epicondyles and 3 regions on the proximal humerus (Fig. 5). The first region is along the anterior rim of the humeral articular surface. The second is superior along the humeral articular surface. The third is in the biceps groove. The key to successful registration is to take a moment to identify the specific area on the humeral anatomy that is to be digitized. The region is identified on the 3D model of the proximal humerus created from the CT scan and projected on the user interface screen. The identification of anatomic landmarks can be helpful to locate the area to register on the proximal humerus. Registration works best when the outline of the registration area is digitized first and then adding additional points as needed until the area and density is appropriate.Figure 5Humeral registration across various landmarks utilizing the probe.

Humeral registration across various landmarks utilizing the probe.

Intraoperative planning adjustment and humeral head osteotomy

Once registration is complete, the humeral plan is reviewed (Supplementary Video S2). With the addition of the epicondylar axis, the humeral version is presented on the user interface. Adjustments can be made to the humeral implant position at the surgeon's discretion. After approval of the plan, the robotic arm is moved to the humeral cutting guide attachment position in automatic mode. The cutting guide is then attached to the robotic arm, and the cut guide is positioned along the planned humeral resection plane in collaborative mode. In this step, the robotic arm will be held on the version, inclination, and height of humeral head resection but can be rotated or translated on the cut plane to allow for optimal positioning of the guide for the humeral cut. The cutting guide is then pinned to the proximal humerus, with the first pin placed in collaborative mode and the second with the robot in static mode (Fig. 6). The robot is held in static mode during the humeral resection. It is critical that the arm position is not changed after pinning the guide to the humerus to avoid damage to the bone. In anticipation of this, it is important to position the arm and retractors for the humeral cut before the guide is pinned to the humerus. The humeral resection is performed using guide with the saw capture. The cutting guide is then unpinned and removed from the robot arm, and the humeral resection is validated using the pointer with the humeral validation tool attached. The user interface will display the version, inclination, and height of the humeral head resection. Values that are within 2° or 2 mm will be displayed with a green check mark. The remainder of the humeral bone preparation for the desired implant is then performed using manual instrumentation.Figure 6Robotic insertion of the humeral cut guide.

Robotic insertion of the humeral cut guide.

Glenoid exposure and coracoid tracker placement

After humeral head resection, the glenoid is fully exposed, and the residual cartilage is meticulously removed to allow optimal contact for reaming. The glenoid reamer can initially be positioned manually to confirm that surgical exposure is adequate and that the robotic arm will have unobstructed access, avoiding interference from retractors or the proximal humerus. The scapula tracker is then attached to the coracoid process using 2 2.5-mm pins with threaded tips (Fig. 7). The first pin is inserted at the base of the coracoid, just anterior to the coracoclavicular ligaments, and angled posteriorly and laterally along to extend down the column of the coracoid attachment to the scapula. The coracoid tracker base that corresponds to the operative side is then placed over the first pin and positioned along the superior surface of the coracoid. A second pin is placed through a guide on the tracker base at an optimal angle to ensure secure fixation, taking care to insert it across 1 cortex to minimize the risk of injury to adjacent neurovascular structures. The scapular array is attached to the base at an angle that provides optimal camera visualization.Figure 7Coracoid array tracker secured on the coracoid process using 2 pins.

Coracoid array tracker secured on the coracoid process using 2 pins.

Glenoid bone registration

Glenoid registration is then performed with a similar method as the humerus, using 3 dedicated regions on the scapula (Fig. 8). The first region is along the anterior rim of the glenoid, the second is the surface of the glenoid face, and the third is at the base of the coracoid process. These regions are sequentially painted using the registration pointer, guided by highlighted zones displayed on the user interface. Optimal registration is achieved by first outlining the perimeter of each region displayed on the user interface and then it with sufficient surface points to meet the required density threshold. After successful registration, the system prompts a verification step in which specific glenoid areas are touched with the pointer. The interface provides real-time visual feedback, indicating both the anatomical location of the pointer tip and its distance from the bone surface, allowing the surgeon to confirm registration accuracy. The verification screen can be revisited at any time intraoperatively if there is concern about possible displacement of the tracking arrays.Figure 8Glenoid registration across various landmarks using the probe.

Glenoid registration across various landmarks using the probe.

Glenoid bone preparation

Glenoid bone preparation is performed after registration verification is completed. The robotic arm is moved in automatic mode to the reaming axis for the glenoid implant based on the pre-operative plan. The glenoid reamer corresponding to the selected implant is then attached to the robotic arm. The glenoid is then reamed with robotic guidance in collaborative mode (Fig. 9). In this mode, the robotic arm will control the version, inclination, and depth of the glenoid reaming while compensating for scapular motion in real time. The robot arm is advanced by the surgeon and should proceed at a slow pace to avoid excessive scapular movements. It is important to advance the robot by pushing in-line with the reaming axis without the need for excessive force. If there is difficulty advancing the reamer, there may be interference with a retractor, or the force applied may be off-axis. The reaming depth is displayed on the user interface, and a green check mark confirms when the planned depth has been reached. The reamer is then removed, and the prepared surface is validated using the glenoid attachment on the pointer. The version, inclination, and depth of reaming will be displayed, and green check marks will appear for less than 2 mm or 2° of variance from the plan. Once validated, standard instrumentation is used to complete glenoid component implantation (Fig. 10).Figure 9Robotic assisted glenoid reaming using an implant specific reamer mounted on the robotic arm.Figure 10(A) Reamed glenoid prepared for baseplate implantation. Trail and soft tissue balancing

A trial reduction is performed to assess and optimize soft-tissue tension and range of motion. While robotic implantation accurately executes pre-operative planning based on the CT scan anatomy, which can assist in soft-tissue balancing, intraoperative adjustments may be necessary to achieve optimal soft-tissue balance. Modularity of the humeral and glenoid components, along with Versa Dial technology for controlling the center-of-rotation offset, allows for optimization of implant positioning for soft-tissue tensioning. Closure and rehabilitation

The subscapularis is repaired and the deltopectoral interval closed, followed by skin closure with absorbable sutures. Post-operative rehabilitation includes sling immobilization for 3 weeks in the rTSA setting and 6 weeks for aTSA setting. Early passive and active assisted range of motion with physical therapy is initiated but avoiding active use of the arm. Light activities are typically permitted at 6 weeks, with progression to full activity by 3 months.

Surgical outcomes

Preliminary data from 62 robotic assisted TSA procedures with a minimum follow-up of 90 days demonstrated high accuracy. Of these, 25 were aTSA and 37 were rTSA. Mean absolute differences between planned and validated placement were 1.12° for humeral version, 0.69° for humeral inclination, and 1.04 mm for humeral depth. For the glenoid component, mean deviations were 0.88° in version, 1.39° in inclination, and 0.69 mm in reaming depth. Over 90% of implants were positioned within 2° or 2 mm of the pre-operative plan for both components. There were a total of 2 (3.2%) complications: 1 (1.6%) patient with acromial stress fracture and 1 (1.6%) patient with a periprosthetic humeral shaft fracture. At final follow-up, 1 (1.6%) patient was reoperated for a cutaneous abscess in the lateral chest wall at 4 months postsurgery. No intraoperative or early post-operative complications related to the robotic system occurred.

Discussion

This is the first article to describe and demonstrate a robotic-assisted shoulder replacement technique involving humeral and glenoid preparation. The surgery was performed using the ROSA Shoulder System, consisting of a robotic arm to assess the humeral cut and glenoid reaming. Robotic-assisted SA can serve as an option to assist with executing the pre-operative plan designed by the surgeon with the objective of obtaining the desired component placement with the hopes of reducing complications and improving outcomes.

Compared to other advanced execution techniques, similar or on occasion less exposure is needed within the surgical field compared to PSI, which allows for more careful handling of soft tissues.32 In addition, having tracking navigators installed during the procedure allows for a complete orientation of the humerus or scapula despite the movement of the patient during the procedure, similarly to NAV surgery. Compared to mixed reality, there are fewer camera disturbances, as the infrared camera communicating with the pin trackers is in the robot and not the surgeon's head.11

As mentioned previously, this type of surgery has already been extensively studied and implemented in TKA and THA. In TKA, this technology has improved the accuracy of bony cuts, femoral and tibial component positioning, and soft-tissue balancing.2,31 In THA, robotics has led more precise reaming of the acetabulum and more accurate positioning of the acetabular cup.2,9,20 Another example of robot-assisted surgery is with spine surgery. In this field, robotic assistance has been primarily used for the placement of pedicle screws, although efforts are continuously being made to expand its applications.7,22 This technique consists of a robotic arm that, through PPS, helps define the position, orientation, and length of the pedicle screws, and also guides their placement.7,22 Studies have shown that robotic assistance improves the accuracy of screw positioning and reduces complications such as blood loss and the need for revision surgery.7,17,30

Large-scale implementation of robotic assisted SA will depend on multiple factors. Currently, robotic surgery for TKA and THA has experienced significant adoption with many centers already equipped with the robot. Therefore, for these centers, adopting robotic-assisted SA will require less barriers. In addition, the use of PPS is common in SA, making the technique familiar to most surgeons. The challenge may lie in centers that have not yet adopted robotic surgery, as they may find it challenging to justify the investment due to the novelty of the technology and lack of long-term outcome data to justify the increased cost.

The disadvantages associated with the implementation of robotic-assisted SA are not limited solely to the costs involved. This surgery will entail a steep learning curve for surgeons already accustomed to other techniques, and it could also introduce a cognitive bias in trainee surgeons. Furthermore, in centers that do not yet have the robot, it will be necessary to modify operating rooms to provide sufficient space for the installation of the robotic unit.

The future challenge for maximizing benefits lies in evolving robotic systems to enable soft-tissue balancing and range of motion assessment. Although these features are not currently part of robotic-assisted TSA, their integration would represent a significant advancement in the procedure.23

Conclusion

The utilization of SA is increasing and is expected to continue rising. Therefore, the implementation of advanced technologies to enhance surgical outcomes remains importance, with robotic assistance serving as an excellent tool to assist with execution.1,32 This study is reports on a robotic-assisted SA technique involving humeral and glenoid preparation. This represents an initial step toward the integration of this technique and highlights some technical pearls to consider for adoption of robotics.
