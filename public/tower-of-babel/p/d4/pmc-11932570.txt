Microscopic evaluation of the effect of low-level laser therapy on platelet-rich fibrin: An in vitro study
Authors: Sruthi Kallayil Suresh, Girija Venkatesh Arishinagodi, Mallanagouda B. Patil
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Background:Platelet-rich fibrin (PRF) serves as a natural fibrin-based biomaterial scaffold, facilitating cellular attachment. Low-level laser therapy (LLLT) may influence PRF properties through its stimulatory effects.Aim:The study aimed to evaluate the effect of LLLT on PRF architectural changes and platelet concentrate values.Materials and Methods:Thirty-four samples from seventeen subjects were included in the study, with 20 mL of blood collected from each participant. Blood was distributed into two 10 mL vacutainer tubes: one served as a control for baseline platelet and leukocyte counts, whereas the other was used for PRF preparation. The PRF samples were vertically cut into two equal parts and divided into two groups: Group I (without laser treatment) and Group II (treated with LLLT). Histological preparations were performed for all samples. The mean and standard deviation of platelet and leukocyte counts in Groups I and II were analyzed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) version 17.1. Analysis of variance (ANOVA) and post hoc tests were employed for comparisons.Results:Platelet and leukocyte values showed statistically significant differences, with greater cellular entrapment in Group II compared to Group I. The residual serum cell count in both groups was lower than the whole blood cell count. Microscopically, Group I displayed dense, thick fibrils with increased spacing distally, whereas Group II exhibited thinner fibrils with gradual density changes toward the distal end.Conclusion:PRF obtained in both groups was similar in size. Laser irradiation altered the fibrin architecture and enhanced platelet and leukocyte entrapment in PRF.

INTRODUCTION

Low-level laser therapy (LLLT) refers to irradiation with red beam or near-infrared lasers characterized by a wavelength of 600–1100 nm, an output power of 1–500 mW, and an energy density of 0.04–50 J/cm2.[1] LLLT has been shown to increase the proliferation of mesenchymal stem cells and enhance the upregulation of growth factors relevant to periodontal regeneration, making it an effective adjunct in periodontal regenerative surgeries.[2]

Advancements in laser technology and the use of platelet-rich fibrin (PRF) in the regenerative surgical field are increasing. However, the question of whether leukocytes or fibrin play a more prominent role in PRF scaffolds remains unresolved. The scaffold material, its architectural design, and its bioactivity significantly influence tissue regeneration and define the ultimate shape of newly formed soft or hard tissue.[3]

Although platelet growth factors play an important role in the biology of PRF, the fibrin architecture and leukocyte content are two key parameters. Most studies rarely assess leukocyte content and almost never analyze the fibrin structure of PRF. Considering the importance of fibrin architecture, which directly influences the biology of all fibrin-based biomaterials,[4] only a few studies have been conducted to explore the architectural changes and cellular composition of PRF.

This study is the first of its kind to evaluate and analyze the architectural changes of second-generation PRF. Its purpose was to examine the composition and architecture of the PRF clot and identify the structural differences between laser-treated and nonlaser-treated PRF.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This descriptive histological in vitro study was conducted at the Department of Periodontics. Ethical clearance was obtained from the Institutional Review Board. Subjects aged 18 to 55 years participated after providing informed consent. The study adhered to the Helsinki Declaration of 1975, revised in 2000.

Medical and dental histories and hematological values were recorded.

Thirty-four samples from seventeen subjects were obtained. Each subject provided 20 mL of blood distributed into two 10 mL test tubes per the WHO guidelines.[5] One test tube was used for baseline cell counts, and the other underwent centrifugation using Choukroun’s PRF protocol (3000 rpm, 10 minutes).[56] PRF clots were split vertically into two parts: Group I (control, 17 samples) without laser treatment and Group II (experimental, 17 samples) treated with laser irradiation.

Laser irradiation

Group II PRF samples were placed in a dappen dish. Laser irradiation (810 nm, 0.1 W, 60 seconds, continuous noncontact mode) was delivered, starting at the red blood cell (RBC) end of the clot and moving upward horizontally. The laser tip was kept 1 mm from the clot.

Histological processing and examination

PRF samples were fixed, dehydrated in graded alcohol (60%, 80%, 100%), cleared in xylene, and embedded in paraffin. Sections (5 μm thick) were cut along the clot’s long axis, stained with hematoxylin and eosin, and examined under light microscopy at ×10 and ×40 magnifications.

Cell count

Platelet and leukocyte concentrations in PRF were evaluated using an automated cell counter. Concentrations were indirectly calculated by subtracting residual serum values from baseline whole blood values.

Histological observations

Macroscopically

All the PRF clots were divided into two sections and the incision lines were clearly visible in between the clots. While irradiating the PRF clots with low-level laser, the color at the end of the PRF clot, that is, near the RBC base, changed into a darker red, which could be appreciated with the naked eye.

Microscopically

The slides were first observed with the naked eye, the purple line could be seen. The slides were microscopically observed from this line of demarcation. This line of demarcation was observed due to the platelet and leukocyte aggregates. The slide was then observed mesial and distal to this junction at all the areas to look for any changes in the fibrin architecture, the type of cells present, and the distribution pattern of the cells entrapped in the fibrin.

The stained sections of PRF clot, in all the groups, were assessed histologically in terms of the following under ×10 and ×40 magnification: (i) Cells: leukocytes, platelets, and RBCs were observed based on whether they are present or absent, its arrangement in clustered or scattered form and the shape of the cells if appreciated. (ii) Fibrin architecture: described based on the arrangement of the fibrin if it is dense or loose, the pattern of fibrin arrangement if it is in a uniform or a haphazard pattern, and also the spacing between the fibrin architecture is described. (iii) Cell distribution in the fibrin architecture: the distribution of the cell aggregates is described as linear or haphazard, and also the demarcation between the fibrin and cells if it is well defined or not.

Group I

Cells

Under ×10, leukocytes, platelets, and RBCs were present, and all were clustered together, and the shape of the cells was not appreciated between the leukocytes and the platelets as they were clumped together. Under ×40, Under ×40, leukocytes and platelets were aggregated but could not be differentiated, RBCs were seen below the interspersed between the leukocyte and platelets. The shape of the RBCs can be appreciated as round and discoid with a central lighter hue than the periphery.

At ×10, leukocytes, platelet aggregates, and fibrin matrix were distinctly demarcated, with linear cell aggregate arrangement. Group II

Cells

At ×10, leukocytes, platelets, and RBCs appeared clustered, with cell shapes indistinct. At ×40, leukocytes and platelets were aggregated but undifferentiated. RBCs were visible below the aggregate layer, interspersed among leukocytes and platelets, showing a round, discoid shape with a lighter center.

At ×10, fibrin showed a dense, uniform arrangement with no visible spacing and clear demarcation from dark purple cells. DISCUSSION

PRF is recognized as a biomaterial with significant healing potential, attributed to its unique fibrin structure that traps circulating stem cells, aiding in the regeneration of large osseous defects by promoting stem cell differentiation into osteoblasts. PRF facilitates cellular adhesion, spreading, and growth factor modulation, contributing to tissue regeneration. This is supported by the extracellular matrix’s components, including collagen, fibronectin, elastin, and proteoglycans. The fibrin scaffold enables microvascularization, guides epithelial migration, and serves as a vehicle for cells involved in tissue regeneration, highlighting its critical role in regenerative therapies.[6]

The fibrin structure’s porosity, rigidity, and pH significantly influence tissue regeneration outcomes. Proper handling of PRF is crucial as delays in usage after centrifugation can lead to dehydration, reduced growth factor content, and compromised structural integrity. Studies, including Dohan et al.,[78] Jiménez-Aristazábal et al.,[9] Jasmine et al.,[10] Bhatnagar et al.,[11] and Bai et al.[12] emphasize the importance of fibrin structure in clinical outcomes. Our study observed clustered leukocytes, platelets, and RBCs in PRF. At ×10 magnification, dense fibrin architecture with uniform arrangements and distinct demarcations was noted. At ×40, a meshwork pattern with compact fibers near the aggregate layer and increased spacing distally was evident.

Future research should explore larger sample sizes, varied age groups, and comparative analysis across healthy, gingivitis, and periodontitis patients. Advanced imaging methods such ase SEM and TEM can provide higher-resolution insights into fibrin architecture, addressing the limitations of light microscopy in accurately identifying platelet and WBC morphology. In addition, studying different laser doses and irradiation intervals could further standardize PRF protocols for optimized clinical outcomes.

CONCLUSION

This in vitro microscopic study explored the use of PRF) as a natural fibrin-based biomaterial that provides a scaffold for cell attachment, proliferation, and differentiation, ultimately forming an extracellular matrix. We examined the biostimulatory effects of LLLT on the architectural changes of PRF, including platelet concentrate values. Our findings clearly demonstrate that LLLT influences the architectural properties of PRF but does not affect cell counts in either group. As advancements in laser technology continue and the application of PRF in regenerative surgery grows, the roles of leukocytes and fibrin in PRF scaffolds remain under debate. Nonetheless, the combination of PRF and laser technology shows great promise and has the potential to significantly enhance our understanding and control of regenerative processes.
