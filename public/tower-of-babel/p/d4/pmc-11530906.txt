Intestinal microbiota composition in broilers fed protein-free or casein-based diets
Authors: Luis A. Rubio
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Growing broiler chickens of the Cobb500 strain were used to determine the effects on intestinal microbiota composition of a protein-free (PF) diet as compared to a diet based in casein (CAS) as the only protein source. CAS was formulated to contain the same amount of protein (190 g kg−1) as a commercial Maize-soy diet which was used as a practical reference. The ileal AA flow (g kg−1 dry matter intake) was significantly higher (P < 0.001) than PF in birds fed protein containing diets (CAS or Maize-soy). Taken as a whole (discriminant and ANOSIM analysis), the intestinal (ileal and caecal contents and ileal tissue) microbiota composition of PF and CAS were significantly (P < 0.001) different from Maize-soy and not different from each other in some cases. RT-qPCR and sequencing analysis of the ileal and caecal microbiota revealed significant (P < 0.05) differences in a number of bacterial groups between broilers fed PF, CAS or Maize-soy diets. The main result was that the lack of protein in the intestinal medium of PF birds resulted in a drop of Lactobacillus spp. counts (on average, 43 in PF vs 1,734 in the Maize-soy diet) and increased Enterobacteriaceae (on average, 419 in PF vs 172 in the Maize-soy diet) and other potentially pathogenic bacterial groups (in both intestinal contents and tissue). Thus, the lack of protein in the intestinal medium of PF birds resulted in a microbiota composition compatible with a pro-inflammatory state, and this effect was somewhat less marked in birds fed CAS. The results reported here suggest that the adverse effects on microbiota composition in broilers fed CAS were less marked than in those fed PF, which would be in line with a preferential use of a highly digestible protein containing diet to determine endogenous AA excretion instead of a PF diet.

INTRODUCTION

The sustainability of future poultry production needs to be improved in order to meet increased human demand for eggs and chicken meat. However, the very high productivity achieved with modern broiler strains has resulted in unintended changes such as musculoskeletal biomechanics, changes in immune response, and implications for the welfare of breeding stock (Zuidhof et al., 2014). At the same time, it is critically important to mitigate challenges to the sustainability of modern poultry production, such as pollution, the depletion of finite natural resources and animal welfare issues (Gunnarsson et al., 2020). In this context, efficiency in feed utilization is a key issue not only for economic, but also for social, welfare and ecological reasons.

Among feed components in poultry diets, protein is not only one of the major ones, but also one of the most expensive. Therefore, both nutritionally and economically, proper protein usage is essential in all feeding systems, and wasteful usage increases the cost of production (Beski et al., 2015). Protein and feed formulations for broilers are currently based on amino acids (AA) composition rather than on protein since it is recognized that birds have a requirement for AA more than for protein per se (Broiler Performance and Nutrition, 2022). Accordingly, in order to manage feed ingredients for compound feeds in a sustainable and economic manner, the proteins in feed ingredients have to be described in terms of their digestible AA contents. In fact, digestible AA values are likely to form the basis of poultry feed formulations in the future (Ravindran and Bryden, 1999; Lemme et al., 2004). But in order to formulate properly, endogenous AA secretions must be quantified to correct apparent AA digestibility (which measures the digestibility of AA of both dietary and endogenous origins) to obtain true digestibility values (that include a correction for endogenous AA secretions), which is considered to be a fundamental characteristic of the feedstuff that is relatively constant across varying protein intakes. It has been reported that up to 25% of the daily protein synthesis may be secreted into the gut (Simon et al., 1983). In consequence, one of the key issues to determine AA digestibility is the quantification of AA endogenously secreted into the intestine. However, reported endogenous AA flow values are very variable depending on the method used, and due to the complexity of the problem a number of procedures have been designed and proposed in the literature (Ravindran and Hendriks 2004; Lemme et al., 2004). The use of protein-free (PF) diets or diets based in highly digestible proteins (casein, hydrolyzed casein) are among the most common procedures due to their simplicity, convenience and cost-effectiveness (Adedokun et al., 2011; Ravindran, 2021). Nevertheless, the information as yet existing in the literature specifically on the study of basal N and AA flows at the terminal ileum in broilers is still quite scarce.

Conflicting results in the literature on protein digestibility values of the different feedstuffs are not rare due to the number and complexity of the factors potentially implicated in the amount of intestinal protein, which ultimately determines protein digestibility values (Fig. 1). In particular, there are indications suggesting that the composition of the intestinal microbiota of broilers is also likely to be modulated not only by the presence of low digestibility dietary carbohydrates, but also to dietary proteins. In fact, dietary protein has been recently shown to modulate intestinal microbiota composition in rats in the absence of other dietary factors (Rubio, 2024). Thus, significant differences in microbiota composition between broilers fed standard vs low protein diets (England et al., 2023), and protein-free vs protein containing diets (Zhou et al., 2023) have been reported, which makes it relevant to study the interaction between chicken intestinal microbiota and protein digestion (Apajalahti and Vienola, 2016). MATERIALS AND METHODS

Animals, Diets and Feeding Regimen

Day-old broiler chickens (Cobb500 strain) were locally purchased and housed in battery cages with grid floor suitable for this age and equipped with heater, feeder and drinker. The cages were located in rooms with ventilation, lighting (23 h light) and temperature controls. Room temperatures for the first, second, and third week were 35, 30, and 25°C, respectively. Feed and water were offered ad libitum. The chicks were fed a commercial Maize-soy diet for 21d before they were placed on the 4 experimental diets for 4 d (d 21–24 of age). Treatments (PF, protein-free; CAS, casein-based diet; Maize-soy, commercial diet) consisted of 6 replicates of 6 chickens each. Feed was offered in flour, and the diets were formulated to meet the nutritional needs of the birds between 1 and 25 d of age (Table 1) except PF which contained no added protein. The Maize-soy diet was a commercial practical diet for broilers which was used as a reference for physiological conditions. Chromium oxide (2 g kg−1 diet) was used as an indigestible marker to determine the ileal AA flow (see below). At 24d of age 12 birds from each treatment (2 birds per replicate) were euthanized (T61 euthanasic), and the contents from the terminal ileum (last 12 cm) collected by flushing with saline and frozen (-80 °C). After flushing, a portion of ileal tissue (0.5 cm) was also taken from the mid-point of the ileum (segment from the Meckel diverticulum and the ileo-caecal junction, and frozen (-80°C). Chemical Analysis and Calculations

The N contents in feed and ileal contents were determined according to the Dumas procedure using a LECO Truspec CN analyser (LECO Corporation, St. Joseph, MI, USA), and chromium oxide in diets and ileal samples by a micro-method involving dry ashing and alkaline fusion mixture (Aguilera et al., 1998). Three replicates for AA in feedstuffs and 2 for ileal samples (50–100 mg) were incubated in screw-capped tubes at 110 °C for 24 h in 6 N HCl (2.5 mL) for hydrolysis. After cooling, samples were diluted (1:4) with Milli Q water, internal standard (α-aminoadipic acid) was added to samples or standard solutions and dried under vacuum in a GyroVap (Howe) system for 2 h. Samples and standards were then re-dissolved in 25 μL of a methanol:water:triethylamine (2:1:1) solution and re-dried for 1 h. Derivatizing solution (phenylisothiocyanate) was added, the tubes allowed to stand at room temperature for 15 min and vacuum-dried again. Samples were re-dissolved in 150 μL of Pico Tag sample diluent (Waters) for free AA analysis, vortex mixed and 10 μL of solution injected for HPLC analysis in a Waters equipment (Rubio, 2003). Tryptophane was not determined. Ileal AA flow (g kg−1 feed ingested) in birds fed protein-free or casein diets were calculated as AA (mg/g ileal content) × (Cr2O3f/Cr2O3i). The AA composition of ileal flows (composition of endogenous secretions) were calculated in protein-free- or casein-fed birds as the amount (mg g−1) of each AA respect to the sum of total AA determined (Adedokun et al., 2008; Khadour 2022).

RT-qPCR Microbiota Composition Analysis

Total DNA was isolated from freeze-dried samples from ileal and caecal contents (40 mg) of twelve broilers per group by using the FavorPrep Stool DNA Isolation Mini Kit (Favorgen-Europe, Vienna, Austria) and following the manufacturer´s instructions. Eluted DNA was treated with RNase and the DNA concentration assessed spectrophotometrically by using a NanoDrop ND-100 Spectrophotometer (NanoDrop Technologies, Wilmington). Purified DNA samples were stored at −20°C until use. Eluted DNA was treated with RNase and the DNA concentration was assessed by using a NanoDrop ND-100 spectrophotometer (NanoDrop Technologies, Wilmington, DE). Bacterial log10 number of copies was determined by using quantitative polymerase chain reaction (q-PCR) (iQ5 Cycler, Bio-Rad Laboratories, Alcobendas, Spain). The 16S rRNA gene-targeted primers and polymerase chain reaction (PCR) conditions used in this study were as in Kapravelou et al. (2022). The different microbial groups quantified included Bacteroides/Prevotella spp., Blautia coccoides/Eubacterium rectale group, Clostridium perfringens; Enterobacteriaceae, Lactobacillus spp., Clostridium leptum/Ruminococcus spp., Salmonella spp. and Bifidobacterium spp. Samples for q-PCR analysis were run in duplicate.

High-Throughput Analysis of Microbial Community

The bacterial diversity of the samples was determined using Illumina technology (MiSeq). Total DNA was isolated from freeze-dried samples from ileum and caecum contents and ileal tissue (40 mg) of 8 broilers per group (n = 8) as described above. Libraries preparation was performed by amplification of the V4–V5 region of the 16S rRNA gene. The first amplification was performed by using primers Mi_U515 (50-GTGCCAGCMGCCGCGGTAA-30) and Mi_E786 (50–GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-30) including partially Illumina adapters Mi_E786 (50–GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-30), and PCR conditions were: initial denaturation at 98°C 30 s; 25 cycles with denaturalization at 98°C 10 s, annealing at 52°C 20 s and extension at 72°C 10 s; and a final extension at 72°C 5 min. Second amplification included barcodes and the rest of the Illumina adapters, and PCR conditions were: initial denaturation at 98°C 30 s; 25 cycles with denaturalization at 98°C 10 s, annealing at 52°C 20 s and extension at 72°C 10 s; and a final extension at 72°C 5 min. All amplifications were performed in duplicate. Total DNA was isolated from freeze-dried faces (20 mg) as described above. Aliquots of 10 μL of each DNA were sent to the IPBLN (CSIC, Granada, Spain) for sequencing.

Microscopy Analysis

Tissue (ileum or caecum) samples were cleaned of remaining digesta with deionized water and then transferred to a fresh solution of 10% neutral buffered formaldehyde after 24 h in the 10% neutral buffered formaldehyde. Samples were then dehydrated and embedded in paraffin wax. Three slides were prepared from each sample, and each slide contained a minimum of 2 sections cut at 4 μm, at least 50 μm apart. Staining was with haematoxylin and eosin, and measurements done with a light microscope at 4x and 40x magnification, by using the image analyses system (CellA Imagen Software, Olympus, Hamburg, Germany) with a monitor. Fifteen well-oriented villi and crypts were selected on each slide to determine villus height and width, and crypt depth. The villus height was determined as the distance from the tip to the bottom of the villi, and the crypt depth as the distance between crypt´s mouth and base. Villus surface was calculated as an area with a polygon length. Mucose thickness was determined as the distance between mucosal epithelium and muscular layer, and the muscularis as the inner circular and outer longitudinal layers of smooth-muscle cells. The crypt density was determined by counting the number of crypts included in a defined area calculated with a marked zone. The number of goblet cells was calculated as percentage of these cells in 2 representative fields at 40x counting goblet and epithelial cells. Values were expressed in micrometers and square micrometers.

Statistical Analysis

The results other than sequencing analysis were subjected to one-way ANOVA and Tukey´s multiple comparison test for differences between means (Minitab Statistical Software Package, Minitab, NY). Significance was established at P < 0.05. Results from high throughput sequencing analyses were obtained by using Quantitative Insights in Microbial Ecology (QIIME2 2021.11). Quality filtering was performed by using default parameters in QIIME2. A sub-OTU (sub-operational taxonomic units) table in biom format was created using Deblur, and alignment and taxonomic assignation was performed by fragment insertion script and against the Greengenes database. Sub-OTUs obtained by Illumina analysis of faecal samples from 20 broilers (5 per treatment) were grouped by bacterial species (obtained from the bar plots produced by QIIME2). Multivariate statistical techniques explored the similarities in broilers faecal microbiota, and identified species accounting for differences observed in these bacterial communities. Bray–Curtis measures of similarity were calculated to examine similarities between gut microbial communities of broilers from the high throughput and qPCR data matrices, following standardization, and square-root transformation. The Bray–Curtis similarity coefficient is a reliable measure for biological data on community structure, and is not affected by joint absences, which are commonly found in microbial data. Analysis of similarity (ANOSIM) was performed to test whether gut microbial communities were significantly different between treatments. Analysis of similarity percentages (SIMPER) was done to determine the overall average similarity in faecal microbial community compositions. Principal component analysis (PCA) was used to study the relationships between bacterial groups. It has been reported that PCA is not appropriate where data contain many ‘zeros’ or where observations (species) exceed the total number of samples. To avoid this limitation, SIMPER analysis was used prior to PCA to select those OTUs responsible for larger dissimilarities, and only those OTUs were used for the PCA analysis. Discriminant analysis (DA) was used to check if the groups to which observations belong were distinct. Statistical tests for differentially abundant families, genera, and functional categories were performed using the linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe) method (Segata et al., 2011) with an alpha value of 0.05 for the Kruskal–Wallis test among classes, and the threshold for the log10LDA score was set at 2.0. Microbial functions were predicted using Phylogenetic Investigation of Communities by Reconstruction of Unobserved States (PICRUSt), based on high quality sequences (Langille et al., 2013). OTUs were normalized by copy number, and metagenomic prediction was performed based on Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) (Kanehisa et al., 2012) by using the QIIME2 version 2021.11 package.

The Simpson (D), Shannon index (H), Evenness (E) and Chao1 (SChao1) indexes of the bacterial community were respectively calculated as:D=1/(Σi=1pi2s),where s is the total number of species in the community and pi is the proportion of community represented by OTU i.H=−Σ(pi*lnpi),where pi is the abundance of each species.E=H/lnS,where S is the total number of species.SChao1=Sobs+[F1(F1−1)/2(F2+1)],where F1 and F2 are the count of singletons and doubletons, respectively, and Sobs is the number of observed species.

High-Throughput Analysis of the Microbial Community

A total of 5,921,116 reads were obtained from the intestinal samples processed through Illumina MiSeq technology. After Deblur, 4,968,370 sequences belonging to 774 OTUs were retained for subsequent analyses.

Among the diversity indices studied (Simpson, Shannon, Evenness, and Chao1) (Supplementary Table 1), in the ileal contents, only Chao1 had differences (P < 0.001) between PF and CAS and Maize-soy at the family, genus or species levels. In the caecal samples, all indices were different between Maize-soy and PF and CAS, which were not different from each other, except for the Chao1 index at the species level. ANOSIM analysis (Supplementary Table 2) of the high throughput results showed that, in the intestinal contents, both at the ileal and caecal levels, PF and CAS were different (P < 0.001) from Maize-soy, but not different from each other, and the same occurred at the ileal tissue level.

Ileal Contents

Analysis of Compositions of Microbiomes with Bias Correction (ANCOM-BC) is a differential abundance analysis of microbiome data proposed by Lin and Das Peddada (2020) which uses relative abundances to infer about absolute abundances taking into consideration the differences in the microbial loads across samples. We used this method to identify key genera discriminating the microbiota of birds fed the different diets. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 2, at the genus level Lactobacillus spp. (W = 62, Maize-soy diet), species from Leuconostocaceae (W = 60, CAS) and Leuconostoc spp. (W = 57, NPC) showed a significant difference (P < 0.05) in abundance in the ileal microbiota of chickens. At the species level L. reuteri (W = 77) and Lactobacillus spp. (W = 77) showed a significant difference (P < 0.05) in abundance in the ileal microbiota of birds fed the Maize-soy diet.

The predicted functions of the intestinal microbiota were identified using the phylogenetic investigation of communities by reconstruction of unobserved states (PICRUSt) method (Langille et al., 2013). ANOVA of the PICRUSt analysis showed that, both at the ileal and caecal levels (Supplementary Fig. 3), predicted functions were differentially enriched in birds fed the PF and CAS diets compared with those fed the Maize-soy diet. In addition, predicted functions PF and CAS were generally not different from each other at the ileal level, and in most cases also at the caecal level (Supplementary Fig. 3A).

DISCUSSION

In the current investigation, feeding PF or CAS diets resulted in an intestinal microbiota composition significantly different from that of birds fed a commercial Maize-soy diet, as summarized mainly in Fig. 2 and Supplementary Table 2. The microbiota composition of treatments PF and CAS was clearly different from the commercial Maize-soy diet, but quite close to each other, and in a number of cases not significantly different. This was also true for the PICRUSt analysis results which showed that both at the ileal and caecal levels the predicted functions in PF and CAS treatments were generally not different from each other at the ileal level, and in most cases also at the caecal level, but were in all cases differentially enriched respect to the Maize-soy diet (Suppl. Figs. 1 and 2).

The absence of dietary protein (as in the PF diet) will profoundly alter metabolism and the animal can no longer be regarded as physiologically normal (Ravindran and Hendriks, 2004). In fact, as also observed here (Table 2), ileal AA flow (g kg−1 dry matter intake) is usually significantly higher in birds fed a protein containing diet due to changes in the amounts and composition of endogenous proteins secreted into the intestine, but also to the presence of undigested dietary protein (Ravindran and Hendriks, 2004). These differences in AA flows with PF or CAS will result in different figures when applied to establish true AA digestibility values.

Microbiota composition and functionality are known to be mainly influenced by the carbohydrate content of the diet because the digestive system cannot degrade most of the plant-derived complex carbohydrates present in a normal diet, while the resident intestinal bacteria encode an arsenal of catabolic enzymes to degrade and ferment a wide range of polysaccharides and glycans of dietary or host origin (Flint et al., 2012). However, the effects on the microbiota composition and/or functionality of the intestinal processing of dietary proteins or AA are much less studied than those of carbohydrates, particularly in broilers (Taylor-Bowden et al., 2024). In the current work, PF and CAS, in addition to the presence or not of dietary protein, also differed in the amounts of maize starch plus dextrose (867 and 578 g Kg−1 for the PF and CAS diets respectively, Table 1). This difference was a consequence of the inclusion of casein in CAS, which was done at the expense of starch plus dextrose in the PF diet. No information could be found in the literature on the potential effect of the amount of starch on AA digestibility or microbiota composition, although Zhou et al. (2023) recently reported that increasing the amylose/amylopectin ratio in a PF diet decreased the ileal endogenous AA losses, and resulted in malnutrition and disruption of gut microbiota homeostasis. This disruption was mainly reflected in increased Proteobacteria and decreased Firmicutes in PF respect to a control corn-soy diet, which is in line with the current investigation (Tables 3, 4 and 5).

As indicated earlier, little effort has been devoted to date to investigate the effect of variations in the amount and/or chemical composition of the dietary proteins on microbiota composition or functionality. In the current work, discriminant analysis of the microbial groups analysed by RT-qPCR (Fig. 2) indicated that, at the ileal level, birds fed the different diets (PF, CAS and Maize-soy) were different, while in the caecal contents Maize-soy diet was different (P < 0.001) from PF and CAS, which were not different from each other. Similarly, ANOSIM analysis (Supplementary Table 2) of the sequencing results showed that, both at the ileal and caecal levels, PF and CAS were different (P < 0.001) from Maize-soy, but not different from each other. Therefore, taken as a whole, the intestinal microbiota composition of PF and CAS were quite close and, as would be expected, very different from Maize-soy.

Significant differences in the microbiota composition of the intestinal contents were found by qPCR analysis both at the ileal (Fig. 3A) and caecal (Fig. 3B) levels. These results were confirmed by sequencing analysis (Tables 4 and 5) for Lactobacillus spp., which were higher (P < 0.05) than PF and CAS in the Maize-soy diet both at the ileal and caecal levels. In addition, a number of other groups (species from Leuconostocaceae, Clostridium spp., Enterococcus casseliflavus, Leuconostoc spp., L. mesenteroides, Streptococcus spp. and Ruminococcus spp.) were higher (P < 0.05) than Maize-soy in PF and/or CAS in the ileal samples. In the caecal samples, except for Lactobacillus spp., Clostridium spp., and species from Lachnospiraceae and Peptostreptococcaceae, which were higher in the Maize-soy than in PF or CAS, in many other cases (species from Coriobacteriaceae, Erysipelotrichaceae and Enterobacteriaceae, Bacteroides fragilis, Oscillospira spp., Coprobacillus spp., Eubacterium dolichum, and Klebsiella spp.) values for PF and CAS were higher (P < 0.001) than Maize-soy.

In an effort to identify the bacterial groups mainly responsible for the differences in microbiota composition, LEfSe (Figs. 4 and 5) and ANCOM (Supplementary Fig. 2) analysis were performed. These 2 statistical analyses showed that Lactobacillus spp., and in particular L. reuteri (ileal and caecal samples), Clostridium spp. and species from Lachnospiraceae and Peptostreptococcaceae (caecal samples) were the groups most likely to explain differences in the case of the Maize-soy diet. A number of other groups were those most likely to explain differences for the PF and CAS diets at the ileal level (Enterococcus casseliflavus, Streptococcus luteciae, Leuconostoc mesenteroides, Leuconostoc spp., Staphylococcus aureus, Pseudomonas viridiflava and Clostridium spp. for PF; Streptococcus spp. and Clostridium spp. for CAS), and at the caecal level (Enterobacteriaceae and Alphaproteobacteria, B. fragilis, Coprobacillus spp., Coprococcus spp. and Bifidobacterium spp. for PF birds; Ruminococcus spp., Enterococcus casseliflavus, Oscillospira spp., species from Coriobacteriaceae, Eubacterium dolichum, Dorea spp. and Klebsiella spp. in the CAS chickens).

The interest of L. reuteri as a probiotic (specifically L. reuteri DSM 17938) has been highlighted on intestinal disorders in humans (Patro-Gołąb and Szajewska, 2019; Cervantes-Barragan et al., 2017), and similar results have been obtained in broiler studies. Thus, inhibition of pathogen (Campilobacter jejuni, pathogenic Salmonella spp. and E. coli) growth (Siddique et al., 2021; Ghareeb et al., 2012), improved histological parameters and birds performance and stimulation of the immune system responsiveness (Chai et al., 2023; Salim et al., 2013) have been reported with probiotics combinations including L. reuteri isolates or with selected specific strains. However, although Lactobacilli are known to be the main colonizing species in the upper gut of broilers (Rychlik, 2020), and a number of their species are generally regarded as a probiotics, this bacterial group has been associated with both high and low productivity (Rychlik, 2020; Diaz Carrasco et al., 2019; Apajalahti and Vienola, 2016). In the current context, the main reason for the drop in Lactobacilli and rise of Enterobacteria was most likely that Lactobacilli are among the most fastidious bacteria, with highly complex nutrient (including AA) requirements, while Enterobacteria are independent of any exogenous AA, requiring only an utilisable sugar and minerals to grow (Apajalahti and Vienola, 2016). This is in keeping with the lower qPCR or sequencing values of Lactobacilli in the ileal contents of PF than in CAS or Maize-soy diets (Fig. 3, Tables 4 and 5). Also, the difference between CAS and Maize-soy in Lactobacilli counts would probably be related with the lower ileal flow of AA in CAS compared with the Maize-soy diet (Table 2), which would result in lower amounts of AA available for Lactobacilli growth in the ileal medium of the CAS birds.

Together with Lactobacillus spp., Clostridium spp. and other species from the Lachnospiraceae and Peptostreptococcaceae families (caecal samples) were the groups most likely to explain differences in the Maize-soy diet (LEfSe and ANCOM analysis, Fig. 6 and Supplementary Fig. 2). Lachnospiraceae are among the major families from the phylum Firmicutes colonising the chicken gut, and its main relevance is linked to the fact that representatives of this family represent the most important butyrate producers, and are among the microbial taxa associated with high productivity in chickens (Apajalahti and Vienola, 2016; Onrust et al., 2015; Diaz Carrasco et al., 2019). Also, Lachnospiraceae are highly sensitive to oxygen, and in consequence are among the first bacterial groups to disappear from gut microbiota during inflammatory diseases due to the production of reactive oxygen species by macrophages and granulocytes (Rychlik, 2020). As for Peptostreptococcaceae, bacteria in this family have been negatively associated with feed efficiency (Liu et al., 2021).

A number of other groups at the ileal (Enterococcus casseliflavus, Streptococcus luteciae, Leuconostoc mesenteroides, Leuconostoc spp., Staphylococcus aureus, Pseudomonas viridiflavaandClostridiumspp.) and caecal (Enterobacteriaceaeand Alphaproteobacteria, B. fragilis, Coprobacillus spp., Coprococcus spp. and Bifidobacterium spp.) levels were those most likely to explain differences for the PF fed birds. Among these, E. casseliflavushas been reported as beneficial, and producers of bacteriocins against C. perfringens (Van Hoeck et al., 2021; García-Vela et al., 2023); Leuconostoc spp. (included in Lactobacillaceae family) have been linked to spoilage of chill stored poultry meat packaged in modified atmosphere (Lauritsen et al., 2019); Coprococcus spp. belong to the Lachnospiraceae family and are therefore potentially beneficial (see above); finally, S. aureus, Coprobacillus spp. (belonging to the Erysipelotrichaceae family) and Enterobacteriaceae are in most cases recognised as pathogenic or potentially pathogenic bacterial groups, and are associated with low productivity (Domanska-Blicharz et al., 2023; Zhou et al., 2023). Although implicated in low productivity in chickens (Diaz Carrasco et al., 2019), there is as jet very little information on the functionality of Alphaproteobacteria in broilers. B. fragilis has been reported among enteric pathogens (Wang et al., 2021), is the most frequent isolate from clinical specimens, and is regarded as the most virulent Bacteroides species (Wexler, 2007), although it has also been associated with good performance in broilers (Stanley et al., 2016).

Some other groups at the ileal (Streptococcus spp. and Leuconostocaceae) and caecal (Ruminococcusspp., Enterococcus casseliflavus, Oscillospiraspp., species from Coriobacteriaceae, Eubacterium dolichum, Dorea spp. and Klebsiella spp.) levels were those most likely to explain differences for the CAS fed birds. Among these, LeuconostocaceaeandStreptococcus spp. are included in the lactic acid bacteria (LAB) group which have a reputation of being ‘generally recognized as safe’ (GRAS), and are rarely isolated from disease, even though some species are pathogenic (Narvhus and Axelsson, 2003); Ruminococcusspp.,Dorea spp. (family Lachnospiraceae) and many non-pathogenic Clostridium spp. are major butyrate producers (Apajalahti and Vienola, 2016; Onrust et al., 2015; Diaz Carrasco et al., 2019); Oscillospira spp. is likely to be a genus capable of producing SCFAs such as butyrate, and has been characterized as one of the next-generation probiotic candidates (Yang et al., 2021); E. casseliflavushas been reported as beneficial as indicated above; no published reference was found for Coriobacteriaceae or E. dolichum in broilers, but Shimitzu et al. (2018) associated E. dolichum with propionate production in humans, and Coriobacteriaceae (phylum Actinobacteria) have been linked to gut barrier and metabolic dysfunctions related with obesity (Zhao et al., 2017). Klebsiella spp. belong to the Enterobacteriaceae family and are usually pathogenic (Köck et al., 2018).

Finally, the microbiota composition was also studied at the ileal tissue level, as it has been linked to a number of activities which are directly or indirectly linked to nutrients absorption (development of submucosal capillary beads, anti-inflammatory functions, integrity of the mucosal barrier, butyrate production, etc.) (Pedroso, 2015). Interestingly, the changes in the microbiota composition found in the ileal tissue very much resembled those observed in the caecal contents (see Tables 4 and 5), such as increased Coriobacteriaceae, Bacteroides fragilis, Oscillospira spp., species from Erysipelotrichaceae, Coprobacillus spp., Eubacterium dolichum, and species from Enterobacteriaceae, together with decreased Lactobacillus spp. and L. reuteri, in PF and/or CAS birds. This adds more to the conclusion that PF and CAS diets promoted a pro-inflammatory microbiota as compared to a commercial Maize-soy diet, which is likely to affect the intestinal AA absorption processes.

In conclusion, the work here described adds information on the effects on the microbiota composition and/or functionality of the intestinal processing of dietary proteins in broilers. The main result was that the lack of protein in the intestinal medium of PF birds resulted in a significant drop of Lactobacillus spp. counts accompanied by increased Enterobacteriaceae and other potentially pathogenic bacterial groups, which has been reported to give place to a pro-inflammatory state (Shin et al., 2015; Apajalahti and Vienola, 2016), which in turn may result in a drop of butyrate producers such as Lachnospiraceae. This effect was somewhat less marked in birds fed CAS. Accordingly, the physiological condition, at least at the intestinal level, is likely to be more affected in broilers fed PF than it is in those fed CAS. This would undoubtedly affect the absorption processes of nutrients including AA. Zhou et al. (2023) has recently proposed that an enzyme-hydrolysed casein method could be a better option to determine endogenous AA excretion than PF diets to maintain normal physiologic levels of endogenous N flow throughout the intestinal tract. The results reported here are in line with this proposition, as the adverse effects on microbiota composition in broilers fed CAS were less marked than in those fed PF, although the histological structure of CAS and PF was similar (Table 6).
