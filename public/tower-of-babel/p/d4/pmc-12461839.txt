Comparative Outcomes Following Candida Bloodstream Infection in Patients With Left Ventricular Assist Devices
Authors: Armaghan-e-Rehman Mansoor, Gayathri Krishnan, Julio C Zuniga-Moya, Benjamin Papadopoulus, Andrej Spec, Ige George
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCandida bloodstream infections (BSI) carry a high risk of adverse outcomes. Left ventricular assist devices (LVADs) are increasingly used to manage end-stage heart failure; however, they carry a risk of infection. This study evaluates the impact of LVAD presence on outcomes in patients with Candida BSI.MethodsA total of 1233 patients with Candida BSI admitted to a tertiary hospital between January 2010 and December 2021 were retrospectively included, with 39 having an LVAD at the time of infection. Demographics, microbiologic data, and clinical factors associated with Candida BSI were recorded. Risk factors for Candida BSI and 30 and 90-day mortality were compared.ResultsPatients with Candida BSI in the LVAD cohort were more likely male (77% vs 54%, P = .01) versus the non-LVAD group, and more frequently had central venous access before infection (67% vs 46% P = .01). Rates of extracorporeal membrane oxygenation were higher in the LVAD group (39% vs 0.4%, P < .0001). Candida parapsilosis was the most common species in patients with LVAD (38% in LVAD vs 16%), compared to Candida albicans in the non-LVAD cohort (24% in LVAD vs 39%). HeartMate2 was the most common LVAD (21 patients, 54%). There was no difference in all-cause 30-day (30.7% vs 34.5%, P = .62) and 90-day mortality between the LVAD and non-LVAD cohorts (38.4% vs 40.7%, P = .77). In an adjusted Cox proportional hazards model, LVAD presence also did not impact 90-day mortality (hazard ratio, 1.12 [95% confidence interval, 0.70–1.77; P = .62]).ConclusionsIn patients with Candida BSI, presence of an LVAD did not impact 30- or 90-day mortality.

Left ventricular assist devices (LVADs) are an increasingly common strategy used in the management of patients with end-stage heart failure [1]. Survival after LVAD implantation has significantly improved with changes in device technology, and LVADs are more often being used as a “destination therapy” compared to their previous primary purpose of durable mechanical circulatory support as a bridge to heart transplantation [1, 2]. However, short- and long-term infection risks remain an important complication [3, 4]. LVAD infections can be limited to the superficial driveline near the abdominal exit site or present as deep-seated infections involving the driveline, pump pocket, and outflow tract. LVADs can also be seeded in the setting of bloodstream infections (BSI) and endocarditis. Presence of BSI in the setting of LVAD infection portends a high likelihood of poor outcomes, with several studies reporting mortality rates ranging from 29.0% to 65.5%, and a strong independent association of BSI with mortality [5–8]. Gram-positive organisms including Staphylococcus aureus and coagulase-negative Staphylococci are the most common causative pathogens in BSI, with gram-negative organisms reported less often [9].

Candida BSI are an important cause of healthcare-associated BSI and associated with a high risk of mortality and morbidity [10]. There are well-established risk factors for the development of Candida BSI, including presence of central venous catheters, prior antibiotic exposure, chronic liver and kidney disease, prior abdominal surgery, and admission to intensive care units [11, 12]. These risk factors have a high prevalence in patients with end-stage heart failure requiring LVAD placement, placing them at increased risk of Candida BSI [13, 14]. Although Candida BSI is uncommonly reported in the LVAD cohort, limited data from small case-series show a high risk of poor outcomes with associated mortality rate as high as 74% [10, 15]. Literature on risk factors, microbiologic etiology, and outcomes after BSI remain sparse, with significant heterogeneity in reported data [16, 17]. In addition, the impact of the presence of an LVAD on outcomes related to Candida BSI remains unknown.

This single-center study uses a robust clinical dataset and applies methodological adjustments for confounding variables to assess the impact of LVAD presence on outcomes in patients with Candida BSI. Additionally, it aims to identify the prevalence of risk factors traditionally linked to Candida BSI and their impact on adverse outcomes in this patient population.

METHODS

Study Population

We retrospectively reviewed data for patients with Candida BSI admitted to Barnes Jewish Hospital between January 2010 and December 2021. This is a 1300-bed tertiary referral center in St. Louis, Missouri, in the Midwest region of the United States. Any patients aged 18 years or older with at least 1 blood culture reported with presence of Candida during the inpatient encounter, or a linked emergency department encounter were included. Our data analysts (D.S., J.C.Z., B.P.) used a database of individuals admitted with an index episode of Candida BSI, and supplemented by manual chart review (A.M., G.K.). We collected demographic information including age at diagnosis, sex, and race. We used International Classification of Diseases, revisions 9 and 10, and Current Procedural Terminology diagnosis and procedure codes to capture clinical data that are potentially associated with development of Candida BSI [11, 18]. This included the following coexisting medical conditions: chronic pulmonary disease, complicated and uncomplicated diabetes, hypertension and liver disease, need for hemodialysis, parenteral nutrition, mechanical ventilation, and central venous access (see Supplementary Appendix 1). Microbiologic data included causative Candida species for Candida BSI. The study protocol was approved by Washington University in St. Louis Human Research Protection Office Institutional Review Board (IRB# 202008126).

Patient Consent Statement

This study does not include factors necessitating patient consent.

Exposure Variable and Covariates

We recorded the presence of an LVAD at the time of Candida BSI as the exposure variable. We explored demographic data, coexisting medical conditions, and procedural intervention and microbiologic data to determine covariates.

Outcomes

Our primary outcome measure was 30- and 90-day mortality after index Candida BSI. For the LVAD cohort, we additionally recorded reported recurrence or relapse of Candida BSI within 1 calendar year of the index BSI episode, as well as need for surgical intervention related to the LVAD. Patients with mortality within 48 hours of diagnosis of Candida BSI were excluded. Study data were collected and managed using REDCap [19] electronic data capture tools hosted at Washington University in St. Louis.

The type of infection in patients with LVAD were defined using the International Society for Heart and Lung Transplantation: 2024 infection definitions for durable and acute mechanical circulatory support devices [20]. Presence of central nervous system (CNS) infection in the context of Candida BSI was defined by new radiologic findings consistent with sequelae of embolic phenomenon, vascular aneurysms, or presence of CNS abscess.

Statistical Analysis

We performed a stratified descriptive analysis with the cohort divided into patients with LVAD and non-LVAD. We used chi-square tests or Fisher's exact tests when necessary for categorical variables to compare these cohorts. We employed the Wilcoxon rank-sum test to assess continuous variables among the 2 groups.

We performed an adjusted Cox proportional hazard model to assess all-cause mortality at 30 days and 90 days among patients with LVAD and non-LVAD. In the adjusted model, we included confounders based in prior causal knowledge [21]. All analysis, tables, and models were performed using SparkR (r-project.org; version 4.2.1).

RESULTS

Comparative Demographics, and Risk Factors of Candida BSI in the LVAD and Non-LVAD Cohort

A total of 1233 patients met inclusion criteria for a diagnosis of Candida BSI during the index admission, of whom 39 had presence of an LVAD at the time of Candida BSI (Table 1). Patients with LVAD were more likely to be male (76.9% vs 53.9%, P = .0085). There was no significant difference in age (mean 52.6 ± 14.3 vs 57.1 ± 15.5, P = .06) or race.

There were no significant differences in the likelihood of most cooccurring medical conditions (Table 1), or prior abdominal surgery, between the 2 groups. Patients in the LVAD cohort were more likely to have complicated diabetes mellitus (25.6% vs 14.2%, P = .0451).

In the LVAD cohort, a statistically significant proportion of patients had a central venous catheter present at the time of Candida BSI compared to the non-LVAD group (26 patients; 66.6% vs 552 patients, 46.2%; P = .0116). The LVAD cohort was also significantly more likely to have had a history of requiring extracorporeal membrane oxygenation support prior to index Candida BSI. There were no statistically significant differences in the rates of enteral or parenteral supplemental nutrition, invasive or noninvasive mechanical ventilation, or hemodialysis need, at the time of or prior to index Candida BSI.

Candida parapsilosis was the most reported species in the LVAD cohort (38.4% vs 15.5%, P = .05), whereas Candida albicans was the most common species in the non-LVAD cohort (38.6% vs 23.8%, P = .05). Candida glabrata was the next most common species (15.3% in LVAD vs 24.6% in the non-LVAD group), followed by Candida tropicalis (10.2% in LVAD vs 7.7% in the non-LVAD group).

Comparative Outcomes of the LVAD and Non-LVAD Cohort

Among the LVAD cohort, all-cause mortality at 30 days was reported in 12 patients (30.7%) versus 413 (34.5%) in the non-LVAD group, P = .62, and in 15 patients (38.4%) versus 486 (40.7%), P = .77 at 90 days. An unadjusted Cox proportional hazards model showed no attributable 90-day mortality of LVAD presence (hazard ratio [HR], 0.89; 95% confidence interval [CI], .56–1.40; P = .617) during the index Candida BSI episode. This finding was consistent for an adjusted Cox proportional hazards model, which also showed no significant impact to 90-day mortality with the presence of an LVAD (HR, 1.12 [95% CI, 0.70–1.77; P = .62]).

Additional Characteristics, Clinical Course, and VAD-specific Outcomes in the LVAD Cohort

Among the LVAD cohort, HeartMate II was the most common device implicated in Candida BSI (22 patients, 56.4%), followed by HeartWare (10 patients, 25.60%) and HeartMate III (7 patients, 17.9%). The potential source of infection was categorized based on International Society for Heart and Lung Transplantation: 2024 infection definitions for durable and acute mechanical circulatory support devices [20]. A primary source of infection was determined in 25/39 patients (64.1%), most commonly central line-associated blood stream infection in 10 (25.6%) patients, followed by device endocarditis in 5 (12.8%) patients. Percutaneous lead infection, infection of the external surface of an implantable component, and endocarditis without evidence of device endocarditis was the likely underlying etiology in 2 patients each. An enteric source of infection was noted in 4 (10.3%) patients. In the LVAD cohort, 11 patients (28.2%) developed a Candida BSI within 30 days of LVAD implantation, and 19 (48.7%) within 90 days of LVAD placement, with a median duration of 89 days from LVAD placement to first positive blood culture.

Nearly all patients (37 patients, 94.9%) had received an antibacterial agent within 30 days of Candida BSI or were concurrently on antibacterials at the time of diagnosis. A history of driveline or pump pocket site infection was reported in 19 patients (48.7%). Staphylococcus aureus was the most common cause of previous LVAD-related infections in 10 patients, followed by Corynebacterium in 3 patients.

Extension of Candida infection to the CNS was documented in 9 patients (22.0%) in the LVAD cohort; ophthalmologic involvement was reported in 3 patients (7.7%). A formal eye examination was performed by Ophthalmology in 20 (56.4%) patients during the index hospital admission. An intensive care unit stay was documented in 31 (79.5%) of patients.

In the LVAD cohort, a formal transthoracic echocardiogram was obtained in 58.5% of patients, and a transesophageal echocardiogram pursued in 17.5% of patients during the index admission. Most patients were treated with antifungals alone (82.9%), with 3 patients receiving antifungals and concurrent surgical intervention. Of these 3 patients, 2 underwent an LVAD exchange and 1 underwent a deep debridement.

In the LVAD Candida BSI group, the most common initial antifungal was micafungin (73.2%), followed by fluconazole (3 patients, 7.3%) and liposomal amphotericin B (3 patients, 7.3%). We did not have treatment data available to perform comparative analysis for the non-LVAD cohort.

In patients with index BSI within 30 days of implantation, 90-day mortality was 45.4% compared to 39.3% in patients with index BSI after 30 days of implantation. When comparing outcomes in patients with index BSI within 90 days of implantation, 90-day mortality was 52.6%, compared to 35.0% in patients with index BSI after 90 days of LVAD implantation. Both comparative analyses did not reach statistical significance (P = .725 and .562, respectively).

In the LVAD cohort, 23 of 25 patients (92%) who completed the initial planned therapy for Candida BSI were initiated on chronic suppressive therapy with an oral azole. The most common choice of therapy was fluconazole in 21 patients, followed by voriconazole in 2 patients. A recurrence of Candida infection was documented in 7 patients on chronic suppressive therapy within a maximum of 1 year from index infection. The cause of recurrence was determined to be incomplete source control of mediastinal infection, cardiac thrombus, self-discontinuation of suppressive fluconazole therapy, and central line–associated BSI in 1 patient each, with an unclear source in the remaining 3 patients.

Of the 2 patients who did not receive chronic antifungal therapy after treatment of initial BSI, 1 patient had recurrence of Candida BSI in 6 months with a different Candida species, and the other patient did not have recurrence of infection for the 2-year period after index Candida BSI for which medical records were available.

DISCUSSION

In our study of 1233 patients with Candida BSI at a tertiary referral center, we did not find the presence of an LVAD to impact 90-day all-cause mortality in a Cox proportional hazards model (HR 1.12 [95% CI, 0.70–1.77; P = .62]). The association of worse outcomes with retained vascular devices in the setting of BSI is well known, with several studies demonstrating an independent association of device retention with mortality [22–25]. Consensus guidelines underscore the importance of device removal to achieve source control [26]. Several studies have also documented the increased risk of mortality with cardiac implantable electronic device infection when device extraction is not pursued [27–29]. In most patients with LVAD infection and BSI, however, device extraction is usually not feasible because of the markedly high perioperative risk of LVAD extraction and reimplantation, and is typically performed only in mechanical circulatory support device (MCS)-specific infections involving the deep driveline or the pump components, or in persistent bloodstream infection despite appropriate antimicrobial therapy [30]. This was demonstrated in our study as well, where only 2 of 39 patients (5.1%) underwent LVAD exchange as part of a management strategy for Candida BSI. Despite the low proportion of patients who achieved “source-control” by means of device extraction, outcomes were similar to the cohort of patients without an LVAD. There are likely several reasons for these findings.

First, there is a known association of poor outcomes attributable to Candida BSI, and invasive candidiasis is well established as a cause of severe sepsis and septic shock regardless of other coexisting medical conditions [31, 32]. In a study by Mazi et al comparing Candida BSI cases with frequency-matched controls, a propensity score-matching model demonstrated a 28.4% risk difference for 90-day mortality attributable specifically to Candida BSI [10]. Notably, patients at the lowest quintile for risk had the highest likelihood of mortality after Candida BSI in this study. In contrast, patients with LVADs are more likely to have ongoing engagement with the healthcare system through a multidisciplinary heart failure management team, and LVAD devices typically have alerts to “low-flow” states [33]. Clinicians may therefore have a higher index of suspicion and earlier recognition of hemodynamic changes and symptoms concerning for infection, including BSI.

Second, although there are no consensus guidelines for management of infections in patients with LVADs, most patients with BSI and suspected LVAD involvement tend to receive long-term secondary antimicrobial prophylaxis [34, 35]. This is similar to the strategy endorsed in consensus guidelines for management of invasive candidiasis and prosthetic valve infective endocarditis when valve exchange is not feasible [26, 36]. In our study, almost all patients (94.9%) were maintained on indefinite chronic suppressive antifungal therapy after initial management of Candida BSI. This practice likely also impacted outcomes positively by reducing the risk of recurrent or relapsed infection.

Candida parapsilosis was the most common causative species in the LVAD cohort in comparison to C albicans in the non-LVAD cohort. The microbiologic difference is likely explained by the known association of C parapsilosis with prosthetic and vascular device infections because of its ability to form biofilms, and its persistence in nosocomial environments [37, 38]. The LVAD cohort also had a significantly higher proportion of patients with central venous access at the time of index fungemia (26 patients, 66.6% vs 552 patients, 46.2%, P = .0116) and extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (15 patients, 38.4% vs 59 patients (4.9%), P < .0001), providing another nidus to favor infection with C parapsilosis over other species.

Overall mortality was lower in our study than prior single-center data, especially when compared to older landmark studies on Candida BSI that reported crude mortality rates of 57% to 61% [39, 40]. In comparison to our data, a single-center study of 23 patients with Candida infections and LVAD presence published in 2010 showed a mortality rate of 91%, and another case series from 2003 of 7 patients with any MCS-related infection secondary to Candida demonstrated an in-hospital mortality rate of 71% [17, 41]. Indeed, a more contemporary European multinational observational cohort study showed a very comparable overall mortality of 40.4% [42]. The reason for this difference is likely multifactorial, with routine availability of echinocandins and improvements in critical care and diagnostics being potential factors. In addition, we used chronic suppressive azole therapy in almost all patients after index Candida BSI episode in the presence of an LVAD, which potentially reduced the likelihood of recurrent infection and subsequent poor outcomes.

Of note, we did not investigate the impact of certain known risk factors for Candida BSI such as history of hematologic malignancy, history of solid organ transplantation or stem-cell therapy, and injection drug use because these factors were not present in the LVAD cohort.

A large proportion of patients in the LVAD cohort received antibiotics (94.9%) close to their index episode of Candida BSI, and nearly half (48.7%) had a history of prior MCS-related infection. Antibiotics are known to cause dysbiosis, a process that has been linked to Candida overgrowth in the gut, which could serve as a reservoir for subsequent BSI [43, 44]. The strength of this association is unclear, but merits further investigation.

There are limited data on the prevalence of CNS disease in patients with Candida BSI, and reported estimates range from 5% in retrospective series to approximately 50% in postmortem studies. This is likely due to the heterogenous definitions for CNS disease used in prior studies, as well as the variability in workup to determine the presence of CNS involvement in Candida BSI, especially for patients who did not have overt neurologic changes [45, 46]. The high proportion of CNS involvement in our cohort compared to other retrospective studies is likely multifactorial; first, we defined CNS involvement as any acute manifestation of a vascular or parenchymal insult to the CNS. In addition, as patients with an LVAD cannot undergo magnetic resonance imaging, elucidating a specific etiology of new CNS lesions and ruling out noninfectious etiologies can be difficult. We were not able to report details of disseminated infection including CNS disease in the non-LVAD cohort, making it difficult to assess the impact of LVAD presence on the likelihood of identifying CNS disease; this could also be the subject of a future study.

Echocardiographic evaluation is a cornerstone of the management of Candida BSI, yet the proportion of patients with an LVAD receiving a transthoracic echocardiogram (58.5%) and transesophageal echocardiogram (17.5) in our study were low. This was likely as receipt of echocardiographic imaging in patients with an existing LVAD may have been thought to not significantly change the planned treatment course, which included prolonged antifungal therapy in almost all patients.

The cohort of patients with LVAD and Candida BSI were predominantly male (76.9% vs 53.9% in the non-LVAD cohort, P = .0085). This is likely secondary to the significantly higher proportion of males with advanced heart failure who receive a VAD, with a recent study estimating a rate of 51.4 per 1000 male versus 14.6 per 1000 female patients with advanced heart failure [47].

There are several unique aspects of our study. To our knowledge, this is the first comparative analysis of Candida BSI outcomes in patients with and without an LVAD, and to- date includes the largest cohort of LVAD patients with Candida BSI. We also reported outcomes at 90 days, instead of in-hospital or 30-day outcomes that have most commonly been reported in prior studies [17, 39, 40]. We feel this more accurately captures attributable outcomes following Candida BSI and recommend this as the standard of reporting in future studies evaluating BSI outcomes in patients with LVADs.

Our study has limitations to note. We were not able to compare differences in initial antifungal therapy between the LVAD and non-LVAD cohorts. Given the limitations of the dataset, we were also not able to compare rates of Infectious Diseases consultation, ophthalmologic evaluation, presence of cardiac devices in the non-LVAD cohort, timing and choice of echocardiographic imaging, procedural interventions unrelated to LVAD, and time to clearance of BSI, which are factors that may impact Candida BSI outcomes [5, 26]. As a substantial proportion of patients with LVAD had an unclear etiology of Candida BSI, we did not compare outcomes for BSI in patients with MCS-related cause of BSI versus those with an unrelated etiology for Candida BSI. As with all observational studies, there is the likelihood of residual confounding, although we believe the use of an adjusted proportional hazard models reduced its impact. Last, our data are reported from a single tertiary care center, which limits generalizability, although referral sites such as our hospital are likely to be the setting for most medical care provided to patients with LVAD-related acute infections.

Overall, we found the presence of an LVAD to not be associated with worse Candida BSI outcomes, with low recurrence or relapse rates while on chronic suppressive antifungal therapy. Clinicians should recognize the strong likelihood of seeding mechanical circulatory support devices such as LVADs in the setting of Candida BSI and maintain close adherence to guideline recommendations for the appropriate and timely management of Candida BSI.
