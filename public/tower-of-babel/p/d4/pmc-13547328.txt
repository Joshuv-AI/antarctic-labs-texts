Comparison of ciprofol and propofol in combination with sufentanil for gastroscopic sedation in people living with obesity: a prospective, randomized, controlled trial
Authors: Jie Zhao, You-Fa Zhou, Ying-Ying Tang, Shao-Yi Wang, Guo-Xiang Wang, Gang Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHypoxemia occurs in people living with obesity undergoing gastroscopy under intravenous anesthesia. Preventing hypoxia in people living with obesity with appropriate anesthetics is therefore important. This study aims to compare the safety and efficacy of ciprofol versus propofol, both combined with sufentanil, for sedation during gastroscopy in people living with obesity.MethodsThis prospective, randomized, controlled trial was conducted at the Endoscopic Center of Hangzhou Red Cross Hospital. Between August 2023 and August 2024, 652 patients were assessed for eligibility. Subsequently, Patients aged 18-65 years, classified as American Society of Anesthesiologists II, with a body mass index (BMI) between 30 and 40 kg/m², were enrolled. Participants were randomly assigned to receive either ciprofol or propofol, both combined with sufentanil, during their gastroscopy. The trial was registered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2300074217).ResultsThe primary outcome, hypoxemia, occurred in 34.3% of propofol patients vs 19.1% with ciprofol (absolute difference 15.2%; 95% CI 4.9--29.9%). For secondary outcomes, the propofol group experienced significantly more apnea episodes (17.9% vs 5.9%; absolute difference 12%; 95% CI 1.3% to 22.8%) and lower minimum SpO₂ values (90 [80 to 95] vs 93 [90 to 97]; absolute difference --3; 95% CI --8 to 1). Endoscopist satisfaction was higher with ciprofol (4 [3 to 5] vs 5 [4 to 5]; absolute difference --1; 95% CI --1 to 0), and injection pain was significantly more frequent in the propofol group (26.9% vs 1.5%; absolute difference 25.4%; 95% CI 14.4% to 36.4%).ConclusionsCiprofol combined with sufentanil for gastroscopy sedation in people living with obesity, results in lower incidences of hypoxemia and injection pain, shorter apnea duration, and higher endoscopist satisfaction compared to propofol.Trial RegistrationChinese Clinical Trial Registry: ChiCTR2300074217; 2023/08/01.Subject terms: Drug development, Randomized controlled trials

Introduction

Gastroscopy is a widelyutilized procedure for examining the upper gastrointestinal tract. Intravenous anesthesia confers patient comfort during these endoscopic examinations; However, it also leads to hypoxemia due to respiratory depression, airway obstruction, and hemodynamic instability. These risks are particularly increased in people living with obesity, with special airway anatomy---such as short necks, and pharyngeal fat deposition1,2. Additionally, people living with obesity may suffer from sleep apnea syndrome, which increases the risk of posterior tongue displacement during general intravenous anesthesia, causing upper airway obstruction and respiratory depression3. Thus, obesity has been identified as an independent risk factor for sedation-related hypoxemia4,5. Extended periods of hypoxia can result in significant complications, such as myocardial ischemia, irreversible neurological damage, or even death. Therefore, preventing hypoxia in people living with obesity during gastroscopy with appropriate anesthetics is particularly important.

Propofol combined with low-dose opioids, a common anesthetic strategy for various endoscopic procedures. However, propofol also has some adverse events, such as cardiovascular and respiratory depression6,7. These risks are increased in people living with obesity due to their pre-existing physiological characteristic; Therefore, the search for alternative sedatives with less adverse events is important.

Recently, emerging evidences suggests that ciprofol, a novel 2,6 disubstituted phenol derivative, may have advantages over current sedatives8,9. Compared with propofol, ciprofol binds more tightly to the γ-aminobutyric acid type A (GABAA) receptors, which has been reported to have the potency of propofol but at a quarter to a fifth of the dosage10. In Phase III clinical trials for sedation during colonoscopy and gastroscopy, the ciprofol group exhibited a notably lower incidence of respiratory depression compared to the propofol group11,12.

In this trial, we compare the efficacy and safety of ciprofol versus propofol, both combined with low-dose sufentanil, for gastroscopic sedation in people living with obesity. Our results demonstrate that ciprofol significantly reduces the incidence of hypoxemia and injection pain, decreases apnea episodes, and improves endoscopist satisfaction compared to propofol. These findings suggest that ciprofol may offer a safer and more comfortable sedation option for people living with obesity undergoing gastroscopy.

Methods

Study design and setting

Ethical approval for this study was provided by the central institutional ethics committee board at Hangzhou Red Cross Hospital (Ethics Committee number: 2023-094, Chairperson Prof Pan Feng) on 18 July 2023. The trial was registered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (http://www.chictr.org.cn/; registration no. ChiCTR2300074217) on 01 August 2023. Patients were recruited from the Gastroenterology Clinics of Hangzhou Red Cross Hospital. Before the enrollment of the first patient, all investigators attended a meeting about the trial. All patients who participated in the study provided written informed consent.

Patient population

The inclusion criteria specified patients aged 18 to 65 years, of either gender, who had an American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status classification of II and a body mass index (BMI) ranging from 30 to 40 kg/m².

Exclusion criteria were: (1) known allergy to any study medications; (2) upper respiratory tract infection within the past two weeks; (3) asthma exacerbation, uncontrolled hypertension, diabetes, hyperthyroidism, or hypothyroidism; (4) psychiatric disorders (e.g., schizophrenia, depression), history of epilepsy, or cognitive impairment; (5) history of substance or anesthetic abuse; (6) abnormal liver function, renal function, or coagulation disorders; (7) pregnant or breastfeeding women; and (8) vulnerable populations (including critically ill patients, minors, illiterate individuals).

Randomization and blinding

Patients were randomly allocated to either the ciprofol or propofol group based on a computer-generated random number table. Blocked randomization in a 1:1 ratio was achieved using the online tool available at “www.randomization.com”. Independent researchers, who were unaware of the group assignments, carried out the outcome assessment and statistical analysis.

Study intervention

The gastroscopy was performed with the patient placed at the left lateral position. During the procedure, supplemental oxygen (4 L/min) was administered to the patient by nasal cannula. Simultaneously, continuous monitoring was conducted for heart rate (HR), electrocardiogram (ECG), and peripheral oxygen saturation (SpO2). Additionally, systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure(DBP) and mean arterial pressure(MAP) were automatically recorded at three-minute intervals. Patients in both groups received intravenous sufentanil (Yichang Renfu Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd.) injection at a dose of 0.1 μg/kg, followed 30 s later by intravenous ciprofol (Liaoning Haiske Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd.) injection at 0.3 mg/kg or propofol (AstraZeneca Pharmaceutical Co. Ltd) injection at 1.3 mg/kg, respectively, administered at a rate of 1 ml/s. Both groups waited until the patient achieved a Modified Observer’s Assessment of Alertness/Sedation (MOAA/S) score of ≤1 before commencing the gastroscopy procedure. During induction of sedation, the anesthetist assesses the MOAA/S score every 30 s. If the MOAA/S score was still >1 at 2 min after the initial dose, an additional 1/4 of the initial dose was injected within 10 s. Sedation was considered unsuccessful if more than three additional doses were required within 5 min, at which point propofol was the only alternative sedative allowed in this trial.

Throughout the examination, the anaesthesiologist managed any associated complications, such as hypotension (blood pressure decreased more than 30% of the basal value), then intravenous ephedrine 6 mg; bradycardia (HR < 45 beats/min), then intravenous atropine 0.5 mg; If the intraoperative HR increases by >100 beats/min or >30% of the basic value, and the increase in blood pressure is greater than 30% of the basic value, or according to the subject’s performance (body movement, nausea, choking cough, etc. the sedative drug is decided), and the additional dose is 1/4 of the induction dose each time, which can be repeated. If there is a decrease in SpO2, according to the degree of decrease to start airway intervention, specific measures include: when SpO2 is less than 95%, increase the flow of inhaled oxygen to 8 L/min; SpO2 less than 90%, the anaesthesiologist lifted the jaw to open the airway to relieve upper airway obstruction. when SpO2 is lower than 85%, placement of nasopharyngeal ventilator. If oxygen saturation still does not improve, the decision to withdraw the gastroscope and perform mask ventilation or even tracheal intubation is based on hypoxia. Following the procedure, patients were transferred to the recovery room for monitoring of SBP, DBP, MAP, HR, SpO2, and MOAA/S. Patients were discharged to the ward only if their Aldrete Score reached a minimum of 9 and if there were no notable adverse effects, such as nausea or dizziness.

Two anesthesiologists were present during all procedures: one conducted the induction and anesthesia monitoring, while the other (the assessor) documented all cases, supervised adherence to the anesthesia protocol, and excluded cases that required rescue sedation outside of the protocol-defined rules.

Study outcomes

The primary outcome measured was the occurrence of hypoxemia during sedation, characterized by an SpO2 (oxygen saturation measured via pulse oximetry) dropping below 90% for more than 10 seconds. The sedation period was defined as the duration from the initiation of the first medication administration to the conclusion of the gastroscopy procedure.

The secondary outcomes included the following: (1) Apnea (the number of patients with apnea for more than 30 seconds). (2) The minimum values of SpO2 during the procedure. (3) The number of requirements for airway maneuvers including increasing the volume of oxygen, airway opening with the jaw-lift maneuver, placement of nasopharyngeal ventilator, removing the gastroscope tube and bag-mask ventilation, or endotracheal intubation for severe hypoxia. (4) The induction time (MOAA/S ≤ 1 after administration of the initial dose), insertion time for the gastroscopy, the awake time (3 consecutive MOAA/S scores of 5 after the last administration of dose), recovery time (3 consecutive Modified Aldrete Score ≥ 9 every 5 minutes after admission to the recovery room). (5) Requirements for sedation remedies. (6) The satisfaction levels for both patients and endoscopists were rated on a scale from 1 to 5, with 1 indicating very dissatisfied and 5 indicating highly satisfied. (7) MAP, HR, SPO2, and MOAA/S at different time points. (8) Complications of Patients (bradycardia, tachycardia, hypotension, hypertension, injection pain, muscle fasciculation, choking cough, laryngospasm, body movement, intraoperative awareness during the sedation period; nausea, vomiting, dizziness, drowsiness, agitation, and fatigue in the resuscitation room and on the first to third postoperative day).

Differences from protocol

The secondary outcomes listed above are those reported in this paper. Compared with the secondary outcomes prespecified in the protocol (Supplementary Note 1), the following outcomes were not reported: respiratory rate, total ciprofol dose, use of vasoactive drugs, time to loss of consciousness, anesthesiologist satisfaction score, and the number of endoscope removals due to hypoxia. Gastroscopy duration was recorded but not analyzed as an outcome. These deviations were due to the focus of the present analysis on clinically relevant respiratory and safety outcomes; the omitted variables were either not systematically recorded or deemed less relevant for the primary study question.

No adjustments were made for multiple comparisons in secondary or subgroup analyses; therefore, all such results should be considered exploratory and hypothesis‑generating. The subgroup analyses presented in Fig. 2 are exploratory.

Statistics and reproducibility

Sample size calculation

According to Kang et al.13, 42.4% of people living with obesity who underwent upper endoscopic procedures experienced oxygen desaturation following sedation with propofol. In our preliminary experiment, the ciprofol group had an oxygen desaturation rate of 20%. To identify an absolute difference of 22.4% in the incidence of hypoxemia (20.0% in the ciprofol group vs. 42.4% in the propofol group with a significance level of 0.05, we calculated that 63 participants per group would be necessary to achieve a statistical power of at least 0.8 for detecting an effect size. Taking into account an anticipated dropout rate of 15%, we decided to recruit 74 patients for each group (PASS 15.0).

Statistical analysis

For continuous variables, the Shapiro-Wilk test was employed to evaluate their distribution. Data following a normal distribution were reported as means with standard deviations (SDs). For data not normally distributed, medians with interquartile ranges(IQRs) were used, and intergroup differences were assessed using the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables were displayed as counts and percentages, analyzed through the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test, as suitable. Baseline data comparisons between groups included standardized mean differences (SMDs) to illustrate any imbalance, with an SMD > 0.1 considered indicative of a meaningful difference. Treatment effects between propofol and ciprofol groups were expressed as absolute risk differences with 95% CIs for binary outcomes and as Hodges-Lehmann estimated median differences with 95% CIs for continuous, non-normally distributed outcomes14. We used multivariable logistic regression or linear regression to adjust the study outcomes for potential confounding factors, including sex, age, height, weight, BMI, modified mallampati score, abdominal circumference, stop-bang questionnaire, hypertension, and diabetes, as well as coronary heart disease. No adjustments were made for multiple comparisons in the secondary or subgroup analyses; therefore, all such results should be considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating, requiring cautious interpretation and external validation. The subgroup analyses presented in Fig. 2 were exploratory. All analyses were performed using R (version 3.4.1; R Foundation for Statistical Computing). P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Reproducibility

All data represent biological replicates (i.e., independent patients; each data point corresponds to a distinct individual). The randomized controlled trial design and adequate sample size ensure the reliability and reproducibility of the findings. The study protocol is publicly available at the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2300074217).

Secondary outcomes

Regarding the secondary outcomes during gastroscopy (Table 2), a significantly higher frequency of apnea episodes was observed in the propofol group than the ciprofol group (17.9% vs 5.9%; absolute difference 12%; 95% CI 1.3% to 22.8%; P = 0.031), and the minimum values of SpO2 of propofol group were significantly lower than that in the ciprofol group (90 [80 to 95] vs 93 [90 to 97]; absolute difference −3; 95% CI −8 to 1; P = 0.006). Also, the need for increasing the volume of oxygen due to hypoxemia was much higher in the propofol group (70.1% vs 54.4%; absolute difference 15.7%; 95% CI −0.4% to 31.9%; P = 0.059). However, the methods of airway maneuvre, including the use of a nasopharyngeal ventilator, mask ventilation, and jaw-lift maneuver did not show significant differences in utilization. One patient in each group had removed the gastroscope tube due to hypoxemia and was ventilated with a mask, but no patient was intubated with a tracheal tube. Additionally, awake time was longer for ciprofol compared to propofol (6 [5 to 9] vs 7 [6 to 9]; absolute difference −1; 95% CI −2 to 1; P = 0.044); the duration of induction time, insertion time, and recovery time in two groups were comparable. Notably, the satisfaction of endoscopists was higher for ciprofol compared to propofol (4 [3 to 5] vs 5 [4 to 5]; absolute difference −1; 95% CI −1 to 0; P = 0.006). Complications of patients

Complications associated with sedation, including hypotension, occurred in seven patients after induction with propofol and in three patients after induction with ciprofol (10.4% vs 4.4%; absolute difference 6.0%; 95% CI −2.8% to 14.8%; P = 0.207), and two of patients in propofol group and three of patients in ciprofol group were observed bradycardia (3.0% vs 4.4%; absolute difference −1.4%; 95% CI −7.8% to 4.9%; P = 1), hypertension and tachycardia were not observed in patients. Muscle fasciculation occurred in two patients in the propofol group and in 4 patients in the ciprofol group (5.9% vs 3%; absolute difference −2.9%; 95% CI 9.8% to 4.0%; P = 0.680). Injection pain showed a significant disparity, with a higher proportion of patients experiencing pain with propofol compared to ciprofol (26.9% vs 1.5%; absolute difference 25.4%; 95% CI 14.4% to 36.4%; P < 0.001). A total of five patients experienced choking cough during gastroscopy, two in the propofol group and one in the ciprofol group developed laryngospasm (Table 3).

Discussion

People living with obesity undergoing gastroscopy are at a higher risk for respiratory depression and hypoxemia. Our study assessed the efficacy and safety of ciprofol-based sedation in this population. We found that ciprofol, combined with sufentanil, led to fewer hypoxemia incidents compared to propofol, which suggests that ciprofol provides effective sedation with reduced respiratory-related adverse events.

Intravenous anesthesia greatly reduces discomfort during gastroscopy, with advancements in anesthetic techniques and the growing emphasis on comfort-oriented medical care, more people living with obesity are now eligible for painless gastroscopy. Previous studies have shown that nasopharyngeal airway devices or combining esketamine with propofol can reduce the incidence of hypoxemia in people living with obesity15,16. Despite these advances, there remains a lack of standardized sedation protocols for people living with obesity. In our investigation into anesthetic agents for people living with obesity, we observed that ciprofol, compared to propofol, offers a significant advantage in reducing hypoxic complications. This finding was consistently validated through preliminary studies and a randomized controlled trial. Ciprofol, when used with sufentanil, demonstrated superior efficacy in maintaining respiratory stability. These results suggest that ciprofol may be a promising alternative to propofol, providing enhanced safety and effectiveness in the sedation of people living with obesity undergoing gastroscopy.

In our previous study, we conducted a preliminary study to determine the effective dose (ED95) of two anesthetic agents to suppress the response to gastroscopy insertion. We found that, with the administration of 0.1 µg/kg of sufentanil, the ED95 for propofol and ciprofol in people living with obesity (BMI 30–40 kg/m²) was 1.32 mg/kg (95% CI: 1.05–1.81) and 0.34 mg/kg (95% CI: 0.21–0.55), respectively (Supplementary Note 1). Consequently, we selected induction doses of 1.3 mg/kg for the propofol group and 0.3 mg/kg for the ciprofol group. In this study, sufentanil was chosen as a pre-anesthetic agent. While obesity increases both the volume of distribution and clearance of sufentanil, it does not significantly alter its elimination rate17,18. Our results demonstrated that the incidence of hypoxemia in people living with obesity undergoing painless gastroscopy with propofol and sufentanil was 34.3%, which is lower than the 40.4% observed in previous studies using propofol alone16. Additionally, none of the patients in either group required rescue sedation, indicating that the selected dosages are appropriate and may serve as a reference for clinical practice.

In our study, 19.1% of people living with obesity in the ciprofol group experienced hypoxemia. This represents a 15% reduction in hypoxemic events compared to the propofol group, indicating a significant advantage in safety for ciprofol. Additionally, airway interventions were reduced, particularly in terms of increasing oxygen flow, in the ciprofol group. Our exploratory subgroup analysis further supported these findings, suggesting that ciprofol may be more effective in maintaining adequate oxygenation in people living with obesity.

The reduction in apnea episodes among people living with obesity in the ciprofol group is noteworthy. Traditionally, obstructive sleep apnea has been considered to the upper airway anatomy, whereby craniofacial structures or body fat reduce the size of the pharyngeal airway lumen, leading to an increased pharyngeal collapse19,20. During wakefulness, the airway remains open due to the high activity of a large number of upper airway dilator muscles, but after the anesthetic sedation, muscle activity decreases and the airway collapses21,22. Additionally, abdominal obesity contributes to the increased risk of apnea. The excess abdominal fat can impede diaphragmatic movement and reduce lung volumes, further compromising respiratory function during sedation23. Ciprofol’s pharmacokinetic profile, characterized by rapid onset and recovery with minimal accumulation, may offer distinct advantages over propofol by maintaining better respiratory function in people living with obesity24.

Hemodynamic fluctuations are among the most common complications in painless endoscopic procedures. Although patients in the propofol group showed a numerically higher incidence of hypotension (10.4 vs. 4.4%), this difference did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.207). Interestingly, this finding contrasts with a previous study conducted on normal-weight patients, where the mean arterial pressure (MAP) of patients in the ciprofol group was significantly lower than that of the propofol group one minute after administration25. People living with obesity often have comorbid conditions, such as hypertension and cardiovascular disease, thus maintaining stable blood pressure and heart rate during sedation is critical. The slower onset and more gradual peak effect of ciprofol may contribute to favorable hemodynamic profiles26. And an increasing number of studies have shown that patients using ciprofol have smoother hemodynamics compared to propofol27–29. However, in our study, the difference in hypotension rates between groups was not statistically significant, and this finding should be interpreted with caution.

Our study revealed that patients in the ciprofol group experienced a longer awakening time compared with the propofol group. However, this difference did not significantly impact clinical usability, as the extended recovery time remained within acceptable limits for outpatient procedures. The satisfaction of endoscopists was notably higher in the ciprofol group, due to the better quality of sedation, and minimal patient movement during procedures. In addition, ciprofol’s formulation as an oil-in-water emulsion, necessitated by its limited solubility in water, leads to milder injection site pain compared to propofol. Furthermore, its lower hydrophobicity and reduced plasma concentrations may also contribute to the decreased incidence of injection pain30.

However, it is worth noting that ciprofol has been reported to cause myoclonus in some cases, which is a consideration for its clinical use10,31. In our study, four patients in the ciprofol group experienced myoclonic movements. The etiology of myoclonus with ciprofol is not entirely understood, but it may be related to its action on the central nervous system, particularly its interaction with GABA receptors. Although these movements were transient and did not lead to significant complications, they warrant further investigation to understand their mechanism and to develop strategies for mitigation. Additionally, both groups exhibited minimal complications in the recovery room and within the first three days post-procedure. This favorable outcome can likely be attributed to the short duration of the gastroscopic procedures and the low doses of sedatives used.

Our trial demonstrated that the combination of ciprofol and sufentanil led to a lower incidence of oxygen desaturation during gastroscopic procedures, offering a superior safety and satisfaction profile compared to propofol. However, it has several limitations. Firstly, we specifically included patients aged 18–65 years with an ASA physical status of II and a BMI between 30 and 40. While these selection criteria allowed us to observe the effects in this particular patient population, they may not fully capture the drug’s efficacy and safety in other age groups, patients with higher ASA classifications, or individuals with morbid obesity (BMI > 40). The exclusion of these groups limits the generalizability of our findings, and further studies are needed to evaluate the applicability of ciprofol in a broader range of people living with obesity, particularly those with more complex medical conditions or higher surgical risks.

Secondly, our study did not monitor end-tidal carbon dioxide (ETCO2), primarily due to the typically short duration of gastroscopic procedures, which generally do not lead to significant carbon dioxide accumulation. However, in longer procedures, such as colonoscopies or therapeutic endoscopies, routine monitoring of ETCO2 is recommended to detect early signs of hypoventilation and prevent potential respiratory complications. Future research should consider incorporating comprehensive respiratory monitoring to better understand the sedative’s impact on ventilation, especially in prolonged procedures.

Third, as a single-center study, our findings require validation in larger, multi-center trials to ensure generalizability and to fully characterize the drug’s profile across diverse clinical settings.

Fourth, we acknowledge that the observed hypoxemia rate in the propofol group (34.3%) was lower than the 42.4% rate used for sample size calculation, which was based on the study by Kang et al. As a result, the actual statistical power to detect the observed effect size (a 15.2% absolute risk reduction) fell below the pre-specified 80% threshold. Accordingly, findings from secondary outcomes---particularly those showing non-significant trends---should be interpreted with caution, and larger, multi-center studies are warranted to confirm these results. In addition, future research may benefit from incorporating more sensitive, patient-centered outcomes and quality-of-recovery metrics to better capture the full spectrum of post-procedural effects beyond the immediate recovery period.

Conclusions

In summary, our study demonstrates that ciprofol, when used with sufentanil for sedation during gastroscopy in people living with obesity, is associated with a lower incidence of hypoxemia and reduced apnea duration, indicating better respiratory safety compared to propofol. It also showed a trend toward fewer hypotensive events, which may be relevant for managing cardiovascular risks in this patient population, although this difference did not reach statistical significance in our study. The reduced incidence of injection pain further enhances patient comfort. These findings suggest that ciprofol, combined with sufentanil, might be a safer and more comfortable sedation option for people living with obesity undergoing gastroscopy.
