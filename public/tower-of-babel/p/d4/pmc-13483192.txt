Establishment and validation of NQICD: a new nutrient profiling model for evaluating Chinese dishes
Authors: Zhuo Sun, Jiangyue Yu, Wei Lu, Xinyi Lu, Xin Cui, Zhengyuan Wang, Qi Song, Shupeng Mai, Zehuan Shi, Liping Shen, Wenqing Ma, Junhua Han, Huiting Yu, Jiajie Zang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWith the increase in dining out, high consumption of salt, oil, and sugar has led to deteriorating diet quality in China. We developed and validated the Nutritional Quality Index of Chinese Dishes (NQICD), a new nutrient profiling model for Chinese composite dishes, addressing a gap not covered by existing models. NQICD was based on seven domains. Its content validity was confirmed against the Dietary Guidelines for Chinese Residents (DGCR), and convergent validity against existing NP models. Criterion validity was assessed using a population-based cohort (n = 3629) with 12-year follow-up, linking i.NQICD to dietary quality, biomarkers, and health outcomes. In content validity, i.NQICD scores (range: 1–10) showed weak-to-moderate correlations with the DGCR (r: −0.39 to −0.21). In criterion validation, each 1-SD increase (≈1.8 points) was associated with lower body mass index (BMI) (−0.13 kg/m²), SBP (−1.69 mmHg), DBP (−1.05 mmHg), and HbA1c (−0.12%). Prospectively, after adjusting for key variables, a higher baseline i.NQICD score was associated with reduced risks of diabetes (HR 0.93, p = 0.024) and all‑cause mortality (HR 0.88, p = 0.011) over 12 years. The NQICD aligns with DGCR goals, demonstrates adequate validity for evaluating Chinese dishes, and can serve as a public health tool to assess their healthiness.Subject terms: Diseases, Endocrinology, Health care, Medical research, Risk factors

Introduction

Dietary changes, including high consumption of sodium, fat, and red meat and low consumption of whole grains, fruits, and vegetables, are driving the rapid rise in noncommunicable diseases (NCDs) across East and Southeast Asia1–3. Among all dietary interventions, improving the nutrition of composite dishes—a staple across the region—is a critical public health priority. In China, dietary patterns centered on composite dishes lead to such dishes accounting for approximately 70% of residents’ energy intake. Typically paired with refined grains (e.g., rice, noodles, steamed bread), these dishes incorporate diverse ingredients such as tubers, starches, legumes, vegetables, and animal foods, making their nutritional profile central to overall dietary health4. However, the preparation of these composite dishes, often reliant on heavy seasonings, leads to excessive intake of fat and salt, with salted condiments alone contributing 82.9% of the sodium in restaurant-prepared composite dishes5. To address this, the Healthy China Initiative (2019–2030) has promoted healthy settings like canteens and restaurants, aiming to improve the nutritional quality of these dishes. Yet the success of such efforts depends on a clear understanding of their nutritional properties—and a specialized evaluation method to assess them remains lacking.

Nutrient profiling (NP), a scientific method for categorizing foods and items based on their nutritional composition, is valued by researchers as a dietary intervention strategy for the population6. NP models are used for front-of-pack labeling, regulating food advertising, implementing food taxes/subsidies, and rating products internally in food enterprises6. NP models have long-term goals of optimizing population health and reducing the risk of diet-related diseases, including obesity and NCDs. Over 100 NP models have been proposed globally, of which more than 60 published algorithms6–8. While most of these were developed in high‑income countries, a growing number of developing countries are also adopting NP models tailored to their unique nutritional challenges9. However, existing NP models may not accurately reflect the nutritional profile of local traditional or onsite-prepared dishes. Therefore, NP models suitable for these foods need to be developed and validated using appropriate food composition data and dietary guidelines6.

To our knowledge, only a few NP models specific to the onsite-prepared foods have been developed globally, including the National Healthy School Canteens Project of Australia and the Nutrient Profile Model for Dishes in Japan Version 1.0 (NPM-DJ (1.0))10,11. The Australian model categorizes school canteen foods into three tiers (green, amber, red) based on nutritional quality, with guidelines recommending green foods be “permanently available” and red foods “not included” on menus10. NPM-DJ (1.0) is developed based on the Health Star Rating (HSR) algorithm, but does not provide a clear health classification for composite dishes11. Moreover, few of these models have undergone rigorous validation.

However, no studies have reported a scoring NP model for classifying “healthy” or “unhealthy” Chinese dishes. To fill these gaps, and in direct response to the Shanghai Municipal Commission of Health’s request, we developed the Nutritional Quality Index of Chinese Dishes (NQICD) as a specialized NP model for Chinese composite dishes. The development of the NQICD commenced in July 2021, and it has been formally incorporated into the Shanghai local standard Specifications for the Establishment of Healthy Canteens (DB31/T 1604-2025) to evaluate composite dishes in community canteens in accordance with its provisions12. Its primary aims are to mitigate excessive salt, oil, and sugar in Chinese cuisine, and to support evidence-based decisions in healthy food choices, recipe reformulation, corporate governance, and public policy. We validated the NQICD by assessing its content and convergent validity using an internal database of Chinese dishes, and further verified its criterion validity by examining associations between individual NQICD scores and dietary quality, biomarkers, and health outcomes—ultimately evaluating its potential population-level health benefits.

Results

Domains and attributes of the NQICD

The basis for setting nutritional thresholds in the algorithm is shown in Supplementary Table 1. Energy (2 points), added oil (2.5 points), added salt (2 points), added sugar (0.5 points), cooking methods involved (1 point), the number of food groups (1 point), and the degree of processing of the main ingredients (1 point) were the main domains (Supplementary Table 2).

Distribution of the NQICD score in the food database of Chinese dishes

The 10,023-dish database includes 28.9% plant-based, 21.1% animal-based, and 49.9% mixed dishes (Table 1). By cooking method, stir-fried (44.7%) was the most common, followed by braised (11.3%), stewed/casseroled/simmered (11.1%), and others. NQICD scores varied by ingredient type (animal-based: 6.0 [4.0, 7.0]; plant-based: 8.0 [7.0, 9.0]) and cooking method (deep-fried: 5.0 [4.0, 6.0]; porridge: 9.0 [9.0, 10.0]) (Fig. 1).

Content validity of the NQICD

Supplementary Table 6 presents the nutritional characteristics of the solid dishes based on a 1–10 scale, classified as “encouraged,” “consumed in moderation,” or “minimized.” Each NQICD point significantly distinguished the content of limiting components (Fig. 2). As scores increased from 1 to 10, energy decreased from 320.30 kcal/100 g to 80.45 kcal/100 g, added oil from 10.45 g/100 g to 1.14 g/100 g, and added salt from 1.91 g/100 g to 0.45 g/100 g. The proportion of animal foods decreased from 77.28 to 13.41%, while FVN increased from 0 to 60.47%, and legumes and milk from 19.96 to 40.78%.

The nutritional characteristics of the liquid dishes are shown in Supplementary Table 7. Like solid foods, liquid foods also showed the above pattern. However, due to sample size and NQICD threshold limitations, liquid food scores were unevenly distributed between 1 and 10 points. Thus, we adjusted the “minimized” and “moderation” thresholds for liquid foods to <8 and 8–9, respectively.

Criterion validity of the NQICD

Demographic characteristics of the included subjects and distribution of i.NQICD

The mean (SD) age of included participants was 52.4 years (16.1), 52.1% were female, and 20.2% had a college degree or more (Supplementary Table 8). The mean (SD) BMI was 23.5 kg/m2 (2.98); blood glucose level, HbA1c: 5.24% (2.13); fasting plasma glucose: 5.20 mg/dL (1.77); TC: HDL-C ratio: 3.44 (1.09); blood pressure level, systolic: 127 mmHg (26.3), and diastolic: 79.6 mmHg (15.3). Among the participants, about 30% of energy intake came from non-seasoned foods (e.g., staples, snacks, fruits, milk, beverages), which were not scored by NQICD.

The mean (SD) i.NQICD was 5.21 (1.80), with the 5th and 95th values of 2.58 and 8.20, respectively (i.NQICD, theoretical range: 0–10). Participants with higher scores were generally older and more educated in Supplementary Table 8. Those with higher i.NQICD scores were more likely to be nonsmokers, drink less alcohol, and be more physically active.

NQICD against a dietary balance score

The relationships between i.NQICD and DBI are shown in Fig. 3. The i.NQICD showed a weak correlation with DBI-DQD (−0.39), DBI-LBS (−0.36), and DBI-HBS (−0.21). Overall, an increase in the i.NQICD score decreased the degree of food intake deficiency (DBI-LBS), food intake excess (DBI-HBS), and overall dietary imbalance (DBI-DQD).

NQICD against clinical risk factors and cross-sectional health conditions

A higher i.NQICD score was significantly associated with more favorable BMI, SBP, DBP, LDL-C, and HbA1c values (Table 2). In the adjusted linear regression models, each SD increase in i.NQICD score (approx. 1.8 points) was associated with a lower BMI (−0.13 kg/m2 [−0.23, −0.02]), SBP (−1.69 mmHg [−2.50, −0.88]), DBP (−1.05 mmHg [−1.55, −0.54]), and HbA1c (−0.12% [−0.20, −0.05]).

NQICD against prospective health outcomes

Cox regression models analyzed the associations between i.NQICD and prospective health outcomes (Table 4). Higher baseline i.NQICD scores were associated with lower risks of diabetes (HR: 0.93 [0.87, 0.99], p = 0.024) and all-cause death (HR: 0.88 [0.80, 0.97], p = 0.011). In the 18–75-year-old subgroup, inverse associations between the i.NQICD score and the risk of hypertension, diabetes, and all-cause death were found (hypertension: HR: 0.93 [0.89, 0.98], p = 0.008; diabetes: HR: 0.90 [0.84, 0.97], p = 0.004; all-cause death: HR: 0.85 [0.74, 0.98], p = 0.023). However, in the ≥75-year subgroup, only the i.NQICD score showed a positive association with stroke risk, but this association was no longer significant after adjustment for hypertension. Over the 12-year follow-up period, the i.NQICD score demonstrated no significant associations with hyperlipidemia, hyperuricemia, gout, anemia, coronary heart disease, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, or chronic kidney disease incidence.

Discussion

This study developed and validated the NQICD, a new NP model for Chinese composite dishes, incorporating chronic disease risk factors, alignment with the DGCR, expert consensus, and targets for nutrients to limit. Content validity confirmed its ability to identify low-salt, -oil, and -sugar composite dishes, with scores well-matched to DGCR goals. Convergent validation showed strong correlations with scores to limit in previous NP models. Cross-sectional and predictive validation linked higher i.NQICD scores to better health metrics (e.g., blood pressure, BMI, HbA1c) and reduced risks of obesity, hypertension, diabetes, and all-cause mortality, supporting its utility for food evaluation and recipe reformulation.

As expected, the correlations between i.NQICD and the Chinese Diet Balance Index (DBI) were weak to moderate (r ranging from −0.39 to −0.21). This is because the DBI assesses overall dietary balance across all food groups, whereas the i.NQICD specifically evaluates the nutritional quality of seasoned composite dishes, which accounted for approximately 70% of total energy intake. Thus, the two measures capture complementary dimensions of diet quality, and the modest correlation does not diminish the robust criterion validity of the NQICD demonstrated by its associations with clinical and long-term health outcomes.

The observed reductions in systolic blood pressure (−1.69 mmHg per SD) and HbA1c (−0.12% per SD), while modest in magnitude, are comparable to those reported for other NP systems and may have population-level public health significance. The borderline significant inverse association with obesity (p = 0.044) suggests a potential effect, although further validation in larger populations is warranted. Regarding prospective outcomes, the inverse associations with diabetes and all-cause mortality were consistent across the whole cohort and the younger subgroup. Null findings for other outcomes (e.g., hyperlipidemia, CKD) may be attributable to limited statistical power or insufficient follow-up duration for these conditions.

Most existing NP models target packaged or ultra-processed foods, which differ substantially from traditional composite dishes. For example, the WHO Pan American Health Organization (PAHO/WHO) NP focuses on processed foods13; the WHO Southeast Asia (SEARO/WHO) and Western Pacific organizations (WPRO/WHO) NPs14,15 include composites but target ready-to-eat/convenience foods, which are ill-suited for traditional or onsite-prepared Chinese dishes14,15. The NQICD fills this gap, offering insights for NP development in Asian composite-dominant diets. It addresses high salt, oil, and sugar intake and declining diet quality from increased dining out in China, supporting effective public health applications.

Practical applications of NQICD include integration into online-to-offline (O2O) food delivery platforms, automated restaurants, and local or national standards. In China, O2O food delivery serves 545 million users, with more than 300 million daily orders16; given that 72% of users prefer local cuisine17, even small per-meal improvements can drive substantial long-term dietary benefits16. In addition, the NQICD has already been incorporated into Shanghai’s local standards to encourage healthier dish reformulation and menu labeling.

This study evaluated the internal validity of the NQICD, establishing a theoretical foundation for its application, while face validity is currently being investigated18. A comparable canteen intervention using dish “traffic light” labels (similar to NQICD) showed increased fruit/vegetable intake and reduced animal/plant food ratios in the intervention group19. Although this short-term study revealed only a slight decrease in body weight in the intervention group (β = −0.43, p = 0.074), it also showed the potential of threshold nutrition labeling of Chinese dishes to shift dietary preferences and provide health benefits19.

Furthermore, the study used multidimensional validation, marking the first NP model validation with a large-scale Chinese composite dish database. Xie et al. developed DNI (adapting NRF), while it lacks large-scale validation and has single-nutrient bias (e.g., stir-fried pork liver scored high in small tests due to vitamin A, conflicting with DGCR’s red meat limits)20. The NQICD constructed here balanced reliability and usability, effective for community interventions. Another strength is the use of energy-weighted i.NQICD scores for criterion validity, which directly links NP-rated healthy foods to objective health metrics21. Owing to the use of objective indicators, predictive criterion validation and concurrent criterion validation are considered to be the most reliable and complex parts of internal validation22,23. In a meta-analysis analyzing NP validity, only nine NP models were included because of their reliable criterion validation22. The FSA-Ofcom NP, HSR, NP Scoring Criterion, Food Compass, Overall Nutrition Quality Index, and NRF Index were found to have intermediate criterion validation22. Our study showed that NQICD reduces health risks, especially blood pressure, aligning with high sodium in Chinese dishes. These findings are comparable to those of established global NP systems. For example, the Food Compass score was associated with a 7% lower risk of all-cause mortality per 1 SD increase in U.S. adults (HR: 0.93)21; while a less favorable Nutri-Score (indicating poorer diet quality) was associated with increased cardiovascular risk (HR: 1.03 per 1 SD) and all-cause mortality (HR: 1.19 for highest vs. lowest quartile) in large European cohorts22.

However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, content and convergent validation showed that 100 g as the evaluation unit may be affected by food density, reducing NQICD’s discrimination for liquid/semisolid foods. However, the 100 g unit is more intuitive than 100 kcal or servings, helping chefs adjust ingredients. A further limitation is that food data, mainly from Shanghai’s canteens/restaurants, reflects Jiangnan diets but lacks high-sodium or high-oil dishes. The sample had more canteen dishes (about 70%) than restaurants (30%), leading to higher scores—unrepresentative of China, as social restaurants typically use more oil, salt, and sugar. For example, our solid dishes had a median [IQR] added oil of 1.97 g/100 g, compared to 2.50 g/100 g in Cantonese dishes24 and 5.6 g/100 g in restaurant dishes from Heilongjiang, Hunan, and Sichuan25. Thus, broader validation across China’s diverse diets is needed. Beyond China, given the growing global interest in Asian cuisines, future research could also explore the applicability of the NQICD framework to composite dishes in other Asian countries (e.g., Japan, Korea, Thailand) to facilitate cross-cultural comparisons. Third, although our criterion validation adjusted for a comprehensive set of demographic and lifestyle factors (age, sex, education, occupation, smoking, alcohol, physical activity), residual confounding cannot be completely ruled out. Dietary patterns and other unmeasured lifestyle factors may still influence the observed associations between i.NQICD and health outcomes.

In summary, this study developed and validated the NQICD, a new NP model specifically designed for Chinese composite dishes. The NQICD aligns closely with the DGCR. Higher individual NQICD scores were associated with more favorable dietary patterns, lower blood pressure and BMI, and reduced risks of diabetes, hypertension, and all-cause mortality over 12 years. These findings support the NQICD as a practical public health tool for assessing dish nutritional quality and guiding healthier food choices in catering settings. Future research should extend validation across broader geographic regions and evaluate its real-world implementation outcomes.

Methods

This study developed the NQICD based on prior NP model methodologies. During its development, domains were determined through panel discussions and expert consultations, and specific nutritional thresholds were set based on existing literature and nutrient distribution profiles from a collected Chinese dish database. The validation for the NQICD included content, convergent, and criterion validity assessments: content and convergent validity were tested against different NP models using standardized recipes, while criterion validity was verified with the Shanghai Diet and Health Survey (SDHS). Finally, the Dish-Choice was developed based on the NQICD. The study flowchart is presented in Fig. 4.

Development of the NQICD algorithm

A panel of 10 experts with expertise in health administration, nutrition, culinary arts, and standards development determined key domains via consensus, focusing on: (1) chronic disease risk factors (e.g., obesity, diabetes); (2) alignment with the Dietary Guidelines for Chinese Residents (DGCR) and nutrient intake recommendations; (3) current status of restricted nutrients (salt, oil, sugar) in Chinese dishes.

The final algorithm uses a 10-point scale (1 = least healthy, 10 = most healthy) across 7 domains: energy, added oil, added salt, added sugar, cooking methods, food group diversity, and ingredient processing degree. Detailed algorithm descriptions are provided in Supplementary Materials, including the domains and nutritional thresholds of the final NQICD algorithm (Supplementary Table 1), the rationale for threshold setting (Supplementary Table 2), the NQICD calculation method, and definitions of terms used in the algorithm (Supplementary Table 3).

Seven preset algorithms with varying weighting schemes were developed and validated. Detailed configurations of each algorithm are provided in Supplementary Table 4, score distributions are summarized in Supplementary Table 5, and associations between algorithm scores and food groups are illustrated in Supplementary Figs. 1–6. Model 6 was selected as the final model because it showed strong correlations with existing NP models (Supplementary Fig. 7).

Food database for verification

A Chinese composite food database containing quantified recipes was developed for testing. Data were collected from September 2020 to January 2025, yielding a total of 11,809 original dish records. During data analysis, we excluded (1) dishes with implausibly high levels of oil and salt (e.g., beyond the 95th percentile of the distribution) or those lacking main ingredients (n = 626), and (2) duplicate dish records with identical dish names, ingredient compositions, calculated nutrient values, and scores (n = 1160), resulting in a final sample of 10,023 quantified recipes. The database includes detailed information on dish names, ingredients, and seasoning weights per serving or per pot, cooking methods, and target consumer groups. Among these, 8879 recipes came from 295 catering institutions and 1144 from published recipe books or cooking SOPs from large-scale collective catering companies in Shanghai.

Dishes were classified in two stages: (1) By ingredient origin (using standard food codes from The Food Composition Tables, 6th edition): dishes with ≥2.5% (W/W) plant-based major ingredients as “plant-based dishes,” those with only animal-based major ingredients as “animal-based dishes,” and those with both as “mixed dishes”; (2) By cooking method: manually classified by two experienced nutrition researchers into stir-fried, braised, stewed/casseroled/simmered, steamed, deep-fried, roasted/grilled/broiled/Teppanyaki, composite staple foods (e.g., fried noodles, fried rice), porridge, and soup.

Content and convergent validity of the NQICD with different NP models based on standardized recipes

Content validity, which involves evaluating the extent to which the NP model can discriminate between and rank product healthiness based on the current scientific literature22, was assessed using LOWESS curves (y = levels of key food groups, x = NQICD scores) to analyze associations between NQICD scores and DGCR key food groups (animal foods, fruits/vegetables/nuts (FVN), legumes/dairy, added oil/salt/sugar). Monotonic trends across NQICD groups1–10 for continuous nutritional variables were tested via the Jonckheere-Terpstra test26,27.

Convergent validity was assessed by comparing the NQICD scores for foods with those determined by a reference NP22,28. We compared NQICD scores with three reference NP models (selected for scoring-based design, applicability to composite dishes, and relevance to Chinese cuisine): FSA-Ofcom29–31, Dish Nutrient Index (DNI)32, and NOVA33,34.

Reverse-scored versions of some reference indices (where higher scores indicate unhealthiness) were used for directional alignment (reversed DNI-LIM3, FSA-A, FSA-T, NOVA). Agreement was tested via the interclass correlation coefficient (ICC).

Calculation of the FSA-Ofcom score

The FSA-Ofcom model was developed to regulate food advertising targeting children in the UK29,35. The FSA-Ofcom model had two algorithms, which were specific to beverages and solid food7. This model considers four restrictive components (energy, sugar, saturated fat, and sodium) and three encouraging components (protein, dietary fiber, and the proportion of fruits/vegetables/nuts)3. The FSA-Ofcom total score (FSA-T) consists of the FSA-A point (FSA-A) and the FSA-Ofcom C point (FSA-C). FSA-A is a score for restrictive nutrients, with a maximum score of 40. A higher FSA-A score indicates a greater number of negative nutrients in the food. In contrast, FSA-C is a score for encouraging nutrients, with a maximum score of 15. Criterion validity of the individual NQICD using the SDHS

Criterion validity (assessing associations between NP-based food consumption and objective health measures23) utilized representative SDHS data of 18–79-year-old adults37. Individuals with abnormal daily energy intake (<500 or >6500 kcal/day) or incomplete key information were excluded.

The individual Nutrient Quality Index for Chinese Dishes (i.NQICD, scored 1–10 via weighted cumulative dish scores) was validated against1: the Chinese Diet Balance Index (DBI)2,38–42; cross-sectional clinical risk factors/health conditions3; 12-year follow-up disease outcomes.

The Ethics Committee of the Shanghai Centre for Disease Control and Prevention approved the SDHS survey protocol for the collection of data and biological samples in 2012 (supported by an unnumbered original document) and the follow-up protocol in 2024 (No. KY-2024-16).

Calculation of the i.NQICD

Individual dietary data were collected via 3-day 24-h recalls1: all Chinese composite dishes consumed (dish names and ingredient amounts per meal)2; non-seasoned foods (e.g., rice, snacks)3; dining locations4; household seasoning usage (weight changes) and number of diners.

Seasoning amounts per dish were estimated from total weighted seasoned foods consumed by household members (excluding non-seasoned items like fruits, snacks, rice, milk). Out-of-home dish seasonings were estimated via home usage references and energy conversion. i.NQICD (theoretical range: 0–10) was calculated by weighting each dish’s NQICD score by its percentage of the individual’s total energy intake from seasoned foods, and then the results were summed.

Calculation of DBI

To assess the correlation between participants’ i.NQICD and an externally derived healthy dietary pattern, we calculated the DBI, a measure of adherence to the balanced diet based on the widely validated DGCR39,40,43. Calculation method has been described in prior studies38–42, and we previously detailed it using 2012 SDHS data. The DBI comprises three scores: the high bound score (HBS) reflecting excess intake, the low bound score (LBS) indicating deficit intake, and the diet quality distance (DQD) measuring overall dietary imbalance. Higher scores in these indicators suggest a greater degree of imbalance in dietary intake41,42.

Cross-sectional clinical risk factors and health conditions

SDHS applied a standardized approach to collect data on clinical risk factors and health conditions37. Trained health workers measured body weight, height, waist, and hip circumferences. Overweight or obesity was assessed using Chinese BMI classifications44. Blood pressure was measured using an electronic sphygmomanometer (Omron HEM-7071). Fasting venous blood samples were collected in the morning. Blood samples were analyzed using a fully automated biochemical analyzer (Hitachi 7080) to measure total cholesterol (TC), triglyceride (TG), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), blood glucose (GLU), and glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c). Cross-sectional health conditions (diabetes, cardiovascular disease, and cerebrovascular disease) were self-reported.

Predictive health outcomes

In 2024, SDHS participants were followed up with disease/death records from the citywide diagnosis and treatment system. Diseases (per ICD-10 and first hospital visit) included hypertension (I10), diabetes (E10, E11, E13, E14), hyperlipidemia (E78), hyperuricemia/gout (E79, M10.000, M10.900), anemia (D53, D64), coronary heart disease (CHD: I20–I25), stroke (I60–I64), chronic kidney disease (CKD: N03, N18), and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD: J44). Patients diagnosed before 2012 were excluded. SCDC built a 24-million-person statutory death registry; district CDC physicians monthly verified death data (public security/funeral home) for accuracy. Deaths before January 1, 2024, were extracted from the provincial death surveillance system.

Data analyses

Data were analyzed using R software (version 4.0.5), with statistical significance set at p < 0.05 (two-tailed). Categorical variables were described using frequencies and percentages, while continuous data were presented as mean ± standard deviation or median (p50) and quartiles (p25, p75) based on distribution. For content validity, LOWESS scatter plots and the Jonckheere-Terpstra test were used to examine associations between DGCR key food groups and NQICD scores. For convergent validity, Spearman correlation and ICC were used to assess relationships with other NP scores.

Criterion validity was assessed by evaluating the relationship between i.NQICD and DBI using scatter plots and Spearman correlation. For cross-sectional biomarkers, health conditions, and predictive health outcomes, i.NQICD was analyzed per standard deviation (SD) unit. Linear regression was used for continuous indicators, logistic regression for odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) of health conditions, and Cox regression for hazard ratios (HR) and 95% CI of predictive health outcomes. Cox regression used the date of the first outpatient visit as the time variable. Multivariate models were adjusted for potential confounders, including age, sex, ethnicity, education level, occupation, smoking status, alcohol drinking, physical activity (MET-hours/week), and total energy intake (kcal/day). Subgroup analysis was conducted by age group (<75 years or ≥75 years).
