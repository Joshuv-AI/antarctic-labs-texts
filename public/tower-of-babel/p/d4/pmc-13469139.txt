Ductile binary FCC materials screened by anisotropic and isotropic elastic criteria: first principle insights into deformation mechanisms
Authors: Junhui Luo, Pengcheng Zhai, Lei Sheng, Xiege Huang, Haoqin Ma, Guodong Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Unlike two-dimensional van der Waals (2D vdW) materials, which achieve deformability through dimensional reduction, bulk materials rely more on their intrinsic crystal structures and chemical bond interactions for ductility. Considering the multi-scale complexity of plastic deformation and failure in bulk materials, we propose using B/G and κ criteria to synergistically pre-screen ductile materials. From 152 binary face-centered cubic (FCC) materials, we screened 16 compounds with good ductility, including zincblende structured FeN and rocksalt structured CdO. First principle calculations show that uniform deformation of the atomic framework is the main deformation mechanism for FeN and CdO. Both materials exhibit significant strain before failure, demonstrating good deformability. For CdO, its two slip systems with lower ideal shear strengths are prone to simultaneous activation, and the < 001> direction exhibits a large tensile strain together with a low ideal tensile strength, both of which may promote its ductility. Generalized stacking fault energy (GSFE) calculations confirm the lowest energy barriers for the most active slip systems, consistent with the ideal shear strength results. Introduction

Inorganic semiconductor materials, owing to their excellent electrical properties, are widely used in functional electronic devices in thermoelectric, optoelectronic, and sensing applications1–3. In sharp contrast to their excellent electrical properties is their general brittleness, which reduces structural reliability and limits the development of related electronic devices. However, with a growing number of deformable and ductile inorganic semiconductors being reported, it will become feasible to develop highly reliable electronic devices in the near future.

In 2018, α-Ag2S and ZnS (in the dark) were reported to exhibit excellent plasticity and ductility at room temperature4,5, which has stimulated interest in exploring ductile inorganic semiconductor and ceramic materials. Afterward, more deformable inorganic materials such as InSe, SnSe2, Mg3Bi2, CrCl3, and AgX (X = Cl, Br)6–10 were reported. Apart from ZnS and silver halides, most of these newly discovered materials are two-dimensional van der Waals (2D vdW) layered. This factor has been applied, with various modifications, to screen deformable 2D chalcogenide semiconductors and layered inorganic semiconductors11,12. It is worth noting that the deformation of 2D vdW materials mainly arises from size-induced bending13, as they are deformable only in single crystals when the number of layers is reduced to a certain extent. Therefore, ductile bulk materials, especially bulk semiconductors, remain underexplored and are worth investigating, as they are expected to compensate for the limited applicability of 2D vdW materials as structural materials.

In bulk materials, due to the complexity of plasticity and fracture, it is difficult to find a universal solution to evaluate the ductility of materials. Elastic constants reflect a material’s resistance to strain and serve as a bridge connecting macroscopic mechanical properties and microscopic chemical bonding characteristics. Since elastic constants can be easily obtained from first principle calculations, screening ductile materials via elastic constants is an ideal and convenient approach. The Pugh’s ratio14 is defined as B/G where B is the bulk modulus and G is the shear modulus. Bulk modulus is used to evaluate fracture strength, while shear modulus is used to evaluate yield strength. It quantifies the competition between plasticity and fracture; a low B/G ratio indicates brittle failure, while a high value indicates ductile failure. Generally, the empirical critical value of Pugh’s ratio for the brittle-to-ductile transition is 1.75. Recently a new ductility factor κ15 has been proposed based on linear elasticity theory. The face-centered cubic (FCC) structure exhibits a high degree of symmetry, and its abundant slip paths, especially those on the (111) plane, contribute to enhanced ductility. In this work, we screened binary FCC inorganic compounds based on two elasticity criteria: the isotropic elastic criterion Pugh’s ratio and the anisotropic elastic criterion κ. First, the elastic constants of 152 stable binary FCC compounds were calculated via first principle calculations. Subsequently, the ductility-related parameters (Pugh’s ratio and κ) were derived. Sixteen materials are identified as theoretically ductile by both of the two criteria, including zincblende structured FeN and rocksalt structured CdO. Finally, we conducted a detailed investigation into the mechanical properties and deformation processes of FeN and CdO, clarifying their deformation and failure mechanisms.

Methods

All first principle calculations were performed using the Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package (VASP) based on density functional theory (DFT). The projector-augmented wave (PAW) method was employed to describe the interaction between the ion nucleus and valence electrons16, and the exchange correlation functional was the Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (PBE) functional within the generalized gradient approximation (GGA)17. The plane-wave cutoff energy of the structural convergence criterion was set to 500 eV, demonstrating good computational convergence. For k-point sampling in reciprocal space, we used the gamma-centered Monkhorst-Pack scheme with a fine resolution of 2π × 0.03 Å−1. The energy tolerance for solving the electronic wavefunction was set to 1 × 10− 6 eV and the force convergence criterion for geometry optimization was set to 1 × 10− 2 eV Å−1. The electron localization function (ELF) was calculated to better understand the chemical bonding. An ELF of 1 corresponds to complete electron localization, while an ELF of 0 corresponds to complete electron delocalization, and an ELF of 0.5 corresponds to a free electron gaseous state18.

To examine shear and tensile deformations, we achieved quasi-static mechanical loading by applying strain to the supercell in the desired direction while allowing the structure to fully relax in other directions19,20. The detailed description of ideal strength calculations is provided in our previous work21–24. In generalized stacking fault energy (GSFE) calculations, a 20 Å vacuum was inserted along the c-axis, with relaxation allowed only in this direction. For FeN, the slab thicknesses of the (111), (110), and (001) slip planes in the GSFE model are 12, 8, and 8 layers, respectively. For CdO, they are 12, 8, and 5 layers. The VESTA software was used for visualizations of the atomic configurations25.

Result and discussion

Material screening

The 152 binary FCC materials were retrieved from the Materials Project database26. Most of these phases have an energy above the convex hull (Ehull) of exactly 0 meV/atom in the database, meaning they are thermodynamically stable. Only three compounds—ZnS, SiC, and MgTe—exhibit an Ehull greater than 0 but no more than 1 meV/atom. Therefore, all phases considered in this work possess excellent thermodynamic stability (or near-stability), providing a solid foundation for assessing their relevance as functional materials. Figure 2 shows a scatter plot of the calculated Pugh’s ratio B/G versus the ductility factor κ for 152 binary FCC materials. Materials in the lower left region have lower κ and B/G values and are predicted to be brittle, while materials in the upper right region have higher κ and B/G values and are considered ductile. Some well-known brittle materials such as the ionic crystal NaCl and the ceramic SiC and materials within the ductility range of the two criteria are labeled, together with 7 FCC elemental metals and typically brittle diamond. We found that 16 binary FCC materials are potentially ductile, including the reported semiconductors ZnS and CdTe and ionic crystal AgCl4,10,28. Typical brittle Ir is located in the lower left area. The other six elemental metals, such as Au, Cu and Al, have good ductility and are located in the upper right area. Therefore, it is effective and credible to screen ductile materials using the combined criteria of Pugh’s ratio B/G and ductility factor κ.

Among the 16 ductile candidates, FeN has the highest Pugh’s ratio (3.474) and the second-highest κ (5.995, second only to HgS) among all zincblende materials. The calculated elastic constants of FeN are C11=340.4 GPa, C12=238.9 GPa, and C44=105.2 GPa, which are consistent with previously reported theoretical values (C11=334.6 GPa, C12=234.6 GPa, and C44=110.6 GPa)29. The rocksalt-structured CdO exhibits high B/G (2.809) and κ (4.351) values, as well as moderate elastic constants: C11=180.3 GPa, C12=97.7 GPa, and C44=46.9 GPa, which also agree well with reported theoretical values (C11=190.9 GPa, C12=101.9 GPa, and C44=48.1 GPa)30. To further verify the effectiveness of the screening, we conducted an in-depth study on the mechanical properties of theoretically predicted ductile FeN and CdO.

Energy explanation of competition between slip systems

To further investigate shear-induced slip and the competition among different slip systems, we calculated the GSFE curves of FeN and CdO along multiple slip systems, as shown in Fig. 11. For FeN (Fig. 11a), partial data points on the glide-set plane with the (111)/\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{<11}\stackrel{\mathrm{-}}{\mathrm{2}}\mathrm{>}$$\end{document} slip system are absent due to the computational non-convergence resulting from the inadequate input atomic configuration with various overlapping atoms. The (111)/\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{<1}\stackrel{\mathrm{-}}{\mathrm{1}}\mathrm{0>}$$\end{document} and (111)/\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{<11}\stackrel{\mathrm{-}}{\mathrm{2}}\mathrm{>}$$\end{document} slip systems have the lowest energy barrier of 1777 mJ/m2 and the second lowest energy barrier of 1846 mJ/m2, respectively. Conclusions

In this work, we screened 16 compounds from 152 binary FCC materials that meet both of the isotropic elastic criterion Pugh’s ratio and the anisotropic elastic criterion \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\kappa$$\end{document}. This synergistic screening strategy can be further applied to other structural bulk materials to discover more potentially ductile materials. The ideal strengths of FeN and CdO indicate that shear deformation is the dominant mode of deformation and failure under external loads. The slip systems most easily activated by shear are (111)/\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{<1}\stackrel{\mathrm{-}}{\mathrm{1}}\mathrm{0>}$$\end{document} and (111)/\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{<11}\stackrel{\mathrm{-}}{\mathrm{2}}\mathrm{>}$$\end{document} for FeN, and (110)/\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{<1}\stackrel{\mathrm{-}}{\mathrm{1}}\mathrm{0>}$$\end{document} and (111)/\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{<}\stackrel{\mathrm{-}}{\mathrm{1}}\stackrel{\mathrm{-}}{\mathrm{1}}\mathrm{2>}$$\end{document} for CdO, which is consistent with the GSFE results. The deformations of FeN and CdO mainly stem from the uniform deformation of the atomic framework under strain, and both materials exhibit significant strain before failure, demonstrating good deformability. Notably, shear-induced perfect dislocation slip along (111)/\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{<1}\stackrel{\mathrm{-}}{\mathrm{1}}\mathrm{0>}$$\end{document} in the shuffle-set plane and maintaining structural integrity of FeN during both shear processes of the (111)/\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{<1}\stackrel{\mathrm{-}}{\mathrm{1}}\mathrm{0>}$$\end{document} and (111)/\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{<11}\stackrel{\mathrm{-}}{\mathrm{2}}\mathrm{>}$$\end{document} slip systems result in the incomplete release of stress, thereby conferring potential ductility. Although the atomic framework of CdO cannot be restored upon failure, its ductility and deformability may be enhanced by two factors: the two slip systems with lower ideal shear strengths are prone to simultaneous activation, and the < 001> direction combines a large tensile strain with a low ideal tensile strength.
