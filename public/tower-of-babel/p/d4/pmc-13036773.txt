Engineering Antimicrobial Peptides via Motif Assembly for Combating Multidrug-Resistant Pathogens
Authors: Sukhvir Kaur Bhangu, Fanyi Li, Rong Xu, Thirimadura Vishna Heshini Mendis, Nicholas G. Welch, James Harris, John D. Wade, Mohammed Akhter Hossain, Helmut Thissen, Wioleta Kowalczyk
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Hybridization of common motifs from known antimicrobial peptides (AMPs) is a promising strategy for designing a new class of antimicrobial agents with improved activity. Herein, the machine learning-generated peptide sequence IIKLLGVLAKFVLGQ-NH2 was utilized for sequence optimization using an alanine scan and alignment with known AMPs to identify the motifs with the highest occurrences. Motifs were then assembled to obtain positively charged peptides with 12 to 18 amino acid lengths and hydrophobicity >60%. The designed peptides exhibit broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity against multiple Gram-negative and Gram-positive bacterial strains, along with excellent pH and plasma stability. The peptides demonstrate high efficiency in eradicating bacterial biofilms with no resistance development over 300 generations against a “superbug” clinical strain of Acinetobacter baumannii. Additionally, AMPs were biocompatible and promoted fibroblast migration, which accelerated artificial wound closure. This study highlights an innovative strategy for engineering highly effective AMPs with significant potential in combating multidrug-resistant pathogens.

Introduction

The increasing prevalence of antibiotic resistance necessitates the development of new ways to combat bacterial infections. It has been estimated that antimicrobial resistance (AMR) could lead to ∼10 million deaths by 2050. In particular, the ESKAPE pathogens  Enterococcus faecium (E. faecium), Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus), Klebsiella pneumoniae (K. pneumoniae), Acinetobacter baumannii (A. baumannii), Pseudomonas aeruginosa (P. aeruginosa), and Enterobacter species­(E. aerogenese) are known for causing a high number of deaths and are listed by the World Health Organization (WHO) as priority strains to tackle antimicrobial resistance. These pathogens have a high tendency to develop rapid resistance to antimicrobial drugs, leading to serious health risks and a wide variety of clinical manifestations. , When these infections are acquired in hospital settings, they pose a greater risk to patient health during various medical procedures, such as major surgeries, including organ transplantation and chemotherapy, or in the management of skin and tissue infections. The treatment of hospital- or community-acquired infections remains challenging due to the emergence of multidrug-resistant (MDR) strains. −

Antimicrobial peptides (AMPs) represent a promising means for meeting this challenge. Unlike current antibiotics that heavily rely on interaction with specific target molecules, usually proteins, AMPs have a non-specific mechanism of action and often induce cell death by disrupting the plasma membrane. Moreover, peptides often have broad-spectrum activity, rapid killing action, and are less likely to induce antimicrobial resistance. Short AMP sequences offer a variety of benefits compared to their larger analogues, including better tunability, biocompatibility, stability in physiological conditions, easier synthesis, and reduced production costs. − Effective AMPs can be designed de novo or by using one or more known sequences as templates with the aim of obtaining improved activity. For instance, it was found that lactoferricin, a 25-mer long AMP, can be reduced to a 6-mer active minimum sequence, and human cathelicidin, a 37-mer long AMP, to a 12-mer active minimum sequence. , Combining fragments of natural AMPs to create a hybrid peptide from the biofunctional motifs has been shown to improve antimicrobial activity and selectivity. Depending on the desired biological activity and physicochemical properties, the generated peptides can also be further modified.

The structural and physicochemical features of the peptides are highly correlated to their antimicrobial properties. Positively charged peptides are particularly effective due to their ability to interact electrostatically with negatively charged microbial membranes. Moreover, hydrophobic motifs of the peptides can bind to phospholipid membranes of bacteria, which can impair the membrane by changing permeability, membrane potential, or the formation of pores, eventually leading to cell death. However, hydrophobicity can also directly impact the solubility of the peptides, potentially leading to aggregation and a reduction in activity. Therefore, balanced cationic amino acid residues, optimum hydrophobicity, an amidated C-terminal part of the peptide, and a free amino group on the N-terminal part of the peptide are important parameters in AMP discovery. For example, it has been reported that amidation of the C-terminal of Protonectarina-MP (INWKALLDAAKKVL, from the venom of the social wasp Protonectarina sylveirae) shows better activity against both Gram-negative and Gram-positive bacterial strains due to enhanced interaction with the target membrane.

In our previous study, we utilized machine learning to generate new antimicrobial sequences based on the algorithmic assembly of common motifs from AMPs consisting of 15 amino acid residues. These were tested against several Gram-negative and Gram-positive bacterial strains. Herein, we utilized the peptide sequence IIKLLGVLAKFVLGQ-NH2, which was generated using our previously published machine learning algorithm and its sequence optimization was performed using an alanine scan and alignment with known AMPs to identify the motifs with the highest occurrences. We show here that common motifs from known peptides, when combined to form new peptide sequences, can not only fight clinical isolates of ESKAPE pathogens but also combat MDR strains. The activity of the designed peptides was also compared to that of pexiganan. Pexiganan is a peptide derived from magainin-2 (which was first isolated from the Xenopus laevis frog). It consists of 22 amino acid residues (GIGKFLKKAKKFGKAFVKILKK-NH2) and has been shown to possess strong antimicrobial activity against various bacterial pathogens that commonly infect the skin as well as soft tissues in humans. − It was used as a positive control as it has undergone phase III clinical trials for its potential use in the topical treatment of bacterial infections associated with diabetic foot ulcers. We also studied the membrane disruption of bacteria by our lead peptides using Stimulated Emission Depletion (STED) and confocal microscopy. The new peptides were further studied for their efficacy in eradicating bacterial biofilms and wound repair using a scratch test.

Results and Discussions

Optimization and Generation of AMP Peptides

Our recent work on antimicrobial peptide generation and prediction using machine learning has provided a list of highly active antimicrobial peptides. The algorithm was built to construct new peptide sequences that were scored for their antimicrobial activity using a machine learning (ML) model. The ML model was trained using known AMPs and non-AMP sequences. In addition, it identifies amino acid motifs and assembles the high-ranking motifs into new peptide sequences.

The algorithm-generated peptide sequence IIKLLGVLAKFVLGQ-NH2 (hereafter referred to as RBWH00, Table ) exhibited the highest predicted antimicrobial activity among the list of peptides from our previous work. In the present work, we further optimized the RBWH00 sequence and designed a set of new sequences through an alanine scan, truncation, alignment, and motif assembly with the aim to obtain peptides with improved and broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity against various Gram-negative and Gram-positive bacterial strains.

First, the alanine scan was performed to check the impact and contribution of each individual amino acid residue on the antimicrobial activity of peptide RBWH00. Alanine scanning is a regular strategy for peptide optimization to identify the key residues of a peptide sequence. The antimicrobial activity of peptides was determined by using a minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) assay. It estimates the lowest concentration of a drug that inhibits the visible growth of bacteria. The MIC value of RBWH00 against S. aureus (ATCC25923) was found to be 5 μM. Table S1 in the Supporting Information shows alanine-substituted analogues of peptide RBWH00, where each amino acid was replaced by an alanine residue, as well as its truncated analogues. All these peptides were synthesized using solid-phase peptide synthesis (SPPS) (Fmoc/tBu strategy), purified on preparative high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) to ≥95% purity (for the majority of peptides; the purity of four peptides ranged from 90% to 94%), characterized using analytical HPLC and liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC-MS), and tested for antimicrobial activity. Table S1 also provides the MIC values of all analogues of the RBWH00 peptide and its truncated sequences against S. aureus (ATCC25923). Peptides were incubated for 18 h with S. aureus ATCC25923 at concentrations in the range of 1.25–160 μM, and the MIC was determined by measuring the optical density (OD) at 600 nm. MIC values suggested that the substitution of the amino acid at positions 6, 7, and 15, which correspond to glycine, valine, and glutamine residues, respectively, did not impact the antimicrobial activity (MIC: 5 μM). Similarly, the amino acid at position 14, when substituted with alanine, only had a slight effect on antimicrobial activity (MIC: 10 μM). However, the replacement of amino acid residues at positions 1, 3, 4, 5, 8, 10, 11, 12, and 13 had very pronounced effects on the antimicrobial activity, leading to 4-fold to 32-fold increases in MIC values or complete inactivity. Truncated analogues of RBWH00 were also found to be ineffective in inhibiting bacterial growth at all studied concentrations. Hence, based on the above results of the alanine scan, we decided to design two shorter sequences of RBWH00, where the last two amino acids were removed, and peptide RBWH00[1–14] (IIKLLGVLAKFVLG-NH2) and RBWH00[1–13] (IIKLLGVLAKFVL-NH2) were synthesized (Table S2). These designed peptides were tested against S. aureus (ATCC25923) and showed an improved MIC value of 2.5 μM compared to that of peptide RBWH00 (MIC value of 5 μM). These results could be attributed to the higher hydrophobicity percentage of the amino acids in the truncated sequences (Table ).

Further to this, new sequences were designed by performing sequence alignment on peptide RBWH00 to identify any homology/similarity to existing AMPs. Following an initial screening process and alignment of peptide RBWH00 using the Antimicrobial Peptide Database (APD3) (refer to Tables S3–S5), we identified two naturally occurring AMPs (Table S3) with antimicrobial and anti-inflammatory properties, i.e., INIKDILAKLVKVLGHV-NH2 (AP00069, Bombolitin IV) and GVIKSVLKGVAKTVALGML-NH2 (AP01547). Both peptides showed 50% sequence similarity to peptide RBWH00 (Table S3). Peptide AP00069 is found in the Megabombus pennsylvanicus species where it is secreted into the bumblebee venom, while AP01547 is derived from frog skin. Next, using the same alignment tool in APD3, we identified a group of AMPs similar to AP00069 and AP01547 (refer to Tables S4–S5). Interestingly, this alignment process showed that the peptides exhibit common amino acid motifs, which might be essential for their activity. Hence, we identified the most common amino acid motifs based on the amino acids present in peptide RBWH00 from the library of existing AMPs using both the APD3 and DBAASP databases. These additional motifs (KK and GK), along with other identified motifs, were empirically arranged to generate new peptide sequences. The motifs were randomly shuffled before they were rearranged into new peptides manually. Antimicrobial Activity of Peptides

To evaluate the broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity of the designed peptides, we determined their susceptibility against clinical isolates of the ESKAPE pathogens using the MIC assay. The particular A. baumannii (AB5075) strain used in this study is classified as a superbug strain, as it is known to be resistant toward multiple classes of antibiotics. P. aeruginosa strain (PAO1) is also intrinsically resistant to antibiotics and various disinfectants. Moreover, the S. aureus (ATCC43300) strain, resistant to the antibiotics methicillin and oxacillin, was also used to demonstrate the activity of lead peptides against MDR pathogens.

Determined MIC values of various peptides against ESKAPE pathogens are given in Table . The truncated sequence RBWH00[1–13] showed better activity than RBWH00 against S. aureus (ATCC25923), A. baumannii (AB5075), and E. faecalis (ATCC19433). Notably, it was active at significantly lower concentrations of 2.5 μM, 9 μM, and 5 μM against S. aureus (ATCC25923), A. baumannii (AB5075), and E. faecalis (ATCC19433), respectively. Other designed peptide sequences were found to be effective against 2–6 bacterial strains, with peptides RBWH11, RBWH15, and RBWH16 being particularly effective against most of the strains. Specifically, lead peptides RBWH11 and RBWH15 showed broad-spectrum activity at much lower concentrations (∼1.5–18 μM) compared to RBWH16 (∼4.6–55 μM) and other peptides (>18 μM). The addition of a lysine residue was found to impact the activity, possibly due to changes in the net charge, which can modify the interaction of peptides with the bacterial membrane. All peptides were found to be effective (∼1.5–40 μM) against A. baumannii (AB5075), which is a pathogen associated with a high mortality rate in nosocomial infections. The designed peptides were active against P. aeruginosa (PAO1) at slightly higher concentrations relative to other strains, with MIC ranging between 18 and 70 μM. In addition, resistant S. aureus (ATCC43300) strain was treated with lead peptide candidates RBWH11 and RBWH15, and these peptides exhibited the same MIC value as the nonresistant strain of S. aureus (ATCC25923). When compared with AP00069 and AP01547 peptides, lead candidates were much more effective in killing the bacteria (Table ). The MIC value of pexiganan, which was used as a positive control, was below 10 μM against all tested strains. However, lead peptides (RBWH11 and RBWH15) displayed better potency than pexiganan against all other tested strains, except for the P. aeruginosa (PAO1) strain. Additionally, it is worth mentioning that our lead peptides are shorter than pexiganan, which would reduce the cost of manufacturing.

To confirm that the antimicrobial activity of the peptides was due to the specific arrangement of amino acids and motifs, scrambled sequences of the peptides RBWH11 and RBWH00[1–13] were synthesized. scRBWH11 (KVILALGLFIKLK-NH2) and scRBWH00­[1–13] (KVILALGLFIVLK-NH2) did not exhibit antimicrobial activity, even at a concentration as high as 400 μM, when tested against all ESKAPE pathogens. To explore the mechanism of action of the designed peptides, S. aureus (ATCC25923) as a representative of the Gram-positive strain and P. aeruginosa (PAO1) or A. baumannii (AB5075) as Gram-negative strains were used in further studies. The leading peptide candidates against S. aureus (ATCC25923) were RBWH00, RBWH11, RBWH00[1–13], and RBWH15, with MIC values between 1.5 and 2.5 μM. Similarly, against P. aeruginosa (PAO1), the top-performing peptides were RBWH10, RBWH11, RBWH14, and RBWH15, with MIC values in the range of 18–36 μM. These peptides were tested to determine their minimum bactericidal concentration (MBC), which is the lowest concentration of a drug that results in the killing of 99.9% of the bacteria (Table S8). The MBC results suggested that the peptides were able to not only inhibit bacteria but also kill the bacteria at concentrations equivalent to the MIC or, in some cases, at 2 × MIC.

Peptide structure is known to be sensitive to pH and salt changes, which can impact its biological activity. , We investigated the stability of lead peptides (RBWH11 and RBWH15) at 37 °C in PBS at pH 7.4 and pH 5 and did not observe any degradation after 24 h of incubation. Peptides usually have a short half-life due to enzymatic degradation in the bloodstream and the gastrointestinal tract. We tested the stability of RBWH11 and RBWH15 peptides in human plasma and estimated that ∼60–70% and ∼35–44% of the peptides were intact after 8 and 24 h of incubation, respectively (Figure S1). Overall, the results suggest that the selected new peptides have broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity against multiple Gram-negative and Gram-positive bacterial strains, show better potency than pexiganan, and have excellent pH and plasma stability.

Interaction with the Outer and Inner Membranes

To study the mechanism of action, the peptide interaction with bacterial membranes was explored. The membrane permeability was assessed using 1-N-phenylnaphthylamine (NPN). NPN, when bound to the phospholipid bilayer, becomes strongly fluorescent, and it is widely used to indicate the outer membrane permeabilization of bacteria. Similarly, the permeability was assessed by DNA-binding dyes propidium iodide (PI) and SYTO-9. SYTO-9 is a membrane-permeable dye that typically labels every bacterium and discriminates bacteria from the background. In contrast, PI is a membrane-impermeable dye that can only enter the cytoplasm when the membrane has been disrupted. Figure a illustrates the NPN fluorescence after incubating the peptides with the S. aureus (ATCC25923) strain, indicating that increasing concentrations of peptides promote the uptake of NPN across the membrane due to enhanced permeabilization. A similar trend was observed when the permeability of the membrane was determined using PI and SYTO-9 (Figure c). Figure c shows the percentage of PI-labeled S. aureus (ATCC25923) strain after incubation with pexiganan, RBWH00, RBWH11, RBWH00[1–13], and RBWH15 peptides at different concentrations relative to the SYTO-9 fluorescence. The results were in concordance with those obtained from the NPN assay, further supporting the conclusion that increasing peptide concentration promotes membrane permeabilization.

Since Gram-negative bacteria have an inner as well as an outer membrane, we utilized P. aeruginosa (PAO1) and determined the outer membrane permeabilization using NPN after incubating with the AMPs (pexiganan, RBWH10, RBWH11, RBWH14, and RBWH15). It was found that the peptides RBWH11 and RBWH15 showed the highest increase in uptake of NPN. The results from the lead peptides RBWH11 and RBWH15 were comparable to those obtained from pexiganan, and a significant increase in the NPN signal was observed as a function of concentration (Figure b). For inner membrane permeabilization, the percentage of the PI-labeled bacteria with respect to SYTO-9 was estimated. For the peptides RBWH11 and RBWH15 at 72 μM concentration, ∼60% of bacteria were labeled with PI after 1 h of incubation (Figure d). The results strongly correlated with the bacterial inhibition or killing activity, indicating that the peptides promote inner membrane permeabilization. Figure e and f shows representative confocal images of S. aureus (ATCC25923) and P. aeruginosa (PAO1) respectively, labeled with PI (red) and SYTO-9 (green) after incubation with various concentrations of peptide RBWH11. The increase in the PI signal can be clearly seen from the confocal images with increasing concentrations of peptides, indicating the disruption of the inner membrane (Figure e and f).

The membrane interaction between peptide RBWH11 and P. aeruginosa (PAO1) bacteria was visualized using confocal microscopy. The decrease in Nile red fluorescence intensity can be visualized over the period of incubation time, which suggests the disruption of membrane integrity. Additionally, the decrease in the SYTO-9 signal after ∼50 min suggests bacterial death after the interaction with the peptide RBWH11. To further explore membrane integrity, STED microscopy was utilized, providing higher resolution than confocal microscopy, with a lateral resolution of 20 nm and an axial resolution of 40 nm. The heterogeneous morphology of bacterial membranes is difficult to study at the molecular level, and STED can be used to identify any ultrastructural modifications. STED images of P. aeruginosa (PAO1) before and after incubation with the RBWH11 peptide (for 90 min at 2 × MIC) were studied. Figure c shows that the bacterial membrane surface experienced significant changes in morphology, which is also evident from the surface plot of the STED microscopy image (Figure d). Furthermore, to directly visualize the interaction of the peptide and membrane, peptide RBWH11 was labeled with fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC), and its colocalization with Nile red-labeled P. aeruginosa (PAO1) was observed using confocal microscopy (Figure e). The colocalization between the labeled peptide and Nile red signal was estimated using the Pearson Correlation Coefficient (PCC), and it was found to be around 0.7. PCC values closer to 1 indicate colocalization between two channels. Figure f shows the uptake of the peptide in bacteria (green), which was also clearly labeled with PI (red), confirming membrane rupturing and death of the bacteria.

Eradication of Preformed Biofilms

Biofilms are formed due to an accumulation of microbes that form a self-produced matrix of extracellular polymeric substances (EPSs). These are usually more resistant to environmental changes and drug treatments than planktonic bacteria. As biofilms account for more than 65% of human infections, antibiotics used to treat planktonic bacterial infections are commonly prescribed to combat bacterial biofilms. The recent development of AMPs has provided a therapeutic application against bacterial biofilms by preventing their formation or eradicating preformed biofilms. We examined the effectiveness of four top-performing peptides against the S. aureus (ATCC25923) and P. aeruginosa (PAO1) strains in eradicating biofilms and compared the results with pexiganan. To quantify the amount of biofilm, the crystal violet assay was used, and Figure a depicts the procedure used to estimate the percentage of eradication of biofilm.

a) Schematic representation of the procedure of the crystal violet assay for the assessment of eradication of bacterial biofilms. b) and c) Graphs representing the percentage of biofilm eradication relative to the control biofilm against Gram-positive S. aureus (ATCC25923) and Gram-negative P. aeruginosa (PAO1) strains, respectively.

Figure b shows the effect of peptides RBWH00, RBWH11, RBWH15, and RBWH00[1–13] at various concentrations after 90 min of incubation on the eradication of biofilm composed of S. aureus (ATCC25923) bacteria. At a 2.3 μM concentration, the peptides showed almost negligible biofilm eradication; however, at a 18 μM concentration, ∼50–60% of the biofilm was eradicated. Similarly, biofilm composed of P. aeruginosa (PAO1) strain showed ∼40–60% eradication at a 9 μM concentration after 24 h of incubation with peptides. The biofilms from Gram-negative strains are difficult to treat; hence, longer incubation with peptides was required. An increase in eradication efficiency was also observed as the concentration of the peptides increased. The peptides exhibited similar efficacy to pexiganan in the eradication of biofilm.

Resistance Evolution

The antimicrobial resistance development study was conducted to evaluate whether S. aureus (ATCC25923) and A. baumannii (AB5075) can develop resistance against peptides RBWH11 and RBWH15, in comparison to a conventional antibiotic (tetracycline). After serial passaging, strains were treated with peptides RBWH11 and RBWH15 at sub-MIC values (Figure a). We discovered that MIC values of peptides against the bacterium remained constant even after approximately 300 generations over a period of 21 days (Figure b, c, e, f). On the contrary, when tetracycline was tested, the MIC values were 4-fold higher than the initial MIC after only 40–50 generations. After 300 generations, the MIC increased 16-fold againstS. aureus­(ATCC25923) (Figure d) and 32-fold against A. baumannii (AB5075) (Figure g). The initial MIC values of tetracycline were 0.16 and 2.56 μg/mL against S. aureus (ATCC25923) and A. baumannii (AB5075), respectively. Overall, these results suggest that, unlike conventional antibiotics, the synthetic peptides did not develop resistance over a much longer period, even in the case of a superbug, which is already resistant to various classes of antibiotics.

a) Scheme of the experimental design used to study resistance evolution. Resistance development of S. aureus (ATCC25923) (b–d) and A. baumannii (AB5075) (e–g) was assessed in the presence of sub-MIC concentrations of peptides RBWH15 and RBWH11, and the antibiotic tetracycline. The initial MIC values were determined from two independent experiments performed in triplicate.

Biocompatibility and Wound Healing Assay

For assessing the biocompatibility of the peptides toward mammalian cells prior to the wound healing assay, their cytotoxicity was evaluated against L929 fibroblasts, human embryonic kidney cells (HEK293), red blood cells (RBCs), and THP-1-derived macrophages. The therapeutic index was calculated between various cell lines and S. aureus and A. baumannii and is defined as the ratio of IC50 concentrations to MIC values.

First, a hemolysis assay against RBCs from rodent blood was performed after incubation with peptides RBWH11 and RBWH15 at various concentrations. It was found that the lead peptides RBWH11 and RBWH15 did not induce significant hemolysis after 1–3 h of incubation, even at high doses of 30–60 μM (Figure S2a) and their therapeutic index was found to be between 20 and 40. The hemolysis assay was also performed on human blood, and the therapeutic index was found to be ∼20 with both lead peptide sequences (Figure S2b). A high therapeutic index is required so that no hemolysis of the host cells occurs during treatment with the peptide. , In addition, since AMPs often cause nephrotoxicity at therapeutic concentrations, we examined the effect of peptides RBWH11, RBWH15, and pexiganan on the HEK293 cell line. The peptides were incubated with cells at MIC, 5 × MIC, and 10 × MIC. Figure a illustrates the representative confocal images of HEK293 cells after incubation with the peptides, suggesting that pexiganan is toxic even at MIC, whereas RBWH11 and RBWH15 peptides did not induce any cell death at MIC values, and their therapeutic index was ∼10 (Figure c). Subsequently, we investigated the cytotoxicity of the peptide RBWH11 and pexiganan, as a control, against human THP-1-derived macrophages (Figure S3). The IC50, where 50% of the cells were viable after incubation with RBWH11, was ∼50 μM as compared to ∼6.25 μM for pexiganan, suggesting that RBWH11 is more biocompatible toward THP-1 cells. Our data are comparable to a previous study that demonstrated that pexiganan can be toxic to THP-1 cells at doses as low as 1.5 μM.

Moreover, to test the toxicology of the peptide in vivo, we performed an intravenous (IV) injection with RBWH15 at a concentration of 0.3 mg/kg in mice (n = 3), with PBS (n = 3) as the control. The mice were weighed for body weight reduction after 24 h. The body weight reduction with peptide injection was 2.97% and was found to be non-significant when compared to the control group (3.07%) as shown in Figure S4. These results suggest that the lead peptides have a favorable drug safety profile.

AMPs can not only inhibit microbial growth but also exhibit a wide range of other biological activities, including anticancer, antiviral, wound healing, and anti-inflammatory activities. In this study, we aimed to determine the wound healing efficacy of the designed AMPs using L929 fibroblasts. Wound healing antimicrobial peptides (WHAMPs) contain motifs that signal cells to begin migrating or proliferating, ultimately facilitating wound repair and closure.

After the incubation of peptides at various concentrations with L929 cells for 48 h, it was found that most peptides were biocompatible and exhibited low toxicity (Figure S5). The lead peptide candidates, RBWH11, RBWH15, and RBWH00[1–13], displayed IC50 in the range of 25–50 μM (Figure S5), which was significantly above their antimicrobial activity. The therapeutic index of the peptides, which is the ratio of IC50 and MIC against S. aureus (ATCC25923) and A. baumannii (AB5075) was found to be in the range of ∼17–34 μM. The wound healing efficiency of the peptides was determined using a scratch assay that resembles an artificially created wound and allows for monitoring of cell migration and wound closure as a function of time. The scratch was created on confluent L929 cell sheets using a pipette tip, and then the cells were washed to remove any cell debris (Figure a). The cells were then incubated with the respective peptides, and the scratched area was imaged and analyzed at time 0, and after 24 and 48 h of incubation. Figure b shows representative microscopy images of the scratch area after incubation with RBWH00, RBWH00[1–13], RBWH15, and pexiganan peptides. The number of cells was counted in the scratched area using ImageJ and compared with the control (without peptide treatment). As shown in Figure c, peptides RBWH00 and RBWH00[1–13] displayed increased cell density in the microscopy images, indicating complete scratch area closure after 48 h. Additionally, for peptide RBWH00[1–13], when the scratch was observed at different peptide concentrations (biocompatible concentrations), an increase in cell density was observed (Figure d and e) after 24 h. After 48 h, 100% of the scratch was covered with cells at peptide concentrations of 5 and 10 μM (Figure d). The scratch test was also performed on other designed peptides. The representative confocal images of L929 cells after incubation for 24 and 48 h with various peptides (at 2 × MIC concentration against S. aureus) are shown in Figure S6. The quantitative analysis of the images (Figure S7) suggested that all designed peptides have a higher number of cells in the scratched area relative to the control after 48 h. Overall, these results suggest that the engineered peptides markedly promoted fibroblast migration, leading to accelerated artificial wound closure.

Conclusion

The present findings suggest that a new antimicrobial peptide can be engineered by combining machine learning tools with manual approaches, such as alanine scans and alignment to motif assembly. The motif identification from the alignment of computationally generated AMPs to the known AMP databases offers a platform technology to improve the activity of peptides. We found two lead peptides, RBWH11 and RBWH15, that exhibited higher antimicrobial activity against all ESKAPE pathogens when compared to similar AMP sequences (AP00069 and AP01547) and pexiganan. These peptides were active against MDR strains and did not lead to the development of resistance even after up to 21 days (∼300 generations). The mechanism of action of the peptides, as determined by using fluorescence-based assays and microscopy techniques, showed a distortion of the bacterial lipid membrane, which led to bacterial death. Moreover, the peptides also showed potential in eradicating bacterial biofilms. All newly designed peptide sequences were biocompatible and enhanced the migration of fibroblast cells in an in vitro scratch test, signifying the wound healing capability of the peptides.

Experimental Details

Materials

N,N’-Dimethylformamide (DMF) was purchased from VWR Chemicals; acetic acid (AcOH), dichloromethane (DCM), formic acid (FA), isopropanol (IPA), ethanol (EtOH), and HPLC-grade acetonitrile (ACN) were purchased from Merck; diethyl ether (Et2O) was purchased from RCI Labscan; N,N’-diisopropylcarbodiimide (DIC), human plasma (P9523), fluorescein isothiocyanate isomer I (FITC), piperidine, and trifluoroacetic acid (TFA) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich; RinkAmide AM resin (0.574 mmol/g) was purchased from ChemImpex; and Oxyma Pure, Fmoc-protected amino acids/building blocks were purchased from Aapptec, Mimotopes, or ChemImpex. Bacterial pathogensEnterococcus faecalis ATCC19433, Staphylococcus aureus ATCC25923, Klebsiella pneumoniae ATCC43816, and Enterobacter aerogenes ATCC13048were purchased from ATCC. Nutrient agar (OXOID, UK) and Mueller Hinton II Broth (MHBII, Cation-Adjusted, BBLTM BD, France) were used for bacterial cultures in this study. Minimum Essential Medium (MEM), nonessential amino acids (NEAAs), and fetal bovine serum (FBS) were purchased from Life Technologies, USA.

Peptide Synthesis

All peptides were synthesized using solid-phase peptide synthesis (SPPS) Fmoc/tBu strategy with microwave synthesis conditions on a CEM Liberty Blue system (the instrument uses stock solutions of all reagents) on a 0.1 mmol scale, using a 5-fold excess of Fmoc-AA–OH (0.2 M in DMF), 0.5 M DIC in DMF, and 1.0 M OxymaPure in DMF. Deprotection was performed with 20% piperidine and 0.1 M OxymaPure in DMF. All amino acid cycles used a single 1 min/90 °C deprotection step and a single 2 min/90 °C coupling. For the final deprotection and cleavage from the resin, the peptide resin was washed with DMF (3 × 1 min), DCM (3 × 1 min), and Et2O (3 × 1 min), and then dried in a desiccator under vacuum. The cleavage cocktail was composed of TFA/H2O/TIS (95:2.5:2.5). The peptidyl resin was treated with 10 mL of the cleavage solution and agitated on an orbital shaker at 100 rpm for 1.5 h. The resin was then filtered off, and the peptides were precipitated with cold Et2O and centrifuged. The crude peptide was then washed twice with cold Et2O and dried in a desiccator under vacuum for 30 min. Finally, the crude product was dissolved in 10% acetic acid and lyophilized before further analysis and purification.

Analytical HPLC, Preparative HPLC, and LC-MS

Peptides (1 mg/mL) were analyzed on an analytical HPLC (with an injection volume of 10–20 μL) and purified on a preparative HPLC (with a 5 mL injection volume containing ∼30 mg of peptide). The Agilent Technologies 1260 Infinity or 1200 Series analytical HPLC was run at a flow rate of 1 mL/min, and the wavelength of the detector was set at 215/254 nm; the column temperature was set to 30 °C. The column used was Phenomenex Luna 5 μm C18(2) 100 Å; 100 × 4.6 mm (PN: 00D-4252-E0; SN: 679499–7; BN: 5291–122). Agilent Technologies 1260 Infinity was used for the purification of crude peptide and was run at a flow rate of 12 mL/min, with the detector wavelength set at 215/254 nm . The column used was a Phenomenex Luna 10 μm C18(2) 100 Å; 150 × 21.20 mm (PN 00F-4253-P0-ax; SN: 554707–1). The HPLC mobile phase was [A] 0.1% TFA in MQ water; [B] 0.1% TFA in ACN.

Peptide samples were also analyzed using a Dionex 3000 HPLC system coupled to a Thermo Scientific Q Exactive mass spectrometer fitted with a HESI-II ion source. Analyses were performed with a reversed-phase HPLC column (ACE Excel 2 C18, 100 × 2.1 mm, 2 μm particle size), using an injection volume of 10 μL, a flow rate of 0.3 mL/min, and a gradient (2% to 100% methanol in 15 min, then held at 100% methanol for a further 5 min). Both mobile phases contained 0.1% formic acid, with the column temperature of 40 °C. Positive ion electrospray mass spectra were recorded in an appropriate mass range at 140,000 mass resolution. The nitrogen nebulizing/desolvation gas used for vaporization was heated to 350 °C in these experiments. The sheath gas flow rate was set to 25, and the auxiliary gas flow rate was set to 10 (both arbitrary units). The spray voltage was 3.5 kV, and the capillary temperature was 300 °C. Some of the mass spectrometry analysis was also performed by MALDI-ToF and Shimadzu 2020 with mobile phase: [A] 0.1% formic acid (FA) in MQ water; [B] 0.1% FA in acetonitrile at a flow rate of 0.4 mL/min using a Phenomenex Luna 3 μm C18(2) 100 Å; 50 × 3 mm (PN: 00B-4251-Y0; SN: 750309–8; BN: 5292–069) column, with the temperature set to 30 °C.

The purity of all peptides was determined by analytical HPLC (details specified above) using a gradient method of 25 to 60% solvent [B] over 15 min, and detection at 215 nm to assess the presence of impurities. The purity of the majority of peptides was determined to be ≥95% (the purity of four peptides ranged from 90 to 94%).

Minimal Inhibitory Concentration (MIC) Assay

The minimum inhibitory concentration is the lowest concentration of a drug that inhibits the visible growth of bacteria and can be determined by reading absorption at 600 nm (optical density at 600 nm, OD@600 nm) using a spectrophotometer. The peptides were subjected to antibacterial testing against Gram-positive and Gram-negative pathogens. All the ESKAPE pathogens (E. faecalis (ATCC19433), S. aureus (ATCC25923), K. pneumoniae (ATCC43816), A. baumannii (AB5075), P. aeruginosa (PAO1), and E. aerogenes (ATCC13048)) were inoculated from nutrient agar plates to obtain a bacterial suspension by incubating the colony of bacteria in a flask containing 10 mL of Mueller Hinton Broth II (MHB) at 37 °C for 24 h with gentle shaking (75 rpm). The bacterial suspensions were then diluted in MHB to 1 × 106 colony-forming units (CFUs) per mL. Next, using serial dilutions in a 96-well plate, different concentrations of each peptide from 400 to 1.5 μM were prepared. 100 μL of peptide solutions at final concentrations within the range of 200–0.375 μM were then added to a 96-well plate containing 100 μL of diluted bacterial suspension. After 18–24 h of incubation at 37 °C, the turbidity of the samples was observed, and the optical density of bacteria was measured by UV–vis at 600 nm. All assays were performed in triplicate.

Minimum Bactericidal Concentration (MBC) Assay

The minimum bactericidal concentration is the lowest concentration of a drug that results in the killing of 99.9% of bacteria or the lowest concentration of peptide at which no viable bacteria can be detected, following the plating of suspensions on agar plates. For MBC determination, 100 μL of suspension was added to an agar plate, starting from the MIC to at least two to four higher concentrations where no visible bacterial growth was present, and was spread uniformly over the plate using a sterile L-shaped loop. The agar plates were then incubated at 37 °C for 24–48 h and monitored for any bacterial colonies.

Antibiofilm Assay

Biofilm assay was performed in a 96-well plate, where 100 μL of pathogens was added to each well and incubated overnight at 37 °C. The planktonic bacteria were removed, and the biofilm formation was confirmed by crystal violet staining under microscopy. After removal of the media, the biofilm was washed twice with PBS and incubated with peptides at various concentrations in triplicate. MHB was used as a control. After 1.5 h (S. aureus ATCC25923) and 24 h (P. aeruginosa PAO1), peptide solutions were carefully removed, and the biofilm was again washed with PBS. The biofilm was then stained using 100 μL of crystal violet solution (1 v/v % in 80% ethanol solution) for 1 h. After staining, the crystal violet solution was removed, and the plate was washed, followed by dissolution of crystal violet samples in 100 μL of acetic acid (30 v/v % in 100% ethanol). The percentage of biofilm relative to the control was confirmed by measuring absorption at 540 nm. Reduction in the absorbance indicated the efficiency of the peptides to eradicate preformed biofilms.

N-Phenylnaphthylamine (NPN)-Based Membrane Permeability Assay

NPN, when in contact with a hydrophobic phospholipid bilayer, can exhibit fluorescence to determine the membrane permeability of peptides at different concentrations. The bacterial suspension at OD@600 nm = 0.5 was resuspended in HEPES buffer (1 mM, pH 7.4). The bacterial suspension (100 μL) was added to the 96-well plate, to which different peptide solutions (100 μL) at various concentrations were added and incubated for 10 min, and the NPN signal was monitored. The percentage NPN signal increase after the addition of peptide, with respect to the control (where no peptide was added), was calculated.

Inner Membrane Permeability Assay

In addition to NPN, membrane permeability was also monitored using the membrane impermeable dye PI and SYTO-9 dye (Live/Dead assay kit, BacLight Bacterial Viability Kit, Invitrogen), which can bind to the nucleic acids in all cells. By calculating the PI-stained cells, the inner membrane permeability can be estimated for Gram-negative bacteria, and the membrane permeability can be estimated for the Gram-positive strain. Since the inner membrane is impermeable to PI, it has been used to differentiate the percentage of bacteria with an impaired or permeable membrane. The bacterial suspension (100 μL) was added to a 96-well plate, to which different peptide solutions (100 μL) at various concentrations were added, incubated for 60 min, imaged using a Nikon C1 (inverted) confocal microscope, and processed using Fiji-ImageJ software for further quantification analysis. In addition, fluorescence was recorded using a fluorescence microplate reader (PHERAstar, BMG LABTECH) at excitation/emission wavelengths of 485 nm/520 nm for green fluorescence and 530 nm/620 nm for red fluorescence.

Resistance Study

The resistance evolution was performed in accordance with a previously reported protocol by Gullberg et al. Bacterial suspensions of S. aureus (ATCC25923) and A. baumannii (AB5075) were obtained by inoculating nutrient agar plates with MHB II. The bacterial suspensions were then diluted in MHB II to 1 × 106 CFU/mL. Next, the bacteria were treated with peptides (RBWH11 and RBWH15) and the antibiotic (tetracycline), and the MIC was determined after 24 h at 37 °C. The bacterial suspension at sub-MIC concentration was obtained, further diluted to 1 × 106 CFU/mL, and again treated with the peptides and antibiotics. The procedure was repeated, and serial passaging was performed every 24 h for up to 21 days (∼300 generations) in the presence of peptides and antibiotics. MHB II that was not treated with peptides or antibiotics was used as a control.

Cell Viability and Toxicity

L929 mouse fibroblast cells were used for the peptide cytotoxicity measurements. Cells were seeded in a 96-well plate at a seeding density of 7000 per well in cell culture media (MEM + 5% NEAA + 10% FBS) and incubated for 24 h at 37 °C, 5% CO2 atmosphere. The cells were washed and treated with various peptides at different concentrations for 24 and 48 h in cell culture media. The cell viability was assessed using MTS solution (3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-5-(3-carboxymethoxyphenyl)-2-(4-sulfophenyl)-2H-tetrazolium, Promega) after 24 and 48 h. After 24 and 48 h, the media was replaced with 100 μL of MTS solution and incubated further for 2–4 h at 37 °C in an incubator. After 2–4 h, the absorbance was recorded at 490 nm using a plate reader, and cell viability was estimated relative to the control (cells not treated with peptides).

THP-1 cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 + 10% FBS, 2 mM l-glutamine (GlutaMAX), and 100 U/mL penicillin/streptomycin (Pen/Strep) at 37 °C, 10% CO2 atmosphere. The cells were seeded in a 96-well plate at a seeding density of 5 × 105 cells/mL, with 200 μL of cells per well. Cells were differentiated into adherent macrophage-like cells by the addition of phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA, 5 ng/mL) for 8 h. Then, the media were replaced with fresh media, and the cells were incubated for 48 h at 37 °C, 10% CO2. After this time, the cells were washed and further incubated with peptides at different concentrations for 6 h in cell culture media. After the 6 h time point, 10 × lysis buffer was added to untreated cells for 15 min. The cells were then centrifuged at 400 g for 4 min, and the supernatant was collected to measure cytotoxicity using an LDH detection assay (CyQUANT LDH Cytotoxicity Assay Kit, Invitrogen-C20300 and C20301) as per the manufacturer’s instructions. The absorbance was then measured using a ClarioSTAR microplate reader at 490 and 680 nm to estimate LDH release relative to untreated and lysed controls.

Human embryonic kidney cells (HEK293) were seeded in a 96-well plate at a seeding density of 7,000 per well in DMEM + 10% FBS, 100 U/mL Pen/Strep, and incubated for 24 h at 37 °C, 10% CO2 atmosphere. The cells were washed and treated with various peptides at different concentrations for 24 h in cell culture media. The cells were then stained with the LIVE/DEAD Viability/Cytotoxicity Kit for mammalian cells (Invitrogen, L3224) as per the manufacturer’s protocol and imaged using a Nikon C1 (inverted) confocal microscope (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan).

For in vivo toxicity, two groups of healthy mice (n = 3) were treated with 0.3 mg/kg of RBWH15 and PBS via intravenous injection. They were monitored for survival and weighed after 24 h. The animal study was approved by the Alfred Research Alliance Animal Ethics Committee (AEC approval number E/8230/2027). Animals were sourced from the Precinct Animal Center, Monash Animal Research Platform, and Melbourne Bioresources Platform.

Wound Healing Scratch Assay

L929 cells were plated in a 24-well plate for the scratch assay. The seeding density of cells was 35,000 cells per well. The volume of media added was 500 μL/well (MEM + 5% NEAA + 10% FBS), and cells were seeded overnight. The media was replaced with 400 μL MEM without FBS and NEAA after 24 h. The scratch assay was performed when wells reached >90% confluency. The scratch was created manually using a 20 μL pipette tip. The cells were washed to remove cell debris and were treated with fresh media containing peptides below their IC50. Cells were then imaged using a confocal microscope before and after 24 and 48 h of peptide incubation. The number of cells was counted using Fiji ImageJ software.

Plasma Stability

The peptides RBWH11 and RBWH15 were dissolved at a 1 mg/mL concentration in water and diluted in human plasma to demonstrate their stability in plasma. 250 μL peptide solution was added into 250 μL of plasma, and the solutions were incubated at 37 °C in a ThermoMixC at 300 rpm for 30 min and 1, 2, 3, 5, 7, and 24 h. After each time interval, a 40 μL aliquot was taken, and serum proteins were precipitated after adding trichloroacetic acid (6% w/v). The samples were stored at 4 °C for 15 min and centrifuged for 4 min at 13,000 g to separate the precipitated proteins, and the supernatant was then analyzed using HPLC to estimate the amount of peptide.

Hemolysis Assay

Fresh blood from C57/Bl6 mice was washed in PBS three times with centrifugation (400 g, 10 min) and dry pelleted. The red blood cells (RBCs) were counted with the CELL-DYN Emerald hematology analyzer and diluted to give a final concentration of 4.5 × 108/mL and then aliquoted in a 96-well round-bottom plate (180 μL per well), to which 20 μL of test peptide in PBS was added. The 0.1% Triton X-100 lysis buffer was used for 100% hemolysis control. The plates were incubated in a humidified atmosphere containing 10% CO2 at 37 °C for 1–3 h, centrifuged at 400 g for 10 min, and the supernatant was then transferred to 96-well flat-bottom plates. The concentration of hemoglobin was determined by measuring absorbance at 405 nm using the ClarioSTAR microplate reader, and the data were presented as a % relative to the lysis buffer control. The murine blood used in the hemolysis assay was scavenged from excess blood collected under ethics approved by the MARP Ethics Committee at Monash University, Australia.

Similarly, the hemolysis assay was performed using human blood. Blood was taken from a healthy donor under ethics approved by the University of Melbourne Human Research and Ethics Committee (approval 2025–33501–71356–3), and all associated procedures were carried out in accordance with the approved guidelines. All participants provided written informed consent. Whole blood was collected with a sodium heparin anticoagulant. The assay protocol was the same as above.
