Self-disgust and its associated factors among patients with malignant lymphoma: a latent profile analysis
Authors: Fang Liao, Ya-ling Chen, Shan-shan Zhang, Yu-hang Song, Zhi-ling Chen, Hao Yao, Hai Yi, Dan Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo evaluate the severity and independent predictors of self-disgust in patients with malignant lymphoma, and to develop and internally validate a clinical nomogram for personalized risk prediction.MethodsThis cross-sectional study included 200 patients with malignant lymphoma from two tertiary hospitals in Chengdu, China (January–July 2025). Self-disgust was assessed using the Questionnaire for the Assessment of Self-Disgust (QASD). Predictors were identified through a combination of LASSO regression and multivariable logistic regression analysis. A predictive nomogram was subsequently constructed and internally validated using bootstrap resampling (1,000 iterations). Model performance was evaluated based on discrimination (AUC), calibration plots, and decision curve analysis (DCA).ResultsThe median QASD score was 30.0 (interquartile range: 24.0–40.0). Multivariable logistic regression identified five independent predictors of the high self-disgust profile. Risk factors included worker status (OR = 4.16, 95% CI: 1.51–11.93, P = 0.006), greater fear of the future (OR = 1.98, 95% CI: 1.29–3.14, P = 0.002), advanced Ann Arbor stage (OR = 1.56, 95% CI: 1.17–2.11, P = 0.003), and negative coping style (OR = 1.16, 95% CI: 1.07–1.27, P < 0.001). Conversely, psychological resilience served as a significant protective factor (OR = 0.91, 95% CI: 0.87–0.96, P = 0.001). The final predictive model demonstrated satisfactory discrimination (AUC = 0.808) and explanatory power (Nagelkerke R2 = 0.341). The Hosmer-Lemeshow test (P = 0.645) and calibration curves (mean absolute error = 0.013) indicated high agreement between predicted and observed probabilities. Furthermore, decision curve analysis (DCA) confirmed the clinical utility of the nomogram.ConclusionSelf-disgust is a prevalent psychosocial burden in lymphoma survivors, primarily associated with existential fear, occupational role disruption, and maladaptive coping. The developed exploratory nomogram provides oncology nurses with a reliable, evidence-based tool for early risk stratification, facilitating targeted, proactive psychosocial interventions to preserve patient dignity.Keywords: Malignant lymphoma, Self-disgust, Nomogram, Prediction model, Oncology nursing, Psychosocial distress

Introduction

Malignant lymphoma poses a significant global public health challenge, with its incidence and disease burden rising rapidly in recent years [1, 2]. As advances in targeted and immunotherapies have improved survival rates, clinical priorities in oncology have increasingly shifted beyond mere survival to focus on the long-term psychosocial well-being of cancer survivors. However, navigating the complex treatment trajectory remains psychologically taxing for patients.

During the prolonged treatment process, patients face a myriad of specific stressors. Unlike cancers requiring disfiguring surgery (e.g., breast cancer), where psychological distress is often intertwined with localized bodily mutilation and altered sexual identity, the psychological burden in lymphoma operates through a different mechanism. The distress predominantly stems from a systemic, “invisible” toll such as pervasive fatigue, functional decline, and the loss of physical autonomy. This fundamental collapse of vitality frequently forces the abrupt suspension of occupational and social roles. These invisible traumas often exacerbate internalized negative emotions, ultimately precipitating self-disgust [3]. Defined as a maladaptive emotional response characterized by intense negative self-evaluation and dissatisfaction with one’s physical or psychological state, self-disgust is prevalent yet frequently overlooked in routine oncology nursing. While stigma primarily focuses on the internalization of external societal devaluation, self-disgust captures a more visceral, internal emotional rejection of one’s own deteriorating state. Consequently, self-disgust provides a deeper, emotion-focused target for psycho-oncology interventions. Persistent self-disgust acts as a potent psychological stressor, closely associated with heightened anxiety, depressive symptoms, and even suicidal ideation [4], leading to social withdrawal, poor treatment adherence, and deteriorated health outcomes [5].

Despite its severe clinical implications, current psycho-oncology research usually evaluates self-disgust as a homogeneous, continuous variable based on total scale scores. This traditional approach assumes that the psychological experience of self-disgust is uniform, fundamentally ignoring the substantial clinical and psychosocial heterogeneity among patients. To address this, latent profile analysis (LPA) is required. Operating under a probabilistic framework, LPA objectively categorizes individuals into unobserved, distinct subgroups (profiles) based on their symptom presentation patterns. By accounting for variable uncertainty, LPA provides a more accurate and data-driven classification of psychological burdens [6].

Translating this nuanced categorization into routine nursing practice requires practical bedside tools to screen for patient susceptibility to high-risk profiles. Therefore, this cross-sectional study aims to identify the latent profiles of self-disgust among patients with malignant lymphoma using LPA, and to explore the multidimensional associative factors of profile membership. Based on these independent predictors, we further constructed and internally validated an exploratory clinical screening nomogram. This preliminary risk assessment tool aims to empower oncology nurses in facilitating the early identification of high-risk individuals and delivering precisely targeted psychosocial interventions.

Methods

Study design, population, and setting

This cross-sectional study utilized a convenience sampling method to recruit patients with malignant lymphoma from two tertiary hospitals in Chengdu, China, between January and July 2025. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) a confirmed diagnosis of malignant lymphoma according to the Chinese Guidelines for Diagnosis and Treatment of Malignant Lymphoma (2023 edition) [7]; (2) aged 18 years or older; (3) currently in a stable treatment phase; and (4) provision of voluntary written informed consent. Patients were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: (1) presence of other primary malignancies; (2) pre-existing severe cognitive or psychiatric disorders that precluded their ability to understand and complete the questionnaires; or (3) concurrent participation in other clinical trials or psychological interventions.

Sample size estimation

The sample size for this study was determined based on the requirements for both LPA and the subsequent development of an exploratory risk assessment model. For the LPA, simulation studies suggest that a sample size of approximately 200 is generally sufficient to accurately identify latent classes, provided that the indicator variables exhibit robust reliability [8].

Regarding the multivariable analysis, although a participants-per-variable (PPV) ratio of 5 to 10 is widely considered acceptable, it is often considered a minimum threshold for stable prediction modeling. To mitigate the risk of overfitting and handle the 27 potential candidate variables within our cohort, we employed LASSO (Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator) regression as a rigorous dimensionality reduction technique [9]. This approach allowed us to concentrate on the most significant predictors, ensuring a more parsimonious model. While we acknowledge that N = 200 may limit the external generalizability of a stable prediction tool, it provides an acceptable foundation for this preliminary and exploratory study aimed at identifying key associative factors and generating a prototype screening nomogram.

Variables and measures

Sociodemographic and clinical questionnaire

A study-specific questionnaire was developed by the research team based on a comprehensive literature review. To ensure content validity and clinical relevance, the preliminary draft was refined through expert panel discussions and a pilot survey, confirming all items were unambiguous. The final instrument captured data across three primary domains:

Sociodemographic and economic characteristics: This section collected baseline demographic data, including age, sex, marital status, educational level, occupational status, and place of residence. Specifically, occupational status was categorized based on the primary nature of employment and social security characteristics in China: “Farmer” refers to agricultural workers primarily tied to rural land; “Worker” encompasses blue-collar manual laborers in manufacturing, construction, or service sectors (importantly, this category includes rural-to-urban migrant workers) who typically engage in physically demanding tasks with less job security and limited access to urban social welfare; “Clerk” refers to white-collar employees, including civil servants and office staff, who generally benefit from structured sick leave policies and stable income; and “Other” includes unemployed, freelance, or retired individuals. Furthermore, economic and family indicators were assessed, encompassing family relationship quality, monthly household income, primary source of income, health insurance status, and perceived financial capacity to afford treatment.

The Questionnaire for the Assessment of Self-Disgust (QASD)

Self-disgust was evaluated using the QASD, originally developed by Schienle et al [10] and culturally adapted and validated in Chinese populations by Jin et al. [11]. This 14-item instrument encompasses two distinct dimensions: personal disgust (9 items, assessing disgust directed toward one’s own physical appearance and personality) and behavioral disgust (5 items, assessing disgust directed toward one’s actions). Responses are rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (“not at all”) to 4 (“completely agree”). The total score ranges from 0 to 56, with higher scores reflecting a greater severity of self-disgust. In the current study, the QASD demonstrated satisfactory internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.944).

The 10-item Connor-Davidson Resilience Scale (CD-RISC-10)

Psychological resilience was measured using the validated Chinese version of the unidimensional CD-RISC-10 [12]. Participants rate each of the 10 items on a 5-point Likert scale from 0 (“never”) to 4 (“almost always”). The total score ranges from 0 to 40, where higher scores indicate a stronger capacity to cope with adversity and greater psychological resilience. The scale exhibited strong reliability in our sample (Cronbach’s α = 0.877).

The Social Support Rating Scale (SSRS)

Multidimensional social support was assessed using the SSRS, a widely utilized instrument developed and validated for Chinese populations by Xiao [13]. The scale consists of 10 items categorized into three domains: objective support, subjective support, and the utilization of support. Items 1–4 and 8–10 are rated on a 4-point Likert scale; item 5 is scored from 1 (“no support”) to 4 (“full support”); and items 6 and 7 are scored based on the number of available support sources (1 point per identified source, or 0 if “none”). The scale showed good internal consistency in this study (Cronbach’s α = 0.761).

The Simplified Coping Style Questionnaire (SCSQ)

Coping strategies were evaluated using the SCSQ, an instrument tailored to Chinese cultural contexts by Xie [14]. It comprises 20 items divided into two independent subscales: positive coping (12 items) and negative coping (8 items). Each item is rated on a 4-point Likert scale (scored 0 to 3). Crucially, due to the bidirectional nature of the subscales, scores for positive and negative coping were calculated separately rather than as a composite total. Higher scores in the respective dimensions indicate a stronger tendency to adopt that specific coping style. The scale demonstrated satisfactory reliability in this cohort (Cronbach’s α : positive coping = 0.86, negative coping = 0.81). In this study, both positive and negative coping subscale scores were treated as continuous variables in subsequent analyses.

Data collection

Data were collected using Wenjuanxing, a widely recognized online survey platform in China. To minimize information bias and ensure data consistency, all data collectors underwent rigorous standardized training regarding scale interpretation, communication protocols, and ethical guidelines prior to study commencement.

Data collection was conducted in clinical settings through face-to-face interactions. Upon approaching eligible patients, researchers provided a brief explanation of the study’s purpose, guaranteed anonymity, and obtained written informed consent. Patients were then guided to access the electronic questionnaire via a QR code using their smartphones. Researchers remained on-site to provide non-directive assistance and clarify any immediate queries. To ensure data integrity, the electronic survey system was configured to mandate responses for all items, thereby effectively eliminating missing data. Furthermore, the system restricted submissions to a single entry per IP address and registered device to prevent duplicate responses. Following data export, strict quality control protocols were applied. Submissions were to be excluded if they met either of the following criteria: (1) an unreasonably short completion time (i.e., < 3 min), or (2) exhibition of obvious straight-lining or repetitive response patterns. Ultimately, a total of 200 completed questionnaires were collected. Upon rigorous quality review, all 200 submissions met the aforementioned criteria and were deemed valid, resulting in a final sample of 200 for subsequent statistical analysis.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R software version 4.5.2 and Mplus version 8.3. Two-tailed P-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Descriptive and univariate analyses

The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Given the non-normal distribution of the data, continuous variables were summarized as medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs), and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages. Bivariate associations were analyzed using Spearman’s correlation for continuous variables. Differences in clinical and psychosocial characteristics across latent profiles were examined using the Mann–Whitney U test, Kruskal–Wallis H test, Chi-square test, or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate.

Latent Profile Analysis (LPA)

To identify unobserved subgroups based on self-disgust patterns, LPA was conducted using Mplus 8.3. Item scores of the self-disgust scale served as indicator variables. Models with 1 to 5 classes were systematically evaluated. The optimal model was determined using fit indices including the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), and sample-size-adjusted BIC (aBIC), with lower values indicating superior fit. The Lo–Mendell–Rubin likelihood ratio test (LMR-LRT) and bootstrapped likelihood ratio test (BLRT) were utilized to compare model improvements (k vs. k-1 classes). Classification accuracy was assessed via Entropy, with values closer to 1.0 indicating higher precision.

Feature selection and nomogram construction

Model development and validation strictly followed the TRIPOD guidelines [15].

To mitigate multicollinearity and identify the most critical predictors of the high-risk self-disgust profile (derived from LPA), a two-stage modeling approach was employed. First, to ensure a comprehensive evaluation of potential confounding factors, all candidate variables were incorporated into a LASSO penalized regression model. Prior to modeling, categorical variables were transformed using dummy coding. A 10-fold cross-validation was conducted to determine the optimal penalty parameter (λ). To ensure model parsimony and effectively prevent overfitting in high-dimensional data, the one standard error criterion (λ1se) was adopted rather than the minimum binomial deviance (λmin). This stricter threshold was applied to extract the most robust set of features with non-zero coefficients for subsequent analysis.

Subsequently, the robust predictors identified by LASSO were entered into a multivariable logistic regression model to ascertain their independent associations with profile membership. To facilitate clinical utility, this final model was visualized as a bedside clinical nomogram. The predictive performance of the nomogram was evaluated across three dimensions: (1) Discrimination was quantified using the Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC); (2) Calibration was assessed via calibration plots with bootstrap resampling (1,000 iterations); and (3) Clinical utility was evaluated using Decision Curve Analysis (DCA).

Ethical considerations

This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the General Hospital of Western Theater Command (Approval No. 2026EC03-ky013). Prior to data collection, all participants were briefed on the study’s purpose, procedures, and their right to voluntary withdrawal.

Reporting guidelines

This study was conducted and reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) Statement for cross-sectional studies.

Results

Sociodemographic and clinical characteristics of participants

The study cohort comprised 200 patients with malignant lymphoma. The median age of the participants was 52.0 years (interquartile range [IQR]: 36.0–62.0). The majority of patients were male (55.5%) and married (74.5%). The most common occupational status was farmer (40.0%) or classified as “other” (40.5%). Educational attainment was generally modest, with 49.0% of patients having completed junior high school or less.

Psychosocially, although a substantial proportion of patients described their family dynamics as harmonious or very harmonious (83.5%), a majority (59.5%) reported experiencing difficult or unaffordable financial burdens related to their medical costs. Furthermore, concerns about becoming a burden to their families (74.5%) and fear of the future (68.5%) were highly prevalent. Despite these psychological stressors, 67.5% of the cohort felt they received full social support from family and friends.

Regarding clinical characteristics, patients were distributed across all Ann Arbor stages: Stage I (31.0%), Stage II (17.0%), Stage III (23.0%), and Stage IV (29.0%). The vast majority of patients (87.5%) had received chemotherapy. Common treatment-related adverse events included alopecia (68.5%) and oral mucositis (61.0%). Additionally, more than half of the patients (53.5%) indicated having only a partial understanding of their illness.

Self-disgust status and latent profile analysis

The median total score of the QASD was 30.0 (interquartile range [IQR]: 24.0–40.0). Given the lack of a standardized clinical cut-off for the QASD, we subsequently employed a data-driven LPA to objectively stratify the severity of self-disgust. The median item scores for the two dimensions were 2.11 (IQR: 1.67–2.78) for personal disgust and 2.10 (IQR: 1.80–3.00) for behavioral disgust, reflecting comparable levels across both domains.

To identify unobserved heterogeneous subpopulations based on self-disgust features, an LPA was conducted exploring one to five class models (Table 1). Although the AIC, BIC and aBIC values generally decreased as the number of classes increased, the 2-class model demonstrated the highest entropy (0.965), indicating good classification precision. Furthermore, the LMRT for the 3-class model was non-significant (P = 0.086), suggesting that the inclusion of a third class did not significantly improve the model fit over the 2-class solution. The estimated model probabilities for Class 1 and Class 2 were 68.3% and 31.7%, respectively. Based on the highest posterior probabilities, patients were assigned to their most likely class for subsequent analyses. The conditional item means for the 2-class solutions are illustrated in Fig. 1. Class 1 (n = 137, 68.5% of the sample) was characterized by consistently lower scores across all 14 items of the QASD, significantly below the theoretical midpoint; thus, it was labeled the “Low Self-disgust group.” In contrast, Class 2 (n = 63, 31.5% of the sample) demonstrated substantially higher scores on all items, with most means exceeding the midpoint of 3, and was consequently labeled the “High Self-disgust group.” Notably, both profiles exhibited a similar fluctuating pattern across the items, yet remained clearly distinct in terms of their overall score levels, reflecting a “parallel” high-low distribution.

Univariate analysis of factors associated with self-disgust

Several sociodemographic, clinical, and psychosocial characteristics differed significantly between the two identified latent profiles (Table 2). Regarding sociodemographic factors, patients in Profile 2 were more likely to report a lower monthly income (P = 0.031) and a higher prevalence of financial hardship regarding medical costs (P = 0.027). Furthermore, individuals in Profile 2 experienced significantly higher levels of psychological distress, characterized by more frequent perceived burdensomeness to their families (P = 0.024) and a more pronounced fear of the future (P < 0.001).

In terms of clinical characteristics, advanced Ann Arbor stage (P = 0.006) and a history of transplant therapy (P = 0.028) were significantly associated with membership in Profile 2. Notably, psychosocial scale scores demonstrated a consistent pattern: Profile 2 exhibited significantly lower levels of psychological resilience (RISC; P < 0.001) and social support (SSRS; P = 0.003) compared to Profile 1. Most importantly, while no statistically significant difference was observed in positive coping styles between the two groups (P = 0.150), patients in Profile 2 reported significantly higher scores for negative coping styles (20.60 ± 4.13 vs. 18.65 ± 4.79, P = 0.001), identifying maladaptive coping as a critical psychological marker for the high-risk profile.

Feature selection using LASSO regression

To identify the most robust predictors of latent profile membership and mitigate potential multicollinearity among the candidate variables, a LASSO regression model was constructed. A 10-fold cross-validation was performed to determine the optimal penalty parameter (λ). The selection process is illustrated in Fig. 2, where the binomial deviance was plotted against log(λ) (Fig. 2B). To ensure model parsimony and minimize the risk of overfitting, the one standard error criterion (λ1se) was adopted as the optimal λ (represented by the rightmost vertical dashed line), which provided a more concise set of predictors than the minimum criterion (λmin).

As shown in the LASSO coefficient profiles (Fig. 2A), the coefficients of non-essential features were shrunk to zero. Ultimately, nine independent variables with non-zero coefficients were identified for inclusion in the final multivariable model. These selected predictors encompassed clinical characteristics (Ann Arbor stage and history of transplant therapy), sociodemographic factors (occupation, marital status, monthly income, and ability to afford medical costs), and psychosocial indicators (fear of the future, negative coping style, and psychological resilience).

Multivariable logistic regression analysis of factors associated with high self-disgust

Multivariable logistic regression analysis was performed to identify independent predictors of high self-disgust. The model demonstrated satisfactory fit and calibration (Hosmer-Lemeshow χ2 = 6.02, P = 0.645; AUC = 0.808, 95% CI: 0.744–0.873). Multicollinearity was not observed, with all variance inflation factors (VIF) ranging from 1.01 to 1.26. The adjusted odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) are visually summarized in the forest plot (Fig. 3) and detailed in Table 3. The final model identified that five variables remained independently associated with the high self-disgust profile. Among the sociodemographic factors, occupational status demonstrated the strongest association; specifically, patients who were workers had 4.16-fold higher odds of belonging to the high self-disgust profile compared to farmers (OR = 4.16, 95% CI: 1.51–11.93, P = 0.006). In terms of clinical and psychological factors, advanced Ann Arbor stage (OR = 1.56, 95% CI: 1.17–2.11, P = 0.003), greater fear of future (OR = 1.98, 95% CI: 1.29–3.14, P = 0.002), and higher negative coping scores (OR = 1.16, 95% CI: 1.07–1.27, P < 0.001) were significant risk factors. Conversely, psychological resilience (RISC score) was a protective factor, with each one-unit increase associated with a 9% reduction in the odds of high self-disgust (OR = 0.91, 95% CI: 0.87–0.96, P = 0.001).

Development and internal validation of the nomogram

Based on the multivariable logistic regression analysis, a comprehensive risk prediction model for high self-disgust in patients with malignant lymphoma was established. To translate these statistical findings into a practical clinical tool, we constructed a nomogram incorporating all independently associated predictors (Fig. 4). This nomogram facilitates rapid bedside assessment and enables personalized risk stratification by calculating a total risk score based on individual patient parameters.

A clinical nomogram for predicting the probability of high self-disgust in patients with malignant lymphoma. The nomogram integrates five independent predictors: occupational status, fear of future, Ann Arbor stage, psychological resilience (RISC score), and negative coping style. To estimate an individual patient’s risk, locate the patient’s value on each variable axis, draw a vertical line upward to the “Points” axis to determine the assigned score, and sum the scores across all five variables. Locate this “Total Points” on the bottom axis and draw a vertical line downward to ascertain the predicted probability of the high self-disgust profile

To rigorously evaluate the model’s predictive performance and ensure its robustness, we conducted internal validation using bootstrap resampling with 1,000 iterations. The model’s discriminative ability was assessed via the area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve, which yielded an AUC of 0.808, indicating satisfactory performance (Fig. 5A). The calibration of the model was evaluated by plotting the nomogram-predicted probabilities against the actual observed outcomes (Fig. 5B). The calibration curve demonstrated good agreement, with the bias-corrected line closely following the ideal 45-degree reference line, further supported by a non-significant Hosmer-Lemeshow test result (P = 0.685).

Finally, to assess the potential clinical utility of the nomogram, we performed decision curve analysis (DCA). The results indicated that utilizing this nomogram to guide clinical decisions provides a higher net benefit compared to both “treat all” and “treat none” strategies across a wide range of threshold probabilities (Fig. 5C). Collectively, these findings confirm that the developed nomogram is a reliable, stable, and clinically valuable tool for identifying patients at high risk for severe self-disgust in the context of malignant lymphoma.

Discussion

The profile of self-disgust in malignant lymphoma

Our study confirms that patients with malignant lymphoma experience a clinically relevant degree of self-disgust (median QASD: 30.0). To accurately contextualize this burden, it is highly instructive to contrast lymphoma with breast cancer—a malignancy frequently characterized by relatively concealable physical alterations but profound psychosocial distress. Extensive literature confirms that body image disturbance and self-loathing in breast cancer survivors are fundamentally intertwined with the localized loss of the breast, which significantly disrupts self-perceived femininity, sexuality, and intimate partnered relationships [16].

In contrast, our findings suggest that the mechanism of self-disgust in lymphoma is driven by a different psychological pathway. Lacking the surgical removal of an organ closely tied to sexual or gender identity, patients with lymphoma endure a more systemic, "invisible" toll. The disease and its aggressive treatments precipitate profound fatigue, physical deconditioning, and a precipitous decline in overall vitality. This invisible burden fundamentally erodes the patient's physical autonomy. Rather than distress over aesthetic attractiveness, this loss of somatic control translates directly into severe occupational and social incapacitation. This mechanism is supported by our multivariable model, which identified occupational disruption (i.e., "Worker" status) as the most potent independent predictor of severe self-disgust. Ultimately, it is this forced transition from an independent, wage-earning contributor to a dependent "sick role" that generates the profound self-disgust observed in this population.

Psychosocial and cognitive drivers: existential fear, maladaptive coping, and resilience

The multivariable logistic regression analysis identified several psychological constructs as critical predictors of self-disgust profile membership. Specifically, fear of the future emerged as a significant independent risk factor (OR = 1.98, 95% CI: 1.29–3.14), alongside negative coping styles (OR = 1.16, 95% CI: 1.07–1.27). In the context of malignant lymphoma, a disease characterized by a prolonged treatment trajectory and the chronic threat of relapse, fear of the future often transcends general anxiety and manifests as profound existential distress. When this existential uncertainty is met with maladaptive coping strategies, such as avoidance, denial, or behavioral disengagement, patients may fail to constructively process and integrate their identity as cancer patients. The perceived inability to control disease progression can lead to an internalization of helplessness, which is often interpreted as a loss of self-worth. In family-centric cultural contexts, this existential apprehension is frequently intertwined with "perceived burdensomeness." As physical autonomy declines, patients may view themselves as a liability to their kin, triggering a self-deprecating cognitive cycle that manifests as severe self-disgust.

Conversely, psychological resilience was identified as a significant protective factor. For each one-unit increase in the RISC score, the odds of belonging to the high self-disgust profile decreased by 9% (OR = 0.91, 95% CI: 0.87–0.96). Resilience appears to function as a cognitive buffer, it may enable patients to reframe their illness experience and decouple their inherent self-worth from their deteriorating physical condition. This mechanism aligns with Krawitz's "Cycle of Self-Hatred" framework [17], suggesting that higher resilience can interrupt the maladaptive feedback loops that fuel self-loathing, thereby preserving psychological integrity despite the oncological burden [18].

Consequently, identifying and addressing these modifiable psychological drivers is essential in oncology nursing. Supportive care models should prioritize the integration of tailored psychosocial interventions, such as Meaning-Centered Psychotherapy or Acceptance and Commitment Therapy, which target existential fear and enhance psychological flexibility. As with the management of physical symptoms, addressing psychological vulnerability requires a structured, multidisciplinary approach. By screening for negative coping patterns and proactively fostering resilience, multidisciplinary teams can better mitigate the emotional impact of the disease and potentially improve the long-term well-being of lymphoma survivors.

Clinical and socio-demographic stressors: disease stage and occupational role disruption

Multivariable logistic regression analysis identified advanced Ann Arbor stage (OR = 1.56, 95% CI: 1.17–2.11, P = 0.003) and "Worker" occupational status (OR = 4.16, 95% CI: 1.51–11.93, P = 0.006) as independent risk factors for elevated self-disgust. The biological progression of lymphoma directly contributes to emotional distress, as advanced stages are inherently coupled with escalating symptom severity, more aggressive treatment regimens, and a profound decline in physical functioning.

Beyond the biological burden, our findings reveal a striking occupational disparity. Patients employed as workers exhibited a more than four-fold increased risk of severe self-disgust compared to agricultural farmers. This phenomenon can be interpreted through the lens of occupational role disruption and identity loss. In the Chinese context, farmers may benefit from more flexible labor structures, persistent access to land as a stabilizing asset, and deeply rooted rural kinship support networks. Conversely, industrial or corporate workers, particularly rural-to-urban migrant workers engaged in heavy manual labor, often rely on rigid wage labor and continuous employment. Unlike government clerks or white-collar professionals who typically have access to structured sick leave, stable basic income, and formal return-to-work pathways, these manual workers lack robust social safety nets. For them, a cancer diagnosis precipitates an abrupt loss of wage-earning capacity, a crisis further exacerbated by strict workplace attendance policies and the tangible threat of job termination.

In contemporary society, particularly within the family-centric cultural context of China, an individual’s perceived value is deeply intertwined with economic productivity and family duty. The sudden inability to work may strip patients of their primary social utility. The transition from a primary breadwinner to a dependent patient can precipitate intense feelings of guilt and perceived burdensomeness. As occupational identity erodes, individuals may increasingly perceive themselves as a "liability" to their families. This internalized shame, driven by role disruption rather than the disease itself, can trigger a self-deprecating cognitive cycle that ultimately manifests as profound self-disgust. Furthermore, while occupational disruption is a universal cancer stressor, the intense self-disgust triggered by perceived family burdensomeness may be especially pronounced in collectivistic Asian cultures. In more individualistic Western settings, patients may process this role disruption differently. Future cross-cultural studies are essential to fully determine the generalizability of these specific mechanisms.

Therefore, addressing occupational distress is essential in comprehensive oncology nursing. As patients mourn the loss of their pre-illness professional identities, clinical care must extend beyond conventional symptom management. Future supportive care models should prioritize the integration of medical social workers and the implementation of vocational rehabilitation or occupational navigation programs. By proactively assisting patients in navigating employment rights, redefining self-worth beyond economic output, and planning for a gradual return to work, multidisciplinary teams can effectively address this modifiable driver of distress, thereby enhancing the holistic recovery of lymphoma survivors.

Interpretation of non-significant predictors

In the multivariable model, several variables originally selected by LASSO regression, including monthly household income, ability to pay, and illness understanding lost their statistical significance. This shift indicates that while these factors correlate with self-disgust in univariate analysis, their influence is likely accounted for by more direct psychological predictors such as resilience and fear of the future.

The lack of significance for income-related variables suggests that self-disgust is primarily driven by internal cognitive appraisal rather than objective financial status. Although financial toxicity is a recognized stressor in oncology [19], its psychological impact appears to be filtered through individual coping traits. For example, high psychological resilience may protect patients from self-deprecating thoughts even when financial resources are limited. Conversely, patients with sufficient resources may still experience severe self-disgust if they lack the adaptive coping mechanisms needed to process the loss of physical autonomy.

Similarly, illness understanding did not independently predict self-disgust. This finding highlights a potential complexity in clinical communication where the effect of health literacy depends on emotional readiness. Awareness of a poor prognosis or severe disease can increase feelings of helplessness if the patient does not have the internal resources to process such information. Consequently, the relationship between disease knowledge and self-worth is likely non-linear and dependent on the patient's current psychological state.

These observations have important clinical implications. Assessments should move beyond demographic or objective medical data to prioritize the patient’s subjective psychological state. Diagnostic disclosure should be viewed as more than a transfer of clinical facts. Instead, it must be tailored to the individual's coping capacity, ensuring that medical information is provided in conjunction with proactive psychosocial support.

Clinical implications and future directions

While identifying independent predictors provides valuable insights, the ultimate goal of developing a nomogram is to support clinical decision-making. To this end, we propose a preliminary conceptual framework for integrating this tool into a bedside screening workflow. Ideally, such assessments should be conducted at hospital admission or prior to the initiation of the first chemotherapy cycle, aiming to identify psychological vulnerabilities before the cumulative stress of treatment begins. Although our Latent Profile Analysis yielded a binary outcome, the nomogram generates a continuous probability of membership in the high-risk self-disgust profile. To translate this into practice, future supportive care models could explore a three-tier risk stratification based on the statistical distribution of these probabilities to guide a "stepped-care" approach:

Beyond risk stratification, our findings underscore the need to address occupational disruption as a distinct driver of self-disgust, particularly among wage-earning patients. Integrating vocational navigation and medical social work into survivorship care may be a critical component of comprehensive psychosocial support. It is crucial to emphasize that this stratification scheme and the suggested care pathways are currently exploratory and conceptual. Because this study did not externally validate these tertile-based thresholds, they cannot yet be recommended as definitive clinical guidelines. Future prospective, longitudinal studies are essential to validate optimal cut-off values and to test the efficacy of this stepped-care model in improving the holistic well-being of lymphoma survivors.

In conclusion, this integrated approach aligns with the NCCN Clinical Practice Guidelines in Oncology for Distress Management [20]. By addressing both the internal psychological traits and the external socio-occupational stressors identified in our model, clinical teams can evolve toward a personalized psychosocial care model, actively preserving patient dignity and enhancing the holistic quality of life throughout the lymphoma trajectory.

Limitations

Several limitations of this study should be noted. First, the cross-sectional design precludes the establishment of causal inferences between self-disgust and the identified associative factors. The temporal direction of these relationships must be validated through future longitudinal studies.

Second, while participants were recruited from two tertiary centers, the relatively small sample size (N = 200) limited our ability to conduct independent external validation. Although we employed bootstrap resampling (1,000 iterations) to correct for optimism, the current model remains inherently preliminary and exploratory. Future multicenter studies with larger cohorts are essential to verify its external generalizability.

Third, while the exploratory nomogram demonstrated satisfactory discrimination and calibration, the final model accounted for approximately 34.1% of the variance in the outcome (Nagelkerke pseudo R²=0.341). This indicates that other unmeasured variables, such as pre-morbid psychopathology, baseline personality traits, or specific family dynamics may likely contribute to the development of self-disgust and should be explored in future studies to optimize model performance.

Fourth, we did not strictly restrict the treatment phase in our inclusion criteria. Although the majority of participants were undergoing active antineoplastic therapy, this sample heterogeneity regarding treatment status could introduce unmeasured confounding effects that warrant stratification in future research.

Finally, to facilitate the construction of a parsimonious model, certain ordinal categorical variables (e.g., Ann Arbor stage and fear of the future) were coded as continuous variables in the regression analysis. While this strategy preserves linear dose-response trends and conserves degrees of freedom in a limited dataset, it relies on the assumption of equal intervals between categories, which is a statistical simplification that may introduce inherent bias.

Conclusions

Self-disgust in patients with malignant lymphoma is a substantial psychosocial burden associated with the convergence of psychological vulnerability, disease advancement, and occupational role disruption. This study identifies low resilience, maladaptive coping, existential fear regarding the future, advanced Ann Arbor stage, and worker status as key factors associated with a high risk of severe self-disgust.

The exploratory screening nomogram developed in this research offers a preliminary, evidence-based framework for proactive risk stratification. While further external validation is essential to confirm its broader generalizability, this tool enables oncology nurses to identify vulnerable patients early in the clinical course. Implementing targeted interventions such as resilience training, meaning-centered psychotherapy, and vocational navigation—may help mitigate these modifiable vulnerabilities. Such efforts are critical for interrupting self-deprecating cognitive cycles, preserving patient dignity, and enhancing holistic survivorship outcomes in this population.
