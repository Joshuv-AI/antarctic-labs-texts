Syntheses and Properties of Pentafluoroethylplumbanes in the Oxidation States +II, +III, and +IV
Authors: Michael Grasse, Lukas Hartmann, Beate Neumann, Hans‐Georg Stammler, Berthold Hoge
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMono‐, bis‐, and tris(pentafluoroethyl)plumbanes of the type Pb(C2F5) n Ar4‐ n (Ar = Ph, Tol, Ani; n = 1–3) are easily accessible by treatment of the corresponding arylchloroplumbanes with LiC2F5. The subsequent treatment with HCl furnishes chloro(pentafluoroethyl)plumbanes. The synthesis of the tris(pentafluoroethyl)plumbate(II) anion is facilitated by treatment of Pb(C2F5)3Cl with PPh3. In addition, formation of Pb(C2F5)4 is achieved starting from Pb(OAc)4 and is fully characterized by multinuclear NMR spectroscopy and by single crystal x‐ray diffraction of the 1,10‐phenanthroline adduct.

Introduction

Even though lead had and still has some very well‐known uses in the history of mankind, the toxicity of lead deters many scientists from researching lead compounds. The Romans used lead for water pipes, as lead is ductive and noncorrosive. Since the 1920s, lead has been widely used as an octane rating booster in gasoline, primarily in the form of tetraethyllead [1, 2], until it was replaced by dialkyl ether derivatives, such as methyl tertiary‐butyl ether (MTBE), or aromatic compounds, such as benzene, in the 1980s. Today lead is still used in lead–acid batteries. Despite the toxicity, there is still progress in the lead chemistry, mostly concerning the use in high‐performance perovskite‐sensitized solar cells [3].

The synthesis and chemistry of perfluoroalkyl lead compounds are not well studied. The first systematic study of trifluoromethylplumbanes was performed by Patorra and Eujen in the early 1990s [4, 5]. Pb(CF3)4 was first prepared by Juhlke and Lagow [6] on a milligram scale by reacting PbCl2 with CF3 radicals. The first pentafluoroethylplumbane, Pb(C2F5)Me3, was reported in 1960 [7, 8], but no spectroscopic evidence was given. Lange and Naumann [9] prepared Pb(C2F5)Me3 by reaction of Cd(C2F5)2·donor with Pb(OCOCF3)Me3 and studied it via NMR spectroscopy.

The C2F5 group shows a significantly higher thermal stability than the CF3 group. This can be impressively demonstrated for the corresponding silanes. Si(CF3)3NEt2 is unstable at room temperature and decomposes violently [10, 11]. In contrast to that Si(C2F5)3NEt2 [12] is stable up to 180°C for more than 2 h. C2F5 groups can be easily introduced with LiC2F5 [13, 14, 15]. This organometallic compound is, in contrast to LiCF3, stable in ethereal solution up to −40°C. It is easily accessible by the deprotonation of HC2F5 with n‐buthyllithium at temperatures around −78°C (Scheme 1).

Synthesis of pentafluoroethyllithium by deprotonation of pentafluoroethane at low temperatures.

Employing freshly generated LiC2F5, a series of group IV and V element compounds have already been prepared in our group, for example Si(C2F5)4 [12], (Ge{C2F5}3)2O [16], Sn(C2F5)3H [17], and Bi(C2F5)3 [18].

In this work we present a series of mono‐, bis‐, tris‐, and tetrakis(pentafluoroethyl)plumbanes. They are prepared with high efficiency on a large scale along with detailed NMR spectroscopic examinations and solid state structure analyses [19].

Electron‐withdrawing perfluoroorganyl groups, such as the CF3 and C2F5 groups, behave as so‐called pseudohalogens, occupying a position between halogens and organyl groups in terms of their chemical properties. While lead(II) halides are significantly more stable than the corresponding lead(IV) halides, the opposite is true for organyl derivatives. In the context of our investigation into C2F5 lead derivatives, we aim to demonstrate that Pb(IV), Pb(III), and Pb(II) derivatives can be stabilized by electron‐withdrawing pentafluoroethyl groups, resulting in unusual redox behavior.

Results and Discussion

Tetrakis(pentafluoroethyl)plumbane could not be prepared via the reaction of LiC2F5 with lead(IV)‐chloride. PbCl4 decomposes slowly at room temperature and therefore must be freshly prepared by treatment of a [PbCl6]2– salt with concentrated sulfuric acid [20]. The PbCl4 originating from this reaction still contains some acid causing the decomposition of LiC2F5. The use of Pb(OAc)4 circumvents this obstacle and tetrakis(pentafluoroethyl)plumbane can be obtained (Scheme 2).

Synthesis of tetrakis(pentafluoroethyl)plumbane from lead(IV)acetate.

The classic reaction with LiC2F5 takes place in Et2O, which, however, could not be separated from the product. This is circumvented by using Me2O. Its low boiling point of −25°C enables the separation of the solvent and product. The yield of the reaction (28 %) is not satisfying, but still better than that for Pb(C6F5)4 from Pb(OAc)4 with LiC6F5 (15 %) [21]. The 19F NMR spectrum displays singlet resonances for both, the CF2 and the CF3 group. The CF2 group also reveals lead satellites with a 2 J(Pb,F) coupling constant of 651 Hz. The 207Pb NMR spectrum exhibits, due to coupling to the 8 (CF2), respectively 12 (CF3) equivalent fluorine atoms, a nonet of tridecets at −415.5 ppm (Figure 1).

The Pb(CF3)4 appears at −562.2 ppm in the 207Pb NMR spectrum [4]. In order to obtain the molecular structure, this novel lead compound is converted into the 1,10‐phenanthroline (phen) adduct. Recrystallisation from chloroform provided single crystals, suitable for an x‐ray structure analysis. [Pb(C2F5)4(phen)] crystallizes in the monoclinic space group P2 1/c. The molecular structure in the solid state is shown in Figure 2 and provides the first example of a structurally characterized hexacoordinated tetraorganylplumbane containing a chelate ligand containing two nitrogen atoms.

The central lead atom exhibits a strongly distorted octahedral coordination sphere with a C1‐Pb1‐C7 bond angle of only 165°. The formally axial C2F5 groups, which are arranged in a trans configuration relative to one another, have a Pb─C bond length that is approximately three pm shorter than that of the cis‐arranged, formally equatorial C2F5 groups. The steric demand of the CF2 subunits opposite of the phenanthroline ligand pushes the two other C2F5 units into the direction of the ligand. The molecular structure indicates that the C2F5 groups are chemically inequivalent and thus two sets of signals are expected in the 19F NMR spectrum. At room temperature only one very broad resonance is observed. Low temperature NMR experiments at −50°C reveal two sets of signals for the C2F5 groups. The resonances for the CF3 units are observed at −80.8 and −81.9 ppm, respectively, while signals for the CF2 units are displayed at −101.0 (2 J(Pb,F) = 995 Hz) and −101.4 ppm (2 J(Pb,F) = 404 Hz). Compared to free Pb(C2F5)4, the absolute value of the 2 J(Pb,F) coupling constants changes by +344 and −247 Hz, respectively. In the 207Pb NMR spectrum, a significant high field shift from −416 ppm for Pb(C2F5)4 to −860 ppm for the phen adduct is observed and the resonance splits into a quintet of quintets.

In order to synthesize functionalized tris(pentafluoroethyl)plumbanes, it would be convenient to use halogens like Cl2 to cleave one lead carbon bond of Pb(C2F5)4 and generate Pb(C2F5)3Cl. However, only decomposition was observed under the applied reaction conditions. As a consequence, aryl substituents as protecting groups were employed. The syntheses of PbAr4 (Ar = Ph, Tol and Ani) via LiAr and ArI is well known in the literature (Scheme 3) [22, 23].

The molecular structure of PbAni4 in the solid state has in contrast to PbPh4 [24, 25, 26] and PbTol4 [27] not been described before. PbAni4 crystallizes in the tetragonal space group I‐4. Figure 3 shows the S 4 symmetrical molecule marked with the S 4 axis.

The structure is best described as a tetrahedron compressed along the S 4 axis, with C‐Pb‐C bond angles of 112.9° and 107.8°. These values and the Pb─C bond length are in good agreement with the literature values for other tetraarylplumbanes [24, 25, 26, 27, 28]. Reaction of PbAr4 with one or two equivalents of gaseous hydrogen chloride at ambient temperatures gave the desired compounds PbClAr3 and PbCl2Ar2 as off‐white solids, after removal of all volatile compounds in vacuo (Scheme 4).

Syntheses of arylchloroplumbanes (Ar = Ph, Tol, and Ani).

Even by using slightly less than one equivalent of hydrogen chloride the triarylchloroplumbanes cannot be obtained selectively. Instead a mixture of PbClAr3, PbCl2Ar2, and PbAr4 is observed. The diaryldichloroplumbanes can prepared with high selectivity by using two or more equivalents of gaseous hydrogen chloride.

For the addition of C2F5 groups, LiC2F5 is prepared in situ at −78°C by the combination of n‐butyllithium with pentafluoroethane in a diethyl ether solution. An arylchloroplumbane is then added at low temperatures. Under these conditions the desired aryl(pentafluoroethyl)plumbanes are afforded with excellent yields on a large scale (Pb(C2F5)2Tol2, 95%, 30 g) (Scheme 5).

Syntheses of ary(pentafluoroethyl)plumbanes from diaryldichloroplumbanes (Ar = Ph, Tol, and Ani; n = 1, 2).

The diarylbis(pentafluoroethyl)plumbanes are viscous liquids with high boiling points. Pb(C2F5)2Ani2, for example, has a boiling point of 145°C at 1∙10−6 bar and can therefore be easily purified by high vacuum distillation. In contrast to that, Pb(C2F5)Ph3 and Pb(C2F5)Tol3 are colorless solids which are hardly soluble in cold n‐pentane. They can be purified from residual diarylbis(pentafluoroethyl)plumbanes by washing the crude product with the alkane.

Single crystals, suitable for an x‐ray structure analysis, of Pb(C2F5)Ph3 and Pb(C2F5)Tol3 can be obtained from n‐pentane solution by slow evaporation of the solvent. Pb(C2F5)Ph3 crystallizes in the triclinic space group P‐1, and Pb(C2F5)Tol3 crystallizes in the monoclinic space group P21/c (Figure 4). Selected bond lengths and bond angles are given in Table 1. The asymmetric unit of the phenyl derivative comprises two molecules with a disorder in both pentafluoroethyl moieties. The tolyl derivate consists of only one molecule with no disorder.

The molecular structures of Pb(C2F5)Ph3 and Pb(C2F5)Tol3 are very similar. In both cases, the central lead atoms reside in the anticipated tetrahedral geometry. The Pb1─C1 bond vectors are in both structures not orthogonal on a plane spanned through the three aromatic carbons bonded directly to the central lead atom. Instead, they are slanted by 3.5° (Pb(C2F5)Ph3) and 3.9° (Pb(C2F5)Tol3). This behavior takes the steric demand of the C2F5 moiety into account.

Treatment of diarylbis(pentafluoroethyl)plumbanes with HCl and subsequent pentafluoroethylation selectively affords the corresponding aryltris(pentafluoroethyl)plumbanes on a large scale (Pb(C2F5)3Tol, 94%, 27 g) (Scheme 6). The obtained lead compounds are colorless liquids with a significantly lower boiling point than their diarylbis(pentafluoroethyl)plumbane analogues (Pb(C2F5)2Tol2: 108°C, 1∙10−6 bar; Pb(C2F5)3Tol: 92°C, 7∙103 bar). Even by using a large excess of HCl only one aryl group is cleaved. The use of HBr or the addition of AlCl3 or AlBr3, respectively, does not afford Pb(C2F5)2X2 (X = Cl, Br), in contrast to the corresponding germanium compound Ge(C2F5)2Br2 from Ge(C2F5)2Ph2 [29].

Syntheses of aryltris(pentafluoroethyl)plumbanes from diarylbis(pentafluoroethyl)plumbanes (Ar = Ph, Tol, and Ani).

Multinuclear NMR spectroscopy is an excellent tool for the characterization of aryl(pentafluoroethyl)plumbanes. Pb(C2F5)2Tol2, for example, can be fully characterized by NMR spectroscopy. All atoms in this compound have an isotope with a nuclear spin of I = 1/2 and are therefore easily observable by NMR. The NMR active isotope of lead is 207Pb with a natural abundance of 22.1%. Lead is the heaviest, nonradioactive element of the periodic table, which also makes 207Pb the heaviest stable isotope with a nuclear spin of I = 1/2 [20].

The shift of the resonance of the lead atom in the 207Pb NMR spectrum is very sensitive toward the substitution with C2F5 groups.

With an increasing number of C2F5 groups a significant highfield shift is observed. For the tolyl substituted plumbanes, chemical shifts from δ(207Pb) = −181 ppm (Pb(C2F5)Tol3) to −360 ppm (Pb(C2F5)3Tol) are observed. The same trend can be observed for trifluoromethylplumbanes and pentafluorophenylplumbanes [30], which demonstrates an unusual shielding effect of perfluoroorganyl groups.

Due to the large 2 J(Pb,F) and nJ(Pb,H) coupling constants the respective number of aryl and pentafluoroethyl substituents can be determined by the multiplicity in the 207Pb NMR spectrum. Figure 5 shows the 207Pb, 207Pb{1H}, and 207Pb{19F} NMR spectra of Pb(C2F5)3Tol.

207Pb{1H} (top), 207Pb{19F} (middle), and 207Pb (bottom) NMR spectrum of Pb(C2F5)3Tol. Decoupling artefacts are marked (*).

The 207Pb{19F} NMR spectrum of Pb(C2F5)3Tol reveals a triplet of triplets of quartets at −306 ppm due to the three lead hydrogen couplings (3 J(Pb,H) = 132 Hz, 4 J(Pb,H) = 51 Hz, and 6 J(Pb,H) = 9 Hz). Due to large coupling constants and very large shift range, decoupling artefacts cannot be avoided. The 207Pb{1H} NMR shows a septet of decets due to the coupling to six fluorine atoms of three CF2 units (2 J(Pb,F) = 511 Hz) and to the nine fluorine atoms of three CF3 units (3 J(Pb,F) = 4 Hz). Consequently, the non‐decoupled 207Pb NMR spectrum reveals a septet of triplets of triplets of quartets of decets (see Figure 5 bottom). In the 19F NMR spectrum two singlets are observed at 84.5 ppm (CF3) and at −102.3 ppm with lead satellites (2 J(Pb,F) = 511 Hz, CF2). The 1H NMR spectrum is characterized by two doublets and a singlet, each of them with lead satellites. Remarkable are the lead satellites at the resonance for the CH3 group, due to a lead hydrogen coupling over six bonds (6 J(Pb,H) = 9 Hz).

To obtain the functionalized chlorotris(pentafluoroethyl)plumbane, the aryl derivatives were treated with gaseous HCl. While the reaction did not proceed selectively for Pb(C2F5)3Ph, Pb(C2F5)3Tol, and Pb(C2F5)3Ani were converted selectively into Pb(C2F5)3Cl, with the former being easier to handle in terms of synthesis and purification (Scheme 7).

Synthesis of chlorotris(pentafluoroethyl)plumbane from tolyltris(pentafluoroethyl)plumbane.

Chlorotris(pentafluoroethyl)plumbane is synthesized via a solvent free reaction of tris(pentafluoroethyl)tolylplumbane with an excess of anhydrous hydrogen chloride. After 2 days of stirring at room temperature the chloroplumbane is obtained as large yellow crystals. They exhibit a vapor pressure of about 6 mbar at room temperature and can therefore be easily sublimated. The 19F NMR spectrum in C6D6/n‐pentane shows two singlets at −84.5 ppm (CF3) and −94.6 ppm (CF2), both with lead satellites. The 3 J(Pb,F) coupling constant of 23 Hz is rather large compared to mono‐, di‐, and triaryl(pentafluoroethyl)plumbanes (3 J(Pb,F) < 8 Hz). The same behavior can be observed for the 2 J(Pb,F) coupling constant (820 Hz) compared to 2 J(Pb,F) = 511 Hz for Pb(C2F5)3Tol. The 207Pb NMR spectrum in C6D6/n‐pentane shows a septet of broad multiplets at −245 ppm. Using the more polar solvent CD3CN, the signal is strongly shifted highfield to −547 ppm. The resonance of the CF2 group is also shifted toward higher field by about 4 ppm. This trend is also observed for Pb Me3Cl, Pb Et3Cl, Pb Ph3Cl, and other triorganylhalogenoplumbanes. The resonance of the central lead atom of PbEt3Cl is shifted from 472 (CHCl3) over 416 (CH3CN) to 305 ppm (pyridine) [31, 32, 33].

The IR spectrum of Pb(C2F5)3Cl (Figure 6) in the gas phase shows the characteristic vibrations of the PbCF2CF3 framework due to the absence of any other organyl groups. The band at highest energy (1330 cm‑1) is assigned to the symmetric and antisymmetric C─C stretching vibrations of the C2F5 groups. The C─F stretching vibration of the CF3 groups gives rise to a band at 1229 cm‑1. The bands at 1149 and 1105 cm−1 are assigned to the symmetric and antisymmetric C─F stretching vibrations of the CF2 groups. At 904 cm−1 the symmetric and antisymmetric Pb─C stretching vibrations are observed. The bending vibrations of the CF2 groups are found at 739 cm‑1. The Pb–Cl vibration is expected to be below 400 cm−1 and is therefore outside of the spectral range of the used spectrometer.

Gas phase IR spectrum of Pb(C2F5)3Cl with assignment of bands.

Pb(C2F5)3Cl is obtained as a crystalline solid and was studied by x‐ray structure analysis. It is located on the C 3 axis in the trigonal space group R3c. The molecular structure in the solid state is shown in Figure 7. The three Cl─Pb1─C bond angles are exactly 90.0(1)° and the C1─Pb1─C1’ bond angles are exactly 120°. The coordination sphere is not tetrahedral as expected but is extended to a trigonal bipyramidal form. The Cl1─Pb1─Cl1’ and the Pb1─Cl1─Pb1’ bond angles of 180° and two nearly identical Pb─Cl distances lead to infinite linear chains of plumbane units in the solid state (see Figure 7, right side). The Pb1─Cl1 and Pb1─Cl1’ distances amount to 261.2 and 267.1 pm, respectively. The small difference of only 6 pm shows the high Lewis acidity of the lead atom. The comparison with PbPh3Cl [34], the only other chlorotriorganylplumbane structurally characterized in the solid state, underlines this. The Pb─Cl distances (270.6 and 294.7 pm) are clearly longer (9, respectively 28 pm) and differ by about 24 pm. Additionally, the chain structure is not linear but bent at the chlorine atoms with a Pb─Cl─Pb1 bond angle of 134.0°.

This also explains the shift of the lead atom in the 207Pb NMR spectra. In nonpolar solvents the structure is rather bridged, like in the solid state, and becomes more and more monomeric in polar solvents.

Besides the direct synthesis of the tetrakis(pentafluoroethyl)plumbane from Pb(OAc)4, Pb(C2F5)4 can also be synthesized from Pb(C2F5)3Cl with LiC2F5 (Scheme 8).

In contrast to diarylbis(pentafluoroethyl)plumbanes, HBr is able to cleave the aryl group in Pb(C2F5)3Tol. The obtained bromotris(pentafluoroethyl)plumbane is not stable toward light and cannot be isolated selectively. When exposed to daylight it dimerizes and hexakis(pentafluoroethyl)diplumbane is obtained (Scheme 9); however, the bromine released in this reaction slowly decomposes the diplumbane.

The reaction of Pb(C2F5)3Cl with SbF3 selectively affords the diplumbane (Scheme 10). To the best of our knowledge, hexakis(pentafluoroethyl)diplumbane is the first perfluorinated diplumbane. The yellow solid has a melting point just above room temperature and sublimates easily in vacuum by heating to 50°C.

Synthesis of hexakis(pentafluoroethyl)diplumbane from chlorotris(pentafluoroethyl)plumbane with antimony trifluoride.

In the 19F NMR spectrum of Pb2(C2F5)6 the CF3 group is observed as a singlet at 82.6 ppm. The signal of the CF2 group is observed at 93.9 ppm with two sets of lead satellites (Figure 8).

Experimental and simulated [27] CF2 region of the 19F NMR spectrum of Pb2(C2F5)6. Lead satellites (*, ▲) and higher order lead satellites are marked (●).

The two lead atoms in hexakis(pentafluoroethyl)diplumbane are chemically equivalent but magnetically inequivalent. This effect influences the signal of the CF2 group in the 19F NMR spectrum. One set of lead satellites arises due to the 2 J(Pb,F) coupling of 600 Hz, while the second set is caused by a 3 J(Pb,F) coupling of 59 Hz. In the isotopologue (C2F5)3 207Pb207Pb(C2F5)3 the two lead atoms are magnetically inequivalent due to the long range lead fluorine coupling. These higher order lead satellites are shown in Figure 9, together with a simulation of the spectrum as a simplified A6A6’XX’ spin system.

Also, a lead–lead coupling over one bond has to be taken into account. 1 J(Pb,Pb) coupling constants vary over a wide range. Examples are Pb2Me6 [36] (+290 Hz) and Bu3PbPbEt3 which exhibits a 1 J(Pb,Pb) coupling constant 8126 Hz [33]. Because the isotopologue R3 207Pb207PbR3 is present in an abundance of only 4% and its signal is superimposed by molecules containing a nonmagnetic lead isotope, the resonances are difficult to observe. In the fully coupled 207Pb NMR spectrum the resonance splits into a septet of septets of decets. To simplify the simulation the 19F decoupling is selectively employed on the CF3 group (Figure 9).

The absolute value for the 1 J(Pb,Pb) coupling was estimated to be 5250 Hz. The experiment and the simulation are in good agreement. The value of the 1 J(Pb,Pb) coupling constant agrees with the values reported for other diplumbanes.

Hexakis(pentafluoroethyl)diplumbane is the first structurally characterized hexaalkyldiplumbane with electron‐withdrawing substituents. Other known diplumbanes mainly represent hexaaryldiplumbanes [27, 37, 38, 39, 40]. The coordination around the two lead centers is basically tetrahedral, with slightly increased Pb─Pb─C bond angles and slightly decreased C─Pb1─C bond angles. The C2F5 groups are in a gauche conformation, which is also found in literature‐known hexaorganyldiplumbanes. Due to the inversion center, the C1Pb1Pb1’C1’ torsion angle is exactly 180°.

The Pb─Pb bond length of 286.1 pm does not differ from other diplumbanes (Table 3). The Pb─Pb bond increases with a larger steric demand of the substituents. The Pb─C bond lengths of Pb2(C2F5)6 (229.6 pm) are the longest compared to the non‐fluorinated diplumbanes (219.8 to 227 pm). This is addressed as the CH3/CF3 substitution effect by Oberhammer and is due to small repulsive electrostatic interactions in the E─CF bond [41]. The conformation between two vicinal carbons is mostly staggered with angles close to the ideal 60°, with the exception of Pb2(p‐Tol)6 and Pb2(c‐Hex)6.

The color of these compounds changes from colorless (Pb2Ph6) over light yellow (Pb2(p‐Tol)6) and intense yellow (Pb2(C2F5)6, Pb2(c‐Hex)6) to orange/yellow (Pb2[Me2{C(SiMe3)3}]6). The color seems to be related to length of the Pb─Pb bond. A strong stretching or a radical character is assumed in the literature [38]. It is also reported that all these compounds are sensitive toward light.

The reaction of Pb(C2F5)4 with iodide or bromide salts affords the tris(pentafluoroethyl)plumbate(II) anion, [Pb(C2F5)3]− (Scheme 11).

Synthesis of the tris(pentafluoroethyl)plumbate(II) anion, [Pb(C2F5)3]−, from Pb(C2F5)4 with halides (cat = PPh4, N(n‐Bu)4; X = Br, I).

The reaction is much faster for iodide than for bromide. It is assumed that the reaction proceeds over a pentacoordinated transition state and that in the second step a reductive elimination takes place. The generation of C2F5Br or C2F5I is clearly proven by 19F NMR spectroscopy. The pentacoordinated intermediate can also be observed by NMR spectroscopy using bromide salts; for iodide salts the reaction is too fast to be monitored. Chloride forms a stable adduct with Pb(C2F5)4 in solution; no C2F5Cl can be observed. A large highfield shift for the CF2 groups can be observed. For the chlorine adduct the CF2 group shifts from −94.0 (free Pb(C2F5)4) to −103.5 ppm.

In this reaction, iodide and even bromide are oxidized from I to +I and in the same step lead is reduced to +II. For the oxidation of chlorine, the oxidation potential of lead(IV) is not high enough. Lead(IV) salts are used as potential oxidation agents since 1930, for example for the glycol cleavage with lead tetraacetate [42].

Due to the still inefficient synthesis of Pb(C2F5)4, however, it would be much more attractive to use Pb(C2F5)3Cl as a starting material. Patorra [43] showed that the reaction of Pb(CF3)3Br with tertiary phosphanes (PR3, R = Me, Ph, NEt2) yields the tris(trifluoromethyl)plumbate(II), which decomposes above 40°C. The reaction of Pb(C2F5)3Cl with PR3 (R = Me, Ph) furnishes [PR3Cl][Pb(C2F5)3] as a room temperature stable compound, which again highlights the increased stability of pentafluoroethyl substituted compounds compared to trifluoromethyl compounds [12]. Characteristic for anionic lead(II) compounds is a distinct lowfield shift of the lead atom in the 207Pb NMR spectrum compared to lead(IV) [33]. While the resonance of Pb(C2F5)4 in the 207Pb NMR spectrum is observed at δ = −420 ppm, it is shifted to 1608 ppm for [Pb(C2F5)3]−. The 1 J(Pb,C) coupling constant is exceptionally large, which agrees with literature values.

While the 2 J(Pb,F) coupling constant decreases by several hundreds of Hertz, the 3 J(Pb,F) coupling constant, which usually is barely observable, now even exceeds the 2 J(Pb,F) coupling constant. The 207Pb NMR spectrum shows therefore a decet of septets (Figure 11).

The 19F NMR spectrum is dominated by two multiplets (Figure 12). Due to short and long range couplings, 3 J(F,F) ≙ 5 J(F,F) = 2 Hz, a triplet of quintets for the CF3 group and a quartet of septets for the CF2 group is observed.

The formation of the chlorophosphonium ion is impressively shown in the 31P{1H} NMR spectrum. The 31P chemical shift of 66 ppm is in agreement with literature references [45, 46]. The spectrum reveals the 35Cl/37Cl isotope shift (Figure 13). In fluorine chemistry, the chlorine isotope shift is well known. The most common reference substance for 19F NMR spectra is the resonance of CF35Cl2 37Cl, the most abundant isotopologue of CFCl3 [47, 48]. The ratio of the 35Cl/37Cl isotopologues is 74.5/24.5 [20], which can be clearly observed in the 31P{1H} NMR spectrum of [PPh3Cl][Pb(C2F5)3].

The isotope shift of 1.23 Hz was measured at 121.5 MHz and 25°C. This value is about half the amount of the value for PCl3 (3.1 Hz, 162 Mhz, ca. −100°C) [49], which can be explained by the temperature‐dependency of the isotope shift [50].

By slow diffusion of n‐pentane into a solution of [PPh3Cl][Pb(C2F5)3] in CH2Cl2 a single crystal suitable for x‐ray structure analysis was obtained. [PPh3Cl][Pb(C2F5)3] crystallizes in the orthorhombic space group P212121 (Figure 14). The crystal structure of [Pb(C2F5)3]− represents the first plumbate(II) anion with fluorinated groups. The lead atom of the plumbate anion is in a pseudo‐trigonal pyramidal and the phosphorus atom of the phosphonium cation is in a tetrahedral coordination sphere. The structure of the cation is consistent with the literature [51]. The Pb─C bond lengths are all close to 239 pm and are longer than for [K{[18]crown‐6}][PbPh3] [52] (232 to 234 pm) or [Li(pmdeta)][PbPh3] [53] (228 to 230 pm). As for [Pb(C2F5)3]−, this can be rationalized by the effects described by Oberhammer [41]. The CPbC bond angles (85.0°–96.2°) are smaller compared to those of [K{[18]crown‐6}][PbPh3] (93.3–96.9).

Selected bond lengths [pm] and angles [°]: Pb1C1 239.4(3), Pb1C3 239.0(3), Pb1C5 238.6(3), P1Cl1 199.01(9), Pb1Cl 436.41(7), C1Pb1C3 96.25(10), C1Pb1C5 85.00(11), C3Pb1C5 95.37(10), Pb1─C1─C2 117.5(2), Pb1─C3─C4 129.2(2), Pb1─C5─C6 114.3(2).

This indicates the absence of a hybridization between the 6s and the 6p orbitals and shows the high s character of the lead lone pair of [Pb(C2F5)3]−, which is consistent with Bent's rule [54]. This was also observed for the isoelectronic and isostructural Bi(C2F5)3 [55]. The bismuthane also shows a trigonal pyramidal coordination and C─Bi─C angles close to 90°. Additionally, the lead lone pair is very unreactive. The plumbate anion can only be methylated with methyl triflate; no reaction can be observed with methyl iodide or allyl bromide.

In the solid state, no Pb–Cl contacts are observed. The lead chlorine distance of 436 pm exceeds the sum of the van der Waals radii (380 pm) [20].

Conclusion

In this work we reported the synthesis of tetrakis(pentafluoroethyl)plumbane along with the solid state structure of the 1,10‐phenanthroline adduct. Furthermore, the synthesis of functionalized tris(pentafluoroethyl)plumbanes is shown. The synthesis employed the use of aryl substituents as protective groups and yielded a series of pentafluoroethyl lead compounds with high yields. Aryl protecting groups can be selectively removed by use of gaseous HCl. The resulting plumbanes, Pb(C2F5) n Ar4‐ n (n = 1 – 3), were extensively studied by NMR spectroscopy and x‐ray crystallography. The reaction of Pb(C2F5)3Tol with HCl selectively leads to Pb(C2F5)3Cl, which showed a pentacoordinated lead atom in the solid state. The reaction with SbF3 furnishes the hexakis(pentafluoroethyl)diplumbane, a stable lead(III) compound. By reaction of the chloroplumbane with PPh3 a phosphonium plumbate is obtained. The molecular structure of [PPh3Cl][Pb(C2F5)3] in the solid states shows the high s character of the lead lone pair.
