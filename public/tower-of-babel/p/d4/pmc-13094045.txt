Synchronous breast carcinoma and diffuse large B-cell lymphoma: a case report and literature review on diagnostic challenges and management implications
Authors: Chunyan Zhu, Lei Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSynchronous breast carcinoma and diffuse large B-cell lymphoma occurring as independent primary malignancies is exceptionally rare. Because these tumors differ markedly in histological origin, biological behavior, and treatment strategies, their coexistence can easily lead to diagnostic pitfalls and therapeutic dilemmas. Introduction

Synchronous multiple primary malignancies (SMPM) are defined as the occurrence of two or more independent primary malignant tumors in a single patient within 6 months of each other [1]. Although rare in clinical practice, they carry substantial clinical significance. Among the various combinations, synchronous breast carcinoma and diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL) represent an extremely uncommon entity. Given the marked differences in histogenesis, molecular features, and therapeutic strategies, this coexistence poses dual challenges in both diagnosis and management.

Breast carcinoma is the most common malignancy among women, and its molecular surrogate subtypes (luminal and non-luminal, HER2-positive, and triple-negative) largely determine treatment strategies and prognosis. In contrast, DLBCL is the most common aggressive subtype of non-Hodgkin lymphoma, characterized by heterogeneous molecular profiles and variable therapeutic responses. A frequent diagnostic pitfall is the misinterpretation of disseminated lymphadenopathy or visceral involvement as metastatic spread from breast carcinoma, leading to erroneous staging and inappropriate therapeutic decisions. To avoid this pitfall, when imaging suggests widespread lymphadenopathy or visceral involvement, distinguishing metastatic disease from synchronous dual primary malignancies typically relies on histopathologic confirmation supported by immunohistochemistry; when clinically feasible, sampling of anatomically distinct lesions can be performed to complete the differential diagnosis [1].

Over the past decade, only sporadic case reports have been published, with highly heterogeneous clinical presentations. In this study, we reviewed 12 cases reported in the literature during the last ten years, together with one case from our own institution, resulting in a pooled cohort of 13 patients with synchronous breast carcinoma and DLBCL. The findings indicate that most patients were middle-aged or elderly women. Breast carcinoma predominantly presented as invasive ductal carcinoma (IDC), with molecular subtypes covering Luminal, HER2-positive, and triple-negative categories. The lymphoma component included both primary breast lymphoma (PBL) and systemic lymphoma (SBL) with multi-site involvement. Molecular subtyping encompassed GCB, non-GCB, and high-risk MCD variants, with some cases showing rare extranodal dissemination to the thyroid, peritoneum, or spleen, reflecting the biological complexity of this disease spectrum.

Against this background, the present study systematically summarizes the clinical characteristics, diagnostic pitfalls, treatment patterns, and follow-up outcomes of these cases. We emphasize the pivotal role of a multidisciplinary team (MDT) in therapeutic decision-making and further explore the potential intersections between breast carcinoma and DLBCL at immunological and molecular levels, aiming to provide a reference for clinical practice and future cross-tumor mechanistic research.

Case presentation

Onset and initial presentation

A 54-year-old female presented in February 2022 with a progressively enlarging, painless mass in the left cervical (neck) region. Contrast-enhanced CT revealed disordered glandular architecture with enhancement in the outer upper quadrant of the left breast, accompanied by multi-station lymphadenopathy, including enlarged cervical (neck), axillary, mediastinal, and para-aortic lymph nodes. These findings were initially interpreted radiologically as breast carcinoma with nodal and systemic metastases. Based on clinical/imaging assessment, the breast lesion measured approximately 2 cm, and imaging showed predominant multi-station lymphadenopathy without typical breast-cancer metastatic sites; because the extensive nodal disease was pathologically attributable to lymphoma, the breast carcinoma was clinically staged as cT2N0M0.

Core biopsy of the left breast lesion confirmed invasive ductal carcinoma (IDC) on H&E sections (Fig. 1). Immunohistochemistry (IHC) demonstrated ER 95% positive, PR 50% positive, HER2 IHC 3+, and a Ki-67 index of ~ 30%.

Treatment and follow-up

From March 2022, the patient received first-line R-CHOP (rituximab 600 mg on day 0, cyclophosphamide 1.1 g on day 1, liposomal doxorubicin 40 mg on day 1, vincristine 2 mg on day 1, and prednisone 100 mg on days 1–5) for six cycles through August 2022, with cycle 1 administered in March 2022. Next-generation sequencing performed during early treatment revealed TP53, MYD88, and CD79B mutations consistent with non-GCB/MCD biology; therefore, zanubrutinib (a Bruton tyrosine kinase inhibitor, 160 mg orally twice daily) was added from cycle 2 in April 2022 as a biology-directed adjunct. Treatment was complicated by pulmonary infection; subsequent cycles were modified by switching the anthracycline to epirubicin from cycle 3 in May 2022, and rituximab was withheld in later cycles in the setting of infection (cycles 4–6, June–August 2022). Zanubrutinib was administered concomitantly with chemotherapy during frontline treatment (from cycle 2), continued in combination with subsequent salvage/bridging regimens, and was also continued orally after ASCT.

After completion of six cycles and an approximate two-month recovery period, the breast carcinoma was managed surgically with a left mastectomy in October 2022. Postoperative pathology confirmed grade II invasive ductal carcinoma with a gross tumor size of approximately 2 cm. Because the axillary/systemic nodal disease was pathologically attributable to lymphoma, breast-cancer–specific axillary nodal staging was not further pursued at surgery. There was no evidence of local recurrence of the breast carcinoma on follow-up.

In February 2023, lymphoma progression was documented, and salvage R-GemOx (rituximab, gemcitabine, and oxaliplatin) plus zanubrutinib was initiated in March 2023, achieving partial remission after three cycles. Despite relapse after prior exposure to zanubrutinib, it was retained in subsequent lines because the patient demonstrated clinical benefit with zanubrutinib-containing regimens (including PR with re-challenge) and alternative options were limited. Because CAR-T therapy was not feasible for financial reasons, bridging RLZ (rituximab, lenalidomide, and zanubrutinib) was started in July 2023, followed by autologous stem cell transplantation (ASCT) with BeEAM conditioning (bendamustine, etoposide, cytarabine, and melphalan) in August 2023, resulting in a short-term complete remission. In December 2023, relapse was confirmed with mediastinal, hepatic, and pulmonary involvement; the disease course was also complicated by autoimmune hemolytic anemia. Due to financial limitations, the patient was discharged with progressive active disease.

Study cohort

This study included a total of 13 patients with synchronous breast carcinoma and diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL), comprising one case from our institution and 12 cases reported in the literature over the past decade. The 12 prior cases were identified through a literature search conducted across major Chinese and English databases (including CNKI, Wanfang, and PubMed) within the past 10 years, using search terms such as “synchronous breast cancer,” “diffuse large B-cell lymphoma,” and “multiple primary malignancies.” Only cases with detailed clinical and pathological data were included. The clinical and pathological features of all cases are summarized in (Fig. 4). All patients were female, aged 47–82 years (median, 55 years), indicating that this disease combination predominantly occurs in middle-aged and elderly women. Notably, Nishikawa et al. [2] described an 82-year-old patient with synchronous breast DLBCL, accessory breast invasive lobular carcinoma, and rectal adenocarcinoma, representing a rare “triple primary” malignancy and highlighting the complexity of the disease spectrum.

The breast carcinoma component was most frequently invasive ductal carcinoma (IDC). Consistent with the baseline predominance of IDC in the general breast-cancer population, IDC also accounted for the majority of reported breast histologies in our currently compiled synchronous cases. Molecular subtypes included Luminal A, Luminal B, HER2-positive, and triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), with Luminal and HER2-positive subtypes accounting for the majority, though a minority of TNBC cases were also documented. Representative reports include the HER2-positive IDC with bilateral breast DLBCL described by Liu et al. [3], and the TNBC with left breast DLBCL reported by Fu et al. [4], underscoring the marked heterogeneity of breast cancer phenotypes in this cohort.

All lymphoma cases were confirmed as DLBCL, encompassing both primary breast lymphoma (PBL) confined to the breast/axilla and systemic lymphoma (SBL) with multi-site involvement. For instance, Zhong et al. [5] reported two contrasting cases: one with right breast IDC and PBL (restricted to the breast, negative for axillary and systemic spread), and another with left breast IDC and SBL (involving both breasts, mediastinal/para-aortic/retroperitoneal lymph nodes, lung, and bone). These carcinomas were classified as Luminal B/HER2 + subtypes, emphasizing the anatomical and biological heterogeneity of synchronous dual primaries. Lian et al. [6] reported a localized PBL case with no relapse during follow-up after standard therapy. By contrast, our institutional case, characterized by the high-risk MCD subtype with widespread dissemination, exhibited a refractory course with repeated relapse–remission cycles. Salemis [7] described a patient with massive intra-abdominal DLBCL who ultimately died from refractory lymphoma. Ho et al. [8] reported an aggressive case of breast IDC with intravascular large B-cell lymphoma (IVLBCL), achieving only transient remission after chemotherapy before succumbing to the disease.

With respect to anatomical distribution, in addition to ipsilateral breast and axillary lymph nodes, unusual presentations have been documented, including bilateral breasts [3], accessory breast [2], thyroid [9], peritoneum, and spleen [7, 10]. Such atypical localizations lack imaging specificity and are prone to misinterpretation as breast carcinoma metastases.

Treatment strategies primarily involved rituximab-based anthracycline regimens (R-CHOP or R-CDOP), with some patients receiving intrathecal prophylaxis or localized radiotherapy. Breast cancer was managed according to molecular subtype, including surgery, radiotherapy, endocrine therapy, or HER2-targeted therapy. Selected cases received novel agents or transplantation under multidisciplinary team (MDT) guidance. For instance, both Fu et al. [4] and our institutional case were treated with the BTK inhibitor zanubrutinib for maintenance, while high-risk patients such as Liu et al. [3] and our case underwent autologous stem cell transplantation (ASCT).

Follow-up outcomes demonstrated that breast carcinoma was generally controllable with appropriate local and subtype-guided therapies, whereas prognosis was largely determined by the lymphoma component. Localized PBL cases often achieved long-term remission after standard chemotherapy (e.g., Lian et al. [6]; Yasmeen et al. [11]). In contrast, systemic disease or high-risk molecular subtypes (e.g., our case, Salemis [7], Ho et al. [8]) were characterized by repeated relapses or early mortality, underscoring the close correlation between prognosis and the biological features of DLBCL.

Discussion

Synchronous multiple primary malignancies (SMPM) are extremely rare in clinical practice, and the synchronous occurrence of breast carcinoma with diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL) is even less common. Analysis of the SEER database, comprising 229,859 patients with lymphoma, showed that approximately 13.9% developed a second primary malignancy, with breast carcinoma being the most common solid tumor among women [12]. Similarly, a retrospective study of 2,632 NHL patients from Tianjin Medical University Cancer Institute and Hospital identified only 97 cases with multiple primary malignancies (3.7%), of which 21 were synchronous (22.8%), further emphasizing the rarity of this entity [13]. In the published literature, only 12 cases of synchronous breast carcinoma and DLBCL have been reported over the past decade. Together with the present case, a total of 13 cases now constitute one of the more systematic compilations available to date.

Case characteristics indicate that most patients were middle-aged or elderly women. The breast carcinoma component was predominantly invasive ductal carcinoma (IDC), with molecular subtypes largely distributed within Luminal and HER2-positive categories, while triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) was documented in only a few cases [3, 4, 6, 11]. Regarding lymphoma, the non-germinal center B-cell (non-GCB) subtype of DLBCL was the most frequent, generally associated with a high Ki-67 index. Some reports involved special variants such as intravascular large B-cell lymphoma (IVLBCL) [8], primary breast lymphoma (PBL) and systemic breast lymphoma (SBL) [5, 6], as well as a “triple primary” combination consisting of breast DLBCL, accessory breast invasive lobular carcinoma (ILC), and rectal adenocarcinoma [2]. Collectively, these reports suggest that this rare coexistence is not entirely incidental, but may reflect specific clinical patterns and underlying molecular mechanisms.

Diagnosis remains the critical challenge in the management of SMPM. When breast carcinoma presents with extensive lymphadenopathy or visceral involvement, imaging frequently mimics metastatic dissemination, often leading to misclassification as stage IV breast cancer and inappropriate palliative treatment. Salemis [7] reported a case in which massive retroperitoneal DLBCL was almost misdiagnosed as widespread metastatic breast carcinoma; similarly, Ueda et al. [10] described a patient whose extensive nodal involvement was initially mistaken for stage IV breast carcinoma. In our case, PET-CT also initially suggested “breast carcinoma with widespread metastasis,” but independent biopsies of the breast mass and cervical/axillary lymph nodes ultimately confirmed the dual primary nature. Another illustrative case was reported by Ho et al. [8], in which IVLBCL manifested with systemic symptoms but limited imaging findings, and was temporarily misinterpreted as a paraneoplastic syndrome. Both previous reports and our findings highlight that the cornerstone of SMPM diagnosis lies in adhering to the principle of “one lesion, one proof”—each suspicious lesion should undergo independent sampling and immunohistochemical evaluation. This approach enables pathological stratification of tumors and prevents the common pitfall of misdiagnosis arising from attributing all manifestations to a single malignancy (Fig. 5).

Treatment strategies

Both previous reports and our present case highlight the principle of “controlling lymphoma first, then addressing breast carcinoma” [3, 6, 9–11]. As a highly aggressive hematologic malignancy, DLBCL is the major determinant of prognosis and should be prioritized with R-CHOP or modified regimens. Importantly, the anthracyclines and cyclophosphamide included in R-CHOP also exert antitumor effects on breast carcinoma, thereby achieving dual disease coverage to some extent [4, 6, 11]. Subsequently, additional therapies for breast carcinoma are implemented based on molecular subtype, including surgery, radiotherapy, endocrine therapy, or HER2-targeted therapy [3, 6, 10]. In selected high-risk lymphoma patients—particularly those with non-GCB or MCD molecular subtypes—treatments such as hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT/ASCT) or novel targeted agents (e.g., BTK inhibitors) have been associated with prolonged survival [3, 7]. Throughout the entire therapeutic process, the role of the multidisciplinary team (MDT) is indispensable: careful sequencing is required to avoid overlapping cardiotoxicity between anthracyclines and anti-HER2 agents [5, 11], while simultaneously ensuring an optimal balance between management of both malignancies (Fig. 6).

In terms of prognosis, almost all published cases as well as our own findings suggest that the overall outcome is primarily determined by the course of DLBCL, whereas breast carcinoma is usually diagnosed at an early stage and can be effectively controlled through standard surgery and subtype-guided therapies [3, 6, 10, 11]. For example, Ueda et al. [10] reported a patient with HER2-positive IDC and systemic DLBCL who achieved disease-free survival for over two years following R-CHOP combined with trastuzumab. By contrast, patients with IVLBCL or the MCD subtype frequently experienced rapid progression and early mortality due to their highly aggressive biology [7, 8]. Moreover, a retrospective analysis by Tanba et al. [14] found that overall survival (OS) was significantly reduced in DLBCL patients with concurrent solid tumors, while lymphoma-specific outcomes (PFS/LSS) remained largely unaffected, suggesting that OS reduction was mainly attributable to competing risks such as the second malignancy, treatment-related toxicity, or comorbid complications. Our institutional case presented an alternative scenario: while breast carcinoma remained stable postoperatively, MCD-subtype DLBCL showed poor sensitivity to R-CHOP and relapsed repeatedly despite sequential BTK inhibitor therapy and autologous transplantation, ultimately determining the prognosis. This underscores that in synchronous dual primaries, high-risk lymphoma subtypes may themselves serve as the dominant factor shaping survival outcomes.

From a mechanistic perspective, several studies have proposed that breast cancer-related antigens may chronically stimulate B-cell proliferation, driving clonal expansion in a manner analogous to Helicobacter pylori-induced gastric MALT lymphoma [7, 15]. Conversely, immune dysregulation caused by DLBCL may impair host immune surveillance against breast epithelial malignancies, thereby potentially increasing the risk of breast carcinoma [3, 9]. Virological factors have also been proposed in the oncogenesis of both tumors, including EBV and MMTV-like DNA sequences [2, 6]; however, these associations were not evaluated in the present patient and should be considered hypothesis-generating. At the molecular pathway level, Bcl-3, initially identified in lymphoma, is also highly expressed in ERα-positive breast carcinoma, where it has been reported to enhance ERα signaling and promote tumor proliferation [16]; whether this axis is relevant to our case remains unknown, as BCL-3 was not directly assessed. In addition, the literature has discussed aberrant activation of HER2-related signaling and the NF-κB/MyD88 axis in breast carcinoma and DLBCL [3, 9, 11], raising a hypothesis of possible pathway overlap and molecular cross-talk; however, this was not directly evaluated in our patient, and causality should not be inferred. Furthermore, large-scale SEER data indicate that women ≥ 50 years with NHL have a significantly increased risk of breast carcinoma, pointing to a possible shared epidemiological susceptibility [12].

With respect to follow-up and management, both previous literature and our findings underscore the necessity of a “dual-track” surveillance strategy. At initial diagnosis, whole-body PET-CT should be performed to establish baseline systemic disease, while breast MRI is recommended for ongoing evaluation of breast carcinoma; serial PET-CT is indispensable for dynamic monitoring of lymphoma [5, 6]. Treatment outcomes must be recorded separately as breast cancer DFS/OS and lymphoma PFS/OS, thereby avoiding composite endpoints that obscure the true disease course [5, 7, 10]. In high-risk DLBCL cases such as the MCD subtype, early assessment for CAR-T therapy or allogeneic transplantation should be considered [3]. Additionally, overlapping cardiotoxicity from anthracyclines and anti-HER2 agents necessitates vigilant monitoring and multidisciplinary coordination. Continuous engagement of the MDT is essential to tailor strategies dynamically and achieve optimal balance in the management of dual primaries [5, 11].

Conclusion

In summary, by integrating one institutional case with 12 cases reported in the past decade, this study compiled a total of 13 patients with synchronous breast carcinoma and DLBCL. We delineated their clinical characteristics, diagnostic pitfalls, treatment strategies, prognostic patterns, and potential mechanisms. Accurate diagnosis depends on multi-site biopsies and immunohistochemistry to avoid misclassification as metastatic carcinoma. Treatment should follow the principle of “lymphoma first”, with multidisciplinary team (MDT) coordination to balance management of both tumors. Prognosis is generally determined by DLBCL, particularly in high-risk molecular subtypes. Mechanistically, immune dysregulation, virological factors, and cross-talk between signaling pathways such as Bcl-3–ERα and HER2–NF-κB/MyD88 may contribute [12, 16]. Future efforts should rely on multi-omics approaches and prospective registries to develop individualized management strategies and improve long-term outcomes in this rare patient population.

Future perspectives

Big data and epidemiological evidence. Current reports are limited to sporadic case descriptions and lack robust epidemiological support. Future studies should leverage real-world datasets such as SEER and Chinese tumor registries to clarify incidence, temporal relationships, and prognostic patterns [12]. Risk stratification models may help identify high-risk populations (e.g., women ≥ 50 years, specific lymphoma molecular subtypes) and quantify the contribution of competing risks to overall survival [12, 14].

Multi-omics exploration of molecular mechanisms. Synchronous dual primaries may share oncogenic pathways. Future research should integrate genomic sequencing, transcriptomics, and immune microenvironment profiling to uncover common events, such as BCL-3/ERα axis dysregulation, HER2–NF-κB–MyD88 pathway cross-talk, and immune checkpoint imbalance [3, 9, 11, 16]. These insights may enable the construction of cross-tumor mechanistic models and inform the development of targeted and immunotherapeutic approaches.

Clinical management and therapeutic optimization. Achieving coordinated treatment of dual malignancies remains a central challenge. DLBCL should continue to be treated with a curative-intent approach involving chemotherapy or transplantation, while breast carcinoma requires subtype-guided individualized therapy [5]. Future investigations should address practical issues such as treatment sequencing (synchronous vs. sequential) and overlapping toxicities (e.g., anthracycline and anti-HER2 cardiotoxicity) [5, 11].

Cross-disciplinary collaboration will help reduce variability in clinical practice, improve long-term survival, and enhance quality of life for affected patients [5, 14].
