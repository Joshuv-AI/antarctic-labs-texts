One is not like the other: Examining the neural response to repetitive low-level blast exposure in experienced military personnel
Authors: Timothy K. Lam, Shawn G. Rhind, Catherine Tenn, Ann Nakashima, Maria Y. Shiu, Kristen King, Norleen Caddy, Janani Vallikanthan, Mike Crouzat, Laura Lester, Oshin Vartanian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMilitary members often report concussion-like symptoms from repetitive low-level blast (LLB) exposure, defined as overpressure from outgoing munitions like rifles and explosive breaching. Typically, the early stages of concussion and other neurological conditions (e.g., Alzheimer's Disease) lead to hyperconnectivity which is a transient and adaptive brain response to strengthen and establish neural connections to restore brain function. Over time, however, chronic hyperconnectivity can contribute to neurodegeneration. To determine whether LLB exposure also exhibits this connectivity trajectory, this study investigated the neural signature of LLB at two time points: At the chronic stage extrapolated from the duration of an individual's occupational career, and following a recent and concentrated blast regimen.MethodsForty-six military breachers and snipers underwent a resting state functional magnetic resonance imaging brain scan before and after a training course. Graph theory was used to study the whole-brain network, cross-validated by a principal components analysis (PCA) conducted post-hoc. The pre-course scan was analyzed separately to examine the neural effects of chronic LLB exposure. The pre- and post-course scans were compared to examine the neural effects of recent blast exposure. Military controls without occupational breaching and/or sniping experience underwent the same protocol.ResultsAt pre-course, breachers and snipers exhibited hyperconnectivity compared to controls. However, after undergoing a recent LLB regimen, only breachers showed hypoconnectivity post-course relative to pre-course compared to controls.ConclusionThe mechanism of repeated LLB overpressure and its associated neural response to this exposure appear to be specific to this condition. Characterizing LLB exposure can help refine assessment and treatment.

Introduction

Military members are often exposed to blast during combat [Gondusky and Reiter, 2005]. Combat-related blasts are typically classified as high-level blast, which is overpressure generated from incoming munitions, such as rocket-propelled grenades or improvised explosive devices (IEDs) [Belding et al., 2021], and is associated with serious health outcomes including amputations [Mamczak and Elster, 2012], hearing deficits [Ballivet de Régloix et al., 2017; Joseph et al., 2018], and traumatic brain injury (TBI), including concussions (mild TBI) [Garber et al., 2014; Regasa et al., 2019]. Research has often focused on high-level blast, yet considerably less is known about the health impacts of occupational exposure to low-level blast (LLB) [Simmons et al., 2020], which is overpressure generated from outgoing munitions, such as heavy weapons (e.g., artillery), rifles (e.g., 0.50 caliber guns), and explosive breaching [Belding et al., 2021]. LLBs are typically found in the context of training and operations, with the repetitive nature of these blasts leading to a cumulative effect on health over time [Elder et al., 2014; Carr et al., 2015, 2016]. Repeated LLB exposure is associated with tinnitus [Nakashima et al., 2022], neurocognitive deficits [Carr et al., 2016], vestibular dysfunction [Littlefield et al., 2016], and alterations in serum biomarkers of neurological injury [Tate et al., 2013; Boutté et al., 2019]. Collectively, the evidence suggests that repetitive LLB negatively impacts brain health and performance.

In contrast to high-level blast, the symptoms of LLB are more subtle, arising from repeated chronic exposure rather than a single blast event. While military personnel exposed to LLB frequently report concussion-like symptoms, such as headaches, memory deficits, and mood changes [Carr et al., 2015; Vartanian et al., 2020, 2022; Di Battista et al., 2025], research on LLB exposure has also shown outcomes across other dimensions, including alterations in cerebral blood flow [Kilgore and Hubbard, 2024], oculomotor behaviour [Hunfalvay et al., 2022], neurocognitive performance [LaValle et al., 2019; Vartanian et al., 2020], immune responses [Vorn et al., 2022; Rhind et al., 2024; Stone et al., 2024], and blood biomarkers like tau or glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) levels [Edwards et al., 2020; Tschiffely et al., 2020]. Despite these physiological and behavioural consequences, the neural signature of LLB exposure is not well understood, including the extent to which they might mimic or diverge from concussion-like outcomes. Elucidating the neural signature that is specific to LLB exposure may yield valuable insights that could inform assessment and treatment strategies distinct from those developed for mTBI.

Brain connectivity

Multiple brain regions work together to perform both simple and complex tasks. A network is composed of anatomically distinct brain regions, also referred to as nodes, in which its neural activity is temporally coupled and thus considered ‘functionally connected’ [Smith et al., 2009]. There are various networks, each specializing in certain brain functions. For example, the frontoparietal network is involved in setting task goals [Dosenbach et al., 2007], whereas the default mode network is implicated in internally-directed tasks, such as emotional processing and autobiographical memory [Raichle, 2015]. Graph theory, which is a branch of mathematics that examines the properties of networks including the efficiency of information transfer or the connection density of a node, can be used to assess and distinguish between different neurological conditions [Bullmore and Sporns, 2009]. Importantly, advancements in graph theory allow for functional connectivity across the whole brain to be studied simultaneously, rather than being limited to individual networks, which in turn provides a more comprehensive analysis approach [Salvador et al., 2005] that may be beneficial in better understanding LLB exposure.

A major focus of network neuroscience is to uncover how the brain responds to injury. Blast-related mTBI leads to disruptions in brain connectivity, though the findings in the literature are relatively mixed with studies reporting both increased [Vakhtin et al., 2013; Huang et al., 2017; Pagulayan et al., 2020; Boroda et al., 2021] and decreased [Sponheim et al., 2011; Vakhtin et al., 2013; Han et al., 2014; Huang et al., 2017] connections throughout the brain. Although studies on LLB exposure suggest it is a distinct entity from mTBI, research on the latter can serve as a theoretical departure point to define a neural connectivity framework specific to LLB exposure. An influential unifying theory proposed by Hillary and Grafman [2017] suggests that brain injury paradoxically leads to enhanced functional connectivity between select nodes in damaged neural networks known as hyperconnectivity. This phenomenon is believed to be a transient and adaptive response in the acute stages of injury, whereby the brain compensates by strengthening and developing new connections between nodes to establish detour pathways and maintain network function. However, in the long run these detours are resource-intensive and inefficient whereby chronic hyperconnectivity may in fact be detrimental to brain health with the accumulation of amyloid-beta (Aβ) deposits and eventually neurodegeneration [Schultz et al., 2017]. As a result, hyperconnectivity in the acute stages of brain injury eventually transitions to hypoconnectivity in the advanced stages. Interestingly, Hillary and Grafman [2017] noted that this hyperconnectivity phenomenon is observed in various neurological conditions, including TBI, stroke, and multiple sclerosis. Given that hyperconnectivity is frequently observed across various disorders, it is plausible that repeated LLB exposure, particularly when it becomes a chronic condition, could also exhibit this connectivity pattern. In this sense the framework proposed by Hillary and Grafman [2017] can be explored as a comparative template to examine connectivity patterns in relation to LLB.

Research on the neural impact of repeated LLB has been limited thus far, with only a few studies that have examined the changes in neural connectivity. Yuan et al. [2019] found that a single day of breacher training consisting of multiple LLBs led to hyperconnectivity involving the auditory network with the middle frontal gyrus and angular gyrus. However, there appears to be divergent results when examining the longer-term impact of repeated LLB exposure on brain connectivity. That is, Stone and colleagues [2020] observed hyperconnectivity in breachers whereas Gilmore et al. [2024] reported hypoconnectivity in Special Operations Forces members exposed to blast. While both studies focused on experienced military personnel, a key difference was that the former defined ‘experienced’ as participants with at least 4 years of military and/or law enforcement experience, whereas the latter included those with at least 10 years of LLB exposure. More recently, Solar et al. [2024] observed that repetitive sub-concussions significantly disrupt neuronal activity and functional connectivity, as shown by magnetoencephalography. Collectively, these studies align with the hyperconnectivity phenomenon proposed by Hillary and Grafman [2017] as military personnel in the early stages of repeated LLB exposure (i.e., those with approximately 4 years of exposure) elicit hyperconnectivity. In turn, as military personnel are continually exposed to LLB in the course of their occupation (i.e., those with at least 10 years of exposure), they may remain in a state of chronic hyperconnectivity making them susceptible to neurodegeneration and brain atrophy which can contribute to the development of hypoconnectivity due to atrophied connections. However, these studies have led to additional questions, such as what would be expected in terms of the neural response in already experienced military personnel when exposed to a recent and concentrated regimen of LLB, and the inflection point in the years or amount of LLB exposure when chronic hyperconnectivity transitions into hypoconnectivity. Therefore, further research is required to consolidate the limited literature in this field and better understand how the impact of repetitive LLB exposure on brain connectivity maps onto the hyperconnectivity phenomenon.

Present study

In this study, we investigated whether blast-exposed individuals, comprised of military breachers and snipers, exhibit distinct neural connectivity patterns due to chronic LLB exposure over their occupational careers, compared to sex- and age-matched military controls. Participants underwent a resting state functional magnetic resonance imaging (rs-fMRI) brain scan before and after a training course. We used graph theory to study the whole brain network globally and at each node, since we did not have any a priori hypothesis on the specific brain networks or regions most likely to be implicated by LLB. The pre-course brain scans were analyzed first to examine the neural effects of chronic LLB exposure involving breachers and snipers compared to sex- and age-matched military controls, while the pre- and post-course scans were compared to examine the neural effects of a recent regimen of LLB exposure. Consistent with the hyperconnectivity literature, we hypothesized that compared to sex- and age-matched military controls, blast-exposed individuals would exhibit hypoconnectivity from chronic LLB exposure since they may have already transitioned to this state after years of chronic hyperconnectivity from numerous years of breaching or sniping experience (Hypothesis #1). In a similar vein, we hypothesized that compared to their own pre-course scans, blast-exposed individuals would exhibit hyperconnectivity from a recent period of LLB exposure, analogous to what has been reported in the acute mTBI literature (Hypothesis #2). Collectively, the findings from this study will provide a better understanding of the effects of LLB on military brain health and contribute towards a preliminary framework of the neural signatures associated with chronic and recent LLB exposures.

Methods

Participants and general procedure

Eighty-two participants provided written informed consent to participate in a study approved by the Defence Research and Development Canada's Human Research Ethics Committee (DRDC HREC). Canadian Armed Forces (CAF) breachers (n = 26) and snipers (n = 20) with extensive exposure to low-level occupational blast from training and operations were recruited for this study. Age- and sex-matched CAF military controls (n = 36) without an occupational history in breaching and/or sniping were also recruited. Participants were excluded if they had any contraindications to magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) (e.g., metal implants or claustrophobia). To foster transparency, requests for data can be made to the corresponding author (TKL).

Participants were informed during recruitment that the study consisted of two sessions. In the initial session (i.e., pre-course), participants completed a questionnaire about their occupational history, such as years of service in the military and years of experience working with explosives. Given the important distinction between low-level and high-level blast in this study, we also probed each participant's blast exposure history and whether they were deployed to a warzone during their career. Participants were asked to report whether they experienced various head injuries in their lifetime such as a concussion or motor vehicle accident, to better characterize the prevalence of pre-existing head injuries in our sample. Participants also underwent an MRI brain scan (procedure described below) during the session. Following the initial session, the blast-exposed individuals participated in a training exercise in which they were exposed to a concentrated regimen of low-level blast events as part of their routine military training. While specific details of the training were classified, general information about the low-level blast exposures were gathered from informal discussions and logbook reviews from a subset of breachers and snipers. The breachers used various explosive charges ranging from 0.08 to 0.7 lbs of trinitrotoluene (TNT) for indoor breaching and 0.5 to 14 lbs of TNT for outdoor breaching. Over the course of their training, the breachers completed at least 50 breaching exercises (indoor and outdoor combined) using ≤1 lb of TNT and over 10 breaching exercises (outdoor only) using >2 lbs of TNT. The snipers used the 0.308, 0.338, and 0.50 caliber rifles and fired an average of 1900 rounds totaled across all three rifle types. After the final training exercise, the blast-exposed individuals returned within 7 days of the conclusion of the course for their second study session (i.e., post-course) and underwent the same MRI procedure as the first session. The average duration between the first and second study sessions for the blast-exposed group was 80 days (SD = 36.9). The sex- and age-matched military controls followed the same procedure as the blast-exposed group, but were instructed to perform their regular typical occupational routine between the two sessions. The average duration between the first and second study sessions for the control group was 54 days (SD = 36.5).

MRI acquisition

Two MRI facilities in Ontario, Canada were used in the acquisition of participant brain scans. The first site was the York University MRI Facility in Toronto that used a 3.0 T Siemens Prisma Fit MRI scanner (Erlangen, Germany), while the second site was the Royal Ottawa Mental Health Centre in Ottawa that used a 3.0 T Siemens MAGNETOM Biograph mMR MRI-PET scanner (Erlangen, Germany). Participants who underwent the two MRI scans for this study attended the same MRI facility at both time points. For the blast exposed group, 33 participants (71.7%) were scanned at site 1 whereas 13 participants (28.3%) were scanned at site 2. For the control group, 23 participants (63.9%) were scanned at site 1 while 13 participants (36.1%) were scanned at site 2. At both sites, we acquired a high resolution T1-weighted structural brain scan along with a rs-fMRI scan, the latter of which required participants to keep their eyes open and fixated on a cross. MRI analysis

Data were preprocessed and analyzed with the CONN v21.a toolbox [Whitfield-Gabrieli and Nieto-Castanon, 2012] and Statistical Parametric Mapping (SPM12; www.fil.ion.ucl.ac.uk/spm/). The preprocessing pipeline first involved a realignment of the rs-fMRI data to a reference image, specifically the first scan from the initial session. This enabled the detection of outlier scans from the rs-fMRI data, which are acquired volumes that exceed a threshold of 0.9 mm framewise displacement or 5 standard deviations in the global blood oxygen level-dependent (BOLD) signal change. Next, slice-timing correction to the middle slice was applied to account for temporal misalignments between the various slices of the rs-fMRI data. The structural and rs-fMRI data were then registered to the standard Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) 2 mm template and a segmentation process was applied to obtain masks of grey matter, white matter, and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF). Lastly, the rs-fMRI data were spatially smoothed using a 6 mm full width at half maximum Gaussian kernel. After these preprocessing steps, the rs-fMRI data were passed through a denoising pipeline to remove potential confounds from the data. This involved a regression of nuisance variables from the data, which included the white matter time series, CSF time series, head motion parameters, outlier scans, and session and task effects. A temporal band-pass filter was applied such that temporal frequencies outside the 0.008 to 0.09 Hz range were removed from the BOLD signal which reduced the potential impact of noise sources (e.g., physiological) on the data and enabled the slow-frequency resting state fluctuations to be solely analyzed.

To conduct a graph theory analysis, a whole brain network was derived from a combination of the Harvard-Oxford Atlas in FMRIB Software Library (FSL) [Jenkinson et al., 2012], which comprised of 91 cortical regions and 15 subcortical regions, along with the Automated Anatomical Atlas [Tzourio-Mazoyer et al., 2002] which contained 26 cerebellar regions, for a total of 132 network nodes. To define the network edges, the mean BOLD time series for each network node was extracted and used to compute a Pearson's correlation matrix (i.e., graph) comprising of every pair of nodes for each participant. A network cost of 0.15 was applied to each participant's correlation matrix as a threshold to reduce the number of false positive edges. Thus, only 15% of the strongest connections in each participant's graph were used in the subsequent graph theory analysis.

The topological properties of each participant's graph were computed across the whole brain network and locally at each node. The graph measures of interest included global efficiency, clustering coefficient, and betweenness centrality. Global efficiency estimates the capacity of information travel between nodes, and is precisely defined as the average of the inverse distances between all node pairs in a graph. Clustering coefficient measures connection density as it represents the proportion of a node's direct neighboring nodes that are connected to each other. Betweenness centrality illustrates a node's importance since it is the proportion of times a node is implicated in the shortest path length between any two nodes in a graph. All measures range from 0 to 1, with values closer to 1 indicating greater efficiency, clustering, or centrality.

To confirm the pattern of results obtained from the graph theory analysis, a principal components analysis (PCA) using the CONN v21.a toolbox was also conducted post-hoc. Here, the rs-fMRI data was concatenated across participants in order to identify common resting state networks (i.e., principal components) across the participant group before the common networks were reconstructed at the participant-level. Eight common networks (default mode, sensorimotor, visual, salience, dorsal attention, frontoparietal, language, and cerebellar) were identified via PCA using a dimensionality reduction of 64 (default value). Comparisons of the intra-network connectivity between the breachers, snipers, and controls were computed to determine the brain regions, if any, within each network impacted from LLB exposure.

Statistical analysis

A comparison of the demographic variables was first computed between the breachers and snipers, and then between the CAF controls and each occupational sub-group. An independent samples t-test was used for continuous variables whereas a chi-square (χ2) test was used for categorical variables. Demographic comparisons were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05. For the graph theory measures, we first compared the blast-exposed group (i.e., breachers and snipers together) versus CAF controls to evaluate whether any network connectivity differences were common across all blast-exposed participants. Next, we separated the blast-exposed group by occupation and conducted three sub-analyses which included: 1) breachers versus CAF controls, 2) snipers versus CAF controls, and 3) breachers versus snipers. The purpose of these sub-analyses was to pinpoint whether a specific occupational group was the primary contributor to the main comparison (i.e., blast-exposed versus CAF controls) or had network connectivity differences that were masked when collapsing across occupation. Thus, an occupational group (e.g., breachers) with larger graph theory values (i.e., efficiency, clustering, and/or centrality) over another occupational group (e.g., controls) would indicate hyperconnectivity for the former whereas smaller graph theory values would indicate hypoconnectivity. To compare the graph theory measures from chronic and recent repetitive LLB exposure, a one-way analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) for Group at pre-course along with a two-way ANCOVA between Group and Time were conducted, respectively. Nine covariates were included in the ANCOVAs: age, education level, years of military service, years of explosives experience, years of breaching experience, years of sniping experience, concussion history, warzone deployment, and scanner site. Graph theory measures for the whole brain were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05. Graph theory measures for the network nodes applied a false discovery rate (FDR) correction to account for multiple comparisons which were considered statistically significant at p-FDR < 0.05 (Benjamini and Hochberg, 1995). For the networks derived from PCA, one-way and two-way ANCOVAs, as described previously, were computed to examine whether intra-network connectivity differences existed between occupations. Similar to the graph theory measures for the network nodes, only voxels with intra-network connectivity values meeting the p-FDR < 0.05 threshold were considered statistically significant. All statistics were performed in SPSS version 22.0 (IBM Corp, Armonk, NY, USA).

Network connectivity from chronic repetitive LLB exposure

Collectively, the blast-exposed group had a higher global efficiency, which is indicative of hyperconnectivity, across the whole brain at pre-course relative to controls [t(71) = 2.39, p = 0.02] (Fig. 1A). When the blast-exposed group was split into subgroups of breachers and snipers, this finding was no longer statistically significant for either group, thereby suggesting that both occupational groups had a significant contribution towards the whole-brain global efficiency result. At the nodal level, the breachers exhibited greater efficiency (i.e., hyperconnectivity) in the bilateral fusiform gyri nodes [left: t(51) = 3.52, p-FDR = 0.06; right: t(51) = 3.80, p-FDR = 0.02] relative to controls (Fig. 1B) and in vermis VI [t(35) = 3.78, p-FDR = 0.04] relative to snipers (Fig. 1C). The snipers did not have any network nodes with statistically significant greater global efficiency compared to controls or breachers. Network connectivity from recent repetitive LLB exposure

Compared with controls, the blast-exposed group had reduced global efficiency across the whole brain post-course relative to pre-course [t(71) = −2.10, p = 0.03]. However, when the blast-exposed group was separated by occupation, this finding remained statistically significant with the breachers [t(51) = −2.69, p = 0.008] (Fig. 2A), but not with the snipers [t(45) = −0.30, p = 0.76], thereby suggesting that reduced whole-brain global efficiency (i.e., hypoconnectivity) was driven primarily by the breachers. A post-hoc examination of the chronic effects of LLB exposure on the PCA networks found that the superior parietal lobule (SPL) (BA 7, X = −12, Y = −62, Z = 68, T = 4.74, p-FDR = 0.03) exhibited greater connectivity (i.e., hyperconnectivity) with other regions in the frontoparietal network in breachers at pre-course compared to controls (Fig. 3A). Discussion

In the present study, we investigated how chronic and recent repetitive LLB exposure affects neural network connectivity in blast-exposed members and CAF controls. All participants were evaluated at two time points. The breachers and snipers underwent a rs-fMRI scan prior to starting a course for a snapshot of their chronic repetitive LLB exposure due to their occupational career, and after a training course in which they experienced a recent, but concentrated, regimen of repeated LLB. In contrast, controls were instructed to maintain their typical occupational routine between the two assessment points. Graph theory, which comprised of measures for network efficiency, clustering coefficient, and betweenness centrality, was used to analyze whether network topology differed across the whole brain and at individual nodes between the blast-exposed and controls. Greater efficiency, clustering, or centrality in one group over another would indicate hyperconnectivity for the former over the latter. Conversely, reduced efficiency, clustering, or centrality in one group over another would indicate hypoconnectivity for the former over the latter. We hypothesized that compared to sex- and age-matched military controls, the blast group would exhibit hypoconnectivity from chronic repetitive LLB exposure (Hypothesis #1). This prediction was not confirmed. That is, the blast-exposed group, comprised of both breachers and snipers, had higher global efficiency across the entire brain, with breachers exhibiting hyperconnectivity in the bilateral fusiform gyri compared to controls and in vermis VI compared to snipers at pre-course. We also hypothesized that compared to their own pre-course scans, blast-exposed members would exhibit hyperconnectivity following a recent regimen of repetitive LLB exposure (Hypothesis #2). This hypothesis was also not confirmed. Instead, the breachers displayed lower global efficiency across the whole brain, with hypoconnectivity in the pre-SMA and right fusiform gyrus, post-course compared to pre-course, which was not observed in controls or snipers. Collectively, our findings suggest that neural network connectivity varies as a function of repeated LLB exposure length, with chronic exposure associated with hyperconnectivity whereas recent exposure is associated with hypoconnectivity.

Connectivity differences in blast-exposed individuals from chronic repetitive LLB exposure

In contrast with our hypothesis, we found that breachers and snipers displayed hyperconnectivity, specifically higher global efficiency throughout the brain, relative to controls at pre-course. Key demographic variables, such as age, sex, years of military service, and concussion history were not different between the groups. There were, however, statistically significant differences in terms of years of experience working with explosives, breaching, sniping, and number of warzone deployments in blast-exposed individuals, all of which are characteristic of the breacher and sniper occupations. While we cannot tease apart the differential effects of low-level and high-level blast on the brain in the present study, it is evident that an occupation that involves functioning in an environment with relatively constant blast exposure is associated with hyperconnectivity. Hillary and Grafman [2017] suggest that a transient state of hyperconnectivity due to an insult to the head is a result of the utilization of more neural resources to re-establish communication in networks disrupted from brain injury. Yet a prolonged elevation of neural resources (i.e., chronic hyperconnectivity) is detrimental to the brain and is hypothesized to lead to brain atrophy and hypoconnectivity [Hillary and Grafman, 2017], analogous to Alzheimer's Disease where the early stages are marked by a hyperconnected brain state that then transitions to a hypoconnected state with neurodegeneration and brain atrophy in the later stages of disease [Targa Dias Anastacio et al., 2022]. However, the blast-exposed participants in our study exhibited hyperconnectivity despite frequent blast exposure accumulated over their occupational career. Our results suggest that repeated LLB exposure may elicit a different neural response than the typical hyper-to hypo-connectivity trajectory seen in other brain injuries/diseases (e.g., mTBI or Alzheimer's Disease). Alternatively, our sample of blast-exposed participants may not have accumulated enough chronic LLB experience yet to exhibit chronic hypoconnectivity. This may explain why our findings align with Stone et al. [2020] who reported hyperconnectivity resulting from chronic repeated LLB exposure, but not Gilmore et al. [2024] who reported hypoconnectivity, as the former included participants with a minimum of 4 years of military experience whereas the latter included military members with at least 10 years of LLB exposure. The blast-exposed group in our study possessed an average of 5.5 years of breaching experience, which lands closer to the study participants' 4 years of breaching in Stone et al. [2020]. It is possible then that a decade of repeated LLB exposure, as found in Gilmore et al. [2024], may represent an inflection point where chronic hyperconnectivity becomes hypoconnectivity and the potential emergence of other neurological symptoms. Nonetheless, further research is required to evaluate the validity of this theory and determine whether a dose-response relationship exists between the amount of cumulative blast exposure and the connectivity profile in individuals. In a similar vein, the rigorous selection that these breachers and snipers underwent in order to work in these occupations may indirectly select for individuals with a neurocognitive profile resilient to brain injury. Studies in military members found that those with higher intelligence quotient (IQ) and verbal skills predicted success on selection and qualification courses for the Special Forces [Farina et al., 2019; Pattyn et al., 2024], which can lead to roles as breachers and snipers. Indeed, in the Canadian context, there is evidence to suggest that members of the Special Operations Forces have fluid intelligence scores that are higher than population norms [Vartanian et al., 2016]. Higher IQ has been associated with reduced age-related cognitive declines [Gazes et al., 2023], and IQ is often used as a proxy for cognitive reserve [Valenzuela and Sachdev, 2006], defined as the lifetime intellectual activities and environmental factors that alter the expected relationship between a person's functional impairment and their underlying brain pathology [Barulli and Stern, 2013]. While it remains to be seen whether enhanced cognitive reserve could function as a protective factor to delay or mitigate the transition to hypoconnectivity in blast-exposed individuals, it is important to consider how individual differences impact the connectivity profile, particularly if this is ever used to support clinical or occupational decisions.

At the nodal level, we found that the bilateral fusiform gyri were hyperconnected in the breachers at pre-course relative to controls. The fusiform gyrus is involved in categorizing visual information, such as faces [Grill-Spector and Weiner, 2014], bodies [Peelen and Downing, 2005], and emotional stimuli [Frank et al., 2019], and processes high spatial frequency information important for interpreting fine details (e.g., reading) [Roberts et al., 2013]. From a military perspective, visual processing represents a fundamental component in basic military tasks, such as target identification and the recognition between civilians and adversarial forces during combat. Furthermore, the fusiform gyrus has been associated with nociception, particularly pain that is chronic in nature [Glass et al., 2011; Lo Buono et al., 2017; Li et al., 2018]. Further work is required to replicate this finding in other breacher samples and examine whether fusiform gyri hyperconnectivity correlates with visual processing ability as it could possess merit in being a valuable biomarker of repeated LLB exposure.

A comparison between occupations yielded vermis VI hyperconnectivity in breachers compared to snipers. Vermis VI is located in the cerebellum which has traditionally been associated with movement coordination and the regulation of balance and posture. More recently, however, the cerebellum has been found to be implicated in other functions including attention, language, and vision [King et al., 2019]. In particular, vermis VI has a role in executing saccadic eye movements [King et al., 2019; van Es et al., 2019] which are important for military members to scan an environment for targets and focus on threats. While this finding has been interpreted from the perspective of vermis VI hyperconnectivity in breachers, implicit in this result is that the snipers have reduced efficiency, and hence hypoconnectivity, of vermis VI relative to the breachers. However, it is unclear how this may map onto neurological or performance differences between the two occupations. Therefore, future studies are required to link variations in vermis VI connectivity with saccadic performance to better understand how hyperconnectivity (or hypoconnectivity) influences brain health as this finding demonstrates that breachers and snipers have distinct neural connectivity profiles which manifest over time likely due to their different training regimens and operational LLB exposure levels. Nonetheless, the identification of the cerebellum here further validates the work by Peskind and colleagues who have consistently found that repetitive blast exposure in military Veterans is associated with reduced cerebellar uptake of glucose on positron emission tomography (PET) scans [Peskind et al., 2011; Meabon et al., 2016; Meabon et al., 2020]. Of significance, their work has highlighted a negative dose-response relationship whereby a greater number of blast exposures correlates with lower glucose metabolism in the cerebellum, which is a marker of neuronal activity [Meabon et al., 2016]. This dose-response relationship aligns well with our observation of cerebellar hypoconnectivity in snipers, but appears to be at odds with cerebellar hyperconnectivity in breachers. Future work is thus required to tease apart the relationship involving low-level blast exposure, cerebellar connectivity, and glucose metabolism.

Connectivity differences in breachers from recent repetitive LLB exposure

Contrary to our hypothesis, we found that only breachers exhibited hypoconnectivity following a recent period of repeated LLB exposure, with reduced efficiency throughout the brain relative to their pre-course scan. Our finding suggests that recent repetitive LLB exposure may impact the ability of the brain to transfer information between its network nodes. Interestingly, our findings here deviate from Yuan et al. [2019] who examined the neural connectivity in SWAT personnel after a single day of breacher training and found that their participants exhibited hyperconnectivity. This difference can be attributed to the fact that Yuan et al. [2019] studied the neural response following a single day of multiple LLBs, whereas our breacher participants were exposed to multiple LLBs over a 2.5-month period. Moreover, it is unclear how much prior LLB exposure the participants in Yuan et al. [2019] received as one's cumulative history of blast exposure may greatly impact one's immediate neural response to subsequent LLB. Indeed, people with chronic LLB exposure show physiological differences relative to those who are relatively unexposed [Tschiffely et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2020; Rhind et al., 2024]. Thus, the cumulative effects of LLB may mediate whether an individual exhibits hyper- or hypoconnectivity following recent LLB exposure. Alternatively, there is reason to believe that both studies are not discrepant, but rather, complementary to each other as participants may initially exhibit hyperconnectivity when exposed to a short and recent LLB (as seen in Yuan et al. [2019] after one day of LLB exposure), but then transition to hypoconnectivity after multiple days, weeks, or months of LLB exposure (as seen in our findings here). This transition could be hastened in military members with greater cumulative LLB exposure as they would be at an advanced stage of exposure burden and become sensitive to the impacts of blast. Taken together, our work and Yuan et al. [2019] serve as an entry point to further study the nuances related to recent LLB exposure and neural connectivity as a function of cumulative blast history.

At the nodal level, we found that recent repetitive LLB exposure also yielded reduced efficiency in the pre-SMA and right fusiform gyrus compared to pre-course in breachers. The pre-SMA is involved in planning motor movements [Deiber et al., 1999] which is important in learning and conducting various tasks. Future studies may consider the use of an assessment that specifically targets the process and execution of complex motor tasks as it may show an association with pre-SMA efficiency in breachers. In regards to the fusiform gyrus, this node displayed connectivity differences in both chronic and recent repetitive LLB exposure. It is unclear whether a hyper- or hypo-connected fusiform gyrus is detrimental, beneficial, or perhaps even benign to brain health. However, the fact that this brain region was found in two separate analyses suggests that this may be an important area to monitor in future studies as it could be sensitive to the amount of LLB exposure, both transiently and cumulatively, which influences whether breachers elicit hypoconnectivity or hyperconnectivity.

When viewing the findings through an occupational lens, it was primarily the breachers, instead of the snipers, who exhibited significant network connectivity differences from controls. This distinction between breachers and snipers can be attributed to the fact that each occupation predominately experiences a different type of blast wave. While LLB is generated from both the use of explosive breaching charges and sniper rifle firing, the blast wave profile for each differs with respect to its peak overpressure and total positive impulse [Kamimori et al., 2017; Lang et al., 2018; Skotak et al., 2019; Thangavelu et al., 2020; Wiri et al., 2023]. For example, one study has reported that explosive breaching wall charges elicited impulse values much greater than recoil from a 0.50 caliber weapon (82 kPa-ms versus 6.9 kPa-ms, respectively) [Wiri et al., 2023]. This discrepancy in impulse values may, in part, explain why breachers showed more connectivity differences compared to snipers, as the former would have been subjected to LLBs that were more forceful and longer in duration than the latter. In a similar vein, the mechanism by which the LLB passes through the skull and brain will also likely differ between breaching and weapon recoil. Blast waves from breaching create high shear stress in the brain [Amponsah et al., 2025] whereas sniper weapon recoil involves rotational head acceleration [Seeburrun et al., 2024]. Although it remains to be seen which mechanism exerts greater forces on the brain, the LLB quantification literature along with the findings here reinforce the suggestion to study these occupations separately as their exposure profile and neurological impact will likely differ.

Intra-network connectivity differences from chronic and recent repetitive LLB exposure

A critique of the graph theory findings is the fact that our analyses do not accommodate for the heterogeneity of blast exposures within our sample of breachers and snipers. To address this critique, we ran a post-hoc analysis using PCA which enabled us to extract resting state networks common across all participants, irrespective of their LLB exposures, in order to compare how intra-network connectivity patterns differed between occupations. We found that breachers exhibited hyperconnectivity between the SPL and other regions in the frontoparietal network from chronic LLB exposure. In contrast, breachers exhibited hypoconnectivity in the SPL and DLPFC with other regions of the frontoparietal network from recent LLB exposure. In a similar vein, snipers displayed hypoconnectivity between the amygdala and other regions composing the salience network following recent LLB exposure. While the specific brain regions from PCA differ from the graph theory analyses, the pattern of results remain the same. That is, breachers exhibit hyperconnectivity from chronic LLB exposure whereas breachers and snipers both showed hypoconnectivity after recent LLB exposure. Thus, convergence across different analysis techniques offer more validity towards our observations of the connectivity patterns as a function of LLB exposure.

The frontoparietal network is involved in setting task goals [Dosenbach et al., 2007] and controlling other brain networks [Marek and Dosenbach, 2018], which is important for the coordination of higher-level cognitive processes, such as multitasking [Lam et al., 2022]. Both the SPL and DLPFC are key nodes in the frontoparietal network. In particular, the SPL is implicated in visual perception and spatial cognition [Wang et al., 2015] which are important processes that enable breachers to navigate, manipulate objects, and interact with their surroundings. Interestingly, identification of the SPL from our intra-network connectivity analysis in breachers mirrors our graph theory analysis involving the fusiform gyrus also in breachers. Although these are distinct brain regions, both were hyperconnected from chronic LLB exposure, hypoconnected after recent LLB exposure, and both regions play a role in visual processing. The DLPFC is a central region for cognitive control with roles in task switching, working memory, and handling conflicting information [Badre and Wagner, 2004; Barbey et al., 2013]. Our analysis found that the DLPFC was hypoconnected in breachers after recent LLB exposure which may contribute to neurocognitive deficits reported following acute blast exposure [LaValle et al., 2019]. In snipers, hypoconnectivity was found in the salience network, specifically with the amygdala. The salience network detects salient information, such as sudden and unexpected stimuli in the environment [Menon, 2015] with the amygdala having a related role in threat detection [Ohman, 2005]. While the amygdala is often hyperactive due to blast exposure which can lead to post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) [Elder et al., 2014], amygdala hypoconnectivity may suggest impaired threat detection or encoding of fear which was found in rats exposed to repeated LLBs [Vaughn et al., 2025]. Collectively, these results further demonstrate the differing connectivity patterns between breachers and snipers which is reasonable given that their tasks, and the resulting blast waves associated with them, differ between the groups. Further research is necessary to validate these intra-network connectivity patterns and their relationships with behaviour, but these results offer additional support of the notion that functional connectivity is altered from LLB.

Neural connectivity framework for LLB exposure

Collectively, the findings from both recent and chronic repetitive LLB exposures suggest that the neural response from repeated LLB overpressure may be specific to this condition. The impact of LLB exposure is highly complex as the blast wave exerts shearing, tensile, and compressive strains on the brain even without direct impact to the head [Fievisohn et al., 2018]. This is unlike other brain injuries, such as concussion, and as such, comparison with other brain injuries or disease states may obscure the distinct neural patterns found in LLB exposure. For example, connectivity differences are often observed in the prefrontal cortex (PFC) following concussion [Czerniak et al., 2015] and TBI [Leskinen et al., 2025], but this may not necessarily be a hallmark in LLB exposure as found in the graph theory metrics for our sample here. Indeed, Nathan et al. [2016] found that midbrain connectivity in the thalamus could distinguish between military members with blast injury from those with non-blast brain injuries. This suggests that while some studies do observe PFC changes as a result of blast [Stone et al., 2020; Gilmore et al., 2024], this may not be a necessity in LLB exposure. Our work here and others [Nathan et al., 2016] have found that brain regions outside the PFC can also be impacted from blast which showcases the complexity and widespread impact of LLB exposure on the brain.

In a similar vein, Hillary and Grafman's [2017] hyperconnectivity phenomenon indicates that an insult to the brain results in hyperconnectivity as a transient state and would progress toward hypoconnectivity in the long-term if multiple insults are sustained. However, this may not be fully applicable in repetitive LLB exposure as the opposite was observed in the military members in our study. That is, a recent but concentrated regimen of repeated LLB elicits hypoconnectivity whereas chronic repetitive LLB exposure accumulated throughout a member's career leads to hyperconnectivity. Recent repetitive LLB exposures may lead to a hypoconnected brain state, whereby the brain may use pre-existing connection pathways that are less efficient to adapt to the subtle brain injury instead of devoting significant resources towards the rewiring of neural connections. Over time, however, ongoing exposure from recent and concentrated repetitive LLB accumulates in the brain which eventually leads to hyperconnectivity in blast-exposed members whereby their brains are required to rewire themselves to compensate from the chronic brain damage to achieve the same output as someone without any repeated LLB exposure in their lifetime. It is unclear how much repeated LLB exposure one needs to accumulate in their lifetime or how long one needs to remain in a hypoconnected state before hyperconnectivity sets in, as the exposure trajectory may differ across individuals. Moreover, the transition from hypoconnectivity to hyperconnectivity in LLB exposure may not be the end state as chronic hyperconnectivity has been linked to neurodegeneration, brain atrophy, and cognitive decline. This may cause individuals to revert to a hypoconnected state again, with this shift becoming irreversible. A prospective study represents the missing piece where breachers and snipers are followed at the onset of their careers and monitored at regular intervals. This may not necessarily be feasible given that military members are frequently deployed overseas with uncertain timelines for retesting, but additional monitoring is a critical next step to understand how repeated LLB impacts the brain both in the immediate and long term.

To the best of our knowledge, our study here is one of the few, if any, that have used the same participants to examine neural connectivity across different LLB exposure timescales. The graph theory metrics and PCA network results both suggest that the connectivity patterns will vary depending on the occupation and timescale under examination. Thus, the impact of LLB on the brain is a dynamic process and there is merit in using rs-fMRI to monitor this neurological progression and its relationship with brain health. While our findings provide more insight into the neural signature of LLB exposure, questions about the role of a person's cumulative blast history and the presence of a critical transition period in the brain must be addressed in order to develop a robust neural connectivity framework for LLB exposure.

Limitations and future directions

Our study contains a few limitations. First, we could not obtain a measure of the precise amount of repeated LLB exposure that the blast-exposed members experienced during their training course, as this was classified information. In a similar vein, a measure of the cumulative blast exposure would have been ideal for the analyses involving chronic repetitive LLB exposure. While we probed for years of experience in explosives, breaching, and sniping, along with history of head injuries, this information did not directly address the amount of blast exposure military members accumulated over the course of their lifetime. Relatedly, some controls had pre-existing breaching experience so it would have been beneficial to know how their blast exposure history compared with the breachers and snipers. However, given that the controls were not breaching on a frequent basis for their occupation and their most recent blast exposure was on average 5 years ago, this pre-existing breaching experience in the controls is expected to have a relatively small impact on the findings. Thus, a measure such as the Generalized Blast Exposure Value (GBEV) [Modica et al., 2021] could be used in future studies to better assess the relationship between cumulative blast exposure history and neural network properties. Second, we did not examine how the pre- and post-course graph theory metrics map onto behaviour, if at all. While the focus of this study was on changes in brain connectivity patterns following repeated LLB exposure, future work can assess whether these neural changes also manifest in any observable behavioural differences between the groups. Third, our study only contained data from two time points which makes it difficult to draw conclusions about the trajectory of neural connectivity following a recent and concentrated regimen of repetitive LLB. It is currently unknown how the brain responds during the acute stage (i.e., within 24 h following the final repetitive LLB event) and how long the hypoconnected state persists in blast-exposed individuals. Collectively, this study sheds additional light on the impact of repeated LLB on brain health, but much remains to be learned about the immediate and chronic effects of occupational blast exposure in military members.

Conclusion

Collectively, these findings reinforce the neurological impact of repeated LLB exposure. Breachers and snipers displayed hyperconnectivity across the entire brain attributable to chronic, occupationally incurred LLB exposure. In contrast, following a recent but intensive regimen of repetitive LLB during training, only breachers showed hypoconnectivity throughout the brain. Notably, this pattern diverges from the hyperconnectivity profile commonly reported in other neurological conditions, suggesting that both the mechanism of LLB exposure and the brain's response to it may be specific to this condition. Further research is needed to validate these findings and the framework proposed here which can be used to eventually inform and optimize assessment and treatment of the cumulative LLB effects in military members.
