Comparison of the Quantitative Myasthenia Gravis and Myasthenia Gravis Activity of Daily Living Scores From a Clinical Practice Perspective
Authors: Wanqing Wu, Malin Petersson, Fredrik Piehl, Susanna Brauner
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroduction/AimsMyasthenia gravis (MG) is characterized by fluctuating symptoms, making the assessment of disease activity challenging. Discordance between the clinician‐reported quantitative myasthenia gravis (QMG) score and the patient‐reported MG activity of daily living (MG‐ADL) has previously been observed. We aimed to compare QMG and MG‐ADL in a clinical practice setting and to relate to the quality‐of‐life instrument myasthenia gravis quality of life 15 (MG‐QoL‐15).MethodsMG patients simultaneously assessed by QMG, MG‐ADL, and MG‐QoL‐15 (n = 177) identified in the Swedish MG registry were included. The distribution of total scores for QMG and MG‐ADL and per‐item score discordance was investigated using the Chi‐squared test and paired t‐tests. Multivariate linear regression models were constructed to identify factors associated with QMG and MG‐ADL.ResultsMG‐ADL exhibited a lower floor effect compared to QMG (14% vs. 28%, p = 0.002) and patients reported significantly worse scores with MG‐ADL than QMG, particularly in bulbar and respiratory items (for breathing item 38% MG‐ADL > QMG vs. 5% MG‐ADL < QMG score). Multivariate analysis revealed that new‐onset status, MG‐QoL‐15 score, and a history of prednisolone treatment were significantly associated with QMG scores, whereas only MG‐QoL‐15 was significantly associated with MG‐ADL. These associations were driven by symptom‐associated questions in MG‐QoL‐15, while the emotional well‐being questions were associated with neither QMG nor MG‐ADL.DiscussionWe observed that MG‐ADL more readily identified residual symptoms in patients with mild disease activity compared to QMG; however, with a stronger influence of quality of life, suggesting a complementarity of the two scores in clinical practice.Keywords: MG‐ADL, MG‐QoL‐15, myasthenia gravis, outcome measure, QMG

Introduction

Fluctuating muscular weakness is a hallmark of myasthenia gravis (MG), posing a challenge for the reliable assessment of disease activity [1]. Both clinician‐reported and patient‐reported disease activity scales have been developed and used in clinical trials and routine practice, each with its inherent merits and drawbacks. Among the most used, the quantitative myasthenia gravis (QMG) score is a clinician‐reported objective measure, reflecting the symptoms at the time of assessment [2, 3]. The MG activity of daily living (MG‐ADL) score is a patient‐reported scale in which patients are instructed to recall symptoms in the last 7 or 14 days [4, 5]. Lastly, the Myasthenia Gravis Composite (MGC) score was constructed by combining both clinician‐reported and patient‐reported items [6, 7].

Historically, QMG has been used as the primary outcome measure for trials [8, 9, 10, 11]. However, since the eculizumab phase III trial [12], MG‐ADL has been the preferred primary outcome [12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20], reflecting the regulatory authorities’ preference for patient‐centered measures [21, 22]. It should nevertheless be noted that the eculizumab trial failed to meet its primary endpoint. Although the predefined worst‐rank ANCOVA approach might partially explain these insignificant finding, the secondary outcome, QMG, analyzed also by worst‐rank ANCOVA, favored eculizumab over placebo, highlighting a potentially important discrepancy between MG‐ADL and QMG [12]. Subsequent trials found significant treatment‐control differences in both MG‐ADL and QMG, with QMG demonstrating seemingly greater power to detect differences [13, 14, 15].

Further, a wide range of correlations between QMG and MG‐ADL has been reported, ranging from weak to strong (r = 0.33–0.85) [23]. Several factors might contribute to this; the self‐reported nature of patient‐reported outcomes may result in non‐disease‐specific factors, such as quality of life (QoL) and psychological factors influencing patients' responses [24]. On the other hand, the fluctuating nature of MG symptoms may result in reduced accuracy of QMG, which, as a point estimate, may produce varying assessments of disease activity compared to the retrospective, period‐based measurement of MG‐ADL. Moreover, the two scales represent muscle group involvement differently; in QMG, 8 of 13 items (62%) assess non‐ocular and non‐bulbar weakness, whereas in MG‐ADL, only 3 of 8 items (38%) focus on these symptoms [25].

Given the discordances that have been reported between the scales and that previous studies have primarily been conducted in clinical trial populations, which tend to include patients with moderate to severe disease [26, 27, 28, 29], we here aimed to compare MG‐ADL to QMG head‐to‐head in a clinical practice setting, and to explore the factors associated with the disease activity scores.

Methods

Study Population

The Swedish MG registry (MG‐reg) was initiated in 2011 as a voluntary, publicly funded, nationwide register, open to all neurology clinics in Sweden, comprising data on demographics, diagnostic workup, as well as longitudinal disease activity and therapeutic interventions. All patients in the registry have a physician‐verified diagnosis.

Participants from MG‐reg who were tested with QMG, MG‐ADL, and myasthenia gravis quality of life 15 (MG‐QoL‐15) at a single visit were selected to form the main study cohort, each contributing one assessment. Two additional cohorts were formed, including MG patients concomitantly assessed with QMG and MGC or MGC and MG‐ADL, respectively. Lastly, a separate cohort was created with patients who completed QMG, MG‐ADL, and EQ‐5D‐3L on the same day.

Disease Activity and Quality of Life Measures

The QMG score comprises 13 items, each scored 0–3, yielding a total range of 0–39 points [2, 3]. The MG‐ADL score consists of 8 items, with each item scored 0–3 and totaling a range of 0–24 [4]. MGC has 10 items, which are weighted based on their association with severe relapses, with a total score of a maximum 50 [6]. For all three measures, higher scores indicate higher disease activity.

MG‐QoL‐15 is a 15‐item MG‐specific health‐related QoL measure, where higher scores indicate lower QoL. Each item is scored 0‐4, with a maximum score of 60 [30, 31]. EQ‐5D‐3L is a standardized health‐related QoL score, where results typically range from 0 (death) to 1 (perfect health) [32].

The MG‐ADL, MG‐QoL‐15, and EQ‐5D‐3L scales were translated into Swedish for the REGAIN trial in 2014 and have been used in the Swedish MG registry since [12]. The use of clinician‐reported outcomes varies across Sweden, where the majority use QMG, and MGC is preferred in Gothenburg, Sweden's second‐largest city. All clinicians are recommended to use MG‐ADL as well as MG‐QoL‐15 and EQ‐5D‐3L; however, its implementation remains at their discretion.

Data Collection and Variables

Basic characteristics and disease activity scores were retrieved from MG‐reg. Index date was defined as the earliest date the patient was tested with disease activity and/or QoL scales simultaneously. New‐onset status was defined as a maximum 12 months from symptom onset to index date, and diagnostic delay as the time from symptom onset to diagnosis. MG patients with a diagnosis of thymoma were defined as thymoma‐associated MG (TAMG). Early‐onset MG (EOMG) and late‐onset MG (LOMG) were defined as non‐thymomatous patients with anti‐acetylcholine receptor (AChR) autoantibodies and an age of onset of 18–49 and ≥ 50 years old, respectively. Remaining patients with antibodies against muscle‐specific tyrosine kinase (MuSK) or low‐density lipoprotein receptor‐related protein 4 were defined as having other antibody‐related MG. Patients with no detectable antibodies against AChR or MuSK were defined as double seronegative MG.

Statistical Analysis

Characteristics of the study population were summarized using means (standard deviations, SD) and counts with frequencies (%).

Potential floor and ceiling effects (F/C effects) were defined as the proportion of participants scoring the lowest (floor) or highest (ceiling) possible score with the given tool [33]. Histogram plots of the total score of QMG and MG‐ADL were fit to visualize F/C effects, with density curves fitted using kernel density estimation. The proportion of patients scoring 0 (floor effect), in remission (defined as scoring 0 and no rescue treatment in the last 3 months before index) or having achieved minimal manifestations (MM, defined as QMG < 3 or MG‐ADL < 2 and no rescue treatment in the last 3 months before index), was compared using the Chi‐squared test.

The distribution of item scores was visualized by bar plots by grouping QMG and MG‐ADL items according to muscle group; MG‐ADL talk and QMG count, MG‐ADL chew and QMG grimace, MG‐ADL and QMG swallow, MG‐ADL breath and QMG forced vital capacity, MG‐ADL brush and QMG arm, MG‐ADL arise and QMG leg, MG‐ADL and QMG double vision, MG‐ADL and QMG eyelid droop. The proportions of patients scoring 0 in each group were compared by Chi‐squared tests. Frequencies of higher, equal, or lower QMG scores vs. MG‐ADL scores across these muscle groups were described using percentages (%). Similar analyses were repeated for MGC and MG‐ADL, and QMG and MGC as a comparison. The mean (SD) of QMG and MG‐ADL score per item was reported, and the difference was computed and compared by paired t test.

Multivariate linear regression models were employed to explore factors associated with QMG or MG‐ADL. To allow for direct comparison of effects (β estimates) across models, MG‐ADL was scaled from 0 to 39 to match QMG (scaled MG‐ADL = MG‐ADL × 39/24). To explore potential overlap between items in MG‐QoL‐15 and disease activity scales, the MG‐QoL‐15 was, in Model 2, replaced by its two categories: MG‐QoL‐15 symptoms [calculated as the average value of the items trouble using eyes (item 2), eating (item 3), speaking (item 9), walking (item 12), and grooming (item 15)] and MG‐QoL‐15 emotional well‐being [average values of the items frustrated (item 1), depressed (item 11), and overwhelmed (item 14)]. A similar multivariate linear regression model was constructed with EQ‐5D‐3L in the cohort of patients who were tested with QMG, MG‐ADL, and EQ‐5D‐3L.

All correlations were evaluated by Pearson correlation coefficients, with correlation < 0.20 considered as very weak, 0.20–0.39 weak, 0.40–0.59 moderate, 0.60–0.79 strong, and > 0.80 very strong [34].

All statistical analyses were performed using RStudio (2024.09.0) with R (version 4.4.1).

Distribution and Score Differences Between QMG and MG‐ADL

A larger potential floor effect of the QMG scores was observed compared to the MG‐ADL, which was more evenly distributed (28 vs. 14%, p = 0.002; Figure 1A). More patients were classified as being in remission or having achieved MM based on QMG compared to MG‐ADL. No ceiling effects were observed for either score. The difference in potential floor effects was mainly driven by respiratory and bulbar items, as well as diplopia (Figure 1B).

As a reference, we compared the distribution of MG‐ADL and QMG to MGC in cohorts concomitantly assessed with MGC and MG‐ADL (n = 85) or QMG and MGC (n = 245), respectively (Figure S1, Table S1). While the correlation between MG‐ADL and MGC (r = 0.71, p < 0.001) was better than that of QMG (r = 0.61, p < 0.001), more patients were classified as having achieved MM based on MGC compared to MG‐ADL. QMG and MGC, on the other hand, exhibited similar distributions, with a strong positive correlation (r = 0.80, p < 0.001).

Higher symptom burden was described by the MG‐ADL scale compared to QMG (Figure 1C and Table 2). On average 61% of scores were concordant across individual items for MG‐ADL and QMG. The correlation between items was generally poor to moderate, primarily for bulbar and respiratory items. For comparison, the difference in scoring of MG‐ADL or QMG and MGC was also investigated (Figure S1C,D). Overall, a higher concordance was observed for MG‐ADL and MGC (65%–86%) and QMG versus MGC (73%–91%), compared to MG‐ADL and QMG.

Discussion

In this study assessing MG disease activity scales in clinical practice, we observed that patients tended to report worse symptoms compared to clinician‐based assessments, primarily in respiratory and bulbar items. Further, MG‐ADL displayed less potential floor effect compared to QMG. In multivariate analyses, MG‐ADL was only associated with MG‐QoL‐15, while QMG was associated with new‐onset status, MG‐QoL‐15, and prednisolone treatment.

We observed signs of a larger floor effect for QMG compared to MG‐ADL. Consequently, some patients classified as having achieved remission or MM by QMG still displayed residual disease burden by MG‐ADL. As MG symptoms fluctuate, typically being worse in the evenings, it is plausible that QMG, as a point estimate often scored earlier in the day, may underestimate the patient's overall disease burden. Our results, however, contrast with previous findings where a larger floor effect has been reported in MG‐ADL [29, 35, 36]. A potential explanation for the discrepancy may be differences in disease severity, as other studies have investigated participants with more severe symptoms. Furthermore, for both scales, the difference between normal and mild (0–1 points) may not be equal to the difference between mild and moderate (1–2 points). This, along with the subjective nature of MG‐ADL, could make scoring more variable. In contrast, the objective QMG items are more likely to yield consistent results.

Having had symptom onset within the previous 12 months, that is, being defined as new‐onset MG, was associated with significantly higher QMG scores compared to having established disease in the multivariate analysis. This difference is likely reflecting the effects of treatments and the disease course per se. On the contrary, MG‐ADL was not associated with the new‐onset covariate. The difference may be attributed to multiple factors, such as a lack of familiarity with both the disease and the scale early in the disease course. However, this needs to be investigated further in larger cohorts.

Interestingly, the association between disease activity scores and MG‐QoL‐15 was primarily driven by the symptom‐specific questions, which in their phrasing are similar to MG‐ADL and correlate strongly with MG‐ADL. No association with the patient's general emotional well‐being was observed, implicating a lesser influence of QoL on both clinician‐ and patient‐reported outcomes.

Our study is limited by its retrospective design, as data was not collected with this specific analysis in mind. Also, this study was conducted in a clinical practice setting in which MG patients generally had mild to moderate disease severity, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other patient groups with differing characteristics. Almost half of the cohort (47%) reported using a cholinesterase inhibitor within 6 h prior to the clinical visit. QMG scores were hence likely influenced by this. However, the retrospective evaluation of the MG‐ADL scores was also likely affected by cholinesterase inhibitor intake. Further, around 10% of patients had used ≥ 10 mg prednisolone for more than 4 weeks in the past year prior to index (median 20 weeks), which in previous studies has been associated with increased risk of steroid‐induced myopathy [37]. The majority of MG‐ADL scores (173 of 177) were physician‐administered, while four were patient‐reported in a web‐based application. When excluding the patient‐reported scores, results were unaffected (data not shown). Lastly, the correlations observed between QMG and MGC, as well as between MG‐ADL and MGC, may be overestimated as physicians might refer to the QMG or MG‐ADL score when assessing the MGC score.

In conclusion, we here explored the discordance between patient‐reported and clinician‐reported disease activity scores, namely MG‐ADL and QMG. MG‐ADL captured more symptoms than QMG, with poor to moderate correlations observed among most individual items. MG‐ADL also exhibited less potential floor effects compared to QMG in patients with lower disease burden. A stronger association with MG‐QoL‐15 was observed in MG‐ADL compared to QMG, primarily driven by the MG‐QoL‐15 questions specifically concerning symptoms, while emotional well‐being had little impact on MG‐ADL and QMG. Future studies with larger sample sizes, particularly in outpatient settings where these measures are most frequently used, are needed to validate our findings.
