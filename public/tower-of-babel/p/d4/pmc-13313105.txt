Late‐Onset Rheumatoid Arthritis and Air Pollution in a Multiethnic Cohort
Authors: Anna H Wu, Chiuchen Tseng, Song‐Yi Park, Daniel O Stram, Kami White, Jun Wu, Cheryl Vigen, Veronica W Setiawan, Xian Yu, Steven Gazal, Christopher Haiman, Lynne Wilkens, Loïc Le Marchand, Iona Cheng, William Stohl
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo evaluate the effects of ambient air pollution on rheumatoid arthritis (RA) incidence in a racially and ethnically diverse population.MethodsThis analysis included 42,152 California Multiethnic Cohort participants, aged ≥65 years (>70% African American and Latino adults) who were enrolled in the Fee For Service component of Medicare (2001–2018). We employed multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression to examine the associations of time‐varying air pollutants based on spatiotemporal models with RA incidence (n = 2,027) after adjusting for demographics, neighborhood socioeconomic status, smoking, work, and other exposures.ResultsRA incidence increased with exposure to fine particulate matter (PM) with diameter ≤2.5 μm (PM2.5) (hazard ratio [HR] per 2 μg/m3 = 1.20 [95% confidence interval (CI) 1.16–1.23]) and nitrogen dioxide (NO2) (HR per 10 μg/m3 = 1.44 [95% CI 1.35–1.52]). Air pollutant levels and risk associations were higher in African American and Latino than in Japanese American and White adults (P heterogeneity < 0.05). The RA‐PM2.5 association was higher in men (HR 1.23 [95% CI 1.15–1.32]) than in women (HR 1.17 [95% CI 1.12–1.21]; P heterogeneity = 0.06). RA associations with PM2.5 (and NO2) did not differ by demographics, smoking, or other lifestyle factors, but the HR associated with PM2.5 was higher among those with high‐risk work (longest occupation in labor/craftsman work and exposed to ≥10 years in one or more of 13 industries; HR 1.29 [95% CI 1.18–1.41]) than those without high‐risk work exposures (HR 1.16 [95% CI 1.12–1.21]; P heterogeneity = 0.04).ConclusionExposure to PM and gaseous pollution associates with increased RA incidence after age 65 years, particularly among African American and Latino adults. Further characterization of air pollution's contribution to racial and ethnic disparities in RA risk is warranted.

INTRODUCTION

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a chronic systemic inflammatory disease of unknown etiology that affects >17 million people worldwide. 1 , 2 Approximately one‐third of incident RA cases develop among those aged ≥60 years. A recent UK population‐based cohort study reported that the incidence rate ratio of RA was 62% higher in 2017 to 2019 than in 2000 to 2002, with the median age at RA diagnosis being 65 years (interquartile range [IQR] 53–77 years). 3 RA onset diagnosed after age 60 years compared with RA before age 60 years tended to show a smaller female excess, a higher proportion of rheumatoid factor (RF)–negative disease, and a more acute onset. 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 These changes over time in the age and sex distribution emphasize the importance of contemporary epidemiologic studies to monitor the role of specific environmental and lifestyle factors in RA development.

Epidemiologic studies have firmly established an increased risk of RA among smokers, those exposed to silica and other toxicants, those with a high body mass index (BMI), and certain hormonal profiles in women. 1 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 The potential role of air pollution in the etiology of RA was first reported in the Nurses’ Health Study (1996–2004), which found elevated risk associated with proximity to roads as a marker of traffic exposure, 13 although further analysis reported no association with exposure to specific air pollutants (fine particulate matter with diameter ≤2.5 μm [PM2.5] and ≤10 μm [PM10], nitrogen dioxide [NO2], and sulfur dioxide [SO2]). 14 Additional studies in the United States, 15 Sweden, 16 Italy, 17 Canada, 18 , 19 the United Kingdom, 20 Taiwan, 21 , 22 and South Korea 23 , 24 also yielded mixed results. These inconsistencies in results may be related, at least in part, to heterogeneity in study design, methods to establish an RA diagnosis, assessment of air pollutants, information on potential confounders (individual‐level lifestyle factors and neighborhood characteristics), and modest sample sizes. 25 , 26 , 27 , 28 Nevertheless, inhaled ambient air pollutants as well as occupational exposures can induce pulmonary oxidative stress and inflammation, triggering autoimmune responses and the risk of RA. 10 , 29

The studies to date have been conducted in largely White populations in North America and Europe or in Asian populations in Asia, with almost no data on minoritized racial and ethnic groups in the United States who often experience a high burden of air pollution with residing near sources of air pollutants because of decades of racial and ethnic segregation. 30 , 31 , 32 Furthermore, this study focusing on late‐onset RA adds new information on lifestyle and environmental risk factors in this growing group of individuals with RA, seldom investigated in previous epidemiologic studies. 7 We report here a study that investigates the role of air pollution in the etiology of RA among major racial and ethnic groups in the United States by leveraging the California component of the Multiethnic Cohort (MEC) study with >70% African American and Latino participants. 33 We present results on the association between RA incidence and long‐term (2001–2018) exposure to PM2.5, PM10, and gaseous pollutants (NO2, nitrogen oxide [NOx], carbon monoxide [CO], and ozone [O3]) in this large multiethnic cohort.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study participants

The MEC is a population‐based, prospective cohort of >215,000 men and women in Hawaii and California (largely Los Angeles County) who completed and returned a 26‐page mailed questionnaire at enrollment (1993–1996) (see the “Covariates” section). 34 The cohort has been linked to the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services (CMS) claims data for MEC participants aged ≥65 years in 1999 to 2018, 35 with ≥2 years of continuous Fee For Service (FFS) coverage, to identify chronic disease outcomes such as RA based on the International Classification of Diseases (ICD) codes included in the claims, in addition to linkage to Hawaii and California death files and the National Death Index for vital status. Participants enrolled in Medicare Part C (Medicare Advantage or Health Maintenance Organization [HMO]) were excluded from our analysis because their medical services were not directly billed to Medicare. Figure 1 shows that this analysis was restricted to California MEC participants (n = 112,004) with air pollution information 33 ; 56,628 were Medicare FFS beneficiaries with available claims data and 55,376 did not have Medicare FFS. Of the 56,628 with Medicare FFS, 8,025 were excluded (referred to hereafter as excluded‐FFS) because they were not of the five major racial and ethnic groups (African American, Japanese American, Latino, Native Hawaiian, and White; n = 2,128), enrolled in Medicare for <2 years (n = 349), exited Medicare before 2001 (n = 1,134), enrolled in Medicare before age 65 years (n = 2,337), had incomplete address history (n = 1,008), or required >50% imputation of PM2.5 data (n = 1,069), resulting in 48,603 participants included in the final analysis. RA case determination

Because of recognized challenges in using Medicare claims to identify incident RA, we used three steps to classify the 48,603 Medicare FFS participants by RA status (non‐RA, 2,847 incident RA with only one claim (RA1), 2,027 incident RA with two or more claims (RA2), and 3,604 prevalent RA) (Figure 1) and also compared main covariates of those with RA and the excluded‐FFS group (Supplementary Table S2). First, we used established CMS Chronic Conditions Warehouse (CCW) algorithms to assign an RA diagnosis using RA‐specific ICD Ninth Edition (714.0, 714.1, and 714.2) and ICD Tenth Edition (M05, M06.0, M06.8, and M06.9) codes to identify patients from Medicare diagnosis‐related files (MEDPAR [hospital inpatient], outpatient claims, and carrier claims). 36 , 37 The use of the CMS CCW algorithm helps reduce the risk of false positives or prevalent cases as this requires a 2‐year reference period in which beneficiaries must have had 11 to 12 months per year of non‐HMO Parts A and B coverage and two or more RA diagnoses listed in hospital outpatient, inpatient, skilled nursing facility, home health agency, or routine outpatient visits from carrier claims. As such, we excluded 3,604 who were determined to have prevalent RA. Second, we additionally excluded 2,847 participants who had RA1 in the RA discovery year as we were concerned that their diagnosis of RA may have been either premature or incorrect by virtue of no succeeding RA claims. Third, we assessed RA medication claims based on Part D prescription data we had from 2006 to 2018 for those with RA1 and RA2 (Supplementary Table S3). We identified those with pharmacologic treatment for RA and those who had claims for steroids (prednisone), methotrexate, and other conventional synthetic disease‐modifying antirheumatic drugs (DMARDs), biologic DMARDs, and targeted synthetic DMARDs. 38 , 39 A total of 42,152 participants were observed from the date of Medicare entry until the diagnosis of incident RA was made, death, or the end of follow‐up (December 31, 2018). Death was identified through linkages to the National Death Index and state death certificate files.

Address history, geocoding, and neighborhood socioeconomic status

The MEC actively maintains up‐to‐date addresses on all participants by periodic mailings of newsletters, follow‐up questionnaires, and linkages to administrative data and registries. 33 These addresses were geocoded and used to assess neighborhood socioeconomic status (nSES) using a composite measure of seven census‐based indicators (education, median household income, percent living with an income <200% of the poverty level, percent blue‐collar workers, percent aged >16 years in workforce without job, median rent, and median house value). 40 , 41 The nSES index was assigned to participants’ census block group at the time of Medicare enrollment and at the end of study follow‐up (ie, RA diagnosis, death, or December 31, 2018). Low and high nSES were defined as quintiles 1 to 3 and quintiles 4 to 5, respectively, based on the nSES distribution of California block groups. 42 Straight‐line distances were calculated from baseline residential addresses to different road classes, 43 and participants were classified as nonmovers if they remained at their residential address at cohort entry to the end of follow‐up.

Air pollution exposure assessment

We generated monthly air pollutant measures for the entire study period from 2001 to 2018. Estimates of satellite‐based PM2.5 were obtained from fine‐resolution geospatial model outputs. This model provided validated and publicly available PM2.5 outputs at a 1‐km resolution over North America by statistically fusing chemical transport model (GEOS‐Chem) predictions and satellite observations of aerosol optical depth with ground‐based observations using a geographically weighted regression. 44 In the Southwestern United States, 0.6 was the cross‐validation R 2 of the total PM2.5 mass concentration. 45 As in previous air pollution studies conducted among California participants in the MEC, we used empirical Bayesian kriging interpolation to estimate each participant's exposure levels for PM10 and gaseous traffic‐related pollutants (NO2, NOx, and CO) and O3 using monitoring data routinely collected by the US Environmental Protection Agency, 33 , 46 which showed a cross‐validation R 2 ranging from 0.65 to 0.75 for different air pollutants in California. 47 Among participants included in our analyses, 90.5% had complete air pollution data and 9.5% had some imputed data (4.6% with <10% imputed, 1.6% with 10%–<20% imputed, and 3.3% with 20%–<50% imputed).

Covariates

At study enrollment (1993–1996), participants completed a 26‐page questionnaire that asked about demographic characteristics (eg, race, ethnicity, and education) and lifestyle factors including body weight, height, smoking history (status, number of years smoked, average number of cigarettes smoked/day, and years stopped smoking), diet, medical history, work history, and hormonal and reproductive factors (among women). The work history section asked two questions. Participants were asked to select their one longest occupation from 10 choices (laborer or farm, factory, clerical/office, sales, manager/administration, crafts, small business owner, professional/technical, unemployed, and other). They were then asked if they worked for at least 10 years in any of 13 industries (more than one response was allowed; metal production/processing, mining/quarrying, cotton/wool/textile, plastic production, gasoline refining/distribution, chemical production/use, rubber/tire manufacturing, shipyard work, farming, furniture/woodworking, automotive repair, pesticide production, and paint production/use). A response of “yes” to one or more of the 13 industries was classified as “yes exposed.” 26 , 48 Responses to the occupation and industry questions were then broadly classified into six levels, based on three groupings of occupation (office/professional work only, labor/craftsman/manufacturing work only, or a combination of both office/professional and labor occupation) and two options of exposure (no or yes) to any of the 13 industries. We considered as “high‐risk work exposures” those who were classified as “yes exposed and worked in labor/craftsman manufacturing occupations” or “yes exposed and worked in a combination of both office/professional and labor/craftsman occupations.” Participants who responded “yes exposed but only worked in office/professional occupations” were not considered as having “high‐risk work exposures” because we assumed that direct exposures were unlikely for office work, sales, or management. These baseline covariates (BMI, moderate or vigorous physical activity, smoking intensity [quit status and pack‐years of smoking], alcohol use, use of nonsteroidal anti‐inflammatory drugs [NSAIDs], work history, and among women, age at menarche, parity, menopause status, and use of hormonal therapy) were adjusted for in our analyses.

Statistical analysis

We estimated RA incidence in relation to PM and gaseous pollutants using Cox proportional hazard models with monthly time‐varying air pollutant exposures. The exposure period extends from January 2001 for Medicare members who began enrollment in January 1999, or 2 years after entry to Medicare for those who began enrollment after 1999, to the end of follow‐up for each risk set. Age in calendar months at entry into Medicare was used as the time variable and defined a series of risk sets based on month/year at RA identification (index case) using age at cohort entry (1‐year age groups) as a stratum variable. Each risk set consisted of all participants who were Medicare FFS beneficiaries and remained alive and uncensored at the time of RA diagnosis. For each member of each risk set (including the index case), we computed an average air pollutant exposure for the period starting from the time of Medicare entry (month/year) up to the age of RA diagnosis of the index case in each risk set based on their residential history. This average exposure was used as the independent air pollution variable of interest.

Cox models included strata variables age (single‐year age categories) and nSES (quintiles) at Medicare surveillance start and were adjusted for race and ethnicity, sex, education, and current nSES; the hazard ratio (HR) estimates were largely unchanged with further adjustment for other covariates shown in Table 1. We estimated HRs and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for RA incidence per common fixed‐unit increase of PM (per 2 μg/m3 for PM10 and PM2.5), NOx and NO2 (per 10 μg/m3), CO (per 100 μg/m3), and O3 (per 10 μg/m3); these fixed units were close to the IQRs of these pollutants. Deviations from proportionality were checked using an analysis of Schoenfeld residuals and we found no violation of this assumption. We evaluated whether air pollution–RA associations differed by sex, race and ethnicity, nSES, distance of residence to major roads, moving status, smoking, high‐risk work exposures, and other factors. To test for heterogeneity by race and ethnicity and across subgroups, we evaluated interaction terms in the model for each pollutant with subgroup membership using a Wald statistic. We also tested for differences in associations by RA medication claims (no vs yes) as a measure of RA severity by conducting a competing risk analysis using a Lunn‐McNeil augmentation approach 49 so that each RA group was fit by a cause‐specific model in a separate stratum. Parameter estimates between having RA medication claims were compared using the Wald test. All statistical tests were based on two‐tailed probability, and a significance level was set at α < 0.05. Analyses were conducted using SAS 9.4 statistical software (SAS Institute, Cary NC).

Ethics approval

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and the protocol was approved by the institutional review boards at the University of Southern California, University of California San Francisco, and University of Hawaii. Study participants provided written informed consent by completing the mailed questionnaire.

Data availability

The RA outcome data were obtained under a data use agreement with CMS that explicitly prohibits data sharing. The MEC baseline data are shared in dbGaP (accession number: phs002183.v1.p1).

RESULTS

This analysis of air pollution and RA incidence after age 65 years included 42,152 California MEC Medicare FFS participants of whom 28.8% were African American, 13.6% Japanese American, 42.8% Latino, and 14.9% White. The mean ± SD duration of follow‐up from Medicare entry to the end of follow‐up was 12.0 ± 5.0 years for the non‐RA group and 6.1 ± 3.7 years for the RA group. Table 1 shows demographics (age, sex, race and ethnicity, and education), neighborhood characteristics (nSES and distance to road), smoking, occupation/exposures, and various lifestyle factors. RA incidence was lower among participants with higher education, those who were physically active, and those who used menopausal hormones (among women), whereas RA incidence was higher among smokers, NSAID users, and those who had “high‐risk work exposures” (Table 1), specifically mining/quarry/cement and paint production industries (Supplementary Table S4). The prevalence of many of the established risk factors were higher among either African American or Latino participants. Relative to other racial and ethnic groups, African American participants displayed higher median baseline (first 12 months) levels of PM2.5, PM10, NOx, and CO whereas levels of NO2 were higher among Latino participants (Supplementary Table S5). The correlations of PM and gaseous pollutants are shown in Supplementary Table S6.

Models that additionally included strata variables for nSES quintiles and adjusted for education continued to show that RA incidence increased significantly with increased exposure to PM2.5 (HR 1.18, 95% CI 1.14–1.22 per 2 μg/m3), PM10 (HR 1.07, 95% CI 1.05–1.10 per 2 μg/m3), and gaseous pollutants (HRs were 1.39, 1.11, and 1.19, respectively, for NO2, NOx, and CO) but was not associated with O3 exposure (Table 2). Heterogeneity across race and ethnicity for PM2.5, NO2, and NOx were observed (P heterogeneity ≤ 0.05). Significantly higher risk (95% CI excluded 1.0) of RA with increasing exposures to PM2.5, PM10, NO2, NOx, and CO exposures were observed among African American and Latino participants but not among Japanese American and White participants.

The RA‐PM2.5 association was higher in men (HR 1.23, 95% CI 1.15–1.32) than women (HR 1.17, 95% CI 1.12–1.21) (P heterogeneity = 0.06); this pattern was observed across race and ethnicity except for White participants (Table 3). The higher HRs for RA associated with PM2.5 in African American and Latino participants were observed across the strata of education, nSES, distance to roads, and moving status. RA‐PM2.5 associations were observed among those without any high‐risk work exposures (HR 1.16, 95% CI 1.12–1.21) and among those with high‐risk work exposures (HR 1.29, 95% CI 1.18–1.41); the HRs differed significantly (P heterogeneity = 0.04), but the RA‐PM2.5 associations did not differ by other covariates including smoking, BMI, NSAID use, alcohol use, and physical activity. The RA‐NO2 associations by high‐risk work exposure and other covariates showed similar patterns (Table 4).

DISCUSSION

In this large prospective study (72% African American and Latino adults) of the MEC leveraging Medicare data, RA incidence after age 65 years was associated with PM (PM2.5 and PM10) and gaseous (NO2, NOx, and CO) pollutants. To our knowledge, this is one of the few studies with large numbers of older‐onset RA and by race and ethnicity, allowing the report on air pollution–RA associations separately for African American, Japanese American, and Latino populations who are seldom included in RA epidemiologic research in the United States. The adjusted HRs between RA incidence and PM2.5 exposure were higher among African American (HR 1.25) and Latino (HR 1.20) participants than among Japanese American (HR 1.10) and White (HR 1.06) participants. The significant RA associations with PM2.5 exposures were found across the strata of education, nSES, smoking, BMI, and high‐risk work history, with evidence of significantly higher HRs among those with a history of high‐risk work exposures than those without such work exposures.

Several reasons may explain the strong RA‐PM2.5 association in the MEC compared with published studies (HRs <1.0 in three studies 14 , 15 , 16 , 21 and HRs >1.0 in five studies 17 , 19 , 20 , 22 , 24 ). First, 72% of the participants in this MEC analysis were African American and Latino adults and they exhibited higher exposures to air pollutants, established risk factors for RA, and likely other unmeasured co‐exposures. Compared with White adults in the MEC, the average baseline levels of PM2.5, PM10, NOx, and CO were 13%, 6%, 16%, and 9% higher, respectively, in African American adults whereas levels of NO2 were 11% higher in Latino adults. Approximately 85% of African American and Latino participants in the MEC lived in low SES neighborhoods at baseline compared to approximately 48% of Japanese American and White participants. Neighborhoods with a high proportion of minoritized racial and ethnic groups may experience co‐exposure to other environmental risk factors (eg, unmeasured air toxics) that may have adverse air pollution–related health effects. 33 In both low and high nSES strata, RA incidence at least doubled in association with PM2.5 exposure among African American and Latino participants who not only were exposed to higher levels of air pollutants but also had higher prevalences of the at‐risk category of the established risk factors including tobacco use, high BMI, physical inactivity, and high‐risk work exposures (Supplementary Table S5). All the HRs for PM and gaseous pollutants (except for O3) in African American and Latino participants were considerably higher than the respective HRs for White and Japanese American participants (Table 2).

Second, our stronger findings are likely related, in part, to our complete, updated residential history from Medicare entry to the end of follow‐up, spanning nearly 20 years and yielding monthly estimates of both PM and gaseous pollutants from 2001 to 2018. Median air pollutant levels during the first 12 months of Medicare entry tended to be higher than the cumulative average levels across the entire follow‐up period (except for O3). Hence, unlike most of the previous studies, we were able to examine the hazard of RA in relation to time‐varying air pollutant exposures across follow‐up years with adjustment for relevant neighborhood level and individual lifestyle factors. Third, our PM2.5 estimates represent more refined exposure assessment at 1‐km resolution across the entire study period with the use of chemical transport modeling coupled with satellite and ground‐based data. 44 Fourth, median baseline PM2.5 levels were higher in Los Angeles County (21.2 μg/m3) than in studies conducted in Canada (4.3 and 9.6 μg/m3), 18 , 19 the United Kingdom (9.9 μg/m3), 20 Italy (16.0 μg/m3), 17 or elsewhere in the United States (16.5 μg/m3). 14 Of the four previous studies that found a positive PM2.5‐RA association, two studies had high median levels of PM2.5 (26.1 μg/m3 in Korea 24 and 34.5 μg/m3 in Taiwan 22 ), risk of RA was significantly increased in association with >20μg/m3 of PM2.5 exposure in the third study, 17 and the fourth study did not have high levels of PM2.5 but had a very large number of RA events (n = 67,676). 19

In this study, we lacked information on clinical measures of RA, severity over time, extra‐articular manifestations of RA, or markers of RA seropositivity such as anticitrullinated protein antibodies or RF. Although Medicare Part D information was available only from 2006 to 2018 in the MEC, we used available medication claims data as an indicator of disease severity and/or access to care. The prevalence of claims for DMARDs was higher among those who had diagnosis in more recent years, but it decreased with increasing age of RA diagnosis (Supplementary Table S3), as has been reported by others. 50 , 51 Comorbidities, polypharmacy, and concerns of age‐related physiologic changes may have contributed to less treatment, especially of DMARDs among older patients with RA. Nevertheless, the comparability in air pollution results irrespective of Medicare claims for RA medications adds confidence to using RA diagnoses based on Medicare claims because participants who took RA medications have a very high likelihood of having bona fide RA that meets the accepted diagnostic criteria. 39 As results on RA and air pollution by seropositivity status are sparse and complex, 28 , 52 there is need of additional well‐designed prospective observational studies with better clinical measures of RA.

Inhalable exposures from air pollution and occupation may increase RA incidence through increased oxidative stress and inflammatory responses in the lung, triggering systemic immune system changes. 10 , 27 , 53 Our finding of a higher HR for RA associated with both PM (PM2.5) and gaseous pollutants (NO2) among those with a history of high‐risk work exposures compared with those without such work exposures is noteworthy (Table 4 and Supplementary Table S4) 8 , 10 and warrants further investigations as the impact of air pollution on RA risk may vary by specific cofactors of interest. In the UK Biobank study, RA incidence was associated with the combined effects of air pollution and polygenic risk scores (PRSs), but the effect of air pollution was strongest among those with the lowest PRS (P trend = 0.0006), intermediate among those with middle PRS (P trend = 0.009), and weakest among those with the highest PRS (P trend = 0.085). 20 Air pollution may promote the transition from RA‐related autoantibodies to RA, having a later role in the development of RA. 26 , 29 In support of the deleterious effects of air pollution on inflammation, circulating levels of C‐reactive protein, a biomarker of systemic inflammation, increased significantly with 12‐month exposure to traffic‐related air pollutants (both PM and NOx) among California MEC participants; the associations were particularly strong among Latino participants, those who lived in low SES neighborhoods, those with overweight or obese, and never or former smokers. 54

Despite the many study strengths, including rich prospective data on relevant individual lifestyle and neighborhood characteristics and time‐dependent measures of air pollution, we did not have information on exposures during travel and time indoors. Nevertheless, reverse causation is not a likely explanation of our results because it would mean that individuals with pre‐existing rheumatic conditions (such as undiagnosed RA) might have relocated to areas with poorer air quality for socioeconomic reasons; our findings on RA and PM2.5 exposures were observed among both movers and nonmovers. Our results based on Medicare RA diagnosis may not be generalizable to the entire RA population because those enrolled in Medicare FFS may differ from those not enrolled in Medicare FFS, which totaled 55,376 (Supplementary Table S1). Compared with MEC participants with Medicare FFS and no RA, the group without Medicare FFS showed largely comparable patterns of covariates and air pollution levels but the proportion of African American participants was higher, and the proportion of Japanese American and White participants was lower among those without Medicare FFS. Thus, our overall results combining across race and ethnicity might be even stronger assuming that the HR estimates for those not included are comparable to those included in our analysis. Another limitation is that our diagnosis of RA was based solely on Medicare claims data without chart review. Although we excluded MEC participants with prevalent RA and those with single RA claims, their distributions of various baseline covariates and air pollution levels were comparable to those of the RA group included in this air pollution–RA analysis.

In conclusion, long‐term air pollution exposure is associated with late‐onset RA in a multiethnic population of adults aged ≥65 years. Although these findings persist across education, nSES, distance from roads, and moving status, subgroup analyses suggest that air pollution may have greater effects in minoritized racial and ethnic groups. Our findings highlight the importance of further investigations to elucidate the role of air pollution in RA development in racially, ethnically, and socioeconomically diverse populations and in younger populations (aged <65 years) and to include refined measures of RA diagnoses and information on genetic profiles whenever possible.
