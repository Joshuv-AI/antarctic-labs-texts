Clusterin marks maladaptive regenerative responses throughout the kidney following ischemia-reperfusion injury
Authors: Agnieszka Masztalerz, Loïck Joumier, Sunny Zhen, Tomoko Takano, Serge Lemay, Mohan Malleshaiah, Alex Gregorieff
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryOur understanding of the regenerative capacity of renal tubules has primarily come from studies focusing on proximal tubules. To gain a broader view of injury responses throughout the tubular network, we employed single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) and lineage tracing to dissect cells expressing Clusterin (Clu), a known biomarker of kidney disease. During development, Clu labeled nephrogenic progenitors, while in response to ischemia-reperfusion injury, Clu was initially upregulated in distal segments. Fate mapping demonstrated that Clu+ collecting ducts were proliferative but ultimately persisted in a de-differentiated state or were lost. By scRNA-seq following IRI, we identified delayed and maladaptive responses in injured proximal tubules and uncovered a “mixed lineage” gene signature comprised of podocyte and parietal epithelial cell markers aberrantly activated in diverse cell types within the medulla. In conclusion, despite labeling multipotent progenitors during nephrogenesis, Clu reactivation in the injured adult kidney signals impaired regenerative responses throughout the tubular epithelium.

Introduction

Acute kidney injury (AKI) represents a major global health problem that is associated with tubular damage and impaired renal function resulting commonly from ischemia, sepsis, exposure to toxins, allergic, or allogenic immune processes, etc.1 Although AKI may resolve spontaneously, incomplete tubular repair may progress to chronic kidney disease (CKD) and end-stage renal disease, characterized by chronic inflammation, fibrosis, tubule atrophy, and permanent loss of kidney function.2 By modeling AKI through ischemia-reperfusion injury (IRI), genetic fate mapping and single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) studies have shown that injury triggers extensive reprogramming of the tubular epithelium.3,4,5,6,7,8 Indeed, it has been shown that differentiated proximal tubules (PTs) acquire stem cell markers (i.e., CD24, CD133, and vimentin) and re-enter the cell cycle to replenish damaged tubules,9,10 bypassing the requirement for a pre-existing progenitor cell population.11 In contrast, separate studies have argued that resident Pax2+ and/or Wnt-responsive progenitors are mobilized to repair injured tubules.12,13 Beyond redeployment of nephrogenic and/or proliferative signals, reports have also highlighted numerous metabolic alterations in PTs.6,14,15,16 Together these phenotypic changes likely provide a temporary survival advantage for regenerating epithelial cells but persistence of the above cellular states may also contribute to maladaptive responses including cell-cycle arrest, epithelial-to-mesenchymal (EMT) transitions, release of pro-inflammatory mediators, and ultimately leading to CKD.7 Finally, it is worth noting that although a clearer picture is emerging of injury responses in the kidney, our insight has largely focused on changes occurring in PTs and consequently the regenerative capacity of the distal segments of the nephron and collecting duct (CD) system remains underexplored.

In previous work, we identified Clusterin (Clu) as a robust marker of a regenerative cell type in the intestinal epithelium that we termed “revival” stem cells (revSCs).17 Injury-induced Clu-expressing cells display characteristics of the primitive gut epithelium and are fueled by de-differentiation of lineage committed crypt epithelial cells to replenish homeostatic intestinal stem cells. Clu is a secreted glycoprotein that has been shown to be involved in cell survival, proliferation, autophagy, proteostasis, etc. in multiple tissue types including the kidney.18 Interestingly, functional studies demonstrated that Clu knockout mice exhibit impaired recovery following IRI,19 and urinary CLU in humans predicts kidney dysfunction in a clinical setting.20,21 Herein, we employed fate mapping studies and scRNA-seq of Clu expressing cells to chart the cell fate changes occurring throughout the renal tubular system in response to IRI. Contrary to the pro-regenerative capabilities of Clu+ cells in the gut epithelium, we provide evidence indicating that Clu primarily marked maladaptive tubular responses during IRI in various renal segments, notably PTs, the loop of Henle and CDs. Our scRNA-seq also revealed the emergence of a novel gene signature featuring podocyte (POD) and glomerular parietal epithelial cell (PEC) identity markers that is ectopically induced in multiple cellular compartments within the medulla.

Results

Clu is a nephron progenitor marker ectopically expressed in the injured distal nephron and CD

Although early studies indicated that Clu is highly upregulated in developing and regenerating nephrons, the identity of the tubule segments expressing Clu has remained poorly defined.22,23,24,25 To address this issue, we first evaluated Clu mRNA expression during nephrogenesis and regeneration in the mouse using fluorescent in situ hybridization (ISH) assays. Analysis of Clu in the developing kidney showed expression in both Wt1+ and E-cadherin+ zones of S-shaped bodies indicating Clu marks, both proximal and distal nephron progenitors, respectively (Figure S1). In a previous study, we genetically labeled Clu+ regenerative cells in the intestinal epithelium by generating Clu-CreERT; Lox-Stop-Lox-Tomato knockin mice (CluTom).17 Thus, to validate Clu as a marker of nephrogenic progenitors in post-natal kidney development, we activated the Clu reporter in neonatal CluTom pups by intraperitoneal (i.p.) injection of tamoxifen to lactating mothers at P1 and harvested weanlings at P27 (Figure 1A). Subsequent analysis of lineage markers confirmed Clu+ progenitors contributed to both proximal and distal tubule segments including LTL+ PTs, Umod+ thick ascending limb (TAL), Calb1+ distal convoluted tubules (DCTs), and Aqp2+ CDs (Figure 1B). Besides contributing to the tubular network, analysis of glomeruli showed Clu reporter activity in Nephrin+ PODs and Ncam1+ PECs (Figure 1C). In untreated adult mice, Clu was detected primarily in the cortical region in Aqp2, Umod, and Calb1-expressing cells, representing the CD, DCT, and TAL, respectively but excluded from LTL+ PT (Figures 1D–1I). Prominent expression of Clu in untreated conditions was also noted in the urothelium (Uro) (Figure 1F). In response to a 24 min unilateral IRI, we observed robust upregulation of Clu throughout the cortex and medulla at 72 h, including in Aqp2+ CD and Umod+ TAL (Figures 1J–1N). In addition, Clu was co-expressed with distal tubule injury marker Lcn2 (Ngal) but remained absent from kidney injury marker 1 (Kim1) positive (Havcr1+) PT at early time points (Figure 1L). Together these experiments confirmed Clu as a robust marker of nephrogenesis and injured tubules.

Analysis of regenerative capacity of Clu-expressing cells by lineage tracing

Previous studies have shown that PT specifically expressing Havcr1 are proliferative and expand following IRI.26 To determine whether Clu+ cells exhibit similar properties, Clu and Havcr1 mRNA detection was performed in conjunction with Edu labeling in kidneys harvested at 1, 3, and 7 days later post 24 min IRI (Figure 2A). A peak of Edu+/Clu+ cells was detected at day 3 and sustained until day 7 post IRI (Figure 2B). When considering location within the kidney, we found that proliferative Clu+ cells were predominantly located in the medulla compared to the cortex (Figure 2C). Analysis of proliferation in Havcr1+ PT revealed a similar trend, with a peak of proliferation at day 3 but the frequency of proliferative cells in this compartment was significantly higher than in Clu+ tubules (Figure 2B). Given our expression data in Figure 1 demonstrating upregulation of Clu in both Aqp2+ and Umod+ tubules of the medulla, we also examined whether these cells incorporated Edu post IRI. Interestingly, Edu was readily detected in Clu+/Aqp2+ cells at day 3 post IRI but largely absent from Umod+ cells, indicating that in the medulla, the CD activate a proliferative program while TAL remain quiescent following IRI (Figure 2D). A previous study indicated that germline deletion of Clu resulted in diminished proliferation of renal tubules following IRI.27 To validate this, we created a Clu null allele by breeding CluCreERT mice from above to homozygosity thereby disrupting exon 2 of the Clu gene. Proliferation throughout the kidney and specifically in Aqp2+ CD was unchanged in CluCreERT/CluCreERT kidneys (Figure 2E). Next, we tested the regenerative potential of Clu+ cells in the IRI model by conducting two independent labeling experiments. Firstly, CluTom mice were injected with tamoxifen 20 days prior to 24 min IRI to permanently label pre-existing Clu+ cells in the uninjured kidney and allow sufficient time for tamoxifen clearance prior to IRI (Figure S2A). In this setting, immunofluorescence analysis showed that Tom+ cells were Edu-negative at 3 days post IRI (Figures S2B and S2C) and the relative abundance of Tom+ cells did not change between 3 and 20 days post IRI (Figures S2B–S2E), suggesting that Clu+ cells in cortical segments of the TAL, DCT, and CD remained quiescent after IRI.

In the second labeling experiment, CluTom were tamoxifen-injected 24 h after IRI to track the fate of de novo Clu+ cells. Tamoxifen is known to persist systemically in mice for several days after injection, potentially allowing for continued labeling of Cre-expressing cells beyond the initial day of injection. To minimize these effects, CluTom were injected with a low-dose tamoxifen (0.4 mg per 25 g body weight) and harvested at various time points following a 24 min IRI (Figure S2F). In contrast to pre-existing Clu+ cells, labeled de novo Clu+ cells incorporated Edu at day 3 (Figure S2G), and expanded in numbers through day 20 but at 35 days post IRI, a significant decline in Tom+ cells was observed in both the cortex and medulla compared to day 20 (Figures 2F and 2G). Thus, fate mapping studies combined with Edu incorporation assays revealed that de novo Clu+ CD cells initially activate a proliferative program, which may potentially fuel their expansion in the medulla. However, we cannot exclude the possibility that the initial expansion of Tom+ cells observed may also be driven by ongoing labeling of newly generated Clu+ cells across the time course caused by the long half-life of tamoxifen. Regardless, despite the evidence of a transient regenerative program and contrary to Clu+ nephrogenic progenitors in neonates, IRI-induced Clu+ cells largely fail to contribute to long-term repair of damaged tubules.

Dedifferentiation is a key feature of regenerating PTs following acute injury.26 Since we observed that a subset of Clu+ CD cells in the medulla proliferated post IRI, we hypothesized that these cells would concurrently activate a dedifferentiation program. To test this, the differentiation status of Clu+ CD cells was monitored at early and late time points following low-dose tamoxifen on day 1 post IRI in CluTom mice. Analysis at day 3, revealed a modest decline in Aqp2 expression relative to untreated conditions (i.e., prior to tamoxifen-induced labeling) (Figure 2H). Moreover, in agreement with our expression data in Figure 1, the Clu reporter was exclusively detected in Aqp2+ CD within the papilla region (Figure 2H), while elsewhere, Tom was also detected in Umod+ TAL and Calb1+ DCT in the outer medulla and cortical regions, respectively (Figure S2H). As anticipated, LTL+ PT and Aqp1+ and EphA7+ loop of Henle cells (or the descending and ascending thin limbs, DTL-ATL) in the papilla region remained unlabelled (Figure S2I). Next, multiplex imaging of Tom, Clu, and Aqp2 was performed at day 20 post IRI (Figure 2I). Interestingly, 48% of surviving Aqp2+/Tom+ tubules analyzed displayed reduced levels of Aqp2 (Figures 2I and 2J, closed arrowheads), while the remaining Aqp2+/Tom+ tubules expressed Aqp2 to comparable levels as Tom-negative tubules (Figure 2I, asterisks), suggesting that only a subset of CD had recovered. Consistent with these results, the percentage of Aqp2+ CD cells expressing Tom was significantly reduced relative to day 3, as well as the overall percentage of Aqp2+ cells (Figures 2K and 2L). Furthermore, we also noted Tom+ tubules in the papilla region with undetectable levels of Aqp2 (Figure 2I, open arrowheads). Considering that in the papilla, tamoxifen induction of the Clu reporter labeled exclusively Aqp2+ CD (Figures 2H and 2I) and not neighboring Aqp1+/Epha7 DTL-ATL cells (Figure S2I), these results suggest that CD can persist long-term following IRI in a de-differentiated state with complete downregulation of Aqp2. As additional validation, we found that these de-differentiated, Aqp2 low or negative CD maintained high levels of Clu, in contrast to Aqp2 high CD that showed low or undetectable levels of Clu, further confirming the presence of a subset of CD in a chronic injury state. Finally, in contrast to the CD, Clu expressing cells in Umod+ TAL remained in a non-proliferative state, post IRI (Figure 2). Consistent with these observations, Umod expression in the outer medulla appeared largely unchanged in Tom+ TALs when comparing day 3 and 20 post Tam injection (Figure S2H). Overall, our fate mapping studies highlight the differential responses induced by IRI across various distal segments, with only Clu+ CD engaging in a de-differentiation/proliferative program while the TAL and DCT remaining relatively quiescent following IRI.

Single-cell transcriptional profiling uncovers differential injury responses across the nephron segments

We previously described BAC-CluGFP transgenic mice that drive GFP expression under the regulatory elements of the Clu loci, thereby faithfully labeling Clu positive cells in vivo.28 As anticipated, we observed high concordance between Clu mRNA and GFP expression in untreated and post IRI kidneys (Figure S3A). Thus, to gain additional insight into the identity of Clu+ cells, we transcriptionally profiled single fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS)-sorted GFP+ cells from BAC-CluGFP mice subjected or not to unilateral 35 min IRI (Figure S3B). Cells were harvested from pooled kidneys of 4 mice per condition (untreated and 2, 7, and 30 days post IRI), and GFP+ cells were sorted and subjected to scRNA-seq using the 10× genomics platform. Clu marks delayed maladaptive responses in injured PTs

By comparing the relative abundance of epithelial populations across our dataset, we uncovered a sharp increase at day 7, post IRI in the proportion of iPTs and loop of Henle cells (DTL-ATL) (Figure 3B), which was further validated when comparing the number of cells expressing specific markers of iPT (i.e., Havcr1, Krt20, Sox9, Vcam1) and DTL-ATL (i.e., EphA7, Scel, Slc4a11) across the time line (Figure 3C). The delayed capture of iPT cells in our dataset implied Clu was upregulated in this population at day 7, which contrasted our earlier findings of mutual exclusion between Clu and Havcr1 at 3 days post IRI (Figure 1K). To confirm these results, we re-examined their expression by ISH at day 7, post IRI and found that double Clu+/Havcr1+ cells accounted for 25% of all iPT cells (Figure 4A). From lineage tracing, we had shown that Clu+ cells of the CDs persisted in a de-differentiated state and unable to contribute to long-term recovery (Figure 2). To determine whether Clu+ PT cells undergo a similar program, we performed lineage tracing experiments in the CluTom model. As expected, CluTom mice injected with low-dose tamoxifen at day 7 and harvested at day 10, showed expression of the Clu reporter in Havcr1+ iPT cells (Figures 4D and 4E). The PT differentiation marker, Lrp2, was downregulated in Tom+/Havcr1+ cells (closed arrowhead), whereas cells with strong apical expression of Lrp2 were invariably Tom-negative (open arrowheads). Of note, tubules with mosaic expression of Lrp2 were also readily detected (asterisk), where Tom was restricted to Havcr1+ cells with downregulated Lrp2.

During kidney development, lineage tracing in post-natal CluTom mice demonstrated that Clu+ progenitors gave rise to both proximal and distal segments (Figure 1). Thus, we next tested whether Clu induction in iPT was indicative of a nephrogenic program in response to IRI that led to repair and re-differentiation of PT. By analyzing CluTom mice harvested at day 27 following injection with low-dose tamoxifen at day 7 post IRI, virtually all Lrp2+ PT lacked Tom+ cells (Figure 4F). In the rare cases, where we observed Tom+ cells in PT they maintained expression of Havcr1, indicating that Clu-labeled PT at day 7 either remained in an injured state or were lost (Figure 4G, arrowheads). These results support the notion that Clu marks a delayed maladaptive response in PT, similar to CD.

Profiling of Clu+ cells identifies a novel population of mixed cell identity

Among epithelial cells, our scRNA-seq data also uncovered a relative increase at day 30 post IRI in the proportion of a population of mixed POD and PEC identity (Figures 3A and 3B). These cells expressed several markers of PODs (i.e., Wt1, Foxd1, Podxl, Lmx1b, Zfp385b, etc.) and PEC (i.e., Wnt16, Ncam1, Nkain3, Akap12, Kcnma1 etc.) (Table S1; Figure 5A). Interestingly, Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) plots showed that this signature was also upregulated in cluster 14, which we associated above with myeloid/Mac cells. Moreover, cluster 14 converged on POD/PEC cells at day 30, while remaining segregated in the UMAP space at earlier time points (Figure 3A). Next, specifically reanalyzing clusters 14, 16, and 19, which compose the entire set of cells enriched for the POD/PEC signature, revealed myofibroblast and Mac-like populations as well as rare subclusters of PODs and cycling cells (Figure 5B). Finally, to confirm induction of these POD/PEC markers in glomeruli, we next assessed their expression in situ. IRI-treated kidneys displayed elevated Clu and Ncam1 relative to sham kidneys in cells surrounding Podxl-stained glomeruli, consistent with expression of these markers in PEC (Figures 5C–5E). Similarly, Wnt16 mRNA was upregulated in PEC surrounding glomeruli following IRI, while Wt1 and Foxd1 mRNAs were upregulated in POD at the periphery of the glomerular tuft (Figures 5C–5E, S5A, and S5B). Furthermore, in support of our scRNA-seq data suggesting the presence of a proliferative subpopulation of POD/PEC cells, we detected Edu+/Ncam1+ PEC cells and Ki67+ cells surrounding glomeruli of IRI-treated kidneys (Figure S5C). Discussion

Our understanding of tubular regeneration has largely depended on studies examining repair of PTs, which comprises the most abundant cell type in the kidney and primary source of proliferative cells following AKI. However, far fewer studies have examined the regenerative capacity of more distal segments of the nephron and CD system. By transcriptionally profiling and fate mapping Clu-expressing cells, our study revealed diverse responses of Clu-expressing cells during nephrogenesis, homeostasis, and regeneration. In the developing kidney, Clu marked renal progenitors contributing to glomeruli, proximal, and distal segments of the nephron, as well as CDs. Under homeostatic conditions, Clu was detected specifically in the cortical segments of distal tubules including TAL and DCT. Taking advantage of this fact, we genetically labeled these segments prior to IRI and found that pre-existing Clu+ distal tubules of the cortex remained quiescent post injury, despite adjacent PTs showing elevated Edu incorporation. On the other hand, Clu lineage tracing post IRI indicated that CD cells uniquely activated a proliferative program, albeit less robust than PTs. Moreover, we found that a substantial subset of Clu+ CD cells persisted in a de-differentiated state following IRI, with reduced or undetectable levels of Aqp2. The early proliferative program displayed by CD coupled to the loss of Aqp2 may partially explain the apparent discrepancy between the observed decline in Clu-labelled Aqp2+ CD cells, amid the overall increase in Clu-labelled cells between day 3 and 20. However, considering the persistence of de-differentiated CD and decline in Clu-labeled CD in long-term tracing experiments, our data indicates that induction of a proliferation program in CD does not necessarily ensure successful repair of damaged CD. These results are consistent with previous fate mapping of Ki67+ proliferative cells coupled to single-nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNA-seq), which showed conclusively that proliferative PT cells give rise to both healthy differentiated PT, as well as PT in a maladaptive state persistently expressing injury markers such as Vcam1 and Havcr1.30

To our surprise, Clu also marked injured Havcr1+ proximal tubules (iPT) in a delayed fashion (>7 days post IRI), and like Aqp2+ CD cells, these cells were lost, or remained in an injured state in long-term tracing experiments. Our fate mapping studies are consistent with recent clinical data indicating that urinary CLU predicts kidney disease and correlates with P21+Ki67-epithelial senescence.21 Furthermore, in light of previously reported scRNA-seq datasets suggesting that iPT can undergo maladaptive responses characterized by dysregulated metabolic, inflammatory, and fibrotic gene programs,3,4,5,6,7,8 it will be interesting in the future to investigate whether similar programs are induced in injured CD.

Although our manuscript suggests that Clu marks a diverse set of tubules undergoing impaired regenerative responses, previous functional studies suggest that the Clu protein may promote repair in the kidney. For instance, analysis of cultured renal tubular cells and Clu knockout mice exposed to IRI, led Du and colleagues to conclude that Clu promotes proliferation and pro-survival autophagy in injured renal tubules.25,31 However in our study, we were unable to validate the pro-proliferative effects of Clu post IRI. As a secreted factor, recent work by Fan et al. demonstrated that Clu may have paracrine effects on neighboring cells as well.32 Indeed, this study showed that Clu derived from injured cardiomyocytes attenuates inflammation and promotes reparative polarization of Macs by binding to Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4). Consistent with these findings, loss of Clu was found to enhance pro-inflammatory M1 Macs following IRI.33 Thus, although our findings imply Clu-expressing tubules are largely on a maladaptive course, the non-cell autonomous functions of Clu may skew immune responses in favor of promoting tissue wide recovery following injury. Future studies will be required to uncover the functional consequences of Clu release from tubules undergoing maladaptive responses on neighboring tubules and/or the stromal compartment.

Analysis of our scRNA-seq dataset also revealed a relative increase in the abundance of cells displaying POD and PEC gene signatures at 30 days post IRI. Specifically, genes such as Wt1, Wnt16, Ncam1, and Foxd1, were found to be upregulated in glomeruli of IRI-treated kidneys, potentially reflecting an injury-induced developmental program in this compartment that is activated in the chronic injury phase. Consistent with this interpretation, our fate mapping studies revealed Clu+ nephron progenitors in neonates contributed to mature POD and PEC in weanlings. Moreover, other studies have found Wt1 increased in response to kidney injury and implicated in POD survival and regeneration.34,35,36 More surprisingly, we also identified ectopic expression of POD/PEC markers in the DTLs-ATLs, Fibs, and Macs, all of which were closely associated with each other in the medulla, suggesting that the spatial organization of these diverse lineages may be a factor driving this unique gene program. It is also interesting to note that this gene signature coincided with extensive expansion of Fibs and Macs within the medulla, leading us to hypothesize that a subset of ectopically expressed POD/PEC genes may promote a fibro-inflammatory program in the medulla. In support of this, Wt1 has been shown to promote fibroproliferation and myofibroblast transformation in fibrotic lung disease.37 Moreover, the induction of Wnt16 in the medulla may reflect enhanced Wnt signaling, which is an established driver of renal fibrosis.38 Indeed, analogous to our findings of ectopic expression of Wnt16 in DTL-ATL cells, a recent report found that injured PTs that maintain Sox9 expression upregulate various Wnt ligands, which in turn support neighboring myofibroblast differentiation and fibrosis.39 Further supporting our findings, other reports also identified Wnt-secreting Macs as key drivers of renal fibrosis.40,41 Further functional dissection of Wnt16 and other POD/PEC signature genes expressed in the medulla will be required to reveal their role in regulating renal fibrosis and inflammation.

Limitations of the study

Our scRNA-seq analysis was limited to mapping transcriptional signatures associated with sorted GFP positive, Clu expressing cells in the injured kidney. As a result, our dataset likely excluded an important number of cell types involved in acute injury responses. Another limitation of our study is that although we were able to detect and quantify a proliferative subpopulation of Clu+ CD induced by IRI, this population was not captured in the scRNA-seq dataset. Consequently, we were unable to compare their transcriptional profile with other proliferative cells, notably PTs. Further studies will thus be required to define the transcriptional states of proliferative CD.

Experimental model and study participant details

Animal model

Clu-CreERT2; Rosa26-LSL-tdTomato (CluTom), BAC-CluGFP, were previously described elsewhere (Ayyaz et al., 2019). Slc34a1-CreERT (Slc34a1tm1(EGFP/cre/ERT2)Bhum/J) and Col1a2-CreERT mice were obtained from Jackson Laboratories and were bred to Rosa26-LSL-tdTomato for lineage tracing.

Animal experiments were carried out in accordance with institutional guidelines for animal experimentation, and the procedures were approved by the Facility Animal Care Committee of the Research Institute of the McGill University Health Centre (AUP# 7908).

Unilateral ischemia–reperfusion injury model

Male mice (10-12 weeks of age) were used for IRI experiments. For ischemia-reperfusion-injury (IRI), mice were anesthetized with 1–3% isoflurane anesthesia. Unilateral kidney ischemia–reperfusion injury (IRI) was performed through a midline incision using microaneurysm clamps applied to the left renal pedicle while maintaining rectal temperature at 36–37°C on a self-regulated thermal pad. Clamping times for each experiment are indicated in the text. Of note, scRNAseq experiments involved a clamping time of 35 min, while other experiments, including validation of scRNAseq data by in situ hybridization, clamping was maintained for 24 min. Mice were sacrificed at indicated timepoints post reperfusion to collect ischemic (left) and control/sham (right) kidneys.

Cre recombinase activity was induced by injection with tamoxifen (Toronto Research Chemicals T5648) dissolved in corn oil via an intra-peritoneal (IP) route. For induction prior to IRI treatment, mice were dosed with 4 mg of tamoxifen 15 or 20 days before IRI. To activate lineage tracing in CluTom mice after IRI, mice were dosed with 0.4 mg of tamoxifen at 1 day or 7 days after IRI. For CluTom lineage tracing in post-natal kidneys, lactating mothers were injected IP with 4mg of tamoxifen at P1.

Method details

Tissue preparation for CluTom lineage tracing

Kidneys were fixed with 10% formalin overnight, then incubated in 30% sucrose at 4 °C overnight, and embedded in optimum cutting temperature compound (Sakura TissueTek) and 4-μm sections were cut. Tomato expressing cells were visualized using the Zeiss Axioscan.

Immunofluorescence and in situ hybridization

Dissected kidneys were fixed in 10% buffered formalin overnight and embedded in paraffin using standard histological procedures. Paraffin embedded kidney sections were steamed in Citrate antigen retrieval buffer (Sigma, C9999) and then blocked with 2% bovine serum albumin in PBS for 30 min at room temperature. After blocking, the sections were incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies against Aqp2 (Sigma, A7310), Calbindin (ABclonal, A0802), E-Cadherin (BD, 610181), F4/80 (Cell signaling 70076), GFP (Abcam, Ab6673), Lrp2 (LSBio, LS-B15411). Secondary antibodies from Cell Signalling Technologies: Alexa-555 (anti-mouse, 4409; anti-rabbit, 4413) or Alexa-647-labeled (anti-mouse, 4410, anti-rabbit, 4414) were used for signal detection. When appropriate sections were incubated with Lotus Tetragonolobus Lectin (LTL)-fluorescein (FITC) solution (Invitrogen, L32480) (1:200 in 2% BSA in PBS) for 1 h, counterstained with DAPI and scanned with Zeiss Axioscan. In situ hybridization (ISH) was performed on formalin fixed paraffin embedded sections as above using a RNAscope Multiplex FL Kit v2 Kit and TSA Vivid Dyes (ACD Bio) according to manufacturer’s instructions. If antibody stainings were combined with RNAscope assays, antibody stainings were done after ISH procedure. Positive cell detection and/or signal intensity was measured using Qupath42 and Zeiss Zen imaging analysis software.

EdU labeling

5mg of EdU (5-Ethynyl-20-deoxyuridine) (Toronto Research Chemicals, E932175) per 100 g of body weight was injected intraperitoneally one hour prior to sacrifice. To reveal Edu staining, sections were incubated with EdU detection buffer (1 M Tris–HCl, pH 8.5, 10 μM AZDye 488 Azide, 4 mM CuSO4; 100 mM L-ascorbic acid) for 30 min at room temperature.

Isolation of kidney cells for scRNAseq analysis

BAC-CluGFP mice were sacrificed at day 2, 7, or 30 after 35 min IRI treatment. Kidneys were perfused with ice-cold PBS. Mice were anesthetized, sacrificed and immediately perfused with PBS from the left ventricle, and kidneys were removed The fresh kidney tissue was finely cut in a Petri dish using a razor blade in 1 mL of cold Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) and digested with DMEM containing Collagenase II (2 mg/mL, Gibco, 17101-015), Dispase II (2mg/mL, Gibco, 17105-041), and DNase I (30 mg/mL, Roche, 10 104 159 001) at 37°C for 20 min (pipetted every 2 min); then 5 min shaken (200 RPM, 37C). Dissociated tissue was passed through a 70 and then 40 μm cell strainer and centrifuged at 1600 rpm for 5 min at 4°C. Pellets were re-suspend in Red Blood Cell Lysing Buffer Hybri-Max (Sigma, R7757) for 5 min at room temperature in order to remove remaining red blood cells. Cells were labelled with DAPI (ThermoFisher #1306); cells negative for DAPI were considered alive Cells were then sorted using FACSAria IIu Cell Sorter (Becton Dickinson). Collected GFP + cells were used for RNA seq.

scRNAseq analysis

Single-cell suspensions were washed and resuspended in PBS with 0.04% BSA. An aliquot of cells was used for LIVE/DEAD viability testing (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Single-cell libraries were generated using the 10x Genomics Chromium Controller instrument and Chromium Next GEM Single Cell 3ʹ GEM, Library & Gel Bead Kit v3.1 (10x Genomics) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, cells suspended in reverse transcription reagents, along with gel beads, were segregated into aqueous nanoliter-scale gel bead-in-emulsions (GEMs). The GEMs were then reverse transcribed in a T1000 Thermal cycler (Bio-Rad) programed at 53°C for 45 min, 85°C for 5 min, and hold at 4°C. After reverse transcription, single-cell droplets were broken and the single-strand cDNA was isolated and cleaned with Cleanup Mix containing DynaBeads (Thermo Fisher Scientific). cDNA was then amplified with a T1000 Thermal cycler programed at 98°C for 3 min, 12 cycles of (98°C for 15 s, 67°C for 20 s, 72°C for 1 min), 72°C for 1 min, and hold at 4°C. Subsequently, the amplified cDNA was fragmented, end-repaired, A-tailed and index adaptor ligated, with SPRIselect Reagent Kit (Beckman Coulter) with cleanup in between steps. Post-ligation product was amplified with a T1000 Thermal cycler programed at 98°C for 45 s, 12 cycles of (98°C for 20 s, 54°C for 30 s, 72°C for 20 s), 72°C for 1 min, and hold at 4°C. The sequencing-ready libraries were cleaned up with SPRIselect, quality controlled for size distribution and yield (LabChip GX Perkin Elmer), and quantified using qPCR (KAPA Biosystems Library Quantification Kit for Illumina platforms). Libraries were loaded on MGI DNBSEQ-G400 and sequenced using the following parameters: 28 bp Read1, 8 bp Index i7, and 150 bp Read2. Raw sequencing data for each sample was converted to matrices of expression counts using the Cell Ranger software provided by 10X Genomics (version 4.0.0).

Sequences were aligned to the mm10 mouse transcriptome using CellRanger’s “count” function (v7.0.1). The resulting gene-barcode matrices were imported and analyzed using the R package Seurat v5.0.2.43 Genes expressed in fewer than three cells and cells expressing fewer than 200 genes were excluded. Cells with more than 70,000 total transcripts were removed, as they likely represent doublets. Additionally, cells in which over 35% of transcripts originated from mitochondrial genes were filtered out, as this may indicate stressed, dying, or damaged cells. Data was normalized using a scale factor of 10,000. To integrate data across samples, integration features were selected using Seurat’s “SelectIntegrationFeatures” function, and sample-specific anchors were identified using “FindIntegrationAnchors”. We then applied “IntegrateData” to correct for batch effects across samples. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed on the integrated dataset, and the top 30 principal components were used to construct a k-nearest neighbor (kNN) graph. This graph was used as input for clustering with the Louvain algorithm via Seurat’s “FindClusters” function (resolution = 0.8). Cell types were annotated manually based on a curated list of marker genes.

To assess GFP expression across cell types, the GFP transcript sequence was appended to the mm10 mouse reference genome using Cell Ranger’s “mkref” function, and sequencing reads were aligned using the “count” function. Detection was defined as cells with non-zero GFP counts. The distribution of log1p-transformed GFP counts across annotated cell types was visualized using violin plots. Importantly, dimensional reduction, data integration, clustering, and cell-type annotation were performed independently of GFP expression; GFP was not included as a variable in constructing the UMAP space or performing clustering analysis.

Quantification and statistical analysis

All statistical analyses and graphs were obtained using Graphpad Prism v10.5.0. Unless otherwise stated each data point in graphs represents the average value of greater than 3 regions of interest obtained from a single mouse. The Gaussian distribution of all datasets was confirmed using the Shapiro-Wilk normality test. Data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Comparisons between two groups were conducted using an unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test. Comparisons between three groups with one independent variable was analysed with an ordinary one-way ANOVA. Comparisons between three groups involving 2 independent variables were performed using two-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test. A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
