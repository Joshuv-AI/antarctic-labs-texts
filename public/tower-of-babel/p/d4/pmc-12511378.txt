Optimizing breast cancer chemotherapy by harnessing gut microbiota with insights from artificial intelligence
Authors: Yi Zhou, Min Jiang, Ke Shen, Tao He, Siyu Zhou, Aibin Shao, Xiaoyu Li, Jiao Wang, Rongrong Wu, Rajeev K Singla, Jonathan P Jacobs, Bairong Shen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractOptimizing chemotherapy for breast cancer (BC) remains a critical challenge. Gut microbiota (GM) dysbiosis, varying across BC subtypes and stages, influences BC development and chemotherapy response through immune and metabolic pathways. Chemotherapy also disrupts the microbiota, creating a complex bidirectional interaction. Integrating artificial intelligence (AI) allows the identification of hidden patterns, deepening our understanding of the microbiota’s role. This integrated approach promises to revolutionize BC treatment with novel insights and personalized interventions.Subject terms: Cancer, Computational biology and bioinformatics, Microbiology

Introduction

BC is the leading cause of cancer-related deaths among women worldwide, with an estimated 2.3 million new cases and 666,000 deaths reported in 20221. Approximately 80% of BC cases are diagnosed in women aged over 50, with 40% being diagnosed in women older than 65 years2,3. Various risk factors, such as diet4, alcohol consumption5, reproductive history6, and genetics7, have been associated with BC. However, only a few BC patients have a genetic predisposition or identified risk factor exposure. Due to this heterogeneity, BC has been categorized into different subtypes based on the expression levels of estrogen receptor (ER), progesterone receptor (PR), and human epidermal growth factor 2 (HER2): Luminal A subtype (ERhigh+, PRhigh+, HER2-), Luminal B subtype [(ERlow+, PRlow+, HER2-) or (ERlow+, PRlow+, HER2+)], HER2 subtype (ER-, PR-, HER2+) and triple-negative breast cancer [TNBC, (ER-, PR-, HER2-)]8,9. The heterogeneity of BC patients poses significant challenges in designing chemotherapy regimens, often resulting in drug resistance, tolerance, and adverse effects (AEs). Tools such as Oncotype DX assist in treatment decision-making and prognosis assessment, but fall short in personalization and intelligence10. Increasing evidence suggests that GM plays a significant role in influencing chemotherapy response. For instance, combining antibiotics with anticancer drugs can enhance or impair therapeutic efficacy11,12 (Fig. 1). Therefore, leveraging artificial intelligence (AI) to integrate multi-dimensional, multi-omic, and multi-modal data sets could provide new insights into BC management.

Antibiotics affect the efficacy of chemotherapy treatment by interfering with the GM.

For example, antibiotic treatment has been shown to negatively impact the effectiveness of trastuzumab. This can be observed through decreased alpha diversity, reduced abundance of specific taxonomic groups, and alterations in the immune signature. Antibiotics have been found to decrease the activation of DCs and the release of IL12p70 when trastuzumab is administered, resulting in reduced efficacy of the drug. However, the administration of rIL12p70 to mice receiving antibiotic treatment restored the efficacy of trastuzumab by increasing the recruitment of NK cells and basal activation. The human body harbors diverse microbes across the skin, gastrointestinal tract, respiratory and genitourinary systems, and even parenchymal tissues like the breast13–15. This microbial ecosystem has co-evolved with humans, becoming an integral and indispensable “organ” of the human body16. Studies have highlighted significant differences between the GM of BC patients and healthy individuals17–20. For instance, compared to the BC patients, the healthy individuals exhibit a high abundance of Coprococcus and Anaerostipes, while Bacteroides and Finegoldia are more abundant in BC patients. Additionally, Varibaculum, Dialister, and Megamonas have been found to dominate in patients with ductal carcinoma in situ. Moreover, 45 microbial species were identified as significantly different between postmenopausal BC patients and postmenopausal controls, although no differences were observed between premenopausal BC patients and premenopausal controls20. Furthermore, the GM has been shown to differ among BC patients at different stages of progression and subtypes21. Nutritional supplements have been shown to contribute to chemotherapy efficacy by harnessing GM, potentially influencing cancer development and drug efficacy (Table 1). This highlights a potential opportunity for manipulating the GM to optimize chemotherapy results.

AI has emerged as a powerful tool for unraveling the intricate interactions and patterns between the GM and BC. Moreover, AI-driven tools such as machine learning (ML) and deep learning (DL) have been employed to identify key microbial signatures and predict chemotherapy responses22–26. These algorithms can model nonlinear relationships and uncover subtle patterns that traditional statistical methods may overlook. AI also enables the identification of microbiota-derived biomarkers, aiding in early diagnosis, prognosis, and therapy optimization26,27. In addition, AI-powered natural language processing (NLP) tools have streamlined the extraction of critical information from scientific literature, accelerating hypothesis generation and experimental design27,28. Furthermore, AI integration in clinical decision support systems has the potential to guide oncologists in personalized chemotherapy regimens by incorporating patient-specific microbiota profiles29,30.

As AI continues to evolve, its application in microbiome research expands to include dynamic modeling of microbiota-host interactions and simulation of treatment outcomes under various scenarios31–33. This review critically examines how AI-driven approaches are advancing research in this domain. By providing a comprehensive synthesis of current advances in “GM-Chemotherapy-BC” interaction and outlining promising future directions, we underscore the transformative potential of AI in elucidating complex host-microbiota relationships and optimizing therapeutic strategies for BC.

GM features in BC patients

GM composition

Unraveling the intricate relationship between GM and BC remains a challenge. Like macro-ecosystems, the intestinal ecosystem is shaped by species diversity, abundance, niche, and interspecies interactions34,35. Disruptions in this delicate balance, known as dysbiosis, can contribute to chronic inflammation, immune evasion that promotes tumorigenesis, and abnormal host cell proliferation and apoptosis36–38.

Emerging research suggests a strong association between GM diversity and BC22,39–41. Microbial diversity is a key indicator of ecosystem stability and a prerequisite for optimal GM function42. Several studies have explored variations in GM diversity across menopausal stages, given the critical role of hormonal fluctuations in BC development. In premenopausal BC patients, the α-diversity was significantly reduced, and the β-diversity significantly differed between BC patients and controls43. Conversely, postmenopausal women exhibit lower Shannon diversity than their premenopausal counterparts44,45, yet case-control studies have shown higher α-diversity in both premenopausal and postmenopausal BC patients20. Conflicting findings further complicate the picture, and another study found that HER2+ BC patients had 12-23% lower α-diversity than HER2- patients21, while others reported no significant differences in microbial diversity based on menopausal status46,47, hormone receptor expression, tumor grade, or disease stage21,48.

The abundance of specific GM taxonomy also exhibits notable differences in BC patients. Studies found significant differences between healthy and BC patients in specific bacterial genera. For example, the metastatic BC group is dominated by Prevotella, Porphyromonas, Peptoniphilus, and Megamonas, while the patients with benign breast lesions have major taxonomics including Lactobacillus, Escherichia, and Coprobacillus. In contrast, healthy individuals have more abundance of Cloacibacillus, Asaccharobacter, Christensenella, Alistipes, Tissierella, Hydrogenoanaerobacterium, Butyricimonas, Acidaminococcus, Oxalobacter, Collinsella, and Eubacterium49. Importantly, microbial composition appears to correlate with BC progression. In a longitudinal study, the Eubacteriaceae family, A. muciniphila, D. piger, C. comes, C. catus, and C. aerofaciens were dominant in the healthy individuals50. On the other hand, patients with aggressive tumors were found to have an overrepresentation of Lachnospiraceae family members, Enterobacteriaceae family members, Clostridiaceae family members, Streptococcus spp., Veillonella spp., Bacteroides spp., and E. ramosum49. Notably, A. muciniphila, D. piger, and B. vulgatus were associated with early-stage BC (N- or ≤pT1)50. In addition, differential microbiota taxonomy was also observed in different subtypes of BC. Compared to Luminal B and TNBC patients, Luminal A exhibited a higher abundance of Verrucomicrobia and Cyanobacteria. While Luminal B had more abundance of Fusobacteria, TNBC showed increased Actinobacteria51. The HER2+ patients had a higher abundance of Alistipes, Enterococcus, and Acidaminococcus, and a lower abundance of Methanobrevibacter, Christensenellaceae, Turicibacter, Clostridium, Blautia, Coprococcus, Ruminococcus, and Desulfovibrio compared to HER2- patients21. Recently, the concept of “guilds”, functionally similar groups of microorganisms, has been introduced to provide a functional perspective on microbial ecosystems39,52. Studies have shown significant changes in the GM composition under pathological conditions compared to healthy individuals40. McCune et al.17 identified Bacteroides and Enterobacteriaceae-dominated guilds as more abundant in the early-stage BC. In contrast, a lower abundance of Clostridiales-dominated guild was observed in the healthy group. Therefore, understanding the GM community should not only focus on specific taxonomy but also consider microbiota clusters to reveal the relationship between GM and BC41,53,54.

The high variability in BC patients and GM profiles, coupled with inconsistent findings across studies, hinders the development of a universal microbiome-BC model. For instance, only two genera (Actinomyces and Shewanella) were consistently associated with BC in multiple studies across the 7 different studies20,43,47,48,55,56. Notably, two other studies failed to identify significant differences (Table 2)45,47. Guild analysis, which emphasizes microbial functional capacity over taxonomic classification, may provide a more robust framework for studying these complex interactions. The relationships between function and composition of the GM within guilds are particularly amenable to AI-driven analyses.

GM estrobolome and BC

The term “estrobolome” was first introduced by Plottel and Blaser and refers to bacterial genes within the GM capable of metabolizing estrogens57. It regulates systemic estrogen levels via β-glucuronidase (BGUS)-mediated reactivation of conjugated estrogens (e.g., oestrone and estradiol), which are inactivated in the liver and reabsorbed after microbial deconjugation in the gut (Fig. 2)56,58,59. This biochemical crosstalk links gut microbes to BC despite anatomical separation60.

Reactivation of oestrone and estradiol by gut microbes derived β-glucuronidase.

In the liver, estrone (E1) and estradiol (E2) are inactivated by UDP-glucuronosyltransferases (UGTs) through conjugation with glucuronic acid, forming estrone-3-glucuronide and estrone-17-glucuronide. These conjugated forms are excreted into the intestine via bile. In the gut, β-glucuronidase enzymes produced by the gut microbes cleave the glucuronic acid moiety, thereby reactivating E1 and E2. The reactivated hormones are then reabsorbed by intestinal epithelial cells and enter systemic circulation, potentially reaching hormone-sensitive target tissues, such as the breast tissues. This process emphasizes the role of gut microbial enzymes in modulating estrogen. The figure is generated by BioRender.

BGUS activity is correlated with age, though strain-specific variations exist (e.g., elevated Lactobacillus but reduced Enterococcus activity in the elderly)61,62. Its pharmacological plasticity is evident: antibiotics like ampicillin elevate fecal conjugated estrogens by depleting BGUS-producing taxa63,64, while long-term estrogen replacement therapy reduces microbial GUS activity via Lactobacillaceae shifts62. Pharmacologic BGUS inhibitors suppress estrogen reactivation65 and alleviate chemotherapy toxicity [e.g., irinotecan (SN-38) in preclinical models]66,67, suggesting therapeutic potential to enhance BC treatment safety and efficacy. However, how chemotherapy shapes estrobolome functionality remains underexplored.

Microbial metabolites associated with BC

Beyond the estrobolome, GM produces a variety of metabolites influencing BC progression and therapy68. In dysbiosis, GM-derived metabolites can disrupt the intestinal barrier, activating inflammatory myeloid cells associated with tumor-related inflammation69. Trimethylamine N-oxide (TMAO), produced from microbial metabolism of trimethylamine-containing nutrients70, enhances antitumor immunity in the 4T1 mouse model by increasing IFN-γ, TNF-α, and CD8 + T cell activation, improving anti-PD-1 therapy efficacy71. Additionally, mice fed a choline-rich diet exhibited higher TMAO levels and infiltration of the CD8+ T cell population, and this positive effect was reversed when a trimethylamine-lyase inhibitor (cutC inhibitor) was co-administered. The linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe) analysis further revealed a positive correlation between Clostridiales and tumor TMAO levels71.

Short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), such as butyrate, have been considered an anti-inflammatory agent as they inhibit the activation of NF-κB in intestinal epithelial cells72. Meanwhile, butyrate can also regulate the immune response by interacting with specific G-protein-coupled receptors expressed on immune cells73. This may explain the lower abundance of Roseburia inulinivorans (a butyrate producer) in postmenopausal BC patients20. Additionally, sodium propionate exerts anticancer effects in mice bearing BC cell xenograft by regulating JAK2/STAT3/ROS/p38 MAPK signaling74.

Lithocholic acid (LCA) and deoxycholic acid are important metabolites derived from the microbial metabolism of primary bile acids75. Early-stage BC patients exhibit reduced serum LCA levels, which are associated with decreased cancer cell proliferation and vascular endothelial growth factor production. The reduction of the baiH gene, a key enzyme in LCA generation, has also been verified in the fecal microbiome of early BC patients75.

The fecal microbial DNA genome of early-stage BC women exhibits a reduced abundance of the bacterial genes CadA and LdcC, which are responsible for bacterial cadaverine production76. Additionally, cadaverine has been demonstrated to influence the pharmacokinetics of 5-fluorouracil and paclitaxel in vitro77. Moreover, an elevated presence of cadaverine has been observed in the serum of BC patients78. Cadaverine is also associated with improved BC outcomes, including reduced tumor mass, fewer metastases, and lower-grade tumors76,79.

The bacterial tryptophan metabolite indole propionic acid (IPA) is suppressed in women diagnosed with BC80. Furthermore, IPA has been identified as a cytostatic bacterial metabolite that specifically targets BC cells by reducing the proportions of cancer stem cells and inhibiting the proliferation, movement, and formation of metastases81. Sari et al. demonstrated that the IPA contributes to the inhibition of epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition and enhances the antitumor immune response through the promotion of oxidative/nitrosative stress and increased production of reactive species in mitochondria80.

Moreover, numerous metabolic pathways or predicted pathways associated with host immunity have been identified as influenced by GM (Table 3). These previous studies support the notion that alterations in the gut microbial community are associated with BC. As mentioned earlier, there have been conflicting findings regarding the association between GM and BC progression. These conflicting and diverse results indicate several points:

The GM is complex, and BC is a heterogeneous disease. Although various gut microbes/guilds related to BC have been identified across different studies, their causal relationships remain largely unclear.

It is essential to consider potential differences between studies conducted by different laboratories using their specific cohorts. These differences cannot be universally generalized to other laboratories. Given the high heterogeneity of BC as a disease, it raises the question of whether additional factors were not considered in the current study. For instance, some studies have found no differences in Faith’s phylogenetic diversity and β diversity between BC patients and healthy controls82. However, in some cases, considering more variables, such as genotype, TNM stage, metastasis, and tumor size, β diversity was able to distinguish patients from control groups83.

GM and BC chemotherapy

Current status of chemotherapy in BC

Chemotherapy remains a cornerstone of BC treatment, serving as a critical component in multimodal therapeutic strategies. However, its application varies based on disease stage, tumor subtype, and individual patient characteristics8,9. First-line regimens typically combine anthracyclines, taxanes, antimetabolites, and platinum-based agents, administered through adjuvant, neoadjuvant, or dose-dependent chemotherapy, tailored to individual therapeutic objectives84. While chemotherapy is a vital treatment, it is associated with significant AEs, including diarrhea, body weight gain, and neurotoxicity, which can impact quality of life and limit treatment use85–87. Dfferent kinds of liposomal and nanoparticle drugs are being developed for BC to reduce side effects and improve drug targeting and efficacy88–91. Additionally, both innate and acquired drug resistance remain significant challenges, prompting the development of novel therapeutic strategies92. Furthermore, genetic, epigenetic, and GM variations influence chemotherapy response, highlighting the importance of personalized medicine93,94.

Chemotherapeutic agents alter the GM in BC

Chemotherapy not only targets cancer cells but also significantly affects GM, leading to alterations in its biodiversity, composition, and metabolism95,96 (Table 1). In previous studies, comparisons of GM before and after chemotherapy, or between patients who received chemotherapy and those who did not, reveal the impact of chemotherapeutic drugs on GM alterations. Paclitaxel reduces α-diversity in 4T1 tumor-bearing mice without affecting β-diversity97, whereas TNBC patients achieving pathological complete response (CR) post-chemotherapy exhibit higher α-diversity98. Doxorubicin treatment over four weeks significantly shifts β-diversity99, and comparisons with healthy controls reveal distinct β-diversity profiles in treated patients, though α-diversity remains unchanged100. Specific taxonomic shifts include enrichment of Betaproteobacteria in rats treated with lapatinib101 and reductions in Proteobacteria, Lactobacillus, and Ruminococcaceae genera with adriamycin, cyclophosphamide, and docetaxel102. In stage I BC patients, post-anthracycline-taxane treatment [anthracycline-taxane (93%), taxane- (7%) based], pN- status is marked by dominance of B. vulgatus, B. obeum, and D. formicigenerans, while pN+ or stage II-III patients show overabundance of E. ramosum, Clostridium species, and others tied to unfavorable prognosis50. Metabolically, 27 differential metabolites were observed in patients (Grade II, ER+, and PR+) after two-cycle FEC100 treatment (5-fluorouracil, epirubicin, cyclophosphamide). Good responders (partial response but no progressive disease) showed higher levels of certain amino acids, while chemotherapy-insensitive patients (who have progressive disease) had increased uracil, tyrosine, and acetone103. In humanized mice, tamoxifen treatment elevates 117 cecal metabolites compared to 75 with placebo104.

This evidence suggests that the effects of chemotherapy on GM are multifaceted and include alterations in biodiversity, microbial abundance, and metabolic profiles. These changes may affect the prognosis of BC patients through drug metabolism, immune regulation, and metabolic interactions. However, due to population heterogeneity and the limited clinical trial data, current research on the changes in GM characteristics before and after chemotherapy remains scarce. A significant knowledge gap persists in understanding the causal mechanisms, particularly regarding how specific chemotherapeutic agents differentially modulate microbial communities and their mechanisms of action.

Chemotherapeutic drugs are affected by GM in BC

The bidirectional exchanges between GM and pharmaceuticals have been described as “pharmacomicrobiomic”105. In addition, intervention of GM by probiotics85,86,106–108, prebiotics12,95,109,110, other nutritional supplements, and even antibiotics is an effective strategy for manipulating chemotherapy drug response (Table 4)11,111–113. This has greatly encouraged attention to the role of the microbiome in influencing drug efficacy in personalized treatment (Fig. 3). Several human and animal microbiome studies on BC treatment have shown various prognoses or responses to chemotherapy94,97,101,107–109.

The interaction with the GM and BC is bidirectional.

Alterations in the GM, characterized by changes in α-diversity, β-diversity, specific taxonomy, and metabolite profiles, can influence the physical and biological barrier functions and immune regulation, thereby affecting the onset and progression of breast cancer. Conversely, BC and its associated treatments can also lead to significant changes in GM composition and function. Furthermore, microbial metabolites and functional genes may be actively involved in modulating chemotherapy-associated adverse effects, drug efficacy, and resistance. These bidirectional interactions open a therapeutic window wherein targeted modulation of GM via probiotics, prebiotics, or antibiotics, in combination with chemotherapy, may potentially enhance treatment efficacy. GM significantly influences chemotherapy efficacy in BC patients. A comparison study of patients before receiving neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NAC) revealed that Bacteroides were enriched in patients who were later proven to be NAC non-responders, while 15 other genera, including Coprococcus and uncultured Ruminococcus sp., predominated in NAC-effective patients (Table 5)114. Notably, Coprococcus and uncultured Ruminococcus sp. are significantly overrepresented in patients with advanced blood CD4+ T cells, and Coprococcus, Dorea, and uncultured Ruminococcus sp. showed positive correlations with tumor-infiltrating CD4+ T lymphocytes (TIL). This favorable CD4+ and CD8+ TIL and increased levels of CD4+ T cells in peripheral blood were observed in NAC-effective patients114. The influence of GM on chemotherapy drug efficacy is further shown by its distinct features altered through antibiotic interventions. In a 4T1 cell-bearing mouse model, co-treatment with antibiotic cocktails resulted in a better response to doxorubicin, as evidenced by reduced tumor size, fewer metastatic lung lesions, and alterations in Ki-67 and caspase-3 immunoreactivity99. Additionally, Prajnamitra et al. reported that the tumor vascular permeability was enhanced, which allowed more liposomal doxorubicin nanoparticles to accumulate in the breast tumor after mice bearing 4T1 cells were treated with antibiotic cocktails40. In another study, distinct GM was created by vancomycin administration and transplanted into mice implanted with MI6 cells using fecal microbiota transplantation. The results revealed that mice receiving fecal microbiota from conventional mice exhibited significantly enhanced tumor growth inhibition when treated with trastuzumab, compared to those receiving microbiota from vancomycin-treated mice12. In addition, BC patients who achieved pathologic complete response (CR, defined as no residual invasive tumor in the resected specimen) to trastuzumab exhibited a higher α-diversity and higher abundance of Lachnospiraceae, Turicibacteraceae, Bifidobacteriaceae, and Prevotellaceae compared with patients who had non-response (NR, defined as presenting residual invasive tumor in the resected specimen). Notably, these GM features were positively correlated with interferon, NO2-IL12, activated CD4+ T cells, and dendritic cells in tumors of CR patients (Table 5)12. These results suggest that the difference in GM is one of the critical factors leading to the difference in the efficacy of chemotherapy drugs.

Chemotherapy often induces AEs such as diarrhea, chemotherapy-related cognitive impairment (CRCI), and weight gain, which may be mitigated by microbial modulation. In a case study, a patient with stage IV BC developed grade Ⅱ chemotherapy-induced diarrhea during treatment. The diarrhea was effectively managed using multi-strain combined probiotics86. Another study suggested that supplementation with probiotic capsules has the potential to alleviate the metabolic profile shift associated with the development of CRCI85. Probiotic supplementation during chemotherapy did not alter the α- and β-diversity of GM. However, plasma levels of p-Mentha-1,8-dien-7-ol, linoelaidyl carnitine, and 1-aminocyclopropane-1-carboxylic acid shifted, with these changes inversely linked to chemotherapy-related CRCI. A rat study later confirmed that p-Mentha-1,8-dien-7-ol alleviates chemotherapy-induced long-term potentiation deficits, synaptic damage, oxidative stress, and glial activation85. Furthermore, patients supplied with probiotics during docetaxel-based chemotherapy experienced smaller body weight gain, lower body mass index, lower body fat percentage, and lower levels of plasma and low-density lipoprotein (LDL). Additionally, the abundance of Bacteroides and Anaerostipes in the GM was negatively correlated with changes in LDL levels in patients who received probiotic supplementation106.

The GM has emerged as a possible element contributing to these variable chemotherapy outcomes, regardless of individual differences between hosts93,94,99,115–117. However, most studies are observational and lack clear evidence of causality; additionally, limited sample sizes and significant population heterogeneity restrict the application of this finding in clinical practice. The integration of multi-omics data driven by AI can significantly promote this process.

GM and AI in BC

High-throughput sequencing technologies have enabled us to characterize massive GM data16,118,119. However, translating these data into valuable insights is still a considerable barrier. The microbiome’s complexity is characterized by vast interindividual variability, dynamic changes over time, and multifactorial influences120. Traditional statistical methods, which emphasize statistical inference, parameter estimation, and hypothesis testing, often fall short in analyzing microbiome data characterized by high dimensionality, sparsity, and compositionality. In contrast, AI-based analytical approaches have emerged as transformative tools in microbiome research, adept at handling unstructured and high-dimensional data. These methods offer automated learning and predictive capabilities, enabling the discovery of intricate patterns and relationships within complex datasets22,121. While exploring various documents on PubMed using search terms, we retrieved 22 documents on December 27, 2024. A word cloud has been generated to analyze prevalent keywords in the abstracts of those 22 documents (Fig. 4). Though the number of studies is still limited, AI-derived methods express their potential in aiding the diagnosis, prognosis, and treatment of BC and facilitating the identification of the relationship between GM and BC.

Artificial intelligence

ML methods, including supervised, unsupervised, and semi-supervised learning, facilitate microbiome-based patient stratification through systematic analysis of microbiome profiles. Supervised learning algorithms such as support vector machines (SVM), random forests (RF), and gradient boosting models (e.g., XGB, LGBM) have been employed to predict chemotherapy response based on GM composition. Unsupervised methods like K-means and Gaussian mixture model (GMM) help distinguish microbial profiles between responders and non-responders122,123. Semi-supervised learning integrates labeled and unlabeled microbiome data, addressing interindividual variability influenced by genetic, environmental, dietary, and lifestyle factors124. DL, a subset of ML, excels at modeling complex, nonlinear relationships in high-dimensional microbiome datasets. Architectures such as artificial neural networks (ANNs) and convolutional neural networks (CNNs) have been applied to identify key gut microbial patterns influencing chemotherapy efficacy and toxicity125. Additionally, natural language processing (NLP) further enhances AI-driven microbiome research by extracting valuable insights from biomedical literature and patient records96. Among NLP models, bidirectional encoder representations from transformers (BERT) are widely used in medical applications126, including BioBERT127, ClinicalBERT128, and PubMedBERT129, facilitating automated text analysis, enabling researchers to uncover microbiome-based predictive biomarkers for BC chemotherapy118,119.

AI in GM-based BC classification

Leveraging the data processing power of AI, we can integrate multi-omics data to classify BC patients’ GM and identify cancer-related biomarkers. Using an RF model with 500 trees, researchers identified 15 key microbial features distinguishing BC patients from healthy controls, nine of which showed significant differences between the two groups. Notably, two of these nine species were identified as significant in RF analysis but not in the univariate test, highlighting the model’s ability to capture complex microbial associations130. Further, another study utilizing sequencing data from multiple publications developed a 10-fold cross-validation RF model based on the top 30 genera from the stool microbiome, achieving a comparable area under curve (AUC) of 68.08%131. However, when they applied to different validation cohorts, the model demonstrated varying AUC values, with the Goedert_USA cohort achieving an AUC of 0.5445, while the Byrd_Ghana cohort achieved an AUC of 0.5918. These discrepancies indicate the importance of accounting for population heterogeneity and lifestyle factors in microbiome-based classification.

Moreover, comparative studies have demonstrated substantial variations in model performance. Kibria et al. applied multiple machine learning models, including SVM, LGBM, RF, XGB, logistic regression (LR), linear discriminant analysis (LDA), and deep neural networks (DNNs), to identify key microbial differences between BC patients and healthy controls in a Ghanaian cohort. Their findings demonstrated that LGBM achieved the highest predictive performance in training data (precision = 0.76, recall = 0.77, F1-score = 0.77, accuracy = 0.85, AUC = 0.93), whereas XGB outperformed other models in test data (precision = 0.62, recall = 0.74, F1-score = 0.67, accuracy = 0.69, AUC = 0.75)132. Additionally, feature selection methods, including information gain (IG), correlation-based selection (CBS), consistency feature selection (CFS), and heuristic filter evaluation (HFE), were applied to refine GM data, followed by the development of nine classification models: naïve Bayes (NB), RF, SVM, RF with AdaBoost (AB), RF with bagging (BRF), naïve Bayes with bagging (BNB), LR, Bayesian networks (BN), and decision trees (DT). These models were used to differentiate between benign, invasive, and control groups. In distinguishing benign from control cases, the BNB model using CFS-selected features achieved the highest performance (AUC = 0.943), while the DT model had the worst performance (AUC = 0.755). For differentiating invasive from control cases, the BNB model again performed best (AUC = 0.829), and the BRF model had the lowest performance (AUC = 0.771). In distinguishing invasive from benign cases, the BNB model using CBS-selected features achieved the highest performance (AUC = 0.920), whereas the NB, BN, and DT models had AUC values below 0.65133. Furthermore, literature-based classification of cancer through GM features has begun to emerge. For example, MarkerGenie, an NLP-enabled text-mining system, has been developed and used to identify 14 microbial markers associated with colorectal cancer. A model constructed using five of these microbial markers achieved an AUC of 0.852, with a sensitivity of 0.692 and a specificity of 0.93526. These examples highlight the potential of NLP to revolutionize the way we analyze and synthesize microbiome-related literature, paving the way for uncovering novel insights into the role of GM in BC classification.

These studies highlight the potential of AI-driven approaches in microbiome-based BC classification while also emphasizing the need for model validation across diverse populations to enhance generalizability.

AI in GM-based BC chemotherapy efficacy

Beyond classification, AI also plays a crucial role in predicting chemotherapy efficacy in BC patients. AI has demonstrated excellent performance in predicting BC chemotherapy efficacy based on differential gene expression and pathological image analysis (Table 6). For example, the RF algorithm was employed to identify genes exhibiting significant differential expression (defined as |log2FC | >5), followed by developing a classification model incorporating a cross-validation strategy. This computational framework demonstrated predictive efficacy for drug sensitivity in 55% of TNBC clinical specimens while simultaneously validating its cross-dataset generalizability (highest AUC 0.914) through rigorous external validation procedures134. The machine learning pipeline IMPRESS integrates multi-stain histopathologic imaging features to predict NAC response in BC, achieving high accuracy (HER2+ AUC 0.8975 and TNBC AUC 0.7674) and outperforming manual pathological assessments through interpretable feature extraction and external validation135. This approach has also begun to be applied in GM research. For instance, in HER2-negative metastatic BC patients treated with cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitors, no significant differences between CR and NR were observed in α- or β-diversity at the phylum or species level. However, an RF model trained on GM composition effectively predicted metastatic BC patients’ responses to CDK4/6 inhibitors, achieving an impressive AUC of 0.946, with a specificity of 0.990 and sensitivity of 0.80422,136. Another notable AI application utilized a 5-fold cross-validation model employing decision trees based on 31 differentially abundant species between CR and NR, the performance is lower (AUC = 0.652). The prediction performance was improved when incorporating metagenomic KEGG pathways and species relative abundance data. This model achieved outstanding predictive performance (AUC = 0.895) in determining chemotherapy responsiveness across BC and other tumor types137.

AI in the management of chemotherapy-induced AEs in BC

AI is also being leveraged to manage chemotherapy-induced AEs through GM analysis. Chemotherapy drugs such as irinotecan, capecitabine, and 5-fluorouracil are well-documented to induce side effects like intestinal mucositis, neurotoxicity, and diarrhea87,138. Gastrointestinal mucositis occurs in 20%-50% of patients receiving 5-fluorouracil139, while recurrent diarrhea affects 50%-80% of those undergoing chemotherapy132. Additionally, the incidence of neurotoxicity in early-stage BC treatment ranges from 11% to 80%140. Studies have shown that these AEs are significantly reduced in germ-free or antibiotic-treated mice, highlighting the role of GM in chemotherapy-induced toxicities141. By integrating AI with personalized microbiome analysis, tailored therapeutic strategies can be developed to minimize AEs while optimizing treatment efficacy. For instance, A non-parametric and non-linear gradient-boosted tree-based predictive model estimated the risk of diarrhea in HER2+ metastatic BC patients aged ≥60 receiving neratinib, achieving an AUC of 0.88 and an area under precision-recall curve of 0.95. Using SHAP values, the model identified patients with higher relative abundances of Ruminiclostridium 9 and Bacteroides sp. HPS0048 had a reduced risk of treatment-related diarrhea22. Similarly, in a study on chronic postoperative pain (CPP) in BC patients, incorporating GM data into a binary logistic regression model improved predictive performance, increasing the AUC from 0.709 to 0.748 and sensitivity from 59.1% to 69.7% compared to using clinical indicators alone142. However, these findings remain preliminary and unapplied clinically. Therapeutic strategies targeting GM profiles have already demonstrated clinical potential in other diseases. A representative example is the XGBoost/Monte Carlo-based “Enbiosis” model already used to improve clinical symptoms in irritable bowel syndrome patients143,144. Nonetheless, their findings highlight AI’s growing potential in GM research for predicting chemotherapy responses, managing treatment-related toxicities, and developing personalized interventions for BC patients.

Challenges of AI in GM and BC research

Previous research has revealed the potential of AI in classifying BC patients based on GM and predicting chemotherapy outcomes. However, several challenges still limit their broader application in this field:

Limited and heterogeneous datasets: Current studies rely on relatively small datasets, most based on 16S rRNA sequencing. The significant variability in findings across different cohorts poses a major challenge to the generalizability and reliability of AI-driven predictions. Differences in sequencing techniques, sample processing, and population heterogeneity further contribute to inconsistencies in model performance, making it challenging to establish standardized AI applications.

While AUC provides a useful measure of discrimination, it does not fully capture the model’s robustness, generalizability, or clinical utility. Relying solely on AUC raises concerns about model reliability, emphasizing the need for more comprehensive validation strategies, such as external cohort testing, cross-validation techniques, and real-world clinical validation.

Perspectives and conclusions

The rapid development of GM sequencing in recent decades has revealed that GM plays a crucial role in the development, progression, and treatment of BC. This offers an opportunity to enhance chemotherapy efficacy and improve patient outcomes by targeting the biodiversity, genera/species, and metabolites of the GM. However, the complexity of the GM and the heterogeneity of BC development present significant challenges in understanding the mechanism of interaction between GM and BC chemotherapy. Encouragingly, the application of AI can speed up this process (Fig. 5).

Although the association between GM and BC chemotherapy outcomes has become evident, the underlying causal mechanisms remain unclear. Results vary depending on microbial species diversity, specific taxa, or metabolites, showing heterogeneity across patients with different characteristics and under various chemotherapy drug interventions. Whether this heterogeneity arises from differences in research data quality or insufficient observational dimensions remains to be further explored. Undeniably, most studies have relied on 16S rRNA sequencing, which greatly limits data depth by excluding microbial transcriptional, metagenomic, and species-level abundance information. Additionally, integrating multi-omics data in existing studies is insufficient, often limited to 16S rRNA sequencing alone or combined with metabolomics, with only a few studies incorporating multimodal data (e.g., clinical variables, imaging, and patient lifestyle factors)135,137,142.

AI promotes the mechanism of action of GM in BC chemotherapy research. By employing AI models, such as graph neural networks and causal forests, to process longitudinal and cross-sectional multi-omics data, and by integrating multimodal data (e.g., imaging, electronic medical records, and omics), it becomes possible to extract cross-scale features (e.g., associations between microbial abundance and tumor imaging)145,146. This approach facilitates constructing a dynamic “microbiome-immune-metabolism” network, identifying key drivers such as specific bacterial strains or metabolites. In addition, it will be necessary to leverage AI to integrate metagenomics, metabolomics, transcriptomics, single-cell sequencing, and spatial transcriptomics, constructing AI-based simulation platforms combined with organoids147,148. These platforms can simulate the potential impact of microbial interventions on chemotherapy efficacy and predict the personalized effects of specific probiotics or antibiotics on patients’ drug metabolism, enabling deeper exploration of mechanisms such as the gut-breast axis, gut-immune axis, and metabolite-epigenetic regulation149,150. AI thus promotes the elucidation of the “GM-Chemotherapy-BC” interaction mechanisms.

AI facilitates personalized longitudinal studies of GM in BC chemotherapy. Longitudinal and large-cohort studies are essential for understanding chemotherapy-induced microbial shifts and their impact on treatment outcomes. However, considering the relatively small cohort sizes and high heterogeneity of cohort data in current studies, the findings have limitations. AI-driven research on highly heterogeneous data, such as that of GM and BC, still requires validation through large-scale cohorts. It is necessary to conduct large-scale, multicenter studies using standardized sequencing protocols and diverse populations to improve model reproducibility. A federated learning framework could support collaborative training across multiple centers while protecting privacy, enhancing model generalizability, and addressing biases arising from multicenter studies151. Combining AI algorithms to develop real-time microbiome analysis tools makes it possible to dynamically optimize chemotherapy regimens, probiotic/prebiotic supplementation, and antibiotic use. For example, increasing the abundance of Coprococcus, which is enriched in patients with good responses to NAC, or mitigating resistance mechanisms associated with Bacteroides, could improve treatment outcomes114.

Large language models accelerate the application of AI in studying GM and BC chemotherapy. Recent developments in large language models (LLMs), such as ChatGPT, have demonstrated capabilities in processing and analyzing complex datasets. However, the LLMs may have data bias, lack of professionalism, and illusion in processing data152. While LLMs can interpret and generate human-like text, their accuracy is enhanced when paired with a knowledge graph (KG). KGs integrate knowledge storage, representation, and reasoning, providing a structured framework for identifying entities and relationships within complex datasets153. By combining KGs with LLMs, researchers can better understand the interactions between GM and BC chemotherapy. Additionally, the structured knowledge within KGs can enhance the accuracy of NLP in extracting information from publications. Hence, we propose utilizing KG-derived AI modeling to explore the complex interactions within massive and high-dimensional datasets. Our team is constructing a KG focused on the relationship between GM and BC development. This KG integrates structured knowledge related to BC development and chemotherapy, including gut microbes, metabolomes, guilds, and treatment information. In addition, to address data consistency challenges in constructing the KG, we are developing a Breast Cancer-Microbiome Ontology to promote data standardization and semantic interoperability154. This ontology will establish standardized terminology, clarify key concepts, and facilitate cross-database data integration. We believe the KG-derived modeling will provide robust underlying data support, further promoting GM, BC progression, and chemotherapy research.

We must also recognize the limitations and challenges of applying AI in this field. For instance, potential privacy risks and the lack of AI explainability in analysis warrant attention. AI requires more than usual computing resources and often requires multi-omics data integration, which may increase research costs and patient burden. Missing data and patient dependency in clinical practice are other challenges for AI applications that require comprehensive and multi-dimensional data. Despite these obstacles, the intersection of GM and AI research offers a novel perspective on BC chemotherapy. The future holds promise for achieving seamless coordination between “precise microbiota intervention” and “personalized chemotherapy design”, ultimately enhancing the quality of life and prognosis for BC chemotherapy patients.
