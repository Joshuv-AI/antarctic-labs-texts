Magnetic Nanoparticles as a Theranostic Platform in Brain Tumor Treatment: Surmounting the Bench-to-Bedside Barriers
Authors: Fengyuan Zhang, Yuanyuan Wang, Qingjun Wang, Cuicui Sun, Yu Jing, Shihui Wang, Jing Shan, Hailin Liu, Zhitu Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMalignant brain tumors, particularly glioblastoma, remain one of the greatest challenges in oncology due to their invasive nature, therapeutic resistance, and protection by the blood–brain barrier. Decades of limited therapeutic progress underscore the need for new treatment strategies beyond conventional modalities. Magnetic nanoparticles have emerged as a promising theranostic platform that integrates high-precision imaging, targeted delivery, and synergistic therapy. In this review, we outline a mechanistic framework for magnetic nanoparticle applications, with a focus on the link between ferroptosis and immune activation. We discuss how the intrinsic properties of magnetic nanoparticles can be engineered to induce iron-dependent ferroptotic cell death, which may help overcome apoptosis resistance and also trigger immunogenic cell death. This magnetic nanoparticle-induced immunogenic cell death may shift the immunosuppressive brain tumor microenvironment from a “cold” state toward a more immune-active phenotype, thereby supporting combination immunotherapy. We also examine key translational challenges and potential solutions, including quantitative magnetic particle imaging-guided therapeutic dosimetry, focused ultrasound-mediated delivery strategies, and issues related to Chemistry, Manufacturing, and Controls and regulatory science. By analyzing these translational challenges, this review aims to highlight practical considerations for advancing magnetic nanoparticle-based therapies toward clinical neuro-oncology.Keywords: magnetic nanoparticles, theranostics, glioblastoma, multimodal therapy, blood-brain barrier

Introduction

Malignant brain tumors exhibit the highest mortality of all cancers, imposing a heavy burden on patients, their families, and society. Neuro-oncology continues to struggle with these aggressive diseases, with only marginal improvements in clinical outcomes over recent decades.

Clinical Landscape and Therapeutic Stagnation

Among primary brain tumors in adults, glioblastoma (GBM) is the most common and aggressive type. According to the latest Central Brain Tumor Registry of the United States (CBTRUS) report, analyzing data from 2017–2021, GBM constitutes 13.9% of all primary brain tumors and an alarming 51.5% of all primary malignant brain tumors.1 The current standard of care, established by Stupp et al in 2005, combines maximal safe surgical resection with radiotherapy (RT) alongside concomitant and adjuvant temozolomide (TMZ) chemotherapy. While this regimen was a landmark at the time, extending median overall survival (OS) from 12.1 to 14.6 months,2 nearly twenty years later it has failed to significantly improve outcomes, with five-year survival remaining below 10%.3 These persistent poor outcomes highlight the limitations of current standard-of-care regimens and underscore the need for alternative therapeutic approaches.4

Concurrently, secondary brain tumors (brain metastases) arising from systemic cancers, such as lung cancer, breast cancer, and melanoma, are associated with poor prognosis. These metastatic lesions often severely degrade the patient’s quality of life, creating an additional layer of clinical challenge.5,6

The Anatomical and Biological Stronghold

The limited efficacy of conventional therapies is closely related to the biological features of brain tumors and the anatomical environment in which they develop. Together, these factors create a major therapeutic barrier.

First, GBM infiltrates widely and diffusely. Tumor cells migrate along white matter tracts and perivascular spaces, penetrating deep into surrounding healthy brain tissue. This microscopic invasion makes a complete surgical cure virtually impossible; some malignant cells are inevitably left behind, serving as seeds for recurrence.7 In fact, over 80% of recurrent GBM lesions arise within a 2 cm margin of the original resection cavity, precisely where these infiltrative cells concentrate.8,9 The high-dose radiation needed to target these residual cells invariably inflicts severe, irreversible neurotoxic damage on adjacent normal brain tissue, leading to long-term cognitive decline and a diminished quality of life for survivors.10,11

Second, and perhaps most critically, is the blood–brain barrier (BBB). The BBB is a highly specialized barrier formed by brain microvascular endothelial cells joined by tight junctions, further reinforced by pericytes and astrocytic end-feet. It is also equipped with efficient efflux pumps like P-glycoprotein that actively expel a wide range of molecules from the endothelium. Together, these features create a defense that prevents roughly 98% of small-molecule drugs and essentially 100% of large biologics from entering the brain parenchyma.12

While it is often assumed that the BBB is uniformly leaky in tumors, this is a dangerous oversimplification. In reality, tumor angiogenesis produces a blood–tumor barrier (BTB) whose permeability is highly heterogeneous. In a necrotic tumor core, the BTB may indeed be compromised “leaky”, permitting some passive drug accumulation via the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect. However, at the invasive tumor margin, the region containing the most aggressive, therapy-resistant cells, the BBB frequently remains intact.13,14 This scenario of a “leaky core, intact periphery” creates a sanctuary for the very cells that must be eliminated, and it is a key reason why nanomedicines relying solely on the passive EPR effect have repeatedly failed in clinical translation.15

The Diagnosis–Therapy Disconnect

Beyond these biological and anatomical obstacles, an even deeper systemic flaw exists in the current care model: a complete disconnect between diagnosis and therapy. For example, conventional MRI is excellent for visualizing the bulk tumor, but it provides little to no insight into whether a therapeutic agent has crossed the BBB and reached the infiltrative tumor margins in adequate concentrations. After treatment, imaging alone often cannot distinguish true tumor recurrence from treatment-related changes such as pseudoprogression, a dilemma that confounds clinical decision-making.16 This situation underscores the critical need for an approach that tightly integrates diagnosis with therapy, a concept known as theranostics. A truly theranostic strategy would not only let clinicians “see” the lesion, but also “see” the drug’s behavior in real time and use that information to actively regulate the treatment process.

The Advent of MNP-Based Theranostics

Taken together, these challenges, incomplete surgical resection, radiation-induced toxicity, and the BBB, require strategies that can improve drug delivery, targeting precision, and therapeutic efficacy. Advances in nanotechnology, particularly magnetic nanoparticles (MNPs), provide new opportunities for brain tumor theranostics.17,18 Among MNPs, superparamagnetic iron oxide nanoparticles (SPIONs) are particularly well suited for brain tumor theranostic applications because of their magnetic responsiveness, biocompatibility, and compatibility with magnetic resonance imaging, magnetic particle imaging, magnetic targeting, and magnetic hyperthermia. Their diverse functions are relevant to several major challenges in brain tumor therapy:

• For “seeing” the tumor: The superparamagnetic cores of SPIONs make them useful magnetic resonance imaging contrast agents and form the basis for next-generation quantitative magnetic particle imaging, which provides imaging with zero background signal.19,20

• For “reaching” the tumor: Their nanoscale dimensions and readily functionalizable surfaces allow SPION-based nanocarriers to be decorated with targeting ligands for blood–brain barrier transport via a “Trojan horse” mechanism, or to be ferried across using external energy sources like magnetic fields or focused ultrasound (FUS).21–23

• For “treating” the tumor: They are not merely passive carriers. SPION-based platforms can act as transducers for magnetic hyperthermia and as catalysts for chemical radiosensitization via Fenton-like reactions. They may also function as biological modulators that shift an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment toward a more immune-active state through ferroptosis-driven immunogenic cell death.24–26

By integrating diagnostic and therapeutic functions, MNPs offer a versatile approach to address the complex biological barriers in brain tumor therapy.27 However, despite promising preclinical data, translation into clinical practice remains challenging because of scientific hurdles as well as major gaps in large-scale manufacturing (Chemistry, Manufacturing, and Controls, CMC) and regulatory science. Accordingly, this review aims to provide a comprehensive and clinically oriented overview of magnetic nanoparticles as theranostic platforms for brain tumors. We organize the discussion around the MNP-driven chain of “imaging–delivery–treatment–monitoring,” with particular emphasis on magnetic particle imaging, blood–brain barrier crossing strategies, ferroptosis-mediated immunogenic cell death, and multimodal therapeutic synergy. By critically analyzing current advances, translational barriers, and emerging solutions in manufacturing, predictive preclinical modeling, and regulatory science, this review seeks to clarify how MNP-based strategies may be advanced toward practical application in clinical neuro-oncology.

Fundamental Principles of Magnetic Nanoparticles in Biomedical Applications

The promise of MNPs in neuro-oncology is grounded in their unique physical properties and sophisticated chemical engineering. Understanding these fundamentals is essential to appreciate both their potential and the design challenges associated with their use.

Core Composition and Key Properties

Among the various magnetic materials, iron oxides are by far the most extensively studied for biomedical use due to their excellent biocompatibility and biodegradability.28 Superparamagnetic iron oxide nanoparticles (SPIONs), primarily magnetite (Fe3O4) or maghemite (γ-Fe2O3), are the workhorses of this field. They have low systemic toxicity, and their degradation product (iron) can enter natural metabolic pathways, making these nanoparticles a preferred choice for clinical development.29

Researchers have explored more complex compositions to push performance boundaries. One approach is doping ferrite nanoparticles by substituting some iron ions in the crystal lattice with other divalent metal ions such as zinc (Zn), manganese (Mn), or cobalt (Co). This doping fine-tunes key magnetic parameters like saturation magnetization and magnetocrystalline anisotropy. For example, adding Zn can significantly increase saturation magnetization, thereby enhancing MRI contrast or boosting heating efficiency in magnetic hyperthermia.30 Another approach involves metal alloy nanoparticles (eg, iron–platinum, FePt) that exhibit extremely high magnetocrystalline anisotropy and chemical stability, translating to outstanding magnetic performance. However, these exotic materials often raise concerns about higher biotoxicity compared to simple iron oxides, which has so far largely confined their use to basic research.31

The Crucial Role of Physicochemical Design

Transforming a magnetic nanoparticle from a basic magnetic material into a sophisticated biomedical tool depends on its physicochemical properties, with superparamagnetism being paramount. Superparamagnetism arises when a magnetic particle is reduced below its single-domain critical size, generally below 20 nm for iron oxide nanoparticles, although this threshold varies with material composition, crystal structure, morphology, and surface modification. Without an external field, thermal energy randomizes the magnetic moments of these nanoparticles, yielding no net magnetization (zero coercivity).20 This behavior is crucial in vivo to prevent particles from clumping together magnetically and forming dangerous emboli. But under an applied magnetic field, their moments align rapidly, producing a very strong magnetic response. This switchable “on/off” magnetism underpins key capabilities like magnetic targeting, magnetic hyperthermia, and magnetic particle imaging.

The biological fate and therapeutic performance of MNPs are tightly linked to their size, shape, and surface chemistry:

• Size: A nanoparticle’s hydrodynamic diameter affects its circulation time, biodistribution, recognition and clearance by the mononuclear phagocyte system (MPS/RES), and its ability to be internalized by target cells.31

• Shape: Morphology is not just aesthetic. For instance, cubic nanoparticles often exhibit a higher specific absorption rate (SAR), a measure of heating efficiency, than spherical particles of the same volume, making cubes superior candidates for magnetic hyperthermia.32

• Surface Coating: Bare MNPs are unstable in physiological environments; they tend to aggregate and are rapidly cleared by the RES.33 Thus, a surface coating is essential both for colloidal stability and for providing a platform to attach therapeutic payloads (drugs) or targeting molecules (antibodies, peptides). Common coatings include polymers like polyethylene glycol (PEG), which forms a hydrophilic stealth layer to reduce protein adsorption and extend circulation time;34,35 natural polysaccharides like dextran, which has been used in early dextran-coated SPIO formulations such as ferumoxides (Feridex), have good biocompatibility;36 and inorganic shells like silica, which is chemically inert and easily functionalized to construct complex multifunctional theranostic platforms.37

Particle Synthesis, Biocompatibility, and Neurotoxicity

The method used to synthesize MNP cores directly determines their quality and performance. Common approaches include:

• Co-precipitation: Chemically precipitating ferrous (Fe2+) and ferric (Fe3+) salts in alkaline solution. This method is simple, inexpensive, and easily scalable, but typically produces particles with broad size distributions and lower crystallinity.38

• Thermal Decomposition: Decomposing organometallic precursors (eg, iron acetylacetonate) in high-boiling organic solvents. This technique offers exquisite control over particle size, shape, and crystallinity, yielding highly uniform, monodisperse MNPs ideal for high-performance uses like MHT and MPI.39

• Microemulsion: Using tiny water droplets in a water-in-oil microemulsion as nanoreactors to confine particle growth, resulting in MNPs of very uniform size.40

Following core synthesis, surface functionalization can create advanced multifunctional nanocarriers. For example, Angiopep-2-functionalized, temozolomide-loaded lipid magnetic nanovectors have been developed to enhance glioma targeting and tumor retention, while enabling alternating magnetic field-triggered drug release in preclinical glioblastoma models.24

Although iron oxide nanoparticles (IONPs) are generally considered biocompatible, using them in the brain demands careful safety evaluation, as potential neurotoxicity is a major translational concern.41 The primary mechanism of toxicity is IONP degradation and the release of free iron ions. Excess iron can participate in Fenton/Haber-Weiss chemistry, converting endogenous hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) into highly reactive hydroxyl radicals (•OH).42,43 This oxidative burst can trigger cascading cellular damage: mitochondrial dysfunction, lipid peroxidation, protein aggregation, DNA damage, ultimately killing neurons through apoptosis or necrosis.44

Once in the brain parenchyma, IONPs are mainly cleared by microglia (resident macrophages) and astrocytes. Chronic activation of these glial cells can itself promote persistent neuroinflammation and secondary neuronal injury. The long-term fate of IONPs in the brain remains incompletely understood. It is thought that particles are gradually broken down in lysosomes and the iron incorporated into normal metabolic pools.20 However, the rate and efficiency of this process, and whether any non-degradable remnants persist, are unresolved questions that impact long-term safety.33

In summary, designing an MNP for neuro-oncology is a complex multi-objective optimization, constantly balancing functionality against safety. For example, ultrasmall particles with core sizes below 5 nm can improve T1 MRI contrast and allow renal clearance, but this may come at the cost of poor heating efficiency for MHT and limited drug-loading capacity. Conversely, larger particles above 50 nm may respond more strongly to magnetic fields but are more readily cleared by the RES and may have difficulty penetrating dense tumor tissue. Similarly, doping with other metals can boost magnetic performance but may introduce new toxicities, and using a cationic surface coating can enhance BBB transport yet increase cytotoxicity.44 Navigating these trade-offs demands a careful, holistic design strategy at each step. SPION-Driven Imaging: Visualizing the Battlefield and Directing the Attack

Accurate imaging is the bedrock of modern neuro-oncology, essential for initial diagnosis, surgical planning, treatment response assessment, and recurrence monitoring. With their unique magnetic properties, MNPs may improve brain tumor imaging by enhancing conventional MRI and serving as tracers for magnetic particle imaging.

SPIONs as T1/T2 Magnetic Resonance Imaging Contrast Agents

MRI is the gold standard for brain tumor diagnosis, offering unparalleled soft tissue contrast and high spatial resolution.49 SPION-based contrast agents work by perturbing the local magnetic field experienced by nearby water protons, thereby shortening their T1 (longitudinal) and T2 (transverse) relaxation times. Most conventional SPION formulations with relatively larger magnetic cores or clustered magnetic domains act as strong T2/T2* contrast agents. They cause rapid dephasing of proton spins in T2-weighted images, appearing as dark (negative) contrast.46 The effectiveness of a T2 agent is quantified by its transverse relaxivity (r2); an ideal T2 agent has a high r2 value and a high r2/r1 ratio.46,50 Clinically used SPION agents, including ferumoxides and ferucarbotran, are classic examples of T2 contrast agents.36 By attaching tumor-targeting ligands to their surface, such SPION-based agents can accumulate in tumor tissue, effectively “painting” the tumor black on MRI.

From a diagnostic standpoint, however, bright (positive) contrast agents are often preferred, since dark signals on MRI can be confused with hemorrhage or calcification. This has motivated the development of MNPs that function as T1 agents. By shrinking the iron oxide core to ultrasmall dimensions below 5 nm, one can produce ultrasmall SPIONs (USPIONs) with dramatically reduced r2/r1 ratios, making T1 shortening dominate their effect.46,51 These USPIONs have major advantages over traditional gadolinium-based T1 agents: they have longer blood circulation half-lives and a superior safety profile, avoiding the risk of gadolinium deposition in the brain and other tissues (an increasing clinical concern). Their extended circulation enables dynamic imaging of tumor vasculature over a longer window and can help differentiate true tumor progression from treatment-induced inflammation. Recent preclinical studies even showed that PEG-coated SPIONs can visualize the vasculature in invasive GBM models with an intact BBB, something not possible with conventional gadolinium agents.52 This capability may help address the “leaky core, intact periphery” problem that allows therapy-resistant cells to persist, extending the role of MNPs beyond contrast enhancement toward in vivo biological characterization. It provides a non-invasive surrogate marker for angiogenesis that may improve surgical planning and the evaluation of anti-angiogenic therapies.

Magnetic Particle Imaging

Magnetic Particle Imaging (MPI) is an emerging molecular imaging modality that differs fundamentally from MRI and PET. Instead of imaging anatomical structures or water proton signals, MPI directly and quantitatively maps the distribution of SPION tracers themselves.53 It exploits the non-linear magnetization response of SPIONs. A strong static gradient field (the “selection field”) is applied, creating a single point or line in space with zero magnetic field (the field-free region, FFR). A second oscillating magnetic field (the “drive field”) is then applied over the region. Only SPIONs located in the FFR (where they are unsaturated) can respond to the drive field by flipping their magnetic moments. This rapid flipping generates a rich spectrum of harmonics that is picked up by receiver coils. By scanning the FFR throughout the region of interest (either by moving it mechanically or shifting it electronically), a 3D map of SPION concentration is reconstructed.54

MPI offers several distinct advantages for neuroimaging. First, MPI has high but tracer- and scanner-dependent sensitivity rather than a fixed universal concentration threshold. For example, in a human-sized MPI brain scanner using Perimag, a commercially available SPION tracer with signal performance similar to ferucarbotran, Graeser et al reported a detection limit of 14.7 ng Fe mL−1, corresponding to 263 pmol Fe mL−1 and 2 μg Fe total. This system used a field-free-point encoding scheme, a 25.699 kHz drive field with a 6 mT amplitude, a reduced selection-field gradient of 0.2 T m−1 μ0−1 in the main encoding direction, and a 100×140 mm2 field of view.55 More recently, Mattingly et al developed a human brain-scale mechanically rotated field-free-line MPI system using Synomag-D SPIONs. This system used a 1.1 T m−1 permanent-magnet field-free line, a water-cooled 26 kHz drive coil producing up to 7 mT peak field, and achieved approximately 1 μg Fe sensitivity over a 181-mm-diameter two-dimensional field of view in a 5-second image, with a spatial resolution of approximately 5–7 mm.56 These examples indicate that MPI sensitivity is determined jointly by the SPION formulation, magnetic core architecture, drive/selection-field configuration, acquisition time, reconstruction algorithm, and receiver noise, rather than by a single universal pM/fM cutoff.57 Second, it boasts extremely high temporal resolution (on the order of milliseconds), enabling real-time tracking of dynamic processes like cerebral blood flow. Third, the MPI signal is perfectly linear with SPION concentration, providing true quantitative imaging. Since the human body has no background MPI signal, images have essentially zero background, yielding an exceptionally high signal-to-noise ratio.58 Finally, MPI uses no ionizing radiation and its magnetic fields penetrate tissue without attenuation, making it safe for repeated, long-term use without depth limitations.54,59

Although still in early stages of clinical translation, MPI has shown immense potential in preclinical studies. It has been used to detect ischemic stroke with high sensitivity,60 measure cerebral blood volume with precision, and track the real-time migration of SPION-labeled therapeutic cells (eg, stem cells or CAR-T cells) to brain tumors.61 MPI’s ability to provide immediate quantitative feedback makes it ideal for monitoring BBB opening procedures. For example, during FUS-mediated BBB disruption, MPI can dynamically visualize and quantify SPION extravasation from the bloodstream into brain tissue, providing direct, real-time proof of drug delivery.62

Scaling MPI from small-animal setups to human-scale scanners presents significant engineering challenges.55 These include generating and sustaining strong magnetic field gradients across a large bore, managing the immense power requirements, and ensuring patient safety by avoiding peripheral nerve stimulation (PNS) from the rapidly switching drive fields.63 PNS currently limits the maximum drive field strength, which in turn caps MPI’s ultimate sensitivity and resolution. Nonetheless, progress is underway: recent prototype MPI scanners for human brain imaging have demonstrated feasibility. These systems, employing novel designs such as mechanically rotated field-free lines, have achieved a spatial resolution of approximately 5–7 mm and a temporal resolution of 5 seconds over a field-of-view (181 mm diameter) sufficient to encompass the human brain. While ongoing challenges in power consumption and noise reduction remain, these achievements mark a critical step toward clinical application.56 A parallel effort is developing clinical-grade SPION tracers optimized for MPI to maximize signal strength.57

Importantly, MRI and MPI should be viewed as complementary rather than competing technologies. The future of theranostics will likely merge them into a hybrid MRI–MPI approach. MRI provides high-resolution anatomical maps of the brain (crucial for surgical planning and structural assessment), while MPI provides real-time, quantitative feedback on the distribution of SPION-based therapeutics. This combination of anatomical information and quantitative feedback may support more precise treatment planning and monitoring.

For therapies that depend on achieving a critical nanoparticle dose (such as magnetic hyperthermia), this combined MRI/MPI approach enables what might be termed therapeutic dosimetry. The greatest challenge in nanomedicine is not designing a potent agent, but ensuring it reaches all parts of a tumor in sufficient concentration, the distribution is always heterogeneous. Without a way to measure that distribution, treatment is essentially blind. MPI may provide this capability by noninvasively mapping the quantitative distribution of SPION-based therapeutics within the tumor. This map is effectively a nanoparticle dose map, a critical piece of information previously unattainable in vivo. The quantitative distribution data can then feed into biophysical models to predict the thermal dose (eg, CEM43, a measure of thermal cell killing64,65) that would result from a given magnetic hyperthermia treatment.

This capability may support the development of a closed-loop feedback system. MPI would image the SPION distribution, that data would be used to calculate the expected thermal dose, and the hyperthermia device parameters (field strength, duration, etc.) could be adjusted in real time to optimize treatment. A final MPI scan would confirm the outcome. Such a closed-loop adaptive strategy may help advance the practical application of theranostics in individualized treatment.

Breaching the Fortress: Delivering SPION-Based Nanocarriers Across the Blood–Brain Barrier

Effective delivery of therapeutic agents to brain tumors is an absolute prerequisite for any successful treatment. The BBB stands as the primary gatekeeper, a sophisticated biological barrier that protects the central nervous system but, in doing so, blocks the vast majority of potential anticancer drugs. Developing safe and efficient ways to ferry SPION-based nanocarriers across this barrier is therefore a central focus in neuro-oncology research.

BBB and BTB

A nuanced understanding of the barrier itself is critical. The BBB’s physical foundation is a continuous layer of brain microvascular endothelial cells sealed together by complex tight junction proteins, which severely restrict the passage of substances between cells (paracellular pathway).66 This physical barrier is reinforced by a functional one: the endothelial cells express a high density of active efflux transporters (eg, P-glycoprotein) that act as molecular pumps, actively ejecting a broad array of drug molecules that manage to diffuse into the cells.67 This entire structure is supported and regulated by surrounding pericytes and the end-feet of astrocytes, which together form the “neurovascular unit.”68

Within a brain tumor, this barrier is altered, forming the so-called blood–tumor barrier (BTB). While the BTB is often described as “leaky” due to disorganized tumor angiogenesis, its permeability is heterogeneous. In the tumor’s core, vessels may be poorly formed and permeable, allowing for some passive extravasation of nanoparticles via the EPR effect. However, at the tumor’s periphery, in the invasive front where individual cancer cells are migrating into healthy brain tissue, the BBB often remains fully intact. This spatial heterogeneity is a critical point of failure for many nanomedicines, as they cannot reach the very cells responsible for tumor recurrence. Therefore, a successful delivery strategy cannot rely on passive, inconsistent BTB “leaks”; it must be capable of actively overcoming an intact BBB.

“Trojan Horse” Strategy: Exploiting Endogenous Transport Pathways

These non-disruptive strategies aim to “trick” the BBB into actively transporting the MNP cargo across. The most prominent is receptor-mediated transcytosis (RMT).69 This elegant approach involves decorating a SPION-based nanocarrier with a ligand that specifically binds to a receptor highly expressed on the BBB endothelial cells. Upon binding, the receptor–nanoparticle complex is internalized via endocytosis, shuttled across the cell in a vesicle, and released into the brain parenchyma on the far side. Several receptors have been targeted for this purpose:

• Transferrin Receptor (TfR): Normally transports iron into the brain, making it a natural target. Conjugating transferrin protein or anti-TfR antibodies to nanoparticles is a classic RMT strategy.70

• Low-Density Lipoprotein Receptor-Related Protein 1 (LRP1): This receptor shuttles multiple ligands, including the small peptide Angiopep-2, which has shown highly efficient transport of nanocarriers into gliomas. Angiopep-2-functionalized nanocarriers, including Ang-TMZ-LMNVs, illustrate the use of LRP1-related glioma targeting and tumor retention in preclinical GBM models, although standardized systemic BBB-crossing efficiency was not reported.24

• Other targets: Receptors such as the insulin receptor,69 lactoferrin receptor, and more recently the insulin-like growth factor 1 receptor (IGF-1R)71 and glucose transporters (GLUT)72 are also being explored for nanoparticle delivery.

A less specific but still effective approach is adsorptive-mediated transcytosis (AMT). This method relies on electrostatic interactions. By giving a SPION-based nanocarrier a highly positive surface charge (for example, coating it with a cationic polymer like polyethyleneimine, PEI, or a cationic peptide), it becomes attracted to the negatively charged endothelial surface (glycocalyx). This adsorption can then induce a non-specific endocytic process, carrying the nanoparticle across.69,70 The main drawback of AMT is that cationic surfaces are often associated with higher cellular toxicity, necessitating a careful balance between delivery efficiency and safety.

“Battering Ram” Strategy: Physical Guidance and Forced Entry

In contrast to the subtle “Trojan horse,” these strategies use external physical force to temporarily breach the BBB or directly pull MNPs across.

FUS with Microbubbles

This is a highly promising physical method. Low-intensity FUS is targeted to a specific brain region from outside the skull while gas-filled microbubbles are injected intravenously.23 As microbubbles pass through the targeted area, the ultrasound causes them to oscillate, mechanically pulling open tight junctions in the endothelium. This creates a transient, reversible opening (lasting a few hours) during which circulating MNPs can extravasate into the brain. Key advantages of FUS are its high spatial precision and noninvasiveness, combined with its reversibility.73 More than 30 clinical trials are currently exploring FUS for brain tumor therapy, with most focused on establishing safety and feasibility in GBM patients.74,75

The potential of this technique has been quantified in recent studies. For instance, research published in mid-2025 established optimized parameters for low-frequency FUS (850 kHz frequency at 125 kPa pressure) to enable the safe and effective delivery of large therapeutic payloads. Utilizing this protocol, investigators achieved a 6.7-fold increase in the delivery of siRNA-loaded lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) directly to glioblastoma tumors in murine models, and a 12-fold enhancement in mRNA-LNP delivery to healthy brain tissue.76 This provides concrete, quantitative evidence of FUS’s capacity to ferry not just small molecules but also complex genetic therapies across the BBB.

Magnetic Guidance

This strategy leverages the intrinsic magnetism of MNPs. For tumors near the brain surface, a powerful external magnet (permanent or electromagnet) is placed on the scalp over the tumor site. As intravenously injected MNPs flow through nearby blood vessels, the magnetic field gradient exerts a force on them, pulling them toward the vessel wall and increasing their local concentration (and thus the probability of crossing into the tumor).77 The major limitation is that magnetic field strength decays very rapidly with distance, making this approach ineffective for deep-seated tumors.

A further advance in FUS-mediated delivery was reported in June 2025 with the development of a new immunotherapeutic agent, the “Fusion Superkine” (FSK). This large, dual-acting protein (IL-24S/IL-15) was engineered into a type 5 adenovirus (Ad.5) vector for gene therapy. This entire viral vector was delivered systemically and targeted to the brain using an innovative, non-invasive “Focused Ultrasound Double Microbubble” (FUS-DMB) technique.78 The successful delivery of a large viral gene therapy vector, a payload far more complex than a nanoparticle, represents an important advance for the field.

Additional Innovative Delivery Pathways

Beyond these mainstream approaches, researchers are exploring other creative routes into the brain:

• Cell-mediated delivery: This uses the body’s own cells as “Trojan horses.” Certain immune cells, monocytes, macrophages, neutrophils, can naturally cross the BBB and home in on sites of inflammation such as tumors. By pre-loading these cells with MNPs, they become living vehicles carrying the therapeutic payload into the brain.79 Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs), which also exhibit tumor-homing ability, are another carrier under investigation. For example, the HEFDS-NK system engineers natural killer cells to carry cationic MNPs, combining the NK cells’ innate tumor-seeking ability with magnetic guidance to enhance targeting of GBM.48

• Intranasal delivery: This route exploits the direct anatomical connection between the nasal cavity and the brain via the olfactory and trigeminal nerves, offering a way to bypass the BBB entirely. Certain drugs or nanoparticle formulations administered intranasally can reach the brain along these neural pathways.80 However, it remains uncertain how efficient and homogeneous this delivery is, and the achievable dose is limited.

In summary, strategies to overcome the BBB fall into two broad classes: elegant and minimally disruptive (“Trojan horse”) or powerful and forceful (“battering ram”). The former leverages physiological pathways but can be limited by factors like receptor saturation or payload size, while the latter offers high efficiency but carries a risk of collateral damage. The optimal strategy depends on the therapeutic goal. To eliminate diffuse, single infiltrating cells at the tumor margin, a method that penetrates an intact BBB (like RMT) is essential. For debulking a large, solid tumor mass, the regional opening provided by FUS may be more efficient. A particularly compelling future approach might involve combining minimally disruptive barrier priming with highly specific receptor-mediated targeting: for instance, using a gentle FUS priming of the barrier, followed by highly specific RMT-targeted MNP delivery to achieve maximal penetration with minimal disruption. MNP-Mediated Synergistic Therapies

No single treatment modality can cure brain tumors, driving the field toward multimodal combination therapies. MNPs can play a central role in such strategies, acting not only as delivery vehicles but also as active agents that contribute to therapeutic synergy. By exploiting their unique physical and chemical properties, MNPs can be used to sensitize tumors to conventional treatments like radiotherapy, immunotherapy, and chemotherapy, creating combined effects far greater than the sum of the parts. These synergies are often interconnected, creating a cascading therapeutic sequence where one treatment modality amplifies the next.

MNPs and Radiosensitization

Radiotherapy (RT) is a mainstay of GBM treatment, but its efficacy is constrained by intrinsic tumor radioresistance and the dose-limiting toxicity to surrounding healthy brain tissue. MNP-based radiosensitization aims to tip this balance, enhancing tumor cell kill without increasing the radiation dose to normal tissue, thereby improving the therapeutic ratio and preserving neurological function. MNPs can sensitize tumors to radiation through multiple mechanisms operating in parallel:

• Physical dose enhancement: Even though iron’s atomic number (Z = 26) is modest compared to high-Z elements like gold, accumulating IONPs in a tumor can locally increase the absorption of X-rays or gamma rays. The interaction generates a shower of secondary electrons (eg, Auger electrons) via the photoelectric and Compton effects. These electrons travel only nanometers to micrometers, depositing their energy near the nanoparticles, especially into the DNA of nearby tumor cell nuclei, thereby boosting the microscopic radiation dose.81

• Chemical sensitization: IONPs possess an enzyme-mimicking activity (“nanozyme” activity) similar to peroxidases. They can catalyze the breakdown of hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) (often elevated in the tumor microenvironment, and also produced by radiation) into highly cytotoxic hydroxyl radicals (•OH) via a Fenton-like reaction.82 Since these reactive oxygen species are the primary mediators of radiation’s indirect cell-killing effects, this catalytic amplification dramatically increases the chemical damage inflicted by a given radiation dose.83,84

• Biological sensitization: Perhaps the most powerful mechanism involves using magnetic hyperthermia (MHT) to disable tumor DNA repair. When exposed to an alternating magnetic field, MNPs generate mild localized heat (~41–43°C). Heat stress has been shown to degrade key DNA repair proteins (eg, BRCA2, essential for homologous recombination repair of double-strand breaks, the most lethal form of radiation damage).85,86 By crippling the tumor cell’s ability to repair DNA damage, MHT makes cells exquisitely sensitive to RT, resulting in catastrophic cell death. This approach is especially effective against otherwise resistant cell populations, such as those in hypoxic tumor regions.

MNPs and Immunosensitization

GBM is notorious for its immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME), rendering it a “cold” tumor that evades immune attack by the body.87 This is a major reason why immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), which have revolutionized the treatment of many other cancers, have shown minimal efficacy in GBM. MNP-based therapies, particularly MHT, offer a powerful strategy to convert these cold tumors into “hot,” immunologically active ones, thereby sensitizing them to immunotherapy. The key mechanism for this transformation is the induction of immunogenic cell death (ICD).

When tumor cells are killed by the thermal stress of MHT, they can die in a manner that actively alerts the immune system. This process involves the release of a suite of “danger signals” known as damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs).88 Key DAMP signals include: exposure of calreticulin on the cell surface (an “eat me” signal), release of ATP into the extracellular space (a “find me” signal), and secretion of the HMGB1 protein. These signals act as an in situ vaccine, recruiting and activating antigen-presenting cells (APCs) such as dendritic cells at the tumor site. The APCs then engulf the dying tumor cells, process their tumor-associated antigens, and present them to T-cells, initiating a robust and durable adaptive immune response specifically against the tumor.89

Beyond inducing ICD, MHT and the MNPs themselves can directly remodel the TME. Mild hyperthermia can increase the expression of stress ligands (like NKG2D ligands) on tumor cells, making them more susceptible to killing by natural killer (NK) cells.90,91 Certain MNP formulations, such as those with a cationic surface, have even been shown to directly activate NK cells.48 Furthermore, compelling studies have demonstrated that IONPs can repolarize the abundant, immunosuppressive tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) from an M2 (“protumor”) phenotype to an M1 (pro-inflammatory, anti-tumor) phenotype,92,93 effectively turning them from “enemies” into “allies” in the fight. By inducing ICD and promoting chemokine release, MHT also facilitates the infiltration of cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs) into the tumor, a critical prerequisite for effective immunotherapy.94 Finally, MNPs can serve as delivery vehicles to carry large-molecule ICIs (eg, anti-PD-L1 antibodies), which cannot cross the BBB on their own, directly into the tumor site to release the “brakes” on T-cell activity.95

MNPs and Chemosensitization

The synergy between MNPs and chemotherapy is twofold, addressing both drug delivery and drug sensitivity. As discussed previously, MNPs are excellent carriers for chemotherapy drugs like TMZ, protecting them from premature degradation and ferrying them across the BBB to dramatically increase their concentration at the tumor site. Moreover, advanced “smart” nanoplatforms can be designed where the drug is encased in a thermosensitive coating on the MNP; upon reaching the tumor, an applied AMF induces the MNP to heat up, triggering a phase transition in the coating and an on-demand burst release of the drug exactly where and when it’s needed, maximizing local efficacy while minimizing systemic toxicity.94,96

MHT itself is also a potent chemosensitizer. The localized heating increases blood flow and vascular permeability within the tumor, which enhances delivery of both the MNPs and co-administered chemotherapy drugs. The heat also increases the fluidity of cancer cell membranes, facilitating the uptake of drugs into the cells. Critically, hyperthermia can interfere with mechanisms of chemoresistance, for example, by inhibiting the function of drug efflux pumps that normally expel chemotherapy agents, thus re-sensitizing tumors that were previously resistant to treatment.97

These synergistic strategies are deeply interconnected, forming a therapeutic cascade orchestrated by the MNP. For instance, the cell death caused by the combination of MHT and RT (via heat-induced DNA repair inhibition) is immunogenic (ICD), which directly primes the tumor for a more effective response to subsequent immunotherapy. The increased vascular permeability created by MHT not only enhances chemotherapy delivery but also opens pathways for newly activated immune cells to infiltrate the tumor. In this versatile network, the MNP functions as the central, driving node, simultaneously a physical energy transducer, a chemical catalyst, and a targeted delivery vehicle, that integrates disparate therapies into a single, cohesive, and powerful treatment regimen.

MNP-Mediated Ferroptosis: The Mechanistic Hub of Synergistic Therapy

While the synergies above are powerful, a deeper mechanistic understanding reveals how they are interconnected through a specific form of regulated cell death. The process of iron-catalyzed generation of cytotoxic radicals via Fenton-like chemistry is now recognized as a potent method for inducing ferroptosis, a non-apoptotic, iron-dependent cell death pathway driven by catastrophic lipid peroxidation.98 This is profoundly significant because many cancer cells, including glioblastoma, exhibit altered iron metabolism and a heightened sensitivity to oxidative stress, making them uniquely vulnerable to this pathway. The ability of MNPs to act as active inducers of ferroptosis, therefore, reframes their role from that of a simple chemical catalyst to a sophisticated biological modulator. Rather than acting solely as sensitizers, MNPs actively contribute to tumor cell death and modulate the local immune microenvironment, which may help limit tumor recurrence. Systemic MNP-Based Therapies for Brain Metastases: Targeting Mechanism Over Location

Brain metastases present a distinct and even more complex challenge than primary brain tumors. They are not a localized disease of the brain but rather an intracranial manifestation of a systemic malignancy. A patient’s survival is dictated not only by control of the brain lesions but also by the status of the primary tumor and other metastatic sites throughout the body. The most common primary cancers that spread to the brain: lung cancer, breast cancer, and melanoma, are now often treated with highly effective targeted therapies and immunotherapies. However, the BBB prevents these life-extending systemic drugs from reaching brain metastases, creating a scenario where the body’s disease is controlled but the brain’s is not.

MNP-based platforms may help address this challenge. While many strategies developed for GBM, such as using RMT or FUS to cross the BBB for localized MHT or radiosensitization, are also applicable to brain metastases, increasing attention is being paid to approaches that target the biological mechanisms of metastasis itself, thereby treating the disease systemically, including its manifestations in the brain. This reflects a shift from a location-based to a mechanism-based therapeutic strategy.

An example of this approach is a therapy targeting microRNA-10b (miR-10b). MiR-10b has been identified as a “metastamiR”, a master regulator that drives invasion, survival, and metastatic potential in numerous cancer types, including breast cancer and even GBM.106 Based on this discovery, a nanotherapeutic was developed consisting of an antisense oligonucleotide designed to inhibit miR-10b (an antagomir) covalently attached to a dextran-coated iron oxide nanoparticle.107,108 This agent, now in clinical development as TTX-MC138,109 uses the MNP core in a dual role: it is both the delivery vehicle for the genetic drug and an MRI contrast agent, allowing for noninvasive, whole-body imaging to track its delivery and accumulation in metastatic lesions.110

After intravenous injection, TTX-MC138 circulates systemically and is taken up by metastatic cancer cells that overexpress miR-10b. Inside the cell, the antagomir binds to and inactivates miR-10b. This inhibition lifts the suppression on downstream target genes (such as the tumor suppressor HOXD10), which in turn causes the cancer cell to lose its stem-like properties, undergo apoptosis, and halt growth. In preclinical models of breast cancer brain metastasis, this nanodrug effectively crossed the BBB, accumulated in brain lesions, and significantly inhibited their growth.111 Even more impressively, when combined with low-dose chemotherapy, systemic administration of TTX-MC138 led to durable, complete regression of established metastases in both the lymph nodes and the lungs.

This case study highlights the potential of MNP platforms to move beyond site-directed delivery for brain metastases. Rather than acting only as carriers that transport payloads to intracranial lesions, such platforms may also be designed to systemically disrupt metastatic mechanisms in cancer cells across multiple anatomical sites. This shift from treating a location to targeting a mechanism may represent an important direction for metastatic cancer treatment, and MNP-based theranostic platforms may provide a useful framework for this approach.

Clinical Translation: Bridging the “Valley of Death”

Despite the compelling preclinical evidence and clear scientific rationale for their use, the journey of MNPs from laboratory success to routine clinical application has been exceptionally slow and arduous.

Bridging this “valley of death” requires overcoming not only scientific hurdles but also, and perhaps more importantly, immense challenges in industrial manufacturing and regulatory approval.

Current Clinical Landscape

To date, the only clinically approved magnetic nanoparticle-based hyperthermia approach for brain tumors involves direct, stereotactically guided implantation of aminosilane-coated magnetite nanoparticles (NanoTherm) into recurrent GBM or the resection cavity.112 The patient is then treated using a high-frequency alternating magnetic field applicator, which induces localized magnetic hyperthermia. A European Phase II clinical trial demonstrated that this therapy, when combined with conventional radiotherapy, extended the median overall survival for patients with recurrent GBM to 13.4 months, a significant improvement over the ~6–8 months expected with standard care.113 A follow-up trial (ANCHIALE, NCT06271421) is currently recruiting patients in Poland to further evaluate this approach.114

Beyond this magnetic hyperthermia platform, the clinical pipeline is sparse, a fact that stands in stark contrast to the thousands of published preclinical studies. The systemic, metastasis-targeting agent TTX-MC138 is now being evaluated in a Phase I/II trial for patients with advanced solid tumors, including those with stable or asymptomatic brain metastases or primary central nervous system tumors. As of May 2026, the trial is listed as closed to accrual, and peer-reviewed efficacy data remain unavailable.115 Other trials are exploring different nanoparticle approaches, such as the gadolinium-based nanoparticle AGuIX for radiosensitization in newly diagnosed GBM (NCT04881032) and the gold-based nanoconjugate NU-0129 carrying nucleic acid payloads in recurrent GBM (NCT03020017). However, overall the field is still struggling to make the leap from bench to bedside. Key Barriers to Translation

The gap between preclinical promise and clinical reality does not primarily reflect a failure of basic science, but rather challenges in engineering, manufacturing, and regulation.

The single greatest bottleneck is Chemistry, Manufacturing, and Controls (CMC).116 The process of scaling up a nanoparticle synthesis from milligram/gram quantities in a research lab to the kilogram scale required for clinical trials and commercialization, while adhering to stringent Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP) standards, is extraordinarily difficult.117,118 Key CMC challenges include:

• Reproducibility: Ensuring that every batch of MNPs produced has identical and consistent physicochemical properties (size, shape, crystal structure, magnetic response, surface chemistry). Minor batch-to-batch variations can dramatically alter a nanoparticle’s in vivo behavior, efficacy, and toxicity.

• Purity and quality control: Developing robust methods to eliminate all process-related impurities (eg, residual solvents or unreacted precursors) and establishing a comprehensive panel of analytical tests to reliably characterize the final product.

• Scalability and cost: Designing manufacturing processes that are not only reproducible but also economically viable and scalable. For example, while thermal decomposition produces high-quality MNPs in the lab, its use of expensive precursors and organic solvents makes it difficult to scale cost-effectively. Continuous-flow microreactor methods are being explored as a more GMP-compliant, scalable path forward.118

Beyond manufacturing, significant biological and toxicological questions remain. As discussed, the long-term safety, clearance pathways, and potential for chronic neurotoxicity of MNPs in the brain require much deeper investigation. Furthermore, nanoparticles can trigger immune reactions, such as complement activation or the generation of anti-PEG antibodies upon repeated administration, which could compromise both safety and efficacy. These issues also need to be considered in the broader context of nano–bio interactions. After entering biological systems, MNPs are rapidly modified by their local microenvironment, including serum protein adsorption, changes in surface identity, redox reactions, and immune-cell recognition. These processes may alter biodistribution, cellular uptake, clearance, and toxicity. For brain-directed applications, translational evaluation should therefore include not only standard physicochemical characterization but also biologically relevant tests, such as protein corona formation, microglial or macrophage uptake, oxidative stress, and repeated-exposure responses.119

Finally, regulatory science presents a major hurdle. Nanomedicines are classified as Non-Biological Complex Drugs (NBCDs), a category for which regulatory approval pathways at agencies like the FDA and EMA are not yet fully established. Evaluation is often conducted on a case-by-case basis, creating significant uncertainty, risk, and cost for developers. This complexity also extends to generics: it is nearly impossible to prove that a “generic” nanoparticle is truly identical to the original product based on standard pharmacokinetic studies alone, which stifles competition and keeps costs high.

Compounding all these issues is the preclinical model gap. The vast majority of exciting preclinical results are generated in animal models that poorly reflect human disease. Subcutaneous tumor models completely ignore the BBB. Orthotopic brain tumor models often use immunodeficient mice, making it impossible to study the crucial interactions between an MNP-based therapy and the host immune system, particularly for immunotherapies. Moreover, these tumors are typically grown from a single, homogeneous cancer cell line, failing to capture the profound genetic and microenvironmental heterogeneity that drives treatment resistance in human GBM. This disconnect between the simplified models used in research and the complexity of the clinical reality is a primary driver of the high failure rate of nanomedicines in clinical trials. Future work must prioritize validation in more sophisticated and predictive models, such as patient-derived xenografts (PDX), genetically engineered mouse models (GEMMs), and advanced in vitro systems like “tumor-on-a-chip” organoids that incorporate a functional BBB and immune components.

Future Perspectives and the Role of AI-Assisted Nanomedicine

Future work on magnetic nanoparticle-based brain tumor theranostics should focus less on adding isolated functions and more on building systems that can coordinate delivery, treatment, and monitoring in a predictable manner. One possible direction is the development of sequentially responsive magnetic nanoparticle platforms. Such platforms may combine blood–brain barrier crossing, tumor microenvironment priming, chemo-thermal or radio-thermal synergy, immune activation, and imaging feedback within the same therapeutic framework. For example, focused ultrasound-assisted delivery and ligand-mediated targeting could improve nanoparticle entry into infiltrative tumor regions, whereas stimuli-responsive drug release and alternating magnetic field-triggered hyperthermia may enhance chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and ferroptosis-mediated immunogenic cell death. If these steps can be coordinated effectively, they may improve therapeutic efficacy while reducing unnecessary toxicity and treatment resistance.

Future MNP-based strategies should therefore move beyond single-function formulations and toward integrated theranostic platforms. By adjusting particle composition, size, morphology, and surface functionalization, MNPs can be designed to support BBB-directed delivery, magnetic hyperthermia, radiotherapy-related sensitization, gene or biotherapeutic delivery, and diagnostic imaging within a coordinated treatment framework.120 This is particularly important for brain tumors, where delivery failure, therapeutic resistance, and limited real-time monitoring are closely linked rather than independent problems.

At the same time, the broader field of GBM nanomedicine is moving from passive tumor accumulation toward mechanism-guided intervention. Nanocarriers are increasingly being used to improve BBB transport, prolong intratumoral drug exposure, and interfere with GBM-associated signaling pathways. However, formulation instability, toxicity, limited efficacy, and poor clinical predictability remain major barriers, indicating that future MNP platforms should combine mechanistic targeting with stronger translational validation.121

For magnetically actuated systems, safety assessment should also consider the biological effects of the magnetic field itself. Magnetic-field parameters may influence nervous tissue through cellular signaling, network activity, and longer-term plasticity responses. Therefore, studies using alternating magnetic fields, magnetic targeting, or related magnetic interventions should clearly report field strength, frequency, pulse pattern, exposure duration, thermal dose, and potential off-target neuromodulatory effects.122

Artificial intelligence (AI) may help support this development, particularly in formulation design and preclinical optimization. Recent studies suggest that AI-driven models can assist in predicting drug encapsulation, release kinetics, surface interactions, colloidal stability, targeted delivery, and the ability of nanocarriers to cross physiological barriers.123 AI-assisted pharmacokinetic and biodistribution modeling may also improve predictions of nanoparticle circulation, tumor accumulation, release behavior, toxicity, and therapeutic exposure.124 More broadly, AI and machine learning approaches in cancer nanomedicine and precision oncology may help integrate nanoparticle physicochemical features, imaging findings, tumor biological characteristics, and treatment-response data to guide more individualized treatment planning.125 In the context of brain tumors, these tools may be useful for optimizing blood–brain barrier transport, magnetic particle imaging performance, magnetic hyperthermia efficiency, individualized therapeutic dosing, and potentially the remote regulation of engineered therapeutic cells such as CAR-T cells.

For clinical translation, these concepts will need to be matched by advances in manufacturing, quality control, regulatory evaluation, and long-term safety assessment. Scalable Good Manufacturing Practice-compliant production, reproducible physicochemical characterization, predictive preclinical models, and clearer regulatory standards for nanomedicines remain essential. In parallel, closed-loop theranostic systems that combine magnetic resonance imaging with magnetic particle imaging may allow quantitative monitoring of nanoparticle distribution and treatment dose. This direction is also consistent with the broader development of smart quantitative molecular imaging nanoprobes, which are shifting cancer imaging from simply “seeing” lesions toward quantitatively “measuring” tumor-associated pathological biomarkers and tumour microenvironmental heterogeneity in vivo.126 Further studies are also needed to clarify how magnetic nanoparticles interact with the brain over time, including their degradation, clearance, and potential neuro-immunotoxicity.

Conclusions

Magnetic nanoparticles offer a flexible theranostic platform for brain tumor management by combining imaging, blood–brain barrier delivery, magnetic hyperthermia, radiosensitization, chemotherapy delivery, and immune modulation. Their ability to induce ferroptosis and immunogenic cell death may help address several persistent limitations of current treatment, including poor drug delivery, therapeutic resistance, and recurrence. However, clinical translation remains constrained by unresolved issues in safety evaluation, scalable manufacturing, quality control, regulatory standardization, and predictive preclinical validation. Future progress will depend on developing magnetic nanoparticle systems that are not only multifunctional, but also reproducible, quantitatively monitorable, and biologically safe.
