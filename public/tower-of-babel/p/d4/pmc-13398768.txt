Impact of system updates for sensor‐augmented pump therapy on glycemic management in Japanese individuals with type 1 diabetes (SAP‐J T1D study)
Authors: Shuichiro Saito, Akane Yamamoto, Yuya Miyazaki, Motoko Kobayashi, Mei Nakatsuji, Seiji Nishikage, Kai Yoshimura, Tomofumi Takayoshi, Wataru Ogawa, Yushi Hirota
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAims/IntroductionSensor‐augmented pump (SAP) therapy has evolved sequentially, but long‐term clinical evidence supporting these advances has remained limited. We here evaluated the impact of updates to SAP therapy on glycemic outcomes in individuals with type 1 diabetes.MethodsThis retrospective observational study (SAP‐J T1D) included Japanese individuals with type 1 diabetes receiving SAP therapy. Treatment efficacy of the MiniMed 620G, 640G, and 770G systems (Medtronic) was evaluated at three predefined time points: baseline (before the switch from 620G to 640G), before the switch from 640G to 770G, and 1 year after the switch to 770G. The primary outcome was the change in time in range (TIR) for continuous glucose monitoring from baseline to 1 year after the transition to the MiniMed 770G system.ResultsA total of 47 participants were included in the analysis. The median age was 45 years (interquartile range, 33–56 years), and the median body mass index was 22.3 kg/m2 (20.4–23.7 kg/m2). TIR increased progressively with each system update, from 69.0% (61.5–75.5%) with MiniMed 620G, to 72.0% (63.5–79.0%) with MiniMed 640G, and to 77.0% (73.0–80.5%) with MiniMed 770G. Time below range decreased after the switch to MiniMed 640G, whereas time above range decreased after that to MiniMed 770G. Time in tight range increased after the transition to the MiniMed 770G system.ConclusionsSequential updates in SAP therapy were associated with stepwise improvements in glycemic management, reflecting the distinct functional characteristics of each system.Keywords: Japanese, sensor‐augmented pump therapy, type 1 diabetes

INTRODUCTION

Type 1 diabetes is characterized by the loss of endogenous insulin secretion as a result of autoimmune destruction of pancreatic β‐cells. Recent studies have shown that the introduction of advanced diabetes technologies can achieve improved glycemic management 1 . One such technology, sensor‐augmented pump (SAP) therapy, has undergone substantial advancement. In recent years, automated insulin delivery (AID) systems that automatically adjust insulin delivery based on sensor glucose (SG) values have become available in Japan. American Diabetes Association guidelines state that AID systems are the preferred insulin delivery method for individuals with type 1 diabetes 2 . In Japan, the MiniMed 620G system (Medtronic, Northridge, CA, USA) was launched for SAP therapy in 2015 and was followed by the MiniMed 640G system (Medtronic) featuring predictive low glucose management (PLGM) in 2018. The latter system suspends insulin delivery when SG values reach the preset low‐glucose limit or are predicted to fall to 20 mg/dL above the limit within 30 min. Furthermore, MiniMed 770G (Medtronic) featuring the hybrid closed‐loop (HCL) system—also referred to as the AID system—that automatically adjusts basal insulin delivery on the basis of current SG values and insulin delivery history was approved in Japan in 2022. However, it is important that the effects of the rapid evolution of SAP therapy and its transition to AID on glucose management be evaluated.

Against this background, two studies conducted in Japan 3 , 4 have examined the transition from the MiniMed 620G system to the 640G system, whereas four studies 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 have examined the transition from the 640G system to the 770G system. Although the two studies on the switch from the MiniMed 620G system to the 640G system both reported a reduction in the duration of hypoglycemia and no additional findings were presented. Among the four studies evaluating the switch from the MiniMed 640G system to the 770G system, there was agreement that time in range (TIR) increased and time below range (TBR) remained unchanged. However, the findings were inconsistent for other outcomes, in particular for the range of hyperglycemia. Moreover, in recent years, time in tight range (TITR), which evaluates glycemic control within a narrower target range than TIR, has been increasingly reported as a useful metric for prediction of clinical outcomes and diabetes‐related complications in individuals with diabetes 9 , 10 , 11 . However, changes in TITR across different SAP therapy systems have not yet been reported for Japanese populations. Previous studies have therefore not fully elucidated the detailed effects of advances in SAP therapy on glycemic management in Japanese individuals with type 1 diabetes, and all available evidence has been limited to short‐term observations.

We conducted the present study to clarify the long‐term impact of sequential SAP system transitions on glycemic management, by evaluating continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) metrics—including TIR—in a single cohort of individuals who transitioned from the MiniMed 620G system to the PLGM‐equipped MiniMed 640G system and subsequently to the HCL/AID MiniMed 770G system.

METHODS

Participants

This study (SAP‐J T1D study) included individuals with type 1 diabetes who transitioned from the MiniMed 620G system to the 640G system and subsequently to the 770G system at Kobe University Hospital after January 1, 2014. Individuals who were aged <20 years, pregnant, undergoing dialysis, or receiving steroid therapy were excluded.

Study design

This retrospective, single‐center observational study was designed to evaluate therapeutic outcomes up to 1 year after switching to the MiniMed 770G system, with baseline defined as the time immediately before switching from the MiniMed 620G system to the MiniMed 640G system. CGM metrics, insulin dosage, as well as hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) and glycated albumin levels were assessed at three time points: before switching to the MiniMed 640G system, before switching to the MiniMed 770G system, and 1 year after switching to the 770G system. CGM metrics were analyzed with the use of CareLink system software (Medtronic) for data collected over the 28 days preceding each evaluation point. The metrics included TIR (70–180 mg/dL), time above range (TAR) (level 1, 181–250 mg/dL; level 2, >250 mg/dL), TBR (level 1, 54–69 mg/dL; level 2, <54 mg/dL), TITR (70–140 mg/dL), glucose management indicator (GMI), coefficient of variation (CV), and glycemia risk index (GRI). GRI 12 was calculated as: (3.0 × hypoglycemia component) + (1.6 × hyperglycemia component), with the hypoglycemia component being defined as TBR level 2 + (0.8 × TBR level 1), and the hyperglycemia component as TAR level 2 + (0.5 × TAR level 1). The proportions of participants who achieved the TIR, TBR, and TAR targets as well as a composite glycemic target comprising TIR, TBR (<70 mg/dL), and TAR (>180 mg/dL) were calculated. Based on the international consensus on CGM targets for type 1 diabetes, CGM targets are as follows: <4% for TBR <70 mg/dL, <25% for TAR >180 mg/dL, and >70% for TIR 13 . This retrospective observational study was conducted in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines.

Outcomes

The primary endpoint of the study was the change in TIR between the pre–MiniMed 640G system period and 1 year after the transition to the MiniMed 770G system. Secondary endpoints included changes in CGM metrics, GRI, insulin dosage, and HbA1c levels between evaluation points.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as the median and interquartile range (IQR). Analysis was performed for three time points: before switching to the MiniMed 640G system, before switching to the MiniMed 770G system, and 1 year after switching to the 770G system. The paired t test was applied for variables with a normal distribution, and the Wilcoxon signed‐rank test for other variables.

Comparisons across the three time points were performed with repeated‐measures analysis of variance (anova) for normally distributed data, and with the Friedman test for other data; if a significant difference was detected, post hoc analysis was performed with the paired t test for normally distributed data and the Wilcoxon signed‐rank test for other data. A P value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analysis was conducted with EZR version 2.80 software 14 .

RESULTS

Baseline characteristics

During the study period, 62 individuals switched sequentially from the MiniMed 620G system to the 640G system and subsequently to the 770G system. Fifteen of these individuals met the exclusion criteria, and 47 were therefore recruited to the study (Figure 1). Baseline characteristics of the study participants are presented in Table 1. The median age was 45.0 years (IQR, 33.0–56.0 years), and 10 participants were male. The median duration of diabetes was 13.0 years (IQR, 7.0–17.0 years), and the median duration of SAP therapy was 3.0 years (IQR, 1.0–3.0 years). The median body mass index was 22.3 kg/m2 (IQR, 20.4–23.7 kg/m2), and the median HbA1c level was 7.1% (IQR, 6.5–7.6%).

Changes in glycemic management

Changes in glycemic management are presented in Table 2. At baseline, TIR was 69.0% (61.5–75.5%), TAR level 1 was 19.0% (14.0–25.5%), TAR level 2 was 4.0% (2.0–10.5%), TBR level 1 was 3.0% (1.5–5.5%), TBR level 2 was 1.0% (0–2.0%), and TITR was 47.0% (36.5–54.0%). TIR increased significantly to 77.0% (73.0–80.5%) at the final evaluation point, 1 year after switching to the MiniMed 770G system (P < 0.01) (Figures 2 and 3). After switching to the MiniMed 640G system, TIR improved to 72.0% (63.5–79.0%), TBR level 1 decreased to 2.0% (1.0–4.0%), and TBR level 2 decreased to 0% (0–1.0%), whereas TAR levels 1 and 2 as well as TITR showed no significant changes. Following the transition to the MiniMed 770G system, TIR increased further to 77.0% (73.0–80.5%) and TITR increased to 53.0% (49.0–59.0%). TAR level 1 decreased to 16.0% (13.0–20.0%), whereas TAR level 2 decreased to 2.0% (2.0–6.0%). TBR levels 1 and 2 remained unchanged. HbA1c levels and GMI did not show significant changes across the study period. The CV decreased significantly after switching to the MiniMed 640G system and remained stable thereafter. The proportion of participants achieving the TIR target varied across system generations, being 44.7% with the MiniMed 620G system, 57.4% with the MiniMed 640G system, and 80.9% with the MiniMed 770G system. The proportion of participants meeting all targets was 12.8% with the 620G system, 23.4% with the 640G system, and 38.3% with the 770G system (Figure 4). Total daily insulin dosage remained unchanged, although basal insulin dosage increased after transitioning to the MiniMed 770G system. Conversely, additional bolus insulin requirements decreased following the switch to the MiniMed 770G system. GRI decreased significantly after switching from the MiniMed 620G system to the 640G system and decreased further after transitioning to the 770G system.

DISCUSSION

We here show that upgrading SAP systems led to a reduction in both TBR and TAR as well as an increase in TIR in Japanese individuals with type 1 diabetes. Internationally accepted CGM targets for type 1 diabetes are as follows: <4% for TBR of <70 mg/dL, <1% for TBR of <54 mg/dL, <25% for TAR of >180 mg/dL, <5% for TAR of >250 mg/dL, and > 70% for TIR 13 . In the present study, the cohort as a whole achieved these recommended targets, and a high proportion of individuals met each of the thresholds, particularly with the HCL system, a form of AID system. Furthermore, a European real‐world study reported values of 72.2% for TIR, 2.3% for TBR of <70 mg/dL, and 25.5% for TAR of >180 mg/dL at 1 year after initiation of HCL therapy 15 . These findings indicate that the effectiveness of the HCL system in Japanese individuals with type 1 diabetes is similar to that observed in European individuals with this condition.

Several studies have examined transitions between SAP systems in Japanese individuals with type 1 diabetes. As far as we are aware, our study is the first in Japan to evaluate within the same cohort sequential switching from the MiniMed 620G system to the 640G system and subsequently to the 770G system, the latter being the first AID system introduced in Japan. We also found that switching from the MiniMed 620G system to the 640G system was associated with a reduction in TBR levels 1 and 2, and that the transition from the 640G system to the 770G system was accompanied by a reduction in TAR levels 1 and 2. These findings are consistent with the distinct characteristics and algorithmic advancements of each system, which supports their validity. The relatively large sample size of our study compared with previous studies likely contributed to the robustness of these findings. In addition, the present study is the first in Japan to describe longitudinal changes in TITR after system upgrades. Of note, the AID system achieved a TITR of >50%, a threshold recommended based on real‐world evidence 16 . We found that TIR increased after the transition from the MiniMed 620G system to the 640G system. In contrast, previous studies in Western countries 17 , 18 have consistently reported reductions in the duration of hypoglycemia after switching from the MiniMed 620G system to the 640G system, but have not shown a corresponding improvement in TIR. Although the reasons for this discrepancy remain unclear, one potential explanation is that, as an example, one of the previous studies reported a relatively low baseline of 1.1% (0.4–2.7%) for TBR of <70 mg/dL, which was lower than that observed in our cohort. It is therefore plausible that the increase in TIR in our study was influenced by an improvement in TBR.

Our findings may represent previously unreported evidence highlighting the importance of hypoglycemia amelioration for optimization of glycemic control with SAP therapy among Japanese individuals with type 1 diabetes. Although improvements in both TIR and TITR were observed in the present study, neither HbA1c levels nor GMI showed significant changes. Whereas a reduction in TAR was achieved, the relatively high TBR at baseline, as mentioned above and which also improved, may have contributed to the absence of changes in HbA1c levels and GMI. GRI has recently been proposed as a comprehensive indicator of glycemic quality, providing a more integrated assessment than reliance on a single metric such as TIR 12 . In the present study, GRI improved with each system transition. The reduction in the duration of hypoglycemia following the switch to the MiniMed 640G system and that in the duration of hyperglycemia after the transition to the 770G system were both reflected as favorable changes in GRI. These findings suggest that GRI may be a useful indicator for evaluation of the overall quality of glycemic management in individuals undergoing SAP therapy.

Our study has several limitations. First, as a retrospective study, it is susceptible to potential data‐dredging bias. Second, because it was conducted at a single center, the generalizability of the findings may be limited. Third, no adjustment for multiple comparisons was performed for the secondary endpoints and so these findings should be interpreted as exploratory. Despite these limitations, our findings—such as the reduction in the duration of hypoglycemia with the MiniMed 640G system and that in the duration of hyperglycemia with the MiniMed 770G system—appear to reflect the characteristic effects of each system and should be considered valid. Moreover, the novel observation that TIR improved after the transition to the MiniMed 640G system highlights the importance of hypoglycemia in glycemic management for individuals with type 1 diabetes in Japan. Fourth, although the MiniMed 780G is the currently used AID system, it was not evaluated in the present study; therefore, the findings should be interpreted as reflecting the stepwise transition from the MiniMed 620G as an SAP system to the MiniMed 640G equipped with PLGM and then to the MiniMed 770G as an AID system.

In conclusion, we have here characterized the impact of transitioning from the MiniMed 620G system to the 640G system and subsequently to the 770G system on glycemic management in Japanese individuals with type 1 diabetes. Our study is the first to characterize the glycemic effects associated with each system in a Japanese population with type 1 diabetes.
