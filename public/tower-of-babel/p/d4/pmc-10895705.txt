Weight-independent effects of Roux-en-Y gastric bypass surgery on remission of nonalcoholic fatty liver disease in mice
Authors: Christopher L. Axelrod, Ingeborg Langohr, Wagner S. Dantas, Elizabeth C. Heintz, Bolormaa Vandanmagsar, Shengping Yang, Elizabeth R. M. Zunica, R. Leigh Townsend, Vance L. Albaugh, Hans-Rudolf Berthoud, John P. Kirwan
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Objective:Obesity is a driver of non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), and interventions that decrease body weight, such as bariatric surgery and/or calorie restriction (CR), may serve as effective therapies. This study compared the effects of Roux-en-Y gastric bypass surgery (RYGB) and CR on hepatic function in mice with obesity and NAFLD.Methods:C57BL/6J mice were fed a high-fat diet to promote obesity. At 16 weeks of age, mice were randomized to sham surgery (sham), RYGB, or CR weight matched to RYGB (WM). Body weight/composition, food intake, and energy expenditure (EE) were measured throughout treatment. Liver histopathology was evaluated from H&E-stained sections. Hepatic enzymes and glycogen content were determined by ELISA. Transcriptional signatures were revealed via RNA sequencing.Results:RYGB reduced hepatic lipid content and adiposity while increasing EE and lean body mass relative to WM. Hepatic glycogen and bile acid content were increased after RYGB relative to sham and WM. RYGB activated enterohepatic signaling and genes regulating hepatic lipid homeostasis.Conclusions:RYGB improved whole-body composition and hepatic lipid homeostasis to a greater extent than CR in mice. RYGB was associated with discrete remodeling of the hepatic transcriptome, suggesting that surgery may be mechanistically additive to CR.

INTRODUCTION

Nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) is the most prevalent hepatic disease and is observed in ~30% of adults globally [1]. Simple steatosis of the liver represents the initial and reversible stage of the disease spectrum and directly contributes to the onset of non-alcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH) and end-stage liver diseases such as cirrhosis and/or hepatocellular carcinoma [2]. Presently, there are no Food and Drug Administration-approved treatments for NAFLD, and therapeutic options are limited to lifestyle and/or behavioral modifications. As such, there is a critical need to identify durable and effective therapies for NAFLD. Obesity is the primary risk factor for NAFLD and is present in >80% of patients at the time of diagnosis [3]. Weight loss-targeted therapies significantly reduce NAFLD severity and are employed as first-line treatments in clinical practice. Progressive weight loss can reverse steatosis, resolve steatohepatitis, and decrease and/or prevent the onset of fibrosis [4]. For example, continuous and/or intermittent calorie restriction (CR) reduces steatosis and progression of NAFLD to NASH in patients with obesity [5]. Similarly, metabolic surgery (MBS) procedures, such as sleeve gastrectomy (SG) and Roux-en-Y gastric bypass (RYGB), decrease adverse liver outcomes by ~80% and are associated with NAFLD remission in patients with obesity [6]. There is broad consensus that CR and MBS are effective means to improve hepatic function in patients with obesity. However, there is immense dispute as to whether MBS improves NAFLD strictly as a function of weight loss. Herein, we performed a head-to-head comparison of RYGB against CR alone for remission of NAFLD in mice with obesity. We hypothesized that RYGB would lower hepatic lipid content to a greater extent than CR. We secondarily hypothesized that RYGB would discretely alter the hepatic transcriptome relative to CR.

METHODS

Animal husbandry

16-week-old male C57BL/6J mice with diet-induced obesity (D12492, Research Diets, New Brunswick, New Jersey) were purchased from Jackson Laboratories (Cat# 380050). At 17 weeks of age, mice were placed on a two-choice diet consisting of high-fat (kilocalorie percentage: carbohydrate, 20; fat, 60; protein, 20, Diet D12492, Research Diets) and low-fat regular laboratory chow (kilocalorie percentage: carbohydrate, 58; fat, 13.5; protein, 28.5, Diet 5001, Purina LabDiet, Richmond, Indiana) for the remainder of study to exacerbate diet-induced obesity as described previously [7]. All mice were single-housed and maintained in a conventional animal facility at 23 to 24°C at a relative humidity of 50% ± 10% on a 12-h light: dark cycle from time of weaning. During the metabolic chamber studies, mice were exclusively provided high-fat diet. Mice were given ad libitum access to food (maintaining ~50 g of food in the hopper) and water unless indicated. Mice were kept individually on corncob bedding except for the periods of food intake measurements, when they were on grid floors. Immediately following the study period, animals were fasted for 4 hours and euthanized to recover organs and collect blood by cervical decapitation. Animal care and experimentation was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee and strictly followed rules and guidelines provided by the American Physiological Society and NIH.

Randomization

Thirty animals were randomized at 20 weeks of age to sham surgery (n = 11), weight-matched (WM) CR (n = 8), or RYGB (n = 11) to ensure equivalence in heterogeneity of body weight as described previously [7]. Animals that died postoperatively were excluded from subsequent analysis. Investigators were blinded to study outcomes prior to analysis. Sample size was determined based upon prior studies examining the effects of RYGB compared with WM CR [7].

RYGB

RYGB was carried out according to a protocol described previously [8]. Briefly, in a jejuno-gastric anastomosis, the mid jejunum was connected with a very small gastric pouch and the other end of the cut jejunum was anastomosed to the lower jejunum, resulting in a 5 to 6 cm long Roux limb, a 9 to 11 cm long biliopancreatic limb, and a 20 to 25 cm long common limb. Animals returned to ad libitum feeding postoperatively.

Sham surgery

Sham surgery consisted of a simple laparotomy without transection of jejunum and stomach. Pre- and postoperative conditions were identical to RYGB animals.

WM CR

To experimentally dissociate reductions in body weight from RYGB-specific effects, a parallel group of mice were calorie restricted to achieve equivalent weight trajectories. Mice weight-matched to the RYGB group were restricted to ~50% to 70% of the caloric intake of the RYGB group. Preweighed amounts of food (kilocalories: 93% high-fat, 7% chow) were given once per day during the light period.

Body composition

Body composition was determined prior to necropsy via nuclear magnetic resonance using an LF110 BCA Analyzer (Bruker Corporation, Billerica, Massachusetts) as described previously [9].

Metabolic chamber study

Whole-body energy expenditure, oxygen consumption, carbon dioxide production, body weight, energy expenditure, and physical activity were monitored at murine thermoneutrality in a metabolic chamber (Phenomaster/Labmaster, TSE Systems, Germany) during the weight stable phase ~15 weeks after treatment as described previously [10].

Intraperitoneal glucose tolerance test

Glycemic control was evaluated 4 weeks after treatment using a 2 h intraperitoneal glucose tolerance test as described previously [7]. Briefly, following a 4 h fast, D-glucose (1.5 mg/kg in saline) was injected into the intraperitoneal cavity. Glucose was measured on a glucometer (Onetouch Ultra) immediately prior to and 15, 30, 60, and 120 min after injection. The incremental area under the curve was determined using the trapezoidal rule.

Liver enzymes

Trunk blood was collected at time of necropsy into ethylenediamine-tetraacetic acid-treated tubes and centrifuged at 4°C and 3000 RPM for 10 min to isolate plasma. Plasma samples were stored at −80°C until the time of the assay. Plasma concentrations of aspartate aminotransferase and alanine aminotransferase were determined using an automated diagnostic platform.

Liver triglycerides

~50 mg of frozen liver tissue was digested in ethanolic potassium hydroxide (1:1 ethanol; 30% KOH) at 55°C overnight. The resultant sample was vortexed and then centrifuged at 21,000 × g for 5 min and the supernatant was collected and treated with 1 M magnesium chloride. The resulting supernatant was incubated on ice for 10 min and centrifuged at 21,000 × g for 5 min, and the resulting supernatant was collected and diluted 25× for subsequent assay. Triglyceride content was determined by commercially available enzyme assay according to manufacturer instructions (Sigma-Aldrich; Cat #MAK266).

Liver glycogen

Liver glycogen content was determined by commercially available colorimetric assay. Briefly, ~10 mg liver tissue was dissolved in ice-cold ddH2O, boiled for 5 min, centrifuged for 5 min at 13,000 × g, and resuspended in hydrolysis buffer. Glycogen content was determined against a 6-point standard curve and reported as the net glycogen concentration in μg/μL.

Liver bile acids

Frozen liver tissue was homogenized, and bile acids were extracted by incubation in 75% ethanol for 3 h at 50°C. The resulting extract was centrifuged to remove debris, and the supernatant was diluted 1:2 in phosphate-buffered saline for assay. Total bile acid concentrations were then determined by commercially available enzyme activity assay (Crystal Chem; Cat #80471).

Histopathology

Histopathological evaluation was employed to evaluate treatment effects on hepatic lipid content, NAFLD severity, and liver architecture. Histopathology was assessed in formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded liver specimens sectioned at 5 μm and stained with hematoxylin and eosin. Sections were scored according to the World Health Organization and Edmonson and Steinder grading classifications [11] by a blinded, board-certified veterinary pathologist. Hepatocyte size and lipid content (steatosis area) were quantified using threshold-based segmentation analysis in ImageJ 4 software as described previously [12, 13]. Briefly, liver sections were captured at 20× magnification using NDP.view 2 software (Hamamatsu Corporation, Japan). Steatosis was quantified as the area of lipid vacuoles relative to the area of the liver section corrected for the presence of empty vesicles via size exclusion and expressed as a percentage. Hepatocyte size was calculated as the average cellular volume containing discrete nuclei [14]. The diagnostic threshold for NAFLD was set as intrahepatic lipid content ≥5.0% as described previously [15], which closely approximates the human criterion [16].

RNA sequencing

An untargeted transcriptome-wide screen of liver tissue was conducted to identify potential mechanisms associated with reductions in hepatic lipid content. ~25 mg of liver tissue was immediately placed into an RNA stabilization solution (RNAlater; ThermoFisher Scientific) at necropsy and stored at −80°C until time of assay. RNA was solubilized and extracted using Trizol. RNA was quantified using a NanoDrop and normalized to 200 ng/μL in nuclease-free water. RNA integrity was assessed using an Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100. Libraries were constructed and sequenced using Lexogen Quant-Seq. Briefly, library generation was performed using an oligodT primer, and double-stranded cDNA was purified with magnetic beads. Libraries were amplified using polymerase chain reaction (PCR), and transcripts were forward sequenced at 75 bp using NextSeq 500 (Illumina). BlueBee software was used to analyze alignment, and DESeq2 was used for differential expression analysis. Differentially regulated messenger RNA transcripts were filtered based on the following criteria: q < 0.1 and a base mean >50 counts. Canonical pathway enrichment was determined using Ingenuity Pathway Analysis. Gene ontology enrichment analysis was determined with ShinyGO using the kyoto encyclopedia of genes and genomes orthology database with a false discovery rate cutoff of ≤0.05. Given the potential for multicollinearity between WM and RYGB animals, we conducted a regularized elastic net regression to further refine genes with predictive potential to explain differences in hepatic outcomes. Multicollinearity was addressed by employing elastic net regression as an approach to regularization as well as by performing model selection and avoiding model under- or over-fitting using the elasticnet in R.

Targeted gene expression

Quantitative PCR was carried out with Power SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Bio-Rad, Hercules, California) using a QuantStudio 5 real-time PCR system (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, California) as described previously [17]. Briefly, intestinal tissue was homogenized in TRIzol, and total RNA was isolated according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Expression was normalized to cychlophilin B (Ppib). Primer sequences were as follows: nuclear receptor subfamily 1 group H member 4 (Nr1h4), forward: TACCACTACAACGCGCTCAC, reverse: GGCGTTCTTGGTAATGCTTC; Ppib, forward: TCCATCGTGTCATCAAGGACTT, reverse: CTCATCTGGGAAGCGCTCA.

Statistical analysis

All data are expressed as means ± SEM unless otherwise stated. Prism 9 software was used for testing statistical significance. The total number of discrete data points are provided in each panel unless otherwise noted. Normality of the data was assessed by Kolmogorov–Smirnov. One-way ANOVA followed by post hoc tests and unpaired Student’s t tests were used to assess between-group differences in quantitative variables where appropriate. Between-group differences in energy expenditure were determined using a generalized linear model with lean body mass and total body mass as covariates using SPSS as described previously [18]. Significance was set a priori at p ≤ 0.05.

RESULTS

RYGB lowers fat mass and preserves energy expenditure independent of food intake

As a function of sustained high-fat diet, body weight gradually increased in sham relative to both RYGB and WM over the course of study (Figure 1A). RYGB and WM similarly reduced body weight relative to both sham and baseline (RYGB: 13.5% vs. WM: 12.5%; Figure 1B). Sham increased both fat and lean mass relative to RYGB and WM (Figure 1C,D). RYGB decreased fat mass relative to both sham and WM CR and preserved lean mass to a greater extent than WM. WM achieved a lower body weight by ~30% reduction in food intake relative to both sham and RYGB, which did not differ from one another (Figure 1E). Similarly, WM decreased energy expenditure by ~17% relative to both sham and RYGB, which again, did not differ from one another (Figure 1F). Thus, energy expenditure was elevated in RYGB- relative to WM-treated animals. The difference between RYGB and WM held true when applying total body or lean mass as a covariate, indicating that the retention of lean mass after RYGB did not solely explain sustained energy expenditure. We additionally compared changes in glucose tolerance, which similarly improved in RYGB and WM animals (Figure 1G). Taken together, RYGB improves body composition and metabolic rate but not glucose tolerance to a greater extent than WM.

RYGB reduces hepatic lipid burden and increases glycogen content to a greater extent than CR alone

Sham animals exhibited diffuse macro- and microvesicular steatosis largely devoid of hepatocyte ballooning and inflammation, consistent with advancing NAFLD (Figure 2A). In contrast, RYGB and, to a lesser extent, WM ameliorated both macro- and microsteatosis (Figure 2B). RYGB and WM had equivalently reduced hepatocyte size, indicative of reduced hypertrophy (Figure 2C). ~90% of RYGB animals were in remission of NAFLD, whereas both sham and WM were 100% positive (Figure 2D). Consistent with histological findings, RYGB and, to a lesser extent, WM, reduced hepatic triglyceride content relative to sham (Figure 2E). NAFLD/NASH severity scores were dramatically reduced in RYGB and WM alike (Figure 2F). Hepatocytes from RYGB-treated animals appeared to be enriched with glycogen. To confirm this, we measured hepatic glycogen content, which was elevated in RYGB animals relative to both sham and WM (Figure 2G). RYGB and WM similarly improved plasma aspartate aminotransferase and alanine transaminase activities relative to sham, indicative of progressively reversed hepatocyte damage (Figure 2H,I). Taken together, RYGB lowers hepatic lipid burden and enhances glycogen content beyond what is achieved by weight loss alone.

RYGB remodels the hepatic transcriptional landscape in favor of lipid and bile acid metabolism relative to WM CR

Dimensional reduction via principal component analysis revealed that both RYGB and WM profoundly altered the transcriptional landscape relative to sham (Figure 3A). Furthermore, RYGB resulted in a transcriptional signature distinct from WM. Compared with sham, WM and RYGB similarly altered the expression of 2863 transcripts (Figure 3B). WM resulted in 632 uniquely upregulated and 553 downregulated transcripts, whereas RYGB resulted in 418 uniquely upregulated and 417 downregulated transcripts. Of the top 25 differentially expressed genes relative to sham, ~20% were common to WM and RYGB, almost all of which have established hepatoregulatory functions impacting lipid metabolism and NAFLD pathophysiology (Adh4, Apoa4, Ces3b, Clec2h, Col1a1, Lfi27l2b, Ly6d, Osbpl3, Sprr1a); Figure 3C). To further dissociate changes between WM and RYGB, we then conducted canonical pathway enrichment and gene ontology analysis (Figure 3D,E). Compared with WM, RYGB significantly enhanced pathways associated with cholesterol biosynthesis, farnesoid X receptor (FXR)/retinoid X receptor activation, fatty acid metabolism, and bile acid secretion. Consistent with these findings, the top differentially expressed transcript between WM and RYGB was solute carrier family 51 subunit beta (Slc51b), a hepatic bile acid transporter. Taken together, these data indicate that WM and RYGB improve hepatic function through shared and discrete mechanisms of action. For RYGB, transcriptional sequencing identified FXR-mediated bile acid transport as a putative regulator of hepatic lipid metabolism.

RYGB-mediated activation of enterohepatic bile acid signaling is associated with enhanced transcriptional regulation of lipid clearance

Given the potential for multicollinearity between WM and RYGB animals, we conducted a regularized elastic net regression to further refine genes with predictive potential to explain differences in hepatic outcomes. Congruent with the differential expression analysis, the bile acid transporter Slc51b was a significant predictive gene that displayed selective enrichment in RYGB animals (Figure 4A). We then measured the hepatic bile acid pool, which was elevated after WM and to a greater extent, RYGB, compared with sham (Figure 4B). Based upon the observations from transcriptomic sequencing and bioinformatic analysis, we sought to validate expression of key enterohepatic signaling nodes. Because surgery modifies the small intestine, we first confirmed activation of the FXR (Nr1h4), which governs bile acid synthesis and transfer. We observed that Nr1h4 expression from the distal small intestine was selectively upregulated by RYGB (Figure 4C). Intestinal Nr1h4 regulates uptake and conjugation of hepatic bile acids via the Ost-α/Ost-β complex (OST-α/OST-β; Slc51b) and small heterodimer partner (SHP; Nr0b2). Consistently, we observed that hepatic Slc51b and Nr0b2 expression were selectively increased by RYGB relative to both sham and WM (Figure 4D,E). Hepatic lipid homeostasis is governed by the rate of de novo synthesis, oxidation/utilization, and the transfer/uptake of lipids to and from the circulatory system [19]. Given this, we explored gene targets that may couple changes in hepatic bile acid regulation with enhanced suppression of steatosis observed with RYGB. We observed that sterol regulatory element-binding transcription factor 1 (Srebf1), the insulin-responsive gene that controls hepatic de novo lipogenesis, was decreased after RYGB relative to sham, which remained unchanged in WM (Figure 4F). ELOVL Fatty Acid Elongase 3 (Elovl3), an Sreb-responsive gene that controls fatty acid elongation, was increased by RYGB relative to sham and WM, which remained unchanged (Figure 4G). Fatty acid synthase (Fasn), which governs fatty acid de novo synthesis, decreased to a similar extent after RYGB and WM (Figure 4H). Nuclear receptor subfamily 1 group H member 3 (Nr1h3), which controls de novo synthesis via the liver X receptor, was increased after RYGB and WM relative to sham (Figure 4I). The peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor α (Ppara), a nutrient sensor that dictates the rate of hepatic fatty acid oxidation, was decreased after RYGB and WM (Figure 4J). In contrast, carnitine palmitoyltransferase II (Cpt2), a rate-limiting enzyme for fatty acid oxidation in the mitochondria, was increased after both RYGB and WM (Figure 4K). Peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor γ coactivator 1α (Ppargc1a), a master regulator of energy metabolism and long-chain fatty acid oxidation, decreased after RYGB and to a greater extent after WM compared with sham (Figure 4L). Cytochrome P450 family 27 subfamily A member 1 gene (Cyp27a1), which is responsible for hepatic cholesterol clearance via bile acid-mediated catabolism, was increased after RYGB relative to both sham and WM (Figure 4M). Fatty acid binding protein 1 (Fabp1), a liver-localized transporter of long-chain fatty acids, was increased after both RYGB and WM (Figure 4N). Solute carrier family 27 member 5 (Slc27a5), which serves dual roles in hepatic fatty acid uptake and bile acid reconjugation, was increased after RYGB relative to sham and remained unaltered with WM (Figure 4O). The albumin (Alb) gene, which transcribes the carrier protein responsible for fatty acid binding, increased after RYGB relative to both sham and WM (Figure 4P). Last, CD36 molecule (CD36), which is critically important for fatty acid transport into hepatocytes, decreased after RYGB and WM (Figure 4Q). Taken together, RYGB increased the hepatic bile acid pool and associated transcriptional factors that contributed to enhanced function of genes that regulate hepatic lipogenesis, oxidation, and lipid clearance.

DISCUSSION

NAFLD is one of the most common and concerning consequences of obesity as it is a primary driver of serious adverse liver outcomes and lacks effective medical interventions. Bariatric surgery operations such as SG and RYGB are efficacious treatments for NAFLD, yet it remains unclear whether weight loss is the sole determinant of steatosis resolution. Here, we compared RYGB, one of the most effective surgical treatments for NAFLD, to WM CR and sham controls to establish potential weight-independent hepatoprotective mechanisms of action. We observed that (1) RYGB decreased fat mass and preserved energy expenditure at an equivalent body weight to CR; (2) RYGB decreased liver fat while enhancing glycogen deposition relative to CR; (3) RYGB and CR result in overlapping yet discrete hepatic transcriptomes; and (4) that RYGB enhances enterohepatic bile acid signaling, which is associated with a larger augmentation of genes that regulate hepatic lipid homeostasis. Collectively, these data support the notion that RYGB resolves steatosis by shared and discrete mechanisms to enhance outcomes achieved by weight reduction alone.

Obesity causes NAFLD when the hepatic influx of fatty acids exceeds that of oxidation and export/secretion. Obesity also drives de novo synthesis of intrahepatic triglycerides, which proceeds in a less controlled manner with increasing insulin resistance [20]. As such, interventions of any kind that (1) lower the hepatic influx of free fatty acids and/or (2) increase the utilization will have a net positive effect on NAFLD. To this end, it was not surprising that CR alone resulted in a 4-fold reduction in steatosis relative to sham. These findings are consistent with a plethora of preclinical and clinical evidence that demonstrates a dose-responsive relationship between CR and improvement in hepatic function [21, 22]. Similarly, treatments that enhance fatty acid oxidation in the absence of weight loss such as short-term exercise training improve hepatic lipid composition, insulin action, and biomarkers of liver function [23–25]. However, in the case of both CR and exercise, NAFLD recurrence is high, which is largely attributable to treatment burden [26].

There is now substantial evidence that bariatric surgery induces significant NAFLD remission and prevents major adverse liver outcomes [6, 27]. However, there remains immense dispute as to whether the metabolic benefit is derived solely from the weight-reduced state or a combination of weight loss and surgical adaptation. Our data illustrate clearly that weight loss is a central component of the RYGB-mediated resolution of steatosis, as there was significant phenotypic and transcriptomic overlap between surgical and CR animals. However, there was a further reduction in whole-body fat and intrahepatic lipid with RYGB compared with CR, indicating an additive mechanism of action. RYGB differs from CR by two means, the first being the creation of a small stomach pouch ~15% of the size of a normal stomach, restricting the desire/ability to eat, and the second being diversion of nutrients from the proximal intestine via the Roux limb. In this view, if RYGB is discretely altering biological processes that affect gastrointestinal and hepatic function, one would expect that the upstream signals would originate from the intestinal segments distal to the Roux limb.

Consistent with numerous reports, we observed that FXR was activated in the distal intestine following RYGB. FXR may directly influence intrahepatic lipid by governing bile acid transcriptional networks that regulate fatty acid homeostasis or indirectly by enhancing hepatic insulin sensitivity. For example, the synthetic FXR agonists GSK2324 and GW4064 reverse NAFLD and adverse liver outcomes in mice by reducing lipid absorption and suppressing fatty acid synthesis [28, 29]. Bile acid species and derivatives that activate FXR such as obeticholic chenodeoxycholic acid similarly lower intrahepatic lipid content [30]. Hepatic bile acid synthesis and transport are controlled by OST-α/OST-β and SHP [31], both of which were selectively activated by RYGB. The OST-α/OST-β heterodimer complex senses the influx of bile acids to the liver, causing repression/activation of downstream targets that augment bile acid synthesis and lipid homeostasis [32]. For example, loss-of-function mutations in OST-α/OST-β cause hepatic hypercholesterolemia and hypertriglyceridemia [33]. Consistently, SHP levels decline in humans and rodents with advancing NAFLD [34]. Suppression of SHP expression via doxycycline and cyclosporin A induces acute hepatic lipid accumulation [35]. Furthermore, overexpression of SHP-1 restricts hepatic lipid accumulation and inflammation in mice with NAFLD [34].

For downstream FXR targets to alter NAFLD, one or more of the requisite lipid homeostatic pathways must be altered. We observed that RYGB was mechanistically distinguished from CR animals by greater suppression of genes regulating de novo lipogenesis and increased lipid oxidation. SREBF1 encodes the hepatic SREBP-1c protein, which mediates the insulin-responsive restriction of endogenous lipid biosynthesis. SREBP-1c expression is elevated in rodents and humans with obesity and NAFLD [36, 37]. Single nucleotide polymorphisms in SREBF1 are associated with increased risk of obesity and type 2 diabetes [38]. Furthermore, overexpression of hepatic SREBP-1c causes NAFLD and obesity. Cyp27a1 localizes to the mitochondria to facilitate cholesterol oxidation to support bile acid formation and lipid export [39, 40]. Stigmasterol, a phytosterol CYP27A1 agonist, lowers diet-induced NAFLD and circulating cholesterol levels in mice [41]. Congruently, loss of CYP27A1 increases hepatic lipid accumulation and inflammation, whereas overexpression in Kupffer cells lowers inflammation and cholesterol accumulation [42]. Furthermore, advanced forms of NAFLD such as cirrhosis are associated with albumin dysfunction and hypoalbuminemia, causing generalized edema and weakness [43, 44]. As such, sustaining circulating albumin concentrations is critical in the management of progressive liver disease. Here, we demonstrate that RYGB stimulates transcriptional activity of the albumin gene, which is required for albumin synthesis. Albumin is also required for lipid oxidation by serving as a transport vehicle for fatty acids of varying lengths, ultimately dictating intracellular uptake and use [45].

CONCLUSION

RYGB improved hepatic function and decreased the severity of NAFLD through weight loss and discrete surgical mechanisms of action in mice with diet-induced obesity. RYGB decreased hepatic lipid content to a greater extent than CR independent of food intake, indicating that evading the expected decline in energy expenditure and more selective reduction of fat mass during weight loss likely accounts for the additive hepatic benefit of RYGB. On a molecular level, RYGB stimulated enterohepatic gene networks that regulate bile acid and lipid homeostasis, which likely mediate reduced intrahepatic lipid content.O
