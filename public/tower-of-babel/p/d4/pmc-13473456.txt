Substrate recognition and transport mechanism of the human proton-coupled amino-acid transporter 1 (SLC36A1)
Authors: Jian Yin, Sensen Zhang, Chang Liu, Min Xie, Yuanzhu Gao, Maofei Chen, Yixue Wang, Meiying Chen, Hongxuan Fan, Zi Yang, Huan Li, Li Liang, Boda Zhou, Xudong Chen, Maojun Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The proton-coupled amino-acid transporter SLC36A1 (hPAT1) is an atypical H⁺-driven carrier and mediates the intestinal absorption of a wide array of zwitterionic amino-acid analogs, including many compounds with central nervous-system (CNS) activity, as well as the activation of the mTORC1 pathway and the export of amino acids from lysosomes, thereby maintaining cellular amino-acid homeostasis. Here, we present the cryo-EM structures of a member of the SLC36 family, hPAT1, in its apo state and in complex with three chemically distinct substrates, including the α-amino acid D-serine, the β-amino acid nipecotic acid, and the heterocyclic drug D-cycloserine, at resolutions of 3.4–3.5 Å. Despite their chemical diversity, all ligands adopt a spatially convergent binding mode, elucidating the structural basis for PAT1’s broad substrate promiscuity. In addition, we identify E270 as a potential proton-binding site. Together, these findings provide structural insights into the molecular mechanism of proton-coupled amino acid transport. Notably, the cryo-EM structure of PAT1 bound to D-cycloserine illustrates a viable oral CNS drug delivery strategy: exploiting polar scaffolds to achieve transporter-mediated intestinal absorption and blood–brain barrier penetration without relying on high lipophilicity.

Introduction

Amino acid transport across membranes is fundamental to cellular physiology, supporting protein synthesis, neurotransmitter recycling, and the regulation of cell growth and metabolism1,2. In metazoans, most amino-acid transporters mediate amino acid uptake via sodium-coupled symport or exchange, achieving high substrate specificity but moderate transport capacity2. Human PAT1 (hPAT1), encoded by SLC36A1, belongs to the SLC36 family and functions via proton coupling to transport small, non-branched zwitterionic α-, β-, and γ-amino and imino acids, N-methylated amino acids, amino acids with four- to six-membered heterocycles, and selected amino acid derivatives3,4. Unlike most amino acid transporters, hPAT1 combines low substrate specificity with high transport capacity. It is abundantly expressed on the apical membrane of enterocytes, where it plays a critical role in bulk nutrient uptake immediately post-feeding5,6.

Due to its broad substrate profile, hPAT1 also facilitates the intestinal absorption of several orally active, hydrophilic, amino acid-like drugs. These include: (i) GABAergic agents—nipecotic acid7,8 and guvacine9 (GAT inhibitors), gaboxadol10,11 and muscimol12 (GABA_A receptor agonists), and vigabatrin13 (GABA transaminase inhibitor); (ii) glutamatergic modulators—1-aminocyclopropane-1-carboxylic acid7 (a partial NMDA agonist) and D-serine14,15 (a co-agonist); and (iii) other therapeutics—D-cycloserine14,16 (antibiotic/antipsychotic), 5-aminolevulinic acid17(photodynamic prodrug), 3-amino-1-propanesulphonic acid18 (anti-addiction agent), betaine3 (for homocystinuria), and β-guanidinopropionic acid19 (antihyperglycemic compound).

Beyond the gut, hPAT1 is localized to lysosomal membranes in various tissues and within neurons in the brain, where it exports small neutral amino acids generated by proteolysis20. This function contributes to intracellular amino acid homeostasis and nutrient sensing, which regulates mTORC1 activation21–23. As the primary site of oral drug absorption, the small intestine—and specifically its transporters—plays a decisive role in determining the bioavailability and pharmacokinetics of hydrophilic drugs24,25. Given hPAT1’s broad substrate recognition and CNS drug relevance, it represents a promising target for enhancing enteric drug delivery.

Here, we report cryo-EM structures of full-length hPAT1 in its apo state and in complex with three representative substrates: D-serine, D-cycloserine, and nipecotic acid. These structures provide mechanistic insights into hPAT1’s transport cycle and substrate promiscuity, and offer a structural framework for the rational design of hPAT1-guided oral formulations to improve the delivery of poorly permeable therapeutics.

Results

Structure determination and overall structure of hPAT1

To prepare homogeneous hPAT1 samples suitable for cryo-EM analysis, we cloned the hPAT1 coding sequence with a C-terminal Strep-tag into the pCAGGS vector and transiently expressed it in HEK293 cells for 48 h. Cells were then harvested, and the protein was purified and reconstituted into lauryl maltose neopentyl glycol (LMNG) micelles (Supplementary Figs. 1, 2). Single-particle cryo-EM datasets were collected under four conditions: apo (PAT1apo), D-serine–bound (PAT1D-serine), D-cycloserine–bound (PAT1D-cycloserine), and nipecotic acid–bound (PAT1nipecotic acid), yielding reconstructions at 3.5 Å, 3.4 Å, 3.5 Å, and 3.4 Å resolution, respectively (Fig. 1, Supplementary Figs. 3, 4and Supplementary Table 1). hPAT1 comprises 476 residues and adopts a compact 55 × 55 Å structure conforming to the prokaryotic LeuT-like fold (Fig. 1a, b). It contains 11 transmembrane (TM) helices, with TM1–5 and TM6–10 forming two pseudo-symmetric repeats, and both TM1 and TM6 are partially unwound at the midpoint of the transmembrane helices, forming the core substrate-binding cavity. In the plasma membrane, the N terminus faces the cytosol and the C terminus faces the extracellular space, whereas in lysosomes the orientation is inverted, positioning the C terminus inside the lumen (Fig. 1b). Extracellular and intracellular loops bridge adjacent TM helices, with a disulfide bond observed between C180 and C32926. Electron density maps also reveal glycosylation at N174 and N183, which aligns with previous reports. No density was detected for the putative glycosylation site N470, due to unresolved flexibility in the C-terminal region.

We determined the structure of the hPAT1D-serine at 3.4 Å resolution. The substrate binds in a mid-membrane pocket formed by residues from TM1, TM3, TM6, and TM8 (Fig. 2b), where a well-defined, non-protein density corresponds to a single D-serine molecule (Fig. 2c). The local resolution of 3.1 Å supported modeling of D-serine and its interactions. The carboxyl group is oriented toward TM3 and TM8, forming hydrogen bonds with Y158 (TM3) at distances of 2.65 Å and 2.55 Å (Fig. 2d). The amino group and hydroxyl side chain point toward TM1 and TM6, forming polar interactions with the backbone carbonyls of G59 (TM1) and F267 (TM6) at distances of 2.61 Å and 2.77 Å, respectively (Fig. 2d). Additional hydrophobic interactions, particularly with F154 and F267, contribute to substrate stabilization (Fig. 2d). To further validate the substrate-binding site and the spatial orientation of the substrate within the binding pocket, we introduced point mutations at key residues and measured their binding affinities using SPR (Fig. 2e). Consistent with our structural analysis, the binding affinities of the Y158A, Y158F, and F267A mutants for D-serine are markedly reduced, whereas the T63A mutation had no significant effect. In addition, guided by the cryo-EM density and the steric and chemical plausibility of the substrate-binding pocket, we performed 1000 ns molecular dynamics simulations on four density-compatible poses of D-serine (Supplementary Fig. 6). Trajectory inspection together with binding energy analysis showed that only one D-serine pose remained stably bound, whereas the other three failed to maintain stable association with the protein after ~100 ns (Supplementary Fig. 6a, c). MM/PBSA analysis of the final 200 ns of the stable trajectory yielded a favorable binding free energy for this pose (ΔGbind = −32.36 ± 5.91 kcal/mol) (Supplementary Table 2). Per-residue energy decomposition of binding-pocket residues indicated that Y158, F267, G59, and F154 contributed to stabilization of the bound D-serine, while T63 also made a prominent contribution (Supplementary Table 3). These results support the proposed D-serine binding mode and are broadly consistent with the structural model and the mutation data.

A π–π interaction between F154 and F267 closes the substrate-binding pocket, whereas polar contacts between T264 and S401 and between L260 and S401, together with extracellular loop 3 (EL3), seal the extracellular gate (Fig. 2f). These interactions might contribute to the observed preference of the transporter toward the inward-open, outward-closed state. A structural comparison with the PAT1apo revealed minimal conformational change, especially in TM1 and TM6, with an overall RMSD of 0.540 Å (Fig. 2g).

Notably, the E270 residue in the unwound region of TM6 adopts a different orientation in the apo structure compared to the substrate-bound (D-serine) state. In the PAT1apo structure, the side chain of E270 extends toward the cavity, whereas in the PAT1D-serine structure, the side chain of E270 flips upward, partially opening the substrate-binding cavity (Fig. 2g, h). Further structural comparisons suggest that if E270 faces the substrate-binding pocket, its side chain may spatially clash with the D-serine, potentially hindering ligand binding (Fig. 2h). The conformational flexibility of the E270 side chain in the transport pocket may be linked to substrate recruitment and binding, enabling diversity and adaptability in substrate recognition and recruitment patterns.

Recognition mechanism of D-cycloserine by PAT1

D-cycloserine is a zwitterionic amino acid analog with broad-spectrum antibiotic activity42–46 and CNS effects47–50. It is an FDA-approved drug used to treat tuberculosis43,44,51 and acts as a partial agonist at the glycine-binding site of NMDA receptors52,53, akin to D-serine. Its lipophilic isoxazolidinone ring enhances blood–brain barrier penetration. Understanding its interaction with hPAT1 reveals its mechanism of oral absorption and provides a model for drug design—highlighting how compounds can be optimized for both oral bioavailability and brain accessibility. The cryo-EM structure of the PAT1D-cycloserine, with a local resolution of 3.2 Å in the binding pocket, revealed a distinct triangular-shaped density consistent with the geometry of D-cycloserine (Fig. 3b). The binding pocket is open to the cytoplasm, as in the PAT1apo and PAT1D-serine structures (Fig. 3c). The isoxazolidinone ring of D-cycloserine extends inward, the amino group points toward TM1, and the carbonyl group orients toward TM3 and TM8 (Fig. 3d). D-cycloserine is stabilized in the binding pocket primarily through polar interactions between its carbonyl group and Y158 (TM3) and between its amino group and G62 (TM1), with interaction distances of 3.34 Å and 3.30 Å, respectively, together with hydrophobic contacts involving F154, F267, Y158 and Y303 (Fig. 3d). SPR measurements showed that the Y158A and Y158F mutations markedly weakened binding to D-cycloserine, highlighting the critical role of Y158. The F267A mutation also reduced D-cycloserine binding, whereas the T63A mutant showed no significant difference compared with the WT (Fig. 3a). Similarly, based on the cryo-EM density and the steric and chemical plausibility of the substrate-binding pocket, we performed 1000 ns molecular dynamics simulations on two density-compatible poses of D-cycloserine (Supplementary Fig. 7). One of these orientations dissociated from the binding pocket after approximately 100 ns (Supplementary Fig. 7a, c). Similar MM/PBSA analysis of the stable D-cycloserine pose also yielded a favorable binding free energy (ΔG_bind = − 23.56 ± 9.31 kcal/mol) (Supplementary Table 2). Per-residue energy decomposition further supported contributions from pocket residues, including G62, Y158, F267, and F154, while T63 also contributed to ligand stabilization, and the overall pattern was generally consistent with the observed interaction network and mutational analysis (Supplementary Table 4).

The PAT1D-cycloserine and PAT1D-serine structures both adopt similar inward-open conformations, with an RMSD of 0.446 Å (Fig. 3e). Despite chemical differences, the ligands bind in a partially overlapping mode (Fig. 3f). The hydroxyl and amino groups of D-serine are spatially aligned with the carboxylate group and the isoxazolidinone ring nitrogen of D-cycloserine, accounting for their similar interaction profiles (Fig. 3f).

Recognition mechanism of nipecotic acid by PAT1

Nipecotic acid, a conformationally restricted β-amino acid analog of γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA), is a well-characterized GABA reuptake inhibitor widely used in neuroscience54–56. By competitively inhibiting GABA transporters (GATs), it elevates extracellular GABA concentrations, thereby enhancing inhibitory neurotransmission. In addition, it exhibits weak agonist activity at GABA_A receptors57. We determined the cryo-EM structure of the PAT1nipecotic acid at 3.4 Å resolution, revealing a teardrop-shaped density within the substrate-binding pocket, consistent with the geometry of nipecotic acid (Fig. 4b). The local resolution within the binding site was also 3.3 Å, enabling precise modeling of the ligand. The binding cavity is open toward the cytoplasmic side, with the charged six-membered ring of nipecotic acid centrally embedded and its carboxyl group directed toward TM3 (Fig. 4b, d). Hydrogen bonding interactions were observed between the ligand carboxyl group and Y158, with a distance of 2.59 Å. Furthermore, the NH group on the ring forms a polar contact with the backbone of F267 at a distance of 3.12 Å. Hydrophobic interactions with F154, Y158, and F267 further stabilize the ligand (Fig. 4b, c). We similarly performed mutagenesis in the binding pocket and evaluated ligand binding by SPR. The Y158A and Y158F mutations completely abolished binding to nipecotic acid, indicating that Y158 plays a critical role in its recognition and binding. In addition, the F267A mutant also lost affinity for nipecotic acid, whereas the T63A mutation showed no significant difference from the wild type (Fig. 4a). These results are consistent with our observations for D-serine and D-cycloserine.

The PAT1nipecotic acid complex adopts an inward-open conformation nearly identical to that of the PAT1D-cycloserine, with a Cα RMSD of 0.510 Å (Fig. 4e). Both structures display highly similar ligand-binding modes. In each case, the carboxylate or carbonyl oxygen of the ligand consistently orients toward residue Y158 on TM3 (Fig. 4f). Notably, the ring nitrogen atoms of nipecotic acid and D-cycloserine occupy adjacent spatial positions and exhibit a similar tilt toward the cytoplasmic side, aligning along TM1 and forming an angle of approximately 34° between their orientations (Fig. 4f). A similar binding mode is observed in the PAT1D-serine (RMSD = 0.503 Å). The carboxylate and amino groups of D-serine occupy positions analogous to those in nipecotic acid within PAT1, highlighting the conserved role of Y158 in ligand recognition and supporting a unified geometric and electrostatic recognition mechanism for structurally diverse ligands (Fig. 4g).

The spatial tolerance of the ligand amino or ring nitrogen atoms appears to be modulated by a polar microenvironment formed by backbone carbonyls in the unwound segments of TM1 and TM6, involving G59, G62, F267, and S268, which support spatially distributed polar interactions.

Structure divergence and mechanistic specialization of PAT1

The PAT family is highly conserved, particularly in the substrate binding pocket, with only minor variations (Supplementary Fig. 8). Key recognition residues Y158 on TM3 and T63 on TM1 are invariant, as are F154 and F267, which form a π–π stack closing the pocket, indicating a shared transport mechanism. The unwound TM1 segment shaping the polar cavity is also conserved—G59 (C in PAT3), G62, T63, G64, and L65 (I in PAT2)—with N60 strictly conserved (Supplementary Fig. 8). Given the role of an aspartate on TM1 in SLC6 transporters for substrate recognition, we speculate N60 may serve a similar function. Sequence divergence occurs mainly in the unwound TM6 region (Supplementary Fig. 8). The higher affinity of PAT2 and PAT4 may stem from subtle substitutions here: in PAT2, G271 replaced by S278 inserts a side chain into the pocket, potentially strengthening binding, while in PAT4, hydrophobic changes (A283 vs. S268, V289 vs. M274 in PAT1) could alter local flexibility and E285 orientation, modulating selectivity (Supplementary Fig. 8). PAT1 orthologues from bovine, murine, and zebrafish show similar conservation, especially around the pocket, underscoring the evolutionary preservation of proton-coupled small amino acid transport and PAT1’s role in amino acid homeostasis (Supplementary Fig. 9).

To assess LeuT fold conservation, we superimposed PAT1D-serine and PAT1D-cycloserine onto zebrafish SLC38A9 (PDB: 6C08). The Cα RMSDs (3.570 Å and 3.502 Å) confirm an inward-open architecture, with TM1b and TM6a nearly identical, while SLC38A9’s TM1a tilts more steeply (Fig. 5a, b). Substrate orientation is partially conserved: Y158 in PAT1 and Y204 in SLC38A9 both recognize the carboxylate of D-serine and leucine, and T63 is preserved (Fig. 5c). However, PAT1 lacks S122, allowing greater amino group mobility for D-serine and D-cycloserine (Fig. 5c, d). Pocket size and chemistry differ: in SLC38A9, leucine’s aliphatic tail projects between TM1 and TM8, stabilized by Q438 and a larger cavity. PAT1 lacks this TM8 shift and polar side chain, resulting in a narrower, highly polar pocket optimized for small zwitterionic substrates (Fig. 5c, d). Previous studies have shown that hPAT1 transports classical substrates with a fixed 1:1 proton-to-substrate stoichiometry58. We therefore compared our structure with that of the proton-coupled amino acid transporter MjApcT (PDB: 3GIA), which also operates with a 1:1 proton-to-substrate transport stoichiometry. In the MjApcT crystal structure, a partially-conserved lysine residue on TM5 extends into the Na2 site (Fig. 6a). Based on this observation, we examined the potential Na1 and Na2 sites within the LeuT fold of hPAT1. Notably, we found that in the potential Na2 site of hPAT1, residue M228 on TM5 protrudes into the site (Fig. 6b). Similarly, in the potential Na1 site, Y303 on TM5 occupies the corresponding position (Fig. 6b). Moreover, the amino acids at the Na1 and Na2 sites are highly conserved across the PAT family. Through structural comparison, we found that M202 on TM6 of MjApcT in the apo state and E270 of PAT1apo adopt similar spatial positions and orientations within the substrate-binding pocket (Fig. 6c). Using the PROPKA program, we predicted that the pKa values of E270 were 7.81 and 7.77 in the apo and substrate-bound states, respectively, which is markedly higher than the typical pKa of glutamate (~ 4.1). We next performed whole-cell [3H]-L-proline uptake assays on wild-type PAT1 as well as the E270A and E270Q mutants (Fig. 6d). After subtraction of background uptake measured in vector-only control cells, WT PAT1 exhibited pronounced pH-dependent substrate transport, consistent with previous reports. Importantly, both E270A and E270Q mutations significantly impaired substrate transport activity, while showing no significant difference between pH 6.0 and pH 7.4 under the conditions tested (Fig. 6d). Immunoblot analysis indicated that these mutations did not substantially affect protein expression levels. It is possible that E270 may undergo protonation during the transport cycle, thereby promoting substrate binding at the central site formed by TM1, TM3 and TM6.

Discussion

hPAT1 is a proton-coupled amino acid transporter of the SLC36/PAT family characterized by broad substrate permissiveness. Our cryo-EM structures of PAT1 bound to three chemically distinct ligands—D-serine (an α-amino acid), nipecotic acid (a β-amino acid), and D-cycloserine (a five-membered heterocyclic compound)—illuminate its substrate adaptability, and the polar nature of the binding cavity contributes to PAT1’s low substrate specificity. Moreover, spatial constraints imposed by residues Y158, F154, and F267 along three directions reduce the pocket volume, and the lack of a polar side chain on TM8 leaves no anchor point between TM1 and TM8 for long substrates like leucine. As a result, PAT1 favors small-sized substrates. These structural determinants collectively define the ligand selectivity and transport capability of PAT1.

During the MD simulations, T63 residue consistently exhibited a relatively large energetic contribution in both systems. Although SPR results suggested that T63 is not a primary interacting residue for D-serine or D-cycloserine in the inward-open state, the per-residue energy decomposition analysis raises the possibility that, in the membrane environment, Thr63 may play a role in stabilizing the substrate-bound conformation and in facilitating subsequent transport-related processes after substrate binding.

By resolving the substrate-bound structure of the PAT1, we provide a molecular framework for enhancing the intestinal absorption of small hydrophilic drugs. This structural insight enables rational design strategies aimed at optimizing oral bioavailability. Traditional approaches emphasize increased lipophilicity to promote passive membrane diffusion, but this often reduces aqueous solubility, increases off-target tissue accumulation, and elevates metabolic burden. Our structure of the PAT1D-cycloserine provides an example in which a small polar scaffold undergoes intestinal uptake through H⁺-coupled transport, while its moderate size and polarity are compatible with blood–brain barrier penetration. This mechanism may inform CNS-targeted drug development strategies that seek to balance oral exposure and brain delivery without relying primarily on lipophilicity.

Here, we present the cryo-EM structures of hPAT1 in apo and substrate-bound states, revealing a conserved binding pocket that accommodates chemically diverse ligands through convergent polar interactions centered on Y158, together with pocket-shaping contributions from F154 and F267. These findings elucidate the structural basis for hPAT1’s broad substrate specificity and its role in proton-driven amino acid transport. Our work establishes a foundational framework for the rational design of small polar therapeutics that exploit hPAT1-mediated absorption and paves the way for further mechanistic and translational investigations into proton-coupled nutrient and drug transporters.

Methods

Materials

Reagents used in this study included NaCl (S9888) and HEPES (4-(2-Hydroxyethyl)piperazine-1-ethanesulfonic acid; H3375), both purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Protease inhibitor Cocktail Tablets (04693116001) were sourced from Roche. A 10:1 premixed solution of LMNG/CHS (NG310-CH210) was obtained from Anatrace. Strep-Tactin resin (2-1208-500) and D-desthiobiotin (2-1000-005) were supplied by IBA. Polyethylenimine (PEI, Linear, MW 25,000; 23966) was obtained from Polysciences.

For cell culture, HEK293F cells (R79007) and ARPE-19 cells (CL-0026) were obtained from Thermo Fisher Scientific and Procell, respectively. HEK293F cells were cultured in SMM 293-TII medium (Sino Biological). DMEM/F-12 medium (PM150312) were puechased from Procell and fetal bovine serum (FBS; 10091148), and Penicillin-Streptomycin Solution (SV30010) were purchased from Gibco and Hyclone, respectively. The PAT1 substrates D-serine (HY-100808), D-cycloserine (HY-B0030), and nipecotic acid (HY-69359) were purchased from MedChemExpress.

Codon-optimized Homo sapien SLC36A1 cDNA (UniProt: Q7Z2H8) was synthesized by Xianghong Biotech and cloned into the pCAGGS vector with an N-terminal twin-Strep tag for cryo-EM studies and SPR. For whole-cell transport assays, WT human SLC36A1 was cloned into pcDNA3.1(+). Point mutations were introduced into the WT construct by PCR-based site-directed mutagenesis. All constructs were validated by sequencing. All plasmids and primer sequences used in this study are shown in Supplementary Table 5. Requests for materials should be addressed to M.Y. (maojunyang@mail.tsinghua.edu.cn).

Cell culture and transfection

For protein expression and purification, the codon-optimized cDNA encoding Homo sapiens SLC36A1 (UniProt: Q7Z2H8), with an N-terminal tandem twin Strep-tag, was cloned into the pCAGGS vector. HEK293F cells were cultured in suspension in SMM 293-TII medium supplemented with 1 × penicillin–streptomycin, at 37 °C in a Multitron-Pro shaker (Infors) set to 120 r.p.m. under 5% CO₂. For transfection, 1 mg of plasmid DNA was pre-incubated with 3 mg of linear polyethyleneimine (PEI) in 50 mL of fresh medium for 25 min and added to 1 L of HEK293F cells at a density of 2.5 × 10⁶ cells per mL. Transfected cells were cultured for 48 h prior to harvesting. For the whole-cell transport assay, ARPE-19 cells were seeded in 24-well plates (PerkinElmer, 1450). After 24 h, cells at 70–90% confluence were transfected with pcDNA3.1(+) plasmids encoding wild-type or mutant PAT1. For each well, 500 ng plasmid DNA and 1 μL P3000 reagent were diluted in 25 μL Opti-MEM, while 0.75 μL Lipofectamine 3000 was diluted separately in 25 μL Opti-MEM. The two solutions were combined and incubated for 15 min at room temperature, then added to the cells. The culture medium was replaced with fresh medium 6 h after transfection.

Protein expression and purification for cryo-EM analysis

For purification of full-length human PAT1, approximately 2 liters of transfected HEK293F cells were collected by centrifugation at 3000 × g. All subsequent steps were performed at 4 °C or on ice. Cell pellets were resuspended in lysis buffer containing 25 mM HEPES (pH 7.4), 150 mM NaCl, 2 mM DTT, 1 mM EDTA, and protease inhibitor cocktail, followed by lysis using a high-pressure homogenizer. Cell debris was removed by centrifugation at 8000 × g for 30 min, and membranes were isolated by ultracentrifugation at 150,000 × g for 1 h. The membrane fraction was solubilized by incubation in lysis buffer supplemented with 1% (w/v) LMNG and 0.1% (w/v) CHS for 2 h with gentle rotation. After a second ultracentrifugation at 150,000 × g for 45 min, the supernatant was passed through a column filled with Strep-Tactin Sepharose resin (IBA). The resin was washed with 50 column volumes (CV) of buffer containing 25 mM HEPES (pH 7.4), 150 mM NaCl, 2 mM DTT, 0.005% (w/v) LMNG, and 0.0005% (w/v) CHS. Bound protein was eluted with the same buffer supplemented with 10 mM desthiobiotin. Eluted fractions were concentrated to ~ 100 μL using a 50 kDa molecular weight cut-off centrifugal concentrator (Millipore) and further purified by size-exclusion chromatography (SEC) on a Superose 6 Increase 5/150 column (GE Healthcare) equilibrated in SEC buffer containing 25 mM HEPES (pH 7.4), 150 mM NaCl, 0.003% (w/v) LMNG, and 0.0003% (w/v) CHS. Fractions corresponding to monomeric PAT1 were pooled, confirmed by SDS-PAGE, and concentrated to 20–25 mg/mL for cryo-EM grid preparation.

Cryo-EM sample preparation and data acquisition

For PAT1-apo, purified protein was directly concentrated to 20–25 mg/mL for cryo-EM sample preparation. For ligand-bound complexes, PAT1 purified in NaCl buffer was concentrated to 20–25 mg/mL and incubated separately with 20 mM D-serine (PAT1D-serine), 20 mM D-cycloserine (PAT1D-cycloserine), or 20 mM nipecotic acid (PAT1nipecotic acid) for 1 hour before being applied to the grids. All cryo-EM grids were prepared using a Vitrobot Mark IV (FEI) operating at 8 °C and 100% humidity. A volume of 3-4 μL of protein sample was applied to glow-discharged holey carbon grids (Quantifoil Au R1.2/1.3, 300 mesh). After an incubation time of 8 s, grids were blotted for 3-5 s and vitrified by rapid plunging into liquid ethane. Initial screening of grids was performed on a Tecnai Arctica transmission electron microscope (FEI) operated at 200 kV, equipped with a Falcon 3 direct electron detector (FEI).

Cryo-EM data for all four PAT1 samples were collected on a Titan Krios G3 transmission electron microscope (FEI) operating at 300 kV, equipped with an energy filter (slit width 20 eV; GIF Quantum LS, Gatan). Micrographs were acquired with a Gatan K3 Summit direct electron detector at a nominal magnification of 105,000 ×, corresponding to a super-resolution pixel size of 0.415 Å. Automated data acquisition was carried out using the EPU software. Each movie was recorded with 32 frames at a frame rate of 0.05 s per frame, resulting in a total exposure time of 1.72 s and an accumulated dose of approximately 50 e⁻/Å² per stack. All 32 frames in each stack were aligned and summed using the whole-image motion correction program MotionCor259 and binned to a physical pixel size of 0.83 Å.

Image processing and 3D reconstruction

A total of 9056, 9683, 7908, and 9283 micrographs (movie stacks) were collected for the four PAT1 datasets, respectively. Image processing was conducted using cryoSPARC60. For the PAT1D-serine dataset, micrographs were first imported, and the CTF corrections were performed using Patch CTF estimation. After deleting low-quality micrographs, 200 micrographs were selected for initial particle picking using the blob picker. Extracted particles were processed through 2D classification, and well-defined class averages were selected to serve as templates for template picking from the whole dataset. A total of 11,436 K particles were picked from 7979 micrographs. These particles were extracted and binned 4 times and subjected to 2D classification. After three rounds of 2D classification, ~ 1796 K particles in good 2D averages were chosen. These particles were re-extracted and binned 2 times, then subjected to the ab-initio reconstruction and the following heterogeneous refinement. Each class map was inspected in Chimera61, and the particles from good classes were selected and then subjected to the next round of ab-initio reconstruction and heterogenious refinement to further remove bad particles. After this operation, 537,327 particles from the best class were selected and re-extrcated to the original pixel size 0.83 Å, and subjected to another ab-initio reconstruction and heterogeneous refinement to further improve data quality. Finally, the best 335,941 particles were used for 3D reconstruction by non-uniform (NU) refinement62 to yield a map at 3.4-Å resolution map with no symmetry imposed. The local resolution map was calculated using local resolution estimation in cryoSPARC and displayed in ChimeraX63. Similar data processing procedures were applied for the other three PAT1 datasets. Finally, 3.7-Å, 3.5-Å, and 3.4-Å resolution maps were obtained, respectively. Please refer to Supplementary Fig. 2 for the workflow of image processing.

Model building

Initial model building of PAT1D-serine was performed de novo in COOT64, as this dataset exhibited the highest overall resolution (Supplementary Figs. 2–4). Before model building, a full-length structure of hPAT1 was predicted using AlphaFold65,66. The predicted model was docked into the 3.4 Å cryo-EM density map using Chimera, followed by manual model adjustment in COOT to generate the atomic model of PAT1D-serine61,67. Model refinement was performed on the main chain of the two atomic models using the real_space_refine module of PHENIX68 with secondary structure and geometry restraints to avoid over-fitting. The refined PAT1D-serine model was then used as the initial template for model building of the remaining three PAT1 structures. After manual corrections in COOT, each model was refined individually using real-space refinement in PHENIX. Cryo-EM data collection and refinement statistics are shown in Supplementary Table 1.

Whole-cell transport assay

ARPE-19 cells were maintained in 24-well plates (PerkinElmer, 1450) and transfected with PAT1 (WT or mutants). Cells transfected with the empty pcDNA3.1(+) vector were included as a background control. 36 h after post-transfection, the culture medium was removed, and the cells were washed with wash buffer containing 140 mM NaCl, 5.4 mM KCl, 0.8 mM MgSO4, 1.8 mM CaCl2, 5 mM glucose and 25 mM MES/HEPES, pH 6 or 7.4. Then the buffer was removed, and cells were incubated with wash buffer containing 50 μM L-proline and 30 nM [3H] L-proline (PerkinElmer, NET483001MC, 93.2 Ci/mmol) at 37 °C. After a certain amount of time, the [3H] L-proline buffer was removed, and cells were washed three times with 0.5 ml ice-cold wash buffer to terminate the reaction and remove residual isotopes completely. Next, 0.5 ml of OptiPhase HiSafe 3 liquid scintillation cocktail (PerkinElmer, 1200.437) was added per well and mixed on a shaker for 10 min. Counts were measured using a MicroBeta2 Microplate counter (PerkinElmer, 2450). All procedures were carried out at room temperature, except the incubation step. Uptake measured in these vector-only control cells was subtracted from all values. The background-corrected uptake values were then normalized to those of WT PAT1 at pH 6.0. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism. Data were analyzed by two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s multiple-comparisons test. A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Immunoblotting

To investigate whether mutation of E270 in PAT1 affects protein expression levels, WT PAT1, E270A, E270Q, or empty vector was transfected into ARPE-19 cells under the same conditions as those used for the whole-cell transport assay. At 36 h post-transfection, the cells were collected and lysed in 2 ml lysis buffer containing 25 mM HEPES (pH 7.4), 150 mM NaCl, 2 mM DTT, 1% (w/v) LMNG, and protease inhibitors. The cell lysates were incubated for 2 h at 4 °C. After centrifugation at 20,000 × g for 15 min, cell debris was removed, and the supernatant was collected. For each protein sample, 20 μL of supernatant was mixed with SDS loading buffer and incubated at 60 °C for 30 min. The samples were separated by a 4–20% SDS–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis gel (GenScript) and transferred onto a PVDF membrane (Millipore). After blocking for 1 h in 5% non-fat milk (Bio-Rad) in PBS containing 0.1% Tween-20 (PBS-T), the membrane was cut into two pieces. The slide containing proteins above 45 kDa was incubated with mouse anti-Strep monoclonal antibody (Bioeasytech, BE2076) diluted 1:5000 for 1 h at room temperature (RT), whereas the other slide containing proteins below 45 kDa was incubated with mouse anti-GAPDH monoclonal antibody (Yeasen, 30201ES) diluted 1:5000 for 1 h at RT. The membranes were washed three times with PBS-T for 5 min each. HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (Bioeasytech, BE0102), diluted 1:5000, was then added and incubated with the membranes for 1 h at RT. The membranes were subsequently washed three more times with PBS-T, and proteins were detected using enhanced chemiluminescence substrate (Pierce) and an Amersham Imager 600 (GE Healthcare).

SPR assay

The binding kinetics between Hpat1 and substrates (D-serine, D-cycloserine and nipecotic acid) were analyzed by Surface plasmon resonance (SPR) assay using Biacore 8 K plus (Cytiva) at room temperature (25 °C). Hpat1 were immobilized on the chip CM5 surface (pH = 5.0), and inhibitors were applied as the analyte via serial dilution. Substrates used were dissolved in lysis buffer and serially diluted with lysis buffer (20 mM HEPES, 150 mM NaCl, 0.003% LMNG) as indicated in figures, sequentially injected into the chip at a flow rate of 30 μL/min for 2 min, and then allowed to dissociate. Data were analyzed using Biacore Insight evaluation software (Cytiva). The data represent the mean ± s.e.m. from three independent biological experiments (n = 3)

Molecular dynamics simulations

Missing unresolved residues in the cryo-EM structure were modeled to generate a complete starting structure for MD simulations. The membrane protein system was then built using CHARMM-GUI69,70. The lipid bilayer was composed of POPC, POPE, POPS, and cholesterol at ratios of 40, 25, 10, and 25%, respectively. The membrane dimensions were 10.41 nm × 10.41 nm, and the overall system size was 10.41 nm × 10.41 nm × 11.76 nm.

Alternative initial ligand poses were generated by manual density-guided modeling. D-serine and D-cycloserine were placed into the substrate-binding pocket based on their fit to the cryo-EM density, while also considering steric compatibility and chemically plausible interactions with surrounding residues. The alternative poses differed mainly in the orientations of the ligands and in the positions of key polar groups within the binding site. Given the spatial constraints imposed by the cryo-EM density, no additional docking-based screening or exhaustive sampling was performed. A limited number of density-compatible and structurally plausible poses were therefore selected as starting conformations for MD simulations.

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were performed using the GROMACS 2021.5 software package71 under constant temperature and pressure (NPT) conditions with periodic boundary conditions. The CHARMM36 all-atom force field was employed, and the TIP3P water model72 was used to represent solvent molecules. During the simulations, hydrogen bonds were constrained using the LINCS algorithm73, and the integration time step was set to 2 fs. Electrostatic interactions were computed using the particle mesh Ewald (PME) method74. Nonbonded interactions were truncated at 10 Å, with updates performed every 10 steps. The temperature was controlled at 300 K using the V-rescale thermostat75, and the pressure was maintained at 1 bar using the Parrinello-Rahman barostat76. Initially, energy minimization was carried out using the steepest descent method to remove steric clashes between atoms. Following this, a 100 ps NVT equilibration simulation was performed at 300 K. Finally, 1000 ns MD simulations were conducted for the D-serine-bound and D-cycloserine-bound systems, with snapshots saved every 100 ps. The simulation results were visualized using the integrated GROMACS tools and VMD (Visual Molecular Dynamics).

After completion of the MD simulations, the 800–1000 ns segment of each trajectory was extracted for binding free-energy analysis. The average binding free energy of each protein–ligand complex was calculated using the MM/PBSA method77,78. In this analysis, ΔG_bind was decomposed into van der Waals (ΔG_vdw), electrostatic (ΔG_ele), polar solvation (ΔG_pb), and nonpolar solvation (ΔG_pbsur) contributions. Per-residue energy decomposition was performed using the molecular mechanics/generalized Born surface area (MM/GBSA) method for residues within approximately 5 Å of the bound ligand. In this approach, each residue’s contribution is decomposed into the gas-phase molecular mechanics energy, the polar solvation energy calculated using the generalized Born (GB) model, and the nonpolar solvation energy estimated using the LCPO model, with further partitioning into backbone and side-chain components79,80.
