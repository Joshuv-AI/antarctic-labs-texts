A diagnostic model based on pulmonary microbiota and host gene expression to distinguish colonization from pneumonia
Authors: Zhangfan Fu, Yuhan Sun, Haijun Yao, Qihui Liu, Qiran Zhang, Jin Hu, Yang Zhou, Ning Jiang, Jingwen Ai, Jialin Jin, Wenhong Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Pneumonia remains a leading cause of global mortality. Conventional diagnostic approaches frequently fail to distinguish microbial colonization from true infection in the lower respiratory tract, complicating clinical decision-making and contributing to antibiotic overuse. Improved diagnostic strategies are urgently needed. In this prospective, single-center study, deep sputum specimens were collected from patients with respiratory colonization (n = 17) and infectious pneumonia (n = 27) admitted to the neurosurgical ICU of Huashan Hospital. Metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) and metatranscriptomic profiling were performed to characterize both the pulmonary microbiota and the host immune response. These features were subsequently integrated to construct a diagnostic model. Microbial community profiling revealed reduced alpha diversity and enrichment of metabolically active pathogenic taxa in the infection group, consistent with a dysbiotic state permissive to invasion. In contrast, the colonization group demonstrated a more balanced microbial ecosystem. Transcriptomic analyses identified 2232 differentially expressed host genes between the two groups. The colonization group showed marked activation of the Wnt, MAPK, chemokine, and focal adhesion pathways, which are functionally implicated in epithelial barrier maintenance and early immune homeostasis. A multi-omics diagnostic model incorporating seven gene features (ANKRD52, ZC3HAV1L, SERPINE3, CDPF1, ZNF720, TAGLN3, and LRRC15) achieved a discrimination between colonization and infection (AUC = 0.951 in the training cohort; 0.875 in the validation set). By jointly analyzing the pulmonary microbiome and host transcriptome, this study provides insight into host–microbe interactions distinguishing colonization from infection and presents a predictive model with potential clinical relevance.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-44972-w.

Introduction

Lower respiratory tract infections remain among the most lethal infectious diseases worldwide, imposing a high burden of morbidity and mortality1. In the United States, non-ventilator hospital-acquired pneumonia (NV-HAP) occurs at approximately 0.55 events per 100 hospitalizations (≈0.96 per 1000 patient-days) and accounts for an estimated 7.3% of in-hospital deaths2. In intensive-care settings, ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP) develops in about 30% of mechanically ventilated adult patients (95% CI 24–37%)3.

Accurate and timely microbiological diagnosis of pneumonia is essential to guide appropriate antimicrobial therapy and thereby reduce mortality. However, determining whether a microorganism detected in the lower respiratory tract reflects colonization or clinical infection remains challenging4.

Although multiplex molecular diagnostic platforms provide greater analytical sensitivity and reproducibility than traditional culture-based methods, this increased sensitivity does not inherently confer clinical specificity; in practice, these assays often fail to distinguish active infection from asymptomatic carriage5. Attempts to infer disease status from microbial load have yielded inconsistent results. In pediatric acute respiratory illness, for instance, Streptococcus pneumoniae density fluctuates with symptom onset and resolution6, and higher pneumococcal burden has been associated with greater symptom severity and prolonged hospitalization7. Nonetheless, when symptoms are driven primarily by immune-mediated or toxin-mediated injury, pathogen burden may not accurately reflect the underlying disease process8.

Recently, the diagnostic utility of mNGS in pneumonia has gained increasing recognition, and several quantitative diagnostic models have been developed to support its clinical application9. However, mNGS pipelines designed for low-biomass fluids such as cerebrospinal fluid perform less effectively in the respiratory tract, where commensal microbiota complicate the identification of clinically relevant pathogens10,11. Peripheral blood transcriptomic signatures can discriminate viral and bacterial etiologies by profiling host immune responses, yet they cannot resolve whether detected airway organisms represent colonization or infection12,13. By simultaneously characterizing pathogens, airway microbiota and host immune responses from respiratory specimens, mNGS offers the potential to distinguish microbial colonization from active infection13. Langelier et al. reported a similar approach, achieving a high AUC for their diagnostic model (0.8–0.96, 95% CI, 0.63–1.00), using bronchoalveolar lavage samples from patients with infectious and non-infectious respiratory failure14. In clinical practice, most patients present with non-severe pneumonia rather than respiratory failure. Bronchoalveolar lavage is resource-intensive, whereas deep sputum collection is considerably less invasive and more feasible.

In this prospective study, we applied metagenomic and metatranscriptomic sequencing of deep sputum to profile airway microbiota and host transcriptional responses across colonization and infection, aiming to identify molecular signatures that can distinguish the two states and improve diagnostic stratification.

Materials and methods

Study cohort and design

This single-center, prospective study was conducted in the neurosurgical Intensive Care Unit (ICU) at Huashan Hospital from December 2020 to April 2022. A total of 17 colonization cases and 27 infectious pneumonia cases were enrolled.

All enrolled patients were admitted primarily for severe cranial trauma. Endotracheal intubation or tracheostomy was performed to secure the airway and facilitate postoperative ventilatory support, rather than for the treatment of pulmonary infection. Pulmonary infection developed more than 48 h after ICU admission and following airway instrumentation, and was therefore classified as hospital-acquired pneumonia (HAP).

On the day of airway instrumentation, deep airway secretions were collected concurrently with routine sputum culture and chest CT examination. Samples were immediately stored at − 80 °C and subsequently analyzed using metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) and metatranscriptomic sequencing to characterize both microbial community structure and host immune responses. These sequencing-derived features were later incorporated into the diagnostic model.

Baseline characteristics, including age, gender, PCT, CRP, and other clinical indicators, were comparable between the two groups, with no statistically significant differences. As expected, only white blood cell count (WBC) and neutrophil count (N) differed between groups, reflecting infection-related physiological changes. Arterial pH remained stable in both groups and thus did not provide additional diagnostic value (Table S1). As most patients were ventilated throughout the sampling period, ventilation status and routine ICU care measures were largely uniform across participants. Prior antibiotic exposure was recorded and is included in the baseline characteristics (Table S2).

To further evaluate model performance, additional deep sputum samples were prospectively collected from colonization (n = 2) and infectious pneumonia (n = 4) patients between April 2022 and January 2023 using the same inclusion criteria. These samples likewise underwent mNGS and metatranscriptomic sequencing for model validation (Fig. S1). The study protocol was approved by the ethics committees of Huashan Hospital, Shanghai, China. All participants in the parent trial agreed to participate in this study and provided written informed consent (Ky2020-1304). All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations.

DNA/RNA extraction, library construction and sequencing

For metagenomic sequencing, a 1 mL portion of sputum was treated with 1 U of benzonase and 0.5% Tween 20 to eliminate host nucleic acids. The solution was then incubated at 37 °C for 5 min. Subsequently, 600 µL of the solution underwent mechanical disruption via bead beating using ceramic beads in a Minilys Personal Homogenizer. Nucleic acids were extracted from 400 µL of the disrupted sample using a QIAamp UCP Pathogen Mini Kit, and quantified with a Qubit dsDNA HS Assay Kit.

For meta-transcriptomic sequencing, a 1 mL aliquot of sputum was centrifuged to pellet cellular material. The pellet was lysed, and RNA was extracted using a Direct-zol RNA Miniprep kit. Sequencing libraries were prepared from 30 µL of DNA and 10 µL of RNA using the Nextera DNA Flex kit and the Ovation Trio RNA-Seq Library Preparation Kit, respectively. Library concentrations were quantified with a Qubit dsDNA HS Assay Kit. Library integrity and quality were assessed using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer and a High Sensitivity DNA kit. Finally, libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NextSeq 550 platform with a 75-cycle single-end strategy.

Microbial taxonomic profiling and de-contamination

We employed a validated metagenomic protocol for comprehensive analysis of microbial community composition15–19. The workflow commenced with Trimmomatic for removing low-quality reads, duplicates, sequences shorter than 50 base pairs, and adapter contaminants. Human sequences were depleted by alignment to the human reference genome (GRCh38) using HISAT2. Ribosomal RNA was removed with SortMeRNA v4.3.7. Taxonomic profiles were generated using Kraken2 v2.0.7 (Kraken2 taxonomic sequence classification system; https://github.com/DerrickWood/kraken2) and Bracken v2.5 (Bayesian Reestimation of Abundance with KrakEN; https://github.com/jenniferlu717/Bracken), both run under default parameters with a standard reference database (https://benlangmead.github.io/aws-indexes/k2). Samples with insufficient DNA/RNA yield or sequencing reads < 10 million were excluded. Reads assigned to detected taxa were normalized to reads per million (RPM) to account for varying sequencing depths and facilitate subsequent decontamination. As detailed in our prior methodological descriptions15,18,20, all wet laboratory procedures were performed under stringent sterile conditions. Negative controls (phosphate-buffered saline or sterile water) were incorporated during nucleic acid extraction and library preparation to detect potential contamination. Additionally, sterile cotton swabs wetted with sterile deionized water were used to swab the surfaces of the centrifuge and biosafety cabinet, thereby establishing a laboratory background microbial profile. These controls were processed in parallel with clinical samples across the entire experimental pipeline. In the bioinformatics workflow, we applied the prevalence-based mode of the Decontam R package v1.12.0 (Identify Contaminants in Marker-gene and Metagenomics Sequencing Data; https://github.com/benjjneb/decontam) to statistically identify contaminant taxa by contrasting sequence prevalences between samples and negative controls18.

Host gene expression, functional enrichment and a cell-type composition analysis

For host gene expression analysis, high-quality datasets were aligned to the human genome reference hg38 using HISAT2. Subsequently, quantification at the gene level was executed with the FeatureCounts tool, which is a component of the Subread package. This tool facilitated the aggregation of gene counts. Utilizing DESeq2, differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were pinpointed by applying established thresholds for the false discovery rate < 0.05 and a fold change > 1.5. The gene set enrichment analysis of the DEGs was conducted using the KEGG databases, with significant pathways being identified through the application of Fisher’s exact test, followed by adjustment according to the Benjamin-Hochberg method. A compilation of immune-related genes and interferon-stimulated genes was derived from the ImmPort database and corroborated with findings from referenced studies, respectively. The relative abundances of immune cell types were deconvoluted using the CIBERSORT algorithm with the original LM22 gene signature matrix and 1000 permutations21,22.

Host/microbe multi-dimension diagnosis modelling

To develop our predictive model, we focused on capturing both microbial and host signatures. Specifically, we analyzed microbial DNA relative abundance (%) and Transcripts per Million (TPM) of host gene expression (GE) both individually and in combination. We first applied LefSe and DESeq2 to identify features that were differentially abundant (adjusted P < 0.05), including microbial taxa significantly associated with disease categories, forming our initial candidate feature pool. Subsequently, sequential forward selection23 and Random Forest algorithm (via the R package “mlr3”) were applied to identify optimal feature combinations from the candidate pool. We then employed a tenfold cross-validation (CV) strategy to assess model performance and estimate the 95% confidence interval (CI) of the area under the ROC curve (AUC). All analyses were conducted in R version 4.1.0 using RStudio to ensure reproducibility and transparency. The study conforms, where appropriate, to the Transparent Reporting of a multivariable prediction model for Individual Prognosis or Diagnosis + Artificial Intelligence (TRIPOD + AI) guidelines24 (Table S3).

Protein–protein interaction (PPI) network construction

Established protein–protein interaction data were retrieved from the STRING public database (https://string-db.org/). A network model was built with proteins as nodes and interactions as edges, while eliminating isolated nodes. Functional annotations were then provided for the proteins in the network, involving enrichment and pathway analysis to elucidate functional roles and interaction dynamics.

Weighted correlation network analysis (WGCNA)

A co-expression network analysis was conducted, employing a β-value of 14, which facilitated the categorization of genes into branches characterized by elevated levels of expression. The identification of distinct modules within this network was achieved through the application of the tree cut algorithm, supplemented by an additional PAM stage for refinement. Subsequently, two binary variables representative of the states of colonization and infectious pneumonia were formulated. These variables were subsequently utilized to compute the relationships between module traits across a comprehensive set of deep sputum samples. To further discern overall differential gene expression, a between-group Kruskal–Wallis test was meticulously applied to the Module Eigengene (ME) values. Functional annotation of the identified modules was performed using the clusterProfiler R package to gain insights into biological significance and potential roles of these gene clusters.

Statistics and reproducibility

After sequence processing, key attributes of microbial composition (Shannon index, Simpson index, Chao1 index, and ACE index) were calculated using the vegan package in R. Differences in β-diversity, quantified by Bray–Curtis distance, were assessed using permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) with the “vegan” package. Principal coordinates analysis (PCoA) and the envfit function were used to identify species contributing to microbial variation. The Mann–Whitney-Wilcoxon test was used for comparing continuous nonparametric data. For categorical data, either the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test was applied, as appropriate. Correlation coefficients between species and clinical factors, and between microbiota and host genes, were assessed using Spearman’s rank correlation analysis with the Hmisc package. Additionally, a multivariable logistic regression model based on conventional clinical indicators was constructed to provide a benchmark for comparison with the multi-omics diagnostic model. All analyses were performed in RStudio (R version 4.1.0). Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05.

Among the top 15 species ranked by relative abundance, clinically relevant pathogens such as Acinetobacter baumannii and Klebsiella pneumoniae were significantly enriched in the infection group, whereas Cutibacterium acnes, Klebsiella aerogenes and Serratia marcescens—commonly considered commensal or opportunistic organisms—were relatively more abundant in the colonization group (Fig. 1C). LEfSe analysis further identified Acinetobacter nosocomialis, a well-known hospital-acquired pneumonia pathogen associated with high mortality, as markedly enriched in infection samples25,26. Conversely, the colonization group exhibited higher levels of oral or environmental microbes such as Candida albicans and Enterococcus faecalis, which may represent background microbial constituents rather than indicators of infection (Fig. 1D).

To evaluate whether antibiotic exposure confounded microbiome variation, α- and β-diversity analyses were stratified by antibiotic status at sampling. Neither α diversity (Fig. S2A, Kruskal–Wallis, P = 0.34) nor β diversity (Fig. S2B, PERMANOVA, P = 0.146) showed significant differences across antibiotic groups.

Additionally, pathogens in the infection group showed elevated transcriptional activity, reflected by significantly increased expression of bacterial (16S/23S) and fungal (18S/28S) ribosomal subunits (Fig. 1E). This enhancement in microbial protein synthesis is consistent with previous findings linking ribosomal transcriptional activity to pathogenic invasion and pneumonia risk27,28.

Collectively, these observations suggest that the infection state is characterized by a collapse in microbial diversity and enrichment of metabolically active pathogens capable of breaching the respiratory epithelial barrier, whereas the colonization state maintains a more balanced microbial ecosystem14.

Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) demonstrated that several KEGG pathways were enriched in the colonization state, including apoptosis, natural killer cell-mediated cytotoxicity, Wnt signaling, chemokine signaling, focal adhesion and MAPK signaling (Fig. 2B). These pathways regulate essential processes for barrier integrity and coordinated immune defense. Pathways with higher gene enrichment, particularly Wnt, MAPK, focal adhesion and chemokine signaling, were considered more reliable and thus selected for focused interpretation (Fig. 2C).

Protein–protein interaction (PPI) network analysis further revealed a regulatory hub involving WNT7A, WNT8B and PORCN, highlighting the central role of Wnt-related signaling in the colonization group (Fig. 2D). Consistent with this finding, Wnt pathway gene expression was markedly higher in colonization samples (Fig. 2E), suggesting enhanced epithelial repair programs that may help contain microbial invasion and prevent progression to clinically apparent infection.

No significant differences in estimated immune-cell proportions were detected between groups, suggesting limited confounding from immune-cell composition (Fig. S3). Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) identified 46 gene modules among the top 5000 most variable genes (Fig. 3A). Although the correlation between the ivory module and disease status was moderate (r = 0.32), eigengene expression within this module differed significantly between colonization and infection groups. PPI analysis indicated that proteins encoded by these genes form a tightly connected network centered around CTNNB1, PIK3R1 and ACTB (Fig. 3B). These core regulators govern epithelial cell adhesion, cytoskeletal organization and intracellular signaling, processes that collectively contribute to epithelial barrier stability and tissue repair.

Integrating these observations with prior analyses suggests that coordinated activation of Wnt, focal adhesion and PI3K signaling in the colonization group may help maintain pulmonary epithelial integrity and restrict pathogen invasion, thereby limiting progression from colonization to active infection29–31.

Molecular classification prediction model for colonization and infectious pneumonia

A baseline clinical model incorporating white blood cell count, neutrophil count and arterial pH achieved an AUC of 0.756 (Fig. S4A), indicating only moderate ability to differentiate colonization from infectious pneumonia.

To enhance diagnostic accuracy, we integrated microbial signatures from metagenomic sequencing with host inflammatory transcriptomic features to construct a multi-omics prediction model using a Random Forest algorithm. The final model comprised ANKRD52, ZC3HAV1L, SERPINE3, CDPF1, ZNF720, TAGLN3 and LRRC15 (Fig. 4A). It achieved an AUC of 0.951 (95% CI: 0.934–0.968) in the training cohort (Fig. 4B). Discussion

Pneumonia remains a leading cause of global mortality among infectious diseases, and diagnostic challenges continue to hinder effective clinical management. A major limitation of current practice lies in distinguishing respiratory colonization from infectious pneumonia, which often leads to diagnostic uncertainty and inappropriate antimicrobial exposure. In this study, we integrated metagenomic and transcriptomic sequencing of deep sputum to investigate microbial community structures and host immune regulation across colonization and infection states, with the aim of developing a diagnostic model capable of improving clinical decision-making.

Our mNGS analysis demonstrated clear compositional differences between colonization and infection states, with higher microbial diversity observed in the colonization group, consistent with the concept of colonization resistance32. A more balanced microbial ecosystem may restrict pathogenic expansion and reduce the likelihood of invasion33. In contrast, the infection group exhibited increased transcriptional activity of pathogenic species, reflected by enhanced ribosomal gene expression, suggesting elevated microbial metabolic activity27,28 that could facilitate epithelial barrier disruption and increase pneumonia risk. These findings provide additional insight into the biological relevance of microbial activity in respiratory infection beyond organism presence alone.

No significant differences in estimated immune-cell proportions were detected between groups, but this result should be interpreted cautiously because cell-type deconvolution from bulk respiratory transcriptomes provides relative, model-dependent estimates and may be insensitive to subtle compositional shifts in heterogeneous specimens34–36. In addition, clinical management around sampling, including antibiotic exposure and potential anti-inflammatory therapy such as systemic corticosteroids, may attenuate detectable differences in inferred composition37. Importantly, a lack of proportion differences does not imply comparable host immune states, and disease-relevant divergence may be more apparent at the pathway and gene-program level, as reflected by our downstream analyses34,36.

In the GSEA analysis, upregulation of Wnt, MAPK, focal adhesion, and chemokine signaling pathways in the colonization group is clinically meaningful, as these pathways support epithelial barrier integrity and coordinated immune regulation38. The Wnt pathway enhances pulmonary epithelial structure and promotes tissue regeneration38. MAPK signaling regulates cell proliferation and migration, facilitating tissue repair while modulating inflammatory responses to avoid excessive damage39. Focal adhesion signaling strengthens cell adhesion and cytoskeletal coupling, contributing to epithelial stability and wound healing40. Chemokine signaling orchestrates immune cell recruitment and inflammatory control, thereby limiting tissue injury41. Taken together, these pathways may reinforce epithelial defense and help prevent progression from colonization toward active infection.

In the WGCNA analysis, the ivory module showed a significant difference in eigengene expression between colonization and infection groups. Within this module, CTNNB1, PIK3R1 and ACTB were more highly expressed in the colonization group and mapped to Wnt, focal adhesion and PI3K signaling pathways, consistent with the results of the GSEA analysis. PPI network analysis further demonstrated that these genes form an interaction hub centered on CTNNB1, PIK3R1 and ACTB, supporting coordinated regulation of epithelial adhesion, cytoskeletal organization and intracellular signaling42–47. These pathways together maintain epithelial stability and promote repair processes, which may be important for restricting pathogen invasion and preventing the progression from colonization to active infection48.

Upregulation of Wnt and other signaling pathways within the ivory module suggests a complex host–microbe interplay in the colonization state. These pathways are essential for maintaining epithelial integrity and modulating immune responses48, which may be pivotal in preventing the transition from colonization to infection. By supporting a more controlled and efficient defense against potential pathogens, this coordinated regulation helps preserve the delicate balance between microbial presence and the onset of infectious pneumonia.

We developed a diagnostic model that achieved an AUC of 0.951 (95% CI: 0.934–0.968) in the training set and 0.875 in the validation set for distinguishing colonization from infectious pneumonia. Compared with previously reported diagnostic models for lower respiratory tract infections, our model not only demonstrates high accuracy but also incorporates biologically relevant host response features. For instance, LRRC15, which has been associated with pneumonia and is known to inhibit pathogen entry into cells, was included as a model feature49. ANKRD52, a regulatory component of protein phosphatase 6 (PP6), contributes to T cell recruitment by modulating AGO2 dephosphorylation, and its dysfunction can impair JAK–STAT interferon-γ signaling and antigen presentation50. ZC3HAV1L, associated with the antiviral protein ZC3HAV1, suggests a potential role in the antiviral immune response51.

Although the clinical baseline model demonstrated only moderate discriminatory ability, its strengths lie in simplicity, rapid availability and bedside applicability, making it practical in urgent clinical settings. By contrast, the multi-omics model provides substantially higher diagnostic accuracy, but its reliance on sequencing-based data requires additional resources and turnaround time. While this approach offers clear incremental diagnostic value, validation in larger cohorts and further workflow optimization will be needed before translation into routine clinical practice is feasible.

While our study provides valuable insights, it has several limitations. The sample size was relatively small, and antibiotic exposure in some patients at the time of sampling may have influenced microbial profiles. However, detailed timing and duration of antibiotic use prior to sampling were not consistently retrievable in this prospective cohort, and antibiotic-related effects cannot be fully excluded. In addition, the sequencing depth of microbial transcriptomic data was insufficient for detailed analysis of antimicrobial resistance genes or construction of a microbiota-only diagnostic model. Larger cohorts and deeper sequencing will be required to further validate and refine this approach.

Conclusion

In summary, this study demonstrates that integrating metagenomic and transcriptomic features from deep sputum can distinguish respiratory colonization from infectious pneumonia and reveal key host–microbe interactions involved in disease progression. These findings provide a foundation for refining diagnostic strategies and support further validation of multi-omics approaches in clinical practice.
