ABSTRACT
Occupational radiation exposure has a clear association with long-term health effects and has a particular impact on women, trainees, and supporting staff. There is a reliance on limited, traditional radiation protection and nonergonomic equipment, which carries associated orthopedic injury rates with adverse impacts on both individual career trajectories and health systems, and with significant challenges to widespread implementation of enhanced radiation protection devices. During the 2025 Society for Cardiovascular Angiography & Interventions Scientific Sessions in Washington, DC, a multidisciplinary Think Tank summit was convened and included representatives from multiple stakeholders, including other professional societies, advocacy groups, and industry partners. The focus of discussion was on the dual dangers of both ionizing radiation and the musculoskeletal injuries associated with current-generation protection equipment. This document summarizes the main points of discussion and outlines actionable next steps to advocate for more widespread implementation of enhanced radiation protection technologies and imaging systems in order to achieve ALARA+ (as low and as light as reasonably achievable).

Introduction

Each year at the Society for Cardiovascular Angiography & Interventions (SCAI) Annual Scientific Sessions meeting, collaborative “Think Tank” conferences are convened for each SCAI clinical practice area. These meetings bring together interventional cardiologists, administrative partners, and industry stakeholders to discuss topics of particular interest to the field. This year’s Think Tank was focused on radiation and orthopedic safety in settings of fluoroscopically guided interventions (Central Illustration). Attendees included a broad multidisciplinary group: Cardiac catheterization laboratory staff, hospital supply chain administrators, industry representatives, radiation physicists, physicians, and representatives from professional societies including SCAI, the Alliance of Cardiovascular Professionals (ACVP), American College of Cardiology (ACC), American Society of Echocardiography (ASE), Heart Rhythm Society (HRS), Occupational Radiation Safety in Interventional Fluoroscopy (ORSIF), Society of NeuroInterventional Surgery (SNIS), Society of Interventional Radiology (SIR), Society for Pediatric Radiology (SPR), Society for Vascular Surgery (SVS), and Society of Vascular and Interventional Neurology (SVIN), who also co-planned the meeting (Supplemental Table S1).Central IllustrationRecommendations for improving occupational safetyin fluoroscopically guided settings.

The objectives of the Think Tank were to: (1) identify the specific occupational health hazards to all personnel working in a fluoroscopic laboratory; (2) describe how laboratory safety affects talent acquisition and retention, with particular attention to women and trainees; (3) discuss the financial challenges and administrative responsibilities associated with broader implementation of enhanced radiation protection devices (ERPDs); and (4) spearhead a “call to action” with specific, measurable, and actionable criteria to foster a safer and sustainable working environment.

Health hazards in settings of fluoroscopically guided interventions

The use of fluoroscopically guided interventions spans multiple medical specialties, enabling minimally invasive procedures. However, it poses radiation risks to both patients and clinicians, and an additional risk of orthopedic injury related to the wearable personal protective gear used to attenuate radiation exposure.1 Substantial progress has been made over the last decade in reducing dose from the source, namely, the x-ray system. At least one imaging vendor has released advanced fluoroscopic technologies proven to simultaneously reduce patient radiation exposure by at least 65% without loss of image quality in both single and biplane fluoroscopy systems used for cardiac and some cerebrovascular interventions.2, 3, 4 However, many practices use biplane fluoroscopy systems from other vendors, which may have different dose reduction capabilities. Studies show a clear association between occupational radiation exposure and increased risk of cataracts,5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11 with findings on its link to atherosclerosis,12 brain and neck tumors,13,14 and breast cancer15 varying across studies. DNA damage and its associated development of chromosome aberrations secondary to ionizing radiation have been linked to increased cancer susceptibility.11 Increased frequency of minimally invasive procedures, longer procedural duration, extreme gantry angles, and need for alternative vascular access approaches increase operator exposure during fluoroscopy-guided interventions. Respondents to a 2023 SCAI survey (N = 298 interventional cardiologists) reported a 6.1% lifetime cancer diagnosis, as compared to a 4.5% reported incidence in a 2014 survey, with 11% and 15% reporting knowledge of a colleague with a brain tumor and other type of cancer, respectively.1 Although it is difficult to compare the results of a small study to national cancer statistics, the study highlights the specific radiation concerns among interventional operators and staff. Importantly, there was wide variation in the type of radiation protection utilized among survey respondents.

Traditionally, clinicians have relied on lead or lead-equivalent radiation protection garments (RPG) that cover the trunk, torso, and thyroid. Over time, additional barrier methods have been developed, including lead caps to protect against brain exposure, lead goggles, ceiling-mounted shields to block scatter, and lower body protection in the form of a table-mounted lead skirt underneath the patient table. Unfortunately, studies continue to show that these systems leave clinicians at risk for substantial cumulative radiation exposure to all uncovered body parts, along with incomplete protection to the covered areas.16 Additionally, although lead-equivalent aprons have historically been the mainstay for radiation protection in the fluoroscopic laboratory, they are associated with high rates of long-term orthopedic injury among operators and staff.1 Furthermore, improperly sized RPG contribute to an additional risk of orthopedic injury and leave the potential for increased radiation exposure through unprotected areas. There have been promising prospective safety and efficacy data performing robotic percutaneous coronary intervention via a radiation-shielded interventional workstation,17,18 with significantly decreased operator radiation dose and orthopedic risk exposure.17 Widespread use of this technology remains limited by workflow and device compatibility.17 The aforementioned 2023 SCAI survey demonstrated high rates of orthopedic injuries among interventional cardiologists. Among respondents, 66% described musculoskeletal pain attributable to wearing RPG or working in the catheterization laboratory, and 79% reported similar injuries in colleagues or staff. Rates were overall similar to prior surveys of interventional cardiologists and interventional radiologists19, 20, 21, 22 and highlighted minimal, if any, reduction in rates of orthopedic injury among clinicians.

This year’s SCAI Think Tank highlighted numerous personal stories from experienced operators, including instances of lumbar and cervical radiculopathy and transient quadriplegia requiring urgent surgery. There was extensive discussion about the mechanics of body position and best practices to mitigate the risk of injury. Prolonged standing, often leaning forward or craning the neck to view monitors, while encumbered by heavy RPG, places immense strain on the musculoskeletal system, particularly the spine (cervical and lumbar), shoulders, and hips. Addressing these detrimental mechanics requires a multifaceted approach to best practices, though many of these remain aspirational without systemic changes to the work environment. Within the classic fluoroscopic laboratory framework, a foundational element is ergonomic equipment placement. Monitors should be adjustable and positioned to allow for a neutral head and neck posture. The procedural table height must be optimized to enable operators to maintain a neutral spine and avoid excessive leaning; however, achieving an ideal height can be challenging when team members of varying statures are involved. Conscious efforts toward maintaining a neutral posture whenever possible are encouraged, though this is often difficult to sustain throughout long and demanding cases. Ensuring properly fitted lead-equivalent aprons that distribute weight as evenly as possible, potentially utilizing newer harness designs that shift the load to the torso and hips, can offer some relief. Incorporating microbreaks and positional changes during lengthy procedures can help to alleviate sustained muscle tension, although the dynamic and often critical nature of interventions limits such opportunities. Although core strengthening and regular conditioning exercises are beneficial for overall musculoskeletal health and resilience and may offer an important adjunctive therapy to minimizing injury among operators and staff, they cannot solely counteract the effects of a fundamentally nonergonomic work environment and may work better as a complementary approach.23

The more recent implementation of ERPDs, or devices and systems, whether standalone or in combination with standard shielding, which offer equal or superior clinician protection without the musculoskeletal strain associated with legacy solutions, represents a promising step forward in nonwearable radiation protection.24,25 Studies of these advanced shielding systems have shown reduced radiation exposure at the head,26,27 thyroid,27, 28, 29 and waist26,28,29 levels, as well as total body radiation exposure.27,30 These systems include mobile pedestal platforms and ceiling-mounted options, depending on the manufacturer. Although the results show superior protection to operators and many staff compared to traditional methods, a clear barrier to implementation is the cost of adding these systems to existing fluoroscopic platforms. There can be a substantial capital expenditure to update current fluoroscopic systems with ERPDs, which can be challenging for some health systems in the absence of institutional or philanthropic support. Additionally, compatibility limitations with biplane fluoroscopy systems can pose challenges. In particular, mobile pedestal platforms may obstruct the movement or positioning of the biplane C-arm, especially during complex neurointerventions and congenital procedures. Clinicians may need to work with manufacturers to develop customized solutions that address unique procedural requirements. Nevertheless, the importance of creating a safe working environment not only for operators but for the rest of the staff cannot be overstated.

Procedural operators are not the only clinicians exposed to radiation or the pitfalls of radiation protection equipment. Fluoroscopy-based interventional fields have increasingly relied upon critical personnel, such as anesthesiologists and neuromonitoring service providers for procedural support, and transthoracic and transesophageal echocardiographers for real-time intraprocedural guidance and evaluation. In this model, the anesthesiologist, neurophysiology monitoring personnel, and imager are placed at the greatest proximity, and thus, the risk of radiation exposure is often similar to or more than the most proximal procedural operator.30, 31, 32 Although specific dosimetric data for neuromonitoring personnel remain scarce, through analogous measurements of personnel in neurointerventional settings,33 one can infer that neuromonitoring staff, often located near the C-arm, likely sustain similar scatter radiation doses. This exposure can be exaggerated with varying gantry angulations. Additional traditional ceiling-mounted shielding can reduce radiation exposure to the imager,31 but certainly has room for optimization with more advanced systems. Some newer, advanced radiation shielding systems are designed to optimize protection of operators and surrounding staff, but either fail to provide protection to the imager or anesthesiologist, who is often at the head of the table, or create additional barriers to proper ergonomics for manipulation of a transesophageal echocardiogram probe. Certain protection systems have demonstrated circumferential protection for all team members in the catheterization laboratory—including cardiac imagers and anesthesiologists at the head of the bed30—in preclinical studies. Although these systems do not appear to share some of the limitations observed with other technologies, this finding will need to be replicated in clinical studies. For procedures such as pacemakers that require close access to the patient, proper placement of supplemental radiation protection pads can reduce scatter radiation exposure by 50%.34 Additionally, real-time dosimetry reporting could accelerate a change in adjusting practice patterns, as the most common current dosimetry is tracked monthly and often reported even less often by individual institutions to their clinicians. Real-time dosimetry reporting has become an important additional mode of identifying immediate areas for improvement and should be included in efforts to introduce novel radiation protection systems.

Women professionals’ perspective

Although many of the occupational risks of working in a fluoroscopic laboratory are borne by all operators and staff, there is a unique and amplified set of challenges for women. Beyond the shared risks of radiation and orthopedic injury, women professionals have distinct concerns related to reproductive health, career progression, and navigating a demanding medical culture. The summit's discussions brought these nuanced issues to the forefront, emphasizing that creating an equitable and sustainable workforce requires a targeted understanding and mitigation of these gender-specific considerations.

The perceived and actual risks of radiation exposure, orthopedic injury, and the associated impact on career trajectory act as a substantial deterrent for individuals considering careers in specialties involving fluoroscopically guided interventions. This is particularly acute for women, for whom concerns about radiation exposure during childbearing years are paramount. The lack of tailored protective equipment and supportive institutional policies further discourages participation from a woman’s perspective. Although the radiation risks to the developing fetus vary by the stage of pregnancy and have been reported as nominal with appropriate protective measures, there remains hesitation to work in a radiation field with any additional exposure to ionizing radiation,35 and the orthopedic risks with additional aprons remain.36 Although there is no “safe” radiation dose, the United States’ legal dose limit for the entire gestation is 5 mSv; this dose limit is 1 mSv in the European Union, Australia, and Israel.37 Importantly, radiation exposure effects are largely derived from studies of survivors from major radiation exposure events, animal studies, and outcomes following radiation treatment in women.35,38 With appropriate shielding, the mean underlead dose of radiation encountered by interventional cardiologists has been reported at 1.02 ± 0.73 mSv/y, while underlead waist and thyroid dosimeters in pregnant interventionalists have shown an average exposure of 0 to 0.1 mSv/mo.38

These reproductive health concerns have a tangible impact on career decisions and progression. Although women now represent roughly half of all medical school graduates, their numbers remain disproportionately low in interventional cardiology, cardiac electrophysiology, and neurointerventional subspecialties, partly due to these concerns.39, 40, 41 The fear of radiation and the physical demands are noteworthy deterrents, contributing to the underrepresentation of women in specialties like interventional cardiology and electrophysiology. This training pathway results in a substantial loss of talent in fields in which women are already burdened by delays in starting families due to the lengthy training pathways and the perceived incompatibility of early career demands with pregnancy and child-rearing.42 In critical fields such as advanced cardiac imaging, there is new attention to the higher doses of radiation sustained by a transesophageal echocardiographer as compared with primary operators,32 with a particular focus on its effects on young women who subspecialize in echocardiography.22

Hospital practice can do more to support women of childbearing age, beyond the well-established increased personal shielding and radiation dosimetry monitoring provided to women once they are pregnant. Although 70% of women surveyed by SCAI expressed a desire for the option to step away from high-radiation environments during pregnancy, this option is not consistently feasible or supported, forcing difficult trade-offs between career and personal well-being. A concerning cultural aspect revealed in the summit was the reluctance of some women to declare their pregnancy early. This hesitation, potentially stemming from fears of negative career repercussions or being perceived as less committed, can delay the implementation of crucial additional protective measures, posing a safety risk for both the mother and fetus.

As such, there was a renewed call for consideration of widespread adoption of advanced radiation protection technologies that reduce or eliminate the need for wearable RPG, the development and implementation of clear and flexible work policies that support women through pregnancy and early parenthood, and improved and targeted radiation safety education. Ongoing education regarding the proven strategies to create minimal fetal radiation exposure is vital to fostering career paths within fluoroscopic laboratories.43 Notably, some institutions have successfully implemented policies such as occupational radiation risk counseling, individualized dosimetry monitoring during pregnancy, and flexible procedural scheduling. These strategies have been shown to support retention and career development among pregnant interventionalists and early parents.35

Supporting staff

The staff in the fluoroscopic laboratory shares many of the same burdens with respect to workplace hazards as the primary operators. They wear heavy RPG for protracted periods and perform a multitude of tasks that can involve awkward postures, patient handling, and repetitive movements, all contributing to a high risk of orthopedic injury. Although everyone in the fluoroscopic laboratory is at an increased risk of musculoskeletal injury, it has been reported that musculoskeletal pain is highest among nonphysician employees.44 Participants in the Think Tank heard stories that many skilled staff members have been forced to leave the profession due to an inability to continue wearing lead, representing a substantial loss to the workforce. Although some institutions provide robust radiation safety training, this is not universal, and awareness of risks and best practices can differ among staff members. However, the prevalent issue of workforce shortages, with vacancy rates for nurses and technologists reaching 40% to 60% in some areas,29 places immense pressure on the remaining personnel, potentially compromising their ability to consistently prioritize all safety measures in high-stress situations. Empowering staff to confidently voice safety concerns and to insist on optimal protective setups before and during procedures, without fear of reprisal, is essential for a truly safe working environment.

Trainees

Trainees also face unique challenges in fluoroscopy-guided settings, and data suggest that they may receive higher radiation exposure when adjusting for procedural complexity.45 During their training, fellows often accumulate higher cumulative radiation doses than experienced attendings due to longer procedure times, being in the first operator position and therefore closer to the radiation source, increased fluoroscopy usage, and more frequent image acquisitions as they master complex techniques. As their primary focus is skill acquisition and patient care, radiation reduction techniques may initially seem secondary. Historically, the training environment has traditionally discouraged trainees from raising concerns about radiation exposure or requesting procedural adjustments for safety, fearing it might be perceived negatively or slow down the workflow. Some Think Tank speakers alluded to past practices where fellows might have inconsistently worn dosimeters if high readings were anticipated, due to fear of repercussions. This underscores the need for a supportive and educational training environment where safety is paramount. Participants also noted that formal education on radiation physics, advanced protection strategies, and long-term biological effects was often lacking in training curricula or was not uniform across fellowship programs. Because this education is paramount to patient and clinician safety, employers should provide structured radiation and orthopedic safety training, which should be completed by all personnel who will be operating fluoroscopic equipment.

Nevertheless, given the increased accountability faced by program directors, trainees can be an effective catalyst for change. There is an emerging trend of fellow unionization, which provides a new avenue for trainees to advocate for safer working conditions. The prospect of Accreditation Council for Graduate Medical Education site visits and the need to demonstrate robust safety policies and practices may further encourage institutions to prioritize trainee well-being.

Financial considerations for implementation of ERPDs: Mitigating shortages of trained personnel

The financial and logistical barriers to retrofitting existing laboratories may be substantial, and potentially daunting for outpatient and office-based laboratories—advanced radiation protection systems currently cost approximately $150,000 to $200,000. However, ORSIF has estimated the cost associated with exposure to interventional fluoroscopy to be $9 million per case of fatal cancer, $200,000 per case of nonfatal cancer, and $12,000 to $45,000 per case of musculoskeletal disorder.46 This valuation does not include the impactful costs associated with the reduction in the number of cases performed, potential litigation, competitive position of hospitals, and the cost of physician replacement, which has been estimated at $500,000 to $1 million for nonspecialty physicians and likely to be higher for interventional physicians.47

Additionally, occupational hazards substantially complicate recruitment and exacerbate existing workforce shortages. The Bureau of Labor Statistics conservatively projects an approximately combined 47,000 open cardiac catheterization laboratory jobs (Cath lab RN, technologists, echosonographers, etc.) annually, beginning in 2020,48 a shortage partly fueled by the demanding and strenuous nature of the work environment. This staffing crisis impacts the ability to maintain fully operational laboratories and can lead to increased costs to health systems from filling positions with temporary employees.49,50 The loss of experienced personnel due to these occupational hazards has far-reaching implications. When seasoned clinicians leave prematurely, their accumulated knowledge, refined technical skills, and mentorship capacity are lost to their institutions and the broader medical community. This can directly impact the quality of patient care, the ability to perform complex procedures, and the training of the next generation of interventionists. The continuity of care can be disrupted, and the institutional memory for managing challenging cases diminishes.

Ultimately, these workforce challenges translate into impacts on patient access and the quality of care. A recent report from the Association of American Medical Colleges projected that physician demand is projected to continue to grow faster than supply over the next decade.51 Shortages of specialized clinicians can lead to longer wait times for procedures, reduced access to care in certain geographic areas, and potentially compromised patient outcomes if procedures are performed by less experienced teams or in environments with high staff turnover and burnout.51 The well-being of the health care provider is intrinsically linked to the quality and safety of patient care; a workforce beleaguered by injury and burnout cannot optimally serve its patients.

Call to action

Effects of radiation exposure and the harmful physical sequelae of classic radiation protection mechanisms can no longer be the “collateral damage” of the fluoroscopic laboratory (Figure 1). Dr James B. Hermiller provided a “call to action” termed “Operation Moral Imperative” during his 2024 SCAI Presidential Address, at which time he called for advanced radiation protection technology to be a mainstay of all new catheterization laboratories and “retro-fitted” within all existing cardiac catheterization laboratories. Failing to implement these systems currently results in a system that does not capitalize on all available technology to protect operators and staff. The biggest barriers to ERPDs are determining which clinical applications are well-suited to the commercially available ERPDs, and more broadly, operator education and acceptance of ERPD. For many operators, ERPDs clearly represent an opportunity to reduce their personal exposure and, at the same time, nearly eliminate the potential orthopedic injuries of personal radiation protection. For these early adaptors, it is clear that the cost of these systems, and the administrative and regulatory language that mandates that operators “wear” RPG while in the fluoroscopic environment, are the most significant immediate barriers to ERPD adoption. Overcoming inertia and risk-aversion in regulatory agencies requires evidence and education. Most recently, Image Diagnostics, Inc reviewed its clinical data with the Michigan Occupational Safety and Health Administration and successfully obtained a finding of equivalent protection compared with RPG, allowing providers to “shed the lead” while using the Protego device.52 However, this approval relates to single-plane fluoroscopic systems and does not address compatibility with biplane platforms commonly used in congenital cardiovascular and neurointerventional procedures, where workflow and equipment may present additional challenges. Applying uniform retrofit standards across all practice settings may not be feasible until technology innovations address the diversity of procedure environments.

The responsibility and contributions of the health care institutions and fluoroscopic platform manufacturers are imperative to this initiative. When federal legislation required the installation of driver and front passenger airbags in passenger cars in the 1990s, it did not put the responsibility on future consumers to find a way to add airbags to vehicles, but rather placed that responsibility on the automobile manufacturers. Over an approximately 30-year period, more than 50,000 estimated lives were saved by front airbags.53 This is in contrast to the clinician within the fluoroscopic laboratory currently being tasked to acquire their “air bag” (ERPD). Following the Needlestick Safety and Prevention Act being signed into law to mandate health care facilities to implement measures to prevent needlestick injuries, there was a substantial and sustained drop in injury rates in hospital workers, with an estimated yearly cost savings of $69 million to $415 million related to injury follow-up.54 State and federal legislation, as well as regulations from federal and state government agencies such as the Occupational Safety and Health Administration and the Department of Health, will be needed for long-term change with respect to radiation exposure and orthopedic injuries. Although there are no Occupational Safety and Health Administration–specific limitations on the weight and duration of weight-bearing, Section 5(a)(1) of the Occupational Safety and Health Act of 1970, known as the General Duty Clause, requires employers to provide employees with a workplace that is free from recognized hazards that could cause death or serious physical harm. This is a standard to which health care institutions are accountable.

In the absence of clear regulation, various legal maneuvers, including class action, mass tort, and medical monitoring requirements, were considered, not because they are ideal, but because they reflect the growing desperation in environments where institutional accountability is missing. Much of this regulation occurs at the state level, where the specific rules and mechanisms for updating the regulations are variable, and this heterogeneity may impede efforts for uniform protection.55 Lobbying the relevant federal regulatory authorities to institute a quality measure for limiting the radiation output of fluoroscopy machines, similar to that governing computed tomography scanners, would potentially accelerate the retirement of legacy fluoroscopic systems. State-level advocacy may be preferred here, however, given the uncertainty of the federal policymaking process.

Institutional support is needed to fund and prioritize these measures to maintain the health and well-being of their clinicians. Reimbursement of institutional costs of implementing ERPD shall be considered as a formal regulation, and legislative advocacy is pursued. Hospitals that do not support these measures may find that clinicians use job opportunities at institutions that are willing to make such an investment. The manufacturers of fluoroscopic platforms must invest in incorporating ergonomic solutions that mitigate injury and more extensive, custom radiation protection technology into their fluoroscopic laboratory framework so that sustainable and effective protection is not left up to individual institutions and clinicians. Manufacturers of advanced radiation protection technologies must continue to innovate their products and reduce product costs to overcome logistical barriers and to match the versatility that exists within various fluoroscopic laboratories, including biplane fluoroscopic platforms, those of congenital and structural cardiac interventions, peripheral, vascular, and neurointerventions, as well as different members of the procedural team. Real-time dosimetry monitoring should be utilized to quickly identify modifiable practice patterns. Although catheterization laboratory accreditation exists in the form of the Intersocietal Accreditation Commission, there is currently no accreditation of equipment within the fluoroscopic space. The future of quality assurance in the fluoroscopic laboratory includes the standardization of this equipment.

As executive and legislative branch advocacy continues, the societies represented in this summit also present a unique collection of expertise to provide resources on proper and effective implementation and workflow for current enhanced radiation protection technologies. It is critical that multisocietal guidelines for mechanisms of radiation protection and prevention of orthopedic injuries be published in order to establish consistent radiation and ergonomic standards across all subspecialties that work within fluoroscopic laboratories. In order to achieve the next generation of “ALARA” (as low as reasonably achievable), we must advocate for the widespread implementation of enhanced radiation protection technologies and imaging systems. ALARA+ (as low and as light as reasonably achievable) expands traditional radiation safety by also addressing the substantial orthopedic risks that health care professionals routinely face in interventional environments.
