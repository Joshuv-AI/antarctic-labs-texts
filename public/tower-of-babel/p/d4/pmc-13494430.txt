Demographic and Physiological Differences Between Fibrotic and Nonfibrotic CT Subtypes of Sarcoidosis
Authors: Ruchi Yadav, Wren L Lippitt, Kum Ju Chae, Jared Rieck, Nichole E Carlson, Shu-Yi Liao, Margaret M Mroz, Briana Barkes, Stephen M Humphries, Andrea Oh, Hye Jeon Hwang, Katherine Parker, Andrea I Fuentealba, Thomas McLaren, Cristina Marrocchio, Tasha E Fingerlin, Daniel A Culver, David A Lynch, Lisa A Maier
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOne approach to categorizing patients using chest CT imaging manifestations of pulmonary sarcoidosis is to use 3 classifications: fibrotic, nonfibrotic, or no parenchymal abnormality. This study aimed to evaluate the relationships between these imaging subtypes and demographic and physiological variables in a large group of patients with sarcoidosis with chest CT imaging.Research QuestionDo individuals with fibrotic sarcoidosis have more severe physiological impairment than those with nonfibrotic sarcoidosis and those with no parenchymal abnormality, and are there CT subtypes of fibrosis that are associated with different physiological subtypes?Study Design and MethodsThe study included individuals with sarcoidosis from 2 large medical centers who were seen between 2008 and 2018 and from the Genomic Research in Alpha-1 Antitrypsin Deficiency and Sarcoidosis (GRADS) consortium and who received high-resolution CT imaging and spirometry and lung diffusing capacity within 180 days (N = 932). Pulmonary function phenotypes were characterized based on spirometry findings and lung diffusing capacity. CT patterns were systematically classified as fibrotic, nonfibrotic, or no parenchymal abnormality based on standardized visual assessment. Multinomial logistic regression analyzed the relationship between pulmonary function and CT categories.ResultsAmong the 942 patients, 353 (38%) had fibrosis, 380 (41%) had nonfibrotic abnormalities, and 199 (21%) had no parenchymal abnormality. Individuals with fibrotic abnormalities were older and had a longer time since diagnosis, more impaired spirometry and diffusing capacity, and larger pulmonary artery diameter than those without (all, P < .001). Among participants with fibrosis, those with a conglomerate mass were 9 times more likely to have a mixed pattern of spirometric impairment (P < .01).InterpretationOur results show that individuals with fibrotic pulmonary sarcoidosis display distinct demographic and spirometric differences. Subtypes of fibrosis exhibit different lung function abnormalities.Key Words: fibrotic phenotypes, high-resolution CT findings, pulmonary sarcoidosis

Take-Home Points

Research Question: As there is the notion that chest radiography is not associated with pulmonary status in sarcoidosis, do individuals with fibrotic sarcoidosis have more lung function impairment and/or are types of fibrotic disease associated with different lung function abnormalities?

Results: Using a large real world data set, we found that about one-fifth of sarcoidosis cases had no parenchymal abnormality, and 41% nonfibrotic findings, and 38% had fibrosis, with the fibrotic cases having longer time since diagnosis, increased pulmonary artery size, worse spirometry and Dlco, as well as different lung function patterns, with conglomerate mass having a mixed pattern and those with mid to upper lobe fibrosis having a reduced risk of restriction.

Interpretation: Defining fibrotic CT features in sarcoidosis may have clinical implications as fibrosis is seen in patients with longer time since diagnosis and different patterns of lung function are apparent based on fibrotic subtype and location; this may have implications for treatment and treatment trials for these patients.

Sarcoidosis is a systemic granulomatous disease that can affect various organs, including the eyes, skin, liver, heart, and central nervous system. In > 90% of sarcoidosis cases, the lungs and thoracic lymph nodes are primarily affected.1 Traditionally, pulmonary sarcoidosis has been considered a restrictive lung disease. However, it is now understood that as many as 30% to 40% of patients with sarcoidosis exhibit normal lung function.2,3 Furthermore, aside from the restrictive pulmonary function phenotype, patients with sarcoidosis can display obstructive, mixed obstructive and restrictive, and isolated diffusion defect phenotypes.2, 3, 4, 5 Differentiating between these pulmonary function phenotypes in patients is crucial, as prognosis and potential interventions may vary accordingly. Notably, the presence of both obstructive and restrictive pulmonary phenotypes has been associated with increased mortality rates,2 and more pronounced impairment in pulmonary function serves as an indicator of more severe disease.6,7

Sharp et al3 reported that the restrictive phenotype was found in 25% of patients in a single-center cohort, and impairments in pulmonary function varied according to race, sex, disease duration, and tobacco use. However, one limitation of the study is that they did not assess the association between phenotype and CT scan appearance. Staging of the severity of sarcoidosis is commonly based on the radiographic Scadding staging system. However, this system is limited by substantial observer variation.8 In addition, although the severity of restrictive and obstructive physiological impairment increases with Scadding stage, physiological impairment is found in > 50% of those with Stage 1 disease and almost 40% of those with Stage 2 disease.9

With sarcoidosis, unlike most other interstitial lung diseases in which chest radiographs are no longer used, CT pattern and extent of abnormality provide important prognostic information. The extent of abnormalities seen on CT scans, even when the correlations are weak, are known to be associated with functional impairment.10 In addition, CT features such as reticulation and air trapping have been found to correlate with indices of airflow obstruction,11, 12, 13, 14 which has been predictive of higher mortality.15 In other parenchymal lung diseases such as hypersensitivity pneumonitis, the distinction between fibrotic and nonfibrotic subtypes has been regarded as pivotal for prognostication and management,16, 17, 18 and antifibrotic treatment is increasingly used for the fibrotic subtype.19,20 In sarcoidosis, however, existing management recommendations do not clearly differentiate between individuals with fibrotic and nonfibrotic subtypes.21 Subtyping sarcoidosis based on CT evidence of fibrosis may also be helpful in improving understanding of pathogenesis.

The aim of the current study was to examine the relationship between CT features, including fibrotic and nonfibrotic abnormalities, and their association with demographic and physiological variables. We hypothesized that individuals with fibrotic sarcoidosis would have more severe physiological impairment than those with nonfibrotic sarcoidosis and those with no parenchymal abnormality and secondarily that specific CT subtypes of fibrosis such as conglomerate masses were more likely to be associated with different physiological subtypes.

Study Design and Methods

Patient Selection

This retrospective study was reviewed by the institutional review boards at National Jewish Health and Biomedical Research Alliance of New York, and informed consent was waived (HS 3211). Patients with a sarcoidosis visit between 2008 and 2018 were included from National Jewish Health and the Cleveland Clinic along with data from the Genomic Research in Alpha-1 Antitrypsin Deficiency and Sarcoidosis (GRADS) consortium.22 Patients were selected if they had a CT scan and spirometry with diffusing capacity of the lung for carbon monoxide (Dlco) within 6 months of their scan (Fig 1) and corresponding clinical data (allowing some missingness).

Consort diagram showing inclusion and exclusion numbers for each cohort. CC = Cleveland Clinic; GRADS = Genomic Research in Alpha-1 Antitrypsin Deficiency and Sarcoidosis; HRCT = high-resolution CT imaging; NJH = National Jewish Health; PFT = pulmonary function test.

Sarcoidosis was defined based on the 1999 American Thoracic Society/European Respiratory Society/World Association Sarcoidosis and Other Granulomatous Diseases statement.1 Patient demographic characteristics, disease duration, smoking status, and ethnicity were obtained as part of the study research questionnaires or from the electronic medical record. Although the race variable was captured with 6 levels (White, Black, American Indian, Hispanic, Mixed, and Other), due to low counts in the American Indian, Hispanic, Mixed, or Other categories, these are reported collectively under the “Combined” category for display in tables.

Pulmonary Function Evaluation

Pulmonary function tests (PFTs) were performed according to American Thoracic Society guidelines.23 Data on FVC, FEV1, FEV1/FVC, and Dlco were collected. Dlco measures were adjusted for elevation to allow for accurate comparison across sites,24 and ComBat harmonization was used to adjust for Dlco acquisition changes that occurred at National Jewish Health.25

To categorize pulmonary function impairment phenotypes, the following criteria definitions were used: (1) possible restriction, FVC < lower limit of normal (LLN) and FEV1/FVC ≥ LLN; (2) obstruction, FEV1/FVC < LLN; (3) possible mixed disorder, FVC < LLN and FEV1/FVC < LLN; (4) isolated gas transfer defect, normal spirometry but Dlco < LLN; and (5) normal phenotype, normal spirometry and Dlco ≥ LLN.

For determining the LLN and normal reference values, reference equations from the Global Lung Function Initiative, which incorporates race-neutral spirometry data, were used.26, 27, 28 Mild (–2.5 < z < –1.65 = LLN), moderate (–4.0 < z < –2.5), and severe (z < –4.0) categorizations were constructed for FEV1 and Dlco.

Radiologic Evaluation

All patients underwent chest CT imaging (majority scan thickness < 1.5 mm). Patients were scanned in the supine position with full inspiration and expiration. Fifty-five participants were excluded because images were technically inadequate with no high-resolution CT image available (Fig 1). Nine expert thoracic radiologists reviewed baseline CT images independently according to a limited and standardized visual assessment tool, masked to the patients’ clinical information. Each image had 1 primary review, and 60 had duplicate reviews. Because achieving consensus was not part of the design, when > 1 radiologist reviewed the image, the primary reviewer’s interpretation was selected for analysis.

A concordance analysis was conducted (e-Table 1), revealing inter-rater agreement on presence of individual CT features in the moderate to substantial range (0.38-0.79), with a value of 0.66 (95% CI, 0.50-0.82) for CT subtype (no parenchymal abnormality, nonfibrotic, fibrotic). Rater evaluations were not found to be inappropriately associated with CT scanner model (e-Table 2).

Each review included evaluation of the following CT features: micronodules, ground-glass opacity, consolidation, conglomerate mass, linear reticular abnormality, honeycombing, mosaic attenuation, architectural distortion, traction bronchiectasis, bronchial wall thickening, cysts, cavity, emphysema, air trapping, and hilar or mediastinal lymphadenopathy (per Fleischner Society guidelines).29 Architectural distortion was defined as the presence of any abnormal displacement of bronchi, pulmonary vessels, fissures, or septa of secondary lobules, and air trapping was assessed using the expiration scan, when available. In addition, the zonal predominance of abnormality in the upper, middle, and lower lung zones was evaluated. CT subtype (no abnormality, nonfibrotic, and fibrotic) was created from the reviews and defined (Fig 2). Fibrotic abnormality was defined as the presence of any of the following: conglomerate masses, architectural distortion, traction bronchiectasis, or honeycombing. Individuals with other parenchymal findings were classified as nonfibrotic. All others were defined as no abnormality.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics for spirometry, patient demographic characteristics, and visually assessed features were computed and stratified based on fibrotic parenchymal abnormality, nonfibrotic parenchymal abnormality, and no parenchymal abnormality. Where P values are computed in tables for demographic characteristics, one-way analysis of variance was used for continuous features and a χ2 test of independence was used for categorical features. Linear regression was used to analyze the relationship of CT subtypes with lung function measures and pulmonary artery diameter, while relationships with mediastinal and hilar adenopathy were analyzed via logistic regression.

The significance of the CT subtype was assessed for each of these models via a partial F test for linear regression or a likelihood ratio test for logistic regression models. In the subset of participants with fibrotic sarcoidosis, multinomial regression models were used to analyze the relationship of conglomerate masses and upper and midlung predominance with pulmonary function phenotype using normal physiology as reference group. All regression models controlled for the same set of demographic adjustment factors: smoking status (ever/never), age, sex, height, and BMI. All regression models were further adjusted for disease duration, which is reported on here as a factor of interest. Missing data were addressed by performing analysis on complete cases only.

For the primary analyses of CT subtype associations with pulmonary function, pulmonary artery diameter, and adenopathy outcomes, 7 tests of overall association were performed. Under convention of an uncorrected P value threshold followed by an overly conservative Bonferroni correction, a highly conservative cutoff P value threshold of < .007 would be suggested. Because these primary outcomes are known to be related and Bonferroni correction is overly conservative (especially for correlated tests), we approximately accounted for multiple comparisons using a P value threshold of < .01 to consider a finding statistically significant. CIs for regression coefficients are reported with 99% CIs.

All analyses were implemented by using R version 4.4.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing).

Results

In total, 932 patients with sarcoidosis met all inclusion and exclusion criteria (mean age, 54.6 years; 426 [46%] male, 502 [54%] female, 4 [< 1%] unknown) and were included in the study (Fig 1; Table 1). Baseline chest CT imaging identified 353 individuals with fibrotic sarcoidosis (38%), 380 with nonfibrotic sarcoidosis (41%), and 199 individuals (21%) without any pulmonary parenchymal abnormalities. The average BMI of participants was 31.0 kg/m2. The racial composition of the cohort was as follows: 139 participants (15%) reported as Black, 713 (77%) reported as White, and 31 (3%) reported as 1 of the 4 racial groups included in the Combined category. There were no statistically significant differences among these demographic characteristics between CT subtypes (P > .01).

Participants with fibrotic sarcoidosis exhibited significantly lower lung function values and larger pulmonary artery diameter compared with individuals with nonfibrotic sarcoidosis or those without parenchymal abnormalities (all comparisons, P < .001). In contrast, participants without parenchymal abnormalities exhibited a significantly lower prevalence of both mediastinal and hilar adenopathy and lower FEV1 and FEV1/FVC values than participants with either fibrotic or nonfibrotic sarcoidosis (P < .001). The CT subtypes were highly predictive of each parameter assessed, with partial F test P values < .001 for all linear regression models and likelihood ratio test P values < .001 for all logistic regression models.

We note that interpretation of results for hilar adenopathy is tempered by moderate rater agreement (Cohen’s kappa 0.52) lower than preferred for unbiased estimation in a modeled outcome. Disease duration was significantly associated with lung function, adenopathy assessments, and pulmonary artery diameter (all, P < 0.001).

Conversely, individuals with a conglomerate mass exhibited 10.73 times the odds of presenting with mixed physiology (99% CI, 2.99-38.49; P < .001) and 3.25 times increased odds of airflow obstruction (99% CI, 1.34-7.91; P < .001) compared with normal physiology. Discussion

In this study, we observed that participants with the fibrotic subtype of sarcoidosis were older, with a longer time between diagnosis and CT imaging. After adjusting for demographic factors, including time since diagnosis, fibrotic sarcoidosis was associated with physiological impairment and an increased pulmonary artery diameter compared with individuals with nonfibrotic sarcoidosis or no parenchymal abnormalities. Moreover, mid or upper lung predominance on CT imaging was associated with decreased likelihood of a restrictive pattern and isolated gas transfer defect, while conglomerate mass exhibited a strong association with mixed physiology.

To our knowledge, this study represents the first large US cohort to investigate not only the association between CT subtypes and pulmonary physiology but also to establish the correlation between CT features and physiology in participants with fibrotic sarcoidosis. Numerous studies have correlated CT features with pulmonary functional abnormalities. Hansell et al12 identified the extent of reticulation in chest CT imaging as a strong predictor of airflow obstruction. In a study cohort of 80 participants with fibrotic sarcoidosis, Abehsera et al30 found that restrictive physiology and low diffusion capacity were associated with honeycombing, and obstructive physiology was associated with bronchial distortion. Similar to the study by Sharp et al,3 we found that disease duration associated with mixed lung function pattern, although we also noted an association with obstructive disease that was not reported in their study. In a more recent study by Obi et al,31 74% of participants with fibrotic sarcoidosis were reported to be Black, in contrast to the 20% found in the current study; Obi et al31 also found associations with worse lung function and reduced survival in White cases. Although we noted worse lung function with fibrosis and longer disease duration, we did not evaluate mortality. Regardless, these differences likely reflect differences in the study cohorts and emphasize the importance of including diverse populations in such studies.

In this study, we conducted a more extensive analysis with a larger patient population than previously reported. Patients with fibrotic sarcoidosis in the current study displayed lower FEV1, FVC, and Dlco values, and an increased pulmonary artery diameter, compared with patients with nonfibrotic sarcoidosis. By correlating CT morphologic changes with pulmonary function alterations in sarcoidosis, we believe that a better understanding of the pathophysiology can be achieved, aiding in treatment planning and facilitating prognostication.

A multinational Delphi consensus study for CT phenotypes in pulmonary sarcoidosis suggested that 3 morphologic phenotypes can be considered as fibrotic sarcoidosis: bronchocentric reticulation with or without dense parenchymal opacification but no cavitation, bronchocentric reticulation and dense parenchymal opacification with cavitation, and large bronchocentric masses.32 Cavitation was relatively uncommon in the current study, seen in about 5% of the fibrotic group and in 1% of the nonfibrotic group. We did not specifically characterize bronchocentric reticulation. However, the large bronchocentric mass subtype in the Delphi consensus probably corresponds to the conglomerate mass/confluent fibrosis type in the current study; this was seen in > 50% of participants with the fibrotic CT subtype and found to have at least 3 times the odds (99% CI, 3-38) of mixed pulmonary function physiology pattern, most likely because of a combination of lung fibrosis and airway distortion from confluent fibrosis. A similar pattern of mixed obstructive/restrictive physiology is found in silicosis with progressive massive fibrosis.33, 34, 35

The current study has several limitations. First, the cohort was predominantly White with relatively mild physiological abnormalities, including obstructive changes as found in other studies.30,31 The findings might not be generalizable to other populations with different racial composition. Yet, other studies have found obstruction. Although we observed associations between CT findings and physiological phenotypes, we were unable to explore potential links between CT imaging or physiological phenotypes and clinical outcomes. Future research efforts should prioritize prospectively designed studies that include comprehensive patient outcome data and longitudinal follow-up. Second, because we only used CT images obtained within 6 months of PFTs, it is possible that the CT image may not reflect the exact morphologic subtype when the PFT was obtained. We were unable to fully characterize the physiological pattern of our patients because we did not have routine plethysmography measurements (lung volumes) for most of them. It is possible that restriction may have been either under-called or over-called. The apparent relationship between age and fibrosis may be due to longer duration of disease in the fibrotic patients in the cross-sectional cohort rather than a biologic phenomenon.

Finally, despite the reading process conducted by expert thoracic radiologists following a standardized process, the potential for bias due to inter-reader variation cannot be ruled out. Partially mitigating this limitation, the formal modeling strategies used primarily assessed individual CT findings as covariates. In this case, reader variation typically reduces power rather than introducing estimation bias. Where CT findings served as outcomes, substantial agreement (low variation) was observed for those findings with the notable exception of hilar lymphadenopathy.

Interpretation

This study found that individuals with fibrotic pulmonary sarcoidosis are older and have had disease longer, have more severe impairment of spirometry and diffusing capacity, and have a higher prevalence of pulmonary restriction than those with nonfibrotic sarcoidosis. Among the patients with fibrotic abnormality, those with conglomerate masses were more likely to have mixed obstructive and restrictive physiology. Additional work is needed to further evaluate the relationship between imaging findings and disease duration; evaluation of a prospectively acquired cohort would also be helpful to determine the prognostic significance of fibrotic findings.
