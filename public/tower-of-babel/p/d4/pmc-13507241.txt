Intuitions about content moderation are misaligned with effective practices for reducing conspiracy beliefs
Authors: Madeline Jalbert, Morgan Wack
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGiven that the spread of false information online often outpaces efforts to address it, it is important to understand i) what content is the most important to remove to prevent false beliefs and ii) whether lay intuitions about content removal are aligned with these findings. In four experiments, participants viewed social media posts containing false evidence for novel conspiracy theories. In Experiment 1 (N = 300), removing posts containing plausible evidence significantly decreased participants’conspiracy belief, while removing posts containing implausible evidence did not. In Experiments 2a-2c (Ns = 350, 352, 351, respectively), we investigated new participants’ moderation preferences for the same posts. Worryingly, participants chose to remove posts containing implausible evidence more often than posts containing plausible evidence (Exp. 2a-2b). And, even when oriented to the goal of reducing belief in the broader conspiracy theory, participants still removed both types of posts at similar rates (Exp. 2c). Our results suggest that relying on lay intuitions to guide content removal decisions may result in prioritizing the removal of content that does not substantively contribute to the spread of broader false beliefs over content that does. These findings have important implications for moderation efforts and highlight the need to look beyond belief in isolated claims when developing effective misinformation interventions.Subject terms: Human behaviour, Human behaviour, Cultural and media studies

Introduction

The spread of online misinformation and conspiracy theories presents significant challenges to networked societies1,2. Among several negative outcomes, misinformation and conspiracy theories have been linked to the intensification of partisan divisions3, deteriorating trust in the media4 and science communication5, and reduced support for public health interventions, including vaccination6. Recent cases of post-election violence in the United States and Brazil have reiterated the severity of the real-world consequences linked to belief in misinformation7. The prominence of these threats has inspired continued research into the spread of misinformation and conspiracy theories. Misinformation and conspiracy theories can emerge when individuals, often operating within highly engaged social communities, selectively connect and interpret evidence to create comprehensive alternative explanations for complex events8. While this process is not new, the accessibility of social media has led to the development of a “participatory conspiracy culture”9, which allows active engagement in the creation, elaboration, and dissemination of false narratives9–11.

The proliferation of connective technologies and the potential harms tied to misinformation and conspiracy theories have prompted calls for researchers to act on their responsibility to protect society by addressing the presence of false content online12. Concern over misinformation among the public has seen content moderation become a central feature of mainstream platforms13. While a widely discussed form of moderation is content removal14, moderation can take on both more and less severe forms, including the reduction of content visibility15 and banning (“de-platforming”) of accounts16. Given the inherent trade-offs between content removal and free speech17, moderation efforts are faced with the challenge of accurately identifying and addressing harmful content while avoiding the unnecessary removal of unharmful content18.

The efficacy of content moderation practices has also been affected by the speed and scale of information transfer on online platforms19,20. With misinformation spreading faster than social media’s ability to address it13, many organizations have turned to “harm reduction” strategies that prioritize addressing the content most likely to cause harm21. Yet the sheer quantity of information that needs to be reviewed has placed increasing strain on members of trust and safety teams19. At the same time, many of these teams, such as those at Meta and X, have implemented mass layoffs22,23. As an alternative means to address problematic content, some platforms have shifted to crowdsourcing models of content moderation, which rely on collections of individual user judgements to tap into the “wisdom of the crowds”24. For example, X’s Community Notes program tasks ordinary users with both identifying and rating the veracity of tagged content25. While recent work to automate fact-checking efforts26 and to integrate LLMs into moderation decisions27 has shown promise, humans remain heavily involved in moderation decisions on mainstream platforms28. Due in part to the sustained involvement of ordinary users in moderation processes, recent research has begun to examine the moderation preferences of ordinary individuals25,29.

In the aim of improving the effectiveness of current moderation efforts, our research explores the alignment between effective moderation practices and individual moderation decisions. We have chosen to focus on the moderation strategy of content removal in this initial work, given the widespread use of this approach. We ask: What type of content should moderation efforts prioritize removing to reduce false beliefs? Are lay intuitions about what content should be removed consistent with these findings? And, if not, how might such moderation efforts be improved to more effectively address false narratives and conspiracy beliefs?

Experiment 1: Designing effective moderation practices

As with the development of everyday beliefs, belief in misinformation and conspiracy theories can emerge by following different lines of evidence30,31. Among prominent election conspiracies, it is not uncommon to find that a single conspiracy involves dozens of individual claims32,33. The content and quality of this evidence can vary greatly. Consider, for example, the false narrative that immigrants are more likely to be criminals than the US citizens. Claims that have recently been used to support this narrative have ranged from false accusations that migrants have accelerated the fentanyl crisis to myths about Venezuela emptying its prisons onto US streets to lies about Haitian immigrants eating dogs and cats34,35. For moderators aiming to prevent the spread of the false narrative about immigrant criminality rates, which claims should they address first?

Unfortunately, most research on preventing the spread of misinformation largely focuses on pieces of misinformation in isolation36. While understanding how to address individual pieces of misinformation clearly has its merits, it is imperative to consider the dynamics of how broader beliefs emerge through exposure to many of these individual pieces of misinformation. We argue that research into addressing the spread of false beliefs and conspiracy theories requires attention not just to how to address problematic content, but also which content should be prioritized to most efficiently and effectively support broader moderation goals of reducing the harm while protecting free speech.

Understanding which content to prioritize involves understanding how isolated pieces of misinformation inform these broader beliefs. In general, the presence of evidence is one of the main criteria individuals draw on to assess whether or not information is true37. When asked to assess the truth of a conspiracy theory or broader narrative based on several different pieces of evidence, individuals may first form a general impression of the strength of the evidence that exists to support that conspiracy theory or narrative. General impressions are typically informed through a weighted averaging process described by information integration theory38,39. In the case of evaluating the evidence for a conspiracy theory or false narrative, the presence of high-quality pieces of evidence should increase the average quality of the evidence, increasing the evaluator’s impression of the strength of the evidence. On the other hand, the presence of low-quality pieces of evidence should reduce the average quality of the evidence, decreasing the evaluator’s impression of the strength of the evidence.

When considering the impact of moderation effects addressing false narratives through content removal, we would thus predict that these efforts would be most effective when they prioritize removing the most compelling pieces of evidence, e.g., in our example, the claims about migrants have accelerated the fentanyl crisis or countries emptying their prisons onto the US streets—as the removal of this content should lower the average quality of the remaining evidence. On the other hand, we would predict that removing the less compelling pieces of evidence first, e.g., that immigrants are eating cats and dogs—should actually increase the average quality of the remaining evidence, potentially increasing belief in the conspiracy theory compared to not removing any content.

We test this hypothesis in Experiment 1, utilizing novel conspiracy theories and social media posts containing plausible and implausible evidence for those theories as our materials. To preview our results, we found that, as predicted, removing a post containing plausible evidence for a conspiracy theory significantly reduced belief in that conspiracy theory, both compared to not removing any posts and compared to removing a post containing implausible evidence. On the other hand, varying from our prediction, we found that removing a post containing implausible evidence had no significant effect on conspiracy belief compared to not removing any posts. Based on these findings, we conclude that moderation efforts should focus on the removal of high plausibility content to reduce the spread of conspiracy beliefs.

Experiments 2a–2c: Intuitions of moderation

In Experiments 2a–2c, we turn to investigating whether individual intuitions regarding what content to remove are in line with our findings from Experiment 1. We had reasons to expect that individual perceptions regarding content removal would not be aligned with what we found to be effective. First, although individuals evaluating information tend to form an overall impression of it through an averaging process, individuals considering how information is presented tend to focus instead on individual pieces of information in isolation, a phenomenon that has been termed the “Presenter’s Paradox”40. In the case of individuals making moderation decisions about what content to remove, individuals may be more focused on the potential harms of individual pieces of content and fail to consider the consequences of addressing them in the context of the broader information environment. This may lead individuals to believe that it is important to remove all evidence supporting conspiracy theories, even when removing this content may actually increase the average strength of the remaining evidence.

In addition, prior research has also shown that individuals exhibit systematic biases in their perceptions of what others find persuasive. Generally, people often consider others to be more susceptible to persuasive messaging than themselves, a phenomenon referred to as the “Third Person Effect”41,42. These findings have more recently been observed in the domain of misinformation, with individuals believing that others are more susceptible to believing misinformation compared to themselves43. Individuals also predict that others (vs. themselves) are more likely to be persuaded by low-quality evidence, whereas they see themselves as more likely (compared to others) to be persuaded by high-quality evidence44,45. If individuals misperceive that many others will be persuaded by posts containing implausible evidence for conspiracy theories, they may view these posts as particularly important to remove, considering that the implausible (vs. plausible) posts are more likely to represent more extreme or non-mainstream views. Taken together, we suspect that this focus on individual claims and failure to appropriately assess how susceptible others are to accepting misinformation will lead individuals to prioritize the removal of posts containing implausible (vs. plausible) evidence.

To investigate this possibility, in Experiments 2a–2c, participants made judgments about the removal of the same social media posts from Experiment, rating how important they thought it was to remove each post and making a forced-choice moderation decision about which post—if any—they would remove for each conspiracy theory. To give further insights into how individuals might make these decisions under different informational and motivational conditions, we varied the key aspects of our design from Experiments 2a to 2c. In Experiment 2a, participants were told that the social media posts had been flagged as potentially containing false information. This was because users involved in crowdsourced moderation, including Twitter/X’s Community Notes program, are often tasked with making determinations about content that has not been verified as true or false25. However, one consideration is that these conditions may lead participants to remove posts containing implausible evidence only because they are more sure that this information is false. Thus, in Experiments 2b and 2b, participants were informed that all posts had been confirmed to contain false information.

In addition, in Experiments 2a and 2b, participants were not given any overarching goals to guide their content removal decisions. Throughout Experiment 2c, we now oriented participants to the moderation goal of reducing belief in the broader false narrative when making their removal judgments. We predicted that, under these conditions, participants might still show a preference for removing implausible (vs. plausible) posts, but also that they may no longer have a removal preference between the two types of posts, as focusing on this overarching goal may increase their likelihood of considering how viewers integrate across these different posts to form broader beliefs.

Methods

The procedures for all experiments reported in this paper were approved by the University of Washington’s Institutional Review Board (IRB). When participants began the experiment, they were provided with an information sheet and indicated their agreement to participate before proceeding.

Experiment 1

The research question, hypotheses, and analysis plan for Experiment 1 were preregistered at https://aspredicted.org/cy2j-ntvg.pdf on December 23, 2024.

Design

We used a within-subjects design with three moderation conditions: no moderation, with one of the posts containing plausible evidence removed, or with the post containing implausible evidence removed. Each participant viewed three sets of posts corresponding to each of the three broader conspiracy beliefs, with one set appearing in each of the three moderation conditions.

Participants

To determine the number of participants to recruit, we conducted a power analysis based on a small effect size of Cohen’s f = 0.1 across our three within-subjects moderation conditions using G*Power 3.146. Using an alpha of α = 0.05, power (1−β) of 0.95, and correlation among repeated measures of 0.5, the power analysis indicated a required sample size of 259 participants. We aimed to slightly overpower our study from this and recruit 300 participants.

Our participants were Prolific participants located in the US with 95%+ approval ratings. We also removed one participant who did not answer any of the key DVs and thus could not be included in any of the analyses. We additionally removed two participants who started the survey, completed it partially, and then started and completed new responses, as they may have made their responses after viewing multiple experimental conditions. We did not preregister this particular exclusion, but felt this was the most appropriate to preserve the validity of the data. Our results remain the same when these responses are included, and we report these analyses in Supplementary Information Section 4. We ended up with 300 complete responses (Mage = 40.66, SDage = 15.75, one person not reporting; 122 male, 172 female, 6 non-binary, 0 choose to self-describe, 0 prefer not to say; 150 White, 6 Hispanic or Latino, 95 Black or African American, 0 Native American or Indigenous, 26 Asian, 1 Native Hawaii/Other Pacific Island, 3 other, 19 multiple selections). The study was estimated to take 4 min, and participants received $0.80 for compensation.

Materials

Generation and selection of conspiracy theories

We first generated false or unverifiable narratives that resembled real conspiracy theories using an LLM. Further details on the generation of the false narratives can be found in Supplemental Information Section 1.

From there, we selected 12 generated conspiracies and pretested them on truth and familiarity in a sample of the US Prolific users (N = 51, approval rating 95%+) to ensure they were not at floor or ceiling levels of belief or judged to be high in familiarity. Participants were told they would see a series of 12 beliefs that some people may hold. They then saw each belief one at a time and rated its truth and familiarity. Perceived truth was assessed with the question “Is this belief true or false?” with a 6-point unnumbered response scale from “Definitely true” (coded as 6) to “Definitely false” (coded as 1). Familiarity was assessed with the question “How familiar are you with this belief?” with a 6-point unnumbered scale from “Not at all familiar” (coded as 1) to “Extremely familiar” (coded as 6). Based on these pretesting results, we selected four conspiracy theories to carry forward in material generation.

Creation and selection of claims containing evidence

We next created five plausible and three implausible claims that could provide evidence for each of the selected conspiracy theories. We then pretested these evidence statements on believability in another group of the US Prolific participants (N = 50, 95%+ approval rating). Participants were shown one of the conspiracy theories and then judged the claims corresponding to that conspiracy theory, responding to the question “How believable is this claim” on a unnumbered 7-point scale from “not at all believable” (coded as 1) to “extremely believable” (coded as 7) with the midpoint labeled “moderately believable”. They did this for each of the four generated conspiracy theories.

Based on the results of this testing, we then selected three conspiracy theories. For each theory, we selected one claim low in believability and two claims higher in believability to use for our plausible and implausible posts. The final selected conspiracy theories (and corresponding ratings from pretesting) were the following: (i) High-speed trains: The development of high-speed rail networks is intended to control and restrict population movement (truth: M = 2.22, SD = 1.14; familiarity: M = 2.27, SD = 1.28), (ii) Earbud recordings: Wireless earbuds like AirPods are secretly recording conversations and transmitting the data to corporations and intelligence agencies (truth: M = 3.31, SD = 1.36; familiarity M = 3.08, SD = 1.48), and (iii) Lab-grown meat: Lab-grown meat is engineered to alter human biology (truth M = 2.67, SD = 1.57; familiarity M = 2.63, SD = 1.44). The believability ratings for the claims selected for our implausible posts ranged from 1.92 to 2.22 (M = 2.06, SD = 0.15), while the believability ratings for the claims selected for our plausible posts ranged from 3.88 to 4.24 (M = 4.03, SD = 0.13). Believability ratings obtained from pretesting for each claim can be found in Supplementary Information Section 2.

Social Media post construction

We then turned these claims into social media posts resembling those found on Twitter/X. So that the content of our posts would be more similar to the content of real social media posts, we added some additional phrasing, made some changes to capitalization and punctuation, and added emojis to some of them, but kept the content of the claims identical to the norming. The profile photos, usernames, and handles of the posts were blurred. Examples of one of the plausible posts and one of the implausible posts can be seen in Fig. 1. All posts can be found in Supplementary Information Section 3.

Measures

Truth judgment

Our key dependent variable was truth judgments of the generated conspiracy belief, assessed with the question, “Is this belief true or false?” with a 6-point unnumbered response scale from “Definitely true” (coded as 6) to “Definitely false” (coded as 1). We note that in the pre-registration for this study, we mistakenly wrote that “Definitely false” would be coded as 7 rather than 6. However, we consistently used 6-point scales across our pretesting and experiments.

Political measures

We additionally included two questions assessing political orientation and affiliation. We assessed political orientation with an adapted measure from the American National Election Studies (ANES) survey. Participants were told, “Here is a 7-point scale on which the political views that people might hold are arranged from ran. Where would you place yourself on this scale?” (Extremely liberal, Liberal, Slightly liberal, Neither liberal nor conservative, Slightly conservative, Conservative, Extremely conservative). We assess political affiliation with a measure from the General Social Survey (GSS): “Generally speaking, do you usually think of yourself as a Republican, Democrat, Independent, or what?” (options: Democrat, Republican, Independent, Other—please specify, No preference, Don’t know).

Procedure

After completing the information sheet, participants were told that we would show them a series of recent social media posts related to beliefs that people may hold. They were informed that we would first show them one of these beliefs and that they would then view two or three posts made on Twitter/X related to that belief. They were also informed that, to protect the privacy of the posters, we had blurred out their profile photos and names. We then previewed the interest and truth ratings they would make. Finally, we told participants not to search online when completing the study and, if they were unsure of an answer, just make their best guess.

Participants were then shown one of the three conspiracy theories, followed by its corresponding posts. As participants viewed each social media post, they answered the question, “How interesting do you find this post?” on an unnumbered 6-point scale from “Very interesting” to “Very uninteresting” with midpoint values of “Interesting,” “Slightly interesting,” “Slightly uninteresting,” and “Uninteresting.” These interest ratings served to ensure that participants were reading the posts. Each participant saw one set of posts in each of the three moderation conditions, and we counterbalanced which moderation condition each generated conspiracy theory appeared in between participants to control for item effects. We also randomized the order in which the conspiracy theories were presented for each participant.

Finally, participants completed basic demographics (age, gender, and ethnicity) and our political orientation and affiliation measures. At the end of the study, participants viewed a debriefing that reiterated that conspiracy theories and posts they viewed were not true and did not have evidence, and informed them of the purpose of the experiment.

Experiments 2a–2c

We preregistered the research questions, hypotheses, and analysis plans for each experiment. Experiment 2a was preregistered at https://aspredicted.org/4yzf-3vws.pdf on January 24, 2025; Experiment 2b was preregistered at https://aspredicted.org/9qrz-9s5w.pdf on February 25, 2025; and Experiment 2c was preregistered at https://aspredicted.org/85jy-r6zf.pdf on February 27, 2025. The general methods for all experiments were the same, so we report them together.

Design

We used a within-subjects design for our importance ratings, with each participant judging the two plausible and one implausible posts corresponding to each of the three conspiracy theories. Each participant also responded to a forced-choice moderation decision for each conspiracy theory.

Participants

Based on a small effect size of Cohen’s d = 0.2 for our importance rating analyses, we conducted a power analysis using G*Power 3.146. Using an alpha of α = 0.05, power (1−β) of 0.95, and a two-tailed test, the power analysis indicates a required sample size of 327 participants. A separate power analysis conducted using simulations for the secondary analysis required at least 320 participants. We thus aimed to slightly overpower our experiments and recruit 350 participants for each of our three experiments, again recruiting participants located in the US with 95%+ approval ratings.

In total, we had N = 350 participants completing Experiment 2a (Mage = 38.06, SDage = 13.16; 139 male, 203 female; 7 non-binary, 0 choose to self-describe, 1 prefer not to say; 221 White, 20 Hispanic or Latino, 37 Black or African American, 1 Native American or Indigenous, 45 Asian, 0 Native Hawaii/Other Pacific Island, 1 other, 25 multiple selections), N = 352 participants completing Experiment 2b (Mage = 37.83, SDage = 13.34; 147 male, 196 female, 5 non-binary, 1 choose to self-describe, 3 prefer not to say; 219 White, 26 Hispanic or Latino, 46 Black or African American, 1 Native American or Indigenous, 34 Asian, 0 Native Hawaii/Other Pacific Island, 3 other, 23 multiple selections) and N = 351 participants completing Experiment 2c (Mage = 38.10, SDage = 12.97; 150 male, 195 female, 5 non-binary, 1 choose to self-describe, 0 prefer not to say; 221 White, 23 Hispanic or Latino, 34 Black or African American, 3 Native American or Indigenous, 37 Asian, 0 Native Hawaii/Other Pacific Island, 2 other, 31 multiple selections). Experiment 2a was estimated to take 6 min and participants received $1.20 for compensation. As the actual median time to complete the study was close to 5 min, we adjusted our estimated time and payment for Experiments 2b and 2c to 5 min and $1.00.

Materials

Our materials were the same stimuli used in Experiment 1.

Measures

Importance of removal

In Experiments 2a and 2b, participants responded to the question “How important is it that this post is removed?” on a six-point unlabeled response scale going from “Not at all important” (coded as 1) to “Extremely important” (coded as 6). In Experiment 2c, this question was modified to “How important is it that this post is removed to prevent the spread of the false belief?“ and participants used the same response scale.

Forced-choice moderation decision

The second key independent variable was a choice between three different moderation options. Participants were shown all three posts corresponding to one of the generated conspiracy theories. They were told to imagine all of the posts above were present on a social media platform. They were then informed that they had three choices: select one of two posts for removal, or do not remove any posts. In Experiments 2a and 2b, participants responded to the question “Please indicate which choice you think would be the best”, and in Experiment 2c, participants responded to the question “Please indicate which choice you think would be the most effective for preventing the spread of the false belief.” In Experiment 2c, participants were additionally given the generated conspiracy theory—labeled as “false belief” at the top of the page so they could be reminded of it as they made their selection.

For response options, participants could choose to “Remove this post” with one of the plausible posts presented, “Remove this post” with the implausible post, or “Do not remove any posts.” Which of the two possible plausible posts was presented as an option to remove was randomized between participants. This was to most closely mirror the different possible moderation conditions in Experiment 1, where we randomized which of the two plausible posts was removed in the plausible post-moderated condition across participants.

Procedure

Following an information sheet, participants were told that, in this study, we were interested in what social media posts people think are the most important to remove. In Experiment 2c (where we included a moderation goal), we also added, “Specifically, we are interested in what social media posts people think are the most important to remove in order to prevent the spread of false beliefs.” Participants were then informed that we would first show them a false belief that people may hold, and then three recent posts made on Twitter/X related to this belief. In Experiment 2a, participants were then told, “These posts were flagged as containing potentially false information,” while in Experiments 2b and 2c, participants were told, “These posts were confirmed to contain false information.” All participants were then told that, to protect the privacy of the posters, we had blurred out their profile photos and names. We then previewed the importance ratings and forced choice ratings participants would be asked to make. As in Experiment 1, participants were told not to search online while they were completing the study, and that if they were unsure of an answer, to just make their best guess.

Participants first went through the importance of removal ratings for the posts corresponding to that conspiracy theory, with the order of the posts randomized for each participant. After participants rated all three posts corresponding to one of the conspiracy theories, they made the forced-choice moderation decision for that set of posts. As in Experiment 1, we also randomized the order in which the conspiracy theories were presented for each participant.

Finally, participants completed basic demographics (age, gender, and ethnicity), our political orientation and affiliation measures, and then viewed a debriefing that reiterated the truth value of the conspiracy theories and posts and informed them of the purpose of the experiment.

Analytic approach

Data for each experiment were analyzed in SPSS Statistics (IBM, version 29), except for the overall forced choice analyses of Experiments 2a–2c, which were conducted in RStudio using the nnet (version 7.20) and purrr packages (version 1.0.4). In addition, the confidence interval for η2 for the ANOVA in Experiment 1 was calculated in RStudio using MOTE (version 1.2.2).

Experiment 1

To investigate the effect of the moderation condition on conspiracy theory belief, we conducted a one-way repeated measures ANOVA on truth ratings and followed up with a simple effects analysis that included a Bonferroni adjustment for multiple comparisons. We report p-values and corresponding 95% CIs adjusted for these multiple comparisons, which can be compared to the standard alpha level of 0.05. Because repeated measures ANOVAs and t-tests are generally robust to violations of normality with larger samples, we did not formally assess assumptions of normality for this test or for the subsequent repeated measures t-tests mentioned. For this ANOVA analysis, we checked that assumptions of sphericity were met using Mauchly’s test.

Experiments 2a–2c

To investigate content removal preferences, we compared the mean importance of removal ratings for the plausible and implausible posts by conducting a paired-samples t-test, and report a repeated measures effect size dz that uses Hedges’ correction for small sample sizes. For each participant, we averaged ratings of the three implausible posts and six plausible posts to generate these means.

Our forced-choice responses were analyzed using a multinomial logistic regression that implemented a bootstrapping procedure with 1000 replications and stratified by participant to obtain robust standard errors. This procedure provides robust standard errors that do not rely on asymptotic distributional assumptions. To compare the likelihood of selecting to remove the implausible posts or no posts to selecting to remove the plausible posts, we ran the model setting the selection of removing the plausible posts as the reference category. We additionally compared the likelihood of selecting to remove no posts vs. removing the implausible post by setting the removal of the implausible post as the reference category.

Due to a randomization error, in Experiment 2a, only half of the participants in this experiment (n = 175) were presented with intended forced-choice options. These options were to remove the implausible posts, remove one of the two plausible posts, or not remove any posts. The other half of the participants were supposed to have the choices of removing the implausible post, removing the second of the two plausible posts, or not removing any posts. However, they were instead given the options of removing each of the two plausible posts or not removing any posts. We thus only included half of the participants who viewed the intended options in this analysis, and note that this means that which of the two possible plausible posts were presented as a removal option did not vary across participants. This was corrected in subsequent experiments.

Results

Experiment 1

We observed a main effect of the moderation condition on conspiracy theory truth ratings, F (2, 598) = 11.02, p < 0.001, η2 = 0.036, 95% CI [0.011, 0.068]. Consistent with our predictions, we found that participants judged conspiracy theories to be less true when a post containing plausible evidence was removed (M = 2.60, SD = 1.61) compared to both no moderation (M = 2.88, SD = 1.61), mean difference = -0.28, 95% CI [−0.53, −0.03], p = 0.024, and to when the post containing implausible evidence was removed (M = 3.11, SD = 1.67), mean difference = −0.51, 95% CI [−0.77, −0.24], p < 0.001. On the other hand, removing an implausible post did not yield a statistically significant effect on belief in the conspiracy theories compared to no moderation, mean difference = 0.23, 95% CI [−0.04, 0.50], p = 0.112. These results indicate that removing posts containing plausible evidence can effectively reduce belief, while removing posts with less plausible evidence may be ineffective. Experiments 2a–2c: Actual moderation preferences

Consistent with our predictions, in Experiment 2a, participants judged that it was more important to remove the implausible posts (M = 4.15, SD = 1.59) compared to the plausible posts (M = 3.70, SD = 1.30), mean difference = 0.45, 95% CI [0.32, 0.59], t (349) = 6.79, p < .001, dz = 0.36, 95% CI [0.25, 0.47]. Our forced choice results mirrored these findings, with participants choosing to remove the implausible posts more than twice as often as the plausible posts, or 51% vs. 24% of the time, b = 0.75, 95% CI [0.51, 0.99], p < 0.001.

These significant effects persisted in Experiment 2b when participants were explicitly told that all of the presented claims had been confirmed as false, although the size of these effects was now smaller both for importance ratings and for our forced choice preferences. Participants judged that it was more important to remove the implausible posts (M = 4.00, SD = 1.59) compared to the plausible posts (M = 3.86, SD = 1.37), mean difference = 0.14, 95% CI [0.02, 0.26]; t (351) = 2.22, p = 0.027, dz = 0.12, 95% CI [0.01, 0.22]). They also choose to remove the implausible posts 32.3% more often than the plausible posts, or 44% vs. 33% of the time, b = 0.28, 95% CI [0.11, 0.45], p < 0.001.

In Experiment 2c, we continued to tell participants that all claims had been confirmed as false, and now additionally oriented participants to the broader goal of reducing belief in the broader false narrative throughout the study, asking participants to rate how important it was to remove the posts to prevent the spread of the false belief and—for their forced-choice removal decisions—which choice would be best to prevent the spread of the false belief. We had predicted that participants may still show the same preference for removing implausible posts as in Experiments 2a and 2b, but also that these instructions may now result in no preference between the two. We found the latter to be the case, with participants giving similar importance ratings for the implausible posts (M = 4.13, SD = 1.59) and the plausible posts (M = 4.14, SD = 1.36), mean difference < 0.01, 95% CI [−0.14, 0.13], t (350) = −0.07, p = 0.945, dz = 0.01, 95% CI [−0.11, 0.10], and choosing to remove both the plausible posts and implausible posts 42% of the time, b < 0.01, 95% CI [−0.16, 0.16], p = 0.977. Consistent with our preregistration, two participants were not included in this analysis as they did not give responses to all forced-choice items. Comparison to no moderation

In each of our three studies, we had also predicted that participants would select to remove both the plausible posts and the implausible posts more often than the third option to remove no posts. Consistent with our predictions, we found that participants chose each of the post removal options more often than they chose to remove no posts, except when comparing the frequency of choosing to remove the plausible posts compared to no posts in Experiment 2a, where both options were chosen 24% of the time, b = 0.02, 95% CI [−0.28, 0.31], p = 0.917. Full reporting can be found in Supplementary Information Section 4.

Exploratory analyses

In each experiment, we conducted exploratory analyses investigating the extent to which our findings were consistent at the level of individual conspiracy theories. In addition, as research has revealed partisan differences in misinformation belief47 and attention paid to perceived biases in social media censorship by citizens48, we explored whether effects varied by political orientation and affiliation.

Effects of conspiracy theory

Given the reduced power to observe significant effects for individual conspiracy theories, we focus broadly on the pattern of our findings here, but report full analysis and descriptives in Supplementary Information Section 5.

In Experiments 1 and 2a, all conspiracy theories showed a directional pattern of results consistent with the overall findings, although the strength of these patterns varied across conspiracy theories. However, the pattern of findings across items in Experiments 2b and 2c showed considerably more variability, indicating that shifts in moderation preferences are likely to vary depending on other attributes of the claims and conspiracy theories above and beyond plausibility. Political orientation and affiliation

To examine whether any of our effects were moderated by political orientation, we conducted an ANCOVA with the political orientation included as a continuous mean-centered between-subjects variable and a moderation condition (Experiment 1) or post plausibility (Experiments 2a–2c) included as a within-subjects factor. To examine whether our effects were moderated by political affiliation, we added political orientation (Democrat, Republican, or Independent) as a between-subjects factor to our truth and importance of removal ratings. We additionally look at the pattern of our forced-choice results across these measures.

Looking across our studies. we observed main effects of political orientation and affiliation on overall conspiracy belief (Experiment 1) and preferences for removing posts (Experiments 2a–2c), Participants who were more conservative (vs. more liberal) and who identified as Republican (vs. Democrat or Independent) had higher levels of belief in our conspiracy theories across moderation conditions, and participants who were more liberal (vs. more conservative) and Democrat (vs. Republican or Independent) indicated it was important to remove the posts overall. However, the impact of the moderation condition and participants’ relative preferences for removing posts containing plausible vs. implausible evidence did not reliably vary across either of these political measures. Although a few exceptions did emerge (forced choice ratings by political orientation and affiliation in Experiment 2b; importance ratings by political orientation in Experiment 2c), these effects represented the minority of comparisons and were not consistent across experiments. Overall, the influence of the moderation condition in Experiment 1 and post plausibility in Experiments 2a–2c were similar across political divides. Full analyses can be found in Supplementary Information Section 5.

Discussion

In this paper, we investigated (i) what type of content moderation efforts involving content removal should target to most effectively address the spread of false beliefs and (ii) whether lay intuitions about content removal are aligned with these findings. In Experiment 1, we found that removing posts containing plausible evidence for novel conspiracy theories reduced belief in those theories. On the other hand, we did not find a significant effect of removing posts containing implausible false evidence on conspiracy theory belief. These findings are partially consistent with information integration theory, which posits that individuals form an overall impression from information by averaging across it38,39. In this case, we had anticipated that individuals would form an impression of the quality of evidence for a conspiracy theory based on the average quality of the evidence presented to them. Because removing the posts containing plausible evidence decreases the average quality of the remaining evidence, we expected this to decrease conspiracy theory belief. On the other hand, we had expected that removing implausible evidence, which increases the average quality of the remaining evidence, would increase conspiracy belief.

There are multiple possibilities for our failure to observe the latter effect. First, because our pattern of observed means was directionally consistent with this effect, one possibility is that we were underpowered to observe this difference. This may have been due to us powering to observe a significant main effect of the moderation condition on belief rather than powering to observe significant simple effects after correcting for multiple comparisons. An additional consideration is that information integration theory describes a process of weighted averaging, in which individuals give more weight to information perceived to be of greater value. This weight may be influenced by properties of the information, such as clarity and redundancy (Anderson, 1971). If features of the implausible posts lead participants to place lower value on them, these posts may have contributed less to participants’ overall impressions relative to the plausible posts, leading to the removal of plausible evidence having the largest effects and making it more difficult to detect the impact of removing implausible evidence.

On the other hand, our failure to find a significant effect of removing an implausible post could also reflect that an alternative account to information integration may provide a better model for how conspiracy beliefs are constructed. For example, perhaps participants were using a summative approach focused on the total amount of plausible evidence. A higher-powered replication of the present Experiment 1 and additional studies investigating the effects of varying the total and relative amount of plausible and implausible posts could help disentangle these possibilities. However, in either case, our findings support the conclusion that removing posts containing implausible evidence resulted in a significant decrease in conspiracy beliefs, while we observed no such effect of the removal of posts containing plausible evidence.

Next, in Experiments 2a–2c, we investigated whether individuals’ intuitions about what content should be removed aligned with these findings, varying specifics of the moderation task across experiments. In Experiment 2a, participants were informed that the posts they were viewing had been flagged as containing potential false information, while in Experiments 2b and 2c, participants were informed that the posts were confirmed to contain false information. In Experiment 2c, we also specifically oriented participants to the goal of reducing the spread of the broader conspiracy belief. Concerningly, in both Experiment 2a and 2b, participants indicated that it was more important to remove the posts containing implausible (vs. plausible) evidence, and selected this option for removal more often in the forced choice. And, in Experiment 2c, participants indicated it was similarly important to remove both implausible and plausible posts, and selected them for removal at similar rates in the forced choice. Thus, across all experiments, participants’ moderation decisions were misaligned with what was most effective for reducing conspiracy beliefs.

We suspect that individuals may fail to prioritize the removal of the plausible claims for several reasons. First, although participants thought it was more important to remove posts containing implausible (vs. plausible) evidence in both Experiments 2a and 2b, this effect was much smaller in Experiment 2b, with dz = 0.36, 95% CI [0.25, 0.47]) for Experiment 2a compared to dz = 0.12, 95% CI [0.01, 0.22]) for Experiment 2b. As the only change for Experiment 2b was confirming the posts contained false information, this indicates that uncertainty about the truth of the plausible claims is a contributing factor to a preference for removing implausible claims.

In addition, we found that orienting individuals to the specific goal of reducing belief in the conspiracy theory shifted individuals from prioritizing implausible claims to choosing both implausible claims and plausible claims at similar rates. This observation is consistent with the idea that individuals considering what information to present may focus more on each individual piece of content than the overall impression that information gives40. In our studies, without a specific moderation goal, individuals may only be focused on the potential harm of belief in individual claims and fail to consider the consequences of addressing these claims in the context of the broader information ecosystem in which they appear. Finally, our results may reflect a “Third Person Effect”41 such that individuals perceive others to be more susceptible to persuasive messages than themselves, particularly when those persuasive messages are low quality44,45.

Implications for moderators and Social Media platforms

Our findings suggest that relying on lay intuitions to guide content removal decisions may result in spending outsized attention addressing content that is not substantively contributing to the spread of conspiracy beliefs or broader false narratives. This allocation of resources would be antithetical to the goal of prioritizing the removal of claims most likely to lead to real-world harms21. It also raises concerns from a free speech perspective17. As content removal involves restricting expression, such restrictions are most defensible when they yield meaningful reductions in harm. Removing content that does not substantively contribute to the spread of false beliefs imposes costs to free expression without comparable benefits. To more effectively meet these goals, our results suggest content removal efforts should consider the quality of the content and prioritize the removal of content that users are the most likely to find compelling over the removal of lower-quality content.

For trained members of trust and safety teams, implementing these insights would likely require only minor recalibrations to current practices, which already see moderators account for factors beyond simple claim veracity in determining prioritization. However, the challenges posed by integrating claim quality assessments become more complex at scale, particularly with the growing popularity of crowdsourced programs and AI-assisted identification systems. As a starting point, moderators, in partnership with researchers, could explore the development of standardized procedures for assessing and incorporating content quality into prioritization decisions within these scaled systems. Researchers and trust and safety teams could also examine whether methods currently being tested for assessing claim veracity might also be useful for assessing claim quality, and investigate different ways of embedding these assessments of claim quality into moderation workflows to inform prioritization decisions. We encourage further research to explore these potential solutions.

Limitations

While our findings offer promising directions for improving moderation practices, several important limitations should be considered when applying these insights to real-world contexts. First, we used novel conspiracies. Our findings may look different for existing conspiracies that individuals have already formed beliefs about. Second, in our studies, participants viewed all posts related to a conspiracy theory back-to-back. In the real world, people may encounter pieces of evidence in different contexts (e.g., across social media feeds, from different sources) and spaced across time. Future research could investigate these dynamics using different exposure conditions and longitudinal designs.

Additionally, given the item effects observed in our exploratory analysis, we expect the influence of moderation to also vary depending on other qualities of the conspiracy theory and posts containing evidence. For example, in our studies, we only pretested claims on believability, but other claim attributes, such as how directly the claim supports a conspiracy or the potential harm it may cause if it were true, are likely to be important inputs that guide moderation preferences. Furthermore, other features of the posts, such as engagement metrics, the type of comments that appear with them, the profile image or handle of the poster, or the presence of emojis or images within the posts—may also influence how the claims are interpreted. In our study, none of our posts showed evidence of engagement (likes, comments, etc.), and we blurred out user profile and handle information. We did add emojis to some of our posts, but did not control for this feature, which may have introduced a potential confound. Thus, future work should examine the extent to which our findings generalize across content where such features are systematically varied.

In addition, our project focuses specifically on belief in broader conspiracy theories as our outcome of interest. However, there are clearly unique harms that can result from belief in individual pieces of content that should additionally be taken into consideration during moderation. For example, if the potential harm resulting from belief in a piece of misinformation is great, this can justify its removal even if only a small number of people find it believable. It is also important to note that while our research focused on the removal of false content, conspiratorial content may also be true. The removal of true conspiratorial content raises additional practical and ethical questions that extend beyond the scope of what our present work was intended to investigate. Thus, while our findings offer helpful insights into what content should be prioritized to meet moderation goals, we would like to emphasize that the decision to remove any one post should not be made solely on the basis of claim plausibility. Other factors, such as the potential harms resulting from belief in the content of an individual post or the truth of the content, are also important inputs.

Finally, our work only investigated the impact of content removal. However, social media companies, media outlets, and researchers have experimented with various forms of online content moderation13,49, including fact-checks50,51, manipulations that draw attention to information accuracy52–54, and adding warning labels to false claims55. An important future extension of this would be to investigate whether the effects of targeting higher vs. lower quality content through other methods of moderation yield effects similar to the ones observed with content removal. If this is the case, there may be benefits for addressing higher-quality content through methods other than removal. For example, one potential concern with targeting the removal of plausible content is that such efforts may undermine trust in platforms if users become aware that ostensibly reasonable claims have been removed. Such dynamics may be less of a concern when implementing other moderation strategies, although this remains an open question.

Conclusion

Our research underscores the critical importance of considering the spread of misinformation not just at the level of individual pieces of content, but also with regard to how individuals integrate across individual pieces of content to form broader beliefs. Taking this perspective allows us to identify what type of content moderation efforts should prioritize removing, and also reveals a critical disconnect between effective content removal practices and individuals’ intuitions regarding moderation. These findings provide important insights and potential solutions for how moderation efforts can more efficiently and effectively address the spread of harmful beliefs.
