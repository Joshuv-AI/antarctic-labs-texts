Phosphoinositide kinase PIKfyve inhibitor apilimod blocks hepatitis E virus infection
Authors: Julian J Ring, Sarah Schlienkamp, Maria L Goussain Darido, Xin Zhang, Olinda Pinto Veiga, Jil Alexandra Haase, Emely Richter, André Gömer, Rebecca Menhua Fu, Viet Loan Dao Thi, Rainer G Ulrich, Mara Klöhn, Daniel Todt, Verian Bader, Konstanze F Winklhofer, Volker Kinast, Johan Neyts, Suzanne J F Kaptein, Eike Steinmann, Yannick Brüggemann
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHepatitis E virus (HEV) is a leading cause of viral hepatitis, yet treatment remains limited to the off-label use of ribavirin, which can have significant side effects and is not suitable for all patients, highlighting the need for new antiviral strategies. HEV infection depends on endolysosomal trafficking of virions to lysosomal compartments to initiate host cell entry. To deepen our understanding of the HEV infection cycle and support the development of novel antiviral strategies, we assessed the antiviral efficacy of compounds targeting components of the endocytic machinery.MethodsUsing an established HEV cell culture model, we screened a panel of experimental and Food and Drug Administration-approved compounds targeting the endocytic pathway. Following the initial screen, the antiviral activity of apilimod was further characterised using infectivity assays, siRNA-mediated knockdown, time-of-addition experiments and mechanistic analyses of endolysosomal organisation and lysosomal protease activity. Efficacy was additionally evaluated in primary human hepatocytes and in a ratHEV infection model in vivo.ResultsFive compounds (apilimod, raloxifen, verapamil, chloroquine and tamoxifen) inhibited HEV infection in a dose-dependent manner. The FYVE-type zinc finger containing phosphoinositide kinase (PIKfyve) inhibitor apilimod exhibited the highest antiviral potency. Requirement of PIKfyve for HEV infection was confirmed by siRNA knockdown, and time-of-addition assays demonstrated that apilimod acts during viral entry without affecting replication. Mechanistically, PIKfyve inhibition disrupted lysosomal organisation, impaired HEV trafficking to late endosomal/lysosomal compartments and reduced lysosomal cathepsin activity. Apilimod significantly reduced HEV infectivity in primary human hepatocytes and decreased viral RNA levels in faeces and tissues in a ratHEV infection model.ConclusionOverall, our data suggest that the endolysosomal protein PIKfyve plays a crucial role during the entry of HEV. Considering the reported safety profile of apilimod in previous human clinical trials, the pharmacological targeting of PIKfyve kinase activity might guide novel antiviral strategies against HEV.

Introduction

The hepatitis E virus (HEV; species Paslahepevirus balayani) is a long overlooked RNA virus and the primary cause of acute viral hepatitis in humans globally. Each year, HEV causes an estimated 20 million infections, 3.3 to 19 million acute cases and 3450 to 70 000 deaths, with wide estimated ranges due to inconsistent surveillance and reporting, especially in low- and middle-income countries.1 2 In addition, HEV ranks sixth among viruses with a high spillover risk, underscoring its significant potential for zoonotic transmission.3 While HEV infections are typically self-limiting and asymptomatic in immunocompetent individuals, they can progress to chronicity in immunocompromised patients and cause fulminant hepatitis in high-risk groups, such as pregnant women.4 5 In addition, rat hepatitis E virus (ratHEV; species Rocahepevirus ratti) has recently been recognised as a zoonotic pathogen capable of infecting humans.6 Current therapeutic options for HEV are limited to the off-label use of the broad-spectrum antiviral agent ribavirin (RBV).710 However, RBV is contraindicated in pregnancy owing to its teratogenicity and its use is further restricted by suboptimal efficacy, poor tolerability and a range of adverse side effects. Moreover, HEV variants in response to antiviral treatment have been identified.1115 Although the emergence of viral variants may contribute to RBV resistance, a causal link to treatment failure has yet to be established.

Viral entry into host cells is a critical step in the infection cycle, influencing host range, tissue tropism and pathogenesis.16 Entry inhibitors are promising antiviral agents, particularly for prophylactic use, as they block the virus at the initial step of infection.17 18 Previous studies have shown that HEV is internalised by cells in a clathrin- and dynamin-dependent manner.1921 Although the exact entry route and interaction partners during quasi-enveloped and non-enveloped HEV ((n)eHEV) virion trafficking are presumably distinct, proteolytic processing by lysosomal cathepsins, particularly cathepsin L (CTSL), appears critical for genome release of both forms.22 23 Nonetheless, the detailed mechanisms and host factors required for HEV cell attachment and entry remain unclear, leaving a scarcity of clearly defined molecular targets.24 In this study, we evaluated the antiviral activity of a panel of experimental and Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-approved compounds targeting various components of the endocytic machinery, with the aim of identifying novel druggable host factors for HEV. Among these compounds, the FYVE-type zinc finger containing phosphoinositide kinase (PIKfyve) inhibitor apilimod showed high antiviral activity against HEV. Consequently, we further evaluated the antiviral efficacy of PIKfyve inhibitors across various hepatocyte model systems and in an in vivo rat model of HEV infection.

Materials and methods

Cell culture

HepG2 (ATCC, Manassas, Virginia, USA, HB-8065) and S10-3 (a kind gift from Suzanne Emerson, formerly at the National Institute of Allergy and Infectious Diseases, National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA) cells were grown in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM-high-glucose, Gibco, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA, #11965) supplemented with 10% (v/v) fetal calf serum (FCS, Capricorn, Ebsdorfergrund, Germany, Lot. Nr. CPC21-4114), 1% (v/v) non-essential amino acids (NEAAs, Gibco, #11140050), 2 mM L-glutamine (Gibco, #25030), 100 IU/mL penicillin and 100 µg/mL streptomycin (Gibco, #15140) (DMEM complete). Huh7 cells (kindly provided by Prof. Ralf Bartenschlager, Heidelberg University, Heidelberg, Germany) were cultured in DMEM-high-glucose (Gibco, #11965) supplemented with 10% (v/v) FCS (Cytiva, Marlborough, Massachusetts, USA, SH30080.03IR25-40, #16291092), 1% (v/v) NEAAs (Gibco) and 10 mM of HEPES (Gibco, #15630080). HepG2/C3A cells (kindly provided by Charles Rice, Rockefeller University, New York City, NY, USA) were grown in Eagle’s minimum essential medium (MEM, Gibco, #11095) supplemented with 10% (v/v) ultra-low IgG FCS (Gibco, #16250-078), 1% (v/v) NEAAs (Gibco), 2 mM L-glutamine (Gibco), 100 µg/mL gentamicin (Gibco, #15710) and 1 mM sodium pyruvate (Gibco, #11360). HepG2 and HepG2/C3A cells were grown on rat collagen-coated (SERVA Electrophoresis, Heidelberg, Germany, #47256.01) cell culture dishes (Sarstedt, Nümbrecht, Germany) and incubated at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator.

Primary human hepatocytes (PHH) were obtained from Primacyt (Schwerin, Germany) as cryopreserved hepatocytes and cultured as described before.25 In brief, PHH were seeded according to the manufacturers’ instructions for the respective donor (for donor information see online supplemental table S1) onto collagen-coated 24-well plates (Primacyt, #CCP-24). PHH were kept in Human Hepatocyte Maintenance Medium (HHMM, Primacyt, #HHMM-500). Primacyt obtained the patient informed consent as stated on their website.

Compounds

Compounds were obtained from MedChemExpress (MCE, Monmouth Junction, New Jersey, USA): K11777 (HY-119293), ponesimod (HY-10569), BAPTA-AM (HY-100545), fulvestrant (HY-13636), simvastatin (HY-17502), niclosamide (HY-B0497), raloxifen (HY-13738), verapamil (HY-A0064), chloroquine (HY-17589A), tamoxifen (HY-13757A), apilimod (HY-14644), YM201636 (HY-13228) and APY0201 (HY-15982). RBV was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, Missouri, USA; R9644). All compounds were dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, Sigma-Aldrich, D2650) to prepare stock solutions, which were stored at −20 °C to −80 °C.

In vitro transcription and electroporation

Plasmids containing either the cDNA full-length genome of the Kernow-C1/p6 virus isolate26 27 or the 83–2-27 isolate28 (a kind gift from Takaji Wakita, National Institute of Infectious Diseases, Tokyo, Japan) were used for infectious viral particle production, and a plasmid encoding the sequence of the assembly-deficient subgenomic Gaussia luciferase reporter replicon (Kernow-C1/p6 strain with a truncated ORF2 replaced with a Gaussia luciferase gene) was used in replication assays. In vitro transcription and electroporation into cells were performed as previously described by Todt et al and Meister et al.29 30 In brief, 5×106 HepG2 or HepG2/C3A cells were transferred in 400 µL of Cytomix (120 mM KCl, 0.15 mM CaCl, 10 mM KPO4, 25 mM Hepes, 2 mM EGTA, 5 mM MgCl2) containing 2 mM ATP (Cayman Chemical, Ann Arbour, Michigan, USA, #14498) and glutathione (Sigma-Aldrich, #G4251) and mixed with 5 µg in vitro transcribed (IVT) RNA. The cells were electroporated using the Gene Pulser System (BioRad, Hercules, California, USA, #1652660) with 975 µF and 270 V for ~20 ms and directly transferred into 10 mL cell culture media and plated on a collagen-coated 10 cm plate for virus production or transferred into 12.1 mL cell culture media, and 2×104 cells per well were seeded on a coated 96-well plate (Sarstedt) for Gaussia luciferase assays.

Production of cell culture-derived HEV

Infectious cell culture-derived HEV (HEVCC) particle production was performed as described by Todt et al and Meister et al.29 30 Briefly, HepG2 cells were electroporated with IVT RNA of either the full-length Kernow-C1/p6 or 83–2-27 HEV strains. To obtain extracellular enveloped HEVCC (eHEVCC), culture supernatants were collected 7 days post-transfection and stored at 4 °C and used within a week. Intracellular non-enveloped HEVCC (neHEVCC) was prepared from cell lysates by trypsinising cells, neutralising with DMEM (Gibco), centrifuging at 200 × g for 5 min and resuspending in 1.6 mL of medium per transfection. Cells were subjected to three freeze–thaw cycles using liquid nitrogen and ice, followed by centrifugation at 10,000 × g for 10 min. The clarified supernatant was aliquoted and stored at −80 °C. Virus titres were determined by titrating neHEVCC and/or eHEVCC onto HepG2/C3A cells, followed by fixation and ORF2-staining at 7 days post-infection (d.p.i.) to quantify focus-forming units per mL (FFU/mL).30

HEV infection assays and compound titration

For HEV infection assays, HepG2/C3A and S10-3 cells (1.5×104 cells/well) were seeded in 96-well plates (Sarstedt) and allowed to adhere overnight. The next day, the medium was replaced with fresh medium containing a dilution series of the test compound or DMSO (Sigma-Aldrich) as control and HEV (Kernow-C1/p6; MOI 0.1–0.2). Each concentration was tested in triplicate. HepG2/C3A cells were incubated for 3–4 days, and S10-3 cells for 5 days, before fixation and immunofluorescence staining. For infections with enveloped (Kernow-C1/p6) and non-enveloped (HEV 83–2-27) viruses, 100 µL medium containing either the respective compounds or DMSO (Sigma-Aldrich) was added to the cells, followed by inoculation with 50 µL of virus-containing medium (HEV 83–2-27) or virus-containing supernatant (Kernow-C1/p6). At 24 hours post-infection (h.p.i.), the medium was replaced with complete MEM (Gibco) containing the corresponding inhibitors. At 5 d.p.i., cells were fixed and processed for immunofluorescence staining. Infections of neHEV (Kernow/C1 p6 and HEV 83–2-27) were quantified using CellProfiler.31 Infections with eHEVCC were quantified by counting focus forming units (FFU/mL) from individual wells.

Gaussia luciferase assay

HepG2/C3A cells were electroporated with IVT Kernow-C1/p6-Gaussia luciferase RNA, as described above and seeded at a density of 2×104 cells per well on a collagen coated 96-well plate (Sarstedt). The inhibitors apilimod (16 nM), YM201636 (80 nM) and APY0201 (16 nM) (all MCE) and DMSO (Sigma-Aldrich) were applied. Supernatants were sampled 4 and 72 hours post electroporation and stored at 4 °C until luminometer reading. Gaussia luciferase activity was measured by adding 20 µL cell culture supernatant per well on a 96-well LUMITRAC 600 plate, followed by the addition of coelenterazine substrate (Carl Roth, Karlsruhe, Germany, #4094.3) and the detection of luminescence using a Centro XS3 LB 960 luminometer (Berthold Technologies, Bad Wildbad, Germany). The microplate reader was set to dispense 50 µL of substrate, followed by shaking for 2 s and reading for 5 s.

Immunofluorescence staining of HEV-infected cells

Cells were fixed with 3% PFA (Carl Roth, #93351) for 10–20 min, followed by one phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) wash. Permeabilisation was performed using 0.2% Triton X-100 (Carl Roth, #3051.3) in PBS for 5 min. After three PBS washes, cells were blocked with 5% horse serum (HS, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA, #ROS-1B) in PBS for at least 1h at room temperature (RT). Polyclonal HEV genotype 3 capsid (ORF2)-specific rabbit hyperimmune serum (4086 and 2101, kindly provided by Rainer G. Ulrich, Friedrich-Loeffler-Institute, Greifswald, Germany)32 was applied at 1:5000 dilution in 5% HS (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and incubated overnight at 4 °C. The next day, cells were washed three times and incubated with a secondary antibody (goat anti-rabbit AlexaFluor488, Invitrogen, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA, #A-11008; 1:1000 in 5% HS) for 2 hours at RT. After three additional PBS washes, nuclei were stained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI, Invitrogen, #D1306; 1:10 000 in H₂O) and washed three more times. Fluorescence images were acquired with a wide-field fluorescence microscope (Keyence BZ-X800E, Keyence, Osaka, Japan) using a 4×0.75- numerical aperture air-objective. DAPI (358 nm) and ORF2 (488 nm) signals were acquired sequentially by using the BZ-X Filter DAPI and BZ-X Filter GFP, respectively. Infections were quantified either by counting foci to determine FFU/mL or by calculating the percentage of ORF2-positive cells relative to total nuclei using CellProfiler.31

Western blot analysis

HepG2/C3A cells were washed twice with PBS and harvested using a cell scraper. Cells were resuspended in 60 µL M-PER buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #78501) containing protease inhibitor mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #A32953). Lysates were frozen at −80 °C for ≥20 min, thawed and centrifuged at 17,000 × g for 20 min. Supernatants were mixed with 1×Laemmli buffer and denatured at 95 °C. Samples and PageRuler Plus Prestained Protein Ladder (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #26619) were loaded onto SDS-PAGE gels, followed by wet transfer to nitrocellulose membranes (Carl Roth, #200K.1). Membranes were blocked with 5% milk in PBS-T (0.05% Tween-20, VWR, Radnor, Pennsylvania, USA, #663684B) for 1 h and cut at ~70 kDa. The upper membrane was incubated overnight at 4 °C with anti-PIKfyve (rabbit mAb, E4X3R, CST, Danvers, Massachusetts, USA, #92839, 1:1000 in 0.5% milk/PBS-T); the lower membrane with HRP-conjugated anti-β-actin (Sigma-Aldrich, #A3854, 1:1000 in 0.5% milk/PBS-T). After three PBS-T washes, the top membrane was incubated with HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (Abcam, Cambridge, UK, ab97051, 1:10,000) for 2 hours at RT. Both membranes were washed and developed using Pierce ECL substrate (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #32109) for 5 min in the dark and imaged using a Celvin S 420 chemiluminescence system (Biostep, Burkhardtsdorf, Germany).

For cathepsin maturation, HepG2/C3A cells were seeded (6×10⁴ cells per well) in collagen-coated 12-well plates (Sarstedt) and incubated overnight. Cells were then treated with either 1% (v/v) DMSO (Sigma-Aldrich), 500 nM apilimod or 500 nM K11777 (both MCE) for 24 hours, followed by lysis and preparation for Western blot analysis as described above. For Western blotting, a methanol-activated PVDF membrane (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany, #GE10600029) was blocked with Intercept TBS blocking buffer (LI-COR, Lincoln, Nebraska, USA, #927-60001) and incubated overnight at 4 °C with human CTSL antibody (goat, Thermo Fisher Scientific, #AF952SP) and Cathepsin B (D1C7Y) (rabbit, CST, #78749) under agitation. After washing with TBS-T, the membrane was incubated with the secondary antibody IRDye 680RD donkey anti-goat (LI-COR, Lincoln, Nebraska, USA, #926-68074) for 2 hours. Following additional washes with TBS-T, bands were visualised using the Odyssey DLx Imager (LI-COR). Anti-glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) antibody (rabbit, Abcam, #ab9485) was used as a loading control and detected with the secondary antibody IRDye 800CW donkey anti-rabbit (LI-COR, #926-32213).

Western blot quantification was performed using FIJI. Following manual background subtraction, the integrated density of each protein band was measured and used for subsequent analysis.

Cell viability and cytotoxicity assays

If not indicated otherwise, cell viability was measured using an 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)−2-5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) assay. Cells were incubated with MTT-substrate (0.5 mg/mL, Biomol, Hamburg, Germany, #15655) dissolved in cell culture medium at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator for 1–2 hours. After removing the medium, 50 µL of DMSO (Sigma-Aldrich) was applied to each well. Following a 15 min incubation on a rocking shaker, the absorbance of each well was read on a microplate absorbance reader (Tecan Group Ltd, Männedorf, Switzerland) at 570 nm. Cells treated with 70% (v/v) ethanol for 10 min prior to MTT assay served as background control.

For the neutral red uptake assay, neutral red medium (40 µg/mL) was prepared 1 day prior to use by diluting a neutral red (Sigma-Aldrich, #N4638) stock solution (4 mg/mL in PBS) in complete cell culture medium (Gibco) and incubating overnight at 37 °C protected from light. On the day of the experiment, the neutral red medium was centrifuged for 10 min at 600 × g to remove precipitated dye crystals. Culture medium was aspirated from the cells, and 100 µL of neutral red medium was added to each well. Plates were incubated for 2–3 hours at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ incubator. Following incubation, the dye-containing medium was carefully removed, and 150 µL of neutral red destain solution (50% ethanol, 49% deionised water, 1% glacial acetic acid) was added to each well. Plates were shaken for 10 min until complete solubilisation of the incorporated dye. Absorbance was measured at 540 nm using a microplate reader (Tecan Group Ltd). Cells treated with 70% (v/v) ethanol for 10 min to the prior assay served as background control. PHH viability was determined using the CytoTox 96 Non-Radioactive Cytotoxicity Assay (Promega, Madison, Wisconsin, USA, #G1780). Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) release in the supernatant was determined 72 hours post infection by transferring 50 µL supernatant to a 96-well plate (Sarstedt), followed by the addition of an equal volume of CytoTox 96 Reagent (Promega) to each well. The reaction was incubated for 30 min and stopped by the addition of Stop Solution. Absorbance signal was measured at 492 nm in a plate reader microplate absorbance reader (Tecan Group Ltd).

Statistical analysis and software

Viral replication kinetics in rat faeces were analysed using in-house R scripts with a linear mixed-effects model including time, treatment (apilimod vs vehicle), and their interaction as fixed effects, with individual mouse included as a random effect. Significance of fixed effects was assessed using Type III analysis of variance (ANOVA). Viral titres were log₁₀-transformed prior to analysis. Area-under-the-curve (AUC) values of faecal viral titres were calculated for each animal and compared between treatment groups using a Wilcoxon rank-sum test, and effect size was estimated using the rank-biserial correlation. Graphs were plotted and statistical analysis was performed with GraphPad PRISM V.10.2.1 for Windows (https://www.graphpad.com). Half maximal inhibitory concentrations (IC50) and half maximal cytotoxicity concentrations (CC50) were calculated using a four parameter non-linear regression model with constrained top (= 100) and constrained bottom (= 0). Images were analysed with FIJI V.2.16.0 (https://imagej.net/software/fiji/) and/or CellProfiler.2 Final graphics were edited using Adobe Illustrator 2024 (https://www.adobe.com/).

Additional materials and methods describing assays used in this study are specified in the online supplemental material.

Results

Evaluation of compounds targeting the endocytic machinery for antiviral activity against HEV

A collection of experimental and FDA-approved compounds that modulate various aspects of endocytic pathways and endosomal membrane dynamics was evaluated for its potential antiviral activity against HEV (online supplemental table S2). To this end, we included apilimod, a PIKfyve kinase inhibitor that disrupts endosome maturation by blocking phosphatidylinositol-3,5-bisphosphate (PtdIns(3,5)P₂) synthesis and inducing cytoplasmic vacuolisation;33 BAPTA-AM, a calcium chelator that inhibits calcium-dependent endocytosis;34 chloroquine, which impairs endosomal acidification via inhibition of V-ATPase;35 fulvestrant, which destabilises endosomal membranes and facilitates endosomal escape;36 niclosamide, a mitochondrial uncoupler that neutralises acidic endosomal vesicles and inhibits clathrin-mediated endocytosis;37 38 ponesimod, a modulator of the sphingosine-1-phosphate receptor that induces receptor internalisation and degradation, thereby altering receptor trafficking;39 40 raloxifen, a selective oestrogen receptor modulator (SERM), promotes epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) endocytosis and downregulation independent of oestrogen receptor modulation;41 simvastatin, which decreases cholesterol synthesis and disrupts lipid raft-dependent endocytosis;42 43 tamoxifen, also a SERM, inhibits endosomal and lysosomal acidification, permeabilises lysosomal membranes and induces lysosomal Ca²+ release;44 45 and verapamil, a L-type calcium channel blocker that enhances receptor-mediated endocytosis of low-density lipoprotein and inhibits viral entry and trafficking of filoviruses and polyomaviruses.4648

An initial test of the inhibitor panel was conducted to assess their impact on the cell viability of HepG2/C3A cells. Subsequently, dose-response assays were performed using non-toxic concentrations following infection with neHEVCC (Kernow-C1/p6) to evaluate the antiviral activity of the selected compounds. Five compounds (ponesimod, BAPTA-AM, fulvestrant, simvastatin, niclosamide) did not exhibit any antiviral effect at the tested concentrations (figure 1A, online supplemental figure 1A). In contrast, the remaining compounds (apilimod, raloxifen, verapamil, chloroquine, tamoxifen) inhibited HEV infection in a dose-dependent manner (figure 1A,B, online supplemental figure 1A,B). To compare the antiviral potency of these compounds, we calculated the 50% inhibitory concentration (IC50) values. Apilimod (0.012 µM) demonstrated the lowest IC50 value compared with raloxifen (2.025 µM), verapamil (5.701 µM), chloroquine (1.841 µM) and tamoxifen (1.165µM) (figure 1C). Apilimod and chloroquine together, at suboptimal concentrations, further reduced HEV infection in HepG2/C3A cells (online supplemental figure 1C,D,E), suggesting that targeting multiple endocytic steps can enhance antiviral efficacy. Importantly, neutral red uptake assays showed no impairment of lysosomal function or cell viability at the apilimod concentrations used (online supplemental figure 1B). Based on its potent inhibition profile, as well as its established role in modulating endosomal trafficking pathways, we selected apilimod for further characterisation and mode of action analysis.

The phosphoinositide kinase PIKfyve is required during HEV infection

Apilimod inhibits the kinase activity of the phosphoinositide kinase PIKfyve, which plays a critical role in endolysosomal function, morphology and biogenesis.49 To determine if PIKfyve is expressed in hepatocytes, we analysed single-cell RNA-sequencing data from the human liver cell atlas.50 Unlike the hepatocyte-specific marker albumin, which was restricted to hepatocytes (figure 2A), PIKfyve was broadly expressed across all cell types (figure 2B). We further examined PIKfyve expression using RNA sequencing data from PHH51 and observed robust expression across all six donors (figure 2C), along with expression of the hepatocyte marker albumin and previously described HEV host factors EGFR, CTSL, ITGB1, ITGA3 and TSG101,24 while the non-hepatic markers SFTPB (lung) and DCX (brain) were not expressed (figure 2C, online supplemental figure 2). These findings confirm that PIKfyve is expressed in hepatocytes and support its potential role in HEV infection. Given that hepatocytes are the primary target of HEV infection, we investigated the role of endogenous PIKfyve during HEV infection by performing siRNA-mediated knockdown of PIKfyve. The reduction of PIKfyve protein expression in HepG2/C3A cells on siRNA treatment was confirmed by Western blot analysis (figure 2D,E). Next, we infected HepG2/C3A cells—either treated with PIKfyve-specific siRNA or a non-targeting control—with neHEVCC (Kernow-C1/p6), followed by immunofluorescence analysis (figure 2F). Silencing PIKfyve led to an approximately 40% reduction in HEV infection events, suggesting that PIKfyve may function as a host dependency factor during HEV infection (figure 2G).

Inhibition of PIKfyve kinase activity restricts HEV infection

To further confirm the requirement of PIKfyve during HEV infection, we evaluated two additional PIKfyve kinase inhibitors—YM201636 and APY0201—to assess antiviral activity while controlling for potential inhibitor-specific (off-target) effects. Similar to apilimod, both YM201636 and APY0201 reduced neHEVCC (Kernow-C1/p6) infectivity in a dose-dependent manner and exhibited IC50 values in the nanomolar range (figure 3A, B and C). Specifically, apilimod showed an IC50 value of 24.69 nM and a half-maximum cytotoxic concentration (CC50) of 1143 nM, YM201636 had an IC50 of 84.27 nM and a CC50 of 1478 nM, and APY0201 demonstrated an IC50 of 4.31 nM and a CC50 of 735 nM. Given that the precise entry route in eHEV and neHEV virion trafficking to endolysosomal compartments is presumably distinct, we next investigated whether PIKfyve is also required during infection with eHEV. Similar to the broad-spectrum cathepsin inhibitor K11777, which impairs both eHEV and neHEV entry, apilimod, YM201636 and APY0201 exhibited a strong (>50%) antiviral effect in eHEVCC-infected cells (figure 3D,E). Accordingly, infection with the wild boar-derived HEV-3 strain 83–2-27 was potently inhibited by all three inhibitors (figure 3F,G).28 Overall, our findings suggest that both eHEV and neHEV depend on PIKfyve kinase activity and further indicate a strain-independent requirement during HEV infection.

PIKfyve kinase activity supports HEV entry by preserving endolysosomal organisation

Next, we investigated which stage of the viral replication cycle relies on PIKfyve kinase activity. To determine whether perturbation of PIKfyve kinase activity affects HEV replication, we electroporated an HEV-3 subgenomic replicon (Kernow-C1/p6-Gaussia luciferase) to bypass the HEV entry process and specifically monitor HEV RNA replication kinetics. We observed no inhibitory effect on HEV RNA replication on inhibitor treatment (figure 4A), suggesting that PIKfyve kinase activity is dispensable for HEV RNA replication and is required during a different stage of the HEV replication cycle. To elucidate the involvement of PIKfyve in the viral entry process, we performed time-of-drug addition assays, initiating PIKfyve kinase inhibitor treatment at various time points with neHEVCC (Kernow-C1/p6) (figure 4B). Consistent with the previously described slow entry kinetics of HEV,23 all three PIKfyve kinase inhibitors maintained their antiviral efficacy when added to cultures before infection and up to 4 h.p.i. (figure 4C). However, a time-dependent loss in antiviral activity was observed for all inhibitors on administration at or beyond 8 hours post-infection, suggesting that PIKfyve kinase inhibitors target the entry phase of the viral replication cycle. A similar inhibitory pattern was observed for the broad-spectrum cathepsin inhibitor K11777 (0.1 µM). In contrast, a different pattern of viral inhibition was observed with RBV (25 µM), which targets HEV RNA replication. RBV retained its inhibitory effect when administered up to 48 h.p.i., but lost its antiviral efficacy when added at 72 h.p.i., highlighting the distinct modes of action during the HEV replication cycle.

Given that HEV entry requires virion trafficking toward endolysosomal compartments and PIKfyve inhibition can disrupt endolysosomal maturation, acidification and protease activity, we next assessed whether PIKfyve inhibition alters endolysosomal organisation and function in our experimental system.22 23 49 Apilimod treatment resulted in enlarged lysosomes and vacuole formation (figure 5A), as well as a decrease in the number of LAMP1-positive and/or CD63-positive compartments (figure 5B), indicating that PIKfyve inhibition disrupts late endosome/lysosome organisation. Nevertheless, neutral red uptake remained intact, demonstrating that lysosomal acidification and membrane integrity were preserved despite these structural alterations (online supplemental figure 1B). To assess whether apilimod treatment alters HEV virion trafficking towards lysosomal compartments, we employed a recently described RNA fluorescence in situ hybridisation (RNA FISH) assay to detect incoming HEV genomes.23 Consistent with our earlier observations, apilimod treatment lowered the number of LAMP1-positive compartments and also led to a lower fraction of RNA-positive LAMP1 vesicles 4 h.p.i. (figure 5C).23 Treatment with apilimod also lowered HEV infection in S10-3 cells, without compromising cell viability and resulted in an accumulation of HEV genomes 24 h.p.i. compared with DMSO-treated controls (online supplemental figure 3), further indicating that PIKfyve inhibition impairs the trafficking and cell entry of HEV virions. Because PIKfyve inhibition has been demonstrated to impair lysosomal protease function, including cathepsin maturation and activity, we next assessed cathepsin B/L maturation and cathepsin B enzymatic activity following apilimod treatment.52 53 Consistent with previous reports, PIKfyve inhibition markedly reduced cathepsin B/L maturation (figure 5D,E) and decreased cathepsin B activity (figure 5F). Collectively, our findings suggest that PIKfyve inhibition disrupts late endosome/lysosome organisation and proteolytic function, impairing the formation of functional lysosomal compartments required for efficient HEV trafficking, uncoating and entry, while lysosomal acidification and membrane integrity remain intact.

Apilimod restricts HEV infections in PHH ex vivo and in rats in vivo

Given that apilimod has been found to be safe and well-tolerated in human clinical trials,49 our results suggest potential novel pharmacological strategies against HEV. To assess the antiviral potential of apilimod in a more authentic cell culture model, we used PHH. PHH preserves a high degree of liver-specific functionality, making them valuable models to investigate hepatic processes, drug metabolism and viral infections.25 Apilimod restricted neHEVCC (Kernow-C1/p6) infection of PHH in a dose-dependent manner (figure 6A,B), while cell viability, measured by LDH release in the supernatant, remained comparable to that of untreated PHHs (figure 6C). Apilimod further inhibited infection of Huh7 cells with ratHEV strain LA-B350 (online supplemental figure 4). Consequently, we investigated the antiviral potential of apilimod in an in vivo HEV infection model using athymic nude rats infected with ratHEV LA-B350.25 54 Animals received once daily treatment with 75 mg/kg apilimod or vehicle control for 12 days, beginning 2 hours before ratHEV infection (figure 6D). No adverse effects or weight loss were observed (figure 6E), suggesting that the administered dose was well tolerated for the rats. Daily treatment with apilimod resulted in a strong reduction of ratHEV RNA in fecal samples from 3 out of 4 animals examined on days 4, 8 and 12 p.i. (figure 6F). Statistical analysis confirmed that viral titres increased significantly over time (type III ANOVA, p<0.001), while baseline viral loads did not differ between apilimod- and vehicle-treated groups (p=0.276). Notably, a significant time-dependent effect of apilimod treatment was observed (p=0.0087), reflecting a slower increase in viral RNA levels in apilimod-treated animals compared with vehicle-treated controls (figure 6F). Consistent with this finding, AUC analysis of log₁₀-transformed faecal viral titres revealed a trend toward a lower overall viral burden in apilimod-treated animals (Wilcoxon test, p=0.0606), accompanied by a large effect size (rank-biserial r=0.875) (figure 6F). In addition, viral RNA levels were significantly lower in the faeces, serum, liver, spleen and intestine on day 12 p.i. on apilimod treatment compared with vehicle-treated animals (figure 6G). Overall, our data suggest an important role for PIKfyve during HEV infection and highlight the potential of PIKfyve kinase inhibition as a promising antiviral strategy against HEV.

Discussion

In this study, we screened a small collection of experimental and FDA-approved drugs that modulate the endocytic machinery and identified the PIKfyve inhibitor apilimod as a potent inhibitor of HEV infection. Our findings demonstrate that HEV relies on PIKfyve-mediated endolysosomal trafficking for successful infection, highlighting a previously unrecognised host dependency that can be therapeutically exploited. PIKfyve mediates the synthesis of phosphoinositides such as PtdIns(3,5)P2, which are vital for late endosomal and lysosomal functions, as well as autophagosome-lysosome fusion.49 Consistent with this role, pharmacological inhibition of PIKfyve by apilimod altered endolysosomal organisation, as evidenced by lysosomal enlargement, vacuole formation and a reduced number of LAMP1- and CD63-positive compartments, while preserving endolysosomal membrane integrity. These alterations indicate defective late endosome/lysosome maturation and compromised maintenance of functional degradative compartments. Consequently, PIKfyve inhibition reduced the fraction of HEV RNA localised within LAMP1-positive compartments early after inoculation, suggesting impaired trafficking of virions to lysosomes. Together, these findings support a model in which HEV depends on intact PIKfyve-driven endolysosomal maturation to reach a lysosomal environment permissive for capsid disassembly and genome release. In addition to disrupting vesicular trafficking, PIKfyve inhibition markedly impaired lysosomal protease function, including reduced maturation of cathepsin B/L and enzymatic activity of cathepsins B.5558 These proteases have been implicated in HEV capsid processing, a step thought to be necessary for efficient genome release into the cytosol. Delayed or incomplete cathepsin maturation therefore provides an additional mechanistic explanation for the observed block in HEV uncoating and reinforces the idea that HEV entry is tightly coupled to lysosomal proteolytic competence. Our findings align with broader findings regarding endosomal and lysosomal trafficking disruptions, which have been proposed as mechanisms for blocking the entry of other RNA viruses, suggesting a conserved reliance on PIKfyve-mediated endosomal trafficking.5658 Future research should aim to elucidate the molecular details of PIKfyve’s involvement in HEV entry to determine their relationship with endosomal escape of the viral genome and its interactions with other host factors, such as integrins and autophagy-related pathways. Additionally, studies should explore whether PIKfyve inhibitors can effectively target HEV strains with varying tissue tropisms or genotypes, including those with narrower host ranges, such as HEV-1 and HEV-2.

Given the limitations and side effects of current HEV treatments, PIKfyve inhibitors offer a promising alternative. In PHH, which mimics the in vivo environment of HEV replication, apilimod exhibited dose-dependent antiviral activity while preserving cell viability, highlighting its translational potential. Although nanomolar potency was observed in HepG2/C3A cells, the apparent IC₅₀ in PHH was shifted to the low micromolar range, likely due to the greater metabolic capacity, transporter activity and protein binding of primary hepatocytes, which reduce effective intracellular drug concentrations.59 60 While our RNAi experiments implicate PIKfyve in the HEV replication cycle, the stronger effect observed with apilimod may reflect both incomplete knockdown, leaving residual enzyme activity to support infection, and the possible involvement of additional, hitherto uncharacterised targets. Supporting on-target activity, two other PIKfyve inhibitors produced comparable antiviral effects. At the same time, these results are consistent with the well-established principle that bioactive small molecules can engage multiple biochemical pathways.61 62 Nonetheless, these observations do not diminish the observed antiviral potency but highlight that multiple mechanisms may contribute to the effects of apilimod in cellular systems.

The efficacy of apilimod was further validated in vivo using an athymic nude ratHEV infection model. Daily apilimod treatment significantly reduced viral RNA levels in faecal samples and viral loads in the liver, spleen and intestine, with no observable adverse effects, emphasising its potential as a safe and effective therapeutic candidate. These findings underscore the role of PIKfyve as a key host factor for HEV entry and its suitability as a target for antiviral therapy and align with recent studies which demonstrated the antiviral effects of apilimod on influenza virus and respiratory syncytial virus in mice.56 However, it is important to note that apilimod treatment in our study was administered prophylactically. It remains uncertain whether apilimod would demonstrate similar efficacy in animals with established ratHEV infection, characterised by high viral RNA levels in the liver and other tissues. To address this, future studies should explore strategies to enhance and sustain antiviral efficacy, such as increasing the dose or dosing frequency (if safety allows), or combining apilimod with other agents, such as RBV to simultaneously target distinct stages of the HEV replication cycle.

Importantly, the IC₅₀ of apilimod determined in our study (25 nM, approximately 10 ng/mL) falls well below the plasma concentrations achieved in human clinical trials, at which no significant systemic toxicity has been reported. Depending on dosage and patient cohort, maximum plasma concentrations (Cmax) of approximately 192 ng/mL have been described,63 64 indicating that clinically administered doses can achieve exposures exceeding the antiviral IC₅₀. Across its clinical development, more than 700 subjects have been treated with apilimod, which has generally been well tolerated, with the majority of adverse effects being mild to moderate.65 Overall, these data support the presence of a potential therapeutic window for apilimod and indicate that the antiviral potency observed in vitro is compatible with clinically achievable, well-tolerated systemic exposures. In conclusion, our study identifies PIKfyve as an important host factor for HEV entry and highlights the therapeutic potential of PIKfyve inhibitors. By targeting a conserved host pathway critical for HEV entry, this approach offers a promising alternative to existing antiviral strategies and may guide novel treatments for HEV and potentially other pathogens reliant on PIKfyve-mediated endosomal trafficking.

Strengths and limitations

The consistent antiviral effects of apilimod observed in vitro and in vivo underscore a pivotal role for PIKfyve during HEV entry and highlight the translational potential of repurposing this clinically evaluated compound with a well-established safety profile. However, as with most small-molecule inhibitors, potential off-target effects of apilimod cannot be entirely excluded, and additional pathways may contribute to the observed antiviral activity. While the ratHEV model provides important in vivo validation, apilimod was administered prophylactically, and its efficacy in animals with established infection and high viral RNA levels in liver and extrahepatic tissues remains uncertain. Future studies should therefore evaluate therapeutic treatment regimens, investigate dose optimisation or combination strategies with agents such as RBV and assess the long-term impact of PIKfyve inhibition on hepatic physiology.

Conclusion

In summary, our study identifies the endolysosomal kinase PIKfyve as a critical host factor required during HEV entry. Pharmacological inhibition of PIKfyve by apilimod potently suppressed HEV infection in vitro and reduced viral burden in a ratHEV infection model in vivo. Mechanistically, our findings suggest that PIKfyve inhibition disrupts late endosome/lysosome organisation and proteolytic function, thereby impairing the formation of functional lysosomal compartments required for efficient HEV trafficking, uncoating and entry. Together, these findings underscore the importance of endolysosomal trafficking in the HEV life cycle and demonstrate that targeting host pathways essential for HEV entry represents a viable antiviral strategy. Given the established clinical safety profile of apilimod, repurposing PIKfyve inhibitors may offer a promising therapeutic approach for HEV infection and warrants further evaluation in therapeutic treatment settings.
