Assessing characteristics of populations seen at Commission on Cancer facilities using Pennsylvania linked data
Authors: Kristine G Reed, Zhaojun Sun, Jonathan G Yabes, Coleman Drake, Nicole Ober, Bruce Jacobs, G J van Londen, Cathy J Bradley, Lindsay M Sabik
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractCommission on Cancer (CoC) accreditation certifies facilities provide quality care. We assessed differences among patients who do and do not visit CoC facilities using Pennsylvania Cancer Registry data linked to facility records for patients diagnosed with cancer between 2018 and 2019 (n = 87 472). Predicted probabilities from multivariable logistic regression indicated patients in the most advantaged Area Deprivation Index quartiles were more likely to visit CoC facilities (78.0%, 95% confidence interval [CI] = 77.5% to 78.6%) compared with other quartiles. Urban patients (74.1%, 95% CI = 73.8% to 74.4%) were more likely than rural to be seen at a CoC facility (62.7%, 95% CI = 61.2% to 64.2%) as were Hispanic patients (88.0%, 95% CI = 86.7% to 89.3%) and non-Hispanic Black patients (79.1%, 95% CI = 78.1% to 80.0%) compared with White patients (72.0%, 95% CI = 71.7% to 72.4%). Differences in demographics suggest CoC data may underrepresent some groups, including low-income and rural patients.

Disparities in cancer care access and quality by race and ethnicity, geographic location, and income are well documented (1). Professional organizations such as the American College of Surgeons’ Commission on Cancer (CoC) develop accreditation standards to ensure consistent high-quality care (2). Patients treated at CoC-accredited facilities are more likely to receive guideline-concordant care (3). Yet, approximately 30% of patients with cancer in the United States do not receive care at CoC-accredited hospitals (4). To our knowledge, no prior studies have compared patients seen at CoC facilities with those not seen at CoC-accredited facilities using patient-level data. Differences between these groups may shed light on care disparities and have implications for interpretation of data from the National Cancer Database (NCDB), which include only patients seen in CoC facilities.

We used data from the Pennsylvania Cancer Registry (PCR) merged with Pennsylvania Health Care Cost Containment Council data. Facilities were classified as CoC-affiliated on the basis of the American College of Surgeons website in 2020 (5). We included patients aged 18 years and older identified in the PCR with a first primary cancer diagnosis between 2018 and 2019 and at least 1 Pennsylvania Health Care Cost Containment Council cancer-related visit record within 365 days of cancer diagnosis. Exclusion criteria were non-Pennsylvania residence, multiple primary cancers, missing or invalid demographic data, or diagnosis at death. Area-level socioeconomic deprivation was assessed using the Area Deprivation Index (ADI) on the basis of 2010 patient census tract of residence and categorized into quartiles (6,7).

We evaluated factors associated with at least 1 cancer-related visit to a CoC-accredited facility using multivariable logistic analysis. Covariates obtained from PCR were cancer type, age, stage, race and ethnicity, sex, ADI rank quartile, and rurality of residence based on rural-urban commuting area codes (8). Race and ethnicity were categorized as Hispanic, non-Hispanic Black, non-Hispanic Other, and non-Hispanic White. Non-Hispanic Other includes non-Hispanic Asian, Indigenous Western populations, Middle Eastern, Pacific Islander, or “Other, not otherwise specified” groups, which were combined because of small sample sizes. Likelihood ratio tests were used, and 2-sided P values less than .05 were considered statistically significant. Average predicted probabilities for treatment at a CoC hospital were estimated on the basis of model coefficients, holding all other variables at their observed distribution, with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) derived using the delta method. The University of Pittsburgh’s institutional review board reviewed and deemed the study exempt.

Patients residing in urban areas had a higher predicted probability of visiting a CoC facility (74.1%, 95% CI = 73.8% to 74.4% patients) compared with patients from large towns (71.4%, 95% CI = 70.5% to 72.4% patients) or rural areas (62.7%, 95% CI = 61.2% to 64.2% patients). Patients living in more advantaged neighborhoods on the basis of ADI had a higher probability of being seen in a CoC-accredited facility compared with other patients. The most advantaged quartile (ADI quartile 1) had a probability of 78.0% (95% CI = 77.5% to 78.6%) compared with the least advantaged quartile (ADI quartile 4) with a probability of 68.4% (95% CI = 67.8% to 69.1%). Across racial and ethnic groups, Hispanic patients had the highest probability of being seen in a CoC facility (88.0%, 95% CI = 86.7% to 89.3%), followed by non-Hispanic Black patients (79.1%, 95% CI = 78.1% to 80.0%), with the lowest probability among non-Hispanic White patients (72.0%, 95% CI = 71.7% to 72.4%).

CoC hospitals provide high-quality cancer care; however, approximately 30% of patients do not receive any care at CoC facilities (1). We assessed demographics of CoC and non-CoC facility patient populations in Pennsylvania. Patients who were younger, living in urban areas, and residing in more advantaged neighborhoods on the basis of ADI had a higher probability of being seen at CoC facilities. Hispanic patients and non-Hispanic Black patients were also more likely to be seen in CoC facilities relative to non-Hispanic White patients; however, this may be because of a higher proportion of non-White patients in our sample residing in urban locations, where CoC facilities tend to be located (Supplementary Table 1, available online).

Other studies have reported characteristics of CoC facilities and composition of patient populations in NCDB data. One study found that CoC facilities were more likely to be academic medical centers, have more beds, perform more operations, and offer more cancer-related services. The facilities were less likely to be critical access hospitals and to be located in rural areas (9). Studies comparing NCDB with other registry data note differences in race and ethnicity and age between NCDB and population-based sources (10,11). To our knowledge, this is the first study to use individual patient-level information from a large population-based registry to compare characteristics of patients who are and are not seen at CoC facilities.

Our study has limitations. First, we assessed CoC status in 2020, although the sample included patients diagnosed in 2018-2019. Changes in CoC accreditation between 2018 and 2020 are not captured. Second, we consider whether patients have any cancer-related visit to a CoC facility but do not assess where they receive cancer treatments. Third, data are from a single state so do not include care received outside of Pennsylvania and may not be generalizable to other states. Further, differences along the rural-urban continuum not captured by our data may explain some findings; rural residents are more likely to be aged 65 years or older, and most Hispanic residents in Pennsylvania tend to live in urban areas (12). Nonetheless, Pennsylvania is the fifth largest state by population and parallels many national demographic distributions (13). Understanding the complex relationship between race and ethnicity, geography, and access to cancer care in other regions and with more detailed data is an important area for future research.

Our study demonstrated important demographic differences between patients who do and do not visit CoC-accredited facilities, highlighting health-care access inequities and potential underrepresentation in the NCDB. The NCDB is used extensively in cancer research (14) and in the development of National Comprehensive Cancer Network treatment guidelines (15). Underrepresentation of older and lower income groups could lead to NCDB samples that are not representative of the general population in terms of comorbidities, social context, and other important characteristics. Research using the NCDB and similar sources derived from more specialized or well-resourced institutions should be interpreted with these differences in mind. Further, future research should consider access to CoC hospitals nationally, work to identify groups that are less likely to be seen at CoC facilities but would benefit from specialized care, and examine potential expansions to commonly used data sources to increase the generalizability of research.
