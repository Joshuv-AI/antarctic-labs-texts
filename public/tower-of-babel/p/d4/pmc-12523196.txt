Text-based messaging to support rheumatoid arthritis care: an analysis of frequency and content of text-messages
Authors: Melissa Sipley, Saania N Zafar, Manuel Ester, Glen Hazlewood, Kiran Dhiman, Alexandra Charlton, Karen L Then, Erika Dempsey, Richard Lester, Alison M Hoens, Diane Lacaille, Sarah Sloss, Cheryl Barnabe, Dianne Mosher, Claire E H Barber
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveText-based messaging support can improve rheumatoid arthritis (RA) care delivery by connecting patients with healthcare providers (HCPs) in an efficient and convenient manner. However, the nature and appropriateness of patient messaging in this new care model are unknown. The aim of this study was to (1) analyze the frequency and nature of text messages patients sent to their HCPs, and (2) to identify patient characteristics associated with higher texting frequency in a pilot of a text-based messaging (using the WelTel platform) added to usual rheumatology care.MethodsSeventy patients with RA participated in a 6-month pilot. Automated “How are you?” texts were sent monthly, and patients were encouraged to respond according to their current situation. Qualitative content analysis was conducted to thematically categorize and quantify common words and phrases. Regression analysis was conducted to determine if a relationship existed between the number of text messages and age, sex, care complexity (using a validated instrument), number of medications, and burden of comorbidities.ResultsA total of 1404 text messages were sent by patients, with 257 messages requiring a response. Three main themes for texting topics emerged: RA symptom reporting, medication management, and COVID-19 questions. Patients with higher care complexity had a higher frequency of texting (p = 0.025); however, no association was observed with other patient characteristics.ConclusionPatients with higher complexity texted HCPs more frequently. Messages were highly aligned with patient care needs. Future directions should include assessing the impact of text messaging-enhanced care on patient outcomes and overall healthcare utilization.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Keywords: Rheumatoid arthritis, Digital Health, Quality of Care

Introduction

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a chronic, autoimmune inflammatory joint disease characterized by joint pain, inflammation, stiffness, and swelling [1, 2]. Without treatment, individuals living with RA can experience a decreased quality of life due to permanent joint damage, and long-term disability [3]. RA treatment centers around early diagnosis and the treat-to-target (T2T) approach which aims to reduce disease activity and prevent disease progression and joint damage [1, 2, 4]. The T2T strategy requires regular follow-up appointments with rheumatologists to monitor patients’ disease activity. However, this may be difficult to achieve due to factors such as physician and patient schedules and a limited rheumatology workforce [5]. Furthermore, patients living with RA may have sociodemographic, clinical, and treatment characteristics that can contribute to the complexity of care and need for increased healthcare supports and/or clinical visits [6].

Virtual care can be leveraged to optimize care for patients between regularly scheduled in-person rheumatology visits. Virtual care utilization has been steadily increasing in Canada since the 1970s, with a marked uptake in 2020 due to the COVID-19 pandemic [7]. Some advantages of virtual care include accessibility of care for rural patients, timely access to care, and decreased travel burden for patients, which is particularly relevant for working individuals who need to take time off-work, older adults and those with disabilities [8].

Multiple methods of virtual care have been evaluated in rheumatology such as traditional telemedicine, videoconferencing, and newer mobile health approaches [9, 10]. Text-messaging is one of the most used modalities of virtual communication overall, including among older adults, making it a highly accessible format for virtual care [11, 12]. Due to its widespread use, virtual care using text messaging has been studied in other populations (e.g. tuberculosis treatment and solid organ transplant) to support individuals with complex treatment regimens [13, 14]. Some of the benefits of using text messaging include the asynchronous short-message format, allowing both parties to reply when convenient, and having a written conversation log that can be referenced later [11, 12]. Some of the concerns pertaining to two-way text-messaging is the increased response burden for healthcare providers (HCPs), and differences in this technologies’ utilization depending on patient characteristics [15, 16].

There is little research investigating the use of simple text-messaging in rheumatology care [11]. The previous studies using text-messaging to manage RA have employed limited two-way automated text-messaging, where the clinic sends health surveys and/or reminders to patients, and they have pre-set options to reply [17, 18]. Although limited two-way automated messaging is an efficient way to collect or provide information, there is no room for conversations to assess and address patient needs, in contrast to conventional two-way text messaging. To our knowledge, the present study is the first to pilot the use of traditional two-way text-messaging to provide interactive virtual care to individuals with RA between regularly scheduled follow-up appointments [19].

We conducted a pilot study using text-messaging to enhance care for RA using the WelTel platform. WelTel is a secure platform used in healthcare specific settings to connect patients with their healthcare provider (HCPs) via two-way SMS text-messaging [20]. We have previously reported on the experiences of both patients and providers using text-messaging to support high quality RA care [19]. Major findings showed that participating patients supported the use of text-messaging to enhance care quality. However, some non-adopting HCPs expressed concern that it could increase their workload, posing a potential barrier to adopting it routinely [19]. Therefore, the present study aims to analyze the frequency and nature of text messages patients sent to their HCPs and to identify which patient characteristics were associated with higher text volumes. These findings will be used to inform future implementation efforts including workforce planning to best support patient care needs in between rheumatology in-person assessments.

Patients and methods

WelTel pilot overview

The current study is a prospective evaluation of a pilot study using SMS text-based messaging using the WelTel platform to optimize on-demand rheumatology care between scheduled appointments [19]. This study was approved by the Human Ethics Conjoint Health Research Ethics Board at the University of Calgary (ethics certificate REB20-0422).

Patients diagnosed with RA were recruited from two university-affiliated, multi-provider rheumatology clinics. Convenience sampling was conducted using in-clinic recruitment, mail-out flyers, and emails sent to patients who consented to be contacted for future studies as part of the Rheum4U Precision Health Registry. Recruitment was staged to support clinician onboarding and the sample size was limited due to reluctance of some HCPs to participate due to concerns about increased workload [19]. Eligible participants were at least 18 years old, diagnosed with RA, and had access to a mobile device. Participants were permitted to use proxies (e.g., family member) to text on their behalf, however, none were utilized in this study. The duration of the pilot was 6-months with the first participant enrolled in August 2021 and the last participant enrolled in May 2022. At baseline, patients completed informed consent forms. Patients also filled out specific patient-reported measures at baseline, 3-months, and 6-months. At the end of the pilot, patients were invited to complete a survey and/or interview regarding their experience with the WelTel platform [19]. The evaluation of patient and provider experiences with the pilot was published elsewhere [19].

Use of the WelTel platform to support text-messaging enhanced care

Figure 1 depicts a flow diagram of the model of care. At the time that this pilot was conducted, virtual communication options for patients and HCPs were not available. Patients would normally contact the rheumatology clinic by phone and were directed to the appropriate provider (rheumatologist or nurse case manager) for consultation. Participants enrolled in the study received a welcome text that informed them about the standard response time of 24–36 h excluding weekends and statutory holidays. Patients then received an automated text message asking, “How are you?” from the WelTel platform on the first Monday of every month for 6-months. If they did not respond to the “How are you?” text, patients received the same text next month. If they did not respond for 12 weeks, an automated follow-up text was sent saying, “We haven’t talked for 12 weeks, how are you doing?”. If no response was given, this message was sent once a week until a response was received.

WelTel intervention overview

As this study was conducted during the COVID-19 pandemic, information about vaccine dose eligibility and recommendations, treatments for immunocompromised individuals, and links to provincial health services information about vaccines and testing were periodically sent to all patients.

Patients also received a text-message containing a link for the Patient Activity Scale-II (PAS-II) survey at 3 and 6-months to monitor disease activity. The PAS-II is a self-reported instrument used to assess disease activity [21]. If a patient’s PAS-II score indicated moderate or high disease activity, a text would be sent to the patient by the clinical pharmacist for the rheumatology clinic (AC) to address this. A text message reminder was sent one week from when the questionnaire was initially sent if it was not completed. Figure 2 depicts the assessment and management of moderate disease activity.

Actions taken for moderate disease activity

Patients had the option to respond according to their current condition. They could also send additional messages anytime and were advised to continue calling the clinic directly if needed. Messages were primarily monitored by the participating pharmacist (AC) from Monday to Friday between 8am and 5pm. The clinical pharmacist is specialized in rheumatology care and is able to address a broad range of patient concerns beyond medication-related questions. If there were messages that fell beyond her scope, she directed them to the appropriate physician for follow-up. Patients were advised not to use WelTel as an emergency service. Patients were also informed that they could stop receiving text-messages at any time and should they wish to resume, they could contact the study coordinator to re-enroll.

Data collection

Baseline demographics

Demographic information was collected via participant surveys and electronic medical record (EMR) chart review. Patients completed baseline surveys using Qualtrics [22] to report on gender, ethnicity, and education. EMR chart reviews were conducted by two research team members (KD, SS) to obtain further demographic and disease information and to minimize participant burden. A standardized EMR chart review tool was developed in REDCap (hosted by the Clinical Research Unit, Cumming School of Medicine, University of Calgary) [23, 24], and pilot-tested prior to use. Thirty-seven charts were reviewed in duplicate, and all discrepancies were addressed and resolved through discussion. Information collected in EMR chart reviews included age, sex, urban/rural location, date of diagnosis, number of rheumatology visits over the last 12 months, and current medication (steroids, non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, first-line disease modifying antirheumatic drugs (DMARDs), targeted DMARDs, and biologic DMARDs).

Care complexity and disease activity

The baseline survey consisted of the Inter-Med Self-Assessment (IMSA) questionnaire [25] and Patient Activity Scale-II [21]. The IMSA questionnaire is a validated patient-reported instrument that is used to assess biopsychosocial complexity and healthcare needs [25]. This questionnaire consists of 20 questions across four domains (biological, psychosocial, social, and health system) and three time points (historical, current state, and future vulnerability and prognosis). The IMSA questionnaire provides a total score out of 60. The patient is considered to have high complexity if the IMSA score reaches the pre-defined cut-off value of 19 [25]. The PAS-II was also collected at 3 and 6-months as part of the text-messaging intervention, as described above.

Comorbidity burden

Disease activity (Clinical Disease Activity Index (CDAI) [26] or Disease Activity Score 28 (DAS28)), based on medical chart data, were reported by treating physicians or calculated by the research team. Comorbidity burden was ascertained by examining problem lists and documented using the Rheumatic Disease Comorbidity Index (RDCI) [27], with scores ranging from 0 to 9, with 0 implying the absence of any listed comorbidity.

WelTel platform/text-messages

The WelTel platform automatically recorded text message metadata; number of messages sent and received, dates and timestamps, written content, turn-around time to respond to patient texts, and the name of the provider that responded to the patient’s concern. To account for differences in participant texting behaviours, multiple messages that were sent consecutively containing information about the same concern were counted as one message. The total number of messages patients sent were counted, however, only those requiring a response from the participating pharmacist were analyzed as themes for evaluating whether certain patient characteristics contributed to higher text message frequency.

Data analysis

To analyze the text messages, qualitative content analysis and quantitative analysis was used. Analyses were completed using NVivo 12 (2020, Lumivero). After de-identifying text messages, important phrases were coded to determine patterns and trends of words used [28, 29]. Codes were generated by MS and reviewed by three members of the research team (CEHB, AC, KD). Patient’s text message conversations were thematically categorized and quantified. Discussions consisted of reviewing text messages in each theme and deciding whether text messages were appropriately categorized. Text messages were first coded and categorized into the four main themes. Sub-themes were created to further specify the concern of each text message. For example, patients experiencing pain, fatigue, swelling, etc. were first categorized into an RA symptoms category. Once all RA symptom text messages were categorized, sub-themes were created to further define each symptom. Finally, codes in each theme and sub-theme were counted to determine frequencies.

Descriptive statistics were used to analyze patient demographic characteristics, volume of patient text messages that required a response, and the average response time to text-messages based on system timestamps. We used univariable linear regression analyses [30] to explore the relationship between number of participant text-messages sent and variables which were hypothesized to impact text-messaging volume including age, sex, number of medications taken at baseline, IMSA scores, and number of comorbidities. Statistical analysis was conducted in STATA (version 18).

Volume of text-messages

Patients sent a total of 1404 text messages to the designated healthcare provider during a 6-month study period. After accounting for multiple consecutive messages about the same topic area and removing messages that indicated the patient was okay (e.g. “I’m ok”, “I’m fine”, etc.), there were 257 text messages indicating concern and requiring a response by the participating pharmacist. The median number of total text-messages sent by patients requiring an HCP response during their 6-month enrollment in the study was 11 (IQR: 7–20) (Fig. 3). The boxplots in Fig. 4 show the distribution of text-messages patients sent per month. Patients sent the most text messages in month 3 with a median of 2 (IQR: 1-4.75) of the study and sent the fewest in month 6 with a median of 1 (IQR: 1–2). The participating pharmacist took a median of 3.19 h (IQR: 1.60–5.85) to respond to a text-message over the course of the WelTel pilot.

Patient characteristics associated with higher text message volumes

Results from the univariable regression analyses are shown in Table 2. Older age, number of medications, number of comorbidities, and sex differences were not significantly associated with higher text messaging volumes (p > 0.05). However, higher IMSA scores, indicating higher patient care complexity, were significantly associated with higher text messaging volume. For every 1 unit increase in IMSA score, the average number of text messages sent by patients increased by 0.67 texts (p = 0.025, CI: 0.09–1.27).

Main themes from text-message analysis

Among the text-messages that required a response from a healthcare provider, four themes emerged: (1) reporting of RA symptoms and requests for advice on RA management, (2) support with medication management and side effects, (3) COVID-19 management and prevention, and (4) assistance with management of other health concerns. These were further categorized into sub-themes to capture the most common concerns. Figure 3 illustrates the frequencies of text-messages in each theme and sub-theme.

Texting topic 1: RA symptom report and requests for advice to help manage

The most common theme determined by counting codes was RA symptoms with 96 (40.3%) text-messages contributing to this theme. Commonly reported symptoms include pain, flare-ups, stiffness, and fatigue. A ‘multiple symptoms’ category was created for this theme since patients often reported experiencing multiple symptoms. The ‘other symptoms’ category included text messages with unique symptoms such as joint immobility and skin issues.

The following is an example text message from the “Multiple Symptoms” category. This participant is experiencing stiffness and fatigue.

Hands and feet are still pretty stiff in the morning but improving, I think! Fatigue comes and goes. (Participant 11)

Texting topic 2: Support with medication management and side effects

Eighty-eight (35.7%) of messages were regarding medications, with over 50% of the texts being questions. Common questions included dose clarification, dosage changes to treat increased symptoms, and new medication concerns. Other categories include insurance coverage, updates on patient response, side effects patients were experiencing, and prescription requests.

The following is an example text message from the “Medication Questions” category. This participant’s question pertains to medication dosages.

I need to clarify dosing for (hydroxychloroquine). Is it 2 pills for 4 days then 1 pill for 3 days or 2 pills 1 day then 1 pill the next day etc? Why not 1.5 pills every day? (Participant 51)

Texting topic 3: COVID-19 management and prevention

Forty-four (18.5%) text-messages were regarding COVID-19 with vaccine questions being the most common. These include inquiries about whether a participant should continue taking their medication while having COVID-19, symptoms they are experiencing, questions regarding COVID-19 tests, and pandemic-related concerns.

The following is an example text message from the “Vaccine Questions” category.

Do you recommend people who have RA get a booster COVID vaccine? (Participant 11)

Texting topic 4: Reporting and assistance with management of other concerns

Thirteen (5.46%) messages included other concerns that required a healthcare provider response but did not pertain to the three main themes. These include exercise recommendations, requests for standing laboratory orders, and comments about waiting for specialist care. This topic contained a smaller volume of text-messages, however, messages in this topic revealed that there was variation in experiences that patients had regarding their RA management.

The following is an example text message from the “Exercise” category. This participant is inquiring about exercise frequency and other exercise recommendations for RA and osteoarthritis management.

I have a question about exercise and my OA and RA. Is there anything that I might do to aggravate either? I have OA in my hips and RA all over; I am doing stretching and yoga and each day I either walk 6km or I do 50 min of aquasize. Does this sound reasonable? Is there anything I should watch for? So far I’ve noticed my left hip is a bit stiff and sore. Does it matter? (Participant 2)

Discussion

This study is part of the evaluation of the WelTel text-messaging pilot, a novel approach to virtual RA care. Herein, we evaluated the frequency and nature of the text messages requiring a response and investigated whether certain patient characteristics were associated with increased text volume. No association was found between demographic factors or medical burden (i.e. comorbidity, medications) and the volume of text-messages. However, patients with higher care complexity scores sent significantly more text-messages during the pilot. The four major types of text messages were related to symptom reporting and management advice, managing medications and side effects, COVID-19 concerns, and assistance with other concerns.

To our surprise, demographic factors and medical burden did not impact the volume of text messages requiring follow-up during the study. Although some associations between age, number of medications, comorbidity burden, or sex and volume of text-messages have been reported in prior virtual care interventions for individuals with chronic diseases, several possible explanations exist for our contrary findings [31, 32]. One of the reasons may be due to the number of years patients in our study had lived with RA. With an average of over 7 years since diagnosis, patients may have already been accustomed to and educated on what they can do to help manage their RA. While this study did not examine the relationship between participant education level and text-messaging frequency, prior research suggests that higher education levels are associated with lower messaging frequency [33]. Given the high education level of our participants, they may have used text messaging more strategically, contacting HCPs only when necessary. The volume of text-messages may be higher among newly diagnosed RA patients. Additionally, older average age and limited age range of patients in this study, personal factors (e.g. texting habits with family members), and timing of the study during the COVID-19 pandemic may have played a greater role in determining the volume of text-messaging.

Interestingly, we found that IMSA care complexity scores were significantly associated with increased text messaging. The IMSA complexity score takes a holistic approach to care complexity by including biological, psychological, social, and health system level factors in the past, current, and future states. Patients with RA often have additional complexity due to comorbidities, and additional psychosocial needs [34]. Patients with higher care complexity in the present study often had additional RA-related questions and other complex care needs (e.g. psychological factors). As the IMSA is a composite score across multiple care complexity domains, future research is needed to understand which domains contribute to higher volume of text-messages. This could inform tailored patient selection for message-based RA care, thus better matching available resources to patient needs.

Text messages received during the WelTel intervention revealed four main categories of concerns: RA symptom tracking and management, medications and side effects, COVID-19, and managing other concerns. The reporting of RA symptoms and requesting advice was common in our text-messaging population. This finding is similar to those of other studies exploring text-messaging in chronic disease populations. A study analyzing the content of text-messaging in patients with cardiovascular disease and type 2 diabetes also labelled one of their categories of replies as “reporting struggle”, in which patients would indicate difficulties with their treatment and/or disease [35]. Our study also categorized text messages as “support with medication management and side effects”. This may be due to the variety of drugs that are often prescribed for patients with RA. Other studies have explored medication use and text-messaging, however these are often medication adherence studies. This was not assessed in our study. The content of text messages highlights the value of virtual care for addressing urgent concerns, as well as the need for improved access to high-quality resources that empower patients in their RA management.

A category dedicated to COVID-19 management and prevention is to be expected as the WelTel pilot was conducted during the COVID-19 pandemic, a period of health uncertainty for many. As COVID-19 continues to be an area of concern for individuals living with RA, risk management for this and other infectious diseases should continue to be emphasized as part of their care [36].

The present study was not without limitations. One drawback of text message data is the variable text messaging habits between patients (e.g. less frequent longer messages versus daily short messages) that may have led to overemphasis or underemphasis of certain topics within the analyses. Since the majority of text messages were answered by a pharmacist, participants may have intentionally directed their concerns, specifically about medications, to the pharmacist, leading to an overestimation of concerns relating to medications. Further, automated text messages sent with information about COVID-19 vaccines may have also biased the frequency and number of text messages regarding this topic. As this was a pilot study with a small sample size, it may have been underpowered to detect meaningful associations using regression analyses. Our sample was a well-educated, primarily white, urban sample with well-established disease. A key limitation to our convenience sampling method is that it may have restricted the diversity of the study population. Individuals from remote and rural communities, as well as other groups (e.g. Indigenous populations), and individuals in early stages of disease remained underrepresented, limiting generalizability of text messaging frequency to these populations. Patients from remote locations or diverse backgrounds often have more complex healthcare needs, which may impact the appropriateness of and experiences with text-message enhanced care. However, we acknowledge that texting services may be especially valuable for patients experiencing difficulty accessing their healthcare providers due to long wait times, limited clinic hours, and geographic distance. Additionally, our eligibility requirement excluded individuals with language barriers or those unable to use a phone and don’t have access to a proxy. These populations often have more complex healthcare needs, and their exclusion further limits the accessibility of text messaging services and the generalizability of our findings. Although this study was restricted to a sample of RA patients in Alberta, WelTel has been used successfully in other contexts including tuberculosis and HIV populations in Canada and Kenya [37–40]. Potential research next steps would be to conduct a pilot study in other rheumatology care centres and in different populations. Additional implementation next steps would be to scale up the text-messaging intervention.

The present study piloted the novel use of text messaging using the WelTel virtual care platform for providing additional RA care in between rheumatologist visits. Our analysis identified the common concerns that patients raise with their care team via messaging. The content of text-messages received was highly relevant and directly related to patient care needs. Patient care complexity was associated with significantly more text-messages to discuss health concerns, highlighting a population who may benefit in particular from the intervention. From a value-based care perspective, text messaging may help prevent avoidable visits and improve outcomes without increasing clinical volume. In an interdisciplinary care model, this has the potential to enhance efficiency by directing concerns to the appropriate provider and reducing physician burden. Additional studies are needed to formally assess the impact of text-messaging enhanced care on patient outcomes such as disease activity, quality of life, and healthcare utilization. As an example, future research could compare treatment outcomes between patients who receive text message support and those who do not, to evaluate the potential clinical benefits of this intervention. Future work should also explore the changes in workload experienced by HCPs and how this model of care can be implemented in local and broader contexts to support effective use of text messaging for rheumatology care.
