Structural dynamics of kappa opioid receptor interactions with β-arrestin 1
Authors: Jianming Han, Eve J Fine, Qianru Jiang, Yuxuan Zhuang, Carl-Mikael Suomivuori, Zi-Wei Chen, Elizabeth Denn, Kyle Whiddon, Kunpeng Li, Alex S Evers, James Fuller, Joshua Carter, Jonathan F Fay, Muyuan Chen, Ron O Dror, Tao Che
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOpioid receptors signal through Gi/o protein and β-arrestin pathways that mediate distinct effects of opiate drugs. While opioid binding and G protein activation are well studied, β-arrestin recruitment remains poorly understood. Here, we determine the complex structure of the kappa opioid receptor (KOR) with β-arrestin1 (βarr1) at 2.60 Å resolution using cryogenic electron microscopy. Structural and mass spectrometry analyses reveal multiple phosphorylation sites and a phospholipid-binding site that specifically enhances arrestin recruitment. The KOR-βarr1 complex adopts a core interaction and exhibits notable differences from other GPCR-βarr1 complexes. Comparisons with the structures of KOR-Nb39 and KOR-Gi1 complexes also reveal distinct structural features in the orthosteric binding site and the KOR-transducer interface that affect signaling bias. Using extensive 3D variation analysis and molecular dynamics simulations, we identify a range of conformational dynamics in both the receptor and βarr1, suggesting an allosteric pathway for arrestin’s entry and exit.Subject terms: G protein-coupled receptors, Cell signalling, Cryoelectron microscopy

Introduction

Opioid receptors are membrane proteins that belong to a subclass of G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs). So far, this family includes four major members, delta, mu, kappa, and nociception receptors (DOR, MOR, KOR, and NOP, respectively)1. Like many other GPCRs, the opioid receptor activation opens downstream signaling by coupling to G protein heterotrimers and β-arrestins and produces different behavioral effects, including beneficial analgesia and adverse abuse liability2. A long-debated topic in opioid receptor research is whether the desirable analgesic effect mediated by G protein signaling can be separated from the undesirable side effects mediated by β-arrestins3,4. Based on this so-called biased signaling hypothesis, several G protein-biased agonists at opioid receptors have been developed, including TRV130, a MOR G protein-biased agonist recently approved by the FDA5. Other studies have challenged the hypothesis that β-arrestins are the culprit of opioid-mediated side effects6–8, and have proposed that other pharmacological properties (e.g., low efficacy) could be the main factor contributing to safety9. Indeed, while it is generally accepted that the beneficial analgesia is mediated by opioid receptor-mediated G protein signaling10, how opioid receptors engage β-arrestins and the functional consequence of opioid-mediated β-arrestin signaling is not well understood.

To address this question, we use KOR as a model system. The KOR is predominantly expressed in pain-related neurons, and KOR-targeting drugs are not associated with addiction or overdose-related death11. The lack of rewarding/euphorigenic effects initially encouraged the development of KOR agonists as non-addictive analgesics12. However, mood disorders such as dysphoria and psychotomimesis have been frequently observed with KOR agonists, which has limited their therapeutic application13,14. Previous studies have demonstrated that the arrestin signaling pathway is involved in KOR-induced dysphoria15, and KOR-biased agonists that preferentially activate the G protein signaling pathway over the arrestin pathway appear to produce effective analgesia without dysphoria16,17. Opioid receptors like KOR primarily bind to β-arrestin1 (βarr1) and β-arrestin2 (βarr2). Previous studies have defined the functions of arrestins in regulating GPCR desensitization, endocytosis, and intracellular trafficking18–20. Beyond these roles, arrestins are involved in the control of multiple cellular signaling pathways as scaffolding proteins21. Whereas the structures of KOR in its inactive state with antagonists22 or active state with a nanobody23 or Gi/o subtypes24,25 have been helpful in understanding the receptor activation and G protein signaling, the structure of the KOR-arrestin complex and the molecular determinants essential for arrestin signaling are still mostly unknown, leading to a poor understanding of the arrestin pathway in opioid receptor signaling.

Here we report the complex structure of KOR coupled to βarr1 using cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM). The structure reveals that βarr1 can adopt receptor-specific orientation to accommodate different GPCRs. This specificity is mediated by the conformational plasticity of the finger loop and C-loop in βarr1 that can fit specific interfaces in GPCRs. Compared to the KOR-G protein-bound state, βarr1 engages different intracellular regions of KOR that may contribute to the signaling bias during KOR activation. Through extensive 3D variation and MD simulations analysis, different intermediate states in KOR and βarr1 are captured, which provide insights into pre-coupling and post-coupling states of KOR-βarr1 interactions.

Results

Strategies for a stable complex of KOR coupled to a βarr1 isoform

The stabilization of GPCR-arrestin complexes has presented a longstanding challenge because of the requisite receptor phosphorylation26 and the weak affinity of the seven-transmembrane domain-arrestin interaction. Optimization of the KOR-βarr1 complex preparation for structural studies required considerable protein engineering. We began with the evaluation of KOR agonists and five subtypes of GPCR kinases (GRKs) on the βarr1 recruitment to KOR and observed that the epoxymorphinan MP1104 is the most potent (Supplementary Fig. 1a, and Supplementary Table S1) and GRK2, GRK3 and GRK5 are more efficacious in arrestin recruitment than other GRKs (Fig. 1a, and Supplementary Table S2). Given the high sequence identity between GRK2 and GRK32, and the previous success with GRK2 and GRK527, we chose the GRK2-GRK5 combination to phosphorylate the receptor. To investigate receptor phosphorylation by GRK2 and GRK5, we performed mass spectrometry analysis and identified the phosphorylation at T8812.48 and S2555.67 as well as at S356, T357, S358 and T363 in the C-terminus of KOR (Supplementary Fig. 1b). Single-point mutations at these sites confirmed their critical roles in βarr1 recruitment (Fig. 1b, Supplementary Fig. 1c and d, Supplementary Table S3). Interestingly, mass spectrometry also identified a phosphorylated residue T366 in βarr1 (supplementary Fig. 1e). However, the T366A mutation had minimal effect on βarr1 recruitment at KOR, even though it could increase the expression level of βarr1 (Supplementary Fig. 1f and g, Supplementary Table S4). Because phosphorylated wild-type KOR only forms a small fraction of complex with βarr1 (Supplementary Fig. 2a), we fused the C-terminal KOR with the tail of vasopressin V2 receptor (V2R tail), which is known to promote the formation of GPCR-βarr1 complexes. As expected, V2R tail substantially increased the efficacy of MP1104-stimulated KOR-mediated βarr1 recruitment (Supplementary Fig. 2b and c). It is worth noting that, while the fusion of the V2R tail enhanced βarr1 recruitment to KOR by introducing additional phosphorylation sites, it appears to alter the kinetics of βarr1 engagement likely due to the sequential phosphorylation events on the V2R tail (Supplementary Fig. 2d).

Previous GPCR-βarr1 structures are frequently observed with an exaggerated tilt of βarr1 due to interactions between the C-edge loop and the hydrophobic interfaces on detergent micelles or nanodiscs28,29. Interestingly, βarr1 in nature has two similar isoforms, an isoform including 419 residues (isoform 1) and an isoform with 411 residues lacking C-edge loop 2 (isoform 2) (Supplementary Fig. 3). To minimize the potential exaggerated tilt of βarr1, we selected βarr1 isoform 2 to examine the interactions between KOR and βarr1 (Supplementary Fig. 3).

To enhance the complex stability, we included Fab30 and Nb32, which have been reported to stabilize the active state of βarr1 and to facilitate cryo-EM particle alignment for the structural determination30,31; We also added the membrane phosphoinositide PIP2 (4,5-bisphosphate (diC8-Ptdlns (4,5) P2), which has been shown to promote βarrestin activation and recruitment32. Incubation of KOR-βarr1 with Fab30, Nb32, and PIP2 led to a stable complex as confirmed by size-exclusion chromatography (Supplementary Fig. 4a) and negative staining imaging (Supplementary Fig. 4b). Different from the V2R tail that significantly enhances the βarr1 recruitment, Fab30 has minimal effects in MP1104-mediated βarr1 recruitment to KOR, suggesting its primary role in restricting the dynamics of βarrestins (Supplementary Fig. 2b and c, Supplementary Table 5). Further cryo-EM data collection and analysis generated a density map at a resolution of 2.6 Å (Fig. 1c and Supplementary Fig. 4c, Supplementary Table 6). This density map enables the confident modelling of βarr1, its interface with KOR and its ligand binding pocket (Fig. 1c and Supplementary Fig. 5). Due to the inherent flexibility of KOR, the density lacks side-chain features in several regions, especially the extra- and intra-cellular loops. To enhance the interpretability of the density while avoiding model bias, the EMReady tool33 was applied to improve the map’s feature without incorporating an atomic model during processing (Fig. 1c).

The interface of KOR-βarr1 complex involves multi-domains

The cryo-EM structure of the KOR-βarr1 complex in a detergent micelle reveals a multimodal interaction network, in which the N domain of βarr1 engages the phosphorylated V2R tail, the interdomain loops of βarr1 engage the seven transmembrane core and intracellular loop 2 (ICL2) of the receptor, and the C domain of βarr1 engages PIP2 and the detergent micelle (Fig. 2a and Fig. 1c). Topologically, the KOR-βarr1 complex adopts a canonical “core”-interacting mode similar to the many other GPCR-arrestin complexes26–29,34, but notably different from the glucagon receptor (GCGR)-βarr1 complex31.

In general, βarr1 engages KOR primarily via the receptor core, ICL2, and V2R tail. Notably, the finger loop of βarr1 inserts into the receptor core by adopting an extended loop configuration, where it mainly interacts with the intracellular ends of transmembrane helices (TM) 3, TM5, TM6, TM7 and helix 8 (H8) (Fig. 2b). The middle loop and C loop are the other two major regions in βarr1 that form the interface via hydrophobic and electrostatic interactions with KOR ICL2 (Fig. 2c). Specifically, the residue Y63 in the N-terminal finger loop and residues L243 and F244 in the C-loop engage in a hydrophobic interactions with residue P16334.50 in the ICL2 of KOR (Fig. 2b, c). In addition, residues L71 and L73 in the C-terminal finger loop engage with the hydrophobic pocket formed by residues in TM3 (R1563.50), TM5 (L2535.65), and TM6 (I2726.33), and the backbone of G72 interacts with the residue R2716.32 in KOR via a hydrogen bond (Fig. 2b). Using the BRET assays with wild type KOR and βarr1, the Y63A and L71A mutation in the finger loop of βarr1, along with the individual alanine substitutions at residues on the KOR side attenuated βarr1 recruitment, although the L73A mutation didn’t affect βarr1 coupling (Fig. 2d and Supplementary Fig. 6a, b, Supplementary Table S7–S8). Interestingly, although other finger loop residues D67, L68, D69, and F75 also interact with the receptor core or ICLs, their alanine mutations were found to significantly enhance βarr1 recruitment efficacy (Fig. 2d, e, and Supplementary Fig. 6b, Supplementary Table S7). In contrast, the D69A mutation in the M2 muscarinic receptor (M2R) was reported to reduce βarr1 coupling to the receptor significantly28. The opposing effects of alanine substitutions in the finger loop on βarr1 recruitment indicate that these residues play distinct roles in mediating βarr1 coupling to GPCRs. In KOR-βarr1, D69 does not directly interact with R1563.50 of the highly conserved D3.49R3.50Y3.51 motif, while in M2R-βarr1 D69 forms salt-bridge interactions with R3.50. R3.50 has been observed to form an ionic lock with E6.30 that stabilizes the inactive state of M2R28 and other GPCRs34,35, however, these ionic lock interactions are absent in opioid receptors36. Thus, the presence of D69βarr1-R3.50 interaction in M2R-βarr1 may help stabilize the complex, whereas this stabilization is absent in KOR. The lower arrestin recruitment in wild-type βarr1 compared to βarr1-D69A suggests a potential side-chain clash between D69βarr1 and KOR due to the dynamic conformation of the finger loop, as will be shown below.

While we couldn’t accurately model the side chains of residues at the TM7 terminus, H8 and part of the C-terminal KOR (unresolved density), the proximity of these regions to the finger loop that suggests potential interactions (Fig. 2b), we thus examined the effects of alanine substitutions at residue L3337.56 and D3348.47 and found these mutations significantly attenuated the βarr1 recruitment (Fig. 2d, Supplementary Fig. 6c, d, and Supplementary Table S8). Instead, L3337.56A mutation can further enhance the Gi1 activation by increasing the basal activity of KOR-Gi1 coupling (Supplementary Fig. 6c, d). This indicates that this region is conformationally dynamic, but still crucial for βarr1 coupling to KOR. Notably, the same mutations were also tested in the KOR-V2R chimera and they produced effects on βarr1 recruitment highly similar to those observed in wild-type KOR, supporting that the major interactions at the receptor-βarr1 interface are shared between wild-type KOR and the KOR-V2R chimera (Supplementary Fig. 6e, f, Supplementary Table S9–S10). Furthermore, the V2R tail has a similar phosphorylation barcode as observed in other GPCR-βarr1 structures (Supplementary Fig. 7a), with two additional phosphorylation sites identified by mass spectrometry (Supplementary Fig. 7b). However, except for the β1-adrenergic receptor (β1AR)-βarr1 structure, the interaction network of the gate loop with the V2R tail in the KOR-βarr1 complex is distinct from the other GPCR-βarr1 complexes, which results in a different conformation of gate loop in KOR-βarr1 structure (Supplementary Fig. 7c). Consequently, two residues, K294 and H295, in the C domain of βarr1 mediate the interactions between the gate loop and V2R tail (Supplementary Fig. 7a, c), whereas only K294 engages with the phosphorylated residue in other GPCR-βarr1 structures (Supplementary Fig. 7c)27–29,31,37,38.

The ICL2 of KOR is important for the stabilization of βarr1 coupling

Outside the receptor cytoplasmic cavity, the ICL2 of KOR adopts a helical structure and rests in a central cleft formed by the finger loop, middle loop, lariat loop, and C-loop (Fig. 2c). The interface of ICL2 of KOR and βarr1 is mainly through extensive hydrophobic interactions. Specifically, L16734.54 in KOR engages in a hydrophobic interaction network with the side chain of residue R65 in the finger loop, residues L129 and I140 in the middle loop, and the side chain of residue R285 in the lariat loop (Fig. 2c, and Supplementary Fig. 8). Additionally, V16434.51 points to a hydrophobic pocket formed by middle loop residue L129, and C-loop residues I241, L243, and Y249 (Fig. 2c). The R17034.57 in KOR is pointing to the N-terminus of the finger loop (Fig. 2a). The extensive interactions mediated by the L16734.54 and V16434.51, and R17034.57 of KOR are crucial for βarr1 coupling, as the alanine substitution of these residues significantly reduced the efficacy of agonist-stimulated βarr1 recruitment (Fig. 2d and Supplementary Fig. 9a–c, Supplementary Table S8). Accordingly, the R65A and L129A mutations of βarr1 also attenuated the βarr1 recruitment (Fig. 2d and Supplementary Fig. 6a, b, Supplementary Table S7). Unexpectedly, the alanine substitution for residues I241, L243 and Y249 in the C-loop could increase the efficacy of agonist-stimulated βarr1 recruitment, likely due to a similar clash as the D69A in the finger loop (Fig. 2d, f, and Supplementary Table S7).

The importance of ICL2 is also supported by the identification of PIP2 in the KOR-βarr1 structure that bridges the C-domain of βarr1 with the ICL2 of KOR (Fig. 2g, h). PIP2 has been implicated in modulating GPCR function by acting as a molecular glue between βarr1 and neurotensin receptor 1 (NTSR1) or GCGR29,31. In KOR-βarr1, the 4,5-bisphosphate group of PIP2 engages an electrostatic interaction network with residue R236, K324, and K326 of βarr1 (Fig. 2g). The alanine or glutamate substitution of residues K324 and K326 can significantly reduce the recruitment of βarr1 to KOR compared to the wild type (Fig. 2d, and Supplementary Figs. 9d, b, and 6b, Supplementary Table S7). The density of the PIP2 lipid-chain tails is too weak to build a confident orientation for this region. In NTSR1- and GCGR-βarr1 structures, the tails of PIP2 form direct interactions with receptor residues in TM1 and TM2, and intracellular loop 1 (ICL1), respectively29,31. When the tails of PIP2 are modeled towards KOR, only the ICL2 of KOR is nearby to form interactions (Fig. 2h). We examined the effect of residues F16934.56 that may be involved in the KOR-PIP2 interactions, and found that F16934.56A could significantly reduce βarr1 recruitment but not G protein activation (Fig. 2h and Supplementary Fig. 9d, b, Supplementary Table S10–S11).

βarr1 displays receptor-dependent conformations upon activation

How βarr1 recognizes functionally distinct GPCRs remains elusive. Unlike a given G protein (e.g., Gi1)39, which engages different receptors through a largely conserved conformation, βarr1 can exhibit receptor-dependent variations in its relative orientation and interdomain arrangement upon activation, such as tilting, rotation, and twisting. To determine the tilt between the receptor and arrestin, we calculated the angle between their longitudinal axes defined in a mass-weighted manner. Specifically, βarr1 is tilted by approximately 78° relative to the longitudinal axis of KOR, closely resembling the Rhodopsin-Arrestin-1 complex26 (Supplementary Fig. 10a). In contrast, the KOR-βarr1 complex shows larger deviations in tilting relative to β1AR, type-1 cannabinoid receptor (CB1), M2R and 5-hydroxytryptamine receptor 2B (5HT2BR)-βarr1 complexes (up to ~14°)27,28,34, and differs substantially from the V2R and NTSR1-βarr1 complexes29,37 (Supplementary Fig. 10a). It is worth noting that both cryo-EM structures of KOR-βarr1 and 5HT2BR-βarr1 complexes were determined using the same βarr1 isoform 2 that lacks C-edge loop 2 for anchoring the membrane. This observation suggests that differences in βarr1 tilting may not be solely dictated by the C-edge loop 2 and may also be strongly influenced by the receptor-βarr1 interface.

In addition to βarr1 tilting, arrestins can undergo horizontal rotations upon binding to the receptors (Fig. 3a). Compared with other GPCR-arrestin structures, the KOR-bound βarr1 displays a similar orientation to that of Rhodopsin-bound Arrestin-1 and β1AR- and M2R-bound βarr1 (within ~10°; Fig. 3a). However, it exhibits larger differences in horizontal rotation compared to βarr1 coupling to CB1, 5HT2BR, and V2R, and differs substantially from NTSR-bound βarr1 (Fig. 3a). Notably, although the V2R tail was used to promote the formation of the KOR-βarr1 complex, both the tilted conformation and rotation of KOR-βarr1 are distinct from those observed in the V2R-βarr1 complex (Fig. 3a, and Supplementary Fig. 10a). Together, these comparisons suggest that the V2R tail primarily acts to facilitate complex formation but does not necessarily dictate the βarr1 tilting and rotation, which appear to remain receptor dependent.

Upon activation of βarr1 by KOR, βarr1 undergoes an ~20.5° interdomain twist relative to the inactive βarr1 conformation (Supplementary Fig. 10b). Interestingly, the extent of βarr1 interdomain twisting appears to be receptor dependent. Among GPCR-βarr1 complexes, the KOR-bound βarr1 shows an interdomain orientation highly similar to that observed in the M2R-βarr1 and β1AR-βarr1 structures (Supplementary Fig. 10c). In contrast, more noticeable differences are observed when comparing KOR-βarr1 with CB1-βarr1, GCGR-βarr1, and NTSR1-βarr1 complexes (Supplementary Fig. 10c). Likewise, compared to V2R-βarr1, other GPCR-βarr1 complexes (e.g., KOR, M2R, β1AR, CB1, and GCGR fused with the V2R tail) and the V2R tail-βarr1 complex exhibit distinct interdomain twisting (Supplementary Fig. 10c), supporting that extent of βarr1 twisting is largely influenced by receptors.

To explore the potential mechanism underlying the distinct conformation of KOR-bound βarr1 compared to other GPCR-bound arrestins, we analyzed the conformational changes in βarr1 by overlaying the structure of KOR-bound βarr1 with those of other GPCR-bound arrestins. The main interface, such as the finger loop and C-loop of KOR-bound βarr1, exhibits high conformational plasticity (Fig. 3b, c). Specifically, compared to the α-helix conformation of finger loop observed in Rhodopsin-bound arrestin-1, as well as 5HT2BR- and NTSR1-bound βarr1 (Fig. 3b), the finger loop in KOR-bound βarr1 adopts an extended loop configuration. In addition, both the finger loop and C-loop of KOR-bound βarr1 undergo large displacements relative to those in other GPCR-bound arrestins (Fig. 3b, c). The high conformational plasticity in these two loops is likely due to their adaptability in accommodating binding with the intracellular regions of different GPCRs. Indeed, we found that most KOR residues interacting with the finger loop and C-loop of βarr1 are not conserved in other GPCRs but conserved within the opioid receptor family (Fig. 3d, and Supplementary Fig. 10d), which suggests that the unique conformation of βarr1 is likely induced by the distinct 3D shapes of intracellular cores formed by these non-conserved residues. Within the opioid receptor family, the KOR-βarr1 interface residues are highly conserved, consistent with their similar interaction pattern compared to the recently reported MOR-βarr1 structure (Supplementary Fig. 10e)40.

Different interfaces engaged by βarr1 and G proteins at KOR

To identify the structural changes when G protein or βarr1 couples, we first compared the transducer pockets in the structures of MP1104-bound KOR-βarr1 and methoxymethyl-salvinorin B (momSalB)-bound KOR-Gi1 complexes25 (Supplementary Fig. 11a). To accommodate the coupling of βarr1 to KOR, TM5, TM6 and H8 appear to adopt additional outward movements by 1.0 Å, 1.5 Å and 3 Å, respectively, as the bulkier finger loop exhibits clashes with TM5, TM6, and H8 of Gi-coupled KOR (Supplementary Fig. 11b). These structural rearrangements are likely to create an intracellular cavity that is larger than in Gi-coupled KOR (KOR-βarr1 1539 Å3 versus KOR-Gi1 1120 Å3). In addition, compared to the structure of KOR-Gi1, βarr1 coupling results in inward displacements of the intracellular end of TM3 and ICL2 by ~1.8 Å and 1.2 Å, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 11a). This is likely due to the finger loop exhibiting a counterclockwise rotation of about 12.5° relative to the α5 helix of Gi1 (Fig. 4a), which drives the inward motion of TM3 and ICL2 through its interactions with TM3 and ICL2. Interestingly, the positions of the finger loop of βarr1 and the α5 helix of G protein transducers in the receptor intracellular core are different across various GPCRs. In KOR, the finger loop of βarr1 and the α5 helix of Gi1 protein are positioned at a similar depth in the receptor intracellular core (Fig. 4a), which is also observed in the CB1 receptor27. However, the finger loop of βarr1 inserts into a shallower position than the α5 helix of G protein transducers in the NTSR1, M2R and 5-HT2BR receptors28,29,38. In contrast, it is seated at a deeper position than the α5 helix of G protein transducers in the β1AR receptor34. In KOR, while the finger loop of βarr1 occupies a site similar to the α5 helix of G protein, the middle loop mimics the the αN helix of G protein (Fig. 4b). Moreover, the interactions between the C-loop of βarr1 and the intracellular ends of TM3 and TM5 are unique and are not observed in the KOR-Gi1 structure24,25 (Fig. 4c). During characterizing the structural changes in the intracellular end of TM3 and TM5 of KOR upon the engagement of Gi1 or βarr1, we observed a striking conformational change in the side chain of R2525.64: in the KOR-Gi1 structure, R2525.64 forms a salt bridge with D341 and hydrophobic interactions with I344 in the α5 helix of Gi1; however, when coupled to βarr1, R2525.64 undergoes a 96° counterclockwise rotation, allowing it to form hydrogen bonds with H1623.56 in KOR and N245 in the C-loop of βarr1 (Fig. 4d). Interestingly, the R2525.64A mutation could slightly increase the efficacy and potency of agonist-stimulated βarr1 recruitment, whereas it had minimal effect on G protein signaling (Fig. 4e and Supplementary Fig. 11c, Supplementary Table S12). Although not the scope of this work, the KOR R2525.64A also significantly affects the βarr2 recruitment by MP1104, which suggests that this residue plays different roles in mediating signal transducer coupling (Fig. 4e and Supplementary Fig. 11c, Supplementary Table S12). In the structure of KOR-βarr1, the density of intracellular loop 3 (ICL3) is unresolved, which indicates the increased flexibility of ICL3 in KOR in the βarr1-coupled state compared to the Gi1-coupled state (Fig. 4a).

As the MP1104-bound KOR-Nb3923 structure is available, we also set out to compare whether the receptor conformation and ligand pose are affected by the binding of the nanobody or transducers. While both receptor adopt fully active states, conformational rearrangements of intracellular TM ends are observed, likely to accommodate the distinct conformations of the finger loop of βarr1 and the CDR2 region of Nb39 (Supplementary Fig. 11d). Specifically, the finger loop of βarr1 inserts deeper into the receptor intracellular core by 3.8 Å than the CDR2 region of Nb39 (Supplementary Fig. 11e), which results in the further outward movement of TM6 and H8 to create a larger space to accommodate its conformation (Supplementary Fig. 11e). Consequently, the intracellular cavity becomes much smaller than in βarr1-coupled KOR (KOR-Nb39 810 Å3 versus KOR-βarr1 1539 Å3). In the orthosteric binding site, MP1104 has an overall downward displacement of ~1 Å and shifts toward TM6 and TM7 by about 0.8 Å when βarr1 is coupled (Fig. 4f). Specifically, the Q1152.60 adopts a rotamer conformation that clashes with the chlorobenzene group of MP1104 in the KOR-Nb39 structure. The striking displacement of the chlorobenzene group of MP1104 shifts L1353.29 toward Y1393.33, thereby rotating Y1393.33 by ~30°. This new rotamer conformation of Y1393.33 affects the MP1104 position in the binding pocket due to clashes between Y1393.33 in the KOR-βarr1 structure and MP1104 in the KOR-Nb39 structure.

As a result of changes in the ligand binding pose, some residues, such as V1082.53 and K2275.39 also undergo displacement (Fig. 4f). The V1082.53A and K2275.39A mutations significantly attenuated βarr1 recruitment while either maintaining or enhancing the G protein signaling (Fig. 4g and Supplementary Fig. 11c, Supplementary Table S13). However, this separation appears MP1104-specific, as both mutations significantly attenuated the activation of βarr1 and G protein when KOR was stimulated with U50,488 (Fig. 4g and Supplementary Fig. 11c, Supplementary Table S13). This indicated that the conformational changes in the KOR ligand-binding pocket are ligand dependent. Notably, K2275.39A exhibited elevated basal Gi1 activity but with no changes in basal βarr1 recruitment (Fig. 4g).

Structural dynamics of KOR-βarr1 interactions

Obtaining high-resolution structures of GPCR-arrestin complexes has long been a challenge due to their intrinsic dynamics and conformational heterogeneity. Consistently, thermodynamic analyses show that, while G protein heterotrimers and βarr1 both adopt relatively stable conformations likely due to the binding of antibody fragment, the receptor in the KOR-βarr1 structure displays greater dynamic variability across the seven transmembrane domains compared to that in the KOR-Gi1 structure (Supplementary Fig. 12a). Inspired by this observation, we performed a 3D heterogeneity analysis on the cryo-EM particle set to visualize the structural variability among the particles.

First, we examined conformational changes in the transmembrane domain of KOR. Using the Gaussian mixture model and a deep learning-based method implemented in EMAN241, we can exclude the impact of the lipid density fluctuation and focus on the dynamics of the transmembrane helices. The analysis yielded two main modes of continuous conformational changes, which were further identified in different frames (six frames of each mode) with distinct transmembrane helix motion (Supplementary Movie 1). The first mode of motion is featured by the “breathing motion” observed intracellular ends of the whole receptor (Fig. 5a). Specifically, along one direction of the conformational change trajectory, the intracellular ends of the TM helices move inward simultaneously, toward the center of the TM domain, with TM4 displaying the largest extent of movement. In fact, from the first to the last frame of the trajectory, the distance between the intracellular ends of TM4 and TM6 (Cα atom of L4.40 and R6.28) changed from 39.4 Å to 32.9 Å (Supplementary Fig. 12b). Unexpectedly, during the movement of the TM helices, we also observed a difference in Cryo-EM density at the finger loop of βarr1. When the distance between the intracellular ends of TM4 and TM6 is 35 Å or larger, the finger loop of βarr1 is completely invisible in the 3D reconstruction (Fig. 5b, and Supplementary Fig. 12b), indicating that it is highly disordered; when the TMs move closer, solid density starts to appear at the finger loop and the interaction between the loop and the tip of TM helices can be modeled from the Cryo-EM map (Fig. 5b). This observation suggests an increasing stability of the finger loop caused by established interactions with the receptor.

On the other hand, the second mode of motion shows a tilting movement of the TM domain with respect to the βarr1 (Fig. 5c, Supplementary Movie 2), as well as a hallmark of twisting between the TM helices. Viewed from the intracellular side, the TM helices rotate counterclockwise as the entire TM domain tilts downward (Fig. 5d), with TM1 and TM6 displaying the largest extent of movement. The change of distances between different frames ranges from 20.4 Å to 12.1 Å (Cα atom of R3.50 and L7.56) (Supplementary Fig. 12c). Along the movement trajectory, the finger loop extends slightly and reaches upward as the TM domain tilts up, further indicating the interaction between the finger loop and the TM helices (Supplementary Fig. 13a, b). The relative distances from mode 1 and mode 2, together with the finger loop density, suggest a specific receptor conformation that is favorable for arrestin coupling.

Next, we shifted our focus to the dynamics of βarr1, using a mask that covers most of the arrestin density, excluding the region around the finger loop. The main conformational change is a subtle bending motion of the arrestin, with both sides of the arrestin bending inward for roughly 3 degrees (Supplementary Fig. 13c, Supplementary Movie 3). The heterogeneity analysis reveals less flexibility in arrestin than in the transmembrane domains of KOR, likely due to the stabilizing effects of the Fab and nanobody on arrestins.

To further investigate the dynamics of KOR interaction with βarr1, especially unmodified KOR and βarr1, we restored the mutations and truncations in both proteins, and performed molecular dynamics (MD) simulations of the MP1104-bound KOR-βarr1 complex and, for comparison, the MP1104-bound KOR-Gi1 complex25. We performed 12 simulations for each complex, each lasting at least 1 µs. In simulation, βarr1-bound KOR is more dynamic than Gi1-bound KOR, in agreement with the thermodynamic analyses, especially in ICL2, ICL3, and the nearby intracellular regions of TM3, TM6, and TM7 (Supplementary Fig. 14).

Interestingly, the βarr1 finger loop displays substantial mobility in simulations of the KOR-βarr1 complex compared to the α5 helix of Gi1 in the KOR-Gi1 complex (Supplementary Fig. 15). The finger loop sometimes moves from the binding mode seen in the structure, where the finger loop is bound centrally in the receptor core, to an alternative binding mode in which the finger loop occupies the space between TM6 and TM7, outside of the receptor core (Fig. 5e). This binding mode occupies about 25% of time in our KOR-βarr1 simulations (Supplemetnary Fig. 16a, b), in which new interactions were formed between V70 in the finger loop and R2716.32 and L3337.56 in the KOR (Supplenmentary Fig. 16c). By contrast, the α5 helix of Gi1 remains bound centrally in the receptor core throughout our KOR-Gi1 simulations (Fig. 5f). The translocation of the finger loop to TM6/TM7 is accompanied by an increased separation of between TM6 and TM7, driven by the movement of TM6 toward TM5 or of TM7 toward TM2 (Supplementary Fig. 16d). This binding mode may represent an intermediate state during the βarr1-receptor dissociation (Supplementary Fig. 16e), as supported by a recent study showing that the finger loop of βarr1 could also interact with the detergent micelles directly without engaging the receptor42.

Discussion

Understanding arrestin signaling has been challenging owing to the weak and dynamic interaction of arrestins with GPCRs. While mammalian cells contain 16 G protein subtypes that can interact with over 800 human GPCRs, there are only four human arrestin subtypes. Of these, β-arrestin1 and β-arrestin2 primarily interact with non-visual receptors43. This raises an important question: how do different GPCRs recognize the same arrestins despite their diverse intracellular regions and conformations? Here we present the cryo-EM structure of the KOR in complex with βarr1. The KOR-βarr1 complex exhibits an interaction pattern involving both the core and tail of the receptor that is similar to that observed in many other GPCR-βarr1 complexes. Extensive comparisons and analyses with KOR bound to different transducers or other GPCR-βarr1 complexes reveal significant differences at the interface between receptors and βarr1 (Fig. 3 and Fig. 4). Specifically, βarr1, including the finger loop and C-loop, can adopt different conformations upon binding to different receptors (Fig. 3). This adaptability supports its ability to accommodate functionally distinct GPCRs.

Phosphorylation codes have been shown to play important roles in arrestin recruitment by GPCRs26. This is consistent with our functional characterization in the presence of different GRKs, which shows significantly different levels of arrestin recruitment (Fig. 1a). In addition, we identified the GRK2- and GRK5-mediated phosphorylation barcode of KOR, which is deposited on the ICL1 (T8812.48), ICL3 (S2555.67) and the C-terminus (S356, T357, S358, and T363) (Supplementary Fig. 1b). Funtional studies found that phosphorylations of these residues were important for KOR-mediated βarr1 recruitment (Supplementary Fig. 1c). It is worth noting that this phosphorylation pattern could be attributed to the dual GRKs and may not reflect the native context in which phosphorylation can be preferentially driven by a specific GRK subtype. Future studies investigating GRK2- or GRK5-mediated phosphorylation individually should help delineate GRK subtype-specific barcodes at human KOR. For capturing the structure of phosphorylated KOR binding to the βarr1, we retained all the potential phosphorylation sites in ICLs and the C-terminus of KOR when constructing the expression vector. However, most of the phosphoresidues identified in the density map are located in the V2R region, such as T380, T381, S383, S384, and S385. Unfortunately, the ICL1, ICL3, and C-terminus of KOR lack sufficient density to determine whether the receptor maintains the phosphorylations observed by mass spectrometry. This is likely due to the conformational dynamics of KOR (Fig. 5) and to phosphorylation heterogeneity arising from the different chromatographic elution times of the non-phosphorylated and phosphorylated peptides (Supplementary Fig. 17).

The engagement of βarr1 with GPCRs can be segregated into two distinct categories: core interactions with the transmembrane core and tail interactions with the phosphorylated C terminus. Similar to other GPCRs, βarr1 interacts extensively with KOR via multiple regions consisting of the phosphorylated V2R tail and receptor core (Fig. 2). The C tail of KOR-V2R displaces the C terminus of βarr1 and forms a tail interaction by binding to a positively charged crevice in the N domain. Although the density of intracellular KOR and βarr1 is mostly resolved, a few residues between the C-terminal KOR and V2R lack density, suggesting that they may not engage βarr1.

We also observed a strong density above the βarr1 C-domain, in the region previously shown to be an inositol phosphate-binding site between the membrane side and the C-domain of arrestin29,31. This is more clearly visualized in the unsharpened map of the complex. PIP2 has been observed in several GPCR-arrestin structures and has been shown to play a key role in anchoring these GPCR signaling partners in the plasma membrane29. In these complexes, the presence of PIP2 in the sample, combined with the insertion of the βarr1 C-edge loop 2 into the detergent micelles or nanodiscs, is probably a key contributor to the stability of the complex. However, in the KOR-βarr1 that lacks the C-edge loop 2, PIP2 appears to be the only mediator linking detergent micelles to βarr1. This absence may contribute to the observed difference in which, instead of connecting the receptor and βarr1 as in other GPCR-arrestin structures, the PIP2 in the KOR-βarr1 structure engages distinct membrane faces of KOR (Fig. 2).

KOR coupled to βarr1 shows considerable structural differences from KOR coupled to Nb39 and G proteins. For example, MP1104 adopts different conformations in the KOR-βarr1 and KOR-Nb39 structures23 (Fig. 4). Notably, these changes appeared to be ligand-dependent, as same mutations produced different functional consequences in response to different ligands (Fig. 4). Similar changes are also observed in CB127 and NTSR129 when coupled to G protein versus arrestin, whereas in other GPCR structures, the ligand conformation remains nearly unchanged regardless of the transducer coupling28,31,38. In addition to differences in the ligand binding pose, a comparison of the KOR-βarr1 structure with the KOR-Gi complex reveals that the cytoplasmic portion of TM6 exhibits a wider outward movement in KOR-βarr1 than in KOR-Gi1 structures (Fig. 4), a feature also observed in other Gi-coupled receptors27.

The comparison with KOR-G protein suggests that KOR and βarr1 interactions are more dynamic than KOR and G protein interactions. Indeed, we observed significant structural heterogeneity in KOR during 3D particle classification. Further processing and analysis of the KOR-βarr1 complex identified subpopulations of KOR exhibiting a highly dynamic range of motion in their transmembrane helices. There is a correlation between helical motion and the stability of the βarr1 interdomain, potentially explaining why the KOR-arrestin interaction requires rotations and exhibits dynamics relative to the inactive arrestin. The extensive protein engineering in arrestins (including thermostabilizing mutations, truncations, and the use of Fab30 and Nb32) may have reduced the dynamics of the arrestin structure observed in the 3D sub-classification relative to those in the KOR structure. Using MD simulations, we observed two major binding modes of the βarr1 finger loop: one within the receptor core and the other outside the receptor core, specifically between TM6 and TM7. This observation suggests an intermediate state in which the finger loop binds to a secondary site on GPCRs. Combining this with a recent study showing that the finger loop can directly interact with detergents rather than the transmembrane core of ACKR342, these data suggest a mechanistic pathway in which arrestins bind to GPCRs, dissociate from GPCRs, interact with lipid bilayers, and mediate receptor internalization (Supplementary Fig. 16e).

Limitations of this study

The use of the V2R tail, Fab30, and Nb32 was necessary for the structural determination of the KOR-βarr1 complex in this study. Therefore, the reported structure may not fully represent the native-state conformation. Indeed, functional studies using wild-type KOR and KOR-V2R indicate that the receptor core interaction network captured by the structure is highly relevant to wild-type KOR, whereas the fusion of the V2R tail appears to alter the kinetics of βarr1 engagement. In addition, our analysis identified several intriguing conformational states that may be important for βarr1 coupling; however, these conformations might be ligand specific, and the physiological relevance of these states in determining G protein and arrestin signaling remains to be studied. In the future, the study of native-state GPCR-arrestin interactions and dynamics is both feasible and essential given the availability of in situ high-resolution imaging techniques (e.g., cryotomography) and time-resolved kinetic approaches (cryoEM or single-molecule fluorescence).

Methods

Constructs cloning and protein expression

To facilitate the formation of the KOR-βarr1 complex, we modified the human KOR and β-arresitn1 constructs accordingly. For KOR, we modified the construct based on the one used in our previous work for the structural determination of the KOR-Gi1 complex25. In brief, the construct 1) lacks C-terminal residues of 364-380, preserving the phosphorylation sites as reported in previous studies; 2) contains the V2R tail (343-371) right after residue 363, and 3) truncates the N-terminal 1-53 and includes two thermostabilized mutations at I135L and S324C. To improve the protein expression and complex stability, thermostabilizing mutations28,31 were introduced into βarr1. In addition, to make it constitutively active, βarr1 was truncated at residue 385 to remove self-inhibition and introduced with mutations including R169E44, I378A, V379A, and L380A45. All primers were designed using the Benchling platform and were ordered from Genewiz.

The Bac-to-Bac Baculovirus Expression System was applied to generate high-quality recombinant baculovirus ( > 10−9 viral particles per ml) for protein expression (KOR and βarr1). To promote the formation of KOR-βarr1 complex, KOR was co-expressed with βarr1, GRK2, and GRK5, by infection of Spodoptera frugiperda Sf9 cells (Expression Systems, 94-001S) at a cell density of 2.5 × 106 cells per ml in ESF921 medium (Expression System) with the P1 baculovirus at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) ratio of 2:2:0.5:0.5. Cells were collected by centrifugation 48 h after infection, washed with HN buffer (25 mM HEPES pH 7.4, 100 mM NaCl), and stored at −80 °C for future purification.

Protein expression and purification of Nb32 and Fab30

The Nb32 was prepared with the same procedure as reported in the previous study46. In brief, the gene coding Nb32 was cloned into a pMESy4 vector and expressed in the periplasm of Escherichia coli strain WK6. The cells containing the Nb32 plasmid were grown in the TB medium supplemented with 100 μg ml−1 ampicillin, 0.1% glucose and 1 mM MgCl2 at 37°C until reaching an OD600 of 0.7 and were cultured for 4 h at 37°C further for protein expression after addition of 1 mM IPTG. After harvesting cells, the cell pellet was then lysed and clarified by centrifuging at 4°C. Subsequently, the recovered supernatant was incubated with Ni-NTA resin in the cold room overnight. The next day, the Ni-NTA resin was washed with buffer (500 mM NaCl, 20 mM HEPES pH 7.4, 10% glycerol, 20 mM imidazole, and 1 mM MgCl2), and eluted with the same buffer supplemented with 300 mM imidazole. The eluted proteins were concentrated using a 10-kDa molecular weight cut-off concentrator and loaded into a desalting column (GE Healthcare) equilibrated with 100 mM NaCl, 20 mM HEPES pH 7.4, 10% glycerol. The purified Nb32 was flash-frozen and stored at −80 °C until use.

The Fab30 gene was cloned into the pET28a expression vector and was expressed in the periplasm of Escherichia coli BL21(DE3) cells. Briefly, cells were grown in 10 ml of LB medium supplemented with 50 μg/ml kanamycin for 4 h at 30 °C and then were inoculated into 1 L of 2 × YT medium at 30 °C for amplification until reaching an OD600 of 0.6. Subsequently, the cell cultures were induced by the addition of 1 mM IPTG and further cultured overnight at 30 °C. The purification procedure for Fab30 is the same as the purification of Nb32. The purified Fab30 was aliquoted and stored at −80 °C.

The purification and assembly of the KOR-βarr1 complex

The cell pellet was thawed and incubated in the phosphorylation buffer containing 50 mM NaCl, 20 mM HEPES pH 7.5, 1 mM MgCl2, 1 mM ATP, 2 mM Na3VO4,10 μM MP1104 agonist (final concentration) and protease inhibitors (500 mM AEBSF, 1 mM E-64, 1 mM leupeptin, 150 nM aprotinin) for 1.5 h at room temperature. Subsequently, the membrane was collected by centrifugation at 64,300 × g for 30 min at 4 °C, and was then solubilized in the buffer (40 mM HEPES pH 7.5, 100 mM NaCl, 5% (w/v) glycerol, 0.6% (w/v) lauryl maltose neopentyl glycol (LMNG), 0.06% (w/v) cholesteryl hemisuccinate (CHS), 1 mM Na3VO4, 10 μM agonist and protease inhibitors) in the cold room. During the solubilization stage, 400 μg of Fab30 and 200 μg of Nb32 are added to stabilize the KOR-βarr1 complex. After 5 h, the supernatant was collected by centrifugation at 92,600 × g for 30 min at 4 °C and incubated with 1 ml TALON IMAC resin (Clontech) and 20 mM imidazole overnight in the cold room. The next day, the resin was collected and washed with 20 ml buffer containing 20 mM HEPES pH 7.5, 100 mM NaCl, 20 mM imidazole, 0.01% (w/v) LMNG, 0.001% (w/v) CHS, 5% glycerol, 1 mM Na3VO4 and 5 μM MP1104. The protein was then eluted with the same buffer supplemented with 300 mM imidazole, concentrated and further purified by size-exclusion chromatography on the Superdex 200 increase 10/300 column (GE Healthcare), which was pre-equilibrated with 20 mM HEPES pH 7.5, 100 mM NaCl, 1 μM TCEP, 0.00075% (w/v) LMNG, 0.00025% (w/v) glyco-diosgenin (GDN) and 0.00075% (w/v) CHS, 1 μM MP1104, 10 μM diC8-PIP2 and 1 mM Na3VO4. Peak fractions were collected, concentrated and incubated with 1 mM Na3VO4, 100 μg Fab30, 50 μg Nb32, PNGase F (NEB), PreScission protease (GenScript), and TEV protease at 4 °C overnight. The next day, the KOR-βarr1 complex was further purified by the same procedure as described above. Peak fractions were concentrated to 3 ~ 5 mg ml−1 using a 100-kDa molecular weight concentrator for the cryogenic electron microscopy analysis.

Cryo-EM grid preparation and data acquisition

The purified sample (3 μl) was applied onto glow-discharged 300-mesh Au grids (Quantifoil R1.2/1.3) and vitrified using a Vitrobot mark IV (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Cryo-EM data collection was performed on a Titan Krios electron microscope (Thermo Fisher Scientific/FEI) operated at a 300 kV accelerating voltage equipped with a GIF-Quantum energy filter with a slit width of 20 eV. The defocus range was set from −0.8 to −1.8 µm. In total, a dataset of about 16,000 movies was recorded with the Gatan K3 direct electron detector at a super-resolution mode with a nominal magnification of ×105,000 (0.414 Å/super-pixel). Each movie was collected with a total accumulated dose of 60 e-/Å2.

Cryo-EM data processing

The data processing workflow is depicted in Supplementary Fig. 4. Cryo-EM data were first processed using Relion 5.047, using Blush regularization for all 3D auto-refine steps. Briefly, after motion correction using the Relion motioncor implementation and CTF estimation on movie power spectra, exposures were excluded based on motion and CTF estimation quality statistics and non-vitreous ice contamination. The remaining 13,798 exposures were used for structure determination. Particles from a random subset of 1000 micrographs were picked by Topaz48 using its included general model, and the picks were processed through 2D and 3D classification to produce a low resolution map. This map was used to re-pick the same micrograph subset via template matching, and those particles were similarly processed through 2D and 3D classification. The union of the Topaz and template particles was used to train a new Topaz model that was used for particle picking across the full dataset. Particles with high-resolution features of the full target complex were selected by several rounds of 2D and 3D classification, resulting in 1,103,218 particles that yielded a map at 3.31 Å global resolution. CTF refinement and particle polishing were then iterated until resolution failed to improve, resulting in a map at 2.72 Å global resolution. To improve the interpretability of the TM helices, signal subtraction was used to focus a fixed-pose 3D classification on that region. A single class representing 163,293 particles showed the best completeness and connectivity for the TM helices. Refinement of these particles yielded a map at 2.99 Å global resolution with better TM features.

The result was imported to EMAN2 for further refinement with the Gaussian mixture model orientation refinement pipeline49. Iterative global orientation refinement of the full protein and focused refinement of the transmembrane domain were performed, resulting in a composite map at 2.6 Å resolution, according to the “gold-standard” FSC. The final map was sharpened using EMReady v1.2 with the default parameters. Afterwards, the deep learning-based heterogeneity analysis was used to extract the conformational changes within the system, focusing on the TM helices and the arrestin separately41. For the first two eigen-motion trajectories of the TM domain, six points were sampled with even spacing in the latent space along the eigen-vector, covering 1% to 99% of the particles along that axis, and 10,000 particles closest to each point were used to reconstruct the 3D volume of that conformation. Each of the 3D volumes was then sharpened using EMReady33 before modeling. To better demonstrate the density of the finger loop throughout the motion, we picked the first and last point along the first eigen-trajectory, and reconstructed one volume from 30,000 closest particles for each point. This yields the invisible/well-defined density of the loop in Fig. 5B. To show the motion of the finger loop during the second eigen-motion of the TM domain, we selected a vector that is parallel to the origin-crossing eigen-vector in the latent space but shifted toward the positive direction of the first eigen-vector. Since the two eigen-vectors are orthogonal in the latent space and represent independent movement, the shifted eigen-vector still showed a similar tilting movement of the TM domain as the second eigen-vector, but the TM helices are tilted more inward and the finger loop density is more solid in the reconstructions along the shifted trajectory. Two maps were reconstructed from 30,000 particles at the first and last frames of the shifted trajectory, which showed the movement of the finger loop during the tilting of the TM domain. For the motion of arrestin, four points were sampled along the first eigen-motion trajectory and one 3D volume was reconstructed from 10,000 particles closest to each point.

Model building and refinement

The initial model was generated by docking models of βarr1, Fab30 and Nb32 from the cryo-EM structure of the GCGR-βarr1 complex (Protein Data Bank (PDB): 8JRV)31, and the KOR and MP1104 model from the active-state KOR–NB391 structure (PDB: 6B73)23 into the cryo-EM map using ChimeraX 1.6.150, respectively. The phospholipid diC8-PtdIns (4,5) P2 was introduced into the model according to the map. The complex model (KOR-βarr1) was manually built in Wincoot 0.9.8.151, followed by several rounds of real-space refinement using Phenix 1.20.152. The model statistics were validated using Molprobity53. Structural figures were prepared using ChimeraX 1.6.150.

To model the conformational change of the system, the Gaussian Mixture Model (GMM)- based model refinement implemented in EMAN2 was performed on the EMReady sharpened maps along the movement trajectories54. This produces one model per frame per motion trajectory, and the movement distance is measured from the models of the first and last frames in ChimeraX.

Molecular Dynamics Simulation and Analysis

The β-arrestin-bound KOR structure (presented in this manuscript) or Gi-bound KOR structure (PDB: 8DZP) was aligned to the Orientations of Proteins in Membranes55 using PDB entry 6B73 (MP1104-KOR-Nb39)23. All stabilizing antibody fragments were removed. The orthosteric ligand in the β-arrestin1-bound KOR structure (MP1104) was maintained. To prepare the MP1104-KOR-Gi simulations, the orthosteric ligand present in 8DZP was removed, and MP1104, the orthosteric ligand present in 6B73, was placed in the orthosteric binding pocket by aligning TM1-4 of 6B73 to 8DZP and copying the MP1104 atoms into the 8DZP entry. Parameters for MP1104 were generated using the CHARMM General Force Field (CGenFF) with the ParamChem server56. No allosteric ligands or PIP2 were included in either β-arrestin-bound or Gi-bound KOR simulations. Prime (Schrödinger)57 was used to model missing side chains and to add capping groups to protein chain termini. The “Crosslink Proteins” tool in Maestro (Schrödinger) was used to model unresolved residues in extracellular loops of KOR. Protonation states of all titratable residues were assigned at pH 7.4, except for D1553.49 and D1052.50 of KOR, which were protonated (neutral) in all simulations, as these conserved residues are reported to be protonated in active-state GPCRs58,59.

For the KOR-Gi structure, the KOR mutation I1353.29L was reverted to the wild-type isoleucine using Maestro. In the Giα subunit, the mutations S47N, G203A, E245A, and A326S were all reverted to their wild-type forms. Additionally, for the Gi subunit, missing residues 2-4 and 235-239 were added; G2 was myristoylated and C3 was palmitoylated to reflect native lipid modifications. The α-helical domain (residues 42-181), which was absent in the structure, was not included. In the Gγ subunit, missing residues 63-71 were also added, and C68 was palmitoylated, to complete the structure.

For the KOR-arrestin structure, the KOR mutation S3247.47C was reverted to the wild-type serine using Maestro. In the arrestin, mutations were reverted to their wild-type identity. Because of the weak density for H8 and the TM7 terminus in the β-arrestin-bound KOR structure, KOR residues L3257.48 and after were modeled in before simulation from the 8DZP structure, by aligning and using Maestro (Schrödinger, LLC) to connect residues S3247.47 and L3257.48 The phosphorylated C-terminal tail of the β-arrestin-bound presented in this manuscript was preserved (residues 357–366).

Using Dabble60, the prepared protein structures were inserted into a pre-equilibrated palmitoyl-oleoyl-phosphatidylcholine (POPC) bilayer and the system was solvated. Sodium and chloride ions were added to neutralize each system at a concentration of 150 mM. The final system of the β-arrestin-bound KOR simulations comprised 221,777 atoms and the system dimensions were ~130 Å × 130 Å × 130 Å. For Gi-bound KOR, the final system comprised 248,474 atoms and the system dimensions were ~107 Å × 135 Å × 171 Å. 12 independent simulations for each condition were run.

We used the CHARMM36m force field for proteins, the CHARMM36 force field for lipids and ions, and the TIP3P model for water molecules61,62. All simulations were performed using the Compute Unified Device Architecture (CUDA) version of particle-mesh Ewald molecular dynamics (PMEMD) in AMBER 2063 on graphics processing units (GPUs).

Systems were first minimized using three rounds of minimization, each consisting of 500 cycles of steepest descent followed by 500 cycles of conjugate gradient optimization. 10.0 and 5.0 kcal∙mol−1 ∙ Å−2 harmonic restraints were applied to protein, lipids, and ligand for the first and second rounds of minimization, respectively. 1 kcal∙mol−1 ∙ Å−2 harmonic restraints were applied to protein and ligand for the third round of minimization. Systems were then heated from 0 K to 100 K in the NVT ensemble over 12.5 ps and then from 100 K to 310 K in the NPT ensemble over 125 ps, using 10.0 kcal∙mol−1 ∙ Å−2 harmonic restraints applied to protein and ligand heavy atoms. Subsequently, systems were equilibrated at 310 K and 1 bar in the NPT ensemble, with harmonic restraints on the protein and ligand non-hydrogen atoms tapered off by 1.0 kcal∙mol−1 ∙ Å−2 starting at 5.0 kcal∙mol−1 ∙ Å−2 in a stepwise fashion every 2 ns for 10 ns, and then by 0.1 kcal∙mol−1 ∙ Å−2 every 2 ns for 20 ns. Production simulations were performed at 310 K and 1 bar in the NPT ensemble using the Langevin thermostat and the Monte Carlo barostat and using a timestep of 4.0 fs with hydrogen mass repartitioning64. Bond lengths were constrained using the SHAKE algorithm65. Non-bonded interactions were cut off at 9.0 Å, and long-range electrostatic interactions were calculated using the particle-mesh Ewald (PME) method with an Ewald coefficient of ~0.31 Å−1, and 4th order B-splines. The PME grid size was chosen such that the width of a grid cell was ~1 Å. Enhanced sampling methods were not used because the primary objective of the MD study was to complement the structural analyses presented, not necessarily to identify minor, but significant, structural features only identifiable through enhanced sampling. As such, enhanced sampling methods were not used. Trajectory frames were saved every 200 ps during the production simulations and included equilibration steps (Supplementary Fig. 18). KOR-arrestin simulations are 1.11, 1.25, 1.10, 1.26, 1.24, 1.26, 1.20, 1.19, 1.27, 1.26, 1.26, and 1.27 µs in length; KOR- Gi1 simulations are 1.31, 1.19, 1.31, 1.23, 1.33, 1.36, 1.39, 1.40, 1.43, 1.00, 1.32, and 1.25 µs in length. For further details on the simulation setup, see Supplementary Table 14.

MD simulations were analyzed using the packages vmd (v 1.9.4) MDAnalysis (v 2.8.0), scipy (v 1.3.1), seaborn (v 0.12.2), numpy (v 1.26.4), matplotlib (v 3.8.4), and sklearn (v1.6.1). For root mean square fluctuation (RMSF) analyses, trajectory frames after 200 ns were aligned to the starting frame on the same Cα atoms whose RMSF is reported (e.g., KOR Cα atoms for KOR RMSF calculations). Then, RMSF was calculated as the square root of the mean squared displacement of each Cα atom from its time-averaged position over the aligned frames.

Identification of phosphorylation sites by mass spectrometry analysis

To achieve comprehensive sequence coverage of the KOR and arrestin, we employed a dual-column chromatographic strategy. Standard C18 columns were utilized to identify short, hydrophilic cytosolic peptides. In contrast, PLRP-S (Polymeric Reverse Phase) columns were specifically integrated to identify long, hydrophobic transmembrane domain (TMD) peptides. By combination with a low-temperature 4 °C and extended trypsin digestion in MS-compatible detergents, this method facilitates deep sequencing of TMD regions as previously described66. C18 chromatographic analysis provided 71% sequence coverage, PLRP-S provided 89% coverage and the combined analyses yielded 98% sequence coverage. In brief, purified KOR-arrestin complex was prepared for mass spectrometric sequencing in detergent solution (0.00075% LMNG/CHS (10:1), 0.00025% GDN, 10 μM MP1104), identical to the conditions used for Cryo-EM analysis. The protein (36 ug/20 ul) was reduced with 5 mM tris(2-carboxyethyl)phosphine for 30 min, alkylated with 5 mM N-ethylmaleimide (NEM) for 45 min in the dark, and quenched with 5 mM dithiothreitol (DTT) for 15 min. These three steps were done at RT. Samples were then digested with 8 μg of trypsin for 7 days at 4 °C. The digestions were terminated by adding formic acid in a final concentration of 1%, followed directly by LC-MS analysis on a Dionex U3000 nano-LC coupled to an Orbitrap Ascend Tribrid mass spectrometer. 5 uL of sample was loaded at a flow rate of 5 uL/min, and washed for 3 min on a home-made C18 or PLRPS precolumn (5 µm particle size, 300 Å pore size from Agilent, 75 μm inner diameter, 2 cm length). Peptides were separated on a home-made C18 or PLRPS analytic column (same packing material as the precolumn, 75 μm inner diameter, 10 cm length) using a 90-min linear gradient ranging from 99% solvent A (0.1% formic acid in LC-MS grade H2O) to 90% solvent B (0.1% formic acid in ACN). The mass spectrometer acquired data throughout the elution process, operating in a data-dependent acquisition mode. MS1 was acquired at high resolution (120,000 resolution) with a range of m/z = 200-2000 and a cycle time of 3 seconds. MS/MS HCD, ETD or CID-fragment ion spectra were collected in the orbitrap analyzer with a resolution of 50,000. Data were acquired and reviewed using Xcalibur 2.2 software (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

LC-MS data were searched against a customized database containing the sequence of the cryo-EM KOR receptor construct and β-arrestin 1 and filtered at a false discovery rate of 1% using PEAKS 12 pro (Bioinformatics Solutions Inc. Waterloo, ON, Canada). Search parameters were set for tryptic or Lysc peptides with a precursor mass accuracy of 20 ppm, fragment ion mass accuracy of 0.1 Da and ≤ two missed tryptic cleavages. Fixed modifications of cysteine alkylation with NEM and variable modifications of methionine oxidation, phosphorylation of serine, threonine or tyrosine and loss of water were included. All phosphorylated peptides identified in PEAKs were confirmed by manual analysis of MS1 and MS2 spectra for accurate charge states and mass accuracy of ≤ 20 ppm.

Bioluminescence resonance energy transfer (BRET1) assay

To measure βarr1 recruitment, human embryonic kidney (HEK) 293T cells (ATCC CRL-11268) were co-transfected in a 1:3 ratio with human KOR containing C-terminal Renilla luciferase (RLuc8) and βarr1 containing an N-terminal mVenus. After at least 16 h, transfected cells were plated in poly-lysine-coated 96-well white clear-bottom cell culture plates in plating media (DMEM + 1% dialyzed FBS) at a density of 40–50,000 cells in 200 μl per well and incubated overnight. The next day, media was decanted and cells were washed twice with 60 μL of drug buffer (20 mM HEPES, 1X HBSS, pH 7.4), then 60 μL of the RLuc substrate, coelenterazine h (Promega, 5 μM final concentration in drug buffer) was added per well, incubated an additional 5 min to allow for substrate diffusion. Afterward, 30 μL of drug (3X) in drug buffer (20 mM HEPES, 1X HBSS, 0.1% BSA, pH 7.4) was added per well and incubated for another 5 min. Plates were immediately read for both luminescence at 485 nm and fluorescent eYFP emission at 530 nm for 1 s per well using a Mithras LB940 multimode microplate reader. The ratio of eYFP/RLuc was calculated per well and the net BRET ratio was calculated by subtracting the eYFP/RLuc per well from the eYFP/RLuc ratio in wells without mVenus-βarr1 present. The net BRET ratio was plotted as a function of drug concentration using Graphpad Prism 10.2.2 (Graphpad Software Inc., San Diego, CA).

Bioluminescence resonance energy transfer (BRET2) assay

To measure the agonist-stimulated G-protein (wild type and mutants) activation by KOR and various mutants, a BRET2-based cell assay was used. Specifically, four plasmids (KOR,Gαi1, Gβ3, and Gγ9) were used, in which Gαi1 is tagged with a luciferase (Rluc8) and Gγ9 is tagged with an N-terminal GFP. Detailed information on the GFP-Gγ9 and Gα-Rluc8 constructs was described previously25. HEK293T cells were then transfected with the four plasmids using a 1:5:5:5 DNA ratio of receptor:Gα-RLuc8:Gβ3:Gγ9-GFP2 (100 ng receptor, 500 ng Gα–RLuc8, Gβ3 and Gγ9–GFP2 for 10 cm dishes). Transit 2020 (Mirus Biosciences) was used to complex the DNA at a ratio of 2 μl Transit per μg DNA in Opti-MEM (Gibco-Thermo Fisher Scientific). Then, 16 h after transfection, cells were plated in poly-L-lysine-coated 96-well white clear-bottom plates in plating medium (DMEM + 1% dialysed FBS) at a density of 40,000–50,000 cells in 200 μl per well and incubated overnight. The next day, the plates were decanted and washed once with 60 μl drug buffer (20 mM HEPES, 1× HBSS, pH 7.4) and then 60 μl drug buffer containing coelenterazine 400a (Nanolight Technology) at a final concentration of 5 μM was added to each well. After 5 min for substrate diffusion, 30 μl 3× drug solutions in fresh drug buffer (20 mM HEPES, 1× HBSS, 0.3% BSA, pH 7.4) were added to each well and incubated for an additional 5 min. Finally, the plates were read on the Mithras LB 940 multimode microplate reader (Berthold Technologies) with 400 nm (RLuc8-coelenterazine 400a) and 510 nm (GFP2) emission filters for 1 s per well. The GFP to Rluc8 ratio was calculated, plotted as a function of drug concentration and analysed using log (agonist) vs response in GraphPad Prism (v.10.2.2).

Characterization of expression levels of β-arresin1 constructs

β-arresin1 constructs are conjugated with a fluorescent tag, mVenus, at their N-terminus. Thus, the expression levels of β-arresin1 mutants in this study are counted as the fluorescent intensity of mVenus. In brief, HEK293T cells were then transfected with 500 ng of each β-arresin1 construct per well for a 6-well plate. Transit 2020 (Mirus Biosciences) was used to complex the DNA at a ratio of 2 μl Transit per μg DNA in Opti-MEM (Gibco-Thermo Fisher Scientific). Then, 16 h after transfection, cells were plated in poly-L-lysine-coated 96-well white clear-bottom plates in plating medium (DMEM + 1% dialysed FBS) at a density of 40,000 cells in 200 μl per well and incubated overnight. The next day, the plates were decanted and washed once with 150 μl 1 × PBS buffer and then 150 μl 1 × PBS buffer was added to each well. Finally, the plates were read on the PHERAstar FSX microplate reader installed with a 488 nm excitation and 520 nm emission filter using the fluorescence intensity measurement program.

Data statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10.2.2. For the BRET1 assay, the log-transformed EC50 values of each mutant were first analysed using one-way ANOVA. If significant, Dunnett’s multiple-comparison test was used to compare each mutant with the wild-type one. To analyze expression levels of the KOR mutant, the Rluc values were normalized to the wild type KOR (normalized as 100%), and then were first analyzed using one-way ANOVA. If significant, a Dunnett’s multiple-comparison test was used to compare each mutant with the wild-type KOR. For the βarr1 expression studies, the mVenus values of each mutant were normalized to the wild type βarr1 (normalized as 100%), and the resultant values were then first analysed using one-way ANOVA. If significant, a Dunnett’s multiple-comparison test was used to compare each mutant with the wild-type βarr1. In one-way ANOVA analysis, the significance threshold was set at α = 0.05.
