Rhino-orbital-cerebral mucormycosis with skull base involvement mimicking an intracranial tumor in an immunocompetent patient
Authors: Diyora Kurambaeva, Kamila Askarova, Aziza Fakhriddinova, Otabek Abdurakhmonov
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Mucormycosis is an aggressive invasive fungal infection that predominantly affects immunocompromised individuals and may be rapidly fatal if diagnosis and treatment are delayed. We report a man in his sixties with no known immunosuppressive conditions who presented with progressive headache, cranial nerve dysfunction, and sinusitis symptoms. Neuroimaging demonstrated an infiltrative skull base lesion with orbital and intracranial extension, initially suggestive of a neoplastic process. Surgical exploration and histopathological examination revealed invasive rhino-orbital-cerebral mucormycosis with extensive angioinvasion and tissue necrosis. The patient underwent surgical debridement followed by systemic antifungal therapy with amphotericin B, resulting in clinical stabilization and partial neurological recovery. This case highlights the importance of considering invasive fungal infection in the differential diagnosis of skull base masses, even in immunocompetent patients, and underscores the critical role of early biopsy and combined surgical and medical management.

Introduction

Mucormycosis is a rare but highly aggressive opportunistic fungal infection caused by organisms of the order Mucorales, with Rhizopus, Mucor, and Lichtheimia species being the most frequently implicated pathogens. Despite its rarity, mucormycosis remains a highly lethal condition, predominantly affecting immunocompromised individuals, including those with uncontrolled diabetes mellitus, hematologic malignancies, or post-organ transplantation immunosuppression [1]. Cerebral mucormycosis typically arises from direct extension of infection from the nasal cavity, paranasal sinuses, or orbit, a condition referred to as rhino-orbital-cerebral mucormycosis (ROCM), which accounts for approximately 30% of all cases [2]. In immunocompetent patients, the diagnosis is often delayed due to atypical clinical and radiological features, frequently masquerading as malignancy or other inflammatory conditions. Early recognition remains challenging but is crucial, as prompt surgical debridement combined with antifungal therapy significantly improves outcomes. This report describes an unusual presentation of cerebral mucormycosis in an immunocompetent patient, initially suspected to be an intracranial tumor.

Case presentation

A man in his mid-sixties with a history of hypertension and atrial fibrillation presented with several months of progressively worsening headache and generalized weakness on February 20, 2025. Three weeks prior to admission, he began experiencing clear nasal discharge and new-onset right eye strabismus. Although radiologic findings demonstrated predominant left orbital involvement, the patient presented with contralateral ocular motor dysfunction. This discrepancy may be explained by extension of the inflammatory process to adjacent skull-base structures with secondary involvement of cranial nerve pathways responsible for extraocular muscle function. He initially sought care from a neurologist, where no definitive diagnosis was made, and his symptoms continued to progress. There was no known history of diabetes mellitus, malignancy, organ transplantation, chronic corticosteroid use, or other immunosuppressive conditions. On the presentation, the patient was afebrile and hemodynamically stable. Neurological exam was notable for mild confusion, lateral gaze deviation of the right eye with impaired extraocular movements, and tenderness over the left maxillary sinus. There were no signs of meningeal irritation. Laboratory workup was largely unremarkable except for mild anemia and elevated fasting glucose levels (ranging from 150-180 mg/dL). Given his ongoing neurological symptoms, a non-contrast CT brain was obtained, revealing a mass at the left skull base measuring 54 × 42 × 39 mm, along with signs of moderate cerebral atrophy.

Imaging findings (CT)

A CT scan of the head and paranasal sinuses demonstrated an osteolytic, infiltrative lesion involving the sphenoid bone, medial orbital wall, and the adjacent skull base. The lesion extended into the left orbit with mass effect on the medial-basal temporal lobe. Histopathological findings

A surgical debridement was performed on March 10, 2025, and histopathological analysis confirmed mucormycosis with extensive necrosis and angioinvasion. Broad, ribbon-like non-septate fungal hyphae were observed on GMS and PAS stains (Fig. 2). The diagnosis of invasive rhino-orbital-cerebral mucormycosis (ROCM) was established.

Treatment and follow-up

The patient was started on intravenous Amphotericin B deoxycholate at 50 mg daily, administered in 500 mL of 5% dextrose, initiated the same day as the histopathologic diagnosis. Over the course of 9 days of antifungal therapy, the patient’s condition showed gradual improvement: his headache intensity decreased, his eye movements partially improved, and the nasal discharge reduced significantly. Discussion

Mucormycosis is a rare but highly aggressive opportunistic fungal infection caused by members of the order Mucorales. It is characterized by rapid angioinvasion, vascular thrombosis, and subsequent tissue necrosis [3]. While classically associated with immunocompromised states as uncontrolled diabetes mellitus, hematologic malignancies, and immunosuppressive therapy an increasing number of cases have been reported in immunocompetent individuals, often presenting with nonspecific symptoms and atypical radiologic features that delay diagnosis and treatment [4].

In our case, the patient had no overt immunosuppressive condition other than moderately elevated fasting glucose levels (150-180 mg/dL). He presented with a prolonged history of sinus congestion, progressive headache, cranial nerve palsy, and new-onset ophthalmoplegia. Radiologic imaging revealed an infiltrative, osteolytic lesion involving the skull base and orbit, initially raising concern for a neoplasm. Definitive diagnosis was established via histopathologic analysis, which revealed extensive necrosis and broad, non-septate fungal hyphae consistent with Mucorales.

Several reports have documented rhino-orbital-cerebral mucormycosis (ROCM) mimicking space-occupying lesions, such as malignancies or granulomatous inflammation, especially when involving the skull base or orbit [3,5]. In our case, the radiologic findings including hemorrhagic sinus opacification, bony erosion, and intracranial extension further contributed to diagnostic ambiguity. Such overlap in imaging features can significantly delay appropriate intervention, particularly in patients without typical risk factors.

The pathogenesis of mucormycosis involves multiple virulence mechanisms, including adherence to endothelial cells, angioinvasion, and evasion of host immunity. Mucorales express CotH (spore coat homolog) proteins that bind to endothelial glucose-regulated protein 78 (GRP78), facilitating vascular invasion and hematogenous spread [6]. Additionally, ischemic and hypoxic tissue environments, combined with elevated iron availability, enhance fungal proliferation—even in the absence of systemic immunosuppression [7]. These mechanisms explain the extensive tissue necrosis seen in ROCM and the rapid progression of disease.

Skull base osteolysis and orbital extension in this patient reflect the advanced and aggressive nature of ROCM. Intracranial involvement, which may occur via direct extension, hematogenous spread, or perineural invasion, is associated with increased mortality and worse neurologic outcomes [5,6]. Early surgical debridement, in conjunction with systemic antifungal therapy, is strongly associated with improved survival in mucormycosis [8].

Current management of mucormycosis centers on early surgical debridement, correction of underlying risk factors, and prompt initiation of antifungal therapy. Liposomal amphotericin B is the preferred initial treatment due to its potent activity against Mucorales and better safety profile. In our case, amphotericin B deoxycholate was used due to limited availability, with careful renal monitoring. Early surgical intervention was performed, leading to clinical stabilization and partial neurological improvement. While posaconazole or isavuconazole may be considered for maintenance or in patients intolerant to amphotericin B, they are not recommended as initial therapy. This approach aligns with current guidelines emphasizing early, aggressive treatment to reduce mortality [9].

Conclusion

This case emphasizes that rhino-orbital-cerebral mucormycosis should be considered in the differential diagnosis of aggressive skull base lesions, even in patients without classical immunocompromising conditions. The disease may closely mimic malignancy both clinically and radiologically, leading to diagnostic delay if tissue diagnosis is not pursued early. Prompt recognition, early surgical debridement, and immediate initiation of systemic antifungal therapy are essential determinants of outcome. Despite its high mortality and rapid progression, favorable clinical stabilization is achievable when a high index of suspicion is maintained and multidisciplinary management is initiated without delay. Ultimately, this case underscores a critical clinical principle: in rapidly progressive skull base lesions, failure to consider invasive fungal infection early may be as consequential as the disease itself.
