Enzymatic Synthesis of NAD+
Authors: Emily Boyle, Olivia Zhang, Yana Cen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD+) is an essential metabolite involved in numerous biological processes, functioning both as a redox cofactor in cellular energy metabolism and as a co‐substrate for NAD+‐utilizing enzymes, including sirtuins and poly(ADP‐ribose) polymerases. Given its central role in maintaining physiological homeostasis and its involvement in a wide range of disease processes, there is growing interest in developing NAD+ and its analogs as chemical probes and potential therapeutic agents. However, the chemical synthesis of NAD+ analogs is often hampered by low yields, poor stereoselectivity, and the formation of undesired byproducts. In contrast, enzymatic synthesis offers a more efficient and selective strategy using readily available starting materials under mild conditions.Herein, we report a two‐step enzymatic strategy for the synthesis of NAD+. In the first step, a ten‐enzyme coupled reaction cascade converts glucose into nicotinic acid adenine dinucleotide (NaAD). In the second step, the isolated NaAD is subsequently converted to NAD+ by NAD+ synthetase. This modular enzymatic platform provides an efficient, scalable approach to NAD+ production and establishes a versatile foundation for the biosynthesis of NAD+ analogs for biochemical and biomedical applications. © 2026 The Author(s). Introduction

Nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD+) is a ubiquitous and essential metabolite that plays central roles in cellular physiology (Lin, 2007). In its oxidized and reduced forms, NAD functions as a key redox cofactor in energy metabolism, linking glycolysis, the tricarboxylic acid cycle, and oxidative phosphorylation (Canto et al., 2015). In addition to its classical role in redox chemistry, NAD+ serves as a consumable co‐substrate for a growing family of NAD+‐dependent enzymes, including sirtuins, poly(ADP‐ribose) polymerases (PARPs), CD38, and related ADP‐ribosyltransferases (Imai & Guarente, 2014). Through these enzymes, NAD+ availability directly influences processes such as DNA repair, epigenetic regulation, stress responses, calcium signaling, and metabolic homeostasis (Covarrubias et al., 2021).

Given the expanding biological significance of NAD+ and its metabolites, there is substantial interest in accessing NAD+ and structurally modified NAD+ analogs for biochemical assays, mechanistic enzymology, metabolic tracing, and therapeutic development (Makarov & Migaud, 2019). Chemical routes often suffer from low overall yields, poor stereochemical control, and the formation of side products that complicate purification. These limitations are particularly pronounced when isotopically labeled or modified NAD+ species are required.

Enzymatic synthesis offers a powerful alternative that overcomes many of these challenges. By leveraging native biosynthetic enzymes, NAD+ can be assembled from inexpensive and readily available precursors under mild aqueous conditions with excellent regio‐ and stereoselectivity. One well‐established enzymatic strategy involves converting simple carbon sources, such as glucose, into nicotinic acid adenine dinucleotide (NaAD) via a multi‐enzyme cascade that recapitulates key steps of the NAD+ de novo biosynthetic pathway (Rising & Schramm, 1994; Tran et al., 2018). NaAD can then be efficiently converted to NAD+ by NAD+ synthetase in an ATP‐dependent amidation reaction. This modular approach has been successfully used to generate unlabeled and isotopically labeled NAD+ with high yields and reproducibility.

The enzymatic synthesis of NAD+ described in this article is organized as a two‐stage process (Figure 1 ). In the first stage, a ten‐enzyme, one‐pot reaction converts glucose into NaAD through a series of coupled transformations that generate ribose‐5‐phosphate, activate it as phosphoribosyl pyrophosphate (PRPP), and incorporate nicotinic acid and adenine to form the dinucleotide intermediate (Figure 1 A). ATP and redox cofactors required for this cascade are continuously regenerated in situ to drive the reaction efficiently to completion. In the second stage, purified NaAD is converted to NAD+ by NAD+ synthetase, which catalyzes the ATP‐dependent amidation of the nicotinic acid moiety (Figure 1 B). This modular design allows each stage to be optimized independently and provides flexibility for downstream adaptations, including isotopic labeling or structural modification of NAD+.

Several enzymes required for the multienzyme synthesis are not available from commercial sources. This article includes protocols for the expression and purification of key recombinant enzymes, including 5‐phosphoribosyl‐1‐pyrophosphate synthetase (PrsA), nicotinate phosphoribosyltransferase (Ta1145), and nicotinamide mononucleotide adenylyltransferase (NMA1). These enzymes are expressed in Escherichia coli and purified by affinity chromatography under native conditions to preserve catalytic activity. This protocol provides guidance on construct design, expression conditions, purification strategies, and storage considerations. Emphasis is placed on maintaining enzyme stability and activity, as well as on quality‐control measures critical to successful integration into multienzyme synthesis.

Following enzyme preparation, NAD+ is synthesized through a two‐step enzymatic process. The first step employs a ten‐enzyme coupled reaction to convert glucose and nicotinic acid into NaAD in a single reaction vessel. This protocol details reaction setup, enzyme ratios, cofactor regeneration systems, incubation conditions, and methods for monitoring reaction progress. Strategies for terminating the reaction and isolating NaAD by chromatographic purification are also described. In the second step, purified NaAD is converted to NAD+ by NAD+ synthetase in the presence of ATP and an ammonia source. This protocol includes optimization of reaction conditions, product verification, and purification of NAD+ suitable for biochemical or cellular applications. Together, these protocols provide a complete workflow from simple starting materials to high‐purity NAD+.

TRANSFORMATION OF PLASMID DNA INTO ROSETTA(DE3) CELLS

This protocol describes the transformation and growth of E. coli Rosetta(DE3) cells with plasmid DNA. The resulting transformants can then be expanded using downstream growth protocols appropriate for the specific plasmid and expression system. This transformation procedure is compatible with any plasmid DNA, provided that the appropriate antibiotic is used based on the plasmid's resistance marker. Overall, the protocol yields a robust population of transformed E. coli cells suitable for large‐scale recombinant protein expression.

RECOMBINANT EXPRESSION, PURIFICATION, AND ACTIVITY ASSESSMENT OF NMA1

This protocol describes the expression, purification, and assessment of the enzymatic activity of NMA1 from S. cerevisiae. NMA1 catalyzes the transfer of an adenyl moiety from ATP to nicotinic acid mononucleotide (NaMN), producing NaAD (Emanuelli et al., 1999). NMA1 is expressed in E. coli using a plasmid‐based expression system and purified under native conditions by affinity chromatography. This procedure yields soluble, catalytically active NMA1 suitable for use in the multi‐enzyme NaAD synthesis described in Basic Protocol 5.

RECOMBINANT EXPRESSION, PURIFICATION, AND ACTIVITY ASSESSMENT OF Ta1145

This protocol describes the expression, purification, and assessment of the enzymatic activity of nicotinate phosphoribosyltransferase (Ta1145) from T. acidophilum. Ta1145 catalyzes the coupling of nicotinic acid (NA) to phosphoribosyl pyrophosphate (PRPP), generating NaMN. This reaction is the rate‐limiting step in NaAD biosynthesis; therefore, the production of enzymatically active Ta1145 is essential to maximize NaAD and NAD⁺ yields in Basic Protocol 5. In this protocol, Ta1145 is expressed in E. coli using a plasmid‐based system and purified under native conditions by affinity chromatography. The method yields soluble, catalytically active Ta1145 suitable for the multi‐enzyme NaAD synthesis described in Basic Protocol 5.

The entire growth and expression process is carried out over two days, after which the cells are harvested by centrifugation. The resulting cell pellet may be stored at −80°C as a stopping point until lysis and purification are initiated.

Culturing steps that require opening culture flasks should be performed within the sterile working radius of a lit Bunsen burner, unless otherwise noted.

Loosely screw on the tube cap to allow gas exchange (threads engaged but not fully tightened), and secure the cap with tape to prevent loosening during overnight shaking.Before picking a colony, briefly pass the pipette tip through the top of the flame (avoid melting), then allow it to cool.

RECOMBINANT EXPRESSION, PURIFICATION, AND ACTIVITY ASSESSMENT OF PrsA

This protocol describes the expression, purification, and assessment of the enzymatic activity of 5‐phosphoribosyl‐1‐pyrophosphate synthetase (PrsA) from E. coli. PrsA catalyzes the formation of PRPP from ribose‐5‐phosphate and ATP. In this protocol, PrsA is expressed in E. coli using a plasmid‐based system and purified under native conditions by affinity chromatography. The method yields soluble, catalytically active PrsA suitable for the multi‐enzyme NaAD synthesis described in Basic Protocol 5.

Enzymatic Activity Assessment of PrsA

PrsA was coupled with recombinant Ta1145 and NMA1 to enable detection of its activity through the formation of the final product, NaAD. Because the direct product of PrsA, PRPP, does not exhibit significant absorbance at 260 nm, direct monitoring of PrsA turnover is not feasible. Coupling PrsA with Ta1145 alone would still limit overall reaction efficiency, as Ta1145 catalyzes the rate‐limiting step in the enzymatic scheme. The inclusion of all three recombinant enzymes, therefore, drives the reaction forward and provides a measurable readout of PrsA activity.

TWO‐STEP ENZYMATIC SYNTHESIS OF NAD⁺

This protocol describes the two‐step enzymatic production of NAD⁺. In the first step, a multi‐enzyme one‐pot reaction employs seven commercially available enzymes together with three recombinantly expressed enzymes described in Basic Protocols 2, 3, and 4 to generate NaAD. The NaAD product is then isolated and purified using an HPLC. In the second step, the purified NaAD is converted to NAD⁺ by NAD⁺ synthetase. This approach provides a straightforward, efficient, and cost‐effective method for producing NAD⁺ from relatively inexpensive starting materials.

Commentary

Critical Parameters

The success of the two‐step enzymatic synthesis of NAD+ described here depends on the careful control of enzyme quality, substrate stability, reaction pH, enzyme stoichiometry, and reaction monitoring. The most critical variables are summarized below. 1.Enzyme quality: The overall yield of NaAD and NAD+ is highly sensitive to the purity, solubility, and activity of the recombinant enzymes described in Basic Protocols 2–4. Expression temperature strongly affects solubility. For Ta1145, induction at 20°C is essential for soluble expression. Higher temperature often results in inclusion body formation. Proteolysis during purification reduces effective enzyme concentrations. Maintain samples at 4°C and minimize processing delays. Freeze‐thaw cycles significantly reduce enzymatic activity. Aliquot purified enzymes prior to storage at −80°C. Enzymatic activity should be validated individually using the HPLC‐based assays prior to incorporation into the ten‐enzyme cascade.2.Substrate stability: PRPP is labile at neutral to alkaline pH and in the presence of divalent metal ions. Prepare PRPP fresh or store aliquot at −80°C to avoid repeated freeze‐thaw cycles. Substrate integrity should be confirmed by HPLC prior to cascade assembly if reaction efficiency declines.3.Reaction pH: All reactions are optimized in potassium phosphate buffer near neutral pH. Confirm final reaction pH after mixing all components.4.Enzyme ratio in the ten‐enzyme coupled reaction: Because the reaction sequence is fully coupled, imbalances in enzyme loading can create metabolic bottlenecks that reduce flux through the pathway. In practice, Ta1145 is the rate‐limiting step; insufficient Ta1145 leads to the accumulation of NA and reduced NaMN formation. Adjusting the Ta1145 concentration often improves NaAD yield more effectively than increasing the expression of downstream enzymes such as NMA1. If intermediate buildup is observed by HPLC, individual enzyme concentrations should be titrated systematically to restore balanced flux through the pathway.5.HPLC method consistency: Consistent HPLC performance is essential for accurate monitoring of reaction progress and product identity. Reliable interpretation of substrate depletion and product formation depends on proper equilibration of the C18 column, stable gradient delivery (0%–20% methanol in 20 mM ammonium acetate), and consistent detection at 260 nm. Column aging, buffer inconsistencies, or incomplete equilibration may shift retention times or broaden peaks. Therefore, authentic standards should be run alongside experimental samples to confirm peak identity and ensure reproducibility across experiments.

Understanding Results

SDS‐PAGE analysis

Successful recombinant expression is confirmed by the appearance of prominent protein bands at the expected molecular weights in post‐induction samples compared to pre‐induction controls (NMA1 ∼48 kDa, Ta1145 ∼82 kDa, and PrsA ∼38 kDa). Following affinity purification, elution fractions should display a dominant band corresponding to the target protein with minimal contaminating bands. For NMA1, thrombin cleavage results in a slight shift in molecular weight relative to the His‐tagged form, and complete or near‐complete tag removal should be evident after the second Ni‐NTA purification. Persistence of additional bands may indicate incomplete washing, partial degradation, or insufficient cleavage efficiency.

HPLC‐based enzyme activity assays

Enzymatic activity is evaluated by monitoring substrate consumption and product formation over time using HPLC at 260 nm. In properly functioning assays, substrate levels decrease steadily over time while product levels increase correspondingly throughout the incubation period. A lack of product formation or irregular peak patterns suggests enzyme inactivation, substrate degradation, or incorrect reaction composition.

Ten‐enzyme coupled synthesis of NaAD

Effective performance of the multi‐enzyme cascade is indicated by progressive NaAD formation and minimal accumulation of upstream intermediates. Significant accumulation of intermediates typically reflects an imbalance in enzyme ratios, most commonly insufficient Ta1145. Reaction completion is determined by the maximum NaAD formation and the stabilization of the product peak area over time.

Conversion of NaAD to NAD+

Successful conversion of NaAD to NAD⁺ by NAD⁺ synthetase is indicated by a decrease in the NaAD peak and the appearance of a distinct NAD⁺ peak at a characteristic retention time. Incomplete conversion may reflect ATP depletion, insufficient enzyme concentration, or suboptimal Mg²⁺ levels. Confirmation of product identity should be performed by comparison to an authentic NAD⁺ standard.

Time Considerations

The complete workflow from transformation to purified NAD⁺ requires approximately 8–15 days, depending on user experience and whether enzyme preparations are performed sequentially or in parallel. Estimated times for each Basic Protocol are outlined below.

Basic Protocol 1: Transformation of Plasmid DNA into Rosetta(DE3) Cells (total time: ∼ 1 day)

Transformation and plating require approximately 2–3 hr on Day 1, followed by overnight incubation (12–16 hr) for colony formation. Plates may be stored at 4°C for several days before proceeding to expression.

Basic Protocol 2: Expression, Purification, and Activity Assessment of NMA1 (total time: ∼3 to 4 days, including overnight steps)

Recombinant expression requires two days: overnight starter culture (∼16 hr), secondary culture growth (∼3 hr), induction (∼16 hr), and harvest (∼1 hr). Lysis and affinity purification typically require one full day (6–8 hr). Dialysis and thrombin cleavage require an additional overnight step plus ∼6 hr for cleavage and tag removal. Activity validation by HPLC requires 4–6 hr. Stopping points include freezing cell pellets at −80°C after harvest or storing purified enzyme in 20% glycerol at −80°C.

Basic Protocol 3: Expression, Purification, and Activity Assessment of Ta1145 (total time: ∼ 3 days)

Expression requires two days, including overnight culture and low‐temperature induction (∼16 hr at 20°C). Purification and dialysis typically require one full day (6–8 hr), followed by overnight dialysis. Activity validation requires 4–6 hr. Because tag removal is not required for Ta1145, the purification process is shorter than for NMA1. Stopping points include freezing cell pellets at −80°C after harvest or storing purified enzyme in 20% glycerol at −80°C.

Basic Protocol 4: Expression, Purification, and Activity Assessment of PrsA (total time: ∼ 2‐3 days)

Expression requires approximately 2 days, including overnight growth and a 4‐hr induction period. Purification and dialysis require one additional day (6–8 hr plus overnight dialysis). Activity validation requires 4–6 hr. Stopping points include frozen pellets or glycerol‐stabilized enzyme stocks stored at −80°C.

Basic Protocol 5: Two‐Step Enzymatic Synthesis of NAD⁺ (total time: ∼3‐4 days including freeze‐drying)

Assembly of the ten‐enzyme NaAD formation requires 1–2 hr to set up the reaction. The reaction typically proceeds overnight. NaAD purification requires 4–6 hr. The purified NaAD fractions can be frozen and lyophilized as a stopping point. Freeze‐drying typically requires overnight drying (12–24 h), plus an additional 30–60 min for reconstitution and concentration determination before the amidation step. Conversion of NaAD to NAD⁺ by NAD⁺ synthetase typically requires 3 hr, followed by analytical verification and NAD⁺ purification (4–6 hr). Purified NAD⁺ fractions may be frozen and lyophilized for storage or downstream use, adding an additional overnight step (12–24 hr).
