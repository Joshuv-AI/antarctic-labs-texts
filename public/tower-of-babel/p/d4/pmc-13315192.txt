Paediatric autoimmune liver disease in Europe, the prospective ERN R-LIVER registry
Authors: Mikkel Malham, Gema Muñoz Bartolo, Antal Dezsöfi-Gottl, Steffen Hartleif, Loreto Hierro Llanillo, Daniel Teglmeyer, Alessio Gerussi, Virag Hives, Norman Junge, Andrea Briem-Richter, Marianne Hørby Jørgensen, ERN RARE-LIVER
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground & AimsMost literature on paediatric autoimmune liver disease (P-AILD) comprises single-centre, retrospective studies. This study aims to describe robust, real-world data for P-AILD during the first year following diagnosis, utilising data from the prospective European Reference Network (ERN) R-LIVER registry.MethodsAll patients younger than 18 years with autoimmune hepatitis (AIH) or autoimmune sclerosing cholangitis (ASC) enrolled in the ERN R-LIVER registry from January 2017 to October 2023 and with >12 months of follow-up were included. Each participating centre recorded data from three time points: diagnosis, 6 and 12 months.ResultsA total of 116 patients with P-AILD were enrolled. Seventy-one patients had AIH1, eight had AIH2, and 37 had ASC. At diagnosis, 27% had cirrhosis. Large duct disease was diagnosed in 45% of ASC. Inflammatory bowel disease was present in 14% at diagnosis. No differences were found in presentation and outcome between AIH1 and 2. Most patients (94%) began treatment with standard therapy (prednisolone with/without thiopurines), and 80% were kept on it during the first year. Complete biochemical remission was achieved by 44 (42%) at 6 months and by 45 (42%) at 1 year, while normal ALT (<45) and IgG (age dependent) levels were observed in 81 (72%) and 53 (51%), respectively, at 12 months. All patients were alive at the end of follow-up, and two required liver transplantation.ConclusionsShort-term survival in P-AILD is excellent. However, less than half of patients with P-AILD patients achieved complete biochemical remission at 1 year, with ASC and cirrhosis as main predictors of failure. These findings emphasise the need for improved therapeutic strategies.Impact and implicationsThis prospective registry study supports current treatment strategies in paediatric autoimmune liver disease by describing real-world immunosuppression use and remission outcomes. These observations are important for paediatric hepatologists and researchers, as they provide contemporary insights into treatment response and also highlight variability in clinical practice. In practice, the results can inform clinical counselling, guide follow-up intensity, and help identify areas where harmonised care pathways and further collaborative research are needed to improve outcomes.Keywords: Paediatrics, Autoimmune hepatitis, Autoimmune sclerosing cholangitis

Introduction

Paediatric-onset autoimmune liver diseases (P-AILDs) are rare, immune-mediated, chronic, and progressive inflammatory liver diseases.1,2 The disease entity includes autoimmune hepatitis (AIH) and autoimmune sclerosing cholangitis (ASC).3,4 The latter is more common in children than in adults, and besides the features of AIH, it also affects cholangiocytes, either as small or large duct disease.1 Especially in ASC, disease characteristics can evolve during the disease course, leading to reclassification as AIH or, more frequently, primary sclerosing cholangitis.2,5,6

In AIH, two different subtypes can be distinguished based on the specific autoantibodies: AIH1 is defined by positivity for antinuclear (ANA), anti-smooth (SMA) antibodies and/or soluble liver antigen-antibody (anti-SLA/LP), and AIH2 by positivity for anti-liver kidney microsomal type 1 (LKM1) and/or anti-liver cytosol type 1 (LC1) antibodies. AIH1 is more frequent than AIH2, which is primarily observed in small children.7,8 The autoantibody pattern in ASC primarily mimics that of AIH1. In children with autoimmune liver disease (AILD), 15–34% do not have detectable autoantibodies;[9], [10], [11], [12] they will be classified as AIH1. The clinical presentation does not differ significantly from adults: ∼15–20% are asymptomatic13 and up to 60% have an acute presentation.8,14 The remaining 20–30% have a chronic presentation with >6 months from debut of symptoms to diagnosis.3,14

Approximately 20–35% of children with AIH have other immune-mediated inflammatory diseases (IMID) at diagnosis or will develop them during follow-up. Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) is the most prevalent extra hepatic manifestation and is more often seen in ASC (22–45%) than in AIH (11–20%)5 with a growing prevalence.15

The standard treatment for paediatric AIH includes systemic steroids alone or in combination with azathioprine, which is added as a steroid-sparing agent.1,2 Second-line therapy includes mycophenolate mofetil in combination with systemic steroids in cases of azathioprine intolerance or insufficient response.1,2,16,17 Cyclosporine and tacrolimus (calcineurin inhibitors) are used as a second or third-line treatment in refractory cases.1,[17], [18], [19], [20] Only a few randomised controlled trials have reported on treating P-AILD,21,22 and most observational studies are retrospective.[5], [6], [7], [8], [9],16,17,19,23 Thus, treatment of P-AILD relies on adult data. Children with AILD differ from adults: they more often develop ASC,15 are more often diagnosed with AIH2,7 more often have a viral trigger,24 are more often diagnosed with seronegative AILD,25 and frequently experience other IMID.8 Hence, there is a need for prospective studies focusing on the disease progression in children. These studies can provide valuable knowledge to enhance management strategies and facilitate the design of paediatric trials.

Using the European Reference Network (ERN) R-LIVER prospective, multicentre registry, we aimed to describe autoimmune liver diseases in the paediatric population according to disease presentation, treatment patterns, remission rates, and development of other IMIDs during the first year of diagnosis.

Materials and methods

Patients <18 years of age who were enrolled in the ERN R-LIVER registry for AIH and ASC and had 12 months of follow-up were included in the study. Pseudonymised data are recorded prospectively from the time of diagnosis using the Castor Electronic Data Capture. The ERN RARE-LIVER administration manages data at the University Medical Centre Hamburg-Eppendorf. Ethics approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of the Hamburg Medical Association (PV5548) and, according to local regulations, from each centre. Six centres with approved data-sharing agreements with the University Medical Centre Hamburg-Eppendorf were invited to participate.

The diagnosis of AIH and ASC was established according to the European Society of Paediatric Gastroenterology, Hepatology and Nutrition (ESPGHAN) Hepatology Committee Position Statement.1 The ESPGHAN criteria rely on specific autoantibodies, presence of elevated IgG, presence of histology compatible with typical AIH, absence of viral hepatitis, NASH or Wilson's disease, following the simplified IAIHG criteria. However, the ESPGHAN criteria include further information on lower autoantibody titres following cut-off for children and evidence of extrahepatic autoimmune diseases or family history of autoimmune disease. ASC was diagnosed if, in addition to the above findings, cholangiography showed signs of large duct disease or if biopsy showed small duct disease. magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) was used in all centres. Results are analysed and presented according to the STROBE guidelines.26

Study population

All paediatric patients (aged <18 years) from the ERN R-LIVER registry diagnosed with AIH or ASC before their 18th birthday between January 2017 and October 2023, with a minimum of 12 months of follow-up available, were included. Patients diagnosed with primary sclerosing cholangitis alone were excluded. Written consent was obtained from parents or guardians.

Data collection

Each participating centre recorded observational data from three time points: diagnosis, 6 months, and 12 months. Information on fibrosis and inflammation was taken from the local pathology report. Although not included in the present publication, data are continuously recorded for every 12 months of follow-up. In a validation step for this study, all centres were asked to confirm the AILD diagnosis and the presence of IBD in their cases at presentation and after 12 months.

Data analysis

We extracted records from the ERN R-LIVER registry for all patients who fulfilled the inclusion criteria. Subsequently, the data were checked for completeness and duplicates. The data set was tested for outliers and plausibility. The included centres were contacted, if necessary, to cross-check the data with patient files. Missing and incorrect values were then coded accordingly. The treating physician classified the AIH subtype based on the antibody profile.

In the data analysis, we explored remission as defined by the combination of alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and immunoglobulin G (IgG) within normal values for age (referred to as complete biochemical remission [CBR]).27 To further explore the response to therapy, and in accordance with the findings by Maggiore et al.,28 normalisation of ALT using both below 45 and 25 U/L were carried out. As the upper limit of IgG increases from 4 g/L to 14 g/L29 during childhood, normalisation of IgG was carried out separately. We further describe IgG divided by the upper level of the normal values for age (IgG × upper normal level [UNL]). Severe hepatitis was defined as ALT >500 U/L and acute liver failure according to the paediatric definition as international normalised ratio (INR) >2 or INR >1.5 with encephalopathy.30 Inflammation and fibrosis were recorded as proposed by Ishak et al.31 or the METAVIR cooperative study group. We grouped the grade of inflammation (minimal, mild, moderate, severe) and stage of fibrosis (none, mild, moderate, severe) based on previous proposals.32,33

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics are percentages or medians with IQR. Blood markers are presented as means with SD. We used Fisher’s exact test for categorical data to compare groups at baseline and Student's t test for continuous data. A two-tailed p value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. To compare categorical variables at 12 months between groups, we calculated relative risks with 95% CI using the AILD subgroup as exposure and the specific categorical variable as outcome. Unless otherwise stated, observations with missing values were omitted from each specific calculation. Sankey diagrams were created with SankeyMATIC.34

Results

A total of 124 patients with AILD were enrolled and completed 1 year of follow-up. Eight patients were excluded as no diagnostic biopsy was recorded. The final cohort included 116 patients (26 from Madrid, 27 from Copenhagen, 27 from Hamburg, 15 from Budapest, 11 from Tübingen, nine from Hannover, and one from Milan, Monza). Of these, 71 patients had AIH1, eight had AIH2, and 37 had ASC. Further baseline characteristics are presented in Table 1. In AIH1 and ASC the majority were male (58% and 57%, respectively), whereas all patients with AIH2 were female. Six patients (5%) were obese with a BMI z-score above +2 SD, and 14 (12%) were overweight with a z-score between +1 and +2 SD. Because of the limitations of the study design with a lack of precise age, these numbers might be overestimated. None of the biopsies were described with metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease during the study period. An additional 57 patients were diagnosed during the study period, but did not reach their 12-month follow-up and were not included in the present study.

Characteristics at diagnosis

Table 2 presents the main diagnostic characteristics. The fibrosis stage was reported either as Ishak (n = 52) or Desmet Scheuer stage (n = 58), and the inflammation grade was reported as either modified hepatic activity index (n = 62) or Desmet Scheuer grade (n = 30). The distribution of the fibrosis grade and inflammation stage between AILD subtypes is presented in detail in Table 2. In short, 24–30% had advanced fibrosis or cirrhosis at diagnosis. Cholangiography was performed within the first 6 months in 94 cases (81%), of which 89 (95%) used MRCP. Among the 37 patients diagnosed with ASC, 35 underwent cholangiography at the time of diagnosis. Large duct disease was identified in 17 of these patients (46%), and the remaining 20 patients had small duct disease on biopsy. Interestingly, only one patient (13%) with AIH2 presented with severe hepatitis, which is lower than in both AIH1 (59%) and ASC (42%).

Autoantibodies

The distribution of positive autoantibodies is presented in Table 2. In AIH1, 40 (57%) patients had more than one positive autoantibody, and six (9%) had no autoantibodies. For AIH2 and ASC, four (50%) and 20 (52%), respectively, had more than one autoantibody, while none and two (6%), respectively, had no autoantibodies detected. Among the nine patients who were seronegative, four had elevated IgG for their age; all had ALT levels >100 U/L, all had classic AIH features on biopsies, and all responded to therapy. Data on LC1 antibodies were not collected routinely and thus were not included in our analysis.

Comorbidities

Twenty-five patients (22%) were diagnosed with additional IMIDs. The distribution among AILD subtypes is presented in Table 1. Pre-existing IBD was found in 7%, 0%, and 30% of patients with AIH1, AIH2, and ASC, respectively. During the 12 months of follow-up, an additional six patients developed IBD (three in AIH1 and three in ASC). Other immune-mediated inflammatory diseases were seen in 8%, 0%, and 0% of patients with AIH1, AIH2, and ASC, respectively.

Discussion

These are the first prospective paediatric data from the R-liver registry, including 124 paediatric patients with AILD followed for over 12 months. The overall outcome at 1 year after presentation is good, with all being alive and only two children requiring liver transplantation.

Our data on disease severity and the distribution of ASC and AIH types 1 and 2 are similar to those of other retrospective paediatric studies. However, our findings do not indicate a difference in presentation and outcome between AIH1 and AIH2, which is contrary to older reports.2 LC1 was not an obligatory option in the database, which might explain the number of patients with AIH2 on the lower end (9.5% vs. 10–20% in most studies7,8,28) and might have blurred the results. However, this finding contradicts the current belief that AIH2 constitutes a more aggressive disease phenotype at presentation.1

Of the nine patients who were seronegative, four had elevated IgG, and all nine had histological changes indicative of autoimmune liver disease. The number of patients who were seronegative is consistent with previous paediatric reports.35 They all had AILD on their initial biopsies, and they all responded to therapy; thus, the diagnosis is reliable. It is unknown if these patients represent a specific subtype of AIH.25 Unfortunately, our study is not designed to further elaborate on this issue.

It is well known that comorbidity with other IMIDs is high in paediatric AILD, with IBD being the most frequent coexistent IMID.5,15 We confirmed that one in five patients with AILD has IBD at presentation, mainly AIH1 and ASC, and a further 5% develop IBD during the first year. This underlines the importance of the recent ESPGHAN recommendation for screening patients with AILD for IBD.36 Interestingly, other IMIDs are primarily observed in patients with AIH1, indicating that classification into subtypes remains relevant even though we did not confirm differences in presentation and outcome.

Historical data has shown that AIH in adults has a high mortality rate of around 55% if left untreated37,38 and standard therapy with azathioprine and prednisolone improves survival.39 The treatment aims to obtain complete remission and avoid the progression of liver damage, minimising the adverse effects of medicines. Data from an adult study indicates that a rapid decline in ALT at the first 8 weeks after initiation of treatment positively influences liver-related mortality and morbidity and the remission rate at six and 12 months.40 In a large cohort of patients with AIH (n = 2,559), CBR within 6 months after immunosuppression did predict a favourable outcome.41 Even though paediatric patients were included, age was not an independent factor, and paediatric data cannot be extracted from this study. However, the results are in line with our findings; normal ALT was achieved in a high proportion of the cases at 6 months (70%), and only nine more cases (7%) achieved normal ALT between 6 and 12 months. Because a small number of patients received second- or third-line therapy, we were unable to perform any sub-calculation for this patient group. However, it highlights the need to improve the treatment regimen for the 25–30% of children who do not achieve remission at 6 months and the need to determine the optimal time for CBR in a paediatric cohort.

Looking into subtypes of AILD in our cohort, we found a trend towards a higher rate of children with AIH2 achieving remission at 6 and 12 months. Patients with ASC were less likely to achieve remission 1 year after presentation, which is in line with other studies.5,16,41 This observation could indicate that ASC is a more severe disease phenotype, but also that clinicians are uncertain what the optimal approach is to these patients as they are not well characterised in the literature with only scarce knowledge of the best treatment protocols.

In our cohort, we found an inverse association between having advanced fibrosis or cirrhosis and being in remission after 1 year. This is in line with a study by Slooter et al.,41 reporting that cirrhosis at presentation is an independent factor of not achieving CBR at 12 months and liver-related death. Thus, early diagnosis and early treatment may benefit patient survival.

In our study, most children began treatment with standard therapy and remained on this regimen throughout the study. Our data confirm the effectiveness of prednisolone and azathioprine in children also. As only minor improvements in remission rate occur between 6 and 12 months, our data indicate that if remission is not achieved by 6 months, a change in treatment regimen could be warranted. When remission was defined as CBR with both normal ALT and IgG values for age, our results were similar to the adult data from the same database (R-Liver); remission at 6 months: 50%, 12 months: 63%.27 This contradicts the belief that AILD is more aggressive in the paediatric population than adult AIH. Other explanations why our findings do not diverge from those reported in the adult literature include better adherence, higher prednisolone doses at induction/kg body weight, and longer periods on prednisolone in children than adults. Epidemiological data have shown higher mortality rates in paediatric-onset AIH than adult-onset AIH, although data are not fully comparable.42,43 Despite this, data are urgently needed to further our understanding of when remission rates should be achieved in children to minimise morbidity and mortality.

We report not only CBR, but also levels of ALT and IgG separately. CBR has, to our knowledge, never been validated in children and may have some challenges in this age group, as the normal level of IgG increases with age. In the R-Liver register, we found a higher IgG level at baseline (IgG × UNL) in children compared with adults (1.7 vs. 1.2 in adults), so time to CBR will naturally take longer in children. Thus, taking this into account, analysing IgG and ALT separately in children may be more appropriate.

Normal ALT at 12 months was not achieved in 22% of our population. It is well known that sufficient azathioprine metabolites, a high 6-thioguanine nucleotide (TGN) level, and an appropriate methylmercaptopurine (MMP) level are predictors of remission, together with the activity of thymidine methyltransferase. Our data indicate that the prescribed azathioprine dose (1.1 mg/kg) was sufficient; however, data on azathioprine metabolism were unavailable.

The collection of data is limited by the scarcity of information on the diagnostic criteria of ASC and additional immune-mediated inflammatory diseases, for example IBD, with a specific interest in the paediatric AILD population. This was overcome by asking each centre to validate the diagnosis and add specific information about IBD. Information on LC1 relevant for AIH2 was not reported routinely. Consequently, the number of patients who were seronegative may be overestimated and the high number of patients (∼50%) with more than one autoantibody is likely to be underestimated.

In conclusion, short-term survival in paediatric AILD is excellent. Most children with AILD are treated with standard therapy. Although the remission rates were comparable with adult data from the same register, new treatment strategies are still needed for the 25–30% not in remission at 6–12 months. This highlights the need for paediatric data evaluating treatment outcomes in large multinational cohorts. These data are the first results of an ongoing registry hosted by ERN RARE-LIVER and can serve as a cornerstone for a better understanding and treatment of P-AILD.
