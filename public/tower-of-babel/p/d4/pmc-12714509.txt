Persistent Ectopy Unmasking Coronary Artery Disease and Familial Hypercholesterolemia in a 32-Year-Old Woman
Authors: Nguyen Van Hinh, Fletcher Bell, Tong Thi Thu Hang, Nguyen Huy Toan, Pham The Tho, Nguyen Thi Kieu Ly, Pham Truong Son
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCoronary artery disease (CAD) is uncommon in young adults although rising in Southeast Asia. Myocarditis is typically considered in cases where viral symptoms precede cardiac injury. Atypical presentations of CAD, including ventricular arrhythmias, can obscure ischemic pathology and delay diagnosis especially in women compared to men.Case SummarySignificant coronary stenosis due to familial hypercholesterolemia was discovered through cardiac magnetic resonance imaging, multislice computed tomography coronary angiography, echocardiography, and coronary catheterization to overturn an original diagnosis of postviral myocarditis.DiscussionCAD in younger people is associated with traditional risk factors such as smoking, dyslipidemia, diabetes, South East Asian descent, and a family history. The identification of a heterozygous low-density lipoprotein receptor mutation confirmed (familial hypercholesterolemia) in this patient and was a critical contributor to this patient's early-onset CAD.Take-Home MessagesPersistent arrhythmias should prompt further evaluation, including advanced imaging, to rule out occult CAD. Myocardial scarring seen after a presumed viral illness does not always indicate myocarditis in a young person.

History of Presentation

A 32-year-old Vietnamese female with a family history of premature coronary artery disease (CAD) (premature death in father and grandfather at around age 50 years from myocardial infarction), a nonsmoker, and employed as a factory worker, presented to 108 Military Central Hospital in Hanoi, Vietnam, with palpitations for 2 weeks without chest pain or dyspnea.Take-Home Messages•Persistent arrhythmias should prompt further evaluation, including advanced imaging, to rule out occult coronary artery disease.•Myocardial scarring seen after a presumed viral illness does not always indicate myocarditis in a young person.

Three months prior to her admission to our service, she experienced fever and constant, sharp, substernal chest pain that did not abate with rest and did not radiate. She was admitted to the General Cardiology Service but not seen by electrophysiology. Her electrocardiogram (ECG) (Figure 1) and echocardiogram gathered at that initial presentation were unremarkable. Serial cardiac enzymes were obtained, initially elevated at 181 (ng/L), which peaked at 2,512 (ng/L). Given her age, sex, and nonanginal pain, her pretest probability for CAD was low.1 Given there was low suspicion for CAD, no workup, including a lipid panel, was sent at that time. Notably our hospital is a 2,000-bed hospital that typically sees 4 to 5,000 patients daily. Access to catheterization lab is available, but resources in this environment are constrained. She was treated with colchicine for presumed myocarditis and discharged 1 week later after her symptoms abated.Figure 1Serial 12-Lead Electrocardiograms on Initial Admission (5 Hours Apart)

Past Medical History

The patient did not have any significant past medical history.

Differential diagnosis

The patient's first admission, characterized by fever, chest pain, and significantly elevated cardiac enzymes, was initially attributed to myocarditis, a common consideration in young patients with preceding viral symptoms.2 Her subsequent presentation and reason for this admission were thought to be ectopy in the setting of resolving myocarditis. A missed acute coronary syndrome (ACS) event in the setting of CAD was not initially considered.

Investigations

A 12-lead ECG showed frequent bigeminal premature ventricular contractions (PVCs) with a left ventricular origin (Figure 2). A 24-hour Holter ECG (done in the hospital; no telemetry is available outside of the intensive care unit) revealed a PVC burden of 50%, all monomorphic without any episodes of ventricular tachycardia.Figure 212-Lead Electrocardiogram Showed Bigeminal Premature Ventricular Contractions With a Left Ventricular Origin (Negative in DII, DIII, aVF, Early Transition in Precordial Leads)

Laboratory tests, including a complete blood count, renal and liver function tests, electrolyte levels, thyroid hormone levels, cardiac enzymes, and C-reactive protein, were all within normal limits. N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide was elevated at 1,912 pg/mL. Her lipid panel was as follows: total cholesterol of 219.04 mg/dL; triglycerides of 141.71 mg/dL; high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) of 27.46 mg/dL; and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) of 158.65 mg/dL.

Echocardiography demonstrated mild left ventricular dysfunction (ejection fraction [EF]: 48%), left ventricular end-diastolic diameter of 53 mm, and end-systolic diameter of 40 mm, without regional wall motion abnormalities.

Given the suspicion of a postmyocarditis scar-related arrhythmia, a cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) was performed (Figure 3). It revealed left ventricular dilation (102 mL/m2) with diffuse, transmural myocardial scarring (50%-75%) involving the basal septum and mid-to-apical inferior wall, overall affecting 14% of the left ventricular mass. Hypokinesis and hypoperfusion were observed in the septal and inferior segments from base to apex in the territory of the right coronary artery (RCA).Figure 3Diffuse, Transmural Myocardial Scarring in Cardiac Magnetic Resonance Imaging

A multislice computed tomography coronary angiography was performed (Figure 4). It showed severe stenosis of the proximal RCA.Figure 4The Multislice Computed Tomography Coronary Angiography Showed Severe Stenosis of the Proximal Right Coronary Artery

Coronary angiography confirmed a 95% stenosis in the proximal and mid-RCA, with an additional 40% stenosis in the proximal left anterior descending artery (Figure 5). A drug-eluting stent was successfully placed in the RCA.Figure 5Severe Stenosis in the Right Coronary Artery on Angiogram Prior to Drug-Eluting Stent Placement

Given the high PVC burden, an electrophysiological study was performed at the same admission. It identified the earliest activation site in the inferoseptal region of the left ventricle, with the earliest local activation time of 32 milliseconds (Figure 6). Radiofrequency ablation from the endocardial surface failed to eliminate the PVCs. A postablation Holter ECG showed a PVC burden reduction to 38% (from 50%), and the patient reported symptomatic relief.Figure 6The Earliest Local Activation Time of 32 Milliseconds in the Inferoseptal Region of the Left Ventricle

Given her family history and the CAD found on angiography, testing for familial hypercholesterolemia (FH) was sent. The genetic panel for FH came back 4 weeks after her admission to our service, demonstrating a heterozygous pathogenic mutation in her LDL receptor (LDLR). The final diagnosis was FH, chronic coronary syndrome, heart failure with mildly reduced EF, and symptomatic ventricular ectopy. Further genetic tests for her 2 children showed that her 11-year-old daughter also had the heterozygous pathogenic mutation in LDL receptor. The lipid panel for her daughter showed total cholesterol of 283 mg/dL, triglycerides of 139 mg/dL, HDL-C of 44 mg/dL, and LDL-C of 212 mg/dL. The patient was started on dual antiplatelet therapy, a high-intensity statin (rosuvastatin), ezetimibe, an angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitor, a sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitor, an aldosterone antagonist, and a beta-blocker.

Outcome and Follow-Up

At a 1-month follow-up, the patient reported no chest pain or dyspnea, and a Holter monitor demonstrated a significant reduction in PVC burden even further to 17% (from 38%). After 2 months on the rosuvastatin and ezetimibe, the lipid panel improved to total cholesterol of 151.6 mg/dL, triglycerides of 132.9 mg/dL, HDL-C of 33.6 mg/dL, and LDL-C of 100.5 mg/dL.

Discussion

This patient's presentation with symptomatic palpitations, shown to be frequent PVCs with a high PVC burden (50% on Holter monitoring), was a pivotal diagnostic clue. Although all modern, advanced cardiac imaging in Hanoi is available, access to it is limited given the high patient volume. Pretest probability is leaned on heavily in this high-volume environment. While PVCs are commonly associated with her original diagnosis of myocarditis, their persistence for many months after the initial admission prompted further investigation for an ischemic substrate.3

Cardiac MRI revealed transmural myocardial scarring in the RCA territory, consistent with ischemia given the correlated angiographic findings.4 Both myocarditis and ischemia can produce similar patterns on cardiac MRI.5 Coronary angiography was critical in confirming the diagnosis of chronic coronary syndrome and guiding management. The electrophysiological study further localized the PVC origin to the inferoseptal left ventricle, an area corresponding to the ischemic territory, reinforcing the link between ischemia and arrhythmogenesis. Unlike myocarditis-related arrhythmias, which are often transient, ischemia-related arrhythmias may indicate a persistent arrhythmogenic substrate, increasing the risk of malignant ventricular arrhythmias.3

CAD in young females is rare, contributing to diagnostic uncertainty and potential delays in recognition and biased care.6,7 In this case, the absence of traditional risk factors likely led to the initial misdiagnosis of myocarditis. However, the family history of premature CAD and the subsequent discovery of significant coronary stenosis in the setting of FH suggest that an ACS or demand ischemia may have been overlooked initially.

The identification of a heterozygous LDLR mutation confirmed FH. FH is an autosomal dominant disorder characterized by impaired LDL cholesterol clearance due to mutations in genes such as LDLR, APOB, or PCSK9.8 With a prevalence of approximately 1:200 to 1:500 in the general population, heterozygous FH significantly increases the risk of premature atherosclerosis, often manifesting as CAD in the third or fourth decade of life.9 In this patient, the LDL-C level of 158.65 mmol/L, while elevated, was not markedly high, which highlights the variability in phenotypic expression of FH. The LDLR mutation likely accelerated atherosclerosis, contributing to the severe proximal RCA stenosis observed. Notably, FH is underdiagnosed, and this case emphasizes the importance of genetic testing in young patients with CAD, particularly those with a family history of premature cardiovascular events.9 Cascade screening of first-degree relatives is recommended to identify and treat affected individuals early, potentially preventing adverse outcomes.9 In this case, by screening for relatives, the patient's daughter was diagnosed with FH much earlier.

Management involved multiple therapeutic approaches including percutaneous coronary intervention, high-intensity statin therapy, and dual antiplatelet therapy for her CAD; heart failure management given her newly reduced EF; and ablation given her symptomatic ectopy. The diagnosis of FH necessitates lifelong aggressive lipid-lowering therapy to achieve LDL-C levels below 70 mg/dL, as recommended for secondary prevention in FH patients.10

Conclusions

This case highlights the diagnostic challenges in differentiating postmyocarditis arrhythmias from ischemic heart disease in young patients. Comprehensive imaging, including cardiac MRI and coronary angiography, is essential in evaluating persistent arrhythmias and unexplained left ventricular dysfunction.
