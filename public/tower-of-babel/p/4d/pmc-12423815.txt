The Genesis and Influence of Pore-Throat Structural Multifractionation in Dense Sandstone Reservoirs as an Example of Chang 8 in Zhenjing Area, Ordos Basin, North China
Authors: Wen Tian, Jiandong Li, Zhengwei Fang, Yingfan Zhao, Jie Xiang, Deyao Sun
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The heterogeneity of the pore-throat structure in tight sandstone is a key factor influencing its reservoir quality and fluid seepage capacity. This study, based on 20 tight sandstone samples from the Chang 8 section in the Zhenjing area of the Ordos Basin, employs a range of experimental techniques, including cast thin section analysis, X-ray diffraction, high-pressure mercury injection, nitrogen adsorption, and nuclear magnetic resonance. These methods, combined with mineralogical and petrological analyses, are used to investigate the fractal characteristics of the pore-throat structure, its genesis, and its impact on fluid movability. The results reveal that the combination of different mineral compositions and pore-throat types leads to three distinct pore-throat structures: Type I, characterized by large pore throats, high connectivity, and weak heterogeneity; Type II, exhibiting a bimodal distribution with well-organized pore throats; and Type III, dominated by intercrystalline pores, which show poor connectivity and physical properties. Overall, the pore-throat structure is highly heterogeneous, exhibiting multiple fractal characteristics with triple fractals as the dominant form and double fractals as secondary. The large pore-throat fractal (D 3) is primarily controlled by the number of large pore throats and is also influenced by pore morphology and the development of interparticle pores. The middle-pore-throat fractal (D 2) is mainly determined by the number of medium-sized pore throats and is constrained by the development of interparticle dissolution pores and microfractures. The small pore-throat fractal (D 1) depends on the content of intercrystalline pores and the variation in the microporosity. The overall heterogeneity of the system reflects the coupling of pore throats across different scales in terms of spatial distribution, geometry, and development. Additionally, mineral composition has a certain effect on the fractal characteristics of the pore-throat structure. Rigid particles promote pore distribution homogenization, while carbonate minerals increase the fractal complexity of pore throats. An increase in clay mineral content suppresses the fractal dimension of small pore throats but enhances the complexity of medium-sized pore throats. The fractal dimension of pore throats is positively correlated with fluid mobility, with D 3 having the greatest influence, followed by D 2, while D 1 has a minor effect. The development of large pore throats enhances connectivity and reduces network complexity, thus improving the fluid permeability and movable saturation. Conversely, the accumulation of clay minerals significantly inhibits the fluid mobility. These findings demonstrate that the nonhomogeneity and multiple fractal characteristics of the microscopic pore-throat structure primarily control the fluid seepage performance of tight sandstone reservoirs, providing critical theoretical support for the evaluation and efficient development of such reservoirs.

Introduction

Tight sandstone gas, as an unconventional natural gas resource, has garnered significant global attention due to its substantial reserve potential. , However, achieving cost-effective production remains a major challenge. Tight sandstone serves as the primary reservoir rock for tight sandstone gas deposits, characterized by low porosity (<10%), ultralow permeability (absolute permeability <1 mD), strong heterogeneity, and weak correlations between gas distribution, production, and complex reservoir properties. These factors introduce considerable risks in the exploration and development of tight sandstone gas.

The microporous structure is widely recognized as a key factor responsible for the low permeability and porosity of sandstones, controlling the physical and chemical interactions within natural porous media, particularly the mobility of fluids and hydrocarbons. Consequently, numerous studies have been conducted over the years to characterize the microscopic pore structure of tight sandstones. , Despite the proposal of a variety of parameters, such as porosity, pore size distribution, volume, and surface area to describe the average pore properties, traditional Euclidean geometry has proven limited in capturing the microscopic inhomogeneities of these materials. As a result, the fractal dimension has been accepted for characterizing the geometry of irregular objects with self-similarity. , A fractal is a geometric structure that exhibits self-similarity across different scales, meaning that its shape retains a certain regularity whether viewed at the macro- or microscale. Three main types of fractal theories are currently used: the Corey (1954) model, the Brooks–Corey model, and the Li (2010) model. It is important to note that, unlike regular mathematical fractals, natural fractalssuch as those found in rocksdisplay statistical self-similarity, occurring within a finite range and represented by a series of fractals with varying dimensions. Given the fractal nature of pores in naturally porous reservoirs, , this concept has been applied to the study of hydrocarbons and reservoir rocks, including coal, shale, and tight sandstones. −

Pore fractal dimensions (D) can be determined using a variety of techniques, including imaging-based methods, fluid immersion, radiation-based approaches, and gas adsorption analyses. However, the determination of fractal dimensions in tight sandstones is dependent on the technique employed, and each method is limited by its ability to fully capture the overall pore structure. Currently, nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) techniques, high-pressure mercury injection (HPMI), and X-ray computed tomography (X-CT) are the most commonly used methods for this purpose. − While many studies have explored the measurement of fractal dimensions in tight sandstones, there is still limited research on the fractal characteristics of microscopic pore-throat structures in these rocks. Specifically, the controlling factors and mechanisms behind the formation of microscopic pore-throat fractals in tight sandstones remain poorly understood. It is widely accepted that the geometry and morphology of pore throats in tight sandstones are the result of coupled effects from sedimentation and diagenesis, with the fractal characteristics of pore throats being influenced by the physical and chemical interactions between minerals and fluids. Recent studies in shale and coal reservoirs have shown that fractal dimensions correlate with organic carbon content, specular reflectance, ash content, volatile matter, and clay mineral content. However, due to the complex petrological, mineralogical, and fluid–mineralogical interactions in tight sandstones, the causes and controlling factors of multifractal porosity in these rocks remain unclear, necessitating further investigation into additional influencing factors.

In addition, the characteristics of movable fluid storage in the pore throats of tight sandstone reservoirs have been a significant focus of research, as the structure of microscopic pore throats plays a crucial role in macroscopic fluid propagation. While some studies have explored the correlation between the geometric properties of pore throats in tight sandstones and fluid transport parameterssuch as movable fluid saturation and movable fluid porositythere is still a lack of in-depth research on how pore-throat D influences these fluid transport properties. The relationship between the pore-throat D and fluid transport parameters remains underexplored. Previous studies on reservoir quality, mineral composition, pore structure parameters, clay mineral content, and the nature of movable fluids in various lithologies suggest that fluid mobility is the result of a complex interplay of factors, including sedimentation and diagenesis. Consequently, there is a need for a comprehensive and systematic investigation into fluid storage characteristics and mobility within the pore-throat space. This study focuses on the tight sandstone reservoir of the Upper Triassic Chang 8 section in the Zhenjing area of the Ordos Basin. A series of tests, including cast thin section (CTS), scanning electron microscope (SEM), nitrogen adsorption (LTAN), HPMI, and NMR repulsion experiments, were conducted on 20 tight sandstone samples. By integrating fractal theory with petrological and mineralogical analyses, as well as fluid movability evaluations, this study quantitatively describes the formation causes and controlling factors of pore-throat fractals and examines their impact on the seepage behavior of movable fluids.

Geologic Background

The Ordos Basin is located in north-central China, and its internal tectonic structure is stable, faults are not developed, and the whole has a tectonic pattern of north–south uplift, westward thrusting, and eastward lifting. ,, The basin can be divided into six first-order tectonic units: the western rim of the Sheltering Impulse Belt, the western Tianhuan Depression, the central Yishang Slope, the eastern Jinsi Flexure Belt, the southern Weibei Uplift, and the northern Yimeng Uplift (Figure a). The Zhenjing area is located in the eastern flank of the Tianhuan depression in the southwestern part of the basin, and tectonically exhibits a gently west-dipping monoclinal tectonics, with locally developed nasal uplift tectonics, and two sets of NE- and NW-oriented fracture systems formed under the action of the regional tectonic stress field provide an important channel for the seepage of hydrocarbons. , The stratigraphy of the area is well-developed, and from bottom to top, it consists of Paleozoic Ordovician carbonate basement, Triassic Extension Formation, Jurassic coal strata, Cretaceous fluvial and lacustrine deposits, and Quaternary wind-formed loess, and the Chang 8 sections of the Upper Triassic Extension Formation of the Zhenjing area were formed in the Late Triassic lake-basin delta foreground environment, and the supply of the NE-oriented Yinshan Paleocontinental material source formed a depositional system dominated by the submerged diversion channel, the estuarine dam, and the middens sand, and the sand body was 3–8 m thick. , The sand body is distributed as an isolated lens with a thickness of 3–8 m and a width of 200–500 m, which is typical of the spatial configuration of the coexistence of “superposed continuous sheet” and “isolated lens”. The reservoir lithology is characterized by fine- to medium-grained feldspathic sandstone, and the reservoir space is dominated by residual intergranular pores and dissolution pores, of which the reservoir porosity ranges from 7.0% to 13.0%, with an average of 9.5%. The permeability is mainly distributed in the range of 0.01–1.00 mD, with an average value of 0.18 mD, which is a typical tight sandstone reservoir with relatively low porosity and permeability, and shows strong nonhomogeneity and significant stress sensitivity, and oil and gas reservoirs have the characteristics of “self-generated and self-storage”, and the black shale in section 7 of the Yanchang section is a high-quality hydrocarbon source rock, which constitutes a good reservoir-cover combination with the overlying Chang 7 mudstone. The black shale of the Chang 7 is a high-quality hydrocarbon source rock, and the overlying Chang 7 mudstone constitutes a good reservoir-cover combination, and the reservoir type is mainly lithological trap. , As of 2023, the area has proven oil geological reserves of more than 1 billion tons and has built several billion-ton oilfields such as Huaqing and Xifeng, with good exploration and development prospects. ,,

Experimental Methods and Methodology

Samples Information and Experimental Methods

Core observations and descriptions were conducted in 20 wells from the Chang 8 section of the Upper Triassic Yanchang Formation in the Zhenjing area of the Ordos Basin. Blocky fine-grained sandstones with favorable petrophysical properties were selected as test samples from the target section of each well (Figure b). In total, 20 tight sandstone core samples were collected (Table ), which were drilled parallel to the stratigraphic direction into cylindrical plugs measuring 2.5 and 3.5 cm in length. These samples were then immersed in a mixed alcohol–benzene solution for more than 20 days to remove any residual hydrocarbons, followed by drying at 120 °C for 48 h. To ensure consistency and accuracy across experiments, the preparation of all samples was carried out using a logically integrated procedure. This process adhered to the principle of conducting all tests on the same specimen, prioritizing nondestructive analyses over destructive tests to minimize sampling discrepancies and avoid any adverse effects from destructive testing. Initial physical property and NMR tests were conducted on the intact core plugs, followed by CTS, SEM, XRD, HPMI, and LTNA tests on intact core samples, which were sectioned at varying thicknesses. The primary experimental techniques used in this study are outlined as follows.(1)Physical properties testing of rock samples: Porosity and permeability were measured using a CMS-300 corer from Xi’an Petroleum University. The measurement process involved gas expansion and pressure transient techniques, with a peripheral pressure of approximately 1.5 MPa, to determine helium porosity and permeability at an ambient temperature of 20 °C. Samples were selected at regular intervals to ensure their representativeness across the range of porosity and permeability typical for tight gas sandstones in the stratigraphic section.(2)Mineral grain composition and XRD analysis: The samples were ground to a thickness of less than 0.3 mm and mounted on slides to create thin sections for examination. These sections were analyzed using a FEI optical microscope at the Key Laboratory of Hydrocarbon Formation Geology, Xi’an Petroleum University. Mineral content and proportions of different pore types were estimated using the point-counting method, with a minimum of 200 points counted per micrograph and a margin of error of 2%. Approximately 1.0 cm thick and 1.0 cm wide samples with polished, and gold-plated surfaces were placed in an FEI Quanta 400 scanning electron microscope for secondary imaging. The samples were dried and ground into powders finer than 40 μm at temperatures below 60 °C. A 40 mg portion of the dried powder was placed in a 10 mL test tube, to which 0.7 mL of distilled water was added, and the mixture was stirred. The particles were then dispersed by ultrasonic waves, and the suspension was poured onto a slide and air-dried for XRD testing. XRD analysis was performed at the State Key Laboratory of Continental Dynamics, Northwestern University, using a Bruker D8 Advance X-ray diffractometer. The test was conducted at a temperature of 24 °C and a relative humidity of 35%, with Cu–Kα radiation (λ = 0.154 nm). The diffraction angle (2θ) was measured with an accuracy of 0.02°, and a slit width of 0.6 mm was used. The Lynxeye_XE-T detector quantified the peak areas of the mineral characteristic diffraction peaks. A mercury porosimeter was employed to apply sufficiently high pressure (55,000 psi, 379.31 MPa) to force mercury into all accessible pores (≥0.00196 μm), allowing for the measurement of pore volume. Application of Fractal Theory

Fractal theory has been widely applied to the study of the pore structure and heterogeneity in tight sandstone reservoirs. Currently, three main fractal models are commonly used (Figure ): the Corey (1954) model, the Brooks–Corey model, and the Li (2010) model. To further assess the applicability of these fractal models in this study, the three models were fitted to experimental data obtained from the HPMI tests. Among them, the Li (2010) model demonstrated clear multisegment linearity and provided sufficient resolution to distinguish between large and small pore throats. Mineral Composition and Contents of 20 Samples

The average quartz content, estimated by point counting of the CTS micrographs (Table ), is 31.9%. A small amount of authigenic quartz fills the pores, reducing the primary pore space. The average feldspar content is 36.6%, with plagioclase feldspar being the dominant type. The average rock debris content is 17.0%, and the average content of fillers is 14.5%, of which authigenic clay minerals make up 9.9%. According to the XRD test results, Illite is the predominant clay mineral in the study area, with an average relative content of 56.7%, followed by chlorite and kaolinite, which account for 23.3% and 14.7%, respectively. The mixed layer of Illite/montmorillonite (I/S) has the lowest content, with an average relative content of 5.3%.

Pore-Throat Types and Morphology

Based on the analysis of CTS and SEM results, six types of pores were identified (Figure , Table ), including residual interparticles pores, feldspar dissolution pores, interparticles dissolution pores, lithic dissolution pores, intercrystalline pores, and microfractures. Residual interparticle pores are primarily retained between grains, where chlorite adheres to the surface, forming a thin film cement, which results in well-developed interparticle pores (Figure a,d). Dissolution pores form as a result of the dissolution of unstable mineral constituents, such as feldspars, volcaniclastic debris, matrix material, and clay minerals, both within and around the rock (Figure a, c, e, and g). Intercrystalline pores are mainly micropores formed by clay cements (e.g., kaolinite, chlorite, and Illite), with pore-throat radii typically less than 0.1 μm (Figure b,f). Volcanic detrital material is dissolved and transformed into chlorite, Illite, and other clay minerals, which contributes to the development of secondary pores (Figure c,g). Microfractures are jagged discontinuities caused by tectonic stress or the diagenesis of brittle minerals with fracture radii typically greater than 1 μm (Figure a).

Pore Structure Characterization Parameters

HPMI, LTAN, and NMR Results

The capillary pressure curves and pore structure parameters are shown in Figure and Table . Based on the morphological characteristics of the Hg injection and drainage curves, the 20 samples can be classified into three groups, each representing a distinct type of pore-throat structure. The capillary pressure curves for Type I samples exhibit a long, gentle slope. The average pore-throat radius (R a) ranges from 0.28 to 0.48 μm, with an average value of 0.36 μm, which facilitates the initial intrusion of mercury. The pore-throat assemblage mainly consists of residual intergranular pores, feldspar dissolution pores, and lamellar or curved throats (Figure c). This type has the highest mercury saturation (S max), averaging 89.09%, and the highest withdrawal efficiency (W e), averaging 35.79%, indicating that Type I pore-throat structures have superior pore-throat connectivity. The capillary pressure curve for Type II samples features two short horizontal sections (Figure d), with R a ranging from 0.12 to 0.32 μm, and an average value of 0.24 μm (Figure e). The pore types are mainly feldspar dissolution and intergranular pores, while the throat types primarily consist of curved lamellae and tubes (Figure f). Type II pore-throat structures exhibit a relatively high withdrawal efficiency, averaging 34.24%, and better pore-throat connectivity, which is attributed to the development of feldspar-dissolved pores. Type III samples show short, flat capillary pressure curves, with gently concave pressure curves, indicating lower pore-throat connectivity compared to Type I samples. These samples are primarily composed of a combination of intergranular pores and tubular throats with the smallest R a value, averaging 0.14 μm, and the lowest W e, averaging 23.51%.

Pore-Throat Structure Parameters and Movable Fluid Parameters Obtained from Various Experimental Results

The low-temperature nitrogen adsorption isotherms of the samples from the study area exhibited an inverse “S″ shape, , and the hysteresis loops were all of the H-type. Based on the differences in the morphology of the adsorption and desorption isotherms, the samples were classified into three typical pore-throat structure types (Figure ). At low relative pressures (P/P < 0.42), the adsorption and desorption isotherms largely overlapped and rose gradually. As P/P 0 increased, the adsorption capacity increased rapidly and capillary condensation occurred. At the maximum relative pressure (P/P 0 = 1), the adsorption and desorption curves nearly coincided. Additionally, the hysteresis loop area gradually increased from Type I to Type III pore-throat structures, suggesting a greater presence of micropores in Type III samples.

Full PSD Characteristics

To ensure testing accuracy, a pore-throat radius of 0.01 μm was selected as the cutoff point. For pore throats with r < 0.01 μm, the LTNA experiment was used, with a testing accuracy range of 1.8–200.0 nm. For pore throats with r > 0.01 μm, the HPMI experiment was selected, offering a testing accuracy range of 3.6 nm to 10.0 μm. In this study, the results from both methods were integrated to produce pore-throat radius distribution curves for each sample (Figure a–c) and NMR-derived T 2 spectra of movable fluid, allowing for a comprehensive characterization of the pore-throat radius distribution in tight sandstone reservoirs (Figure d–f). The pore-throat radius distribution curves for each sample exhibited single or double peaks, predominantly within the range 0.01–1.00 μm (Figure a–c), indicating a complex pore-throat structure within the reservoir. Figure d–f illustrates the comparison between the full-size pore-throat radius distributions converted from the NMR T 2 spectra and those obtained from LTNA and HPMI experiments. The peak ranges of the two curves show strong consistency, confirming that the pore-throat radius distributions derived from the NMR T 2 spectra effectively capture the full-size distribution of interconnected pore throats in tight sandstones.

Fractal Models and Multifractal Distributions

According to fractal theory, , pores exhibiting similar fractal characteristics should display a linear relationship on a double logarithmic plot of the cumulative pore volume fraction versus T 2. Taking samples Z5, Z10, and Z16 as examples, Figure shows that the fractal curve is not represented by a single straight line but can be divided into multiple segments at specific T 2 values. These segments are linearly fitted based on the distribution of data points, allowing for the calculation of fractal dimensions. The fractal dimensions corresponding to different pore sizes are derived from the slopes of these line segments, resulting in fractal parameters that characterize the pore structure. These parameters are categorized into D 1, D 2, and D 3, corresponding to shorter, medium, and longer T 2 domains, respectively (Figure and Table ). Here, we need to explain the relationship between the three-stage fractal dimension and the pore-throat radius. The three-stage or two-stage are according to the characteristics of the tight sandstone analysis curve, that is, D1, D2, and D3. Of course, there is a transition zone between each segment on the fractal curve, which is not a strict dividing line. However, its physical meaning is the fractal dimension of different levels of the pore throats. Here, D1 mainly corresponds to the further division of pore throats into small, medium, and large, three types of key pore throats according to the radius. D2 mainly corresponds to medium pore throats and D3 mainly corresponds to large pore throats. That is, D1 is used to indicate the fractal dimensions of interparticles dissolution pores and intercrystalline pores with a smaller pore-throat radius, and D2 and D3 are used to indicate the fractal dimensions of medium and larger pore throats, respectively. The results indicate that tight sandstones predominantly exhibit a triclinic fractal structure, followed by a bifractal structure, consistent with the multifractal behavior typical of low-permeability sandstones. D 1 values range from 0.34 to 0.94, with an average of 0.61, which is significantly less than 2. This suggests that interparticle dissolution pores and interparticle pores with smaller pore-throat radii do not conform to the fractal model described by eq . However, D 1 can still effectively represent the fractal nature and heterogeneity of these microscopic pores and throats[11]. In contrast, D 2 and D 3 are primarily distributed within the ranges 2.08–2.60 and 2.72–2.97, with average values of 2.36 and 2.88, respectively, indicating that medium and larger pore throats adhere to fractal principles. This suggests that self-similarity increases with larger pore-throat diameters.

Discussion

Causes of Multiple Fractals in the Pore Structure

Influence from Petrology

Previous studies have demonstrated that the particle size and arrangement of matrix particles in tight sandstones significantly influence the geometry of pore throats, , which, in turn, affect the multifractal distribution of pore-throat structures. Statistical analysis (Figure a,b) reveals that D 1 is negatively correlated with the median particle size and skewness, indicating that larger particle sizes contribute to greater homogeneity within micropores. Conversely, D 1 is positively correlated with the sorting coefficient (Figure c), suggesting that poorer sorting increases the fractal dimension of pore-throat radii in tight sandstones, thereby enhancing their heterogeneity. Interestingly, these correlations are reversed at the medium-to-large pore scale (Figure ). This observation suggests that at larger scales, increased particle size and improved sorting actually enhance the fractal dimension of medium-to-large pore throats, thereby increasing heterogeneity within this size range.

Influence of rock particle properties on multiple fractals of the pore-throat structure.

Influence from Mineralogy

The correlation between the fractal dimension and mineral composition, as depicted in Figure , underscores the critical role of mineralogical characteristics in controlling pore-throat architecture. A reduction in the rigid particle content (Figure a), accompanied by an increase in ductile components (Figure b) and carbonate mineral enrichment (Figure c), results in a diminished framework grain fraction and enhanced ductile deformation. These changes collectively lead to a more complex and irregular pore-throat structure (Figure a). Furthermore, carbonate mineral enrichment promotes the development of microfractures (Figure a), which contributes to a progressive increase in the fractal dimension of the pore network. The increasing trend of D1 reflects a marked enhancement of pore-throat heterogeneity. Conversely, an elevated clay mineral content leads to a notable decrease in D1, indicating a transition toward a more homogeneous pore-throat configuration (Figure d). This behavior is predominantly attributed to the relatively uniform morphology and orderly spatial arrangement of intercrystalline pores associated with kaolinite- and chlorite-rich assemblages within the tight sandstones of the study area (Figure b,f).

Influence of petrologic and mineralogic properties on multiple fractals of pore-throat structures. D 1, D 2, and D 3 are the fractal dimensions for small-sized pore, medium-sized pore, and large-sized pore, respectively. D avg is the average value of D 1, D 2, and D 3.

The correlation between the fractal dimension and mineral content is relatively weak in the medium-to-large pore-throat range. Although an increase in rigid grain content can slightly elevate the D2 and D3 values (Figure a)a trend primarily attributed to the development of microfractures (Figure a)this effect remains limited, as evidenced by the relatively small number of microfractures observed in samples with high rigid grain content (Figure a). However, further increases in ductile grains, carbonate minerals, and clay minerals introduce greater variability and complexity into the trends of D2 and D3. Detailed analysis reveals that elevated Illite content may moderately enhance the fractal dimensions of pore throats. Moreover, substantial carbonate cementation not only increases the geometric irregularity of pore throats but also significantly raises the surface roughness of interparticles pores, feldspar dissolution pores, and microfractures. These changes contribute to enhanced self-similarity within the pore system and reduce the homogeneity of the macropore-throat network.

In general, when the clay content is below 5%, its increase leads to greater surface roughness of the pore throats, more irregular pore geometries, and increased heterogeneity. However, the degree of self-similarity tends to improve with the enhanced extent of pore filling (Figure c,d). Notably, this positive effect is more pronounced when chlorite is the dominant clay mineral. Moderate amounts of chlorite contribute to the effective preservation of interparticle pores (Figure d). Conversely, a higher carbonate content adversely affects the homogeneity of larger pores (Figure e). These observations suggest that variations in mineral composition exert significant control over the multifractal characteristics of the pore-throat system with notable differences in how specific minerals affect pore-throat structures at different scales (Figure ). Overall, the average fractal dimension (D avg, representing the mean of D1, D2, and D3) exhibits a strong correlation with the fractal behavior of medium-to-large pore throats under varying mineral compositions. This finding further confirms that the fractal properties and heterogeneity of the pore-throat network in tight sandstones are primarily governed by the characteristics of larger pore throats.

Effect of Pore-Throat Properties on Fractal Dimension

The effect of the pore type was assessed by examining the relationship between the fractal dimension of different large and small pore throats and their content of major pore types. The negative correlation between the content of interparticles, pores, and D 3 suggests that interparticles and pores reduce the fractal dimension of large pore throats, which in turn allows for a decrease in inhomogeneity (Figure a). Feldspar solvation pores have the opposite effect, possibly because feldspar solvation pores complicate the shape and edges, thereby increasing the surface roughness (Figures b,e and b). The heterogeneity of medium-sized pores is negatively affected by the increased pore complexity caused by inter- and intragranular solvation (Figure c). However, the presence of microfractures reduces the fractal dimension to some extent, favoring the homogeneity of the pore-throat distribution (Figure d). The heterogeneity of small pores rises with the increase in the content of feldspar solution pores and intercrystalline pores (Figure e,f).The relationship between D avg and the content of pore types suggests that the fractal dimension of the system is more significantly affected by interparticles, feldspar solution pores, and intercrystalline pores and almost unaffected by intragranular solution pores, feldspar solution pores, and microfractures (Figure ).

Influence of rock pore-throat type on multiple fractals of pore-throat structure. D 1, D 2, and D 3 are the fractal dimensions for small-sized pore, medium-sized pore, and large-sized pore, respectively. D avg is the average value of D 1, D 2, and D 3.

Based on the pore-throat size classification criteria established by previous studies, ,, this study utilized HPMI and NMR experimental data to classify the pore throats in the study area into three categories: small micro-orifices (r < 0.1 μm), medium pore throats (0.1 μm < r < 1 μm), and large pore throats (r > 1 μm). The volume fractions, number frequencies, and average radii of each pore-throat type were quantified (Table ). Analysis of the relationship between the geometric characteristics of the pore throats and their fractal dimensions (D 1, D 2, and D 3) (Figure ) reveals that the fractal dimension of small pore throats is primarily influenced by the radius of the microporous throats (Figure b), followed by their volume fraction (Figure a), with minimal impact from their number (Figure c). In contrast, the fractal dimension of medium pore throats is mainly controlled by the number of intermediate-sized pores (Figure d–f) and is less affected by their volume fractions and radius. For large pore throats, heterogeneity is primarily determined by their number and shows a weak negative correlation with both the volume fraction and pore diameter (Figure g–i).

According to the characteristics of the tight sandstone analysis curve, three sections or two sections are divided, namely, D 1, D 2, and D 3. Of course, there is a transition zone between each section on the fractal curve, which is not a strict dividing line. However, its physical meaning is the fractal dimension of different levels of pore throats. Here, D 1 mainly corresponds to the further division of pore throats into small, medium, and large, three types of key pore throats according to the radius. D 2 mainly corresponds to medium pore throats, and D 3 mainly corresponds to large pore throats.

Notably, increases in the volume fraction, radius, and number of small micro- and mesopore throats lead to a rise in the D avg of the system. However, changes in these parameters for large pore throats exhibit the opposite trend (Figure ). This comparison suggests that the mechanisms by which large and small-to-medium pore throats influence reservoir heterogeneity differ significantly: large pore throats tend to reduce system complexity by forming dominant seepage channels, whereas small-to-medium pore throats enhance heterogeneity by increasing microstructural irregularities.

Effect of Multiple Fractals of the Pore Structure on Fluid Mobility

Figure illustrates the correlation between D and fluid mobility parameters across different pore-throat scales. The fractal dimension of microporous throats (D 1), which are predominantly associated with the intergranular pores of clay minerals, has a minimal impact on fluid mobility (Figure a). This is primarily due to the strong hydrophilic nature of clay minerals, which significantly restricts internal fluid movement, as these minerals serve as the primary host medium for microporous throats. In contrast, the fractal dimensions of medium (D 2) and large pore throats (D 3) exhibit a clear modulation effect on fluid mobility (Figure b,c). This is reflected in the monotonic decrease in fluid saturation and fluid porosity with increasing fractal dimensions, suggesting that greater structural complexity in pore throats significantly impedes fluid mobility. Correlation analysis reveals that the influence of pore-throat fractal dimension on fluid mobility intensifies with an increasing pore-throat scale (D 3 > D 2 > D 1). Among these, the geometric heterogeneity of large-scale pore throats (D 3) plays the most dominant role in controlling the overall fluid mobility of the system, followed by medium-scale pore throats (D 2), while the impact of microporous throats (D 1) is negligible. This scaling effect is further validated by the observed correlation between the mean fractal dimension (D avg) and the fluid mobility parameters (Figure ).

Influence of Other Factors on Fluid Mobility

Effects of Clay Minerals on Fluid Mobility

The filling effect of clay minerals significantly influences the pore structure characteristics of tight sandstone reservoirs, with substantial variations in their impact on movable fluid depending on mineral composition, storage state, and relative content. The results (Figure ) indicate that the relative content of Illite exhibits a weak negative correlation with MFS and MFP (Figure a). In contrast, the contents of the Illite-smectite (I/S) mixed layer and kaolinite show no significant correlation with fluid mobility (Figure b,d). Interestingly, the effect of chlorite on fluid mobility is nonlinear: moderate amounts of chlorite inhibit the growth of secondary minerals by coating particle surfaces, which is beneficial for pore preservation (Figure c). However, an excessive chlorite content reduces fluid mobility. Microstructural analysis reveals the underlying control mechanisms. As the dominant clay mineral in the study area, Illite predominantly accumulates in a filamentous and curly morphology on particle surfaces and at pore-throat junctions. This distribution not only segments primary pores but also significantly increases the specific surface area and roughness of pore-throat surfaces, thereby degrading pore-throat connectivity (Figure g). Overall, there is a clear negative correlation between the degree of clay mineral filling and reservoir quality: higher degrees of mineral filling lead to greater pore structure heterogeneity and poorer pore-throat connectivity. These findings provide an important mineralogical basis for understanding the variations in the fluid mobility within tight sandstone reservoirs.

Pore-Throat Structure Parameters

Among these, R 50 exhibits a wide distribution range, but its mean value is relatively low , (Table ). Notably, R 50 shows a significant positive correlation with parameters related to the movable fluid (Figure b). As R 50 and R a increase, both the saturation and porosities of the movable fluid also increase. However, this growth is not proportionately reflected in effective porosity. In contrast, the correlation between R max and movable fluid parameters is weak (Figure a–c), suggesting that the development of macroporosity contributes only marginally to the overall reservoir mobility. Nevertheless, the presence of macroporous throats remains important for enhancing permeability, as increases in R max generally improve the reservoir’s physical properties.

Influence of pore-throat structure parameters on the reservoir fluid mobility.

The strong negative correlation between the D parameter and movable fluid parameters indicates that as the D value increases, the complexity of the pore-throat structure also increases, resulting in reduced reservoir mobility. Conversely, as shown in Figure d,e, the movable fluid parameters are positively correlated with the pore-throat sorting coefficient (S p) and negatively correlated with the homogeneity coefficient (H c). This finding contradicts the traditional understanding that a smaller S p represents better pore-throat sorting and a more uniform size distribution in reservoirs. In the context of tight sandstone reservoirs, a low S p often implies that the pore space is dominated by small pore throats, leading to a reduced effective porosity for movable fluids.

To characterize the pore-throat connectivity, S max and W e were selected as key parameters in this study. The analysis results indicate that S max exhibits almost no significant correlation with movable fluid parameters (Figure f), suggesting that a larger reservoir capacity does not necessarily enhance fluid mobility. In contrast, W e shows a strong positive correlation with movable fluid parameters (Figure g), indicating that good pore-throat connectivity significantly improves the fluid seepage efficiency. This finding implies that fluid flow in tight sandstone reservoirs is primarily constrained by the connectivity of micropore throats. Even if the pore volume exceeds the critical pore diameter, MFS remains low if micropore-throat connectivity is inadequate.

Additionally, variations in S BET have a notable impact on fluid flow (Figure h). The analysis shows a clear negative correlation between movable fluid parameters and S BET, indicating that as S BET increases, both MFP and MFS decrease, resulting in poorer fluid seepage capacity. This effect is mainly attributed to the fact that higher S BET values increase the surface roughness of pore throats, which in turn enhances the saturation of bound fluid and reduces the effective permeability of the reservoir.

Conclusions

Through a comprehensive study involving CTS, SEM, XRD, HPMI, LTNA, and NMR analyses on 20 tight sandstone samples from the Chang 8 section in the Zhenjing area of the Ordos Basincombined with mineralogical and petrological investigationswe explored the causes and controlling factors of the multiple fractal characteristics of pore-throat structures in tight sandstone as well as their impact on fluid mobility. The main conclusions are as follows:(1)The pore-throat structure can be classified into three types. Type I: Characterized by a large number of large pore throats, high pore-throat connectivity, and weak nonhomogeneity. Type II: Features a bimodal pore size distribution, low fractionation, good pore-throat configuration, and high pore-throat connectivity. Type III: Dominated by intergranular pores, small pore-throat radii, poor pore-throat connectivity, and poor rock physical properties.(2)The pore throats of tight sandstone exhibit multiple fractal characteristics, with triple fractal as the dominant form and double fractal as a supplementary one. The fractal dimensions at different pore-throat scales are influenced by D 3, controlled by the number of large pore throats and pore morphology; D 2, affected by the number of medium pore throats and the development of microfissures and dissolution pores; and D 1, primarily governed by the variation in intergranular pores and micropores.(3)The mineral composition influences both pore distribution and pore-throat fractal characteristics: An increase in rigid particle content leads to a more homogeneous pore distribution. Carbonate minerals enhance the fractality of the pore-throat structure; an increase in clay content reduces the fractality of small pores but increases the fractality of medium pore throats; pore fillers have a greater impact on the fractality of large and medium pores, while matrix particles have a more significant influence on small pores.(4)The effect of the pore-throat fractal dimension on fluid mobility increases with the pore-throat scale.
