Letter Re: “Artificial intelligence-powered real-time multimodal model for predicting recurrence and survival in head and neck cancer: a multicenter, multinational study”
Authors: N Li, X Liu, S Zhang
Published: 2026

We read with interest the study by Jung et al.1 in ESMO Open on an artificial intelligence-powered, real-time multimodal model for predicting recurrence and survival in patients with head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) following curative-intent surgery. We commend the use of clinically accessible multinational data, which greatly enhances clinical feasibility, and the collection of longitudinal data, enabling real-time, dynamic updates of the RADAR score—a dynamic prediction of recurrence or death within 1-5 years after surgery. However, several aspects merit further discussion.

Firstly, although the study utilized multinational data, external validation was limited to only two medical centers. The authors used a random 80/20 split for the development and internal validation cohorts to assess the model’s performance; however, the absence of a fully independent external validation cohort undermines generalizability. Laboratory benchmarks and electronic medical record protocols can vary widely across institutions, and reliance solely on internal validation may mask overfitting to site-specific data distributions. Therefore, we recommend validating the model on a fully independent external cohort to establish the RADAR score as a robust global tool.

Secondly, the model’s clinical adaptability across clinical stages and tumor subsites could be improved. Although a total of 68 features were used for model training, clinical staging warrants further subgroup analysis, as factors are unevenly distributed in the datasets and significantly influence clinical outcomes. Notably, the study included HNSCC patients who underwent curative-intent surgery, yet the clinical staging also encompassed stage IVA and IVB patients, who are typically not considered candidates for curative surgery in routine clinical practice, raising questions about the inclusion criteria. The post-operative trajectory and recurrence risk for stage IV patients differ from those of early-stage I/II patients. In addition, subgroup analyses could extend beyond primary tumor sites to include other tumor subsites. Therefore conducting subgroup analyses by clinical stage and tumor subsite would better address clinical heterogeneity, particularly when integrating longitudinal molecular markers and radiomic features.2,3

Finally, although the Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) algorithm can handle missing values, in real-time applications the model will rely on its internal mechanisms to address missing variables at specific timepoints. Clinicians should be aware that the completeness of input data can still affect the reliability of individual predictions. Therefore it is crucial to implement logic that triggers alerts for substantial missing data in key variables during deployment. In addition, as shown in Supplementary Table S1, we note that 38 laboratory variables lack essential details regarding their collection. Providing clear information on data collection frequency and its distribution across timepoints is vital to ensure the model’s reproducibility and broader applicability in other clinical settings.

We highly appreciate this study for developing the dynamic RADAR score via integrating multimodal longitudinal clinical and laboratory data, providing a novel approach for real-time prognostic prediction in HNSCC. Future work is warranted to optimize the model through independent external validation, subgroup analysis for tumor subsites and clinical stage, and deployment-ready safety logic for missing data, which will be critical for translating the RADAR score into routine clinical practice.
