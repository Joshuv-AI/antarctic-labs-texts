The Impact of Denture Use on Cognitive Function in the Elderly: A Clinical Study
Authors: Anche Sampath, Naga Phanindra Madhavarapu, G Shyni Rani, Palakayala Balaswamy, Husnara Shaik, Y. Dhanush Mohan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjective:This study evaluates the impact of denture use on cognitive function in elderly individuals with significant tooth loss.Materials and Methods:A total of 130 participants (aged 60–70 years) were divided into two groups: the Denture group (n = 65) and the Control group (n = 65). The Denture group received complete or partial dentures, while the Control group had natural dentition. Cognitive function was assessed at baseline, one month, and three months using the Hopkins Verbal Learning Test (HVLT), Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE), and Verbal Fluency Test (VFT). Data analysis involved independent t-tests and repeated measures ANOVA.Results:At baseline, there were no significant differences between groups. However, at one and three months, the Denture group exhibited significant improvements in HVLT, MMSE, and VFT scores (P < 0.05) compared to the Control group. Intragroup analysis confirmed progressive cognitive enhancement in denture users.Conclusion:Denture use positively influences cognitive function, likely due to restored mastication, improved nutrition, and enhanced social interaction. These findings underscore the need for oral health interventions in elderly populations to mitigate cognitive decline.

INTRODUCTION

The global aging population is growing, with neurological disorders like dementia becoming increasingly prevalent.[1] Dementia, a progressive decline in cognitive function, impacts memory, language, and judgment, significantly reducing the quality of life for affected individuals and their caregivers.[2] Emerging research suggests a potential link between oral health and cognitive function, particularly the impact of tooth loss on neurological decline.[3] Tooth loss may impair masticatory function, reducing cerebral blood flow and neurotransmitter activity, leading to cognitive deterioration.[4] Dentures can restore chewing ability, improve nutrition, and enhance social interaction, potentially mitigating cognitive decline.[5] However, the precise mechanisms remain unclear. This study investigates whether denture use can positively influence cognitive function in elderly individuals with significant tooth loss. By comparing cognitive outcomes in denture users versus non-users, it aims to provide insights into the role of oral rehabilitation in preserving cognitive health among the aging population.

METHODOLOGY

This clinical study included 130 participants aged 60–70, divided into two groups: the Denture group (n = 65) and the Control group (n = 65). The Denture group received complete or partial dentures, while the Control group had natural dentition. Participants with neurological, psychiatric, or cardiovascular conditions were excluded.

Cognitive function was assessed at baseline, one month, and three months post-intervention using three validated tests: Hopkin’s Verbal Learning Test (HVLT), Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE), and Verbal Fluency Test (VFT). The Denture group received custom-fitted prostheses, while the Control group underwent routine dental care.

Statistical analysis was conducted using IBM SPSS, with independent t-tests comparing cognitive scores between groups at each time point. Repeated measures ANOVA was used to analyze cognitive changes within groups over time. Ethical approval was obtained, and informed consent was secured from all participants before the study commenced.

RESULTS

The study found a significant improvement in cognitive function among the Denture group compared to the Control group over three months. At baseline, no significant difference in HVLT, MMSE, or VFT scores was observed between groups. However, at one and three months post-intervention, the Denture group showed significantly higher scores (P < 0.05), indicating cognitive benefits from denture use. Intragroup analysis within the Denture group revealed a progressive increase in cognitive scores, confirming the positive effect of improved mastication on brain function [Tables 1 and 2].

DISCUSSION

Comparison with existing literature

The association between oral health and cognitive function has been widely studied, with evidence suggesting that tooth loss negatively impacts neurological processes. Reduced masticatory efficiency due to missing teeth has been correlated with lower hippocampal activity, a brain region crucial for memory and learning.[2] Studies have demonstrated that chewing enhances cerebral blood flow, stimulating neural activity and promoting synaptic plasticity.[3] Consequently, individuals with poor dentition are more prone to cognitive impairment.

Our findings support research indicating that denture use restores essential masticatory function, helping maintain cognitive abilities in aging populations. Previous studies have shown that edentulous patients who receive dentures exhibit significant cognitive improvements over time, similar to the trends observed in our study.[4] Furthermore, epidemiological studies suggest that tooth loss is an independent risk factor for dementia, emphasizing the need for early intervention through dental rehabilitation.[5]

Possible mechanisms underlying the observed effects

The mechanisms linking denture use to cognitive improvement are multifaceted, involving chewing-related neurological stimulation, enhanced nutrition, and social interaction.

Chewing and Brain FunctionMastication plays a crucial role in brain activity. The act of chewing activates the somatosensory and motor cortices, increasing neuronal activity and blood flow to the hippocampus.[6] This stimulation is particularly vital for elderly individuals, as diminished cerebral perfusion is a known risk factor for neurodegenerative diseases. By restoring chewing function, dentures may reduce cognitive decline by maintaining neuronal integrity and preventing hippocampal atrophy.[7]Nutritional BenefitsTooth loss often leads to dietary modifications, with individuals avoiding hard or fibrous foods essential for cognitive health. Nutrients like omega-3 fatty acids, antioxidants, and vitamins B6 and B12 are critical for brain function and neuroprotection.[8] Inadequate nutrition due to chewing difficulties has been associated with faster cognitive deterioration, emphasizing the importance of maintaining a diverse diet. Our study suggests that denture use restores masticatory efficiency, enabling better dietary intake, which may contribute to improved cognitive outcomes.Social and Psychological FactorsEdentulous individuals often experience self-consciousness, social withdrawal, and depression, all of which are linked to cognitive impairment. Social interaction plays a fundamental role in mental well-being, stimulating cognitive processes through engagement and communication.[9] The use of dentures improves facial aesthetics and speech, thereby encouraging social participation and reducing the risk of isolation-induced cognitive decline.

Clinical implications

These findings emphasize the importance of oral health interventions in geriatric care. Dental practitioners should recognize that restoring lost dentition is not only beneficial for oral function but also crucial for cognitive well-being. Routine cognitive screening for edentulous elderly patients may help identify those at risk of cognitive decline and prompt timely interventions, including prosthetic rehabilitation.[10]

Given the growing elderly population, integrating oral health with cognitive care could reduce the overall burden of dementia and neurodegenerative disorders. Policymakers and healthcare professionals should advocate for improved access to denture therapy, especially in aging societies where oral health is often overlooked.

Limitations and future directions

While this study provides compelling evidence supporting denture use for cognitive enhancement, certain limitations must be acknowledged. The follow-up period of three months, though sufficient to observe short-term cognitive improvements, does not clarify long-term effects. Future studies should incorporate longitudinal assessments to determine whether these cognitive benefits persist over years.

CONCLUSION

In conclusion, this study highlights a significant positive impact of denture use on cognitive function in elderly individuals with substantial tooth loss. The results reinforce the growing body of evidence that oral rehabilitation plays a crucial role in maintaining brain health, likely through mechanisms involving chewing stimulation, improved nutrition, and enhanced social engagement. As global populations continue to age, integrating dental prosthetics into cognitive health strategies may offer a simple yet effective intervention to reduce dementia risk and improve overall quality of life.
