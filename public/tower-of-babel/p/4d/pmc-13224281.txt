Evolving landscape of haemophilia care in India: Balancing innovation, access, and sustainability
Authors: Uday Yanamandra, Neeraj Sidharthan, Joseph M. John
Published: 2026

Haemophilia care in India is presently undergoing a profound transition. For decades, the country’s approach was defined by limited access, episodic treatment (on demand), and stark regional disparities with significant musculoskeletal complications.1 Today, the convergence of low-dose prophylaxis (LDP), non-factor therapies such as emicizumab, and early advances in gene therapy has created a moment of unprecedented possibility. Yet, India’s progress remains uneven, marked by pockets of excellence, persistent gaps, and the urgent need for system-level strengthening. This editorial synthesises these evolving trends, with particular emphasis on real-world insights from clinicians, State programmes, and patient organisations who are shaping the next chapter of haemophilia care in India.

Epidemiology and underdiagnosis

Although the estimated number of persons with haemophilia in India approaches ∼140,000, only about 26,352 patients are currently diagnosed and registered, according to the World Federation of Haemophilia Annual Global Survey (WFH-AGS 2024), highlighting a substantial diagnostic gap in the country.2

From episodic care to prophylaxis: a slow but steady shift

Historically, 96% of patients relied on episodic treatment therapy, resulting in high annual bleed rates, progressive arthropathy, and preventable disability. The introduction of low-dose prophylaxis, validated by Indian studies and later reinforced by the Jawaharlal Institute of Postgraduate Medical Education and Research (JIPMER) randomised trial, marked a turning point.3 Early Indian cohorts demonstrated dramatic reductions in annual bleed rates (from ∼37–40 to 1–4 per yr), improved school attendance, and better functional outcomes.3,4 However, adoption remains inconsistent. Of the many States implementing low-dose prophylaxis, a few such as Kerala, Karnataka, Tamil Nadu, and Maharashtra have adopted State-funded programmes, whereas many other States continue to rely predominantly on episodic care.5,6 However, translating these recommendations into practice will require sustained political commitment, reliable supply chains, and continued advocacy. While humanitarian aid programmes currently help bridge a portion of this gap, long-term access must ultimately be secured through strengthened government-supported programmes.

Therapeutic innovations: promise with practical caveats for India

Extended half-life (EHL) products and non-factor therapies are altering the treatment paradigm by reducing infusion frequency and potentially improving adherence, particularly given India’s access constraints and travel burdens. In India, real-world data from observational studies on glycopegylated EHL Factor VIII and Factor IX strategies suggest clinical benefits in reducing bleeding and improving joint status, with a note on gains in adherence and QoL, while also highlighting the need for individualised prophylaxis and laboratory testing (e.g., pharmacokinetic profiling).7 These findings support the potential value of EHL strategies in resource-limited settings, provided costs and monitoring are managed.7

Emicizumab: A catalyst for equity and innovation

So far, only a few developments have reshaped India’s haemophilia landscape as profoundly as emicizumab. Within the limitations of funding, it was initially prioritised for inhibitor-positive patients. However, its low-dose, low-frequency regimen has proven transformative, particularly in resource-constrained settings.8,9 Studies from King Edward Memorial (KEM) Hospital, JIPMER, and other centres have demonstrated its cost-effectiveness and real-world impact, prompting several States, most notably Kerala, to incorporate it into public programmes.10-12 Beyond clinical outcomes, emicizumab has helped address sociocultural barriers, such as enabling safer care for infants and young children, reducing caregiver burden, and improving adherence in families previously overwhelmed by frequent intravenous infusions.13,14

The role of patient organisations, especially the Hemophilia Federation of India (HFI), has been pivotal in advocating for equitable access and influencing State-level policy decisions.

Many States lack budgetary provisions and continue to face gaps in prophylaxis availability, inhibitor management, and access to bypassing agents. Addressing these disparities is essential to ensure that innovation does not widen inequity.

Decentralisation: A quiet revolution

One of the most encouraging shifts is the decentralisation of haemophilia care. Several States now provide factor concentrates at district and taluka hospitals, reducing travel burdens and improving timely access. Kerala’s model, where prophylaxis continues beyond the age of 18 for those already initiated, demonstrates how policy innovation can directly improve long-term outcomes.15 However, home therapy, a global standard of care, remains largely unavailable in India, with only a few anecdotal reports. Without home-based infusion capability, even patients on prophylaxis remain vulnerable to delays in bleed management, particularly in rural areas.15

Gene therapy: Promise tempered by reality

The landmark lentiviral autologous transplant conducted at Christian Medical College (CMC) Vellore represents a milestone, and ongoing work on non-viral vector approaches reflects the country’s growing gene therapy research capacity.16 Yet, the global landscape has become more cautious. The discontinuation of certain Adeno-associated virus (AAV)-based programmes due to declining factor levels underscores the uncertainties surrounding durability and long-term safety. India faces additional challenges, including a high prevalence of anti-AAV antibodies, limited laboratory infrastructure for long-term monitoring, and the absence of a regulatory framework for gene therapy follow up.17,18 Gene therapy remains aspirational but not out of reach. Moreover, emerging non-viral and CRISPR-based approaches (LNP-CRISPR) are being explored, though they remain at the preclinical/early clinical stages and require substantial validation and safety data before widespread adoption in India.19

Rebalancing therapies and the need for laboratory readiness

Rebalancing therapies are expected to enter the Indian market by 2026 and represent the next frontier. However, their safe deployment requires upgraded laboratory capabilities, including validated assays for novel agents, harmonised One-Stage Assay/Chromogenic Substrate Assay (OSA/CSA) protocols, and robust pharmacovigilance systems.20 Many Indian laboratories, especially in district hospitals, which already lack basic haemophilia identification and inhibitor testing, are not geared to monitor these therapies. Without investment in laboratory modernisation, the benefits of innovation will remain confined to a few tertiary centres.

The road ahead: A framework for equitable progress

To translate innovation into impact, India must pursue a coordinated, equity-focused strategy under the following steps:

Strengthen registries and real-world data systems

Accurate data is the foundation of policy. India’s registry coverage remains patchy, with <10 States maintaining functional registries. A coordinated national registry framework with standardised reporting across haemophilia treatment centres will enable the generation of real-world evidence, improve pharmacovigilance, and guide rational resource allocation.21,22

Expand and standardise HTCs

Multidisciplinary care, including physiotherapy, genetic counselling, and psychosocial support, must become the norm, not the exception.23

Scale prophylaxis through tiered access models

Efforts for universal coverage of on-demand therapy with factors and abolition of blood product infusion in cases of bleeds, followed by universal prophylaxis with low-dose prophylaxis, have a huge role to play. Extended half-life and low-dose emicizumab can be introduced based on clinical need and cost-effectiveness.13,21,22

Prepare for gene therapy and rebalancing therapies

This includes anti-AAV screening pipelines, regulatory frameworks, and laboratory upgrades.16

Improving the laboratory infrastructure

Implement harmonised testing protocols for FVIII/FIX activities, including OSA and CSA cross-validation, and ensure calibrator availability for products with assay-specific measurement issues, particularly for emerging therapies with unique pharmacodynamics.

Prioritise equity across States and systems

Address social determinants of health, including rural-urban gaps, travel burdens, and healthcare access, in planning prophylaxis programs, consistent with Indian data showing substantial travel distances for care and rural representation among patients.13 Integrate telemedicine, microclinics, and outreach programs to bring prophylaxis and education closer to patients. The contrast between Kerala and other States must be addressed through national policy alignment.13

Overall, India stands at a pivotal moment in haemophilia care. Science is advancing rapidly, and the country has demonstrated the capacity to innovate, adapt, and lead. But progress must be inclusive. The future of haemophilia care in India will depend not only on new therapies but on the systems, policies, and partnerships that ensure every patient, regardless of geography, socioeconomic status, or institutional affiliation, can benefit from them. A balanced, patient-centred, and equity-driven approach is essential to ensure that India’s evolving landscape becomes a story not just of innovation but of transformation.
