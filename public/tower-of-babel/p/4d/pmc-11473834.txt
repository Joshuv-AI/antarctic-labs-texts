The correlation between Life’s essential 8 and cardiovascular disease and mortality in individuals with nonalcoholic fatty liver disease: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Meng Sun, Yong Qiu, Lei Zhang, Guo Chen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
It is currently unclear whether there is a connection between Life’s Essential 8 (LE8) and cardiovascular disease (CVD), as well as mortality in people with nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD). Our goal was to explore these relationships by examining data collected in the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) from 2005 to 2018. We identified eligible participants with NAFLD based on NHANES 2005–2018 data. CVD status was acquired through self-reported information, and using the National Death Index, mortality data were prospectively matched. The diagnosis of NAFLD relied on noninvasive biomarkers. The research involved 9094 individuals who were identified as having NAFLD, with a mean age of 52.05 years. Each incremental LE8 score exhibited a significant association, leading to a 3%, 3%, 4%, 3%, 3%, 4%, and 4% reduction in the odds of experiencing CVD, ischemic heart disease, congestive heart failure, coronary heart disease, heart attack, angina, and stroke in individuals with NAFLD. A strong correlation was found between maintaining a superior level of Cardiovascular Health (CVH), as shown by a LE8 score ranging from 80 to 100, and a reduced occurrence of CVD and its various forms in NAFLD (all p for trend < 0.0001). Likewise, LE8 demonstrated protective benefits on mortality in NAFLD, showing that following a high CVH (in contrast to low CVH) was linked to reductions of 64%, 71%, and 74% in all-cause, CVD, and cancer mortality, respectively. Restricted cubic spline analyses suggested noteworthy dose-response relationships between LE8 and CVD, specifically its types in NAFLD, and a nonlinear correlation with CVD mortality. Interaction analyses highlighted age and race as significant effect modifiers. CVH, as evaluated by LE8, demonstrated an independent association with decreased odds of CVD and mortality risk in individuals with NAFLD. Our findings substantiate that adhering to LE8 may alleviate the excessive burden of CVD and mortality in the context of NAFLD.

Introduction

Nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) is identified by the buildup of fats in liver cells that is more than 5%, without the presence of other causes of long-term liver damage like heavy drinking1. It is becoming a primary reason for liver transplants, liver cancer, and deaths related to liver disease. Public health and healthcare systems around the world are struggling to cope with this burden.

NAFLD is commonly acknowledged as the liver-related aspect of metabolic syndrome, affecting various systems2, including the cardiovascular system3. CVD is one of the commonest extrahepatic complications in patients with NAFLD/NASH and a leading cause of death4,5. A growing body of evidence suggests that NAFLD significantly increases cardiovascular disease risk6. Moreover, individuals with NAFLD have a greater risk of all-cause mortality in comparison to those who do not have the condition7. Given the substantial burden of CVD-related complications and mortality in patients with NAFLD, identifying risk factors for CVD development, assessing CVD risk, and intervening from a multidisciplinary and integrated perspective are crucial for preventing CVD and reducing excess mortality8,9.

The American Heart Association (AHA) unveiled Life’s Essential 8 (LE8) in 2022 as a more precise method for assessing cardiovascular health (CVH) in the general population, surpassing the previous Life’s Simple 7 (LS7) measure10. Since its release, LE8 has been validated in several studies for its efficacy in predicting CVD prevalence11–15. Furthermore, LE8 scores and NAFLD have been found to have a significant negative correlation16–18.

However, LE8 scores have not yet been examined in relation to CVD and mortality risk, including cardiovascular mortality, in patients with NAFLD. Understanding these relationships could aid in reducing CVD rates and mortality by improving patient adherence to LE8 guidelines. This study aims to address these questions using the latest available data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES).

Methods

Study design and population selection

NHANES is an ongoing survey program that uses intricate probability sampling to offer a sample of the U.S. population that is representative on a national level. All surveys conducted by the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS) must receive authorization from the NCHS Ethics Committee, and participants must sign informed consent forms. The NHANES dataset can be easily obtained by the public, as participant information is collected and anonymized beforehand, eliminating the requirement for approval from local ethics committees19.

In this study, we included participants with a fatty liver index (FLI) ≥ 60 from seven consecutive cycles of NHANES (2005–2018). We excluded individuals younger than 20 years (n = 1110), pregnant women (n = 269), those with alcoholism excessive (n = 31111), and those with chronic viral hepatitis (n = 300). Further exclusions included 2641 participants with missing LE8 data, 9 with missing survival data, and 1 with missing CVD data. Additionally, 769 participants with missing other covariates were excluded. Final analysis was conducted on 9094 patients after screening (Fig. 1).

Definition of LE8 score

The LE8 rating includes lifestyle choices (eating habits, exercise, smoking, and sleep quality) and physical health indicators (BMI, cholesterol levels, blood sugar, and blood pressure), all assessed on a scale of 0-100, with an unweighted average calculated for the overall LE8 score10. LE8 scores classify CVH as low (0–49), moderate (50–79), or high (80–100)10. In short, the Healthy Eating Index-2015 (HEI-2015) was used to evaluate diet quality, with exercise intensity, nicotine exposure, and sleep health data obtained from surveys. Technicians at the Mobile Examination Center measured weight and height to calculate body mass index (BMI) by dividing weight in kilograms by height squared in meters. Non-HDL-C levels were used to evaluate blood lipids, while fasting glucose or glycated hemoglobin were used to measure blood glucose, and systolic/diastolic readings were taken by trained professionals to assess blood pressure. Detailed LE8 score assignment criteria are provided in Supplementary Table S1 and can be explored further in previous related studies10,14,20.

Diagnosis of NAFLD

The FLI was used to diagnose NAFLD, which originally proposed by Bedogni et al., is a non-invasive method for detecting NAFLD, based on common body measurements and serologic parameters, including BMI, waist circumference (WC), triglycerides (TG), and γ-glutamyltransferase (GGT)21. The FLI has demonstrated its accuracy and clinical significance in screening and diagnosing NAFLD in numerous epidemiologic studies22. The FLI is calculated using the formula: (e0.953*loge (TG) + 0.139*BMI + 0.718*loge (GGT) + 0.053*WC − 15.745) / (1 + e 0.953*loge (TG) + 0.139*BMI + 0.718*loge (GGT) + 0.053* WC − 15.745) * 100. Suspected NAFLD was diagnosed with an FLI of 60 or higher, after ruling out other potential causes of liver damage like alcoholism excessive (3 or more drinks per day for men, 2 or more drinks per day for women), and chronic hepatitis B or C virus infection.

Diagnosis of CVD

A standardized questionnaire was used in NHANES to diagnose CVD. Responding affirmatively to any of the listed conditions indicated self-reported CVD, including congestive heart failure (CHF), coronary heart disease (CHD), angina pectoris, stroke, or heart attack. Ischemic heart disease (IHD) was identified based on self-reported history of CHD, angina, or a heart attack23.

Mortality information

We monitored individuals who participated in NHANES from 2005 to 2018 and had NAFLD until the end of 2019, collecting mortality information by cross-referencing with the National Death Index database. The mortality results we examined encompassed deaths from any cause, CVD, and cancer. The ICD-10 was utilized to pinpoint particular factors contributing to mortality. CVD mortality encompassed fatalities associated with heart conditions (I00-I09, I11, I13, I20-I51) and strokes (I60-I69), whereas cancer mortality was determined by codes C00-C97.

Potential confounding covariates

We selected several key demographic variables as potential confounders, such as age, sex, ethnicity, level of education, income-to-poverty ratio (PIR), and marital status. These variables were self-reported by participants in their NHANES demographic profiles. Ethnicity was categorized based on NHANES criteria into Non-Hispanic White, Non-Hispanic Black, Mexican American, other Hispanic, or other races. Educational attainment was classified into three categories: below high school, high school diploma, and above high school. Marital status was dichotomized as single or non-single.

Statistical analysis

NHANES study designs are distinct and intricate, with analytic recommendations suggesting the use of weighted analyses in epidemiological studies to guarantee the sample’s representativeness of the entire population. We utilized suitable weighted statistical methods with the assistance of R version 4.2.3 and EmpowerStats. Statistics were defined as having a P-value less than 0.05 as statistically significant. Baseline analyses were performed using CVH subgroups (low, moderate, and high CVH) as determined by LE8. Weighted ANOVA was utilized for analyzing continuous variables presented as mean ± standard error, whereas weighted chi-square analysis was employed for categorical variables presented as number and percentage.

Multivariate adjusted logistic regression analyses were used to determine the separate impacts of LE8 on CVD and particular cardiovascular conditions in individuals with NAFLD. We created various logistic regression models, such as an unadjusted model, a model partially adjusted for age, gender, and race (Model 1), and a fully adjusted model accounting for age, gender, race, education level, PIR, and marital status.

Participants were followed until death or the end of 2019. Cox proportional hazards regression analyses were utilized to investigate the association between LE8 and mortality in NAFLD, creating crude, partially adjusted, and fully adjusted models. The partial adjustment model accounted for age, gender, and race, while the fully adjusted model included additional adjustments for education, PIR, marital status, and baseline CVD history. Survival probabilities were compared across various levels of CVH (low, moderate, and high CVH) using Kaplan-Meier (KM) survival analyses and log-rank tests.

In order to confirm the connection between LE8 and CVD and mortality in NAFLD, we utilized restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis for further validation of the dose-response relationship. Stratification and interaction analyses were performed to determine if the relationship between LE8 and outcomes remained the same in different subgroups and to discover any possible factors that could alter the effects. In order to validate the reliability of our results, we performed sensitivity analyses24.

During the sensitivity analysis, the United States Fatty Liver Index (USFLI) was utilized as a well-established noninvasive indicator to detect NAFLD, incorporating factors such as age, ethnicity, weight, glutamic aminotransferase, insulin levels, and blood glucose levels. If the USFLI score is equal to or greater than 30, and other chronic liver diseases are excluded, it indicates the presence of NAFLD.

Results

Baseline analysis based on CVH

Based on the LE8 score, we divided the initial NAFLD group into three categories: low CVH (0–49), moderate CVH (50–79), and high CVH (80–100), as illustrated in Table 1. The low, moderate, and high CVH groups consisted of 1994, 6677, and 423 participants, respectively. Individuals with superior CVH were typically younger, with a higher PIR, and had higher probabilities of being male, Mexican American descent, non-single, and have completed at least high school education (all p < 0.0001). Patients with NAFLD who exhibited high CVH levels experienced a notable decrease in the occurrence of CVD, IHD, CHF, CHD, heart attack, angina, and stroke (all with p-values less than 0.0001).

Association of LE8 with prevalence of CVD and specific types of CVD in NAFLD

The correlation between LE8 and the occurrence of CVD and its specific types in NAFLD was analyzed using logistic regression analysis with multivariate adjustment, as shown in Table 2. A strong inverse correlation was discovered between LE8 and CVD and all specific cardiovascular conditions in NAFLD. In Model 2, an increase in LE8 for each fraction was linked to a 3%, 3%, 4%, 3%, 3%, 4%, and 4% decrease in the occurrence of CVD, IHD, CHF, CHD, heart attack, angina, and stroke, respectively (odds ratios [ORs] and 95% confidence intervals [CIs] of 0.97 (0.96, 0.97), 0.97 (0.96, 0.98), 0.96 (0.95, 0.98), 0.97 (0.96, 0.98), 0.97 (0.96, 0.98), 0.96 (0.95, 0.97), and 0.96 (0.95, 0.97); all p < 0.0001). Higher CVH, as classified by LE8, were significantly linked to lower risks of CVD and its subtypes in individuals with NAFLD compared to those with low or moderate CVH (all p-values for trend < 0.0001). Specifically, in comparison to individuals with poor CVH, those with high CVH had significantly reduced risks of CVD, IHD, CHF, CHD, heart attack, angina, and stroke by 69%, 67%, 89%, 65%, 68%, 79%, and 62% respectively.

Association of LE8 with mortality in NAFLD

Based on a median follow-up period of 87 months (with an interquartile range of 48–130 months), 1053 individuals with NAFLD died (8.65%), including 352 (2.83%) from CVD and 257 (2.16%) from cancer (Table S2). We first examined the independent effect of LE8 on mortality in NAFLD using Cox proportional hazards regression analysis (Table 3). After accounting for factors like age, gender, ethnicity, education, income, marital status, and previous history of CVD, each point increase in LE8 was linked to a 3% decrease in the risk of death from all causes and CVD, as well as a 1% decrease in the risk of death from cancer (hazard ratios25 and 95% CIs: 0.97 [0.96–0.98] for all-cause, 0.97 [0.96–0.98] for CVD, and 0.99 [0.97-1.00] for cancer mortality; p < 0.0001, < 0.0001, and = 0.02, respectively). Higher CVH was related to a significant decrease in overall mortality and mortality related to specific causes in individuals with NAFLD (p < 0.0001 for overall and CVD mortality, and 0.04 for cancer mortality). When LE8 was categorized, high CVH was associated with 64%, 71%, and 74% reductions in all-cause, CVD, and cancer mortality, respectively, compared to the low-CVH group. Survival probabilities for all-cause, CVD, and cancer mortality were significantly associated with CVH levels based on KM survival analyses and log-rank tests (p < 0.0001 for all-cause and CVD, p = 0.002 for cancer mortality). Higher CVH was linked to improved survival probabilities (Fig. 2).

RCS analysis

RCS analysis revealed significant dose-response associations between LE8 and CVD prevalence and its specific types in NAFLD. LE8 exhibited significant linear correlations with the prevalence of CVD (p = 0.9582 for nonlinearity), IHD (p = 0.8575 for nonlinearity), CHF (p = 0.6293 for nonlinearity), CHD (p = 0.2138 for nonlinearity), heart attack (p = 0.3529 for nonlinearity), angina (p = 0.1464 for nonlinearity), and stroke (p = 0.6857 for nonlinearity) in NAFLD (Fig. 3A-G). We also found that LE8 showed a dose-response relationship with all-cause mortality (p = 0.0784 for nonlinearity) (Fig. 3H) and cancer mortality (p = 0.5111 for nonlinearity) (Fig. 3J) in NAFLD, while the relationship with CVD mortality was not linear (p = 0.0057 for nonlinearity) (Fig. 3I).

Stratified and interaction analysis

A stratified and interaction analysis of LE8 and CVD, as well as all-cause, CVD, and cancer mortality was conducted in NAFLD. The relationship between LE8 and CVD in NAFLD was consistent in the majority of subgroups, but an important effect modifier was age (p for interaction < 0.0001) (Fig. S1). Similarly, the association between LE8 and all-cause mortality in NAFLD was largely unaffected by covariates, except for race (p for interaction = 0.02) (Fig. S2). Lastly, the correlation of LE8 with CVD (Fig. S3) and cancer mortality (Fig. S4) in NAFLD was not influenced by any covariates, indicating the stability of the results.

Sensitivity analysis

USFLI served as a proxy diagnostic indicator for NAFLD in sensitivity assessments. The significant associations of LE8 with CVD prevalence (Table S3) and risk of mortality (Table S4) in the USFLI-NAFLD cohort remained largely consistent, demonstrating the reliability of our findings. In addition, the International Consensus Panel recently recommended renaming NAFLD as metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatopathy (MASLD) and redefining its diagnostic criteria. In this paper, additional analyses were performed in the MASLD population. The results showed that a significant negative association between LE8 and CVD prevalence (Table S5) and mortality risk (Table S6) remained in the MASLD population.

Discussion

The results of this large nationally representative population-based study suggest that CVH assessed by the AHA’s updated LE8 score is strongly linked to CVD and mortality in NAFLD. Our cross-sectional analysis revealed a remarkable inverse connection between LE8 and the odds of CVD, IHD, CHF, CHD, heart attack, angina, and stroke in NAFLD, demonstrating a clear dose-response relationships. In the longitudinal cohort analysis, we found that LE8 provides a notable safeguard against all-cause, CVD, and cancer mortality in NAFLD. All-cause mortality and cancer mortality showed a linear association with LE8, but CVD mortality was nonlinear. Age was identified as an effect modifier in the association of LE8 with CVD in NAFLD, while race markedly affects the LE8’s protective effect on all-cause mortality. Our study demonstrated that this novel approach to CVH assessment is robustly related to CVD and mortality in NAFLD, supporting the rationale for maintaining good CVH levels to alleviate the burden of CVD and mortality in NAFLD.

Recently, NAFLD was recommended by an international consensus panel to be renamed MASLD due to exclusive disease diagnostic criteria and stigma26,27. The exclusive diagnostic term NAFLD, which did not include etiology and pathogenesis, has been limited by the global epidemics of obesity and diabetes, as well as by the increasing alcohol abuse in this group. MASLD, known as Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatopathy, refers to the condition in the presence of coexisting heart metabolic risk factors and the occurrence of hepatic steatosis in the absence of a significant history of alcohol consumption27,28.

Emerging epidemiological evidence suggests that there is a high degree of concordance between the definitions of NAFLD and MASLD, meaning that 99% of patients with NAFLD meet the diagnostic criteria for MASLD26,29,30. Therefore, we believe that the original NAFLD population data can be used to approximately represent the MASLD population, greatly reducing the need for reanalysis after the implementation of the MASLD nomenclature. Of course, for the reliability of the conclusions of this trial, we performed additional analyses of the MASLD population, which showed that LE8 remained significantly negatively associated with CVD prevalence and mortality in this group.

The pathogenesis of NAFLD/MASLD is heterogeneous and not always associated with metabolic syndrome, insulin resistance and increased cardiovascular risk[31–33]. This adds to the challenge of predicting clinical outcomes in NAFLD/MASLD. Although many models have been developed to predict the risk of CVD morbidity and mortality in patients with NAFLD, their reliability remains to be confirmed. Therefore, there is a need to unearth reliable indicators that can predict the risk of mortality and CVD incidence in patients with NAFLD.

Previous prospective cohort studies have shown that CVH, as defined by LE8, is strongly associated with all-cause and CVD-specific mortality in US adults13. In addition, prior researches have investigated the association between LE8 scores and the development of NAFLD. Wang et al. found that CVH as assessed by the LE8 was associated with the incidence of NAFLD, and that good cardiovascular health reduced the incidence of NAFLD34. Huang et al.18 demonstrated in a Chinese cohort that a modified LE8 (excluding diet) was associated with incident NAFLD diagnosed via ultrasound, showing greater sensitivity compared to LS7. Another study based on the UK Biobank found an inverse association between LE8 and new-onset severe NAFLD, independent of genetic risk factors17. Aimuzi et al. also analysed the NAFLD population using the UK Biobank and found that healthier LE8 scores may help reduce the increased risk of suffering NAFLD from air pollution[35]. Additionally, a cross-sectional study using NHANES 2017–2018 data suggested that LE8 is nonlinearly related to NAFLD diagnosed by transient elastography, with demographic feature-specific effects16. This is not inexplicable. NAFLD and CVD share similar pathogenesis and risk factors. CVD is the leading cause of death in the NAFLD/MASLD population. The most recent definition of MASLD places greater emphasis on cardiometabolic risk factors. Cardiometabolic risk factors play a key role in the pathogenesis of CVD. As a result, people with NAFLD have a much higher burden of CVD and are at high risk for CVD morbidity and mortality compared to people without NAFLD/MASLD. This in turn calls for effective and implementable prevention strategies to reduce CVD risk and the associated mortality burden. Therefore, in this paper, we would like to explore the relationship between LE8 and mortality and CVD prevalence in patients with NAFLD, and whether LE8 can be a reliable predictor.

Most of the previous studies have been exploring the relationship between LE8 and the risk of mortality and CVD prevalence in the general population. Higher LE8 scores have been consistently linked to a reduced occurrence of CVD and its specific types in numerous high-quality cohort studies. Analysis of UK Biobank information by Li et al.12 demonstrated that high CVH evaluated by LE8 was inversely correlated with the incidence of CVD, CHD, and stroke (CVD: HR [95% CI] = 0.36 [0.33–0.40]; CHD: HR [95% CI] = 0.34 [0.30–0.38]; stroke: HR [95% CI] = 0.45 [0.37–0.54]). Furthermore, the connection between LE8 and CHD was influenced by genetic susceptibility to CHD. Zhang et al. suggested that the protective effect of LE8 was more pronounced in individuals with high polygenic risk scores for CVD36. Another analysis from the UK Biobank demonstrated the preventive effect of higher LE8 scores on major adverse cardiovascular events37. A prospective Chinese cohort study showed that both baseline CVH and long-term CVH during follow-up, as assessed by LE8, were related to the risks of CVD and all-cause mortality11. An analysis of the Framingham Heart Study also revealed a negative correlation between LE8 and incident CVD38. Not surprisingly, a cross-sectional analysis utilizing NHANES also consistently suggested a negative correlation between LE8 and the risk of CVD in the overall population20.

For analyses of mortality, it is well documented that higher rates of LE8 attainment are associated with lower rates of all-cause mortality and CVD mortality in the general population. Two cohort studies using NHANES showed that with higher LE8 scores, U.S. adults had lower all-cause mortality and CVD mortality13,14. A different study in Finnish men over time also found a significant connection between LE8 and lower all-cause and CVD mortality rates15. Therefore, it is conceivable that improved CVH evaluated by LE8 is linked to increased life expectancy in the general population39,40.

Recent studies have explored the relationship between LE8 and mortality in some specific populations. In specific populations, LE8 continues to show potent mortality-preventive effects. Higher LE8 adherence was independently related to lower mortality in populations with chronic kidney disease41, diabetes42,43, and CVD44.

Previous studies prompted our interest in the role of LE8 in predicting mortality and the prevalence of CVD in patients with NAFLD, and we conducted a correlation analysis. Our study is the first to show that LE8 is linked to a reduced likelihood of CVD and its major specific types in individuals with NAFLD, highlighting the public health value of CVH assessed by LE8 for CVD prevention in NAFLD. In addition, the robust protective effect of LE8 on mortality in NAFLD, particularly all-cause and CVD mortality, indicating that promoting CVH in individuals with NAFLD may have important public health implications for reducing premature mortality. LE8 may be useful for CVD and mortality risk assessment and stratification in NAFLD populations.

Interaction analyses revealed that age is an effect modifier of the relationship between LE8 and CVH in NAFLD, with the protective effect of LE8 being more pronounced in younger age groups. CVH evaluated by LE8 in NHANES showed significant age-specific and declined with age45. Previous studies exploring the relevance of LE8 to other conditions have also demonstrated a significant moderating effect of age16,41,43,46. Our findings emphasize that adherence to LE8 in younger and middle-aged populations (< 45 years of age) may yield more significant benefits in protecting against CVD among patients with NAFLD. Additionally, we discovered that race had a notable effect on the correlation between LE8 and all-cause mortality in patients with NAFLD. Racial inequalities in CVH status among U.S. adults have been documented45. A recent study utilizing NHANES data showed that the relationship between socioeconomic status and LE8 is also race/ethnicity specific47. These results suggest racial differences in the impact of LE8 on all-cause mortality in NAFLD and highlight the need to consider the heterogeneity of the protective effect of LE8 across different racial groups.

Strengths and limitations

Our research has several significant strengths. This is the first to demonstrate the role of the AHA’s updated LE8 in CVD and mortality prevention in the NAFLD population through a national population-based study, highlighting the public health significance of the new CVH assessment approach in reducing the excess CVD and mortality burden of NAFLD. Our analysis utilized the LE8 standard assessment method proposed by the AHA, consistent with previous relevant studies using NHANES. The generalizability of our findings is improved by the suitable weighting, extensive sample size, and diverse racial composition of NHANES.

Nevertheless, there are constraints in our research. The examination of the association between LE8 and CVD in NAFLD is inherently cross-sectional, which precludes drawing temporal inferences and may still be influenced by residual confounding factors. Future prospective cohort studies are needed for further confirmation. Additionally, the CVD diagnoses in our study were based on self-reports in the questionnaire, which may be subject to recall bias. Nonetheless, numerous epidemiologic studies using the NHANES questionnaire have demonstrated good agreement. Diagnosing NAFLD relied on noninvasive biomarkers that could be inaccurate. However, the results were largely validated in the USFLI-NAFLD cohort, suggesting the robustness of the conclusions. Future validation using more accurate NAFLD diagnostic modalities, such as imaging, is needed. Finally, we were unable to obtain the participants’ genetic susceptibility profiles for CVD due to a lack of relevant information. Individual differences in genetic variants could potentially influence the results.

Conclusion

In a nationwide population-based study, strong correlations were found between high LE8 scores and lower rates of CVD and mortality risk in individuals diagnosed with NAFLD. There were dose-response relationships between LE8 and the odds of CVD and its specific types, and a nonlinear correlation with CVD mortality in NAFLD. Age and race influenced the role of LE8 on CVD and all-cause mortality, respectively. These observations emphasize the underlying preventive value of updated CVH assessments for reducing the excess CVD and mortality burden in individuals with NAFLD. It will be necessary to conduct future high-quality cohort studies to confirm these findings.
