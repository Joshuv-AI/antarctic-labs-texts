Successful treatment of a fluconazole low-susceptibility Kodamaea ohmeri bloodstream infection with micafungin: A case report and literature review
Authors: Shinobu Hirayama, Masakazu Sasaki, Hinako Murakami, Fumiya Komatsu, Aiko Matsumoto, Yuki Sato, Mariko Otani, Maki Sakamoto, Kazuhiro Tateda, Takahiro Matsumoto, Takashi Sugita
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Kodamaea ohmeri (formerly Pichia ohmeri) is a rare yeast-like fungus usually isolated from environmental sources such as insects and fruits, and human infections are uncommon. Since the first report in 1998, however, cases of bloodstream infection have been increasingly described, mainly in Asia. We report a case of K. ohmeri fungaemia associated with a peripherally inserted central catheter (PICC). A man in his sixties with diabetes mellitus developed fever during hospitalisation while the PICC was in place. Yeast-like fungi were detected in blood cultures and identified as K. ohmeri by MALDI-TOF mass spectrometry. Antifungal susceptibility testing showed susceptibility to micafungin and other echinocandins, and reduced susceptibility to fluconazole. Removal of the PICC and administration of micafungin led to resolution of clinical symptoms and negative blood cultures. A review of 52 published cases revealed a mortality rate of 30.8%, with about half associated with catheter use. As K. ohmeri is capable of biofilm formation, removal of the infection source appears crucial for favourable outcomes. This case represents successful treatment of a fluconazole low-susceptibility K. ohmeri fungaemia with micafungin, suggesting that this drug may be an effective therapeutic option.

Introduction

Kodamaea ohmeri (formerly Pichia ohmeri) is a yeast-like fungus that is widely distributed in the environment and can be isolated from various sources such as insects and fruits [1], [2], [3]. Although human infections caused by this organism are relatively uncommon, sporadic cases have been reported, particularly in Asia, since the first description in 1998 [4]. Among reported cases, approximately 92.5% have been classified as invasive fungal infections, the majority being cases of fungaemia [5]. Several nosocomial outbreaks attributed to this organism have also been documented [6], [7], [8].

The mortality rate of K. ohmeri bloodstream infection has been reported to be approximately 34.9% [5]. In addition to early antifungal administration, removal of the infection source and assessment for complications are essential for improving clinical outcomes. Regarding antifungal susceptibility, isolates with reduced susceptibility to amphotericin B and fluconazole have been reported [9], [10], highlighting the need for careful selection of antifungal agents.

Here, we describe a case of K. ohmeri fungaemia associated with catheter-related infection that was successfully treated with micafungin. We also reviewed the clinical characteristics of previously reported cases of K. ohmeri fungaemia.

Case

A man in his sixties with a history of diabetes mellitus, weighing 54.2 kg, was brought to our hospital with sepsis due to a urinary tract infection, complicated by dehydration and uraemia. Blood cultures yielded Escherichia coli, and treatment with ceftriaxone was initiated. Although blood cultures subsequently became negative, inflammatory markers rose again on day 8 of hospitalisation. Imaging studies suggested portal vein thrombosis and a hepatic abscess, and Bacteroides thetaiotaomicron was detected in blood cultures. On ultrasonography, the liver abscess appeared as an irregularly shaped hypoechoic lesion measuring approximately 50 mm in maximum diameter in segment 7 of the liver, with no detectable internal blood flow signals. The lesion was diagnosed as secondary to superior mesenteric vein thrombosis; however, percutaneous drainage could not be performed because the abscess location made puncture technically difficult. Therefore, conservative management with antimicrobial therapy in combination with edoxaban tosilate hydrate was initiated. Antimicrobial therapy was switched from ceftriaxone to meropenem, followed by de-escalation to sulbactam/ampicillin or amoxicillin/clavulanic acid. Antimicrobial therapy was continued for 47 days until abscess resolution was radiologically confirmed.

On day 38, the patient developed cardiopulmonary arrest due to aspiration of food and required ventilatory support and central venous catheter (CVC) placement. On day 41, the CVC was replaced with a peripherally inserted central catheter (PICC). On day 74, the patient developed fever of 38.9 °C, with a white blood cell count of 13,100/μL and a C-reactive protein level of 3.4 mg/dL. Blood and sputum samples were collected for culture. Gram staining of the blood culture revealed yeast-like fungi, prompting removal of the PICC and initiation of micafungin at 100 mg/day for presumed Candida infection. Pseudomonas aeruginosa was isolated from sputum, and piperacillin/tazobactam was co-administered(Fig. 1). After fungemia onset, no endophthalmitis was detected, and echocardiography revealed no abnormalities such as vegetations. Antifungal susceptibility testing, performed according to the CLSI method [11], showed the following minimum inhibitory concentrations (μg/mL): amphotericin B 0.06, miconazole 0.5, itraconazole 0.03, voriconazole 0.06, flucytosine ≤ 0.12, fluconazole 4, micafungin 0.06, and caspofungin 0.25. The isolate was susceptible to micafungin and other echinocandins but showed reduced susceptibility to fluconazole.

By day 80, the patient’s temperature had decreased to 36.5 °C, and the β-D-glucan level was 68.5 pg/mL. No evidence of thrombosis or abscess formation was observed, and the clinical condition gradually improved. Blood cultures obtained on day 79 were negative. Micafungin therapy was continued for 19 days after confirmation of culture negativity and was then discontinued (Fig. 1). No recurrence of inflammatory response was observed during treatment.

Discussion

A PubMed search using the keywords “Kodamaea ohmeri” and “Pichia ohmeri” up to February 2025 identified 161 publications. Of these, 42 reports describing cases of K. ohmeri bloodstream infection were analysed (Supplementary). Reports not available in full text or published in languages other than English were excluded. Outbreak reports were also included to evaluate treatment approaches and clinical outcomes.

In total, 52 cases of fungaemia were identified, with 16 deaths (30.8%) (Table 1). The reported mortality rate of invasive candidiasis caused by Candida albicans ranges from 20% to 50% [12], suggesting that K. ohmeri fungaemia carries a similarly high risk of mortality. Underlying conditions included haematological malignancy (n = 9), gastrointestinal disorders such as necrotising enterocolitis, short bowel syndrome, suppurative peritonitis and pancreatitis (n = 9), rheumatic disease (n = 3), burns (n = 2), organ transplantation (n = 1), and HIV infection (n = 1). The present patient had a prolonged hospital stay with limited mobility and was bedridden. Possible routes of K. ohmeri entry included colonisation or contact transmission via healthcare workers, and the infection was considered primarily catheter-related through the peripherally inserted central catheter (PICC). However, no other K. ohmeri isolates were detected in the same ward during the same period, suggesting that this was not a nosocomial outbreak.

Use of a central or peripheral venous catheter was documented in 27 cases (51.7%). Some cases without such documentation likely involved catheter use, such as those in premature infants or patients receiving chemotherapy. Other cases involved the use of medical devices such as dialysis catheters or mechanical ventilators. K. ohmeri is known to possess biofilm-forming ability [13], [14], and removal of the infected device is considered crucial for treatment success. In the present case, prompt catheter removal likely contributed to the favourable outcome.

Amphotericin B was the most frequently selected antifungal agent. In contrast, fluconazole was used in seven fatal cases. Although no established CLSI breakpoints exist, many isolates have shown reduced susceptibility to fluconazole (Table 2), which may contribute to treatment failure. Susceptibility to amphotericin B is generally retained, and many successful outcomes have been reported; however, attention must be paid to potential nephrotoxicity [15]. With the recent global spread of Candida auris, echinocandins have increasingly been selected as empirical therapy for yeast infections [16]. In the present case, the K. ohmeri isolate was highly susceptible to micafungin, and monotherapy with this agent achieved successful clinical resolution. Although reports of treatment with micafungin remain scarce, this agent may represent a promising therapeutic option owing to its favourable profile in both susceptibility and safety.

In this case, fluconazole–low-susceptibility K. ohmeri fungemia was successfully treated through early catheter removal, prompt micafungin initiation, and an appropriately determined treatment duration after confirming the absence of complications. However, reports on micafungin use for treating K. ohmeri infections remain limited. Further studies involving a larger number of clinical isolates are needed to clarify the efficacy of micafungin against this pathogen.
