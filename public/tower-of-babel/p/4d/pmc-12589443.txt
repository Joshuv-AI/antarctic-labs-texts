Geometry optimization of broadband, planar, spiral inductors via continuous trace width modulation
Authors: J Cañada, J M López-Villegas, N Vidal, S F Nagle, L F Velásquez-García
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractVariable-width planar inductors—commonly referred to as tapered inductors—have been shown to outperform their constant-width counterparts at specific target frequencies. This study extends that concept to the design of broadband inductors operating from DC to the low RF band (up to several hundred MHz), leveraging continuous trace width modulation to enhance performance. Circular spiral inductors with both constant and continuously varying trace widths were designed to have identical nominal inductance, and were subsequently simulated, fabricated, and experimentally characterized. DC performance was evaluated by measuring the magnetic flux density at the inductor center as a function of applied voltage and current, while RF performance was assessed through reflection coefficient measurements, from which frequency-dependent inductance and quality factor were extracted. Across the entire frequency range investigated, variable-width inductors consistently outperformed constant-width designs: they generated stronger magnetic fields at DC under equivalent bias conditions, and exhibited higher quality factors in the low RF range. These results demonstrate that continuous trace width modulation enables the design of high-performance inductors, optimized not only at specific frequencies, but also across broad spectral ranges.Subject terms: Engineering, Materials science, Physics

Introduction

The availability of high-performance, passive components strongly influences the design of electronic circuits. Reactive components such as capacitors, inductors, and transformers are widely used for implementing passive circuits, or as part of active circuits including impedance matching networks, resonators, and amplifiers. The operating frequency of these circuits ranges from quasi-static (i.e., near DC) to the highest radio frequencies (RF), such as the microwave range from 300 MHz to 300 GHz. Unfortunately, due to parasitic effects, there is no one component that can be used in the entire frequency band. In the case of reactive components, parasitic effects limit the bandwidth by fixing a maximum operating frequency (namely self-resonant frequency), and reduce the quality factor Q, defined as the ratio of stored to lost energy per excitation cycle.

Inductors and transformers are typically implemented using metal spirals in various shapes. Toroids with/without magnetic cores are commonly used for low-frequency applications, while planar spirals are the preferred implementation for RF applications. In both cases, unless a superconductor material is used, the finite electrical conductivity of the conductive path leads to losses in the spiral that can be modeled with a series resistance. Moreover, the turn-to-turn interaction inside the spiral provides an alternative path for the signal that reduces the inductor bandwidth; this effect can be modeled with a parallel capacitance. According to this model, the associated series resistance Rs and/or parallel capacitance Cp of an inductor must be reduced to optimize its performance.

This study focuses on planar spiral inductors due to their ease of fabrication and integration using well-stablished planar technologies on a variety of substrates such as the most common printed circuit board (PCB) substrate: FR41, low-temperature cofired ceramics (LTCC)2, semiconducting substrates3–5, and in multi-chip module (MCM) technologies6. Additionally, planar inductors fabricated via additive manufacturing have been demonstrated7 and used to implement three-dimensional solenoids8, further broadening their applicability. Planar inductors are typically implemented in square and octagonal shapes due to their design and layout simplicity. However, circular spirals, although more complex to design, are more suitable for high-performance applications due to their improved symmetry and continuity in the current path, without corners and steps in width. Consequently, circular spirals were utilized in this study.

When optimizing the layout of a planar spiral inductor, it may seem logical to implement the spiral with a wide trace to reduce its parasitic resistance. However, wide-trace spirals occupy large areas, which could be a concern when implementing inductors on the latest integration technologies (i.e., a single inductor could come at the cost of millions of nano-scale transistors). Additionally, focusing on the quasi-static band of the spectrum or on magnetic applications, wide-trace spirals generate weaker magnetic fields at their center compared to narrow-trace spirals. Furthermore, in the RF range, the time-varying magnetic field across the inductor generates eddy currents in the conductive traces. The magnitude of these currents is proportional to the magnetic flux intersecting the trace, which increases with trace width, thereby elevating the series resistance. Consequently, the increase in losses is proportional to the square of the trace width. Hence, a trade-off is required between the reduction of ohmic losses and the increase of magnetically induced losses. The parallel capacitance in the planar spiral inductor model quantifies the electrical coupling between turns, which affects bandwidth. This coupling is influenced by factors such as turn width, turn spacing, and the overall turn-to-turn interaction perimeter, i.e., the length of adjacent conductor edges between neighboring turns. For a given turn spacing, wider traces increase the interaction perimeter, leading to a higher capacitance. Consequently, for a specific inductance and turn spacing, spirals with wide traces will exhibit higher parasitic capacitances and reduced bandwidths, while compact spirals with narrower traces generally exhibit higher self-resonant frequencies. However, this often comes at the expense of increased resistive losses, highlighting a design trade-off between minimizing losses and maximizing the self-resonant frequency.

This work explores the extent to which a planar spiral inductor geometry can be optimized to maximize frequency coverage, from low frequency to hundreds of MHz, using a common strategy that maximizes inductor performance in the entire frequency band rather than for a specific frequency. For a given inductance value, the main objective at the lower end of the frequency band is to obtain strong magnetic fields and flux. Conversely, at the higher end of the frequency band, the intention is to increase the quality factor and the self-resonant frequency of the inductors as much as possible. To achieve these goals, this study posits that an accurate design of variable-width spiral inductors can effectively fulfill most performance requirements across the entire frequency band of interest from near DC to hundreds of MHz. It is expected that, by modulating the trace width of the inductors, it is possible to reach a balance between device resistance and compactness that yields performance enhancements across a broad frequency band.

Trace width modulation was introduced in the early 2000s as an effective strategy to minimize inductor losses at target RF frequencies9. Since then, several studies have built on the technique to optimize square10–14 and octagonal15–19 planar inductors. Hsu and colleagues reported that, for a given device footprint, tapered inductors can yield higher quality factors than their constant-width counterparts11. Also, Liu et al. and Pei and colleagues reported on the beneficial effect of modulating both the width of the conductive trace and the spacing between turns to compensate for the variation in magnetic flux density across the inductor12,13. Furthermore, Manikandan et al. demonstrated three-dimensionally stacked, tapered inductors and corroborated the smaller footprint of variable-width inductors16. Additionally, Vanukuru and colleagues investigated the effect of reverse excitation on the performance of variable-width inductors17, as well as their implementation using thick metal traces18. These works demonstrate the benefits of trace width modulation when designing high-performance planar inductors; however, in these studies, the device optimization is conducted around a target frequency, and they only contemplate square and octagonal inductors with a discrete implementation of width modulation, i.e., each loop or segment has a constant width. This work investigates the use of trace width modulation for the implementation of versatile, broadband inductors, studying their performance from DC up to several hundred MHz. The objective is not only to reduce ohmic losses, but to minimize the overall losses comprising both ohmic and eddy-current contributions across the broadest possible frequency band. A comprehensive analysis of ohmic and magnetically induced losses, both theoretical and experimental, is presented in9. In that study, the loss distribution in a square inductor geometry is examined, and a systematic method is proposed to minimize losses under narrow-band operation. Its conclusions can be extrapolated to other inductor geometries and frequency bands.

This study utilizes circular spiral inductors, which can be defined and tuned using parametrized equations, and which enable the implementation of continuous width modulation. While square and octagonal inductors are typically preferred for the implementation of integrated circuits (ICs), circular inductors can yield higher performance, and they are compatible with many other manufacturing techniques, such as PCB processes and additive manufacturing. Furthermore, circular spiral inductors can be discretized into octagonal and square inductors if the technology requires doing so, making them a more versatile design.

The rest of this report is organized as follows: Sect. “Design of continuously-varying-width, circular planar spiral inductors” presents a detailed description of the design procedure for variable-width spiral inductors with a circular shape, including theoretical considerations and electromagnetic (EM) simulation results, to evaluate and optimize inductor performance; Sect. “Device fabrication” outlines the fabrication process of the inductor prototypes on FR4; in Sect. “Characterization”, the experimental characterization of the fabricated prototypes is presented; Sect. “Discussion” discusses the results obtained and their potential impact, to then introduce tentative future research directions; finally, Sect. “Conclusion” summarizes the work.

Design of continuously-varying-width, circular planar spiral inductors

Constant-width spirals

Archimedean spirals, i.e., curves in which the radius increases linearly with the polar angle θ, are the simplest type of spiral and commonly found in nature. Archimedean spirals have been used to implement engineering devices such as broad-band antennas20, mechanical springs21, and microfluidic mixers22. Furthermore, variations of the Archimedean spiral geometry, such as introducing a third dimension, have also been used to design broadband conical inductors23. The geometry of a constant-width, circular planar spiral inductor can be defined as the region between two Archimedean spirals of distinct initial radii. Design of inductor prototypes on FR4 substrates

In this study, two sets of inductors with constant and variable width were explored. The inductors were designed using CST Studio Suite (Dassault Systèmes, Vélizy-Villacoublay, France). Each set comprised three inductors with the same nominal inductance: one inductor featured a variable-width spiral design, while the other two devices implemented constant-width spiral designs, differing in trace width and number of turns. The design methodology for all devices is characterized by the following constraints: (i) turn-to-turn spacing and inner spiral radius were fixed at = 0.4 mm and rin = 2 mm, respectively; (ii) the number of turns was restricted to discrete integer values to ensure the consistent connection of the inductor spirals to SMA connectors; (iii) turn width and growth factor were limited to 0.7 < < 3.5 and 0 < < 1, respectively, to avoid extreme designs with excessively narrow or wide strips, or a combination of both; and (iv) the equivalent inductance was fixed for each set of devices (269 nH for one set and 412 nH for the other) to enable a fair performance comparison within each set. As a result, a variable-width inductor with geometrical parameters between those of the constant-width inductors was obtained for each set, resulting in self-consistent sets of samples. The inclusion of two constant-width inductors (one narrow, one wide) per set makes it possible to verify the trade-offs of using different constant widths, as introduced in Table 1, while the addition of a variable-width inductor enables the study of width modulation as an alternative optimization strategy, all while designing for the same inductance.

A model created in CST Studio Suite was used to fine-tune the geometrical parameters that make each inductor design match its target inductance. For each inductor geometry, finite element method (FEM) simulations were conducted to determine scattering parameters as a function of frequency. From the raw simulation data, the equivalent inductances of the structures were extracted. The geometries were then fine-tuned, and the process was repeated until the target inductances were achieved. The model (Fig. 3) comprises a conductive spiral on an FR4 substrate and an SMA edge connector, emulating the later-fabricated devices. Use of an SMA edge connector results in minimal insertion loss at frequencies well above the 200 MHz reached in this work. The outer end of the spiral is connected to the pin of the SMA connector via a metal trace, while its inner end is connected to a metal trace on the back plane of the substrate through a via drilled at the center of the board. The other end of the back trace is connected to the body of the SMA connector, and thus to the shielding of the SMA cable. Alternatively, the inner end of the spiral could be connected to the body of the SMA connector using a wire bridge. However, such implementation would increase the nominal inductance of the devices—affected by the bridge area—and could compromise their mechanical stability. The increase in quality factor is attributed to the balance between ohmic losses and magnetically induced losses introduced by the width profiling. Figure 6 shows the current density distribution across the spirals of the three inductors belonging to the 269 nH set. Notably, the current density distribution is not uniform. Instead, a redistribution of current density is observed, attributed to the magnetic field distribution throughout the structures. This is reflected by regions of high current density in red and areas of very low current density in blue. Since losses depend on the square of the current density, any nonuniform redistribution of current density inevitably leads to increased losses. Therefore, the devices exhibiting the highest current density or the most pronounced nonuniformities also present the greatest losses. As can be appreciated in Fig. 6, the constant-width inductors exhibit a high degree of nonuniform current density redistribution, while the variable-width inductor shows the most uniform distribution, indicating reduced losses—consistently with its higher quality factor.

Device fabrication

Figure 7 shows the fabricated devices. The inductors were made on double-sided FR4 substrates with 18 µm copper foil using an LPKF Protolaser U4 (LPKF, Garbsen, Germany). The LPKF Protolaser U4 features a 355 nm nanosecond pulsed laser, galvanometer-scanner-guided across a 50 mm by 50 mm field of view (FOV), and extended to a 230 mm by 300 mm scanning area using software that stitches together multiple FOVs. With 18 µm copper on FR4, the tool achieves a 15 µm spot diameter that is capable of repeatably resolving 50 µm traces with 20 µm spacing. A copper rivet was placed in the via hole at the inner end of the spirals to ensure electrical contact between both sides of the PCB.

Characterization

The fabricated inductors were characterized at both DC and RF to examine the effect of trace width variation on performance across the full frequency range.

Characterization in RF

The RF characterization of the inductor prototypes was performed by measuring scattering parameters. Raw reflection coefficient data were measured for all inductors from 1 to 500 MHz using an E5071C vector network analyzer equipped with an N4433A electronic calibration unit (Keysight Technologies, Inc., Santa Rosa, CA, USA). The electronic calibration unit was used to establish the SMA connector as the reference plane for the S-parameter measurements. A model derived from open, short, and load-terminated SMA connectors could be used to de-embed the connector. However, such a model would also include an average series resistance due to soldering. While this resistance is typically negligible, de-embedding it can result in an unrealistic, non-passive behavior in high-Q devices, such as the prototypes fabricated in this study. To avoid this, each device under test was defined as the combination of the spiral and the SMA connector. This choice introduces bias—mainly a small series resistance (~ 0.1 Ω), series inductance (~ 5 nH), and parallel capacitance (~ 1 pF)—,but the bias is consistent across all devices, ensuring valid and meaningful performance comparisons.

Characterization in DC

The experimental setup used to characterize the devices in DC comprised a N6700B Low-Profile Modular Power System Mainframe (Keysight Technologies, Santa Rosa, CA USA), a GM08 Gaussmeter (HIRST Magnetic Instruments Ltd., Falmouth, United Kingdom) equipped with a commercial, 5 mm-diameter, axial probe (0.2 mm by 0.2 mm active area), and a linear positioning stage with a resolution of 10 µm. The Gaussmeter has a calibrated resolution better than 1% for DC measurements and a repeatability better than 0.5%. The DC characterization of the devices entailed measuring the magnetic flux density generated by the inductors along their axis while controlling the bias voltage (Fig. 12) and while controlling the DC current (Fig. 13). The I-V characteristics of the inductors were also recorded (Fig. 14).

The data show that, under the same bias voltage, the variable-width inductors generate stronger magnetic fields compared to the constant-width designs (Fig. 12). The superiority of the variable-width inductors is attributed to the drop in device resistance compared to that of the constant-narrow inductors, and the increase in compactness compared to the constant-wide inductors. The differences in device resistance can be appreciated in the I-V characteristics of the devices (Fig. 14). As expected, the magnetic flux measurements performed while exciting the inductors by directly controlling the current (rather than voltage) show that the more compact the inductors are, the higher magnetic fields they generate (Fig. 13). Consequently, the constant-narrow inductors exhibit the highest magnetic fields for the same injected current. However, the magnetic fields generated by the variable-width inductors are only slightly less intense (< 13%) than those generated by the most compact inductors.

Discussion

The results show that the variable-width inductors outperform their constant-width counterparts throughout most of their bandwidth. As shown in Fig. 8 and Fig. 9, the quality factor of the inductors of variable width is as-good or better than those of the associated constant-width designs from 1 MHz until past their maxima, indicating similar or fewer losses. Only in the vicinity of the self-resonant frequency, the quality factor of the most compact, constant-width inductor becomes higher than that of the variable-width design. Moreover, the variable-width inductors exhibit maximum quality factors approximately 10% higher than those exhibited by the constant-width inductors. Also, for both sets of samples, the self-resonant frequency of the variable-width inductor is approximately 90% of the maximum value, which corresponds to the narrow, constant-width inductor, indicating a good broadband operation. These features support the choice of designing enhanced broadband filters and impedance matching networks using variable-width inductors.

The DC characterization of the devices demonstrates that variable-width spirals also have the potential to perform better than constant-width spirals for near-DC electromagnetic applications. The magnetic field that a solenoid can generate for a given electric current grows as the inductor becomes more compact (i.e., it packs more turns in the same area). Therefore, narrower conductive traces are generally preferred. However, when power is supplied via a fixed voltage (rather than current), the resistance of the inductors comes into play, limiting the current that can flow through an inductor for a certain bias voltage, hence limiting the magnetic field that can be generated. The effect of resistance is particularly critical in planar inductors due to their geometry: as the spiral grows outwards, its loops become longer and less compact, contributing more to the resistance of the device and less to the generated magnetic field. Moreover, narrower conductive traces also result in higher heat dissipation and operating temperature, introducing an additional resistance-governed factor. The variable-width design approach described in this work leverages these trade-offs: by implementing progressive trace widening, the resistance of the outermost loops is reduced without compromising the compactness of the innermost loops, effectively integrating the advantages of both narrow and wide traces unto a single design. Magnetic flux density measurements demonstrate that, when excited via a voltage source, variable-width spirals generate higher magnetic fields than their constant-width counterparts (Fig. 12). This result, together with the superior performance of variable-width inductors in the RF spectrum, demonstrates that trace width modulation is an effective, universal strategy for inductor enhancement in the DC to RF range.

The reported performance improvements have been achieved by implementing continuous, linear trace width growth, as described in Sect. “Variable-width spirals”. However, it should be noted that other width modulation strategies are possible and might yield superior performance. Additionally, varying the spacing between loops can also introduce additional enhancements, as reported in the literature12,13, with the proposed circular spiral design enabling the study of continuous spacing variations, rather than discrete. Furthermore, substrate materials such as Rogers RO3003 and Rogers RT/duroid 5880 could increase both the SRF and in ways that favor one design strategy over another, for example due to differing electric field distributions. These considerations outline potential future directions for this work.

Conclusion

This study reports the comparative exploration of the electromagnetic behavior of variable-width and constant-width planar inductors. Variable-width inductors were implemented as circular spirals featuring constant turn spacing and a trace width that grows linearly with the polar angle. Unlike square and octagonal inductors, circular inductors enable continuous width modulation, and their geometry can be defined and tuned using parametrized equations. The results of this study demonstrate that variable-width inductors outperform their constant-width counterparts across the entire frequency band from DC to a few hundred MHz. In the RF frequency band, variable-width inductors exhibit quality factors that are as good or better than those of constant-width inductors, and they reach maximum quality factors approximately 10% higher than those of constant-width inductors. Notably, this performance improvement was achieved without optimization for a specific operating frequency. In DC operation, the variable-width inductors generate the strongest magnetic fields at their center under constant voltage excitation. Variable-width inductors benefit from reduced ohmic losses without compromising compactness; at the same time, width profiling mitigates magnetically induced losses, enhancing RF performance—particularly the maximum quality factor—without significantly lowering the self-resonant frequency. These findings demonstrate that spiral width profiling enables the broadband optimization of planar inductors.
