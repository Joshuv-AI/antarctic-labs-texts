A novel approach for enhancing the potency of kinase inhibitors using topological water networks
Authors: Re Gin Jeoung, Anand Balupuri, Nayoung Lim, Sungwook Choi, Nam Sook Kang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOptimizing the potency of kinase inhibitors remains a major challenge due to the structural conservation of kinase binding pockets. While several computational methods have been developed to address this issue, most overlook the crucial role of water molecules within the binding site. Our research aims to address this gap by examining topological water networks (TWNs) within the target protein. We identified specific TWN-derived patterns in kinase binding sites which align with known crystallographic kinase fragments. Our findings reveal the potential of TWNs to significantly improve the identification and optimal placement of promising fragments within protein binding sites. Here, we propose TWN-based fragment growing (TWN-FG) method that enhances kinase inhibitor potency by leveraging the topological characteristics of hydration networks. TWN-FG successfully explains structure–activity relationship (SAR) trends of known kinase inhibitors and has been applied to design and synthesize a potent mixed lineage kinase 1 (MLK1) inhibitor. Introduction

Protein kinases play a critical role in regulating intracellular signal transduction pathways and have long been key therapeutic targets for various diseases, including cancer, inflammatory disorders, and neurodegenerative conditions [1]. Consequently, extensive efforts have been made to optimize specific protein kinase inhibitors as therapeutic agents [2]. However, due to the conserved nature of kinase binding pockets, achieving selectivity and potency remains a significant challenge.

To address this issue, various computational methods have been employed for kinase inhibitor design and optimization. These include quantitative structure–activity relationship (QSAR) models [3–7], pharmacophore modeling [8–11], molecular docking [12–15], virtual screening (VS) [16–19], molecular dynamics (MD) simulations [20–22], quantum mechanics/molecular mechanics (QM/MM) calculations [23–25]. More recently, artificial intelligence (AI)-based approaches, including machine learning (ML) [26–28] and deep learning (DL) [29, 30], have further enhanced predictive capabilities. Despite these advances, these methods tend to overlook the role of water molecules within the protein's binding pocket.

Water molecules play essential roles in maintaining protein structure and mediating dynamic interactions between proteins and ligands. Within the active site of proteins, water molecules often act as bridges between the protein and ligand or are displaced upon complex formation. Several research groups have highlighted the importance of water molecules in drug discovery. They have shown that predicting water molecule positions and understanding hydration sites can enhance protein–ligand binding affinity [31–34]. While studies on water molecules often rely on free energy calculations using methods such as WaterMap [35, 36], STOW [37] and MobyWat [38, 39], these approaches are computationally expensive and require extensive simulations. Additionally, individual binding free energy of each water molecule is insufficient to capture the cooperative effects between neighboring water molecules without further analysis [40].

To address these limitations, we developed a topological water network (TWN) analysis method. In our previous study, we demonstrated that TWNs can be leveraged to gain insights into the inhibitory activity and selectivity of kinase inhibitors, without relying on computationally intensive free energy calculations and while accounting for the cooperative behavior of neighboring water molecules [41]. We further demonstrated that the formation of these TWNs is influenced by the surrounding amino acid residues within the protein [42]. More recently, we introduced the TWN-based fragment screening (TWN-FS) method, which enables efficient identification of suitable fragment binders for specific hydration-influenced subpockets [43]. Building on this framework, the present study introduces a TWN-based fragment growing method (TWN-FG) for the potency optimization of kinase inhibitors. While TWN-FS was primarily designed to identify suitable fragments for binding within predefined hydration regions, such as the adenine pocket of kinases, its scope was limited to evaluating fragment compatibility within fixed sites. It did not address how fragments should be elaborated once an initial anchor was identified. In contrast, the current TWN-FG method advances beyond fragment screening by systematically analyzing the hierarchy of cooperative hydration regions surrounding the bound ligand. TWN-FG not only identifies favorable fragments but also prioritizes optimal directions for fragment extension based on TWN-derived hydration topology, thereby enabling rational prediction of growth vectors and structured fragment optimization. While several established methods, such as WaterMap [35, 36], GIST [44], and FTMap [45], analyze hydration thermodynamics or identify interaction hotspots within binding pockets, these approaches primarily focus on individual water molecules or solvent-accessible regions. In contrast, TWN-FG emphasizes the collective topological organization and dynamic connectivity of water networks, enabling extraction of hydration-encoded structural templates that guide fragment growth direction (Table 1). Importantly, TWN-FG is not intended to replace thermodynamic or hotspot-based approaches, but rather to complement them by introducing a network-level hydration perspective into fragment optimization.

Conceptual and methodological comparison between TWN-FG and established fragment analysis tools

In this study, we first performed TWN analysis on protein binding pockets to identify hydration patterns and categorize distinct hydration regions. We then compared these hydration regions with ligand fragments from the KinFragLib database [46] to explore fragment growth directions and optimal substituent types. This comparative analysis revealed a significant correlation between TWN-derived hydration patterns and crystallographic fragment orientations, suggesting that hydration patterns can provide valuable insights into effective potency-driven drug design strategies. Next, we applied this approach to a series of well-characterized kinases, including anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK), aurora/IPL 1-related kinase 1 (AURKA), cyclin-dependent kinase 2 (CDK2), checkpoint kinase 1 (CHK1), dual specificity tyrosine-phosphorylation-regulated kinase (DYRK1A), mitogen-activated protein kinase 1 (ERK2), maternal embryonic leucine zipper kinase (MELK), pyruvate dehydrogenase kinase 1 (PDK1), serine/threonine-protein kinase pim-1 (PIM1) and spleen tyrosine kinase (SYK). These targets were selected based on the availability of co-crystal structures with diverse ligands, ranging from small fragments to drug-like molecules, along with supporting SAR data. In each case, we found that fragments extended along high-TWN-frequency directions exhibited enhanced inhibitory activity compared to those extended along low-frequency paths. Finally, we leveraged this insight to design and synthesize novel inhibitors for mixed lineage kinase 1 (MLK1), demonstrating that TWN-FG method leads to improved potency.

Together, our findings establish a novel TWN-based framework for kinase inhibitor optimization. By incorporating the topological hydration network into early design stages, our approach facilitates rational selection of substituents and growth directions, offering a promising route to more potent and selective kinase inhibitors.

Material and methods

Retrieval of protein and ligand structure

The kinase-ligand interaction fingerprints and structures (KLIFS) database provides a standardized structural alignment of kinase domains extracted from numerous protein data bank (PDB) entries [47]. This alignment facilitates comprehensive comparative analysis of both kinase structures and their bound ligands. For this study, we obtained X-ray crystal structures of eleven human kinases namely ALK (PDB 3LCS) [48], AURKA (PDB 2XNG) [49], CDK2 (PDB 4ERW) [50], CHK1 (PDB 1NVR) [51], DYRK1A (PDB 6EIQ) [52], ERK2 (PDB 4ZZN) [53], MELK (PDB 5M5A) [54], PDK1 (PDB 1OKY) [55], PIM1 (PDB 1YHS) [56], SYK (PDB 1XBC) [57] and MLK1 (PDB 3DTC) [58] from the KLIFS database. The selection was restricted to structures exhibiting the DFG-in (active) conformation to ensure consistency in our analysis of the binding pocket. Furthermore, we used protein structures originally derived from ligand-bound complexes, but all non-protein components, including the bound ligand, were removed. This approach was adopted to preserve an open conformation of the binding pocket, which is suitable for the TWN analysis. These structures were subsequently prepared for analysis by removing non-protein components and subjecting them to the 'Prepare Protein' module in Discovery Studio 2024 (BIOVIA, San Diego, CA, USA). This process involved adding missing amino acid residues and hydrogen atoms, assigning correct bond orders and formal charges, and optimizing protonation states at a physiological pH of 7.4. The fragment library employed in our analysis was KinFragLib version downloaded on January 15, 2025, a curated subset of ligands derived from human kinases within the KLIFS database. KinFragLib categorizes fragments based on their specific locations within the kinase binding site such as adenine pocket (AP), front pocket (FP), gate area (GA) and solvent-exposed region (SE). This library covers protein information for 179 kinases, including ALK, AURKA, CDK2, CHK1, DYRK1A, ERK2, MELK, PDK1, PIM1 and SYK. Our region-based approach, centered on hydration sites (TWN formation sites), differs from KinFragLib's method for defining binding sites. Consequently, fragments from regions with low TWN occurrence were omitted from our analysis [see Fragment classification section]. The exact number of fragments used for each kinase is provided in Table S1.

Molecular docking

To perform the SAR study on kinase inhibitors (see Understanding the potency optimization of kinase inhibitors section), we collected chemical structures and their reported inhibitory activities from the relevant literature. The corresponding X-ray crystal structures of the target kinases were retrieved and processed for analysis. For compounds lacking co-crystal structures, molecular docking was performed on the processed kinase structures using the CDOCKER module [59] of Discovery Studio 2024 (BIOVIA, San Diego, CA, USA). Docked poses were selected to reproduce the experimentally observed binding mode of reference fragment compounds within the same chemical series. Compounds sharing overlapping core substructures with the corresponding reference ligand were required to preserve the hinge-binding orientation and core placement within the binding pocket. All ligands were energy minimized using the CHARMm force field. Pose similarity was quantitatively evaluated by calculating the RMSD of the shared core substructure relative to the reference crystal ligand. Only poses within 2.0 Å RMSD were retained for further analysis. All analyzed compounds satisfied this criterion (maximum RMSD = 1.51 Å), and the corresponding values are provided in the Table S2. This approach ensured that the docking results were guided by experimental structural evidence.

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulation

MD simulations were conducted with GROMACS version 5.1.4 [60] and the CHARMM27 all-atom force field [61]. The protein structure without the co-crystalized ligand was used for MD simulation. The protein was placed in a cubic box and solvated with the TIP3P water model [62]. Counter ions were added to neutralize the system, followed by energy minimization through 50,000 steps of the steepest descent algorithm. Equilibration was performed in two steps. Initially, the system was equilibrated for 100 ps under the NVT ensemble (constant number of particles, volume and temperature) at 300 K using the V-rescale thermostat [63]. Subsequently, it was equilibrated for 200 ps under the NPT ensemble (constant number of particles, pressure and temperature) at 1 bar using the Parrinello–Rahman barostat [64]. The production run lasted for 10 ns with restraints on the protein, using a time step of 1 fs. Periodic boundary conditions (PBC) were applied throughout the simulation. Bond constraints were managed using the linear constraint solver (LINCS) algorithm [65]. Short-range electrostatic interactions were calculated using a 12 Å cutoff, while long-range electrostatics were computed with the Particle mesh Ewald method [66]. For each protein, 1,000 frames of 10 ns MD trajectory were collected for TWN analysis. The rationale for employing 10 ns restrained simulations and an assessment of sampling sufficiency are provided in the Supplementary Data (Sect. 1.2).

Identification of topological hydration regions (Rgs)

Water molecules interact through hydrogen bonds to form cyclic structures called TWNs. In TWN analysis, the interaction potential energy between water molecules was computed using Lennard–Jones and Coulomb potentials, based on the rigid TIP3P water model. A hydrogen bond was defined as an interaction with an energy threshold of -2.25 kcal/mol (see Supplementary Data Sect. 1.1 for more details) [62]. While TWNs can have rings of different sizes (3, 4, 5 or 6 members), this study focused specifically on the structure of 4-membered rings due to their resemblance in size to fragments (MW ≤ 300) utilized in fragment-based drug design. A comparative analysis of different ring sizes (R3-R6) is provided in the Supplementary Data (Sect. 1.4), demonstrating that four-membered motifs offer a balanced representation of hydration topology in kinase binding pockets. We analyzed TWNs within the binding site of each kinase across all MD frames and extracted these TWNs for subsequent analyses. To ensure consistent comparison of kinase-inhibitor interactions, we adopted the KLIFS residue numbering system. This standardized approach defines the catalytic cleft using 85 residues, enabling comprehensive analysis of interaction patterns. We defined the kinase binding site as a 10 Å sphere centered on the midpoint between KLIFS residues 17 and 51, as this region covers all co-crystallized type I inhibitors from the KinFragLib database. This radius provides a unified reference frame for comparing TWN regions across diverse kinase systems. After extracting the TWNs, we proceeded to identify topological hydration regions (Rgs). The overall process consists of three key steps: pattern identification, pattern grouping, and region identification (Fig. 1). In the pattern identification step, recurring hydration patterns were extracted from MD trajectories using all TWNs as references, with water sets defined by four oxygen atoms aligned within 1 Å of a reference (Fig. 2A and B). This 1 Å threshold was used to group neighboring water molecules into topological networks, capturing water geometry and local packing within the binding pocket without explicitly computing solvation free energies. This approach is consistent with established hydration analyses such as WaterMap, which are based on inhomogeneous solvation theory [67, 68]. In the pattern grouping step, patterns (P) with nearby centroids (< 1 Å) were merged into grouped patterns (GPs), and union frequency (UF) values were calculated to reflect their stability (Fig. 3A). The rationale for employing 1.0 Å to merge patterns into groups are provided in the Supplementary Data (Sect. 1.3). This process allowed us to define water sets occupying equivalent spatial positions across different conformations. In the region identification step, GPs were ranked by UF, with redundancies removed. The resulting non-overlapping GPs were designated as the final Rgs (Fig. 3B).

Fragment classification

Fragments were categorized based on their structural characteristics and their position within topological hydration regions (Rgs). A fragment was assigned to a Rg if at least four of its atoms were located within 2.5 Å of the Rg's centroid (Fig. 4A). Only atoms within this 2.5 Å radius were considered for this assignment (see Supplementary Data Sect. 1.5). Fragments were categorized into unsaturated and saturated groups based on the presence of double or triple bonds. Additionally, fragments were classified as either cyclic or acyclic. A fragment was defined as cyclic if all its atoms within the ring were located within 2.5 Å of the Rg's center; otherwise, it was classified as acyclic. Finally, as shown in Fig. 4B, fragments were assigned to one of four classes namely Class I (unsaturated cyclic), Class II (unsaturated acyclic), Class III (saturated acyclic) and Class IV (saturated cyclic).

In vitro enzyme assay

MLK1 inhibition was evaluated by using the Eurofins Kinase Profiler service. For the initial fragment hit FS1, a single-point inhibition assay was performed at 10 μM. For the optimized compounds FG9a-d, kinase activity was assessed using a radiometric filter-binding assay based on a full 10-point concentration–response curve. Installation and dependencies

TWN-FG requires installation of Python 3.9 or a later version. The source code is available on GitHub (https://github.com/RgJeoung/TWN-FG) and has been integrated into a Conda environment. Additionally, we provide a yaml file (TWN-Region-Analysis.yaml) containing all the necessary dependencies for researchers to use with ease. The source code includes scripts for generating TWN-derived hydration patterns (TWN-Patterns.py) and regions (TWN-Regions.py) from the 4-membered ring TWNs. Furthermore, an executable file is available for extracting TWNs from the MD frames. All source codes are freely accessible. The input files consist of MD trajectory from CHK1, 4-membered ring TWN files from CHK1, and boundary files used for CHK1 TWN region analysis. These are provided as example files and can be modified based on the user's analysis needs. The output files are TWN-patterns and TWN-regions. These are in sdf format, where the TWN-pattern contains the coordinates of the oxygen atoms of water molecules in the reference TWN, and the TWN-region contains the coordinates of the oxygen atoms of all involved TWN-patterns. It also contains frequency and union frequency, and other TWNs within the pattern or other patterns within the region. Additionally, computation log files are provided. The TWN-region-analysis takes a few minutes on a single CPU core, from identifying TWN-patterns to TWN-regions.

For each region, we computed NVAavg and classified overlapping KinFragLib fragments (Table 3). The Pearson correlation coefficients (r) between NVAavg and the percentage of fragments belonging to each chemical class are summarized in Table 4, and the corresponding p values are provided in Table S3. It should be noted that the unit of analysis in this study is the hydration region rather than individual fragments, resulting in a limited number of regions per kinase. Therefore, effect sizes (correlation coefficients) and structural interpretability are emphasized over strict per-kinase significance thresholds. When data from all kinases were pooled, a moderate positive correlation was observed between the percentage of Class I fragments and NVAavg (r = 0.52), which remained highly significant (p = 1.97 × 10⁻10), indicating a robust global association across systems. At the individual kinase level, positive correlations were consistently observed (r ≥ 0.31), with particularly strong correlations in CHK1, DYRK1A, ERK2, PDK1, PIM1, and SYK (r ≥ 0.63). Regions with NVAavg values exceeding 0.90 displayed a high abundance of unsaturated cyclic (Class I) fragments. This suggests that highly stable and topologically well-defined hydration regions preferentially accommodate rigid, π-electron–rich frameworks. In contrast, correlations for other fragment classes were weaker and less consistent across targets, indicating that lower-NVAavg regions may permit greater chemical diversity.

Understanding the potency optimization of kinase inhibitors

We examined whether our TWN-based approach, TWN-FG, could elucidate the potency optimization of known kinase inhibitors. We identified compound series for ten kinases and found corresponding crystal structures in the PDB, ranging from small fragments to larger molecules. Among these, five kinases (CDK2, ERK2, PIM1, ALK, and CHK1) are discussed in this section, while the remaining kinases (DYRK1A, AURKA, PDK1, MELK, and SYK) are provided in the Supplementary Data. However, since not all kinases had structures covering the full range, from small fragments to larger compounds, we utilized docking to predict binding poses for some kinases. We included 3D configuration details in the Supplementary Data. The specific PDBs used for analysis included: 2VTA, 2VTI, 2VTL, 2VTN, 2VTP, 3EZR for CDK2 [70, 71], 4QP6, 4QP7, 4QP8, 4QPA for ERK2 [72], 5DHJ, 5DIA for PIM1 [73], 2XP2, 7JYT, 7JYS, 7JYR, 7JY4 for ALK [74, 75], 2C3L, 2C3K for CHK1 [76], 6S11 for DYRK1A [77], 2W1D, 2W1F, 2W1E for AURKA [78], 5HO8, 5HKM for PDK1 [79], 4UMR, 4D2W, 4D2V for MELK [80], 4FYN, 4FZ6, 4FYO for SYK [81]. All co-crystal and docking-derived ligand structures were aligned to the protein structures used in the MD simulations. The ligands were subsequently extracted for analysis. The inhibitory activities reported in the literature were rationalized based on the ligand structures and the topological hydration regions (Rgs).

CDK2 inhibitors

The optimization of CDK2 inhibitors began with fragment compound 1a and involved systematic exploration of substitutable positions on the indazole core. To explore favorable expansion directions from 1a, we identified topological hydration regions (Rgs) within the CDK2 binding site (Table S4). In contrast, the 7-position did not correspond to favorable Rg, indicating a sterically constrained and less productive vector.

Substitutions at the 4 or 5 positions of the indazole ring led to modest improvements in CDK2 activity, while modifications at the 6 or 7 positions were less favorable due to steric hindrance from the nearby Phe80, as previously reported in the literature [70]. While the limited performance at the 4-, 5-, and 7-positions (corresponding to Rg6, Rg8, and undefined regions, respectively) aligns with their relatively lower UF values compared with Rg2, TWN-FG highlights the 6-position (Rg2) as a viable and underexplored expansion direction. This possibility was further supported by superimposing co-crystal structures of two distinct CDK2 inhibitors, 1c (PDB ID: 2VTI) [70] and 1d (3EZR) [71], which confirmed that structural extension toward Rg2 is geometrically accessible. Moreover, as shown in Table S5, SAR data for the scaffold of compound 1d demonstrate that this direction enhances not only CHK1 activity but also CDK2, with a 140-fold increase in potency from 5a to 5b.

Nevertheless, the original study did not attempt direct substitution at the 6-position, but instead employed a scaffold hopping strategy by replacing the indazole ring with a pyrazole core, yielding compound 1e. From this new scaffold, chemical expansion toward Rg2 led to compound 1f, which achieved over a 100-fold increase in potency.

Subsequent optimization extended into Rg8 (UF = 266, NVAavg = 0.56), via hydrophobic substituents on the R1 position such as cyclohexyl (1g), phenyl (1h), and 1,5-difluorophenyl (1i) (Table S6). Despite the relatively low UF of Rg8, optimization of shape complementarity and hydrophobic interactions led to strong binding. In particular, compound 1i demonstrated exceptional potency (IC50 = 3 nM), likely due to strong hydrophobic interactions, and efficient replacement of unstable water molecules.

The derivatives, 1j and 1k, involved modifications to the substituent occupying Rg5 (Table S4). The original 4-fluorophenyl group was replaced with 4-piperidinyl and 3-piperidinyl. These changes resulted in the loss of interactions with Rg3 (UF = 446, NVAavg = 0.63), causing a decline in potency. However, in compound 1k, the nitrogen of the piperidine ring formed a hydrogen bond with the backbone NH of Asp86, partially restoring activity.

ERK2 inhibitors

Compound 2a, the co-crystal ligand of PDB 4QP6 [72], binds to the adenine pocket of ERK2 by forming hydrogen bonds with the hinge residues Glu106 and Met108. This pocket is covered by Rg1 (UF = 627, NVAavg = 0.94), which exhibits the highest UF among all topological hydration regions (Rgs) in the ERK2 binding site (Table S7). TWN-FG revealed three accessible Rg from the 5H-pyrrolo[2,3-b]pyrazine core: Rg17 (UF = 99, NVAavg = 0.74) near the 1-position, Rg6 (UF = 200, NVAavg = 0.84) adjacent to the 2- and 3-positions, and Rg2 (UF = 539, NVAavg = 0.90) extending from the 7-position (Fig. 5B). Meaningful Rg (which has high UF) was not detected near the 6-position. Among these, Rg2 exhibited the highest UF, followed by Rg6, and Rg17 with the lowest. Potency data presented in Table S8 revealed that expansion toward Rg2 led to the most potent compound 2d (LE = 0.55). In contrast, expansion into Rg17 resulted in compound 2b with the lowest ligand efficiency (LE = 0.29). Figure 6B illustrates the stepwise optimization of compound 2a, progressing through 2i and 2j to 2p. As outlined in Table S9, introducing an aromatic ring into Rg6 (R1-position) enhanced compound activity, likely due to the formation of an additional hydrogen bond with Lys114. The co-crystallized ligand 2j from PDB 4QP8 (IC50 = 280 nM) [72] features an additional pyridine ring compared to compound 2i. Occupying Rg2, 2j exhibited a 20-fold increase in potency over compound 2i (IC50 = 5900 nM). While Rg2 and Rg6 exhibited relatively higher NVAavg values (0.90 and 0.84, respectively), Rg3 displayed a lower NVAavg value of 0.70. This indicated that Rg3 had enough space to accommodate not only aromatic rings but also other fragments with non-planar geometry. Compound 2 k-p shows that both aromatic ring and aliphatic carbon chain extensions into Rg3 (R2-position) contributed to improved activity (Table S10).

PIM1 inhibitors

Compound 3a, the co-crystal ligand of PDB ID: 5DHJ [73], binds to the adenine pocket of PIM1 by forming hydrogen bonds with Lys67 and the hinge residue Glu121. This pocket is covered by Rg1 (UF = 665, NVAavg = 0.95) and Rg4 (UF = 413, NVAavg = 0.96), which are occupied by 1H-pyrazolo[3,4-c]pyridine and pyridine moieties, respectively (Table S11). TWN-FG revealed three accessible Rg from the pyridine moiety of 3a: Rg5 (UF = 334, NVAavg = 0.84) near the 2-position, Rg6 (UF = 313, NVAavg = 0.92) adjacent to the 5-position, and Rg11 (UF = 207, NVAavg = 0.77) extending from the 4-position (Fig. 5C). Compound 3b expanding into Rg11 with a lower UF showed decreased potency (Ki = 3,500), while compound 3c expanding into Rg6 with a higher UF exhibited improved potency (Ki = 6). Figure 6C includes the stepwise optimization of compound 3a, progressing through 3d to 3e. According to the docking study, replacing the pyridine ring of compound 3a with a 1-methyl-1H-pyrazole maintained the hydrogen bond with Lys67 and preserved occupancy of both Rg1 and Rg4. Further elaboration by introducing a cyclohexane-1,3-diamine moiety toward Rg8 (UF = 258, NVAavg = 0.55) led to compound 3e, which exhibited an approximately 19-fold increase in potency. ALK inhibitors

According to previous studies, the pyrazole ring of crizotinib plays a critical role in ALK binding by forming a CH–π interaction with Gly1202, while the 2,6-dichloro-3-fluorophenyl group enhances binding affinity through hydrophobic interactions [73]. These key interaction features are well captured by the topological hydration regions (Rgs) (Fig. 7). We identified various Rg within the binding pocket of ALK (Table S14). Rg10 (UF = 129, NVAavg = 0.95) effectively represents the pyrazole ring, and Rg9 (UF = 141, NVAavg = 0.73) partially reflects the characteristics of the 2,6-dichloro-3-fluorophenyl group. The high NVAavg value of Rg10 aligns with our general observation that conjugated ring systems, particularly those involved in CH–π interactions, tend to form well-defined Rg. Rg9 successfully reproduces the shape and position of the benzene ring; however, the small hydrophobic pocket formed by Asn1254 and Gly1269, originally occupied by the fluorine atom, is extremely limited in size. It is approximately 1.4 Å in radius, which corresponds to the van der Waals radius of a fluorine atom; therefore, it cannot support a stable water network, resulting in the absence of Rg. Notably, the fluorine at the C3 position was found to fill this small pocket. Other pharmacologically relevant moieties are also clearly captured by the Rg: the hinge-binding aminopyridine corresponds to Rg1 (UF = 496, NVAavg = 0.95); the linker connecting the aminopyridine and aryl groups occupies Rg3 (UF = 309, NVAavg = 0.93); and the solvent-exposed piperidine ring maps to Rg8 (UF = 149, NVAavg = 0.71). The presence of these well-defined Rg is consistent with the high binding affinity of crizotinib, which exhibits an IC₅₀ value of 1.8 nM.

CHK1 inhibitors

Foloppe et al. used crystal structure analysis to determine that compounds 5a and 5b exhibit different activities against CHK1 (Table S5) due to a buried pocket [76]. We identified various topological hydration regions (Rgs) within the binding pocket of CHK1 (Table S16). Compound 5a occupied Rg1 (UF = 622, NVAavg = 0.93) and Rg10 (UF = 171, NVAavg = 0.82), allowing for extensions toward Rg2 (UF = 435, NVAavg = 0.62) and Rg4 (UF = 344, NVAavg = 0.96), which guided the design of 5b and 5d, respectively (Fig. 8B). Three positions (4-, 5- and 6-positions) were identified as favorable vector by TWN-FG and 5-, and 6-positions with Rg2 and Rg4, respectively, was favorable than 4-position with Rg11(UF = 168, NVAavg = 0.83) (Fig. 5E). While Rg2 showed a higher UF compared to Rg4, suggesting a potential advantage for extensions toward Rg2, patent data [82, 83] indicated that compounds extending toward Rg4 (5b, 5 h-n) possessed superior potency to those toward Rg2 (5c-g) (Table S17). A detailed analysis revealed that Rg4 comprised a small number of topological hydration patterns and a high NVAavg value of 0.96. Conversely, Rg2 contained numerous patterns and a lower NVAavg value of 0.62. These findings suggested that while Rg2 displayed a higher UF, Rg4 corresponded to a buried pocket with a limited number of patterns, resulting in a higher entropic benefit for compounds that fit within it.

Rule-based hierarchical model of TWN-guided fragment growth

Comparative analysis across ten kinase systems revealed that fragment optimization consistently followed recurrent topological principles rather than target-specific idiosyncrasies (Table 5). These principles define a hierarchical rule-based framework governing productive growth directions. Rule 1 (Primary anchoring rule) states that the hydration region with the highest UF value frequently serves as the first anchoring site during fragment optimization. For example, in CDK2, Rg1 exhibited the highest UF value and functioned as the primary anchoring point, forming hydrogen bonds with the hinge region. This observation supports the notion that highly connected cooperative hydration regions preferentially define dominant anchoring vectors. Rule 2 (Adaptive expansion rule) describes that fragment expansion toward regions surrounding the core scaffold that possess relatively high UF values often leads to potency enhancement. For example, in ERK2, extension toward both Rg6 and Rg2 resulted in effective potency improvements, indicating that growth into neighboring cooperative hydration regions can provide productive optimization pathways. Rule 3 (Non-productive vector rule) highlights that vectors lacking coherent hydration topology generally show limited SAR progression. In several CDK2 substitution series, modifications directed toward poorly defined peripheral regions resulted in minimal potency gain, consistent with fragmented or unstable hydration organization. Rule 4 (Occupancy rule) emphasizes that, within a prioritized Rg, greater spatial occupation and topological coverage of the hydration region correlate with enhanced potency. For example, in ALK (Table S15), substituents occupying Rg8 demonstrated progressively improved activity as their steric volume increased (H < F < Cl). This trend suggests that increased occupation of the cooperative hydration region contributes directly to potency enhancement. Finally, Rule 5 (Design prioritization rule) integrates UF and NVAavg into a two-parameter descriptor that hierarchically ranks fragment growth directions. For example, in PIM1 (Table S13), expansion toward Rg4, characterized by a high UF value, resulted in higher potency for saturated ring derivatives compared to other substituents, supporting the prioritization of highly connected hydration regions during optimization. Collectively, these rules demonstrate that TWN-FG captures a generalizable topology-based framework for fragment growth rather than isolated case-specific rationalizations.

In-house compound optimization guided by TWN-FG

Having established a generalized rule-based framework for TWN-FG across diverse kinase systems (Table 5), we next sought to prospectively apply this strategy to the design and optimization of inhibitors targeting our protein of interest, MLK1. MLK1 was selected because this protein has limited structural information and few known inhibitors, making it challenging to explore using conventional approaches. Following the same procedure used in our previous analysis, we performed topological hydration region (Rg) identification for MLK1 (Table S18). Rg2 (UF = 347, NVAavg = 0.97), located in the hinge-binding area, satisfied the criteria of the Primary anchoring rule (Rule 1) and was selected for TWN-based fragment screening (TWN-FS). Through this process, we identified a hit compound, FS1, showing 82% inhibition against MLK1 at 10 μM. For details regarding the TWN-FS protocol, please refer to our previously published work [43].

Since no co-crystallized MLK1 structure existed for FS1 or related scaffolds, FS1 was docked into the MLK1 binding pocket (PDB 3DTC). To guide pose selection, structurally similar ligands co-crystallized with other kinases were searched. A closely related compound bound to PAK4 (PDB 5I0B) was identified [84]. Structural comparison using the ProBiS tool revealed significant similarity between the binding pockets of MLK1 (PDB 3DTC) and PAK4 (PDB 5I0B), with a Z-score of 2.31 [85]. Based on this, we selected the docked pose of FS1 by referencing the co-crystallized conformation and interactions of the similar ligand with PAK4. The selected pose of FS1 was further validated by RMSD value of 0.146 Å over the shared substructure relative to the reference ligand (Table S2). Molecular docking of FS1 into the MLK1 binding pocket revealed that it occupies both Rg2 and Rg7 (UF = 196, NVAavg = 0.75). Figure 9 illustrates the fragment optimization strategy from FS1 to compound FG9d. Docked poses of FS1 analogues (FG9a-d) were selected based on the FS1 docked conformation and satisfied the same RMSD-based substructure criterion (≤ 2.0 Å), ensuring preservation of the core binding mode (Table S2). In the course of this design, we identified two extendable regions within the MLK1 binding pocket, namely Rg4 (UF = 276, NVAavg = 0.94) and Rg1 (UF = 538, NVAavg = 0.72), and synthesized four derivatives, FG9a-d, to grow the molecule in those directions [see Chemical synthesis of MLK1 inhibitors in the Material and Methods section]. Our synthetic strategy was guided by the availability of fragments derived from previously studied kinase inhibitors in our in-house library, for which synthetic routes had already been established. This allowed us to directly substitute fragments occupying both Rg4 and Rg1 regions, leading to the synthesis of FG9a-d compounds without intermediate compounds.

As shown in Table 6, all 4 synthesized compounds, FG9a-d, exhibited strong inhibitory activity against MLK1, with IC₅₀ values ranging from 26 to 219 nM. The docked poses of FG9a-d provided insights into the observed activity differences (Table S18). These differences can be explained by Adoptive expansion rule (Rule 2) and Occupancy rule (Rule 4). Compounds with an S conformation (FG9a and FG9d) were able to extend toward and occupy Rg1, while those with an R conformation (FG9b and FG9c) failed to occupy Rg1 and instead occupied a lower UF region, Rg13 (UF = 122, NVAavg = 0.97). This series of optimizations demonstrates that TWN-FG can provide a practical and effective direction for structure-based potency enhancement of kinase inhibitors.

Discussion

A major hurdle in optimizing the potency of kinase inhibitors is the remarkable structural similarity among kinase binding pockets. Although various computational approaches have been developed to address this problem, most methods overlook the critical role of water molecules within the binding site. Water molecules form cooperative hydrogen-bonded networks that stabilize protein architecture and modulate ligand recognition. To capture these effects, our group previously developed topological water network (TWN) analysis, which systematically identifies cooperative hydration patterns without relying on computationally intensive free-energy calculations. TWN-FS applied this framework to evaluate fragment compatibility within predefined hydration regions. Building upon this foundation, TWN-FG represents a methodological progression that translates hydration topology into directional growth guidance. By hierarchically ranking hydration regions and mapping them to ligand extension vectors, TWN-FG enables rational fragment evolution rather than simple fragment selection, providing a TWN-driven strategy for kinase inhibitor optimization.

A central feature of this approach is the union frequency (UF) metric, which quantifies how consistently a given region is occupied by TWNs during MD simulations. While solvation effects are often treated as abstract unless supported by binding free energy calculations, UF offers a numerical descriptor that captures this phenomenon without such computational demands. In this study, we concentrated on topological hydration regions (Rgs) with high UF values, as these regions typically represent the most favorable sites for fragment growth. Importantly, regions not highlighted are not irrelevant; rather, they play comparable roles but are less favorable or less frequently occupied. Supporting this, our analysis revealed a strong correlation between UF values and the proportion of fragments occupying each region across multiple kinases. As shown in Table S19, Pearson correlation coefficients exceeded 0.5 for all kinases, indicating that TWN regions with higher UF are more frequently occupied by fragments, whereas regions with lower UF are less frequently utilized.

To illustrate the relevance of UF in guiding fragment design, we examined specific kinase systems. For PIM1, inhibitors occupying Rg11 with lower UF show decreased potency compared to those targeting Rg6 with higher UF (compound 3b and 3c in the Table S11 and S10). This is consistent with the general observation that high-UF regions tend to favor fragment binding. Interestingly, CHK1 presents an exception: inhibitors occupying Rg4, despite its lower UF compared to Rg2, display higher affinity (compound 5b and 5d in Tables S3 and S14). Such case is indeed a rather rare example. Across the analysis of 10 kinases, most followed the general trend predicted by UF, and we therefore highlighted CHK1 as an exceptional case. We reason that the increased activity at Rg4 arises not only from entropic favor but also from the formation of a few stabilizing hydrogen bonds. From the TWN perspective, Rg4 can also be regarded as a region with sufficiently high UF, where a limited number of water patterns with high NVAavg values indicate a well-buried pocket. This provides an additional explanation for the observed activity. In this sense, the higher UF of Rg4 should not be interpreted merely as a marker of pocket occupancy, but rather as an indicator of a compact and preorganized environment that favors ligand binding. By contrast, in PIM1, both Rg11 and Rg6 are located in more open regions, where the difference in UF can be more pronounced in terms of solvation effects.

To generalize these observations, we extended our analysis by selecting ERK2 as a reference system. Specifically, we examined how the regions defined in ERK2 correspond to those in the other nine kinases in our dataset. For clarity, we adopted the classification of kinase binding pockets proposed by the Paul group [86], in which Rg1 corresponds to the adenine pocket 1 (AP1), Rg2 to the hydrophobic pocket, Rg3 to the phosphate-binding region, Rg4 to adenine pocket 2 (AP2), Rg5 to the sugar region, and Rg6 to the hydrophobic channel. The Supplementary Table S20 summarizes this mapping, showing the Rgs of ERK2 alongside their corresponding positions in the other kinases. Interestingly, the regional assignments captured the physicochemical characteristics of each sub-pocket quite well. With only a few exceptions, the adenine and hydrophobic pockets as well as the hydrophobic channel, consistently exhibited high NVAavg values, whereas the phosphate-binding region and sugar region generally displayed lower values. This pattern highlights the highly conserved nature of kinase binding sites. In particular, AP1 displayed universally high UF and NVAavg values across all kinases, consistent with its role as the binding site for the adenine moiety of ATP and the aromatic character of the adenine ring. By contrast, the phosphate-binding region and the sugar region show marked variability in UF values depending on the kinase, suggesting that these regions are relatively flexible or context-dependent across kinases, which may influence fragment interactions.

Considering the fragment analyses reported in this study, the ten kinase case studies, and the practical optimization of MLK1 inhibitors demonstrate that TWN-FG can identify preferred fragment growing directions and offer intuitive, practical guidance for potency enhancement. Moreover, because TWN-FG identifies water networks without requiring explicit free energy calculations, it is expected to be computationally more efficient than other water-based modeling tools. While certain SAR trends may also be interpreted through conventional explanations such as steric complementarity or direct protein–ligand interactions, TWN-FG provides a complementary mechanistic perspective by characterizing intrinsic hydration topology of the binding site. Unlike traditional analyses that depend on existing ligand-bound structures, TWN-FG derives fragment growth preferences directly from cooperative water network patterns, independent of prior ligand knowledge. Although the present study includes retrospective and prospective validations using kinases with known co-crystal ligands, this ligand-independent feature suggests that the framework may be particularly valuable in early-stage discovery settings, where structural or SAR information is limited. Nevertheless, TWN-FG has some limitations. First, in shallow or desolvated pockets, such as those observed for some ALK inhibitors, poorly formed water networks reduce its effectiveness. This limitation may be alleviated by complementing TWN-FG with conventional tools such as docking to address regions where hydration networks are less informative. Second, the current implementation relies on protein-restrained conditions during MD simulations. While accounting for full protein flexibility would be ideal, quantitatively analyzing TWNs under such conditions remains highly challenging due to the coupled dynamics of both protein and water molecules. This limitation is not unique to TWN analysis but also applies to other computational approaches such as molecular docking and pharmacophore modeling, where the reliability of results strongly depends on the initial protein structure. In this study, we adopted a pragmatic approach by analyzing TWNs based on a relatively static protein backbone while allowing side chains to remain flexible. This strategy enables consistent identification and quantification of TWNs and is conceptually similar to established methods such as WaterMap, which also employ static protein conformations for hydration site analysis. Third, although TWN-FG focuses on cooperative water-water connectivity, protein-water interactions, including hydrogen bonding, electrostatic interactions, and van der Waals forces, also play important roles in determining water stability and displacement potential. The current framework does not explicitly incorporate protein-water interaction energies as quantitative descriptors. However, the observed water-water connectivity patterns are inherently shaped by the surrounding protein environment, as residue geometry and electrostatics constrain the formation and persistence of cooperative hydration networks. Thus, protein–water effects are partially embedded within the emergent topological patterns captured by TWN-FG. Fourth, the current framework does not explicitly account for electrostatic properties, which restricts its standalone predictive power. For example, as shown in Table S15, 4e (with a Me substituent, an electron-donating group) and 4d (with a Cl substituent, an electron-withdrawing group) exhibited markedly different inhibitory activity against ALK despite similar sizes. TWN-FG cannot directly capture such electrostatic effects, though incorporating appropriate descriptors could potentially address this limitation. Finally, although binding free energy calculations for individual water molecules are not required, the reliance on MD simulations entails considerable initial computational costs. Precomputing TWN regions and organizing them into a reusable library could mitigate this issue and offer substantial time-saving advantages.

Building on these limitations, we envision several directions for future development. First, we aim to incorporate additional descriptors into TWN-FG, including explicit protein-water interaction terms (e.g., hydrogen-bonding and electrostatic descriptors) as well as TWN shapes. While the current method provides intuitive guidance for fragment growth, adding these descriptors could enhance the quantitative accuracy of the analysis. Notably, our NVAavg analyses have shown meaningful correlations between fragment class and TWN shape, suggesting that including TWN shape as a descriptor, alongside hydrogen-bonding interactions with surrounding residues, could provide more deeper insights. Polarizable force fields such as AMOEBA [87] or CHARMM Drude [88] could also help provide a more accurate description of water dynamics. Although these could, in principle, be incorporated into TWN simulations, such an implementation would increase the computational cost by approximately 5–tenfold. Therefore, we intend to explore this option carefully in future work to ensure that the added accuracy justifies the practical trade-offs. Second, we are exploring the integration with artificial intelligence approaches, including machine learning and deep learning, to further strengthen the method. Although TWN-FG does not rely on binding free energy calculations for individual water molecules, it still requires substantial computational resources due to the MD simulations. Incorporating AI could enable faster predictions and broader applicability. Finally, we are considering the development of a precomputed TWN library. By storing TWN regions identified from MD simulations, such a library would allow rapid and reusable application of TWN-FG to new targets, thereby enhancing both efficiency and practicality.

Conclusions

In this study, we advanced the application of topological water networks (TWN) by developing a TWN-based fragment growing method (TWN-FG) for the rational design of kinase inhibitors. By quantifying hydration patterns into topological hydration regions (Rgs), we successfully captured the geometric and cooperative properties of water molecules within binding sites. Notably, we observed a positive correlation between Rg geometry, measured by the average normal vector angle (NVAavg), and specific fragment class (Class I). This supports the idea that Rgs can inform fragment growing strategies by aligning with experimentally validated binding orientations. To evaluate the practical utility of this approach, we conducted systematic case studies on ten different kinases. As a result, TWN-FG effectively explained the structure–activity relationship (SAR) of these kinases. Furthermore, the method was successfully applied to design and optimization of MLK1 inhibitors, achieving a progression from an initial screening hit (FS1) to a low-nanomolar compound (FG9d). This case exemplifies how TWN-FG can guide the potency optimization of kinase inhibitors. In conclusion, TWN-FG provides a powerful paradigm for improving potency, particularly in kinase inhibitor optimizations, and holds promise for broad application in next-generation drug discovery platforms by leveraging the topological properties of hydration networks.
