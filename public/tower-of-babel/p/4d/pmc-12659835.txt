Factors determining compliance with recommended intravitreal injections for retinal diseases among out-of-pocket, fully subsidized (non-paying), and insured patients: Real-world evidence from India
Authors: Anthony V Das, Brijesh Kashyap, Brijesh Takkar, Ragukumar Venugopal, Chirantan Chatterjee, Mehul C Mehta, Michael W Stewart, Gudlavalleti V S Murthy, Rohit C Khanna, Taraprasad Das, Raja Narayanan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Purpose:To determine the factors associated with compliance with recommended intravitreal injections (IVIs) for retinal diseases among out-of-pocket, insured, and fully subsidized (non-paying) patients.Methods:This retrospective observational hospital-based study included patients recommended for IVI in at least one eye between November 2011 and May 2022. Three cohorts of patients were assessed: out-of-pocket payers, insured, and those fully subsidized (non-paying) by the hospital. The main outcome measure was the compliance rate, defined as the proportion of patients receiving an injection after one was recommended. The impact on the visual outcome in each cohort was a secondary outcome.Results:Overall, 50,408 IVI were recommended for at least one eye and were included in the analyses. Male patients were more common (66.72%), and the mean age was 55.36 ± 16.39 years. The overall compliance rate with the first recommended injection was 51.37%, which increased and eventually stabilized with subsequent recommendations to approximately 80% (range: 77.48%–82.93%). Insured patients had a higher compliance rate with the first recommendation compared to the non-insured (100% vs. 49.84%; P < 0.00001), and non-paying patients had a higher compliance rate after the first recommendation compared to paying patients (62.51% vs. 48.66%; P < 0.00001). Patients with insurance achieved a better visual outcome at the last follow-up compared to other cohorts.Conclusion:Insured patients have a much higher compliance rate with recommended IVIs compared to out-of-pocket or non-paying patients. The high non-compliance rate among non-paying patients suggests that factors other than the direct cost of the injections may impact compliance with recommended treatment.

Anti-vascular endothelial growth factor (anti-VEGF) injections are the standard of care for various retinal vascular diseases. the improvement in visual outcomes with therapy and the ease of delivery have led to the rapid adoption of this treatment modality. However, the high frequency of visits required to administer these drugs according to an effective treatment regimen places a significant burden on the patients, their caregivers, and the health system.

Known barriers to compliance with a treatment regimen include cost, patient anxiety, access to care, and unsatisfactory treatment outcomes.[1] An economic evaluation of the costs and benefits to patients with neovascular age-related macular degeneration (nAMD) reported that the visual acuity improvement associated with anti-VEGF treatments generated $5.1–$8.2 billion in patient benefits and $0.9–$3.0 billion in societal value (patient benefits with respect to treatment costs) over 3 years.[2] The global anti-VEGF market for retinal diseases exceeded $7.0 billion in 2020, making VEGF inhibitors among the most expensive drugs in Medicare, with an additional $5.21 billion in fee-for-service spending reported in 2019.[34]

In India, out-of-pocket payments account for 54.78% of the current expenditure for anti-VEGF therapy.[5] Furthermore, studies have shown that nearly 50% of patients requiring anti-VEGF therapy are lost to follow-up, with the most common reasons being inadequate finances and no perceived improvement in vision with therapy.[6] Therefore, as patients require frequent follow-up and long-term care, adherence to an injection regimen must be understood in relation to different payment categories and with respect to their visual outcomes.

This study assesses the compliance rate with recommended intravitreal injections (IVIs) among out-of-pocket, insured, and fully subsidized (non-paying) patients at a large tertiary eye care network in India. The institute provides care to all socioeconomic segments of society and has the ability to test the impact of choice of expenditures in clinical settings.

Methods

Study Design and Approvals: This retrospective observational hospital-based study included all patients who were recommended to receive IVIs for at least one eye between November 2011 and May 2022 at a multitier ophthalmology network spread across four neighboring states (Telangana, Andhra Pradesh, Odisha, and Karnataka) of India.[7]

All patients completed a standard consent form for electronic data privacy at the time of registration. the study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the local institutional review board (approval number: LEC-BHR-R-101).

The clinical data of each patient who underwent a comprehensive ophthalmic examination were entered into a standardized template in a browser-based electronic medical records system (eyeSmart EMR) by uniformly trained ophthalmic personnel who were supervised by an attending ophthalmologist.[8]

Cases: Eligible patients were identified via a search of the eyeSmart EMR and included those for whom IVIs (ranibizumab, bevacizumab, brolucizumab, aflibercept, or ziv-aflibercept) were recommended for one or both eyes. The most common indications for treatment were diabetic macular edema (DME), nAMD, and retinal vein occlusion. In all the cases, the IVIs were recommended based on clinical examination by a trained retinal specialist.

Exclusion criteria included those with the prespecified macular diseases for whom injections were not recommended.

Data Retrieval and Processing: Data that adhered to the search criteria were retrieved from the electronic medical record and segregated into a single Excel (version 2013) spreadsheet. Retrieved variables included data on demographics, clinical presentation, ocular diagnosis, visual acuity, IVI drug recommended, and compliance with the recommendation for treatment (at least one injection). Standardized definitions were used for geographic locations and socioeconomic status.[9]

The cost of the injection was calculated in Indian Rupees (INR) and then converted to US Dollars (USD) at the current (July 16, 2024) exchange rate of 1 USD = INR 83.57.[10] Data were retrieved up to a maximum of 10 injections per patient.

Statistical Analysis: Descriptive statistics using mean ± standard deviation and median with interquartile range (IQR) were used to evaluate the demographic data. The Chi-square test (Stata software, Stata Corp. 2015. College Station, TX: Stata Corp LP) was used to evaluate differences in the categorical groups. Continuous variables were checked for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Wilcoxon signed-rank tests were performed to assess the difference between the paired samples of baseline visual acuity and final visual acuity. The Mann-Whitney U test was applied to assess the difference between independent groups of patients who underwent injection and those who did not. The R software (version 3.5.1) was used to perform a multiple logistic regression analysis using Akaike’s information criteria, with stepwise elimination modeling.

The odds ratio (OR) and 95% confidence interval (95% CI) of the variables significantly associated with the risk of not taking an injection on the first visit are reported. A probability value (p) of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Definitions: The study defines “compliance rate” as the proportion of injections performed divided by the number recommended. The study categorizes patients according to three payment categories (out-of-pocket, non-paying, and insurance) according to how treatment costs were financed.

Out of Pocket: Out-of-pocket payments were made by 39,046 (77.46%) patients, 26,439 (67.71%) of whom were males, and the mean age was 56.35 ± 15.68 years, with a median of 58 years (IQR: 50–66). Patients came from urban (19,820; 50.76%), metropolitan (5,039; 12.91%), and rural (14,187; 36.33%) regions. bevacizumab was prescribed to 31,937 (81.79%) patients. The conversion rate after the first visit was 46.7% (18,234/39,046). After the first visit, the average conversion rate improved to 78.75% (range: 75.79%–82.1%).

The mean visual acuity at baseline was 0.85 ± 0.80 logMAR and at the final examination was 0.80 ± 0.86 logMAR, which was statistically significant (P = <0.001). The overall compliance rates for all patients are listed in Fig. 1.

Non-paying: A total of 9,820 (19.48%) patients were non-paying, 6,190 (63.03%) of whom were males, and the mean age was 50.08 ± 20.5 years with a median of 55 years (IQR: 45–64). Patients came from urban (5,026; 51.18%), rural (3,831; 39.01%), and metropolitan (963; 9.81%) regions. bevacizumab was recommended for 9,054 (92.2%) patients, with a compliance rate at the first visit of 62.51% (6,170/9,870), which improved to 84.38% (range: 82.67%–85.48%) over the next nine visits.

The mean visual acuity at baseline was 1.1 ± 0.93 logMAR, and the mean visual acuity at the final examination was 1.03 ± 1.01 logMAR, which was statistically significant (P = <0.001). The proportions of patients’ financial status by years are shown in Fig. 2.

Insurance: There were 1,542 (3.06%) patients who had public (1,099; 71.27%) or private (443; 28.73%) health insurance, 1,004 (65.11%) of whom were male, and the mean age was 48.52 ± 24.37 years with a median of 57 years (IQR: 44–65). Patients came from urban (702; 45.53%), rural (569; 36.9%), and metropolitan (271; 17.57%) regions. bevacizumab was recommended for 1,164 (75.49%) patients, with a compliance rate of 100% (1,542/1,542) at the first visit, and compliance rates of 80.01% (range: 73.68%–88.24%) over the next nine visits.

The baseline visual acuity of insured patients was 0.8 ± 0.79 logMAR, and the visual acuity at the last examination was 0.71 ± 0.77 logMAR (P = <0.001) [Fig. 3]. The recommended drug and the category of patients across all visits are shown in Supplementary Table 2.

Logistic regression identified several factors associated with failure to receive any injections despite the ophthalmologist’s recommendation [Fig. 5]. Males were found to have a higher probability of receiving first injections compared to females (OR = 0.95; P = 0.008), patients from rural areas exhibited a lower likelihood of receiving injections compared to those from metropolitan areas (OR = 1.14; P < 0.001), and non-paying patients were found to be significantly less likely to receive injections compared to paying patients (OR = 1.67; P < 0.001). Additionally, drugs with higher costs tended to have lower compliance rates, except brolucizumab. Patients with severe blindness showed worse compliance overall [Supplementary Table 5].

Discussion

This study sought to describe the compliance rates for receiving IVIs among patients presenting to a multitier ophthalmology hospital network in India who are paying out-of-pocket, insured, and non-paying, by using electronic medical records-driven big data analytics. In this study, more than half of the patients did not receive an IVI at the first visit, but the compliance rate improved with the second injection and thereafter stayed relatively stable. At the first visit, the compliance rate among insured patients was higher than for those without insurance and similarly higher among non-paying patients compared to patients paying out-of-pocket.

In India, a high proportion (54.78%) of healthcare expenditures are out-of-pocket,[5] and patients with several morbidities have a greater proportion of out-of-pocket payments in India compared to those with non-communicable diseases (NCD) alone (63% vs. 46.7%).[11]

A cross-sectional study among rural and urban households in a resource-rich setting in Puducherry revealed that most (67%) patients paid out-of-pocket for their healthcare. According to the district blindness control society (DBCS) scheme, factors associated with higher out-of-pocket expenditure in patients undergoing cataract surgery were treatment of systemic comorbid conditions and nonmedical travel expenses. The mean cost incurred was INR 1,700.[12]

IVIs are most commonly administered for eye-related complications of chronic NCDs, such as diabetes mellitus and hypertension. When IVIs are recommended, these patients must incur an incremental cost for the ophthalmic care on top of their regular spending for the chronic systemic diseases. In addition to the medical costs, out-of-pocket expenditure for inpatient and outpatient care also includes non-medical costs, such as travel, lodging and food, and opportunity costs.[13]

In this study, only half of the patients (51.37%) who were recommended to have an IVI were administered one at that visit. This could be explained by the high cost of the injections and the out-of-pocket expenditures that now confronted these patients. After the first injection, however, the compliance rate for injections among out-of-pocket and non-paying patients improved to 80.08%. Reasons for improved compliance with ongoing treatment are unclear, though we speculate that it might be related to perceived improvement in the visual and anatomic outcomes of the retinal disease with treatment. Prospective studies of patients who are noncompliant would be needed to uncover the underlying reasons. Among the insured patients, the coverage drops beyond the first injection. Possible reasons include insurance denials, caps for annual coverage, difficult insurance navigation, and low reimbursement rates. Actuarial analysis shows that an increase of annual health premium cost by an additional price of INR 25 among a pool of 1 million policyholders would cover the cost of IVIs based on the current prevalence of DME and wet age-related macular degeneration in India (unpublished data).

Universal Health Coverage (UHC) aims to provide all individuals and communities with health services while alleviating an imposed financial hardship.[14] Our data shows that non-paying patients had a higher likelihood of receiving injections (compared to paying patients), but despite having the cost of their care covered, a significant proportion of them failed to receive an injection at the first visit. This could be due to the high indirect cost of care associated with a new ongoing treatment regimen, which still must be borne by the poor patients.

The sample sizes of the three payor-based groups are not balanced, but the higher compliance rates after the first injection visit indicate that provision of insurance and similar cost coverage policies positively impact patients’ adherence to treatment regimens for chronic retinal diseases. Importantly, this increased compliance is associated with better visual outcomes.

This study shows that insurance has a major impact on the compliance rate of patients. In line with the concept of value-based care, the patients with insurance had a higher compliance rate at the first visit and had better visual outcomes compared to uninsured patients. All the patients (100%) with insurance received the prescribed care at the first visit, compared to only 49.84% among the non-insured patients. The national sample survey (NSS) 7th round showed that approximately 14% of the rural population and 19% of the urban population had healthcare insurance coverage in India, and among them, about 13% of rural and 9% of urban population were covered by government sponsored health insurance (Pradhan Mantri Jan Aarogya Yojana (PM-JAY) or Ayushman Bharat).[15] The ambitious project of the PM-JAY has so far issued 125.5 million insurance cards, reimbursed 10.9 million hospital admissions, and empaneled 22,796 hospitals.[16] With the conversion of over 150,000 primary health centers to Ayushman Bharat Health and Wellness centers, the government of India aims to further its mission of UHC.[17] Though the proportion of patients (3.06%) who had insurance was low in our study, we had sufficient data to determine that if the direct cost of the treatment is covered, then the chance of patients receiving it is significantly higher.

High-income countries allocate a significantly larger share of their gross domestic product (GDP) to healthcare compared to India. The United Kingdom, for example, ranks second globally, with healthcare spending accounting for approximately 8% of its GDP. In contrast, the United States dedicates over 17% of its GDP to healthcare. While the UK operates a unified, publicly funded healthcare system for all its citizens, the US healthcare landscape is characterized by a fragmented structure with multiple payers and providers.

As India advances its efforts to strengthen its healthcare system, it must proceed with caution, learning from the experiences of other developed countries. The US, despite facing persistent challenges related to escalating healthcare costs, disparities in access, and health equity, demonstrates strong performance in disease management outcomes. For instance, five-year cancer survival rates in the US are approximately 40% higher than those in the UK. Additionally, the proportion of patients with diabetes receiving timely treatment in the US is more than six times that in the UK and double that in Canada.[18] To better understand the reasons for improved compliance among patients with insurance, one must understand the concerns of patients without insurance. Treatment Burden Score (TBS) provides a single quantitative measure of the overall burden on patients receiving IVI for various eye conditions.[19] TBS scores for IVI in the US showed that the frequency of injections did not influence the TBS. Anxiety, inadequate information about the disease and treatment, and the burden on family members were possible reasons for lower TBS.

Cost Sharing Reduction plans by the US government help to remove some of the financial burden from patients, thereby allowing for improved compliance.[20] Our study observed a similar increase in the utilization of healthcare by patients who had been previously uninsured.

As India takes its initial steps into value-based healthcare, accurate data will be needed to guide decisions and establish policy. We have shown that insurance support increases the likelihood of complying with recommended care, but our study has several limitations, including its retrospective design. Though our overall sample is large, only a small proportion of patients had private insurance, and this might not be representative of the entire Indian payor system. nevertheless, our study of different payor characteristics within a single network provides new insight into insurance-based compliance trends associated with IVIs.

Conclusion

We present a large dataset that details the compliance rates of patients recommended to receive IVIs. Insurance plays a major role in ensuring timely care and better visual outcomes.
