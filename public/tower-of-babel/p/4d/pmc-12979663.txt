Study on sound insulation performance of walls in traditional timber dwellings of the Miao ethnic group in Qiandongnan
Authors: Xiaohu Huang, Wei Zhao, Zongsheng Huang, Yan Wang, Youcui Du, Longyong Du, Yuanduo Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Traditional timber-framed buildings in Southwest China hold international recognition for their historical, cultural, artistic, and social value, yet their physical performance adequacy for modern living remains unexamined. Field tests on Miao dwellings in Jidao Village, Guizhou, characterized wall sound insulation and assessed contemporary suitability. Key results indicate: Newly “L-shaped” dwellings exhibited optimal wall insulation. Critical frequencies in modern walls aligned with experimental ranges, traditional walls showed lower experimental than theoretical values. Traditional exterior wall insulation increased with enclosure (“U-shaped” courtyard > “L-shaped” > “linear layout”, Max≈2 dB), while internal walls showed no significant differences. Enclosed layouts attenuated noise transmission, enhancing overall insulation. Traditional and newly “Linear-type” dwellings showed comparable performance, but new “L-shaped” dwellings with double-layer timber and embedded rock wool (30 mm) outperformed others by 8–14 dB. Exterior and bedroom walls critically influenced overall insulation, directly impacting daily life. Enhancing insulation while preserving ethnic features is essential.

Introduction

The architectural art of Miao vernacular dwellings embodies a rich accumulation of cultural components and ecological adaptation wisdom. It encompasses practices such as textile arts1, embroidery, rituals, and responses to local climate and topography2, collectively forming a construction system with distinctive cultural and regional characteristics. As a typical representative in Miao settlements in southwestern China and even Southeast Asia, this type of traditional timber-structure dwelling holds unique historical and cultural value and enjoys international recognition. With growing dual emphasis on cultural heritage preservation and the improvement of living quality in urban and rural development, how to enhance its physical performance while preserving its traditional appearance has become a critical issue. However, whether its physical performance meets modern living requirements, particularly regarding acoustic comfort, has not been systematically studied. In 2023, noise complaints accounted for 61.3% of all ecological environment reports in China, ranking first among various types of pollution, with noise issues being particularly prominent around airports3 and in educational spaces4–6. Although timber-structure buildings offer ecological advantages, their walls often exhibit inferior sound insulation performance compared to masonry or concrete structures due to the lower material density7. Especially under the influence of the coincidence effect, lightweight thin-panel walls are prone to sound insulation dips in the mid-to-high frequency range, compromising speech intelligibility and residential privacy8–11.

Globally, a certain foundation of acoustic research has been established concerning the walls12–14 and building elements15–18 of traditional timber structures. For example, Japanese research has conducted measurements and simulations on the sound insulation performance of traditional machiya wooden townhouses, pointing out the weak sound insulation of paper-wood sliding doors (fusuma) and wooden plank walls19 in the low-frequency range. Nordic studies have focused on the acoustic retrofitting of historical timber structures, improving sound insulation through resilient connections and filling cavities with sound-absorbing materials while emphasizing the preservation of the original appearance20–22. However, these studies have predominantly concentrated on mainstream timber construction types within their respective regions. There remains a lack of targeted acoustic performance analysis and exploration of performance enhancement pathways for the traditional wall systems specific to the multi-ethnic regions of southwestern China, which often feature mixed constructions integrating bamboo-wood weaving and rammed earth plastering23,24.

In light of this, this paper takes typical timber-structure Miao dwellings in Qiandongnan as the subject of study. Through field measurements and numerical analysis, it investigates the sound insulation performance of their traditional walls. The study aims to: (1) reveal the sound insulation spectral characteristics of this type of wall under typical construction, and analyze its acoustic weaknesses and their physical causes; (2) based on measurement results and acoustic theory, propose optimized wall design strategies for sound insulation that are suitable for preserving the traditional appearance. This research provides a scientific basis for the ecological retrofitting and livability enhancement of vernacular dwellings in ethnic regions.

Study area profile and research methodology

Study area profile

This study was conducted in Qiandongnan Miao and Dong Autonomous Prefecture (26.21°–28.63° N, 107.45°–109.79° E), Guizhou Province, Southwest China. Encompassing 29,817 km2 with a population of approximately 4.653 million, the prefecture features complex topography dominated by mountains (70% of terrain) and hills interspersed with river valleys. Elevations range from 300 to 2490 m (mean: 1000 m). The region experiences a humid subtropical climate characterized by. Mean annual temperature 14–18 °C. Annual precipitation 1200–1600 mm. Relative humidity 80–85%. Annual sunshine duration ≈1400 h. Qiandongnan represents a multi-ethnic convergence zone where historical development has forged distinctive linguistic, religious, and socio-cultural systems. The Miao and Dong ethnic groups constitute the cultural core, with their festivals, attire, and performing arts defining regional cultural identity. Notably, the Miao population exceeds 40% of the prefecture’s total, making this area one of China’s primary Miao enclaves. Traditional Miao dwellings exemplify adaptive vernacular architecture. Elevating structures above ground level, ensuring structural integrity, on mountain gradients (15°–35° inclination), providing moisture/flood-proofing and wildlife deterrence. These terrain-responsive designs embody profound sociocultural values while demonstrating exceptional environmental synchronization. Consequently, Miao timber architecture provides an ideal typology for investigating. Ecological retrofitting of ethnic traditional buildings. Preservation of historical-cultural significance. Sustainable habitat development in ethnic regions.

Sound insulation measurement methods and test specimens

Research ideas

As illustrated in Fig. 1, this study began by establishing the central scientific question. Subsequently, a rigorous selection and categorization of dwelling samples was conducted, encompassing both traditional and newly built structures with different layouts. During the comprehensive on-site measurement phase, three core categories of data were collected synchronously: indoor and outdoor noise spectra, building physical construction details (structure, materials, sealing), and environmental parameters (temperature, humidity, wind speed). Based on this, the study proceeded to the analysis stage: characteristic patterns were visually identified through spectral comparisons, sound insulation indices were calculated to rank performance, and influencing factors were analyzed for key frequencies. Ultimately, this entire process forms a coherent cycle, translating analytical conclusions into optimized strategies that are both scientifically sound and culturally appropriate. This approach accomplishes a complete workflow from problem identification, through empirical data analysis, to the formulation of design guidelines.

Measurement principles and acoustic performance requirements

This study adhered to Acoustics—Field measurement of sound insulation in buildings and of building elements—Part 1: Airborne sound insulation (ISO 16283–1:2014). Testing comprised two sequential procedures. Sound pressure level difference measurements under white noise excitation. Reverberation time measurements in adjacent rooms, enabling calculation of weighted sound reduction index.

Sound Insulation Testing Protocol. A dodecahedron loudspeaker generated broadband white noise. Sound pressure levels were recorded on both sides of wall assemblies using precision sound level meters. Data streams were retrieved via File Zilla and processed in dBTrait6 software to determine. Weighted average sound pressure levels, Standardized level differences.

Reverberation Time Measurement. The interrupted noise method was employed. Pink noise emitted from a dodecahedron source was stabilized before abrupt cessation. Sound level meters captured decay curves over 4–5 s. Data acquisition used File Zilla, with dBTrait6 computing reverberation times and deriving room absorption coefficients via the Sabine formula.

Testing methodology

Testing was conducted on each type of vernacular dwelling wall assembly, with five measurements performed per wall and results averaged. Sound insulation metrics were evaluated across the 100 Hz to 3150 Hz frequency range. All procedures strictly adhered to GB 50118–2010: Acoustic Design Code for Civil Buildings, including instrumentation specifications. The following parameters were calculated for every wall type: standardized level difference, weighted sound reduction index25, critical frequency, and coincidence effect magnitude—enabling comprehensive sound insulation performance analysis.

The Standardized level difference represents practical sound insulation performance under actual occupancy conditions, accounting for room absorption and reverberation time to quantify sound energy attenuation in real-world scenarios. Weighted sound reduction index characterizes inherent sound insulation capacity under laboratory conditions, expressed as a single-number rating of theoretical sound-blocking potential, spectrum adaptation terms further indicate performance against low- or high-frequency noise. Critical frequency identifies the onset frequency of sound insulation dips caused by bending wave-sound speed matching, revealing vulnerable frequency bands.Coincidence effect explains the physical mechanism of insulation dips through wave-coupling theory, reflecting efficient sound-structure energy transfer at specific frequencies and incidence angles. Collectively, these parameters establish a multidimensional evaluation framework. provides baseline material performance, validates real-world effectiveness, and coincidence effects guide frequency-specific structural optimizations.

A standardized white noise source was regulated via remote control to maintain background noise ≥ 10 dB below received levels26. The core requirement is to establish a fully diffuse sound field in the receiving room and avoid direct sound dominance. To achieve this condition, we adopted a configuration where the sound source was positioned in an open field outside the building, and the receiving room was located inside. In this setup, the key sound transmission paths comprise: (1) the direct line-of-sight distance from the external sound source to the building’s exterior wall (typically 3–5 m), (2) the path of sound wave transmission through the tested exterior wall, and (3) the paths involving multiple reflections within the receiving room. This total acoustic path length significantly exceeds 7 m, thereby effectively ensuring the diffuse field condition (Fig. 2).

To conduct this study, field investigations were undertaken in five Miao villages of Southeast Guizhou: Kala, Qingjiang, Qianhu, Langde, and Jidao. These surveys identified three representative cliometrician typologies of traditional Miao timber dwellings: “Linear-type”, “L-shaped”, and “U-shaped”courtyard configurations—each exemplifying ecological intelligence and cultural continuity. Figure 4 illustrates the location, plan, elevation, and section drawings of six case-study dwellings. Traditional “Linear-type” dwellings encompass “square shaped”, “Four-Row Three-Bay”, “Seven-Row Six-Bay” layouts (Fig. 3). Horizontally distributed along mountain contours, they feature lightweight timber frames and cantilevered corridors that simultaneously adapt to rainy climates and minimize material usage. The two-story structure includes. A raised ground floor for livestock stabling, an upper living level partitioned by plank walls and furnishings, an interstitial void beneath flooring that absorbs livestock noise. “L-shaped” dwellings center around semi-enclosed courtyards. Their angular design resolves geom antic (feng shui) considerations while integrating fire pits with ancestral altars, uniting familial life and sacred space. Acoustically, the “L-shaped” attenuates external noise through courtyard reverberation, and the room transitions create natural sound barriers. Courtyard-style (tripartite) dwellings employ “U-shaped” symmetrical layouts. The enclosed courtyard moderates microclimate and hosts communal activities, where greenery and spatial depth absorb ambient noise. Tiered courtyard walls buffer living quarters from noisy. Totemic motifs on gatehouses chronicle clan histories, embodying Miao ritual protocols through spatial hierarchy.Collectively, these typologies demonstrate how ingenious spatial design and material applications achieve residential comfort and cultural preservation within resource constraints.

2.Primary Construction Techniques

2.Primary Construction Techniques

Primary Construction Techniques

Traditional Miao timber walls utilize locally sourced fir planks as non-load-bearing enclosures. Vertically oriented planks are precision-joined through tongue-and-groove joinery embedded in column grooves, reinforced horizontally with cross-ties. Key adaptations include. Humidity response. Natural wood shrinkage accommodates moisture variations. Ventilated foundations. Stilted construction preventing dampness. Structural integrity. Top pressure bars locking wall assemblies. Surface treatment. Carbonized preservative layer from hearth smoke, tung oil finishes highlighting wood grain. Cultural expressions. Carved motifs (butterflies, birds) integrating spiritual symbolism with ventilation. This craft epitomizes stilted architecture’s human-nature symbiosis. Stress dissipation through anisotropic fir properties. Climate resilience via joinery flexibility. Practical-spiritual unity through ornamentation and preservation techniques.

Modern constructions leverage fir’s porous microstructure through carbonization and preservative treatments. Two typologies were investigated (Figs. 6, 7). “Linear-type”. Single-layer fir plank walls. “L-shaped”. Multi-layered composite assemblies featuring. Fir structural framing. rock wool (30 mm) interlayers. Decorative surface panels. Air cavities and resilient connections disrupting sound bridges. Tongue-and-groove seam sealing. Asymmetric wall configuration for sound wave scattering. These innovations balance timber aesthetics with acoustic performance enhancement.

Data processing

The measurement data were transferred from the sound level meters to a computer via FileZilla software. Subsequently, statistical analysis was performed using dBTrait 6 and Excel software to obtain the standardized sound pressure level differences across frequencies for the interior and exterior walls of each building. Following corrections for reverberation time and background noise, the apparent sound insulation values for each wall were calculated, from which the weighted sound reduction indices (Rw) were derived. Finally, a paired-sample t-test was conducted using SPSS 26.0 software, with the significance level (α) set at 0.05, to enhance the reliability of the experimental findings.

Result and analysis

Analysis of sound insulation in external walls of vernacular dwellings

Analysis of sound reduction index of exterior walls in vernacular dwellings

Figure 10 demonstrates a sequential decrease in the critical frequencies of external walls across the studied dwellings: Newly built “Four-Row Three-Bay” dwellings (630–800 Hz); Traditional “Four-Row Three-Bay” and “L-shaped” dwellings (400–500 Hz); Traditional “seven-bay six-span” and “U-Shaped” Courtyard 1 dwellings (315–400 Hz); and Traditional “square shaped”, newly built “L-shaped”, and “U-Shaped” Courtyard 2 dwellings (200–250 Hz). This ordering indicates that critical frequency varies with dwelling age and typology, even when nominal wall thickness is identical. A key finding is the deviation between experimental values and theoretical predictions for traditional dwellings, whereas results for newly built constructions aligned with theoretical ranges. All dwelling typologies exhibited significant fluctuation in sound level difference (2–10 dB) within the 100–315 Hz range, revealing poor stability in the low-frequency sound insulation performance of fir timber walls. In contrast, from 315 to 3150 Hz, the level differences stabilized and showed a linear growth of approximately 3 dB per octave above their respective critical frequencies, which is consistent with mass-law expectations. This pattern demonstrates a fundamental mechanistic difference: low-frequency performance is vulnerable due to inadequately controlled structural vibrations, making it heavily resonance-dependent, while mid-to-high-frequency performance is more stable as the structural response becomes uniform with minimal resonance impact. Performance comparisons across frequency bands further clarify these trends: In the 100–200 Hz range, the newly built “L-shaped” type achieved the highest insulation, the traditional “Four-Row Three-Bay” type the lowest, with others showing comparable performance (± ≤ 1.5 dB); In the 200–500 Hz range, the newly built “L-shaped” type again performed best, the traditional “square shaped” type performed worst, and others were comparable (± ≤ 2 dB); In the 500–3150 Hz range, the newly built “L-shaped” type remained superior, the traditional “Four-Row Three-Bay” type was inferior, and other types showed minimal variation (± ≤ 1 dB).

Comparative analysis of sound insulation performance between exterior and interior walls of vernacular dwellings

Traditional Miao dwelling walls exhibit a dual paradox in acoustic performance versus functional zoning. Exterior walls, constructed as massive solid barriers (1–2 m) to embody the ethical symbolism of “defense against external and internal threats,” fail to mitigate modern noise due to material gaps, yielding sound insulation values (≤ 30 dB) below functional efficacy. Bedroom walls, designed with specific orientations and dimensions to reinforce the ritualistic principle of “gender segregation,” feature acoustically transparent structures (wooden plank walls ≤ 25 dB/lattice doors) that physically undermine privacy needs. Interior partition walls, prioritizing “harmony between humans and nature” through mobility (movable partitions ≤ 15 dB) or adhering to feng shui taboos (decorative perforated walls), sacrifice acoustic isolation. This results in severe sound pressure conflicts, such as kitchen chopping noises (85 dB) intruding into study quiet zones (35 dB). These findings reveal a core conflict: traditional culture employs symbolic spatial divisions (orientation/dimensions/feng shui) to signify functional purposes, yet the comprehensive lag in acoustic technology creates a severe disconnect between the functional integrity of physical space and the cultural-ethical expectations of privacy.

Discussion

Characteristics of sound insulation performance of traditional miao wooden dwellings walls

The sound insulation performance of walls in traditional Miao timber-framed dwellings is fundamentally determined by their material composition and construction details, manifesting distinct characteristics in the frequency spectrum. The sound insulation quantity of a wall is collectively influenced by the surface density of the panels, the stiffness of the studs, the infill material, and the layout. Generally, higher surface density and stiffness, along with infill materials of greater bulk density, contribute to better sound insulation performance31. The sound insulation curve is typically stiffness-controlled at low frequencies, follows the mass law in the mid-frequency range, and is dominated by the coincidence effect at high frequencies. Sound insulation dips often occur in both the low and high-frequency regions, leading to performance degradation. Field measurements also revealed that the degradation of the elastic modulus in aged timber can cause these insulation dips to shift towards lower frequencies, creating discrepancies with theoretical predictions32.

Currently, regions such as Scandinavia, Canada, and Japan are at the forefront of research on the acoustic performance of engineered wood products (e.g., Cross-Laminated Timber (CLT), glulam)33,34. Their modern timber wall systems systematically achieve high sound insulation ratings (often with weighted sound reduction indices Rw exceeding 45–50 dB) through the use of high surface density prefabricated panels, resilient connections, modular cavity absorbent layers, and damping coatings. These systems allow for precise control over the coincidence effect and structural flanking paths. In contrast, Miao dwellings embody an adaptive strategy of “low-tech, high wisdom.” For instance, the “courtyard-house” layout forms an acoustic gradient, and their double-panel with cavity construction implicitly aligns with the mass-spring-mass principle35,36. The fundamental distinction lies in the fact that modern systems rely on industrialisation and numerical simulation to achieve predictable high performance, whereas traditional practices are based on experiential knowledge, presenting inherent limitations, particularly in low-frequency and impact sound insulation.

Consequently, enhancement pathways can involve the localized integration of appropriate technologies while respecting the traditional framework. This could include: preserving the layered concept while introducing resilient studs or acoustic break components to mitigate flanking transmission37,38; judiciously incorporating engineered wood elements to increase surface density and stiffness where feasible; and filling cavities with high bulk density sound-absorbing materials (e.g., rock wool) to suppress resonance and the coincidence effect[,39, 40, while carefully considering the breathability inherent to traditional walls. Composite constructions integrating thermal insulation can synergistically improve both acoustic and thermal performance, potentially raising the weighted sound reduction index above 40 dB41,42, an approach for which Japan provides effective integration precedents.

In summary, systematically regulating the parameters of mass, stiffness, and damping can enhance the full-frequency sound insulation of walls. This offers a culturally sensitive acoustic improvement strategy for similar timber vernacular dwellings globally. Its core lies in the creative integration of local wisdom with modern acoustic principles, aiming to provide academically valuable and practically significant support for the living conservation and sustainable development of traditional villages.

The relationship between the sound insulation performance of walls in traditional Miao timber dwellings and traditional construction wisdom

Traditional Miao timber dwellings predominantly employ thin wooden planks, with a thickness of only 2–3 cm, as the primary wall material. This results in a characteristically low baseline sound insulation, typically within the range of 20.5–25.5 dB. Such walls exhibit significant sound leakage and flanking transmission defects. Neither the plank walls nor the internal lightweight partitions incorporate dedicated sound insulation detailing, leading to the easy transmission of both airborne and impact noise. Within the compact floor plans, where bedrooms are often adjacent to public activity areas, acoustic interference is pronounced. The floor construction in the stilted (ganlan-style) dwellings is relatively lightweight, causing noticeable transmission of impact sounds from activities such as walking or moving objects on the upper level. Furthermore, functional spaces like the fire pit and kitchen are frequently situated close to bedrooms, amplifying domestic noise and indoor reverberation. This performance gap exhibits a context-dependent impact on daily living comfort. It may have been largely tolerable within the traditional context characterized by low ambient noise levels and single-family occupancy patterns. In contrast, within modern environments featuring high-intensity noise sources and increased residential density, this gap leads to significant practical issues, including compromised privacy, noticeable leakage of intelligible speech, and heightened susceptibility to sleep disturbance. With the widespread adoption of modern household appliances, these inherent acoustic deficiencies have become more prominent, creating an urgent need to improve the acoustic environment through functional and structural updates43.

The above discussion indicates that a dynamic perspective is needed to examine the temporal evolution of traditional building performance. This study finds that the sound insulation performance of traditional timber walls of the Miao ethnic group in Qiandongnan exhibits characteristics of “dynamic attenuation.” Although the structural type is the same, the approximately 200-year-old sample TTCD1 shows a weighted sound reduction index approximately 1 dB higher than the approximately 50-year-old sample TTCD2. This difference stems from the combined effects of construction techniques, maintenance conditions, and intensity of use. Therefore, the acoustic assessment and retrofitting of traditional dwellings must systematically incorporate diachronic factors such as construction date, craft heritage, and maintenance history to accurately understand their performance evolution and develop precise, low-intervention enhancement strategies.

In response to the inherent limitations of the material’s sound insulation, Miao dwellings have evolved a dual system of wisdom integrating cultural adaptation and spatial modulation. The first is a cultural adaptation mechanism, which transforms certain everyday noises into socially meaningful symbols. For instance, the sound of a loom is perceived as a symbol of household productivity, and conversations around the fire pit reinforce familial bonds, thereby psychologically reducing the perceived disturbance of the noise. The second is the active modulation of spatial form, which creates a gradient of sound insulation through three typical layouts: the linear (“linear layout”) layout, with weak enclosure, offers the poorest sound insulation; the L-shaped (“L-shaped”) layout provides some acoustic buffering through semi-enclosure, resulting in moderate performance; and the courtyard (“U-shaped”) layout, with strong enclosure, creates multiple interfaces and acoustic transitions, improving the sound insulation by approximately 10 dB compared to the linear layout.

This traditional wisdom of “cultural resilience + morphological adaptation” embodies an acoustic design strategy of “compensating for material limitations through spatial design,” offering a significant paradigm for the acoustic optimization of modern timber dwellings. However, the performance ceiling of this system remains around 28–32 dB, which struggles to meet the modern need for residential acoustic privacy requiring sound insulation of ≥ 35 dB. Therefore, alongside the living conservation of traditional dwellings, it is imperative to innovatively integrate contemporary sound insulation technologies to achieve the dual enhancement of cultural heritage preservation and physical performance.

The enlightenment of modern craftsmanship wooden dwellings walls on improving the sound insulation performance of traditional dwellings walls

Traditional Miao timber dwellings commonly employ thin wooden plank walls, which typically achieve a weighted sound reduction index (Rw) of less than 30 dB, with low-frequency insulation often falling below 20 dB. Additionally, impact sound transmission through floors is a pronounced issue. In contrast, modern timber construction systems have achieved a significant improvement in wall sound insulation through systematic enhancements in both layout configuration and wall thickness. Although the performance may not yet reach the 45 dB requirement of the Chinese residential standard GB 50118–2010, this approach provides a clear direction for subsequent research and the acoustic retrofit of traditional dwellings. Future research and upgrades for traditional dwellings can focus on the following areas:

Structural Optimization: The application of modern light-gauge steel studs and prefabricated assembly systems44 not only improves construction efficiency and safety but also offers flexible support for acoustic design. Studies indicate that appropriately increasing stud spacing within structurally permissible limits can effectively enhance sound insulation performance45. Furthermore, acoustic optimization of connection points—such as the use of resilient rubber pads46, soft gaskets, and other flexible materials—can significantly reduce flanking transmission and mitigate structure-borne sound.

Material Upgrades: The core strategy involves rationally increasing the wall surface density and incorporating high-performance acoustic materials47. For example, adding gypsum board or high-density panels to the interior or exterior side of the original wooden planks can significantly improve the sound insulation while preserving the external appearance48,49. Installing industrial-grade acoustic underlays (e.g., rubber mats, polyurethane damping boards) between studs and panels or within floor joists can effectively suppress vibration transmission. Concurrently, selecting insulation materials with appropriate density and thickness (e.g., fiberglass wool, polystyrene boards) enables synergistic improvement in both thermal and acoustic performance50,51. Notably, novel environmentally friendly sound-absorbing materials (e.g., acoustic panels based on date palm midribs52) also offer potential for low-cost and sustainable acoustic improvements.

Integration of Function and Construction: Modern timber construction emphasizes acoustic planning in spatial layout and incorporates techniques such as open design and concealed acoustic treatments53. Furthermore, some advanced construction technologies offer valuable references for the modernization of traditional dwellings. For instance, periodic annular groove structures54 can be used to modulate the vibration characteristics of wooden panels, and technologies like Thermally Pre-treated Engineered (TPE) timber panels55 can simultaneously enhance the thermal and acoustic performance of building envelopes.

To improve acoustic performance while preserving traditional appearance, the following comprehensive measures can be implemented: seal gaps in original walls using traditional craft techniques and add concealed composite sound insulation layers on the interior side (e.g., “rock wool + gypsum board + antique-style finish”); retrofit doors and windows with double glazing and sealing strips to reduce airborne sound leakage; combine these with reasonable layout adjustments and employ functional decorations like sound-absorbing tapestries and perforated partitions to balance acoustic effectiveness with cultural expression. Through these targeted measures, the sound insulation of traditional timber walls can be progressively enhanced to approach or meet the 45 dB requirement of the GB 50118–2010 standard. This allows for the significant improvement of living comfort while protecting historical character, achieving a balanced development of cultural heritage preservation and quality of life.

Limitations and prospects of the study

This study has several limitations. In terms of samples and data, the conclusions drawn from eight case studies primarily reveal underlying physical mechanisms; their generalizability requires validation with an expanded sample set. Furthermore, key material parameters relied on estimations, lacking direct support from mechanical tests. Regarding the measurements, environmental fluctuations and non-ideal boundary conditions on-site introduced uncertainty. The study also did not systematically investigate the effects of flanking transmission, the acoustic weak points of doors and windows, or variations in sound source types. With respect to the research scope, modern reference systems, such as reinforced concrete walls, were not included for comparative analysis. These limitations define the boundaries within which the findings are applicable and suggest future directions for expanding the sample size, refining measurement techniques, and broadening the scope of comparative analysis.

Building upon the existing foundation, future research should establish a complete investigative cycle encompassing “mechanism verification—simulation and optimization—practical validation”. This can be operationalized in three sequential steps. First, conduct fundamental experimental verification. Acoustic performance of walls should be determined under standardized conditions. Crucially, direct mechanical testing of reclaimed old timber combined with controlled accelerated aging experiments should be performed to quantify the degradation models of key mechanical parameters, such as the elastic modulus of wood. This constitutes the core priority for verifying and refining the “material aging—sound insulation degradation” mechanism. Second, undertake numerical simulation and optimization. Based on the clarified mechanisms, refined modelling approaches (e.g., coupled Finite Element/Statistical Energy Analysis) should be employed for parametric analysis. This allows for the systematic assessment of the acoustic potential inherent in both traditional and retrofitted constructions and the quantification of complex sound transmission paths, such as flanking transmission. Finally, implement long-term monitoring and evaluation. Post-occupancy evaluations of retrofitted dwellings are essential. By integrating objective acoustic data with residents’ subjective feedback, the long-term effectiveness and social acceptability of the improvement measures can be comprehensively examined. The ultimate goal is to synthesize this process into an evidence-based guideline for performance enhancement.

Conclusion

The results indicate that the newly built “L-shaped” dwelling utilizing a “double-layer cedar plank with rock wool (30 mm) filling” construction achieved the optimal sound insulation performance for its exterior wall (33.5 dB), bedroom wall (37 dB), and internal partition wall (33.5 dB). This represents an improvement exceeding 30% compared to traditional single-layer walls, strongly validating the effectiveness of increasing material thickness and incorporating sound-absorbing fills. Among the traditional dwellings, the sound insulation of exterior walls improved with a higher degree of spatial enclosure (courtyard > L-shaped > linear, with a maximum difference of approximately 2 dB). This suggests that enclosed layouts can attenuate sound energy through multiple reflections. In contrast, the sound insulation of internal partition walls showed no significant variation across the different traditional dwelling types. Considering the characteristic terraced distribution of traditional dwellings along slopes—where lower units require protection from noise originating above, and upper units need optimized internal acoustics—sound insulation design should adhere to the principle of "construction-first, form-assisted, and terrain-adapted." This means prioritizing the enhancement of baseline performance through double-layer composite structures with acoustic insulation, utilizing spatial morphology to gain an auxiliary benefit of approximately 2 dB, and implementing targeted reinforcements in key areas based on specific topographic conditions. The new "L-shaped" design provides an empirical model for the retrofit of traditional structures. Subsequent research should integrate modern timber construction technologies to further develop terrain-adaptive designs. This approach can facilitate the progressive enhancement of sound insulation in traditional timber walls toward relevant standards, such as the 45 dB requirement outlined in GB 50118–2010 (Code for Design of Residential Buildings), thereby providing a valuable reference for their performance optimization.
