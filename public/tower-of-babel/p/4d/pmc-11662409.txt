Comparison of rhythm versus rate control of atrial fibrillation in heart failure subgroups: Systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials☆
Authors: Rabbia Siddiqi, Anas Fares, Mona Mahmoud, Kanwal Asghar, Ragheb Assaly, Ehab Eltahawy, Blair Grubb, George V. Moukarbel
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Patients with concurrent heart failure (HF) and atrial fibrillation (AF) have poor outcomes. Randomized clinical trials comparing rhythm control approaches to rate control of AF have yielded conflicting results and there is a paucity of updated and comprehensive evidence summaries to inform best practice in HF patients. We therefore conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis to compare outcomes with rhythm versus rate control of AF in various subgroups of HF patients. In HF patients overall, we found high certainty evidence that rhythm control decreased all-cause and cardiovascular mortality (hazard ratio [HR, 95 % confidence interval] 0.64 [0.43–0.94]) and HR 0.50 [0.34–0.74] respectively). Rhythm control was associated with decreased HF hospitalization (risk ratio [RR] 0.79 [0.63–0.99], moderate certainty), but did not significantly decrease thromboembolic events (RR 0.67 [0.32–1.39], low certainty). The mean difference in left ventricular ejection fraction [LVEF] from baseline to last follow-up was greater in rhythm control group by 6.01 % [2.73–9.28 %] compared with rate control. Subgroup analyses by age, HF etiology (ischemic or non-ischemic), LVEF, presence of diabetes and hypertension did not reveal any significant differences in treatment effect. The survival and hospitalization reduction benefit of rhythm control of AF in HF patients likely reflects the success of catheter ablation especially in HF with reduced ejection fraction. These data are important to guide shared decision-making when managing AF in HF patients.

Introduction

Atrial fibrillation (AF) and heart failure (HF) are widely prevalent, and often co-exist due to common risk factors such as increasing age, male gender, obesity, hypertension, diabetes, and ischemic heart disease [1]. Heart failure, irrespective of etiology, predisposes patients to developing AF. Similarly, AF may not only induce tachycardia-mediated cardiomyopathy, but also worsens outcomes in preexisting HF through atrial remodeling, increased atrial stiffness, and loss of the atrial contribution to stroke volume [2]. A meta-analysis found that the presence of AF increased the relative mortality risk by 20% in HF with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) and by 7% in HF with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF), compared with patients having HF without AF [3]. This underscores the importance of effective and timely management of AF and its complications in patients with HF.

Historically, rate control strategies have been the mainstay of AF management, with a limited role for rhythm control approaches. Evidence for benefit of rate control largely came from the AFFIRM (Atrial Fibrillation Follow-up Investigation of Rhythm Management) trial in which patients who were randomized to rhythm control with antiarrhythmic drugs (AAD) and/or direct current cardioversion (DCCV) had no survival benefit and an increased adverse event rate compared with patients in the rate control arm [4]. However, an on-treatment analysis of AFFIRM data suggested that maintenance of sinus rhythm (but not necessarily use of AAD) was associated with longer survival [5]. Rhythm control strategies have later evolved considerably, and subsequent trials have had varying success with this approach [6]. In the AF-CHF (Atrial Fibrillation and Congestive Heart Failure) trial, outcomes were comparable between rate and rhythm (AAD and/or DCCV) control arms [7]. The EAST-AFNET 4 (Early Treatment of Atrial Fibrillation for Stroke Prevention Trial) trial, which enrolled both HF and non-HF patients with AF, demonstrated benefit of rhythm control (with AAD, electrical cardioversion, or catheter ablation) over rate control in reducing the composite of cardiovascular death, acute coronary syndrome, stroke, or HF hospitalization [8]. More recently, CASTLE-AF (Catheter Ablation versus Standard Conventional Therapy in Patients with Left Ventricular Dysfunction and Atrial Fibrillation) and CASTLE-HTx (Catheter Ablation for Atrial Fibrillation in Patients with End‐Stage Heart Failure and Eligibility for Heart Transplantation) studies confirmed survival benefit of catheter ablation in HF patients [9,10].

Previous meta-analyses have been limited to assessing the comparative efficacy of catheter ablation and medical therapy of AF in HF [11,12]. However, to our knowledge there is no meta-analysis updated with latest trial data that addresses the overarching question of rhythm control versus rate control of AF in HF. Furthermore, prior meta-analyses have had a narrower focus, either studying outcomes by left ventricular ejection fraction or type of atrial fibrillation or only cryoablation technique. These did not assess outcomes by clinical and demographic characteristics such as HF etiology, New York Heart Association (NYHA) class, age, gender, and presence of comorbidities [13]. We therefore sought to perform a comprehensive updated systematic review and meta-analysis to synthesize the evidence from contemporary randomized controlled trials (RCTs) pertaining to rhythm versus rate control of AF in HF, focusing especially on outcomes by clinical characteristics. We aimed to identify clinical subgroups that may benefit more from one strategy over the other. We also aimed to estimate absolute risk differences between the two treatment strategies across a range of patient important outcomes.

Methods

This meta-analysis was conducted in accordance with PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis) guidelines [3]. A protocol was registered in PROSPERO [14].

Data sources and search strategy

A comprehensive search of PubMed, Embase, and Cochrane library from database inception to October 24, 2023, was performed by an experienced librarian. Search terms included “atrial fibrillation”, “heart failure”, “antiarrhythmic”, “ablation”, “cardioversion”, “cardiomyopathy” and “rate control”. Search strategies for each database can be found in Table S1. All citations were downloaded as EndNote 21 files and imported into Rayyan AI which is a collaborative interface for screening articles [15]. Study screening, selection, and data extraction processes were conducted by two reviewers independently.

Study selection and data extraction

Studies were included if they were randomized controlled trials assessing a rhythm control strategy versus only rate control in patients with AF and concomitant HF. Rhythm control strategies could include either AAD, DCCV, or catheter ablation.

Exclusion criteria were post-cardiac surgery atrial fibrillation, crossover studies, observational prospective or retrospective studies, and conference abstracts. Additionally, studies were excluded if they did not identify HF status of participants, if they did not report outcomes separately for patients with HF, or if they reported analyses other than intention-to-treat.

The systematic review included all eligible studies, while the meta-analysis included only those that provided analyzable outcomes data.

Study outcomes

The primary outcome of interest was all-cause mortality. Secondary outcomes included cardiovascular (CV) mortality, thromboembolic events, HF hospitalization, and left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) percentage change from baseline. After review of available data, we also included the primary composite outcome of given trials, which most trials defined as composite of all-cause death and HF events, although definitions varied across trials (Table 1). Statistical analysis

Effect estimates were pooled using a generic inverse variance weighted DerSimonian and Laird random effects model [16]. Summary effect estimates were calculated as hazard ratios (HR) or risk ratios (RR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) for dichotomous outcomes. If studies did not report HR for time-to-event outcomes, we computed incidence rate ratio using MedCal as an approximation for HR [17, 18]. For the outcome LVEF percentage change from baseline, the summary effect was expressed as mean difference between rhythm and rate control groups and the corresponding standard deviation.

Heterogeneity was evaluated using the Cochrane Q statistic and quantified using I2 statistic [19]. To explore heterogeneity in pooled results, we conducted leave-one-out sensitivity analyses to identify outliers and influential cases [20]. Outlier studies are regarded as those whose individual effect differs significantly from the pooled effect, while influential cases are those studies whose omission significantly changes the pooled overall effect estimate [21]. If more than one influential case was detected, we again conducted meta-analysis after excluding all the influential cases.

Publication bias was unable to be formally assessed due to the low number of studies included [22]. A test of interaction was performed to assess subgroup differences. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were conducted using R Foundation for Statistical Computing package “meta” v4.11.0.

Risk of bias and certainty of evidence assessment

Risk of bias was assessed for primary outcomes for the studies included in the meta-analysis using the Cochrane Risk of Bias tool version 2.0 [23]. Certainty of evidence was evaluated using the GRADE approach [24]. A summary of findings table expressing absolute effect estimates was generated using the MAGICapp, with baseline risk estimates derived from the control arms of included studies [25].

Results

Search and study characteristics

Our search initially identified 9845 records, of which 3145 were removed as duplicates (Fig. 1). Of the remaining 6700 records, 6629 were excluded after title/abstract screening and 58 after full text review. Ultimately 13 studies met our eligibility criteria and were included in the systematic review, and 11 of these studies contributed data to the meta-analysis.Fig. 1PRISMA diagram of study selection process.Fig. 1

A total of 4615 patients were included in these 13 trials. Study characteristics are presented in Table S2. Rhythm strategies included: catheter ablation only (n = 5); anti-arrhythmic drugs and/or electrical cardioversion (n = 5); catheter ablation and/or electrical cardioversion (n = 1); ablation and/or anti-arrhythmics (n = 1); and a combination of all 3 modalities in 1 study. Rate control strategies included beta-blockers, calcium channel blockers, and digoxin. CASTLE-AF and CASTLE-HTx allowed amiodarone in either arm, and 31 % and 47 % of patients respectively in the medical therapy arms received amiodarone at baseline. The median age of participants was between 55 years and 73 years and the proportion of male patients ranged from 61% to 96 %. Median follow-up varied from 6 to 61 months. Most trials enrolled patients with reduced or mildly reduced EF. Only 4 trials (EAST-AFNET, RAFT-AF, RACE, and CAFÉ II) enrolled HF patients irrespective of EF.

Meta-analysis of 6 RCTs showed a statistically significant reduction in all-cause mortality with rhythm control compared to rate control (HR 0.64, 95 % CI 0.43–0.95) with significant heterogeneity (I2 76 %, p < 0.01; Fig. 2a). Secondary outcomes

Cardiovascular mortality

Meta-analysis of 5 RCTs showed a statistically significant reduction in cardiovascular mortality with rhythm control compared to rate control (HR 0.50, 95 % CI 0.34–0.74) with significant heterogeneity (I2 75 %, p < 0.01; Fig. 2b). No outlier was identified, however AF-CHF and CASTLE-HTx were identified as influential cases (Fig. S1b). Upon excluding both influential cases, heterogeneity was eliminated (I2 0 %) and pooled effect remained consistent with primary analysis (HR 0.51, 95 % CI 0.42–0.63), p < 0.01).

Heart failure hospitalization

Meta-analysis of 5 RCTs showed a statistically significant benefit of rhythm control over rate control in reducing HF hospitalizations (RR 0.79, 95 % CI 0.63–0.99; I2 59 %, p = 0.04; Fig. 2c). No outlier was identified, however AF-CHF and CASTLE-AF were identified as influential cases (Fig. S1c). Upon excluding both influential cases, heterogeneity was eliminated (I2 0 %) and pooled effect remained consistent with primary analysis (RR 0.78, 95 % CI 0.61–0.98), p = 0.04).

Thromboembolic events

Meta-analysis of 3 RCTs showed a benefit of rhythm control over rate control in reducing thromboembolic events however this did not reach statistical significance (RR 0.67, 95 % CI 0.32–1.39; I2 48 %, p = 0.15; Fig. 2d). No outlier was identified, however RACE was identified as an influential case (Fig. S1d).

Composite primary outcome

Meta-analysis of 6 RCTs showed a statistically significant reduction in the composite outcome with rhythm control compared to rate control (RR 0.74, 95 % CI 0.59–0.94) with significant heterogeneity (I2 73 %, p < 0.01; Fig. 2e). CASTLE-HTx was identified as an outlier but not as an influential case (Fig. S1e). The definition of the composite outcome varied between trials (Table 1).

Left ventricular ejection fraction percentage change from baseline

Meta-analysis of 7 trials showed that the mean increase in LVEF from baseline to study follow-up was significantly greater with rhythm control than with rate control (mean difference between rhythm and rate control groups: 6.01 positive percentage points, 95 % CI 2.73–9.28; I2 90 %, p < 0.01; Fig. 2f). EAST-AFNET was identified as both an outlier and an influential case (Fig. S1f).

LVEF (reduced vs preserved)

Subgroup analysis of 2 RCTs showed comparable benefit of rhythm control in patients with reduced LV function (HR 0.63, 95 % CI 0.42–0.96) and in patients with preserved LV function (HR 0.82, 95 % CI 0.47–1.43; p-value of interaction = 0.46; Fig. 3b). Reduced EF was defined as <45 % in RAFT-AF and <40 % in EAST-AFNET, while preserved EF was >45 % in RAFT-AF and >50 % in EAST-AFNET.’

NYHA classification (class 1/2 vs 3/4)

Subgroup analysis of 4 RCTs showed similar pooled effect of rhythm control in patients with NYHA class 1 or 2 (HR 0.62, 95 % CI 0.35–1.11) and with class 3 or 4 (HR 0.55, 95 % CI 0.21–1.44; p-value of interaction = 0.83; Fig. 3c).

Age (<65 years vs ≥ 65 years)

Subgroup analysis of 3 RCTs showed comparable benefit of rhythm control in patients <65 years of age (HR 0.36, 95 % CI 0.10–1.31) and in older ( ≥ 65 years) patients (HR 0.78, 95 % CI 0.49–1.22; p-value of interaction = 0.27; Fig. 3d).

Hypertension

Subgroup analysis of 3 RCTs showed comparable benefit of rhythm control in patients with hypertension (HR 0.68, 95 % CI 0.53–0.88) and in those without hypertension (HR 0.78, 95 % CI 0.47–1.30; p-value of interaction = 0.63; Fig. 3e).

Diabetes mellitus

Subgroup analysis of 3 RCTs showed comparable benefit of rhythm control in patients with diabetes (HR 0.67, 95 % CI 0.33–1.37) and in those without diabetes (HR 0.39, 95 % CI 0.18–0.83); p-value of interaction = 0.31; Fig. 3f).

Risk of bias and certainty of evidence

Risk of bias was assessed for the primary outcomes and was judged to be low overall in the included studies (Fig. S2). Certainty of evidence was assessed as high for all-cause mortality and cardiovascular mortality, moderate for HF hospitalization, composite outcome and change in LVEF, and low for thromboembolic event. For three outcomes, we rated down the level of certainty for indirectness arising from variation in definitions of respective outcomes (Table 1). In addition, the outcome of thromboembolic events was rated down for imprecision evidenced by wide confidence intervals, arising from fewer total participants. Significant inconsistency was seen in LVEF change from baseline (I2 = 90 %, p < 0.01), possibly because of non-uniform methods used to assess EF across trials including echocardiography, radionuclide ventriculography, and cardiac magnetic resonance imaging. Discussion

In this systematic review and meta-analysis of 13 RCTs and 4615 patients, we summarized the evidence on rhythm versus rate control of AF across the HF spectrum, with attention to clinically relevant subgroups. We found high certainty evidence that rhythm control decreased all-cause mortality by 36 % (ARD: 50 fewer deaths per 1000) compared to rate control. This result was influenced primarily by the CASTLE-HTx trial, which demonstrated an extreme benefit of catheter ablation in a higher risk cohort of end-stage heart failure with reduced ejection fraction. Interestingly a recent meta-analysis that only focused upon HFpEF patients also noted a similar mortality benefit of rhythm control (odds ratio 0.74, 95 % CI 0.67–0.81; p < 0.001); however, this analysis was limited to observational cohort studies [26].

With respect to secondary outcomes, the greatest magnitude of benefit of rhythm control was observed for the outcome of cardiovascular mortality (HR 0.50, 95 % CI 0.34–0.74), with absolute risk reduction of 48 fewer events per 1000. Heterogeneity in the pooled result was attributable to CASTLE-HTx and AF-CHF. The benefit remained consistent in sensitivity analyses excluding these two trials, without loss of precision (HR 0.51, 95 % CI 0.42–0.63), suggesting robust evidence for rhythm control in reducing cardiovascular death in patients with AF and HF. Similarly, for HF hospitalizations, the benefit of rhythm control was significant and persisted after exclusion of influential studies (AF-CHF and CASTLE-AF). This suggests that the benefit of rhythm control in cardiovascular death and HF hospitalization, unlikely all-cause mortality, is not entirely driven by the CASTLE trials.

It is worth noting that the AF-CHF trial which contributed a substantial sample size (n = 1376) to the pooled results, did not offer catheter ablation at all, while both CASTLE trials, offered ablation upfront and showed the greatest benefit across outcomes. This suggests that benefit of rhythm control may largely depend upon the modality used, and the success rate of maintaining sinus rhythm. Indeed, sub-analysis of CASTLE-AF comparing rhythm control with only AAD to rate control found no benefit of AAD in reducing composite of all-cause mortality and HF hospitalization [27]. On the other hand, outcomes in EAST-AFNET did not differ between catheter ablation and anti-arrhythmic drugs [28]. The question of comparative efficacy of ablation over drugs in AF and HF has been studied in previous meta-analyses, which found mortality and quality-of-life benefit with ablation, compared with medical therapy [11,12]. A network meta-analysis, published even before the release of CASTLE-HTx results, concluded that catheter ablation of AF outranked AAD, rate control, and atrioventricular nodal ablation in HF-related quality-of-life measures, mortality and HF hospitalizations [29].

We found moderate certainty evidence of rhythm control in reducing the composite outcome. Sensitivity analysis did not significantly change the composite outcome effect estimate. Although no significant benefit was seen with rhythm control in decreasing thromboembolic events (HR 0.67, 95 % CI 0.32–1.39), this was judged to be low certainty evidence, limited by small sample size. This result is explained by the inclusion of the RACE trial, in which anticoagulation was prematurely discontinued upon conversion to sinus rhythm, and notably, all thromboembolic events occurred in the setting of subtherapeutic international normalized ratio (INR).

In our meta-analysis, the mean difference between rhythm and rate control in LVEF change from baseline was 6.01 %, similar to that estimated by a previous study comparing ablation with medical therapy [12]. There was serious heterogeneity (I2 = 90 %, p < 0.01) among studies, likely due to different modalities and different time-points for measuring LVEF, as well as variability in proportions of reduced or preserved EF patients included.

To our knowledge this is the first pooled subgroup analysis comparing rhythm versus rate control strategies in particular subsets of HF patients. Subgroup analyses can be valuable in identifying populations which may derive greater clinical benefit (or conversely, experience greater harm) from an intervention. Considering the etiology of HF, we found comparable reduction in the risk of the primary composite outcome between ischemic and non-ischemic HF. We did not detect any statistically significant difference in the treatment benefit of rhythm control in reduced LVEF (HR 0.63, 95 % CI 0.42–0.96) and in preserved LVEF (HR 0.82, 95 % CI 0.47–1.43). Only two trials, EAST-AFNET and RAFT-AF contributed to the analysis for this outcome, and even among this cohort, patients with HFpEF constituted a minority (36 %). Moreover, the cut-off used to categorize EF varied; with RAFT-AF classifying LVEF ≤45 % and >45 % as reduced and preserved respectively, and EAST-AFNET classifying LVEF <40 % and >50 % as reduced and preserved respectively. Thus, limited confidence can be placed in this subgroup comparison. There is a lack of randomized trial data on rhythm control of AF in HFpEF patients, as most contemporary trials have excluded patients with HFpEF. Previous meta-analyses have relied on observational studies to ascertain benefit of either strategy in this subgroup of patients, and report 15–25 % risk reduction in all-cause mortality with rhythm control [26,30]. One study evaluated the efficacy of catheter ablation of AF in HFpEF patients and found no difference between HFrEF and HFpEF patients in recurrence of atrial arrhythmias, cerebrovascular events, hospitalizations, and all-cause mortality [31]. Randomized controlled trials investigating catheter ablation of AF versus medical therapy in exclusively HFpEF patients are currently underway (CABA HF [NCT05508256] and AMPERE [NCT04282850]) and should address the gap in evidence for this population.

Subgroup analysis for age showed greater benefit in younger (<65 years) patients (HR 0.36; 95 % CI 0.10–1.31) than older (≥65 years) patients (HR 0.78; 95 % CI 0.49–1.22), however the difference was not statistically significant. A recent systematic review that defined elderly patients as >75 years found that their risk of AF recurrence following catheter ablation was similar to that in younger patients but rates of major complications were significantly higher in the elderly (RR 1.30, 95 % CI 1.10–1.54) [32].

Our study adds to current literature by comprehensively addressing the contentious question of rate versus rhythm control approach in patients with AF and concurrent HF. We only included RCTs to avoid bias arising from retrospective observational data. Additionally, we assessed the quality of the summary effect estimates by following the GRADE approach to adjudicating certainty of evidence. We calculated absolute risk differences between treatment arms, which helps to contextualize the results of the meta-analysis.

There were some limitations to the present study as well. First, there is considerable heterogeneity among interventions, populations, and trial methods across the included studies, introducing indirectness in the summary results. In order to address this, we conducted rigorous sensitivity analyses including leave-one-out approach to detect influential cases. When these were detected, we re-calculated the pooled effect to assess robustness of estimates. Second, there were several studies which did not report all the outcomes of interest and therefore were not included in analysis, limiting the precision of our results. Third, subgroup analyses must be regarded as hypothesis-generating only as their power to detect true differences is limited by low number of studies. Finally, approximating incidence rate ratios as hazard ratios may introduce error if the proportional hazards assumption is violated; unfortunately we could not test for this in the absence of original time-to-event data.

In conclusion, our meta-analysis pooling randomized data indicates that compared with rate control alone, rhythm control of AF in patients with HF is associated with significantly lower all-cause mortality, cardiovascular mortality, and HF hospitalization, and with significantly greater improvement in LVEF percentage. Evidence from CASTLE-AF and CASTLE-HTx trials unequivocally favors early rhythm control with catheter ablation as the optimal strategy in managing AF. These data will help guide the shared decision making in AF management, at least in HFrEF patients. There remains underrepresentation of HFpEF patients at present resulting in paucity of data to inform treatment options. Further efforts are needed to improve outcomes in this subset of patients.
