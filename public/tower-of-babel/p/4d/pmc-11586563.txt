Observed and expected reliability of echocardiographic volumetric methods and critical change values for quantification of mitral regurgitant fraction in dogs
Authors: Kerry A. Loughran, Marc S. Kraus, Erin J. Achilles, Terry Huh, Éva Larouche‐Lebel, Laura K. Massey, Anna R. Gelzer, Mark A. Oyama
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundReliability of echocardiographic calculations for stroke volume and mitral regurgitant fraction (RFMR) are affected by observer variability and lack of a gold standard. Variability is used to calculate critical change values (CCVs) that are thresholds representing real change in a measure not associated with observer variability.HypothesisObserved intra‐ and interobserver accuracy and variability in healthy dogs help model CCV for RFMR.AnimalsReliability cohort of 34 healthy dogs; allometric scaling cohort of 99 dogs with heart disease and 25 healthy dogs.MethodsAccuracy, variability, and CCV of 2 observers using geometric and flow‐based echocardiography were prospectively compared against a standard of RFMR = 0% and extrapolated across a range of expected RFMR values in the reliability cohort partly derived from cardiac dimensions predicted by the allometric cohort.ResultsAccuracy of methods to determine RFMR in descending order was 4‐chamber bullet (Bullet4CH), mitral inflow, cube formula, and Simpson's method of disks. Intraobserver variability was relatively high. The CCV for RFMR ranged from 28% to 88% and was inversely related to RFMR when extrapolated for use in affected dogs. For both observers, the Bullet4CH method had the lowest intraobserver CCV (Operator 1:28%, Operator 2:41%). Interobserver strength of agreement was low with intraclass correlation coefficients ranging from 0.210 to 0.413.Conclusions and Clinical ImportanceEchocardiographic volumetric methods used to calculate stroke volume and RFMR have low accuracy and high variability in healthy dogs. Extrapolation of observed CCV to a range of expected RFMR suggests observers and methods are not interchangeable and variability might hinder routine clinical usage. Individual observers should be aware of their own variability and CCV.

INTRODUCTION

Echocardiography is routinely performed to assess cardiac structure and function. A variety of different single and multidimensional geometric and flow‐based measurements can be used to calculate total stroke volume (TSV), forward stroke volume (FSV), and mitral regurgitant fraction (RFMR), which is the difference between TSV and FSV expressed as a percentage of TSV in dogs with mitral regurgitation (MR). In humans with MR, RFMR is used to stage disease, assess progression, and predict morbidity and mortality. 1 , 2 In dogs, RFMR is infrequently measured. Instead, MR severity is indirectly assessed using linear dimensions of the left ventricle (LV) and left atrium (LA) and subjective assessment of the color flow Doppler jet of MR, which primarily are used because of their ease of measurement. 3 In dogs with MR, echocardiographic studies measuring cardiac volumes typically suffer from a lack of a gold standard, such as angiography, cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (CMR), or 3‐dimensional (3D) echocardiography. 2 , 4 , 5

Previous studies of intra‐ and interoperator variability in humans and dogs have calculated critical change values (CCV), which also are referred to as reliability coefficients and are the minimal detectable thresholds above which repeated testing reliably represents a real change in the measure not associated with observer variability. 6 , 7 , 8 High accuracy and low variability produce low CCV and observers can reliability stage as well as detect small changes in disease progression whereas low accuracy and high variability produce high CCV that hinder staging and reliable detection of longitudinal changes in disease. Thus, CCV are relevant to the prognosis and treatment of individual subjects as well as being an important consideration in epidemiologic and efficacy studies that utilize echocardiographic endpoints.

Echocardiographic calculation of RFMR is very dependent on operator reliability. Previous investigators stated that “centers that are successful [with these methods should] first use the technique[s] to ensure that calculations of mitral inflow [which represent TSV] and aortic outflow stroke volumes [which represent FSV] match in patients without mitral or aortic disease…before applying it to patients with MR.” 9 In other words, observers should self‐assess their reliability and accuracy in healthy subjects so as to determine their ability to reliably detect changes in affected subjects. Accordingly, we hypothesized that the assessment of accuracy and reliability of operators to measure TSV and FSV in healthy dogs would produce CCV for RFMR that could be extrapolated to expected examination of affected dogs and inform operators which methods were associated with the highest accuracy, lowest variability, and highest reliability. Our aim was to compare the accuracy and intra‐ and interoperator variability of 4 different geometric and flow‐based echocardiographic techniques in healthy dogs and to calculate CCV for RFMR across a range of expected RFMR values. These results can help clinicians better understand the expected effect of operator reliability associated with the different methods in affected dogs. We hypothesized that we would be able to find methods with good balance among accuracy, reliability, ease of acquisition, and prediction of clinically useful CCV for RFMR suitable for use in dogs with MR.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

A prospective study was designed. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the University of Pennsylvania and owner informed consent was obtained. Two populations of dogs were studied. The first included apparently healthy dogs owned by staff, faculty, and clients of the Matthew J. Ryan Veterinary Hospital of the University of Pennsylvania. This cohort was termed the reliability cohort and used to determine the accuracy, intraobserver repeatability, and interobserver reproducibility of different echocardiographic measurements and calculations. Exclusion criteria included the presence of a heart murmur, clinical signs related to cardiovascular disease, clinically important arrhythmias such as atrial fibrillation or frequent ectopic beats, and congenital or acquired heart disease on echocardiographic examination, including valvular insufficiency on color flow Doppler beyond what was regarded as trace.

Two‐dimensional (2D) and Doppler echocardiographic examinations (EPIQCvx, Philips Healthcare, Andover, Massachusetts, USA) were performed on dogs that were manually restrained and laterally recumbent on an echocardiographic examination table, which allowed the observer to obtain images from the dog's dependent side. Dogs in the reliability cohort were examined by 2 board‐certified cardiologists (MAO and MSK) who each performed 3 separate examinations on the same day for every dog in the study for a total of 6 examinations for each dog. Measurements were done by the cardiologists using commercial software (IntelliSpace Cardiovascular 5.1 Philips Healthcare) performed in triplicate, and the average value was used for subsequent analysis. Measurements and calculations were performed using previously described methods 10 , 11 and included the following from the right parasternal short axis 2D view: thickness of the interventricular septum at end‐diastole, thickness of the left ventricular free wall at end‐diastole, internal diameter of the LV at end‐diastole (LVIDD) and end‐systole (LVIDS), diameters of the LA and aorta, the left atrium‐to‐aorta diameter ratio, and area of the LV at end‐diastole (LVADR) and end‐systole (LVASR); the following from the right parasternal long axis view: the length of the LV at end‐diastole (LVLDR) and end‐systole (LVLSR) and diameter of the aortic sinotubular junction (DSTJ‐R); the following from the left cranial view: diameter of the aortic sinotubular junction (DSTJ‐L); the following from the left apical 4‐ and 5‐chamber views: LV area at end‐diastole (LVADL) and end‐systole (LVASL), velocity time integral of mitral inflow (VTIMI), diameter of the mitral valve annulus (DMVA) at the tips of the open leaflets of the mitral valve, velocity time integral of aortic outflow (VTIAo) at the aortic sinotubular junction, and length of the LV chamber at end‐diastole (LVLDL) and end‐systole (LVLSL), which was measured from the plane of the mitral valve annulus to the LV chamber apex. The LV TSV was calculated using the cube, 4‐chamber bullet method (Bullet4CH), Simpson's method of disks (SMOD), and mitral valve inflow (MI) methods as previously described. 10 Briefly, the cube method calculated LV volume at end‐diastole and end‐systole as LVIDD 3 and LVIDS, 3 respectively. The SMOD method used automated assessment of LV volume by tracing the LV endocardial border (excluding papillary muscles) and automatically summing the volumes of 20 disks as π4∑n=120D2L20 with D = LV diameter and L = LV length. The Bullet4CH method multiplied LVADR or LVASR by LVLDR or LVLSR using the formula (5/6) × AL where A = LV area and L = LV length. The MI method multiplied VTIMI with the circular area of the mitral valve annulus calculated as 𝜋 × (DMVA/2). 2 The LV TSV was calculated as the difference between LV volume at end‐diastole and end‐systole or the volume of MI. The LV FSV was calculated as the product of VTIAo and the circular area of the STJ, which was calculated as 𝜋 × (DSTJ/2). 2 The RFMR was calculated as ([TSV‐FSV]/TSV) × 100%. The heart rate (HR) during VTIAo and VTIMI measurement was calculated using electronic calipers to measure the duration between consecutive R wave‐to‐R wave intervals on the timing ECG or between 2 consecutive Doppler aortic outflow or mitral valve inflow signals and dividing this number by 60 to obtain beats per minute.

To decrease variability associated with the measurement of FSV, we recruited a second cohort termed the allometric cohort to calculate an allometric formula that predicted DSTJ based on body weight (DSTJ‐predicted) that could be used as an alternative to observed DSTJ‐R and DSTJ‐L. The recruitment target for this cohort was 125 dogs, which is regarded as a minimum number necessary to develop allometric formulae. 8 , 12 Dogs with and without heart disease were recruited from clients, staff, and students. Exclusion criteria included previously diagnosed systemic hypertension or morphologic abnormalities of the aortic root or aorta, such as in dogs with subaortic stenosis or patent ductus arteriosus. Dogs in the allometric cohort were examined by a board‐certified cardiologist or resident in training and DSTJ‐R and DSTJ‐L were measured as previously described. 11 Data were used to generate allometric formulae for DSTJ‐predicted based on body weight. 12 , 13 Briefly, the proportionality constant (a) and scaling exponent (b) of the allometric equation Y = aM b where Y is DSTJ‐predicted and M is body weight were calculated and confidence intervals (CIs) generated, creating a regression of best fit for the data. 8 , 14 The 95% CI of the scaling exponents specific to healthy dogs and dogs with heart disease in the allometric cohort were compared. The Bland‐Altman method was used to compare observed and expected values derived from observed DSTJ‐L vs DSTJ‐R.

Statistical analysis

Epidemiological terms related to observer variability are used in the study. 13 , 15 Repeatability refers to an observer's ability to produce the same result as the first when he or she performs a second measurement on the same sample (ie, intraobserver variability). Reproducibility is the ability of a different observer to obtain the same measurement as the first observer (ie, interobserver variability). Both sources of variability influence reliability, which is a measure of precision and consistency reflecting the stability of measured values and ability to achieve common consensus between observations. 13 Mathematically, reliability is the ratio of true variance over the sum of true variance and error variance. 16

Descriptive statistics of dog signalment and diagnostic results were generated. Variables were assessed for normality using Shapiro‐Wilk tests and reported as either mean and SD or median and interquartile range (IQR). Comparisons between or within groups were analyzed with t‐tests or analysis of variance as appropriate. The average RFMR for each method and observer was reported. Intraobserver and interobserver variabilities were calculated as the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) using a 2‐way mixed or random effects model based on single measurement and absolute agreement. 16 , 17 Interobserver ICC was calculated by pairing results of each of the 3 studies performed by each observer for a total of 102 pairs of observations. For ICC, the strength of agreement was rated as excellent (0.75‐1.00), good (0.60‐0.74), moderate (0.40‐0.59), or poor (<0.40). 15

The CCV associated with intraobserver variability of each observer and each echocardiographic method were calculated using previously described methods. 8 , 13 , 18 Briefly, CCV was calculated as Ζα×2×SEM where Ζα=1.96 and represents the standard normal deviate for probability of α=0.05 and SEM is the SE of the mean intraobserver measurements. 13

We used observed measures of operator variability from the reliability cohort to predict CCV across an expected range of RFMR values under a condition that the observed variability of TSV and FSV is a percentage of the measured value (eg, variability of TSV is 10% of the TSV value). The relationship between CCV and expected RFMR ranging from 0% to 90% was modeled by inputting the variability into calculations of TSV and FSV and examining the effect on RFMR. An observer's reliability measured in healthy dogs predicts CCV as having an inverse relationship with RFMR in affected dogs. In a dog with RFMR = 0%, FSV = TSV such that the ratio (FSV:TSV) equals 1. As MR develops, TSV increases relative to FSV and FSV:TSV <1. In the setting of severe MR and RFMR >>0% such that FSV:TSV <<1, variability in TSV yields a smaller CCV compared with when FSV:TSV = 1. For example, a 10% difference in TSV measurement in a dog with RFMR = 0% and FSV:TSV = 1 causes a variability in RFMR of 9% (RFMR = [1.1‐1.0]/1.1 = 9%) whereas in a dog with RFMR = 64% and FSV:TSV = 0.36, a 10% difference in TSV measurement causes only 3% of variability in RFMR (RFMR = [1.1‐0.36]/1.1 = 67%). As disease worsens and RFMR increases, we predicted CCV would decrease, making detection of small change more reliable in dogs with high RFMR vs those with low RFMR. For simplicity, we chose to use a model in which variability was a proportion of TSV or FSV, but the predicted inverse relationship remains true if variability is a fixed amount of TSV and FSV (see Supporting Information S1 and S2).

RESULTS

Thirty‐four dogs were recruited into the reliability cohort. The cohort's median age was 4 years (IQR, 3‐5 years) and mean body weight was 20.8 ± 8.1 kg. Eighteen dogs (18/34, 53%) were females and 16 (16/34, 47%) were males. Initial experience in the reliability cohort suggested high variability associated with FSV and prompted us to decrease variability by calculating allometric formulae for DSTJ‐predicted based on observed DSTJ‐R and DSTJ‐L. One‐hundred twenty‐four dogs were recruited into the allometric cohort. The allometric cohort's median age was 9.5 years (IQR, 7‐11 years) and median body weight was 9.3 kg (IQR, 6.4‐19 kg). Fifty‐three dogs (53/124, 43%) were female and 71 (71/124, 57%) were male. The allometric cohort included 25 healthy dogs (25/124, 20%), 88 dogs (88/124, 71%) with myxomatous mitral valve disease (MMVD), and 11 dogs (11/124, 9%) with other forms of heart disease, including 5 with arrhythmogenic or idiopathic cardiomyopathy, 2 dogs with pulmonary hypertension, and 1 of each of the following: heart base mass, pulmonary valve stenosis, tricuspid dysplasia, and mild idiopathic pleural effusion. DISCUSSION

Our main finding was the low accuracy and high intra‐ and interobserver variability associated with volumetric echocardiographic measurements. A unique feature of our study was the assessment of measurement accuracy in healthy dogs followed by prediction in dogs with MR, which differs from many previous reliability studies that either avoided assessment of accuracy altogether or used echocardiographic measurements performed by experienced observers as the gold standard. 7 , 11 , 19 , 20 Accurate measurement of RFMR in dogs is important in many ways, including comparison of prognostic and therapeutic thresholds in human patients and dogs, establishment of standard treatment guidelines based on disease severity, interpretation of studies from different investigators and subject samples, and better understanding of the pathophysiology of MMVD at different stages of disease severity. In veterinary studies, a widely accepted and readily accessible gold standard for RFMR does not exist, which complicates study design and interpretation, and in our study, prompted use of healthy subjects without MR as a gold standard. In human patients, a well‐recognized gold standard for evaluation of cardiac volumes is CMR, for which limited studies are available in dogs, 21 and CMR is not widely available or practical in routine clinical practice. In human patients with mild or moderate MR, 2D or 3D transthoracic echocardiographic methods can achieve moderate to good agreement with CMR‐derived results, but the strength of agreement can be considerably lower in patients with severe MR. 5 , 22 , 23

Our results indicated operator accuracy was low and could vary within and between experienced cardiologists in clinically meaningful ways. In our study, low accuracy caused by disparities between FSV and TSV was particularly noted using the SMOD method. Limitations of the SMOD method are well known but not easily avoided and include foreshortening of the LV in the left apical view, which results in underestimation of LV length and area, and the parallel orientation of the LV endocardium to the ultrasound beam resulting in poor visualization when tracing the LV area. 24 In our study, the SMOD and cube methods generally resulted in TSV << FSV and RFMR << 0%. The Bullet4CH and MI methods generally result in FSV ≅ TSV and RFMR values approximating 0%, but the precision of measurements is low with wide ranges. The MI method relies on fewer geometric assumptions than the other methods by using Doppler VTIMI to calculate TSV. One potential disadvantage of the MI method is the additional effort needed to obtain left apical views and Doppler traces of mitral E and A waves and the potential effect of heart rate on VTI. For both observers, accuracy was highest using the Bullet4CH method. Potential advantages of the Bullet4CH method over other area‐length methods, including SMOD, is the measurement of the LV cross‐sectional area and LV length from more familiar and routinely obtained right‐sided views such that foreshortening and other pitfalls may be easier to avoid.

Intraobserver and interobserver variability was high for all methods and likely for several reasons. First, small changes in linear measurements, such as LVIDD or DSTJ‐L, are compounded by the need to square or cube values to obtain measures of area or volume, respectively. We attempted to eliminate 1 particular source of variability by using DSTJ‐predicted, which remained unchanged for each subject throughout the study. The use of predicted values in place of observed values can decrease variability associated with longitudinal or repeated measurements but might do so at the expense of accuracy. Second, differences in heart rate and rhythm can affect Doppler‐based measurements such as VTI and can produce variability both within subjects and between observers if measurements are obtained during different rates and rhythms as occurred in our study. In veterinary species, variability in heart rate during examination can be particularly high, whether because of sinus arrhythmia at rest or tachycardia when stressed. In our study, the subject heart rate varied significantly between observers and might have affected results. Third, we measured interobserver variability differently from previous studies. 7 , 8 In our study, each observer measured using images they obtained rather than having 1 set of images measured twice by 2 different observers. Our method is termed test‐retest, 25 which is the variability between 2 separate examinations both performed and measured by 2 separate observers. This method typically exhibits more variability than 2 observers measuring the same images because it represents both the variability of image acquisition and measurement. We chose the test‐retest method because it more faithfully mimics clinical practice wherein different observers might obtain and measure their own sets of images during separate hospital visits.

In our study, reliability estimates produced relatively low ICC and relatively high CCV values for RFMR as compared with previous reports. 8 , 10 , 20 Critical change values are an important clinical metric because they represent the amount of change that an observer must measure to confidently conclude that the measured change is real and not because of observer variability. Thus, low CCV are desirable. In our study, the lowest intraobserver CCV were associated with the Bullet4CH method, and when coupled with the relative accuracy of the method, suggests the Bullet4CH method as a reasonable first candidate for observers to consider for clinical use. Comparison of our CCV results with previous studies is difficult because of differences in study design and study population. In 1 study of 57 stage B1 and B2 dogs with mean RFMR of 27% and 66%, CCV using the SMOD and cube method, were 19.6% and 24.1%, respectively. 20 In our study, we advanced the understanding and clinical utility of CCV by demonstrating the concept that CCV based on an observer's baseline variability will change over a range of expected RFMR. Our results suggest observers more confidently detect change in RFMR in dogs with more severe MR as compared with those with mild MR, that is, for any given proportion of observer variability, CCV values are lower in dogs with severe MR and higher in dogs with mild MR. The CCV associated with all 4 methods was particularly high in dogs with RFMR <50%, meaning that large changes in longitudinal RFMR are necessary before a clinician can confidently state that a real change has occurred. If true, this hinders the clinical utility of RFMR quantification in dogs with mild subclinical disease, which can affect important treatment decisions, monitoring, and prognostication in this population of interest.

Another important result of our study is that experienced cardiologists obtained different CCV based on their individual amounts of variability, meaning that CCV might substantially differ from observer to observer. In our study, both cardiologist observers had >20 years of clinical practice experience, including having performed many thousands of echocardiographic examinations, and did not expect the amount of variability that was observed. Along with results from other studies involving operators with variable amounts of experience, 19 , 20 , 26 individuals should measure and understand the limits of their individual CCV rather than assuming they are the same as other reported observers. This practice has important consequences on how echocardiographic results are interpreted by a single operator as well as by others. For example, a measured change in RFMR of 25% might reliably indicate disease progression if obtained by 1 observer but not by another and be further affected by the method each observer uses. Our results expand existing knowledge by emphasizing the potentially inconsistent relationships among observer, method, and subject on CCV. This observation has implications for dog care as well as the generalizability of echocardiographic studies if performed by individuals with different CCV. We also found that the order in which methods were ranked as most to least reliable on the basis of CCV was different between the 2 observers. Thus, different observers might rely on different methods to consistently obtain the most reliable results and the reliability of 1 method and 1 observer is not necessarily interchangeable with another.

The amount of variability we reported is high but likely is a worst‐case scenario. As previously recommended, results in healthy subjects should prompt observers to specifically identify and decrease both intraobserver and interobserver variability through self‐testing, assessment, and collaborative review with others in the same practice before applying methods to affected subjects. 9 In this way, observers can identify and correct practices that lead to inaccuracies and high variability and then iteratively test revised methods until desired results are achieved. Doing so is particularly important if observers wish to make calculations of RFMR both with high accuracy and reproducibility. Our study did not address or perform any of these corrective steps nor determine whether or how such efforts might have yielded a change in results. In our study, we sought to mimic routine practice conditions to obtain a baseline assessment of variability. The 2 observers performed echocardiograms without extensive collaboration, meaning that aside from instructions to adhere to published guidelines, observers did not train or attempt to closely harmonize their individual practices. As such, our results likely reflect the real‐world effectiveness rather than efficacy of the described methods. Our study design using healthy dogs was highly sensitive to variability in FSV and TSV, which could quickly result in deviations of subsequent calculations of RFMR away from the expected result of RFMR = 0%. Estimates of variability, such as ICC, are sensitive to data range. 13 Calculating ICC for RFMR in healthy dogs likely results in a relatively lower ICC because such dogs have a narrower range of RFMR as compared with what would be expected in a cohort of affected dogs.

Our study had some important limitations. We studied only a few of the many different methods and techniques used to calculate cardiac volumes. Accuracy of methods in healthy hearts might be different than accuracy in diseased hearts especially because geometric assumptions of the ventricle might change as disease increases. For example, the LV is known to assume a more spherical shape as it dilates, 27 which affects the assumption of the prolate ellipse shape used in the cube method, for example. The use of a standard of RFMR = 0% prevented the calculation of traditional accuracy metrics such as the coefficient of variation that uses the expected value as a divisor. In human medicine, the American Society of Echocardiography recommends a volumetric approach to assess MR severity in people, 2 particularly because regurgitant volume and RFMR are important metrics in the decision to pursue treatment options and form recommendations. 28 , 29 The prognostic importance and clinical utility of monitoring RFMR has not been well established in veterinary medicine and further study to demonstrate whether a similar approach would be useful in dogs is required. Despite these limitations, our findings stress the need to understand and standardize echocardiographic practices and methodologies that can be adopted, learned, and dependably practiced by different observers in clinical settings. Reliability studies are important because they address the veracity of clinical measurements upon which important decisions are made.

In conclusion, echocardiographic quantification of TSV, FSV, and RFMR is challenging and characterized by low accuracy, high variability, and differences in the reliability of various methods that are operator‐dependent. Observers might benefit from assessing their own accuracy and reliability in healthy dogs and calculating personalized CCV for clinical use to confidently quantify and detect changes in RFMR in affected dogs. Studies in different dog and observer samples can help better establish and understand the utility of RFMR in diagnosis and monitoring.
