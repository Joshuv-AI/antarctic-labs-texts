Simplifying Vascular Closure in Transfemoral TAVR
Authors: Jinho Lee, Osung Kwon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Corresponding Author

Transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) has evolved remarkably over the past decade, expanding from a therapy for inoperable or high-risk patients to a standard treatment across the entire surgical risk spectrum.1 As procedural success has become routine, the focus has shifted toward enhancing long-term valve durability, minimizing complications, and improving procedural efficiency.2,3 With advances in valve technology and imaging, catastrophic aortic complex complications, such as annular rupture or coronary obstruction, have become increasingly rare. Consequently, attention has shifted to complications once considered relatively benign, among which vascular access complications remain a leading non–aortic complex concern in transfemoral TAVR.4 Despite ongoing technological refinement, large-bore arterial access continues to pose an inherent risk, with vascular complications still associated with bleeding, prolonged hospitalization, additional intervention, and worse clinical outcomes.5 Accordingly, optimizing vascular access and closure strategies has become as a central component in contemporary TAVR practice.

Currently, the suture-mediated Perclose ProGlide system (Abbott Cardiovascular) is the most widely adopted approach for large-bore femoral closure, with the double-device preclose strategy serving as the long-standing default for transfemoral TAVR.6,7 The device achieves hemostasis by advancing a pretied monofilament suture loop to the adventitial surface of the arteriotomy. Because a single suture subtends only a limited arc of the puncture circumference, the double-device preclose strategy became the standard approach during the early era of transfemoral TAVR, when delivery sheaths ranged from 18- to 22-F.7

The rationale for revisiting this standard in contemporary TAVR rests on 3 converging arguments. First, modern delivery systems now use sheaths in the 14- to 16-F range, substantially smaller than the arteriotomies for which the double-device strategy was originally designed, such that a single suture loop may provide geometrically sufficient coverage in favorable anatomy. Second, the procedural environment surrounding vascular access has itself improved: preprocedural computed tomographic planning now enables precise identification of optimal femoral access sites, and ultrasound-guided puncture reduces the risk for posterior wall injury and off-target entry. Third, the adjunctive protamine administration for heparin reversal facilitates postprocedural hemostasis.8 Furthermore, rotational deployment of 2 sutures may introduce loop interference within the tissue tract, potentially contributing to device failure and femoral artery stenosis.9,10 Accordingly, a single-device strategy offers several practical advantages, including reduced procedural complexity, shorter closure time, and lower device cost. Despite its intuitive appeal, robust evidence supporting routine single-device closure remains limited. Most previous comparisons between single- and double-closure strategies have been observational and derived from relatively small cohorts or single-center experiences.10,11

In this context, the Single-Closure Trial, reported in this issue of JACC: Asia, addresses this important evidence gap.12 This investigator-initiated, multicenter, randomized, noninferiority trial is designed to enroll 876 patients undergoing transfemoral TAVR across 25 centers in China. Participants are randomized to either a single-ProGlide closure strategy or the conventional double-ProGlide strategy. The primary endpoint is a composite of access-site or access-related major and minor vascular complications during the index hospitalization, defined according to the Valve Academic Research Consortium 3 criteria.4 Secondary endpoints include vascular complications at 30 days, bleeding events, unplanned vascular interventions, device failure, mortality, and procedural metrics such as time to hemostasis and length of hospital stay. By adopting a randomized design across multiple centers with standardized outcome definitions, the Single-Closure Trial represents an important step toward generating higher-quality evidence in this area.

Several limitations merit consideration. As the investigators partly acknowledge, certain procedural elements that may influence vascular outcomes are left to operator discretion. For example, ultrasound-guided puncture, which is known to reduce vascular complications, is not mandated within the protocol. Similarly, the administration of protamine for heparin reversal is not standardized. Because protamine can significantly influence bleeding and hemostasis, variability in its use may affect vascular outcomes.8 Nevertheless, such flexibility may also reflect real-world clinical practice and enhance the pragmatic relevance of the study. Another limitation is the absence of a formal cost-effectiveness analysis. This point is particularly relevant because one of the primary motivations for adopting a single-device closure strategy is cost reduction. Although demonstration of noninferior clinical outcomes would indirectly support procedural simplification, dedicated economic analyses would provide valuable additional insights.

If the Single-Closure Trial confirms the noninferiority of a single-ProGlide strategy, the implications for TAVR practice could be meaningful. Procedural simplification has become an increasingly important goal in structural heart interventions. As TAVR expands to younger and lower risk populations, attention is shifting toward improving efficiency, minimizing complications, and optimizing resource use. At the same time, vascular closure strategies will likely remain individualized. Patients with severe peripheral artery disease, heavy femoral calcification, or unfavorable vascular anatomy may still require a more robust closure approach. Thus, rather than replacing current strategies entirely, the findings of the Single-Closure Trial may help refine patient selection and procedural decision-making.

Of note, the trial has already enrolled more than two-thirds of its planned population since its initiation in 2024, suggesting that results may become available in the near future. The structural heart community therefore awaits the outcomes of this study with considerable interest. As TAVR continues to mature, procedural refinement will increasingly define the next phase of innovation. Optimizing vascular closure represents an important component of this effort. By rigorously comparing single- and double-ProGlide strategies in a randomized framework, the Single-Closure Trial may help determine whether, in contemporary TAVR practice, less can indeed be more.
