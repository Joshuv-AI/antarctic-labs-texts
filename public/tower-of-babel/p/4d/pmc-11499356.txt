Comparative Cost-Effectiveness of Atezolizumab Versus Durvalumab as First-Line Combination Treatment with Chemotherapy for Patients with Extensive-Disease Small-Cell Lung Cancer in Japan
Authors: Munenobu Kashiwa, Miho Tsukada, Ryo Matsushita
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and ObjectiveRecent trials have shown that immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), atezolizumab and durvalumab, in combination with chemotherapy, are effective in treating extensive-disease small-cell lung cancer (ED-SCLC). However, owing to the expensiveness of ICIs, monetary issues arise. The cost-effectiveness of ICI combination treatment with carboplatin plus etoposide (CE) as first-line therapy for patients with ED-SCLC was examined to aid public health policy in Japan.MethodsIMpower 133 and CASPIAN data were used to create a partitioned survival model. Medical expenses and quality-adjusted life years (QALYs) were considered. The analysis period, discount rate, and threshold were set at 20 years, 2%, and 15 million Japanese yen (JPY) [114,068 US dollars (USD)] per QALY, respectively. The incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER) was calculated by gathering reasonable parameters from published reports and combining the costs and effects using parametric models. Monte Carlo simulations, scenario analysis, and one-way sensitivity analyses were employed to quantify uncertainty.ResultsAfter comparing atezolizumab plus CE (ACE) and durvalumab plus CE (DCE) with CE, it was found that the ICERs exceeded the threshold at 35,048,299 JPY (266,527 USD) and 36,665,583 JPY (278,826 USD) per QALY, respectively. For one-way sensitivity and scenario assessments, the ICERs exceeded the threshold, even with considerably adjusted parameters. For the probabilistic sensitivity analyses, there was no probability that the ICER of the ICI combination treatment with chemotherapy would fall below the threshold.ConclusionACE and DCE were not cost-effective compared with CE as first-line therapy for ED-SCLC in Japan. Both these therapies exhibited high ICERs.

Key Points

Adding immune checkpoint inhibitors (atezolizumab or durvalumab) to chemotherapy for lung cancer improves survival; however, immune checkpoint inhibitors are very expensive in Japan.Despite improved outcomes, the combination treatments with chemotherapy are not considered cost-effective based on standard public health thresholds in Japan.

Introduction

Lung cancer is the world’s deadliest cancer, with 1.8 million deaths in 2020, accounting for 18.0% (14.3% and 13.7% for men and women, respectively) of all cancer deaths [1]. This is the highest cancer mortality rate for men and the second highest for women [1]. In 2020, the total number of fatalities caused by lung cancer in Japan was estimated to exceed 75,000, amounting to 19.9% of all cancer-related deaths [2]. Lung cancer is the primary cause of death among men and the second most common cause of death among women, after colorectal cancer [2]. Small-cell lung cancer (SCLC) is a specific kind of lung cancer, representing approximately 10–15% of all lung cancer cases [3]. It exhibits early and extensive metastasis, resulting in an exceedingly unfavorable prognosis [4]. Limited-disease SCLC (LD-SCLC) is defined by several clinical trials as “lesions confined to the same side of the chest as the tumor [5]. It involves the lymph nodes on the opposite side of the chest and above the collarbone, without cancerous fluid in the heart or the sac surrounding the heart.” By contrast, extensive-disease SCLC (ED-SCLC) refers to cases that do not meet these criteria [5]. The incidence rates for LD- and ED-SCLC range from 30 to 40% and 60–70%, respectively [6].

For performance status (PS) 0–3 in ED-SCLC, the worldwide standard of care has been platinum + etoposide (PE) therapy since the 1980s. Although SCLC is a rapidly growing, aggressive tumor with early lymph nodes and distant metastases, it is characterized by high sensitivity to radiation and drug therapies. The overall and complete response rates of PE therapy for patients with ED-SCLC range from 50 to 80% and 0–30%, respectively [4, 7, 8]. However, even after the initial treatment, 80–90% of patients relapse or progress, with a median overall survival of only approximately 10 months [7]. If the initial treatment is successful and the recurrence occurs more than 60–90 days after the initial treatment, the recurrent SCLC is considered a sensitive relapse. In this case, drug therapy is more effective and the survival time is longer. If not, they are classified as refractory relapses [9].

Recently, the efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) has been demonstrated in ED-SCLC, and the combination of platinum and etoposide with atezolizumab or durvalumab is a new treatment option [10, 11]. Atezolizumab and durvalumab are humanized anti-programmed ligand 1 (PD-L1) monoclonal antibodies that inhibit the binding between PD-L1 and its receptor, programmed cell death 1 (PD-1). They enhance the cytotoxic activity of cancer antigen-specific T cells and inhibit tumor growth. In addition, they have fewer side effects compared with conventional cytotoxic anticancer drugs [12].

In 2018, Horn et al. conducted a global phase III study (IMpower 133), comparing atezolizumab plus carboplatin plus etoposide (ACE) in four cycles followed by maintaining atezolizumab alone, with carboplatin plus etoposide (CE) plus placebo in patients with ED-SCLC and PS 0–1 [10]. The combination group had a significantly longer overall survival (OS), the primary endpoint, and progression-free survival (PFS), the secondary endpoint, compared with the placebo group (OS 12.3 versus 10.3 months, p = 0.007; PFS 5.2 versus 4.3 months, p = 0.02). In 2019, Paz-Ares et al. conducted a global phase III study (CASPIAN study) to compare the clinical efficacies of four cycles of platinum combination therapy with durvalumab (DCE) followed by single-agent durvalumab maintenance, with platinum combination chemotherapy (chemotherapy) in patients with ED-SCLC with PS 0. In the interim OS analysis, the primary endpoint of the OS was significantly longer in the DCE group than in the chemotherapy group (13.0 versus 10.3 months, p = 0.0047) [11]. Based on these findings, platinum combination chemotherapy with ICIs, such as atezolizumab or durvalumab, is recommended for ED-SCLC (PS 0–1) as a new standard therapy [5, 13–15].

However, the high cost of atezolizumab and durvalumab in Japan raises concerns regarding its economic impact. To the best of our knowledge, only studies from the USA and China have examined the cost-effectiveness of ICI combination treatment with chemotherapy for patients with ED-SCLC, and the Japanese healthcare system has not undergone any analytical evaluation [16–23]. A cost-effectiveness assessment of atezolizumab and durvalumab in the Japanese universal health insurance system would be valuable to worldwide research. Thus, based on the results of the IMpower 133 and CASPIAN trials, this study compared the cost-effectiveness of atezolizumab versus durvalumab as combination treatment with chemotherapy as first-line treatment for patients with ED-SCLC from the perspective of Japanese medical insurance payers. Furthermore, this study compared the cost-effectiveness of ACE and DCE therapies with that of CE therapy to support medical policy decision-making.

Methods

Model Structure

A partitioned survival model was developed using data from the IMpower 133 and CASPIAN trials to analyze the cost-effectiveness of ACE, DCE, and CE treatments for patients with ED-SCLC [10, 11]. Only direct medical expenditures were examined from the perspective of Japanese medical insurance payers. The model cycle length was 4 weeks. Until cycle 4, durvalumab with chemotherapy was administered every 3 weeks in IMpower 133. Subsequently, only durvalumab was administered every 4 weeks. In the CASPIAN trial, atezolizumab with chemotherapy was administered every 4 weeks. The model included the three following health states: death, PFS, and progressive disease (PD) (Fig. 1). Based on survival analysis, the model began with the PFS status and progressed through each cycle to the PD state and, finally, death status. Patients who transitioned into the PD status were given the best supportive care and further therapy. Indirect comparisons may have been made with the control chemotherapy in both trials, but individual comparisons were made with an emphasis on accurate assessment of survival curves. Survival curves were extrapolated over a 20-year analysis period. The 5-year relative survival rates for Japanese patients with stages III and IV SCLC were 17.9% and 1.9%, respectively [24]. The 5-year relative survival computed by the model was compared with the 5-year relative survival of Japanese patients with SCLC to validate the present model. Following the Third Edition of the Central Social Insurance Medical Council’s standards for the examination of cost-effectiveness assessments, estimates of the costs and QALY were discounted at an annual rate of 2% [25]. The incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER) of ACE or DCE compared with CE was computed by dividing the incremental cost by the incremental benefit. The highest threshold used in the Japanese cost-effectiveness evaluation approach, 15 million Japanese yen (JPY) (114,068 US dollars (USD)), was selected as the willingness-to-pay (WTP) threshold. In this analysis, the health economics analysis plan was not prepared in advance. Model design and analysis were conducted using R, a programming language for statistical computing and graphics developed by the R (version 4.3.1) [26].

Model structure

Target Population

The IMpower 133 and CASPIAN studies enrolled patients with histologically or cytologically verified ED-SCLC. The inclusion criteria for the patients were as follows: 18 years or older, no prior chemotherapy treatment, Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group PS score of 0 or 1, and proper functioning of major organs. The study excluded patients with current or past autoimmune or inflammatory diseases, uncontrolled concurrent disease, prior ICI therapy, known allergies or hypersensitivities to ICIs, etoposide, carboplatin, cisplatin, or any excipient. Many of the patient characteristics, including the mean age of patients, the proportion of patients aged ≥ 65 years, sex, the distribution of performance status, smoking status, and the proportion of patients with a history of brain metastases, were similar in the IMpower 133 and CASPIAN studies. Hence, they were regarded as comparable in this analysis.

Intervention

In IMpower133, the control group only received CE. However, in CASPIAN, both CE and cisplatin with etoposide were used, with carboplatin being administered in 70% of patients. Cisplatin administration necessitates a lengthy hydration infusion and, in certain cases, hospitalization. In this research, only CE treatment, which does not require hydration, was employed as a comparison under the premise that outpatient chemotherapy was being studied. Patients in the CE group received four cycles of intravenous carboplatin [area under the curve (AUC), 5 mg/mL/min] and etoposide (100 mg/m2). Patients in the ACE group received four cycles of intravenous atezolizumab (1200 mg) on day 1 of a 21-day cycle with CE, followed by atezolizumab (1200 mg) maintenance therapy every 3 weeks. Patients in the DCE group received four cycles of intravenous durvalumab (1500 mg) on day 1 of a 21-day cycle with CE, followed by durvalumab (1500 mg) maintenance therapy every 4 weeks. In clinical trials, all the patients were treated for disease progression, unacceptable side effects, or toxicities. Furthermore, the study was stopped if patient consent was withdrawn.

Cost Estimates

The study estimated direct medical costs, such as medication costs, supportive care costs, treatment-related significant-adverse-event management, and end-of-life care costs, from the perspective of Japanese medical insurance payers (Table 1). The medical care costs were calculated by matching the cost of cancer treatment in the Japanese medical insurance system to the administration schedule of each regimen. The examination fee was determined based on the CASPIAN trial examination schedule. Urine testing, hepatitis virus testing, electrocardiography, coagulation brain testing, and PD-L1 protein testing were estimated at the start of treatment, while hematologic testing was performed monthly and computed tomography was performed every 2 months. The cost of platinum was modeled as the cost of carboplatin, assuming outpatient chemotherapy and the clinical usage of carboplatin over cisplatin, as over 70% of patients treated with DCE received carboplatin in the CASPIAN trial, a prospective cohort analysis of ED-SCLC patients treated with ICI by Morimoto et al. [27], and a retrospective cohort study of ED-SCLC patients treated with ICI by Takeda et al. [28]. Drug prices were obtained from Japanese social insurance medical fees [29] and the drug price standard [30]. Hospital treatment costs were estimated based on the diagnostic group classification score table [31]. According to the Japanese Society of Nephrology, the average height and weight of a Japanese person is 1.70 m and 63 kg, respectively. Accordingly, the body surface area of the patients in the simulation population was assumed to be 1.73 m2. The dosage was calculated based on the average body weight and body surface area of the Japanese population. Every 3 weeks, ACE comprised atezolizumab 1200 mg, carboplatin 421 mg (area under the curve of 5), and etoposide 519 mg. The prophylactic antiemetics used were fosaprepitant, palonosetron, and dexamethasone. Following a pattern similar to ACE, DCE comprised four doses of durvalumab 1500 mg every 3 weeks, and then a maintenance dose of durvalumab 1500 mg every 4 weeks.

Cost parameters

JPY Japanese yen, USD US dollar

Costs incurred after primary therapy were estimated using the JMDC database, Inc. [32]. The JMDC database is one of the largest commercial databases of real-world data (RWD) that stores subscriber ledgers, receipts (inpatient, outpatient, and dispensing), and medical care data received from insurers. The database assigns a unique ID to each participant so that they can be tracked if they change hospitals or visit multiple institutions. However, the elderly (75 years or older) were excluded. Stata/SE (version 16.1, StataCorp LLC, 2023) was employed to extract the data of 26,006 people with insurance related to SCLC over the past 15 years and obtained the average cost in the 12 months prior to death. The posttreatment difference by primary therapy difference was not provided; thus, it was considered nonsignificant. Treatment-related adverse events of grade 3 or 4 with an incidence ≥ 5% in the IMpower 133 and CASPIAN studies were included in the analysis. The cost of treating febrile neutropenia was estimated based on a study by Miyake et al. [33] examining the cost of febrile neutropenia in breast cancer patients. Costs were obtained by multiplying the incidence of adverse events by the unit cost of treatment. JPY costs were converted into USD, using an average exchange rate of 131.5 yen per USD in the most recent year, which was 2022 [34].

Parameter and Utility Estimation

Using the WebPlotDigitizer [35], Kaplan–Meier curves were analyzed for the IMpower 133 and CASPIAN trials, and individual patient data were reconstructed using a method reported by Guyot et al. [36]. To determine the optimal distribution, Akaike and Bayesian Information Criteria were followed, and visual evaluation was performed. Log-logistic distributions were fitted for survival curves of ACE, DCE, and CE therapies in the IMpower 133 trial, and Weibull distributions were fitted for survival curves of CE therapy in the CASPIAN trial and extrapolated (Fig. 2). The distribution parameters are shown in Table 2. The fitted curves were deemed suitable as the cumulative distribution function of the model curve was determined to be similar to the survival rate of the data source. The effects were assessed using utility values. Utility values were derived from a study by Kuehne et al. on utility values throughout chemotherapy in ED-SCLC patients undergoing first-line chemotherapy [37], with a PFS of 0.714, PD of 0.662, and mortality of 0 (Table 2). Utility declines after an adverse event were derived from a study by Nafees et al., who examined utility values for the second-line treatment of metastatic non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) [38].

Sensitivity Analysis

Estimates of the parameters were derived from various information sources. To evaluate the influence of uncertainty when estimating parameters for the results, sensitivity analyses were conducted using confidence intervals, standard errors, or a ± 20% range of variation for each parameter. To evaluate model uncertainty, one-way and probability sensitivity analyses were conducted. One-way sensitivity analysis is an approach in which parameters are varied throughout a range of potential values to examine their influence on the ICER. One-way sensitivity analyses were conducted with standard error variances for utility values and 20% variances for the other parameters. The medicine price fluctuation range over the threshold of 15 million JPY (114,068 USD) per QALY was investigated in an exploratory manner. During the probabilistic sensitivity analysis, the ICER was stochastically evaluated by performing 10,000 Monte Carlo simulations in which probability distributions were set for the model parameters, and random numbers were simultaneously generated. For each parameter, costs followed a gamma distribution, adverse event rates and treatment intensity followed a beta distribution, and costs and utility values after second-line treatment followed a normal distribution. To randomly sample the curve parameters of the model, the correlation was set by the variance–covariance matrix. Tables 1 and 2 show the ranges and distributions of the parameters evaluated in the sensitivity analyses.

Scenario Analysis

For the base analysis, we selected utility values from the most recent report for patients with ED-SCLC on first-line therapy by Kuehne et al. [37]. This was considered the most plausible because the background of the patient group was common to this analysis population. In addition, Veddai et al. reported a PFS and PD of 0.700 and 0.600, respectively [39], and Labbé et al. reported a PFS and PD of 0.720 and 0.520, respectively [40]. Nafees et al. [41] investigated the utility values of those with advanced NSCLC and obtained a PFS and PD of 0.673 and 0.473, respectively. The scenario analysis was performed to confirm the variance in the ICER when each utility value was used.

Results

Base Case Results

Table 3 presents the results of the base case analysis. Based on the IMpower 133 trial, the QALYs for ACE and CE therapies were 1.110 and 0.877, respectively. The total costs of ACE and CE therapies were 18,547,501 JPY (141,046 USD) and 10,389,673 JPY (79,009 USD), respectively. The ICER of ACE therapy was 35,048,299 JPY (266,527 USD) per QALY. For the CASPIAN study, the QALYs for DCE and CE were 1.290 and 0.792, respectively. The total costs of DCE and CE therapies were 26,707,876 JPY (203,102 USD) and 8,455,594 JPY (64301 USD), respectively. The ICER of DCE therapy was 36,655,583 JPY (266,527 USD) per QALY. In the base case model, the 5-year relative survival rates obtained were 4.5%, 7.7%, 2.3%, and 0.3% in the ACE, DCE, CE CASPIAN, and CE IMpower 133 trials, respectively.

Sensitivity Analysis

The effect on the ICER and the results of the one-way sensitivity analysis were depicted in a tornado diagram (Fig. 3), including the results of the top ten parameters that affected the ICER. The ICI prices exerted the highest impact on the ICERs in the ranges of 30,530,506–39,566,092 and 32,128,866–41,202,301 JPY per QALY for ACE and DCE therapies, respectively. The price adjustments of 90% and 95% for atezolizumab and durvalumab, respectively, resulted in ICERs of 14,718,230 JPY (111,926 USD) and 15,116,174 JPY (114,952 USD) per QALY. This was close to the WTP threshold of 15 million JPY (114,068 USD) per QALY.

Discussion

This study assessed the cost-effectiveness of atezolizumab and durvalumab as combination treatments with chemotherapy as the initial treatment for patients with ED-SCLC in Japan. The ICERs for ACE and DCE were approximately 35 and 37 million JPY, respectively. Cost estimation using receipt data showed reasonable results in RWD. Both treatments had ICERs that significantly exceeded the threshold of 15 million JPY per QALY, indicating that the efficiency of ICI combination treatment with chemotherapy was significantly worse. The price of durvalumab was more than twice that of atezolizumab; however, their ICERs were similar. This could be because the most current long-term outcome report was used as the data source, resulting in a longer OS in the DCE arm of the CASPIAN research than that reported. According to sensitivity and scenario analyses, the cost-effectiveness of ACE and DCE therapies likely exceeded the threshold set for chemotherapy in the cost-effectiveness evaluation system. The sensitivity analysis revealed the need for significant price reductions, estimating acceptable pricing for atezolizumab and durvalumab to be 10% and 5% of their current prices, respectively. However, such reductions are not feasible. It is recommended to conduct clinical trials that explore smaller dosages, longer intervals between treatments, and target more effective subpopulations to enhance efficiency.

Several studies from the USA and China have evaluated the cost-effectiveness of ACE and DCE compared with CE in the treatment of previously untreated patients with ED-SCLC. Zhou et al. compared the cost-effectiveness of ACE and CE in previously untreated patients with ED-SCLC in the US healthcare system using a Markov model, reporting an ICER for ACE of 528,810 USD/QALY [16]. Li et al. compared the cost-effectiveness of ACE and CE in the Chinese healthcare system using a Markov model, finding an ICER of 489,013 USD/QALY for ACE [17]. Zhang et al. compared the cost-effectiveness of DCE versus CE in treatment-naïve patients with ED-SCLC in the US healthcare system, reporting an ICER of 355,448 USD/QALY for DCE [19]. All these studies conclude that atezolizumab and durvalumab combination treatment with chemotherapy is not more cost-effective than chemotherapy alone. The calculations were based on the unit prices in each country, differences in drug prices and insurance systems in each country, as well as differences in analysis models. When assessed according to cost-effectiveness thresholds in the Japanese healthcare system, the combination of atezolizumab and durvalumab with chemotherapy was no more cost-effective than chemotherapy alone, and it showed a consistent trend of economic challenges with prior analyses conducted in the USA and China.

Here, the model was extended beyond the clinical trial observation period, although this extrapolation introduces some ambiguity since the long-term OS of patients with ED-SCLC was modeled using a parametric distribution. The clinical measurements of the 5-year relative survival rate of Japanese SCLC patients were compared with the 5-year relative survival rate computed using this model [24]. While the distribution of features in the CASPIAN trial challenged comparability, enrolling approximately 10% and 90% of stage III and IV patients, respectively, the model was considered valid given the absence of significant differences [11].

Based on the JMDC database, the cost data following disease progression might not fully represent the Japanese health insurance system overall. Although the JMDC database does not contain data on individuals older than 75 years, the prevalence of older adults with lung cancer is evident. Moreover, claim data were not gathered for research purposes; hence, they could be incomplete, potentially compromising the accuracy of cost estimations and not fully reflecting the total cost within the Japanese health insurance system. However, sensitivity analysis evaluated post-progression costs and found that they did not affect the threshold.

This study has some limitations. First, it assumed that the efficacy and practicality of data from published studies applied to the Japanese medical environment. Owing to the dearth of RWD, each study was searched and examined for relevance while considering quality. At the time of the analysis, this method was deemed appropriate; however, it should be updated with new results from future clinical studies. Second, the utility values used were based on data from a prospective cohort study examining utility values in patients with ED-SCLC in Canada, not Japan, which may have affected the results. The one-way sensitivity analysis revealed that altering utility values had minimal influence on the ICER and no effect on the threshold. Future quality-of-life research could refine the proposed model. Third, because of the selectivity of carboplatin in clinical trials and Japanese cohort studies and the high feasibility of administration in chemotherapy, we assumed the costs of platinum drugs to be those of carboplatin [11, 27, 28]. This analysis may have overestimated the cost of chemotherapy because carboplatin is slightly more expensive than cisplatin. However, the results of the sensitivity analysis showed that varying the cost of carboplatin had only a minimal effect on the model results. Fourth, adverse events included in the model were grade 3 or 4 with an incidence of at least 5% and were assumed to be costly. Therefore, only febrile neutropenia was included, and costs and utility values for other adverse events were not included in the model. Thus, it is possible that costs were underestimated and utility values overestimated. However, the results of the sensitivity analysis confirmed that changing the cost and utility value reduction of adverse events had only a minimal effect on the ICER.

Conclusion

In the Japanese medical system, ACE and DCE were shown to be less cost-effective than CE as first-line treatments for ED-SCLC. Both therapies have comparably high ICERs, indicating the need for more clinical research and pricing adjustments to address economic concerns.
