Accurate quantification of the bacterial inoculum and tissue load in a preclinical model of implant-related infection
Authors: Qi Sun, Dzenita Muratovic, Paul H. Anderson, Rebecca Sawyer, Helen Tsangari, Lucian Bogdan Solomon, Gerald J. Atkins, Dongqing Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AimsOrthopaedic device-related infections are a major and growing clinical complication of joint arthroplasty surgery. Preclinical models are vital for developing strategies for prevention and treatment of these infections but suffer from imprecise determination of pathogen load during the experimental infection. We sought to establish a protocol for the accurate measurement of pathogen load using genome-based quantification in a preclinical implant-related infection model.Methods Staphylococcus aureus strain attachment to stainless steel pin implants was quantified using both colony-forming unit (CFU) counting and genome copy counting, utilizing droplet digital polymerase chain reaction (ddPCR). An optimized protocol was then applied to a trans-tibial periprosthetic joint infection (PJI) mouse model. Pathological peri-implant bone loss and residual bacteria were monitored by in vivo micro-CT and immunostaining. Fixed and demineralized bone histological sections were used to measure bacterial tissue load by direct DNA isolation and ddPCR quantification, and host gene expression by reverse transcription ddPCR analysis.ResultsAttachment studies of strain ATCC25923 to pins revealed that bacterial load peaked and stabilized after 20 minutes, with ddPCR analysis showing superior reproducibility and higher yields than CFU counting: ddPCR (mean 46,532 genome copies/mm² (SD 14,413; 33,904 to 62,235), CFU: 3,751 CFU/mm² (SD 4,936; 743.1 to 9,448) . Similar findings were recorded for three other strains tested, demonstrating the inaccuracy of CFU analysis. Pins coated with ATCC25923 for 20 minutes were selected for the mouse model. At 18 days post-infection, ddPCR analysis of 20 mm tibial histological sections from individual mice demonstrated residual bacterial loads (mean bacterial genomes/µm) between mice of 1.50 to 3.46 (0.36 to 6.73) and 8.64 to 39.14 (5.27 to 47.86). The potential for performing transcriptomic analysis was also demonstrated using the above tissue section preparation.ConclusionOur approach of ddPCR quantification from histological sections offers improved accuracy and reproducibility for readouts of bacterial load from input to endpoint, independent of bacterial culturability, in a preclinical model of PJI.Cite this article: Bone Joint Res 2026;15(4):375–382.

Introduction

Periprosthetic joint infection (PJI), a class of orthopaedic device-related infection, is a major clinical complication of joint arthroplasty surgery, predicted to increase in incidence with increasing surgical demand.1 Pathogen cultures from intraoperative periprosthetic tissue biopsies or fluids is considered a major diagnostic criterion for determining the presence of a PJI.2 However, the sensitivity of periprosthetic tissue culture is influenced by the inherent culturability of the causative microorganisms and quality of the host tissue, with culture-negative rates reportedly ranging from 7% to above 50%.3,4

Animal models of PJI and other musculoskeletal infections, such as fracture-related infection, are widely used in preclinical research. Inoculum size and bacterial persistence are important determinants of establishment of an experimental infection and disease resolution, respectively,5 however accurate assessment of these is problematic. Bacterial load or presence typically assessed by colony-forming unit (CFU) analysis from either homogenized or sonicated host tissues, histological staining (e.g. Gram staining and transmission electron microscopy (TEM)), bioluminescent imaging of lux-transformed bacteria, and quantitative PCR (qPCR) analysis.6 However, CFU analysis is influenced by a number of factors, including efficiency of tissue processing and retention of bacterial viability, and the inherent culturability of the bacteria in question, including possible pathogen phenotypic switching to low- or non-culturable growth states, as documented for the most prevalent pathogen in PJI, Staphylococcus aureus.7-9 Gram-staining of bacteria within the complexity of host tissue can be challenging to assess: while potentially definitive, it is difficult to quantify due to the small tissue areas and volumes typically analyzed. In vivo bioluminescent imaging, on the other hand, lacks quantitative accuracy and has considerable technical limitations. qPCR, while more precise, often suffers from substantial loss of target sample sequence during DNA extraction, reducing its reliability, especially in chronic infections or samples with low bacterial load.

In this study, we developed a method to quantify the bacterial genome copy number from embedded bone histological sections by ddPCR in a preclinical model of implant-related S. aureus infection.

Methods

Clinical isolate S. aureus strains, WCH-SK2 (SK2),10 WCH-SK3 (SK3),11 and 142B,12 and ATCC25923 were used for the comparison of initial attachment. A single colony from each strain was picked and grown in terrific broth (TB; Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) overnight to reach the plateau phase with the conditions of 37°C and 200 rpm agitation. Stainless steel pins (Australian Entomological Supplies, Australia), 0.3 mm in diameter and 38 mm in length, were chosen as the implants. For bacterial attachment, autoclaved pins were placed in 1 ml of static plateau phase bacterial suspension at 1 × 10⁹ CFU/ml individually in 5 ml tubes with a suspension coverage of 10 mm. Pins were then removed and air-dried for five minutes prior to analysis.6 Two control groups were included for in vitro assessment: 1) heat-killed bacterial suspension under 95°C for 20 minutes; and 2) bacterial supernatant after 3000× g centrifugation for five minutes. Firstly, pins were placed in the suspension of ATCC25923 for five minutes, 20 minutes, and one hour to determine the optimum coating time. The optimized time length of 20 minutes, as shown in Figure 1b, was then applied for coating comparisons for SK2, SK3, and 142B strains. Attached bacteria were enumerated by either CFU counting following their physical removal at 35 kHz of ultrasonication for five minutes, then subsequently counting bacterial number post-CFU development on agar plates after 24 hours or by the measurement of genome copy number using ddPCR (QX200 Droplet Digital PCR system; Bio-Rad, USA). After sonication, the inoculated pins were placed and incubated on agar plates in an incubator overnight to verify the presence of remaining adherent bacteria. For measurement of genome copies of pin-attached bacteria, the pins were removed from suspension and placed in microbiology bead-tubes containing glass beads of 0.1 mm in diameter (Omni International, USA), with 500 µl DirectPCR buffer (Viagen Biotech, USA). Attached bacteria were physically ruptured at a speed of 3 m/s for 30 seconds, using a BR24 Elite tissue homogeniser (Omni International). The homogenate was digested in DirectPCR buffer (Viagen Biotech) with 200 µg/ml proteinase K added at 55°C for 40 minutes to release DNA . Bacterial attachment characteristics were confirmed by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) using a SU7000 UHR-SEM (Hitachi, Japan). Pins were stabilized in fixative comprising 4% w/v, paraformaldehyde-1.25% w/v, glycolaldehyde in PBS, 4% w/v sucrose (pH 7.2) and then in 2% w/v, osmium tetroxide in water. Subsequently, pins were embedded in resin blocks and prepared for SEM imaging.

Standardization of initial bacterial input on implants. a) Confirmation of attachment of SK2, SK3, 142B, and ATCC25923 on pins following 20 minutes coating by scanning electron microscopy. b) Time-dependent attachment to implants after five, 20, and 60 minutes by Staphylococcus aureus ATCC25923 strain. c) Attachment of S. aureus strains SK2, SK3, 142B, and ATCC25923 after 20 minutes, comparing quantification by sonication/colony-forming unit (CFU) counting and genomic DNA isolation/droplet digital polymerase chain reaction. Data are shown as means (standard errors of the means)). Note that no statistically significant differences between strains were evident.

Following attachment optimization, pins coated with ATCC25923, as described above, or sterile saline (control) were chosen as implants in a trans-tibial infection mouse model, adapted from the study by Li et al,6 using healthy eight-week-old female C57BL/6 mice. Mice were randomly allocated to an infection group and a control group (n = 3/group) and anaesthetized with isoflurane before the surgical procedure. The surgical site was shaved and skin sterilized using betadine before incision exposure of the anterior proximal tibia. A guide hole was drilled with a 27-gauge needle, and either S. aureus or sterile saline coated pins were inserted trans-cortically, with a length of ~6 mm of pin remaining in the bone. Post-surgical pain relief (0.3 mg/ml buprenorphine) was administered subcutaneously for two to four hours, depending on clinical signs of pain. Animal health and wellbeing were monitored daily throughout the experimental period. In vivo micro-CT (Skyscan 1276, Bruker, Belgium) scanning was performed on day 1, 4, 7, 11, and 18 postoperatively. 3D images were reconstructed with NRecon software (v. 1.7.4.6; Bruker, Belgium). At 18 days postoperatively, mice remained anaesthetized under isoflurane after their scan and were humanely euthanized by cervical dislocation and tissues collected for analysis. Pins were removed, and the operated tibia was then put through a process of paraffin embedding post-decalcification in TheraLin fixative (Grace Biolabs, USA) at room temperature for seven days. A 5 µm histological section was used for haematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining to serve as a reference for assessing tissue integrity and guiding interpretation of the adjacent anti-S. aureus immunostaining. A rabbit polyclonal antibody targeting S. aureus (AB20920; Abcam, UK) or negative control rabbit Immunoglobulin G (IgG) were used at a 1:100 dilution for staining at 4°C for one hour. Primary antibody signals were tagged by Alexa Fluor 647-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody (AB150083; Abcam).

A single 20 μm section was used for total DNA and RNA isolation. For DNA isolation, sections were placed in a microcentrifuge tube and deparaffinized using xylene, followed by graded ethanol washes (100% then 70%), then centrifuged to obtain the deparaffinized tissue pellet. The deparaffinized tissue pellet was digested in DirectPCR buffer (Viagen Biotech) containing 200 μg/ml proteinase K at 55°C overnight and then heat-inactivated at 85°C for 15 minutes to terminate enzymatic digestion. For RNA isolation, the deparaffinized tissue pellets were alternatively digested in TRIzol reagent for RNA isolation using the Direct-zol RNA Miniprep Plus system (Zymo Research, USA), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. All procedures following the institutional biosafety guidelines, and animal usage were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of the University of South Australia (No. U04-23).

The quantification of genome copies and gene expression as mRNA copies were performed using the QX200 ddPCR system according to the manufacturer’s instructions and as previously described.10 Primers targeting a mouse genome-specific sequence within the single-copy type X collagen gene (Col10a1) or the S. aureus genome sequence within the single-copy tuf gene were used for the amplification of host and pathogen signals, respectively. The mRNA copy numbers of a panel of bone-enriched host genes. The sequences of all primer sets are listed in Supplementary Table i.

Statistical analysis

Results were plotted as means and standard errors of the means. The significance of genome copy quantification among mice was evaluated using a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) test using GraphPad Prism 10.2 (GraphPad, USA), where applicable.

Results

The pattern of attached bacteria of SK2, SK3, 142B and ATCC25923 was confirmed by SEM (Figure 1a). The pin surface post-homogenization and DirectPCR buffer (Viagen Biotech) digestion confirmed complete bacterial removal (Supplementary Figure aa). The numbers of S. aureus genome copies were found to be ~10 fold higher compared to CFU numbers, following sonication. Furthermore, sonication failed to remove all attached bacteria, evidenced by post-sonication culture (Supplementary Figure ab). Moreover, genome counting yielded better reproducibility in measurements (Figures 1b and 1c). For strain ATCC25923, attachment peaked and became stable at 20 minutes (Figure 1b), therefore, a coating time of 20 minutes was maintained for all S. aureus strains. The ATCC25923 strain exhibited the most reproducible attachment characteristics (Figure 1c) and hence was chosen for the in vivo experiments. To examine the potential contribution of non-viable bacteria to genome counts, a suspension of 100% heat-killed bacteria and post-viable bacteria supernatant resulted in 7% and 2% of ddPCR signal, respectively, compared to live cell suspensions (Supplementary Figure ac), confirming that viable bacteria constituted the majority of the ddPCR signal in attachment assays.

Following implantation of pins coated with ATCC25923, infection-mediated bone loss and deformation surrounding the pin was first observed at day 11 (Figure 2b) and immunostaining confirmed S. aureus persistence in peri-implant bone tissue at the experiment endpoint (Figure 2c).

The specificity of antibody staining was confirmed by the use of an isotype-matched negative control antibody (see Supplementary Figure ad).

Total DNA prepared from multiple sections of embedded tissue quantified by either by standard qPCR (Supplementary Figure aef) or ddPCR revealed consistent bacterial genome copies between animals (Figure 3), whereas no bacterial signal was generated from control mice (data not shown). Host gene expression was also examined by reverse transcription (RT)-ddPCR for levels of Gja1, encoding connexin 43, Cola1, encoding type I collagen, Bglap, encoding bone gla-containing protein/osteocalcin, and Spp1, encoding osteopontin, which all remained stable between samples (Figure 3c).

Each data point shows a ddPCR result from one DNA sample with system-generated error; graphs show the mean (standard error of the mean (SEM)) of three samples per mouse. For genome copy ratios, the means (SEMs) from three samples are shown. Note that none of the comparisons reached statistical significance, suggesting reproducibility between samples.

Discussion

In this study, we showed that quantifying bacterial genome copy numbers by ddPCR after extracting DNA from paraffin-embedded infected mouse bone sections using one-step extraction buffer represents a viable method for assessing bacterial load and confirming and monitoring infection in such models. This approach is similarly useful for examining gene expression from the histologically decalcified and fixed bone sections via RT-ddPCR.

Currently, the conventional measurement for enumerating bacteria in preclinical models is CFU assessment on agar plates. However, the accuracy of such measurements is impacted by the efficiency of bacteria recovery from the tissue or implant, phenotypic, and genotypic diversity and growth characteristics of each bacterial strain, as well as the conditions of culture.13 Bacterial load is also commonly assessed by bioluminescent imaging of lux-transformed bacteria in vivo and quantified using real-time PCR from the tissue sample.6 However, this method has limitations, including a potentially weak signal intensity, in particular from hard bone due to poor tissue penetration of the bioluminescent signal, difficulty in transforming certain bacterial strains, and potential alterations in bacterial phenotype or virulence in doing so.14-16 In this study, we sought to avoid the above issues and assayed the bacterial load by quantifying pathogen genome copy number using ddPCR. A direct PCR buffer was used to maximize bacterial DNA retrieval, as we previously reported.10 Quantification of pin-attached bacteria is potentially complicated by the variable formation of biofilms in a strain- and time-dependent manner, which affects both the number of attached bacteria and the homogenization conditions, which should therefore be optimized prior to experimentation. In order to perform DNA extraction from mouse tibial bone carrying bacteria, we chose to process bone tissue into histological sections and combine this with the direct DNA lysis buffer for sampling. Serial sectioning allows combining DNA analysis with (immuno)histological identification of infected areas, therby optimizing quantification of tissue-resident bacteria. The direct DNA-releasing approach with the one-step lysis buffer reduces sample loss that occurs during purification step(s) in the traditional approach, and maximizes the release of genomic DNA.10 ddPCR was employed to replace qPCR in this study for its advantages of being more accurate and providing absolute quantification,17 without requiring a standard curve, such as that needed for a qPCR approach.18 Further, RNA can also be extracted from the pathological sections for gene expression. Host gene products of interest could also be investigated using immunostaining using this approach.

This study has a number of limitations. While we demonstrated the superiority of ddPCR-based genome counting of bacteria to traditional CFU-based counting for the purposes of quantifying bacterial input and residual bacterial tissue load in an implant-related infection model, we did not compare ddPCR and CFU in vivo at the experimental endpoint. This was primarily due to the practical problems of efficient viable bacteria extraction from host bone tissue. However, even if viable bacteria could be extracted, it is likely in this chronic infection model that phenotypic change might reduce culturability regardless.7-9 Furthermore, we used a small sample size of mice (n = 3) to establish the concept of ddPCR efficacy in a preclinical setting, however this did not afford us the statistical power to, for example, examine the differences between bacterial strains in terms of infection dynamics. In addition, there remains the possibility that some of the ddPCR signal retrieved from mouse tissue was from non-viable bacteria. We demonstrated here that if pins were coated in a suspension of completely heat-killed bacteria, or with viable bacteria supernatant (possibly containing free bacterial DNA) that this yielded 7% and 2%, respectively, of the signal from a viable bacterial suspension, which we consider to be minimal. In the case of tissue, a similar analysis was not possible, however intact and therefore likely viable bacteria were detected by immunostaining.

In conclusion, we describe a new approach to quantify both the inoculum and the residual bacterial load from the embedded tissue sections via ddPCR in a mouse trans-tibial infection model. This approach would be applicable to most other preclinical models of bone-related infection and offers advantages of accuracy and reproducibility compared to traditional histological or bioluminescent imaging approaches, with accurate quantification of both bacterial and host cell load via genome-based detection. Further, ddPCR offers absolute quantification without the need for standard curves, simplifying the workflow. The use of a direct PCR buffer minimizes sample loss and allows the analysis of fixed tissue sections, which is especially beneficial for detecting low bacterial loads. Finally, our approach allows simultaneous histological and molecular analyses, giving precise quantification of bacterial load and visualization of bacteria within the same tissue context.
