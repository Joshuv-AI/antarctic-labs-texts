A very rare case report of circumflex aorta in the cervical vertebral region in an 8-year-old child
Authors: Amirhossein Jalali, Mohammad Mahdavi, Yaser Toloueitabar, Seyed Salaheddin Nabavi, Mahmoud Ganjifard, Shokoofeh Derakhshan, Seyyed Ebrahim Hosseini Zargaz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAlthough circumflex aorta is a rare congenital disease, it can be diagnosed at different ages.Case presentationThis congenital disease has been reported as both right circumflex aorta and left circumflex aorta. To the best of our knowledge, circumflex aorta extending into the cervical/cervical vertebral region is exceptionally rare, with very few comparable reports in the literature. Therefore, in this case, we present our experiences regarding surgical considerations in these patients.ConclusionAlthough circumflex aorta is a rare congenital disease given the elevated aortic arch in these patients, meticulous care must be taken to protect the recurrent laryngeal nerve, thyroid gland, and parathyroid glands.Keywords: Circumflex aorta, Cervical vertebral, Congenital disease

Introduction

The circumflex aortic arch originates from the ascending aorta toward the right side, courses posteriorly behind the trachea and esophagus to emerge on the left side of these structures, and then continues as the descending aorta. This anomaly can be asymptomatic or result in severe respiratory failure associated with tracheal and esophageal compression [1, 2]. A retro-esophageal transverse arch, passing over the vertebral column at the T2-T3 level above the tracheal carina, is characteristic of the circumflex aorta. In this case report, we describe a case of circumflex aorta where the transverse arch was located in the neck, near the C7 vertebra.

Case report

The case we report is of an 8-year-old child who, upon transthoracic echocardiography, was diagnosed with right circumflex aorta and a cervical arch exerting compressive effects on the trachea above the carina, forming a complete vascular ring (Fig. 1). Additionally, an aneurysm originating from the right subclavian artery (RSCA) and the right common carotid artery (RCCA) and the patent foramen ovale (PFO), and separating the left subclavian artery from the descending aorta were diagnosed. The patient underwent general anesthesia, and the incision was made as a median sternotomy and total thymectomy, extending the skin and subcutaneous incision to the right neck. Control was obtained from the branches of the LCCA, RCCA, and RSCA. The patent ductus arteriosus (PDA) ligament was ligated and cut. A portion of the vascular ring was heparinized, and the ascending aorta and right atrium were cavalated. The patient underwent cardiopulmonary bypass aiming at achieving deep hypothermia at 20. The aorta was clamped and a venous cannula was inserted into the inferior vena cava to establish negative pressure. The right atrium was incised, and suction was placed in the superior vena cava to close the PFO. The right atrium was then closed, and the venous cannula was redirected into the right atrium. Once the temperature reached 20, the aortic arch was clamped to the right of the esophagus, cut exactly where it passed posterior, and its proximal end was sutured in two layers (with the establishment of total circulatory arrest and partial circulatory arrest for ten minutes). Subsequently, the aortic arch was freed from posterior part of the esophagus and the anterior part of the vertebral column and pulled anteriorly and to the left (uncrossing). A Foley catheter, size 22, connected to the second arterial line, was inserted into the descending aorta and its balloon inflated, establishing full blood flow (completion of TCA and PCA). Then, the ascending aortic artery cannula was inserted into the left common carotid artery (LCCA), and by cutting the aneurysmal RSCA, the third arterial cannula was inserted into the RCCA. At this stage, a longitudinal incision was made on the posterior and medial part of the ascending aorta, below the LCCA, and an end-to-side anastomosis was performed (complete repair of the aortic aneurysm). The Foley catheter was removed from the descending aorta. The aortic arterial cannula was repositioned from the LCCA into the ascending aorta, and the ascending aorta was clamped above this point. Simultaneously, as the patient was warmed and the heart cavities were de-aired, the aorta was declamped, and the heart began to beat in sinus rhythm. The remaining portion of the ascending aorta was sutured longitudinally in a lateral direction to reduce its diameter to ten millimeters. Additionally, the aneurysmal portion of the RSCA was sutured longitudinally on one side, and its distal end was also sutured. An end-to-side anastomosis was performed between this portion and the incised area of the lateral RCCA. The third arterial cannula was removed from the RCCA. In this way, the aneurysmal portions of the subclavian and right common carotid arteries were repaired. The patient was successfully weaned from the pump and discharged from the intensive care unit (ICU) after 10 days.

Discussion

Circumflex retro-esophageal right aortic arch is a very uncommon anomaly. Embryologically, a circumflex right aorta with ectopic LSCA results from retraction of the left dorsal aorta between the LCCA and the LSCA, and the right dorsal aorta forms the distal part of the arch.

It has also been hypothesized that the third aortic arch gives rise to the proximal aortic arch, while the distal arch is formed by the fourth arch, which can explain the elongated transverse arch as well as the relatively higher position of the proximal aortic arch [3].

Circumflex aorta is a rare vascular ring in which the transverse portion of the arch passes through the posterior medial line of the trachea, usually at the level of the carina or just above it, and then descends on the opposite side of the vertebral column. However, in the case we reported, the ascending aorta extended higher than the carina, reaching the level of the neck and the side of the upper cervical vertebrae. Thus, the transverse arch of the aorta was at the cervical level. To the best of our knowledge, no such case has been reported. Typically, in reported cases, the ascending aorta or the arch has been normal, but in our case, the RSCA and the RCCA were aneurysmal [4].

Regarding surgical methods for treating circumflex aorta, various approaches have been described, ranging from simple division of the arterial ligament to complete anatomical correction (uncrossing). Without aortic arch hypoplasia or obstruction, division of the arterial ligament alone has been used to relieve symptoms of vascular ring. However, division of the arterial ligament alone does not alleviate compression of mediastinal structures from the posterior esophageal arch portion. Therefore, the technique of aortic uncrossing was described by a French group in 1984 in three infants with circumflex aortic arch, all of whom had previously undergone ligamentum division alone but remained ventilator-dependent. These three patients were extubated after aortic uncrossing [5, 6].

Conclusion

Despite the complexities and potential complications stemming from surgery, several key points should be considered when dealing with a circumflex aorta in the cervical region. Firstly, the sternal incision should be made above the sternal notch and along the right sternocleidomastoid muscle (SCM) of the neck to facilitate exposure of the operative field. Secondly, given the elevated aortic arch in these patients, meticulous care must be taken to protect the recurrent laryngeal nerve, thyroid gland, and parathyroid glands.
