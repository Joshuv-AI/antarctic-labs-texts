Relationship between shift scheduling, sleep knowledge and sleep quality in medical trainees: protocol for a global cross-sectional survey
Authors: Cheng Zhang, Karen Spruyt, Morenikeji Komolafe, Mike Mutschelknaus, Lourdes M. DelRosso, Joerg Steier
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAlthough many studies suggest that shift work and nocturnal on-call rotations are associated with adverse outcomes in medical trainees’ health, evidence regarding the optimal frequency and duration of shifts that balance trainee well-being, patient safety, and medical education remains limited. Additionally, the level of medical trainees’ knowledge about sleep medicine and its possible relationship with their well-being during on-call periods remains unclear.MethodsThis study protocol was designed in collaboration between professional sleep physicians from the USA, UK, Nigeria and France as a global multi-centred, cross-sectional, online survey conducted through a secure online link (REDCap 14.0.42, Vanderbilt University, USA). The proposed design covers demographic characteristics, different specialties, work conditions, on-call situations, sleep, mood, and social issues, as well as other relevant health factors. The methodology includes univariate, multivariate analyses, and regression models. The intended analysis aims to investigate the impact of on-call duties, as well as sleep knowledge, on the sleep quality and well-being of medical trainees.DiscussionThrough this study, we expect to explore how different shift scheduling patterns and levels of sleep knowledge may be associated with the sleep quality of medical trainees. In addition, cross-cultural comparisons among diverse linguistic and cultural contexts will be examined.Trial RegistrationThe study is registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT06706453).

Introduction

Background

Sleep is a vital physiological process for humans. Good sleep is essential for maintaining physical and mental health, as well as for optimal learning and work performance. On-call rotations and shift work can lead to acute or chronic sleep deprivation and disrupted sleep rhythms, negatively affecting cognitive function and overall health for the general population (1-3). Continuous night shift work is associated with an increased incidence of cardiovascular risks, diabetes, mood disorders and cancer (4,5). In addition, traditional risk factors for obesity include excessive energy intake and a lack of physical activity. However, recent research has suggested that insufficient sleep and disrupted circadian rhythms are also significant contributors to individual weight gain (6) and immune-metabolic dysregulation in adipose tissue (7).

Medical trainees frequently face challenging night shifts due to the nature of their profession. In Europe, the introduction of the European Working Time Directive in 2003 limited the average working hours to 48 hours per week, while it was previously not uncommon to have up to 36-hour shifts when being on-call. Over the past 20 years, many studies have explored the impact of on-call and shift work on the health of medical trainees and the patients they care for.

Night shifts can cause acute sleep deprivation. This may lead to decreased alertness (8,9) and impairments in executive functions, such as reduced attention and problem-solving ability (2).

Frequent shifts increase the likelihood of chronic sleep deprivation (9), which can result in attention (10,11), cognitive (12) and memory (13) impairments. Night shifts and sleep deprivation can also negatively impact mood, leading to increased occurrences of depression (14,15) and burnout (16). These issues affect not only the performance of medical trainees but also the quality of patient care by potentially increasing the risk of medical errors (17-19).

More and more countries are recognizing the negative impact of long duty hours on both medical trainees and patients, and are implementing regulations to limit the working hours and shift times (20). In 2003, the Accreditation Council for Graduate Medical Education (ACGME) of the United States (US) issued rules for working time restrictions, stating that residents should not exceed a maximum of 80 hours of work per week (on average over 4 weeks), and the duration of a single shift should not exceed 24 hours (21,22). In 2011, the ACGME made further revisions to the duty hour regulations, limiting interns (first postgraduate year) to a maximum of 16 hours for a single shift and no more than six consecutive nights of night float (21,22). In the European Union, working hours for medical trainees are limited to 48 hours per week (based on a 26-week average) (23,24), and the UK has similar working hour regulations (WHR) (24). In Canada, there is currently no national agreement on working hours. In 2011, the Fédération des Médecins Résidents du Québec (FMRQ) established a maximum continuous working time of 16 hours for all residents (20). Australia also lacks a national uniform standard. However, Queensland specifies a working time of 38 hours per week in the hospital, excluding on-call hours (25). In New Zealand, contracts for resident doctors stipulate that the maximum rostered working hours per week should not exceed 72 hours (25). In Colombia, residents cannot work for longer than 12 consecutive hours and more than 66 hours a week (26). In some countries in Asia and Africa, the specific implementation restricting the working hours of medical trainees varies by region and hospital.

Rationale and knowledge gap

Overall, limiting working hours has a positive impact on medical trainees’ sleep, helps prevent burnout, improves emotional well-being, and enhances other health-related outcomes, according to current research and several systematic reviews (27-29). However, the existing evidence shows considerable heterogeneity, with some studies indicating that simply limiting working hours does not improve patient care or medical trainees’ well-being. Scheduling of work shifts is important, as merely reducing consecutive working hours may lead to an increase in night shifts and a diminished resting period and even more medical errors, which could be detrimental to both residents and patients (29,30). On the other hand, compressed working hours result in reduced teaching opportunities and have a negative impact on their education (30,31). Furthermore, it remains unclear whether reducing duty hours effectively improves sleep duration and quality among residents, or whether residents fully recognize the value of sleep and prioritize it as a means of safeguarding their well-being.

Moreover, there is currently a lack of global comparative research focusing specifically on shift work, sleep knowledge, and sleep health among medical trainees. Existing studies tend to be limited in scope or geography. For example, the study by Trockel et al. was conducted solely in the US, limiting its generalizability beyond the US context (32). Another study, conducted in Colombia during the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic (33), focused on sleep quality, insomnia, and stress among healthcare workers, but its findings are constrained by both the specific national context and the unique circumstances of the pandemic. Similarly, an umbrella review of studies from Africa explored insomnia, anxiety and depression among healthcare professionals during COVID-19 (34), but did not address other dimensions of sleep, such as sleep knowledge or the impact of shift work.

Our study aims to address this gap by conducting a comprehensive, multi-dimensional assessment to better understand how on-call duties and sleep knowledge are associated with sleep quality and the overall well-being of medical trainees globally. By identifying and comparing cross-national differences, we hope to uncover systemic challenges and contribute to the development of more effective shift scheduling strategies that enhance the health, performance, and safety of medical trainees worldwide.

Objective

In summary, the benefits of simply restricting working hours are inconclusive, and the effectiveness of additional measures, including sleep health education, remain largely unclear. To gain a more comprehensive understanding of medical trainees’ shifts and sleep patterns, we plan to conduct a global multicentred survey study, aiming to exploring the associations of shift frequency and duration, as well as the level of knowledge about sleep hygiene and sleep disorders, on the well beings of medical trainees across different specialties. The findings will help inform the development of more appropriation-call schedules and contribute to improving education on sleep medicine.

On the other hand, as a global multicenter survey study, we also aim to explore differences in sleep scheduling, sleep quality, and health metrics among medical trainees across diverse cultural and linguistic contexts.

Methods

Type of research/study design

The study is registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT06706453). It is a cross-sectional, global survey designed in English via a shared link on REDCap (14.0.42, 2024; Vanderbilt University, Nashville, TN, USA). The survey has been developed by sleep experts from America, Europe, and Africa. It will be conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. It has been approved by the King’s College London ethics committee (No. MRA-23/24-45277), and will be obtained from the respective local ethics committees of other participating sites. Convenience sampling will be used to select participants, aiming to capture a broad range of medical trainees from various healthcare settings. The survey will be distributed through accessible channels, including medical institutions and professional networks, ensuring ease of participation for eligible individuals. To increase the generalizability and comparability of the data across diverse healthcare settings and sociocultural backgrounds, the survey will be developed in several languages, including English, French, Spanish, and possibly Mandarin. Currently, it is approved in English, with additional language approvals pending (French, Spanish, and Chinese are planned). The feasibility of conducting the survey in different regions is currently under evaluation and subject to obtaining the necessary approvals.

The survey will be expected to take approximately 40–45 minutes to complete. Consent will be obtained via an initial mandatory question with a hard stop (yes/no), and only continues if ‘yes’ is selected. No further informed written consent will be required from the participants due to the survey’s anonymous nature. The participants in the survey are able to discontinue at any time and without providing any reasons.

Outcome measures

The primary outcomes of this study are the effects of on-call and shift work on medical trainees’ sleep patterns, as measured by the total sleep duration (delta hours on vs. off call).

Secondary outcomes include the knowledge [Visual Analog Scale (VAS) 0–10 points] and perception of sleep (burnout symptoms, sleepiness, as measured by the ESS 0–24 points, insomnia related symptoms, as measured by the ISI, 0–28 points, social issues when on-call), attitudes, and practices related to sleep, and their impact on the sleep patterns (delta on vs. off call), and the overall self-perceived health of medical trainees (VAS 0–10 points).

There are about 133,700 doctors in secondary and tertiary care in the UK, and 37,300 General Practitioners (GPs) in primary care (35). We assumed a total of 171,000 doctors currently practice medicine in the UK (N). The expected margin of error was set at 0.05 (error margin, alpha). This estimation returned a result of participants for the online survey of n=399; however, given that only a portion of about 50% of doctors are trainees the number was reduced by 50% (n=200). This study initially involved a UK-based sample. Considering the planned expansion to other countries such as the US, Nigeria, and France, as well as potential inclusion of additional countries like India and China, we increased the estimated sample size by a factor of 10, resulting in a target of approximately 2,000 participants.

Data collection, management, and monitoring

The survey will be conducted entirely online with participants being anonymous. Data fields are not mandatory, so answers can be left blank before moving on. Patients are explained the background, content, procedure and data collection of at the beginning of the survey.

As sponsor/co-sponsor, King’s College London, UK has a responsibility to ensure the safety and integrity of the information collected about participants. Specialist teams within King’s College London will continually assess and ensure that data are held in the most appropriate and secure manner on password protected institutional computers, in agreement with General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) compliance.

Completed or partially completed surveys will be included if the following minimal dataset is obtained:

Data will be excluded if any of the above criteria has not been achieved or provided. False and non-sensible entries will be monitored and deleted prior to commencing the analysis.

The verified database will be frozen prior to analysis, and a time-stamped version will be utilised for the final analysis. After the dataset is locked, authorized researchers will have access to the data and can download it for further analysis.

Biases and solutions

Response bias may arise if only a certain cohort of medical trainees participate in the survey, potentially leading to imbalances in departments, training year, gender, or availability of the survey among the enrolled trainees. To mitigate this bias, we plan to implement a diversified recruitment strategy, utilizing multiple channels such as social media, email, and in-person announcements to reach a broader audience. Additionally, we will regularly monitor participant demographics throughout the recruitment period to promptly identify and address any imbalances, including targeted outreach to underrepresented groups.

Social desirability bias may arise when participants tend to answer questions based on societal expectations rather than their true thoughts. To minimize this bias, the protocol adopted an anonymous format.

Moreover, we did not utilize objective measurements such as actigraphy or smart wearable devices to assess participants’ sleep cycles. Instead, self-reported data were used, which may introduce recall bias and subjective inaccuracies.

Missing data is another important source of bias in the protocol. To mitigate this and ensure the integrity of the data, we designed the questionnaire to encourage participants to complete the survey as much as possible. For the subsequent analysis, we will primarily rely on the actual data collected.

Data analysis/statistical considerations

Descriptive data will be provided for each column/answer. Continuous variables that follow a normal distribution will be expressed as mean ± standard deviation, while non-normally distributed data will be presented as median (interquartile range, 25–75%). Categorical data will be displayed as number and percentage. For comparison of multiple groups, either analysis of variance (ANOVA) or non-parametric tests will be used, based on normality. Correlation analysis will utilise Pearson correlation or Spearman correlation depending on normality. Multivariate analysis will employ logistic regression or multiple linear regression. The mixed effects regression models will be applied to analyse the impact of different durations of on-call shifts on the health of medical trainees. SPSS 23.0 Statistics for Windows (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA) will be used for statistical analysis. The level of significance will be assumed for any P value <0.05.

Discussion

Although much of the literature highlights the negative impact of shift work on health, the evidence regarding the effectiveness of interventions such as working hour restrictions, schedule adjustments, sleep hygiene education, or pharmacological treatments remains limited and heterogeneous. This protocol outlines a study designed to gather data on the status of medical trainee’s shift work frequency and durations, knowledge of sleep, the impact of shift work on their sleep, and the effects of these factors on their physical and mental health. By combining subjective assessments, such as self-reported sleep quality and sleep hygiene habits, with standardized sleep scales, such as the ESS and the ISI, this protocol aims to provide a comprehensive assessment of the sleep health of medical trainees, which will be a basis for exploring and modelling measures to improve sleep quality while being on-call.

Currently, there is a lack of large-sample, international multi-centered studies in this field. This study protocol will be implemented in collaboration with four countries, including the USA, UK, Nigeria, and France, which allows for a global evaluation of sleep patterns, on-call work practices, and sleep hygiene knowledge among medical trainees across different cultures and regulatory environments. Reasonable and efficient scheduling of on call shifts often needs to consider multiple factors, such as individual, cultural, and social influences. The current survey, which will cover different languages and countries, will provide us with valuable information, including, but not limited to, the variations in working hours, on-call schedules and sleep patterns of medical trainees. Although implementing this protocol alone may not be sufficient to create a globally adaptable and actionable scheduling system for on call rotas, it will significantly contribute to raising a culture of awareness of the issues involved and help to develop a foundation for an evidence-based policy for shift work.

Different studies have indicated limited knowledge of sleep medicine among medical trainees (38-40). Although there have been studies proposing sleep hygiene education as a non-pharmacological intervention to mitigate the negative effects of shift work, the impact of enhanced sleep hygiene education on the sleep quality and quality of life of medical trainees during on-call periods remains uncertain (41,42). The aim of the survey is to assess the relationship between medical trainees’ knowledge and their sleep and health. Consideration of the findings of the survey will provide evidence on whether improving sleep education can effectively impact on relevant outcomes and support the development of tailored sleep education programs for medical trainees.

As to the pharmacological interventions to improve the sleep of medical trainees, several medicines including melatonin, antihistamines, and non-benzodiazepine and modafinil have been studied (43-46). Modafinil has been approved in the US for shift work disorder (47,48). However, there is still limited evidence regarding the aspects of pharmacological interventions that improve sleep quality in medical trainees, including indications, timing of use, selection of medication types, dosage, and side effects. Therefore, this protocol also includes a question related to the use of sleep medications among medical trainees (e.g., use of sleeping tablets at night or stimulants by day, or other drugs).

Additionally, the anticipated outcomes of this study will contribute to the development of targeted policies that balance work-hour restrictions with practical strategies to improve sleep health in medical trainees. A reduction of working hours may not be sufficient to improve sleep and well-being among trainees, nor patient care, as seen in some previous studies (30,31,49). In some cases, it could even be harmful, as it may lead to reduced time for education, disruption of patient care and increased frequency of shifts due to shorter on-call durations (30,31,49). Therefore, strategic scheduling is essential to balance the workload, the quality of medical education and patient care, although this may be particularly challenging in the context of medical training.

However, some research suggests that guaranteed rest time between two shifts (e.g., >11 hours) in the work schedule can improve the sleep of healthcare workers (50,51). Other studies have proposed a strategy of 12-hour night shifts, instead of the traditional 24-hour on-call schedule with multiple night shifts should be arranged together, allowing medical trainees to gradually adjust their sleep schedules over consecutive nights (52). It is also recommended to provide nap breaks to keep up alertness and work efficiency (52). Strategic scheduling, however, remains highly complex, as it needs to account for many factors including commute time, family care and responsibilities, and physical and social activity. For example, in areas where the distance between home and workplace is considerable, frequent shift changes may not be feasible. This survey, as outlined in the protocol includes questions about commuting time for subsequent analysis. Moreover, the demand for nap breaks may differ significantly across cultures and lifestyles. The survey also incorporates questions specifically related to napping practices and a range of other dimensions. By gathering more comprehensive data, the protocol aims to establish a foundation for evidence-based policy implementation regarding shift schedules.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. Firstly, the study does not use objective sleep or cognitive function assessment measures (e.g., smart wearable devices or specific modules) and relies on self-reported data (53,54), which may lead potential bias and more miss data. Secondly, this study does not employ regular or daily assessments, but rather rely on a one-time cross-sectional evaluation. The results reflect average values over a period, which lacks details and be subject to recall bias. Thirdly, the cross-sectional design limits the ability to infer causation between sleep hygiene knowledge and sleep quality. Lastly, differences in cultural attitudes toward work hours and sleep may influence responses, which could affect generalizability.

In conclusion, this survey will provide essential data to improve our understanding of the relationship between on call and medical trainees’ sleep and health, which provides a basis for developing more acceptable on-call systems, strategical shift schedules, and tailored sleep education. Striking a balance between safeguarding of physical and mental health of medical trainees, ensuring high-quality patient care, and maintaining the standard of medical education must be prioritized when considering future medical work, and sleep plays an essential role in this.
