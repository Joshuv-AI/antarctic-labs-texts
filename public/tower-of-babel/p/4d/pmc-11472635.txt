HIV and body mass index are associated with prolonged corrected QT interval among people with drug-resistant tuberculosis on bedaquiline-containing regimen in Uganda
Authors: Lodiong Jackson Dumo Lodiong, Jonathan Izudi, Timbine Amos, Bright Twinomugisha, Nicholas Kulaba, Boniface Amanee Elias Lumori
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Corrected QT (QTc) prolongation among Ugandan patients with drug-resistant tuberculosis.•Bedaquiline regimen induced QTc prolongation in Uganda.•The prevalence of QTc prolongation in Uganda.

Introduction

In 2022, there were projected to be 410,000 cases of multidrug-resistant or rifampicin-resistant tuberculosis (MDR/RR-TB) worldwide. Just 175,650 people were diagnosed and began treatment in 2022, which is still less than the pre-pandemic figure of 181,533 persons in 2019 and represents nearly two of every five individuals in need [1]. The treatment of drug-resistant tuberculosis (DR-TB) is challenging due to a high pill burden, high cost, intolerability, and long treatment duration, among others [2,3]. In 2019, the World Health Organization recommended the use of an oral bedaquiline-containing regimen for the treatment of people with DR-TB. This recommendation was based on programmatic data that show that people with MDR/RR-TB treated with an all-oral, bedaquiline-containing regimen (BDQ-LFX/MFX-ETO-E-Z-Hh-CFZ) had better treatment success than unfavorable outcomes, including a significant decrease in loss to follow-up [4]. Evidence from a meta-analysis reports that a bedaquiline-containing regimen has a high culture conversion rate and a marked reduction in mortality rates, including significantly better treatment outcomes, in people with DR-TB compared with the older kanamycin- or capreomycin- and moxifloxacin-based standard regimens [[5], [6], [7]].

Bedaquiline has a concentration-dependent bactericidal effect and it is widely distributed in tissues due to its high affinity (>99%) for plasma proteins, including a long terminal half-life of approximately 5 months [8]. Its main metabolic pathway is N-demethylation, thus creating an M2 metabolite catalyzed by the cytochrome P450 (CYP) isoenzyme 3A4. Bedaquiline and M2 metabolite cause corrected QT (QTc) interval prolongation by blocking the potassium channels of the cardiac myocytes, thus delaying repolarization. QTc interval prolongation—the unusual interval between the time the heart muscles contract and relax—potentially leads to arrhythmia such as torsade de pointes and sudden death [9]. With a long half-life, bedaquiline and M2 metabolite trigger QTc interval prolongation with a potential persistence after treatment completion [10].

Although the bedaquiline-containing regimen shows better outcomes among people with DR-TB, QTc interval prolongation is a major concern. Among many factors, BMI is one of the factors that has been linked to QTc interval prolongation among people with DR-TB on a bedaquiline-containing regimen. Ardhianto et al. [11] found that a high BMI was correlated with QTc interval prolongation among people receiving a bedaquiline-containing regimen.

Barvaliya et al. [12] reported that people with lower BMI who received treatment with a bedaquiline-containing regimen have increased rates of QTc prolongation. In Uganda, more than 95% of people with DR-TB had been treated with a bedaquiline-containing regimen when the regimen became available in 2020. Despite concerns regarding the safety of the bedaquiline-containing regimen on cardiac performance among people with DR-TB, there is limited information on the magnitude of prolonged QTc interval and the associated factors among people with DR-TB in Uganda.

We investigated the magnitude and factors associated with QTc interval prolongation among people with DR-TB aged 15 years and older at three tertiary-level hospitals in Uganda. This information is critical in improving the quality of clinical care provided to people with DR-TB in Uganda.

Methods and materials

Study setting and data source

Data for this study were retrospectively abstracted from the medical records of people with DR-TB who received a bedaquiline-containing regimen between January 2020 and December 2021 at three regional referral hospitals, namely, Mbarara, Mulago, and Masaka. All three sites have a DR-TB clinic that uses the same data collection system and performs electrocardiogram at baseline (before treatment) and during the follow-ups: at 2 weeks and monthly until the DR-TB treatment is completed. All the electrocardiogram examinations were performed by trained clinicians and a senior cardiologist proofread all prolonged QTc interval cases. We retrieved all available records (census) from people with DR-TB treated with bedaquiline-containing regimens, namely, all-oral, individualized, and long regimens. The data were abstracted by three trained research assistants and routinely examined for accuracy, completeness, and consistency by the principal investigator. We included sociodemographic, clinical, and laboratory data from participants who had a microbiologically confirmed DR-TB, had a baseline electrocardiogram, and were aged ≥15 years. We excluded those without baseline or follow-up electrocardiogram data.

The sociodemographic data include the age in completed years but later categorized as 15-24, 25-34, 35-44, and ≥45 years; sex (male or female); HIV serostatus (positive or negative) including anti-retroviral therapy (ART) status and ART regimen for those with HIV; and history of alcohol consumption and smoking (yes, no, and not reported).

Clinical data included the category of DR-TB (new, relapse, treatment failure, and loss to follow-up), pulmonary tuberculosis (TB) (yes or no), and drug sensitivity test profile (MDR/RR-TB was defined as TB caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis strains that are resistant to at least rifampicin and isoniazid [13], pre-extensively DR-TB defined as MDR-TB plus resistance to a fluoroquinolone or a second-line injectable agents [14], and extensively DR-TB defined as TB that is resistant to any fluoroquinolone and at least one of three second-line injectable drugs [capreomycin, kanamycin, and amikacin]), in addition to multidrug resistance [13]), baseline weight in kilograms, and baseline height in meters.

We computed and reported the participants’ body mass index (BMI) as weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared and categorized the BMI as underweight (BMI <18.5 kg/m2), normal (BMI 18.5-24.9 kg/m2), and overweight/obese (BMI ≥25 kg/m2). Laboratory data included the baseline hemoglobin, aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), creatinine, potassium, and bilirubin. We categorized the baseline hemoglobin as ≥11.6 g/dl for females versus ≥13 g/dl for males to depict no anemia and <11 g/dl for females versus <13g/dl for males to depict anemia. Creatinine level was categorized as normal if ≤114.9 umol/l and high if >114.9 umol/l. AST was considered normal if it was 8-33 U/l and high if >33 U/l. ALT was normal if it was 4-36 U/l or high if >36 U/l. Sodium was considered normal if it was 135-145 mmol/l, low if <135 mmol/l, and high if >145 mmol/l. Potassium was considered normal if it was 3.5-5.5 mmol/l, low if <3.5 mmol/l, and high if >5.5 mmol/l. Bilirubin was taken as being normal if it was in the 1.71-20.5 umol/l range and high if it was >20.5 umol/l.

Study design

We used the retrospectively collected data to design a matched case-control study, with cases as participants with QTc interval prolongation and controls as those without QTc interval prolongation in 1:1. The study design was appropriate because QTc interval prolongation was uncommon or a rare event in the retrospective cohort of people with DR-TB treated with a bedaquiline-containing regimen. Here, cases and controls were identified from the same cohort so the information obtained represents the exposure experience of all controls and cases in the cohort. Furthermore, the design controls for sampling and measurement biases associated with traditional case-control or cohort designs, and the matching was important in removing confounding by known factors, such as age and sex.

Study variables

QTc interval prolongation was the primary outcome and it was defined as an increase in QT interval by 60 milliseconds in electrocardiogram from the baseline or ≥500 milliseconds during follow-up for men and women [15], computed using the Fridericia's method as QTc = QT/(RR)1/3 [16]. Participants with a prolonged QTc interval were considered as cases, whereas those with normal QTc interval were taken as controls. The primary exposure was the participants’ baseline BMI measured in kg/m2. The potential covariates included sex, age, alcohol consumption and smoking history, hypertension, HIV, obesity, anemia, the type of bedaquiline-containing treatment regimens, drug sensitivity test profiles, electrolyte levels (sodium and potassium), creatinine, AST, ALT, and bilirubin level.

Statistical methods

Sample size and statistical power

We analyzed retrospectively collected data for a defined period so all available records were retrieved. We did not compute a sample size rather than the study's statistical power based on a priori hypothesis. We hypothesized that there would be a difference in the mean BMI between people with MDR-TB with QTc interval prolongation (cases) compared with those without QTc interval prolongation (controls). We tested this hypothesis in a two-sided t-test at a 5% level of statistical significance for a 1:1 case-to-control ratio.

Data analysis

We computed the mean and SD for numerical data when normally distributed and the median and interquartile range when skewed. Frequencies and percentages were used to summarize categorical data. We matched the cases and controls 1:1 using their propensity scores, generated in a logit model with the exposure as QTc prolongation (cases vs controls) and the covariates as the age categories, sex, and history of TB treatment, as well as TB disease classification. Participants with prolonged QTc interval (cases) who failed to get appropriate matches from those with normal QTc interval (controls) based on propensity scores were excluded. We saved the matched data set and then used it for the bivariable and multivariable analyses.

In the bivariable analysis, we cross-tabulated the covariates with the outcome (cases vs controls) and tested differences in the outcome with categorical using the two-sided chi-squared test if the cell frequencies were large (≥5) or Fisher's exact test if it was small (<5).

Mean differences between cases and controls were tested using the two-sided Student's t-test if the data were normally distributed, otherwise the Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used. To determine the factors independently associated with being a case compared with a control, we fitted a multivariable conditional logistic regression analysis, adjusting for the matched pairs and clinically relevant and statistically significant variables. We reported the unadjusted and adjusted odds ratio (OR) and the 95% confidence interval (CI). The analysis was performed in Stata version 15 and R version 4.2.1 (2022-06-23 ucrt).

Ethical issues

We received ethical approval from the Mbarara University of Science and Technology Faculty Review Committee (MUST-2022-443). Privacy and confidentiality were upheld with unique codes.

Results

Study profile

There were 245 records available between January 2020 and December 2021. Of the records, 152 were from Mulago National Referral Hospital, 49 were from Mbarara Regional Referral Hospital, and 44 were from Masaka Regional Referral Hospital. We excluded 92 records with reasons: 12 were not on a bedaquiline-containing regimen, 21 had no baseline electrocardiogram data, 55 had no follow-up electrocardiogram, and four were under 15 years of age. Of the 153 eligible records, 39 (25.5%) had prolonged QTc interval. Statistical power

Based on our hypothesis, we found that the study had 87.7% statistical power in a two-sided t-test at a 5% level of statistical significance for a case-to-control ratio of 1:1 (30 cases and 30 controls), considering a mean BMI among cases and controls as 20.9 ± 2.8 kg/m2 vs 18.8 ± 2.4 kg/m2 (P = 0.014), respectively.

Discussion

In people with DR-TB treated with a bedaquiline-containing regimen, we determined the magnitude and the risk factors for prolonged QTc interval using data from three large referral hospitals in Uganda. We found that 25.5% of the participants had prolonged QTc interval and increasing BMI is associated with a higher likelihood of QTc interval prolongation, whereas HIV is associated with a lower likelihood of QTc interval prolongation. Compared with the 10-20% of otherwise healthy individuals having QTc values outside the normal range at a point in time [17], the magnitude of prolonged QTc interval among people with DR-TB on a bedaquiline-containing regimen in this study is high. Previous studies conducted in South Africa [18] and India [19] report that three in 10 people with DR-TB on a bedaquiline-containing regimen had a prolonged QTc interval, which is nearly comparable with our finding.

The association of increasing BMI with a higher likelihood of QTc prolongation is comparable to the Indonesian study which analyzed the side effect of QTc interval prolongation in a bedaquiline regimen in DR-TB [11]. A previous study found a connection between the M2 metabolite of bedaquiline and QTc prolongation [20]. However, it has also been observed that those with higher BMI have lower bedaquiline Cmin and, consequently, lower M2 metabolite concentrations and, hence, lower risk of QTc interval prolongation [21]. Nevertheless, increasing BMI, a risk factor for obesity, is potentially associated with elevated plasma fatty acid concentrations that might have led to prolonged cardiac repolarization and correlation with QTc interval prolongation, including higher plasma adrenaline concentrations [22].

Our finding that HIV infection confers protection against QTc interval prolongation is unique. First, all the people with HIV-associated DR-TB are on ART due to the Universal Test and Treat policy implementation in Uganda since 2017 [23]. ART plays a crucial role in treating HIV but the effect on QTc interval prolongation varies. One study reported that individuals who are ART-naive with HIV have a higher frequency of QTc interval prolongation than those on ART and healthy controls (individuals without HIV) [24]. The factors contributing to QTc interval prolongation in people living with HIV (PLHIV) include high viral load, low clusters of differentiation 4 count, chronic inflammation, and exposure to certain medications such as protease inhibitors, as well as opioids, antipsychotics, antibiotics, and antifungals, that are mainly used as adjunct medications [25]. The concentration of bedaquiline and its M2 metabolite is known to increase with protease inhibitor use, potentially raising the risk of toxicity [26]. However, the participants in this study were on the reverse transcriptase inhibitors comprising tenofovir, disoproxil, fumarate, and lamivudine, combined with dolutegravir, an integrase inhibitor.

Another study on PLHIV with suppressed viral load showed that older age, female sex, and higher BMI are associated with QTc interval prolongation [27], highlighting the importance of monitoring QTc interval among PLHIV, especially before initiating medications known to prolong QTc intervals. Therefore, although ART may not completely protect against QTc prolongation, its use appears to be associated with a lower risk of QTc interval prolongation compared with people with HIV who are not on ART. Overall, because all the individuals with HIV in this study are on life-long first or second-line ART, our finding of ART reducing the occurrence of QTc interval prolongation compared with individuals without HIV is plausible. We did not find an association between QTc interval prolongation and AST, contrary to studies conducted in South Africa [18] and India [19].

Our findings have implications for clinical practice, guidelines, and research. The findings suggest a need to identify people with DR-TB at a high risk for QTc interval prolongation through routine anthropometric measurements, such as weight, height, and BMI, and a need to monitor people with DR-TB for QTc interval prolongation. The need for collaboration with professionals in the field of cardiology in managing people with DR-TB cannot be over-emphasized. Developing TB and HIV treatment guidelines that allow the careful assessment of people with DR-TB on a bedaquiline-containing regimen regarding the risk for QTc interval prolongation is important.

Study strengths and limitations

To the best of our knowledge, our study is among the first to investigate QTc interval prolongation and the associated factors among people with DR-TB on a bedaquiline-containing regimen in Uganda. Our results provide preliminary data to inform future research into QTc interval prolongation among people with DR-TB on bedaquiline-containing regimens in Uganda and elsewhere. Data analyzed are from three large DR-TB treating hospitals in Uganda; therefore, the findings provide a good picture of QTc interval prolongation among people with DR-TB in Uganda. The use of the World Health Organization–recommended Fridericia's formula to define QTc interval prolongation adds another strength. Fridericia's formula is the best measure of the QT correction rate and its potential to predict mortality is good [28]. Cases and controls were from the same population, hence minimizing selection biases. The study limitations include a small sample size, although all available data from January 2020 (the time when the bedaquiline-containing regimen was fully introduced in Uganda) and December 2021 were analyzed. However, this limitation is mitigated by the study's adequate statistical power (87.7) based on the a priori hypothesis. Laboratory data, namely, hemoglobin, AST, ALT, creatinine, potassium, and bilirubin were categorized to allow comparison between participants with normal vs abnormal values. The categorization of numerical data leads to loss of information and reduced statistical efficiency but is important for clinical care decisions.

Conclusion and recommendations

Among people with DR-TB on a bedaquiline-containing regimen, the prevalence of QTc interval prolongation was 25.5%. Increasing BMI is associated with a higher likelihood of QTc interval prolongation, whereas HIV is associated with a lower likelihood of QTc interval prolongation. There is a need for routine anthropometric measurements to identify individuals with DR-TB at a high risk for QTc interval prolongation, including monitoring them for a prolonged QTc interval. The TB and HIV treatment guidelines for individuals with DR-TB on bedaquiline-containing regimens should include risk assessment for prolonged QTc interval.
