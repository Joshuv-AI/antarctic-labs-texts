Variations in genotype- and position-dependent seed tuber sprouting linked to skin morphology in white Guinea yam
Authors: Olajumoke Olaleye, Kohtaro Iseki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWhite Guinea yam (Dioscorea rotundata) is a staple tuber crop in West Africa; however, its vegetative propagation is constrained by irregular dormancy and unpredictable sprouting, which reduce planting efficiency and yield stability. This study examined how tuber skin and cortex morphology influence sprouting across 20 D. rotundata genotypes selected from an F₁ cross-population with a relatively uniform genetic background. Tubers were divided into head, middle, and tail parts to evaluate positional effects. Periderm thickness, cortex thickness, skin hardness, and surface roughness (fractal dimension) were quantified, and sprouting was assessed under screenhouse conditions. Analysis of variance showed that genotype and tuber position accounted for 30% and 20% of the total variation in sprouting time, respectively. Head sections had thinner periderm and generally sprouted earlier than middle or tail sections. Regression analysis indicated that thinner periderm and higher fractal dimension were associated with earlier sprouting, whereas greater skin hardness was associated with delayed emergence; cortex thickness showed inconsistent effects across tuber positions. These morphological traits explained 10–25% of the variation in sprouting time, indicating that additional physiological and genetic factors dominate sprouting responses. Overall, tuber skin morphology exerts a minor yet measurable influence on dormancy breaking and early sprouting in D. rotundata. Practically, our results suggest that selecting planting materials from head positions and using periderm thickness and surface texture as screening traits can help reduce sprouting variability and improve establishment, providing actionable targets for breeding and seed-yam management aimed at more uniform sprouting and efficient seed-yam production.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-43709-z.Keywords: Tuber dormancy, Sprouting, Skin morphology, Yam, Dioscorea rotundataSubject terms: Genetics, Plant sciences

Introduction

White Guinea yam (Dioscorea rotundata) is a staple tuber crop in West Africa, playing a central role in food security and rural livelihoods and accounting for more than 94% of global yam production1; FAOSTAT2. Despite its importance, yam cultivation and seed-yam production are constrained by considerable variation in tuber dormancy and sprouting, which disrupts planting schedules, reduces stand establishment, and ultimately affecting yield stability3,4. Yams are propagated vegetatively using tubers that serve both as food and planting material. After harvest, tubers enter dormancy for approximately 30–150 days depending on genotype and harvest conditions, resulting in unpredictable sprouting among planting materials5–7. Improving sprouting uniformity is therefore essential for efficient seed-yam systems and mechanized planting.

Sprouting marks the transition from dormancy to active growth and is characterized by the emergence of shoots and roots from dormant buds5,7. Unlike potato (Solanum tuberosum) and many other tuber crops, yam tubers lack clearly identifiable surface nodes and buds, except near the head corm where visible shoot meristems can differentiate during storage. In local practice, cut tuber pieces (setts) that retain an intact skin surface capable of producing a shoot are used as planting material. Although setts typically show no external signs of sprouting at planting, sprouting loci and bud primordia usually differentiate on the skin surface of the proximal (head) side within a few days of planting8.

This transition is regulated by complex genetic, hormonal, and environmental interactions5,9,10. Temperature and moisture act as key triggers, together with hormonal regulation involving gibberellins, cytokinins, and abscisic acid11. Previous research has largely focused on environmental or hormonal manipulation of dormancy, such as the use of plant growth regulators, gamma irradiation, or electric fields3,12–15. Although there are methods that can shorten dormancy, they are often costly, environmentally unsafe, or impractical for smallholders. A more sustainable approach lies in exploiting natural morphological variability within the tuber that may influence sprouting behavior.

Beyond physiology, intrinsic structural features of the tuber, particularly the periderm (skin) and cortex layers, may also influence sprouting. The periderm serves as a protective barrier affecting gas diffusion, moisture regulation, and respiration around the bud region15, whereas the cortex facilitates nutrient mobilization and water transport during sprout emergence. Yam tubers consist of three major positions: the head, middle, and tail. These sections differ in nutrient reserves, hormonal concentrations, and tissue compactness, resulting in variable sprouting potential16,17. Understanding these structural differences is important for improving sprouting uniformity and predicting their transition from dormancy to growth.

However, the influence of tuber skin and cortex morphology on sprouting in yam remains poorly documented. Most studies emphasize environmental regulation or physiological control of dormancy, with limited attention paid to structural traits that may underline sprouting differences among genotypes and tuber sections. A clearer understanding of these internal features could offer morphological indicators that are useful for breeding and selecting genotypes with reliable sprouting responses. In this context, we investigated the relationships between tuber skin and cortex traits and sprouting responses in white Guinea yam across selected genotypes and tuber positions. We hypothesized that variation in these morphological traits contributes to shaping dormancy release and sprouting variability among genotypes and tuber positions.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and growth conditions

This study was conducted from December 2023 to June 2024 at the International Institute of Tropical Agriculture (IITA), Ibadan, Nigeria (7° 29′ N, 3° 54′ E). A total of 20 genotypes of D. rotundata (Supplementary Table S1) were selected from F1-derived clonal progenies developed by a bi-parental cross between TDr97/00777 and TDr04-219. The F1 seedlings were obtained in 2013, followed by vegetative propagation, and the genotype selection was based on consistent sprouting performance observed over a three-year period (2021–2023). F₁ progenies were chosen instead of genetic resources, as they offer a controlled genetic background derived from known parental lines, allowing clearer evaluation of trait variability and genotype-specific effects on sprouting behavior. Tubers of these genotypes were harvested in December 2023 at the experimental field in IITA. Tubers weighing approximately 1–2 kg were cleaned and stored in a yam barn under controlled conditions (25–28 °C and a relative humidity of 90%) from December 2023 to April 2024.

Each tuber was cut transversely into two sections to show the periderm, cortex, and starchy flesh. The transverse cuts were further divided into three positions: head, middle, and tail. The head, middle, and tail positions were identified based on tuber polarity, with the head representing the proximal end attached to the vine and the tail representing the distal position (Fig. 1). A fruit hardness tester (FR-5105, A-GAS, Tokyo, Japan) was used to measure the skin hardness of each tuber position. A total of 180 samples (20 genotypes × 3 tuber positions× 3 tubers) were analyzed. One-half of each transverse cut was used for skin morphological evaluation, while the other half was used for sprouting evaluation.

Evaluation of skin morphology and sprouting in head, middle and tail positions of white Guinea yam tuber.

Skin morphological evaluation

Tissue blocks containing the cortex and periderm layers were obtained from each of the transverse tuber cuts. Using a razor blade, 2-µm thick sections were prepared by hand microtome (THK, Kenis, Osaka, Japan) sectioning with a razor blade. Each microscope section was photographed using an optical microscope (AS-Z40-L, MicroAdvance, Osaka, Japan) with a CCD camera (AS-300 C-D, MicroAdvance, Osaka, Japan). A total of 540 section images corresponding to 20 genotypes, 3 tuber positions, 3 microscope sections, and 3 tubers were obtained. The thickness of corky periderm and cortex layer, and fractal dimension of the corky periderm (Fig. 1) were determined for each image using image analysis software (ImageJ version 1.43u; National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA). All ImageJ measurements were calibrated with a scale bar.

The fractal dimension (D) was quantified using the Fractal Box Count tool in ImageJ based on the box-counting method (Supplementary Figure S1). Briefly, the binarized image (foreground = target object) was covered with a square grid of box size ε, and the number of boxes containing at least one foreground pixel, , was counted over a series of ε values. ε was varied from 200 to 1000 pixels, and the relationship between and ε was evaluated on a log–log scale. D was estimated as the negative slope of the linear regression of against logε: . A larger fractal dimension value indicates that the surface had more irregularities and greater roughness.

Sprouting evaluation

The sprouting evaluation was conducted in a screenhouse at the IITA under controlled conditions, with average minimum and maximum temperatures of 25 °C and 28 °C, respectively, and relative humidity maintained at 90%. Tuber setts weighing 100 g were cut from the head, middle, and tail positions of the second transverse half of each tuber to eliminate the effect of sett size on sprouting18. The setts were treated with a fungicide to prevent microbial infections and planted on May 15, 2024, in black plastic pots (12 cm diameter, 10 cm height) filled with heat-sterilized peat substrate KLASMAN TS3, consisting of 40% white peat moss and 60% black peat moss. The substrate was moistened until fully saturated. Each sett was placed on top of the substrate and covered with tissue paper to minimize moisture loss. There was a total of 180 pots (one sett per pot), consisting of 20 genotypes, 3 tuber positions, and 3 tubers. The number of days to sprouting was recorded as the day when the first visible bud reached approximately 0.5 cm in length for each pot, corresponding to stage 5 of the sprouting process defined by Onwueme8. The total shoot fresh weight and total root fresh weight were evaluated 10 weeks after planting. When multiple buds emerged from a sett, shoot and root fresh weights were summed across all shoots and roots produced by that sett; however, most setts produced only a single bud.

Statistical analysis

Multiple comparison analyses were performed to detect significant differences in days to sprouting among the accessions and in skin morphological traits among the tuber positions (head, middle, and tail). The effects of genotype and tuber position on the days to sprouting and the skin morphological traits were evaluated using an analysis of variance (ANOVA). The F-values and percentage of contribution (ρ) were calculated from the type-II sum of squares and mean squares, and then compared among factors. Results

In the sprouting experiment, most setts produced a single visible bud. Although multiple buds occasionally emerged from a single sett, such cases were infrequent, and secondary buds showed minimal elongation and did not develop into independent shoots, indicating strong apical dominance in D. rotundata. The analysis revealed substantial variation in sprouting time among the 20 genotypes and among tuber positions (head, middle, and tail). Across genotypes, setts derived from the head position sprouted earlier (mean, 18.4 days after planting) than those from the middle and tail positions (27.6 and 28.5 days, respectively), though the magnitude of this earliness varied among genotypes (Fig. 2). In 17 of the genotypes, the head position had an earlier mean sprouting date relative to the middle and/or tail positions; however, in six of these genotypes, positional differences were not statistically significant. The positional responses could be broadly grouped into patterns such as head-dominant (five genotypes: head < middle ≈ tail) and graded polarity (four genotypes: head < middle < tail), highlighting genotype-dependent variation in the strength of tuber-position effects. Collectively, these results indicate that tuber position influences sprouting behavior, but the magnitude and statistical detectability of this effect depend on genotype-specific characteristics.

To determine whether early sprouting was associated with enhanced plant growth, correlations between sprouting time and growth vigor measurements (shoot and root fresh weight) were analyzed. These relationships were weak and statistically non-significant for all tuber positions, with correlation coefficients ranging from R2 = 0.01 to 0.11 (Fig. 3). Thus, earlier sprouting did not translate into greater biomass accumulation, suggesting that shoot sprouting and subsequent growth vigor are controlled by distinct physiological processes.

Discussion

Variations in tuber skin morphology, including periderm thickness, fractal dimension, and skin hardness, showed only a modest association with sprouting time among yam genotypes but may still contribute to early dormancy release. The associations of periderm thickness and fractal dimension with earlier sprouting suggest that a more developed and textured skin layer may support conditions favorable for dormancy breaking, consistent with reports that periderm properties can influence gas exchange, water retention, and respiration during the transition to active growth15,20. Conversely, the negative association between skin hardness and sprouting suggests that more rigid skin may hinder bud emergence, as reported for other root and tuber crops21. However, because skin traits explained only 10–25% of the variation in sprouting time, additional factors (e.g., internal water status and endogenous physiological regulation) are likely to govern much of the remaining variability.

While genotype strongly influenced sprouting, the consistently earlier sprouting of head positions compared to the middle or tail positions indicates a clear position-dependent effect within tubers. Similar positional differences have been reported in yam and other tuber crops and have been linked to variation in the developmental status of meristematic tissues and the distribution of internal reserves along the tuber axis8. The significant genotype × position interaction further suggests that positional effects are not uniform across genotypes, likely reflecting genotype-specific internal characteristics associated with dormancy release and meristem activity22,23. In addition, the distribution of enzyme activity and hormonal balance can vary among head, middle, and tail positions12,24, and such internal gradients may differ among cultivars due to variation in storage patterns and tissue organization6. Consequently, tuber position can influence sprouting time in a genotype-specific manner.

The absence of a significant correlation between sprouting earliness and early growth vigor in observed in the present study indicates that earlier emergence does not necessarily translate into greater shoot or root development. Post-sprouting growth in yam appears to depend more on metabolic activity and resource availability than on the timing of sprout emergence itself25,26. This decoupling is plausible because processes that trigger sprouting, such as hormonal signaling and periderm-related constraints, may differ from those required to sustain vigorous growth, including continuous nutrient mobilization and efficient canopy photosynthesis. Consistent with this view, Orkwor et al.26, reported that shoot vigor and tuber yield are more closely associated with genotype-specific growth potential than with dormancy duration.

Nevertheless, earlier sprouting can still confer agronomic advantages by extending the effective growing period and accelerating canopy establishment, which can contribute to higher tuber yield27,28. In our previous study, earlier sprouting was found to be associated with earlier flowering and higher tuber yield29. Because late flowering can increase competition for assimilates between reproductive development and tuber enlargement, delayed sprouting may indirectly reduce yield by shifting subsequent phenology. In the present study, sprouting dates differed by more than 20 days (Fig. 4), corresponding to nearly a one-month delay in vegetative development for plants derived from middle or tail tuber positions relative to head positions. Yield losses associated with such growth delays are expected to be larger in female than in male plants, likely because females intrinsically flower later29. Taken together, these findings suggest that seed-tuber skin morphology may influence tuber yield indirectly through its association with sprouting timing and downstream phenology; however, additional studies that jointly evaluate skin traits, sprouting, flowering, and final yield across environments are needed to quantify this pathway and clarify the underlying mechanisms. One limitation of this study is that these traits explained only 10–25% of sprouting variation and were assessed under screenhouse conditions; further validation across field environments is needed.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that variation in tuber skin and cortex morphology is associated with sprouting behavior in D. rotundata. Thinner periderm and higher surface roughness (fractal dimension) tended to be associated with earlier sprouting, whereas greater skin hardness was associated with delayed sprout emergence. Sprouting time was also strongly influenced by genotype, and plants derived from head positions consistently sprouted earlier than those from middle or tail positions. In contrast, sprouting earliness was not related to subsequent shoot or root vigor, indicating that earlier emergence alone does not necessarily confer stronger early growth. Collectively, these findings clarify the contribution of intrinsic tuber morphology to dormancy breaking and sprouting variability and identify periderm thickness and fractal dimension as practical phenotyping traits linked to sprouting performance. From an applied perspective, prioritizing head-derived planting materials and incorporating these skin traits into selection criteria may help improve sprouting uniformity and seed-yam production efficiency, providing actionable targets for breeding and seed-yam management.
