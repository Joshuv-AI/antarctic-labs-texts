Solving multi-depot closed-path multiple traveling salesman problem using k-means++ hierarchical clustering and neural combinatorial networks
Authors: Chun-Sheng Zhao, Li-Pei Wong, Chey Fung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Traditional methods for solving the multiple traveling salesman problem (MTSP), such as metaheuristics, face a well-known trade-off between computational speed and solution quality. Neural combinatorial networks (NCNs) offer an efficient alternative for addressing traveling salesman problems (TSPs) but lack a native decomposition mechanism for MTSP and suffer from performance decay at scale. To mitigate these limitations, this study proposes a two-phase optimization algorithm, k-means++ hierarchical clustering with neural combinatorial networks (KHC-NCN), tailored for the symmetric Euclidean 2D multi-depot closed-path MTSP (MDCP-MTSP). The k-means++ clustering fulfills the inherent requirement of MTSP to group cities, while the end-to-end NCN enables rapid and high-quality optimization of the segmented subproblems, thus accelerating the overall solution process. The proposed coarse-to-fine hierarchical clustering and solution merging of subproblem tours alleviate the performance degradation typically observed when NCNs are generalized to large clusters. Comprehensive experimental evaluations on typical symmetric Euclidean 2D TSPLIB instances illustrated KHC-NCN’s superior optimization quality and reliability compared to numerous traditional metaheuristics. Furthermore, across 44 test cases derived from 11 TSPLIB instances with varying numbers of salesmen and scaling to 1060 nodes, KHC-NCN produced shorter tours in nearly 73% of cases, achieved orders-of-magnitude mean end-to-end wall-clock speedups, and simultaneously improved solution quality by an average of at least 3% over traditional clustering-based algorithms. In the same benchmark, KHC-NCN demonstrated competitive solution quality against LKH-3 and outperformed the neural solver POMO, both applied to the MDCP-MTSP setting through problem reformulation, while achieving substantial computational speedups. Finally, an MDCP-MTSP benchmark on all available symmetric Euclidean 2D TSP instances from the TSPLIB has been established, offering a reference for future research within this domain.

Introduction

The multiple traveling salesman problem (MTSP), a generalization of the classic traveling salesman problem (TSP), concerns coordinating multiple salesmen to collectively visit a set of cities. This work focuses on a particularly relevant variant: the multi-depot closed-path MTSP (MDCP-MTSP), where salesmen start from and return to multiple distinct depots, collectively visiting all cities exactly once while minimizing total travel distance. This variant models a wide range of real-world applications, including transportation and delivery1–3, multi-robot task scheduling4,5, disaster management6,7, wireless sensor network data collection8,9, among others10–12. As an NP-hard problem that introduces the added complexity of optimally partitioning cities among depots and salesmen, developing efficient and effective solution methods for MDCP-MTSP remains a challenge13.

The traditional approaches for MDCP-MTSP are primarily adapted from the broader MTSP field. Heuristics, particularly the metaheuristics used in practice, form the cornerstone of contemporary approaches. Algorithms such as genetic algorithms (GA)14–16, ant colony optimization (ACO)17,18, and particle swarm optimization (PSO)15 address the computational intractability of exact methods19,20 with practical runtime. However, unlike theoretical approximation algorithms which offer provable guarantees on solution quality, these practical heuristics provide no such assurances and may sometimes fail to find satisfactory solutions at all. Beyond this fundamental limitation, they still incur substantial computational overhead for large instances due to iterative search, and require costly re-optimization for each new problem instance.

A popular strategy to mitigate the complexity of MDCP-MTSP is a “divide-and-conquer” approach that leverages clustering21. This involves leveraging algorithms (e.g., k-means) to partition the problem into smaller, more manageable sub-tours, which are then solved using traditional optimizers like GA, ACO or PSO2,9,12,22–25. This two-phase paradigm, akin to strategies employed in other domains such as multilevel image segmentation26,27, effectively reduces computational complexity and has demonstrated improved performance. However, the performance ceiling of these methods is ultimately bounded by the capabilities of the underlying metaheuristic solver. As such, they inherit the fundamental trade-offs of traditional methods: the pursuit of higher solution quality often comes at the cost of increased computation time, and vice-versa.

Recently, neural combinatorial networks (NCNs) have emerged as a disruptive paradigm for solving combinatorial optimization problems (COPs)28–37. Trained via reinforcement or supervised learning, NCNs can generate high-quality solutions for problems like TSP in an end-to-end manner with millisecond-level inference times. Their ability to generalize across problem instances of the same distribution without retraining presents a compelling advantage over instance-specific metaheuristics. This suggests a potent alternative for the “solver” phase in a divide-and-conquer framework. However, critical barriers prevent their direct application to complex problems like MDCP-MTSP: firstly, NCNs are primarily designed for single-agent routing problems, and thus lack mechanisms to capture interactions and coordination among multiple agents38; secondly, they exhibit performance degradation when applied to problem sizes larger than those encountered during training39,40.

To bridge the specific limitations of both traditional and neural network approaches, a two-phase algorithm for the symmetric Euclidean 2D MDCP-MTSP, termed KHC-NCN, is proposed, which integrates k-means++ 41 hierarchical clustering with neural combinatorial networks. Here, “hierarchical” denotes a coarse-to-fine, top-down splitting strategy that progressively partitions the original instance into NCN-suitable subproblems; it does not denote an agglomeration step after k-means++ to enforce contiguous depot regions, nor does it refer to a multi-resolution design for route balancing. The key innovation is this hierarchical splitting and subsequent solution merging mechanism, through which subproblems are systematically constructed to match the effective operating range of a pre-trained NCN and their optimized sub-tours are merged into a complete solution. Through this mechanism, the scalability limitation of NCNs is addressed, allowing their rapid, high-quality inference to be leveraged while the performance degradation typically suffered on large problems is mitigated.

Related work

Traditional metaheuristics for MTSP

Early and prevalent approaches to MTSP are dominated by population-based and nature-inspired metaheuristics. As cataloged in the upper portion of Table 1, GA have been a cornerstone. Carter et al.14 introduced a specialized two-part chromosome encoding to reduce redundancy in the MTSP solution space. This inspired further algorithmic diversification and hybridization. For instance, Changdar et al.16,17 designed genetic-ant colony optimization hybrid algorithms for MTSP under uncertain environments, while Jiang et al.18 developed the ant colony-partheno genetic algorithm (AC-PGA) to handle MTSP with constraints on the number of cities per salesman. Comparative studies, such as the one by Zhou et al.15 that evaluated PGA, PSO, and IWO for MDCP-MTSP, underscore the field’s exploration of diverse metaheuristic families.

These algorithms are celebrated for their flexibility and global search capabilities. However, their application to MDCP-MTSP exposes inherent scalability challenges. The combinatorial explosion is not only from tour sequencing but also from the need to solve the city-to-depot assignment problem. This often necessitates problem-specific encoding schemes and operators, and the iterative convergence process becomes computationally prohibitive for instances exceeding a few hundred nodes. Decomposition-based methods for MTSP

Recognizing the scalability limits of monolithic metaheuristics, the field has increasingly adopted a “divide-and-conquer” paradigm. This strategy is particularly well-suited for MDCP-MTSP, as clustering naturally maps to the fundamental requirement of assigning cities to multiple depots. The approach typically uses algorithms like k-means to partition the problem into smaller, single-salesman TSP sub-tours, which are then solved efficiently with dedicated optimizers. The diversity and prevalence of this two-phase framework are evident in the lower portion of Table 1. Steven et al.24 demonstrated that combining k-means or agglomerative clustering with ACO yields superior solutions for multi-depot MTSP (MMTSP). Similarly, Xu et al.22 combined the k-means algorithm with GA and proposed a two-phase metaheuristic for addressing MTSP with workload balance. Silva et al.2 modeled route scheduling for self-driving cars as MTSP and proposed hybrid approaches using k-means in conjunction with GA and ant colony system (ACS), outperforming GA, ACS, and PSO without any pre-processing. Zheng et al.12 formulated the cleaning and disinfection task for dockless shared bikes as an extension of the multi-depot MTSP and developed the k-GA-adjustment algorithm to generate solutions. The algorithm first combines k-means clustering with a GA (k-GA) to find the lower bound solution, then reallocates bike assignments from overloaded staff to those with lighter workloads to achieve balanced task distribution. Zhang et al.9 modeled path planning tailored for multiple unmanned aerial vehicle (UAV) data collection within internet of things (IoT) scenarios as an MTSP and presented a two-phase algorithm. They first applied the k-means algorithm to cluster IoT terminals, and then solved each independent TSP using an improved GA enhanced with 2-opt operators, which locally refines inferior individuals before the genetic operations are performed. Considering UAV flight time, the coverage radius, and the ground-to-air system’s achievable data rate, Ibrahim et al.23 proposed an algorithm for optimizing multiple UAV-based aerial IoT gateway flying routes based on k-means and PSO, reducing flight distance and energy consumption. Tong et al.43 proposed a stepwise approach based on k-means and the donkey and smuggler algorithm (K-DSA) to solve MTSP and achieved outstanding performance on TSPLIB instances. While looking for a better solution, improving the execution efficiency of the algorithms has also attracted the attention of scholars. Baydogmus25 proposed a parallel k-means elitist ACO approach for TSP/MTSP, achieving a 30% efficiency improvement through GPU parallelization. Other two-phase approaches based on clustering have also been explored in recent studies6,11,44,45.

While clustering effectively reduces problem complexity and has demonstrated improved performance, the final solution quality of these methods remains constrained by the optimization capabilities of the second-phase metaheuristic. This creates a fundamental dependency where improvements in tour length inevitably require the computational expense of extensive iterative search, mirroring the core trade-off of the underlying algorithms.

Neural combinatorial networks for COPs

In recent years, neural combinatorial networks have emerged as a promising paradigm for addressing traditional COPs through end-to-end learning. A summary of representative neural combinatorial networks is presented in Table 2. The field was catalyzed by two milestone studies. Vinyals et al.28 introduced the pointer network (Ptr-Net), which adapted the sequence-to-sequence architecture to output permutations of the input sequence. Trained via supervised learning, Ptr-Net demonstrated that neural networks could directly generate feasible solutions for problems like the TSP, offering rapid inference and encouraging generalization across instances. An important leap was made by Bello et al.29, who addressed the bottleneck of requiring optimal solution labels by training Ptr-Net using reinforcement learning (RL), specifically the REINFORCE algorithm. Building on these foundations, the field has progressed through a series of architectural and algorithmic refinements. Subsequent research has focused on enhancing model architectures, including the simplified encoder by Nazari et al.30 for reduced complexity, the integration of graph neural networks by Ma et al.32 for better structural reasoning, and the highly influential Transformer-based models, notably the attention model by Kool et al.31 that set a state-of-the-art standard, and the subsequent refinement by Bresson et al.35. These approaches, along with others33,36,37, have successfully been extended to a wider range of COPs, including the knapsack problem and various vehicle routing problem (VRP) variants. However, as the problem scales in Table 2 reveal, these studies have predominantly focused on small instances (typically \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$n \le 100$$\end{document}), with only a few works32,37 exploring \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$n \ge 500$$\end{document}.

Despite these advances, the literature reveals a persistent gap: these models remain architecturally designed for single-route construction and exhibit limited generalization to large problem sizes, making them unsuitable for direct application to MDCP-MTSP.

Preliminary

Multi-depot closed-path MTSP

The multi-depot closed-path MTSP is a variant of MTSP43,47. In this variant, given a set of cities \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$V=\{v_1,v_2,...,v_n\}$$\end{document} and m salesmen where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$m < n$$\end{document}, each salesman starts from a different city (depot) in V, collaboratively visit all the cities, and return to their respective starting depots. Each city, except the depots, must be visited exactly once by only one salesman. The objective is to minimize the total distance traveled by all m salesmen. Neural combinatorial optimization

This section provides the technical background on neural combinatorial optimization necessary to understand the proposed method. The core approach is end-to-end learning34, in which a deep neural network is trained to directly generate solutions for a specific type of COP from its input instances. This approach draws an analogy between COPs and the classic sequence-to-sequence learning paradigm in machine translation, where the input is a sequence of problem features (such as a sequence of city coordinates), and the output is a sequence representing the solution (such as the ordered list of cities to visit). Therefore, the encoder-decoder architecture originally developed for machine translation has become a widely adopted framework for constructing end-to-end models. A typical procedure for solving TSP using this approach is illustrated in Fig. 1.Fig. 1A typical procedure of the end-to-end approach for solving TSP. The process begins with the “Input” stage, where the coordinates of cities are provided. The “Encoder” stage transforms the input data into internal representations. The “Decoder” stage, illustrated with multiple time steps, sequentially constructs the solution path. Finally, the “Output” stage delivers the optimal solution to the TSP.

For a given sequence of cities \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$V={\{v_i\left( x_i,y_i\right) \}}_{i=1}^n$$\end{document}, the encoder takes the coordinates \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left( x_i,y_i\right)$$\end{document} as input and embeds them into high-dimensional vectors \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$H=\{h_1,h_2,\ldots ,h_n\}$$\end{document}. These embeddings could be considered as the high-dimensional feature representations of the cities extracted by the encoder. After that, the embeddings are fed into the decoder, and the decoder calculates a probability distribution for the node to visit next based on the encodings and the current state of the partial tour, such as the visited nodes, the unvisited nodes, the starting node, the previously visited node, etc. Then, the predicted probabilities serve as pointers, guiding the selection of the node to build the solution. The decoder constructs the solution in an autoregressive manner, as shown in Fig. 1, selecting one node \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\pi _i$$\end{document} from unvisited nodes at each decoding step \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$t=i$$\end{document} based on previous decisions until the complete solution is constructed. Finally, the decoder generates and outputs a complete tour \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tau \left( \pi _1,\pi _2,\ldots ,\pi _n\right)$$\end{document} as the solution.

This model can be trained using either supervised learning or reinforcement learning. Once trained, the model can solve a problem by performing an encoding and decoding procedure on the input data through a simple forward calculation of the neural network. Compared to traditional iterative optimization methods, this approach can instantly deliver a solution to a COP without iterative searching, enhancing solving speed. Furthermore, it can be applied to solve unseen instances of the same type of problem with similar characteristics, thus demonstrating generalization capabilities.

Proposed method

This study introduces a two-phase optimization framework that integrates k-means++ hierarchical clustering with neural combinatorial networks to address MDCP-MTSP. In the first phase, the k-means++ algorithm is employed to partition all cities into m first-level clusters, with each cluster assigned to a specific salesman. However, since k-means++ does not account for factors such as node distribution density, it easily results in unbalanced clusters where some contain a disproportionately large number of nodes. This issue is particularly pronounced in large-scale MTSPs with a limited number of salesmen, where clusters with a large number of nodes are common. These oversized clusters not only complicate the associated routing sub-problems but also exceed the effective operating range of end-to-end models, whose performance typically degrades when applied to instances larger than those encountered during training. To mitigate this challenge, a second-level k-means++ clustering procedure is imposed to further subdivide these oversized clusters. This ensures that each subproblem remains within a manageable size, allowing the end-to-end model to maintain high solution quality. Each resulting cluster, whether from the first or second level, is independently formulated as a standard TSP instance and solved using a pre-trained model. For first-level clusters that are decomposed into multiple sub-clusters, a solution merging mechanism is introduced to integrate the individual sub-tours into a unified tour for the corresponding salesman.

In the first-level clustering stage, k-means++ is utilized to partition all cities of MDCP-MTSP into m first-level clusters. A cluster refinement step is then applied to adjust singleton clusters to ensure each cluster contains at least two nodes. Subsequently, the algorithm enters a hierarchical solving phase. For each first-level cluster, the algorithm checks whether the number of nodes does not exceed the threshold \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(1+\alpha )\times s$$\end{document}. If the condition holds, the cluster is solved directly by the neural network solver, producing a tour for that first-level cluster. Otherwise, the oversized cluster is further divided into second-level clusters using k-means++, where the number of second-level clusters is determined as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$k_i=\lceil n_i/s\rceil$$\end{document} (rounded up), where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$n_i$$\end{document} is the node count of the ith oversized first-level cluster. After refinement to eliminate singleton clusters, each second-level cluster is independently solved by End2end_solver, yielding corresponding sub-TSP solutions. Once all second-level solutions are generated, a merging operation integrates them into a single tour representing the corresponding first-level cluster. A critical remaining consideration when employing a pre-trained neural combinatorial network is its ability to generalize to unseen problem instances. The performance of the NCN solver within the framework hinges on the similarity between the TSP sub-problems it receives and the distribution of instances on which it was trained. Through reinforcement learning, the NCN learns a policy for constructing solutions sequentially. This policy encodes generally effective strategies for the problem type, rather than memorizing specific instances, which is the foundation of its generalization capability. Prior studies31,35 have established that this generalization is most effective when instances share key characteristics with the training data, including problem type, underlying data distribution (e.g., points uniformly sampled from a unit square), a comparable problem size, and a consistent distance metric. Performance degradation is typically observed when these characteristics are violated, including when a model is applied to much larger instances and different data distributions39,48.

The proposed KHC-NCN framework is explicitly designed to enforce these characteristics, thereby ensuring the robust performance of the neural solver. This is achieved through several deliberate design choices. (1) Problem type alignment: the clustering phase decomposes the original MDCP-MTSP into multiple standard TSP sub-problems, ensuring that the NCN is exclusively applied to the problem type for which it was trained. (2) Coordinate scale normalization and distribution alignment: the coordinates of each TSP sub-problem are min-max normalized to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$[0,1]^2$$\end{document}, eliminating the influence of absolute scale and projecting the data into the model’s expected coordinate space. (3) Problem size control: the hierarchical clustering mechanism constrains the number of cities in each cluster, keeping each TSP sub-problem within the range where the NCN performs effectively. (4) Consistency of the distance metric: the entire algorithm operates on the Euclidean distance metric, matching the NCN’s training setup. By integrating these mechanisms, the proposed approach ensures that the neural solver operates on well-formed, normalized sub-problems that closely match the characteristics of its training data, thus securing reliable and high-quality performance.

Cluster refinement in KHC-NCN

The objective of cluster refinement is to resolve singleton clusters. When a cluster contains only the depot node itself, it forms a degenerate route that visits no customers, thereby requiring necessary modifications. Distinct resolution strategies are applied based on the clustering level. For first-level clusters, the number of clusters m must equal the number of traveling salesmen and remain invariant, as each cluster is exclusively assigned to one salesman. To preserve this constraint, a donor-based adjustment strategy is employed: for each singleton cluster, the algorithm identifies the nearest node from eligible donor clusters (containing at least three nodes) and transfers that node to the singleton cluster. For second-level clusters, an absorption strategy is employed: it iteratively selects singleton clusters, locates the nearest cluster based on node proximity, and merges the singleton node into that target cluster. After each singleton resolution operation, the centroids of the relevant clusters are updated. The pseudocode for this process is shown in Algorithm 2.

To address this, the problem of determining the merging order is formulated as a TSP, where the nodes correspond to the centroids of the sub-clusters. To balance computational efficiency and solution quality, the farthest-insertion heuristic is employed to solve the TSP and thereby determine the merging order.

Experimental studies

Experimental setup

Datasets

To assess the practical advantages of the proposed KHC-NCN algorithm, a series of comparative experiments are conducted on various problems formulated for MDCP-MTSP. TSPLIB is a well-known repository of benchmark instances for the traditional TSP and its variants. However, there is no established open benchmark for assessing MTSP algorithms. The prevalent practice involves adapting instances from TSPLIB by configuring multiple salesmen, thus enabling the evaluation of algorithmic performance in MTSP scenarios15,20,43,50. Therefore, all test data of this study are sourced from TSPLIB and consist exclusively of symmetric 2D problems.

Implementation and platform

The algorithm KHC-NCN is implemented in Python. The k-means++ component is facilitated by the KMeans class from scikit-learn51. The class offers several hyperparameters to customize the clustering behavior. Specifically, the parameter init is set to k-means++ to activate k-means++ clustering. The parameter max_iter, specifying the maximum number of iterations for updating cluster assignments, is set by default to 300. To enhance the efficiency of the clustering computation, parallel processing is enabled by setting the parameter n_jobs to -1, allowing multiple initializations to run concurrently using all available CPU cores. The software platform used in this study is PyTorch 1.11.0, Python 3.8, and Ubuntu 20.04. The hardware comprises an Intel Xeon Platinum 2.5 GHz 12-core processor with 40 GB RAM.

Neural TSP solver component

For the end-to-end neural network component, rather than proposing a new architecture, the attention model (AM) of Kool et al.31 is adopted as the TSP solver for each cluster. AM is a Transformer-based encoder-decoder model. The encoder is a stack of Transformer encoder blocks. Each block consists of two sublayers: a multi-head attention layer that performs message passing among nodes and a position-wise fully connected feed-forward layer. Each sublayer employs a residual connection and batch normalization. Unlike the standard Transformer encoder, positional embeddings are omitted to preserve permutation invariance. The decoder is an autoregressive pointer that uses masked attention and a context vector summarizing the partial tour, including the embeddings of the first visited node, the last visited node, and a mean-pooled graph summary. A binary mask prevents selecting visited nodes. At each step, the decoder attends to the encoded nodes to select the next city, producing a permutation without revisits.

In Kool et al.’s study, AM is trained with policy-gradient REINFORCE using a greedy rollout baseline. The baseline follows the self-critical idea of Rennie et al.52 and is updated periodically: at evaluation checkpoints, the current best policy is frozen and used to compute a deterministic greedy rollout. This baseline serves as a variance-reduction control variate, stabilizing gradient estimates and encouraging improvements over the model’s own greedy performance without requiring optimal labels. Separate models are trained at fixed sizes. Specifically, AM-TSP20, AM-TSP50, and AM-TSP100 are trained on 20-node, 50-node, and 100-node instances, respectively, with node coordinates sampled uniformly in \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$[0,1]^2$$\end{document} and Euclidean edge costs.

Under greedy decoding, AM-TSP20/50/100 evaluated on 20/50/100-node problems sampled uniformly from \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$[0,1]^2$$\end{document} yield average gaps of 0.34% / 1.76% / 4.53%, respectively. Therefore, AM-TSP20 is adopted as the cluster-level solver, since it delivers the best solution quality at its target scale. Prior studies39,40 examined AM’s scalability and generalization. While AM performs well at its trained size, performance degrades under size shift and distribution shift. For example, on larger problems sampled in the same way as training (uniform in \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$[0,1]^2$$\end{document}), applying AM-TSP20 to 200-node instances yields an average optimality gap of 66.22%39. Under the distribution shift to TSPLIB Euclidean instances with sizes 51–200, the average gap is 22.66%39. These results motivate the design: cluster sizes are capped to keep the NCN within a reliable regime, and hierarchical clustering is employed to scale to large global instances.

Unless otherwise noted, the public AM-TSP201 checkpoint released by Kool et al. is used as-is. No fine-tuning is performed. The model runs on CPU in single-thread mode and constructs solutions via greedy decoding. The parameter s denotes the ideal cluster size used to form subproblems for the neural cluster-level solver. Since AM-TSP20 is adopted as the embedded solver, s is set to 20 to steer subproblems towards the solver’s most effective operating range. If the embedded solver is replaced (e.g., AM-TSP50/AM-TSP100 or another model trained at a different scale), s should be adjusted accordingly to match the solver’s training scale. The cluster-size relaxation factor \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha$$\end{document} is set to 0.9 based on preliminary tests, as it provided a good balance between high solution quality and stability across test cases.

Baseline algorithms

To facilitate a comprehensive comparison with established algorithms, the proposed algorithm is first evaluated on problem instances studied in previous studies by Zhou et al.15 and Tong et al.43. These studies focus on MDCP-MTSP and investigate a range of classic algorithms, such as PSO, PGA, ABC, K-DSA and others, thereby providing solid baselines for evaluating the proposed approach. Nonetheless, differences in software implementations and hardware platforms preclude a direct comparison of computational efficiency across these studies. Additionally, these investigations have primarily focused on small-scale problems, with a notable dearth of research into larger-scale scenarios. Therefore, to comprehensively evaluate the time efficiency and solution quality of the proposed algorithm on larger-scale problems, three representative clustering-based two-stage baselines, namely k-GA, k-ACO, and k-PSO, are implemented within the same software and hardware environment as the proposed KHC-NCN. These algorithms integrate k-means-based problem decomposition with classical population-based optimization methods and have been widely adopted for solving MTSP variants 5,9,12,22,25,53,54. Comparative experiments across a variety of larger-scale problems are conducted to assess the performance of KHC-NCN relative to these methods.

To further evaluate the proposed algorithm against state-of-the-art solvers from different paradigms, LKH-3 and POMO are additionally employed as baseline methods. LKH-3 is an expert-designed heuristic solver for TSP and is widely regarded as one of the most powerful solvers in practice. POMO is a representative end-to-end neural combinatorial optimization framework and has demonstrated outstanding performance on standard TSP benchmarks. Since neither LKH-3 nor POMO directly supports the MDCP-MTSP formulation considered in this study, problem adaptations are applied to enable their application. Both solvers are evaluated on problem instances transformed from the original MDCP-MTSP formulation. The specific adaptation strategies and their implications are discussed in the Comparison with LKH-3 and POMO section. For LKH-3, the solver is accessed via the elkai2 Python library (2.0.1), utilizing its default settings. For POMO, the publicly available pre-trained POMO-TSP203 model is employed with greedy decoding and no augmentation.

For consistency with the experimental protocol, all algorithms implemented and evaluated in this study, including the proposed KHC-NCN and all re-implemented baseline methods, are independently executed 30 times on each test instance, and the reported solution quality and computational time correspond to the average over these independent runs, unless otherwise stated. For comparative methods whose results are directly adopted from existing literature and cannot be re-executed under the same experimental environment, the reported values are taken as published and are used solely for reference rather than for statistical comparison.

Evaluation metrics

To evaluate the performance of the proposed KHC-NCN algorithm in solving MDCP-MTSP, four quantitative performance metrics are employed: win rate, distance, percentage deviation (PD), and computational time. The win rate reflects how often an algorithm outperforms other comparative algorithms across multiple test cases, serving as an indicator of reliability and consistency. The distance metric quantifies the total tour length of the solution, indicating the overall solution quality by calculating the cumulative distance traveled by all salesmen. In this study, the Euclidean distance is adopted, and the values between cities are calculated following the TSPLIB benchmark’s nearest integer rounding approach for consistency. Let x[i] and y[i] denote the coordinates of city i. The distance \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$d_{ij}$$\end{document} between city i and city j is computed as follows:7\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \begin{aligned} xd&=x[i]-x[j], \\ yd&=y[i]-y[j], \\ d_{ij}&= \left\lfloor \sqrt{xd \cdot xd + yd \cdot yd} + 0.5 \right\rfloor , \end{aligned} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sqrt{\, \cdot \,}$$\end{document} denotes the square root function, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\lfloor \cdot \rfloor$$\end{document} represents the floor function, which rounds down to the nearest integer. Adding 0.5 before applying the floor function rounds the result to the nearest integer, in accordance with TSPLIB’s specification.

The PD quantifies the relative improvement of the proposed algorithm over a comparative algorithm and is defined as8\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} PD = \frac{Distance_{comparative}-Distance_{proposed}}{Distance_{comparative}} \times 100\%. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}This definition is consistently applied across all tables, where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Distance_{comparative}$$\end{document} refers to the mean tour length obtained by a comparative algorithm, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Distance_{proposed}$$\end{document} denotes the mean tour length obtained by the proposed KHC-NCN algorithm. A positive PD indicates superior performance by the proposed algorithm, with larger values signifying a greater degree of improvement in solution quality.

Additionally, the time metric (in seconds) serves as an indicator of computational efficiency. The end-to-end wall-clock runtime per run is reported for each (instance, m, algorithm) test case, including all algorithm-specific steps required to produce the final solution (e.g., normalization, clustering/decomposition, problem transformation and tour splitting, second-stage refinement, and merging when applicable). Only one-time overheads that are amortized across repeated runs are excluded, namely dataset file I/O for all methods and model loading and initialization for neural methods. Notably, in some cases, the absence of time information in comparative studies makes time-based comparisons unfeasible.

To ensure a robust and rigorous comparison of algorithmic performance, the Wilcoxon signed rank test 55,56 is employed as the primary method to assess whether the performance differences between the proposed algorithm and each baseline are statistically significant. The Holm-adjusted p-values are reported to quantify the strength of statistical evidence. For completeness, a paired t-test is also conducted under parametric assumptions. To complement statistical significance testing and quantify the magnitude of observed differences, effect sizes are reported accordingly: Cliff’s \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\delta$$\end{document} for the non-parametric Wilcoxon analysis, and Cohen’s d for the parametric t-test.

Comparison with k-GA, k-ACO and k-PSO

The study by Tong et al. 43 demonstrates that incorporating k-means-based decomposition into traditional metaheuristics can enhance their performance in solving MTSP. Motivated by this finding, this section compares the proposed KHC-NCN with three representative clustering-based two-stage baselines, namely k-GA, k-ACO, and k-PSO, implemented under the same software and hardware environment.

GA, ACO, and PSO are all population-based iterative algorithms whose solution quality and computational cost are governed by a fundamental time-quality trade-off. Increasing the population size and the number of iterations generally improves solution quality but increases computational time, making direct comparisons with the proposed algorithm less meaningful. To ensure a balanced and fair comparison under a unified experimental protocol, the following experimental settings are adopted for all subsequent experiments in this section. The population size is fixed to 30 and the maximum number of iterations is set to 500 for all three algorithms. Early stopping is not employed. Based on commonly adopted parameter settings and prior empirical studies, the GA parameters are configured as follows: selection ratio of 0.2, mutation rate of 0.05, and crossover rate of 0.9. For ACO, according to Kefi et al.’s study57, the parameters are set to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha =1$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta = 5$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\rho = 0.35$$\end{document}, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Q = 1000$$\end{document}. For PSO algorithm, based on Zhou et al.’s study15, the inertia weight is set to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$w = 1.1$$\end{document} and both acceleration coefficients are set to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$c_1 = c_2 =2$$\end{document}. Comparison with LKH-3 and POMO

This subsection compares the proposed KHC-NCN against two state-of-the-art solvers: the TSP heuristic LKH-3 and the neural combinatorial optimizer POMO. As neither method directly supports the MDCP-MTSP formulation, the problem setup is adapted to accommodate the technical constraints of each solver.

For LKH-3, an MTSP-to-TSP transformation is employed following the general reduction principle commonly used in the MTSP literature 63. Specifically, given a set of depot nodes for an MDCP-MTSP instance, the inter-depot distances in the cost matrix are assigned a sufficiently large constant (e.g., \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$1.0 \times 10^7$$\end{document}) to prevent direct transitions between depots. The resulting TSP instance is then solved using LKH-3. The obtained tour is subsequently segmented at depot nodes to recover multiple closed tours, each associated with a depot. In contrast, POMO operates solely on node coordinates and computes Euclidean distances implicitly, which renders distance-matrix-based MTSP-to-TSP transformations inapplicable. As a result, both depot and customer nodes are treated as regular nodes of a TSP instance and solved using POMO, after which the resulting tour is segmented at depot nodes to recover multiple tours. Unlike LKH-3, where MTSP-specific constraints are explicitly embedded through problem reformulation, this approach involves no dedicated modeling of the multi-tour structure and performs only a global TSP-level optimization, which may lead to degraded solution quality. Given the high computational efficiency of the end-to-end POMO solver, a second-stage refinement is therefore applied to each segmented sub-tour using POMO, incurring only marginal computational overhead. This additional local optimization compensates for the quality loss introduced by the direct problem simplification and yields a more robust and meaningful baseline.

With these reformulations, LKH-3 and POMO can be applied to the MDCP-MTSP, but the reformulation may also affect their performance. For LKH-3, the conversion and segmentation overhead is negligible, whereas solution quality is mainly influenced by a structural mismatch: after splitting a global TSP tour, each segment must be closed into a cycle, and the added closure edge is not guaranteed to yield the shortest cycle for that segment, which can introduce suboptimality. For POMO, global TSP-level optimization is combined with a second-stage local refinement on each split segment to obtain a more meaningful baseline. This refinement benefits solution quality but incurs additional runtime overhead.

In the MDCP-MTSP formulation considered in this study, depot locations are not pre-specified. Accordingly, a set of depot nodes is sampled from the node set for each experimental run, which introduces run-to-run performance variability for LKH-3 and POMO. However, consistent with the experimental protocol, each test instance is independently solved 30 times using different sampled depot sets. Averaging over these independent runs mitigates this variability and provides a stable estimate of the expected performance of LKH-3 and POMO. In terms of solution quality, across the 44 tests, KHC-NCN delivers tour lengths that are on average 3.20% shorter than LKH-3 and 21.45% shorter than POMO. Moreover, 43 out of 44 test instances fall into the Instances of large advantage category. These results collectively confirm that KHC-NCN delivers superior solution quality with high statistical confidence. Comprehensive benchmarking on TSPLIB instances

To establish a reliable reference for future studies, KHC-NCN is tested on all available symmetric Euclidean 2D (EUC_2D) TSP instances from the TSPLIB dataset with various numbers of salesmen, and the results are reported. To our knowledge, this is the first complete benchmark conducted on the full set of these instances, providing a comprehensive basis for evaluating and comparing algorithms within this domain.

According to the official documentation 64, TSPLIB contains 78 TSP instances in the symmetric EUC_2D category. However, lin318 and linhp318 have identical coordinates and are treated as a single instance in this study. Consequently, the 78 EUC_2D instances are converted into 77 unique MDCP-MTSP instances. The city counts of these instances range from 51 (eil51) to 18512 (d18512), and they are categorized into three scales: small, medium, and large. Instances with fewer than 300 cities are classified as small-scale, those with 300 to 2999 cities as medium-scale, and those with 3000 or more cities as large-scale. Discussion

The performance of the proposed KHC-NCN, especially its advantages over traditional metaheuristics, is not merely an empirical outcome but a direct consequence of its “structure-and-infer” design. This foundational paradigm shift, which intelligently decomposes problems to leverage modern neural solvers, mechanistically explains the observed performance gains.

First, the improvement in computational efficiency stems from a fundamental shift from iterative search to structured inference. Traditional metaheuristics rely on costly, stochastic search loops. In contrast, KHC-NCN replaces the bulk of this computation with a highly efficient process: k-means++ clustering structures the problem, and the pre-trained neural solver then performs rapid, deterministic inference on the resulting subproblems. This architectural difference is the primary driver of the observed orders-of-magnitude speedup. Second, the high solution quality is secured by the hierarchical clustering mechanism, which actively manages the effective operating range of the neural component. By second-level partitioning the problem, it ensures that every sub-task presented to the neural solver is tailored to its capabilities. This design directly counteracts the known performance degradation of neural solvers on larger instances, allowing them to consistently perform as reliable policies. The quality of the final solution is thus a direct result of solving a set of well-sized problems optimally. Finally, the algorithm’s robustness is a product of its reduced stochasticity. While traditional population-based methods have randomness deeply embedded in their core operations, the stochasticity in KHC-NCN is largely confined to the initial clustering. The subsequent neural inference is deterministic, leading to more stable and reproducible outcomes compared to the inherent fluctuations of metaheuristics.

Failure cases on TSPLIB suggest two main limitations of KHC-NCN. First, the embedded neural solver is trained on uniformly sampled Euclidean instances, while TSPLIB exhibits different spatial distributions and structures; this distribution shift can degrade generalization and yield suboptimal sub-tours. Second, the decompose–optimize–merge strategy currently relies on largely local merging decisions, which may miss globally optimal reconnections. These issues are most visible on small instances where strong, well-engineered metaheuristics can reach near-optimal solutions and where a few suboptimal reconnections constitute a noticeable fraction of the total cost (see Tables 3 and 5). On larger instances, the number of subproblems and merges increases, so subproblem-level errors may accumulate, and the impact of imperfect merging can be amplified, contributing to gaps versus strong local-search methods such as LKH-3 on some cases (see Table 9). Future work will explore domain-adaptive training or fine-tuning and more globally informed merging to improve robustness under distribution shift.

Conclusion

By integrating k-means++ hierarchical clustering with neural combinatorial networks, this paper introduces a two-phase optimization algorithm for the multi-depot closed-path multiple traveling salesman problem. Leveraging the inherent requirement of grouping and optimization in MDCP-MTSP, this method fully exploits the stability and effectiveness of k-means++, along with the rapid and high-quality solution capabilities of neural combinatorial networks. Additionally, the hierarchical clustering and solution merging mechanisms alleviate the performance degradation of NCNs when handling large-scale clusters, enhancing the method’s adaptability. Extensive experimental evaluations demonstrated substantial improvements over numerous comparative methods, including traditional metaheuristics and clustering-based approaches, as well as the neural TSP solver POMO, yielding superior optimization quality, reliability, and computational efficiency. For small-scale problems, the algorithm can provide high-quality solutions almost in real-time. For larger-scale problems, it maintains and amplifies its performance advantage, reducing computation costs. Furthermore, this study has established a benchmark with all available symmetric EUC_2D TSP instances of TSPLIB, providing a basis for future research. These findings demonstrate that integrating hierarchical clustering with advanced neural combinatorial network techniques is a promising direction for solving MDCP-MTSP.

Future work may explore several directions, including advanced clustering algorithms beyond k-means++; a comparative study of diverse merging mechanisms for second-level clusters; alternative decoding strategies (e.g., beam search and sampling) for quality-latency trade-offs; systematic analysis of the impact of the heuristic components such as cluster ordering methods and edge-binding variants; and adaptation to more general multi-depot routing problems or to energy-constrained routing variants.
