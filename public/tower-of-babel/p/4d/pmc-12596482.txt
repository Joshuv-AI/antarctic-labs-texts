Predicting the Need for Tube Thoracostomy in Blunt Trauma Patients With Occult Pneumothorax: Observation Versus Intervention
Authors: Nilay Çavuşoğlu Yalçın, Muharrem Özkaya
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesOccult pneumothorax is increasingly diagnosed in trauma patients due to widespread use of computed tomography (CT), yet its optimal management remains controversial. This study aimed to identify clinical and radiological predictors of deterioration requiring tube thoracostomy and to develop a predictive model to guide management decisions.MethodsIn this retrospective single-centre study, 166 patients with blunt trauma-associated occult pneumothorax were analyzed. Clinical and radiological variables—including subcutaneous emphysema, haemothorax volume, pneumothorax size, mechanical ventilation, and rib fractures—were evaluated for association with delayed tube thoracostomy. A weighted multivariable logistic regression model addressed class imbalance, and model performance was assessed using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis.ResultsOf 166 patients, 17 (10.2%) required delayed tube thoracostomy. Subcutaneous emphysema (odds ratio [OR] 20.10, P = .001) and mechanical ventilation (OR 17.30, P = .002) were the strongest independent predictors of deterioration. Haemothorax volume also showed a significant association (OR 1.06, P = .045). Other factors, including pneumothorax size, rib fractures, age, and sex, were not predictive. The predictive model demonstrated excellent discrimination (area under the curve [AUC] = 0.97), suggesting potential for clinical risk stratification.ConclusionsPhysiological indicators such as subcutaneous emphysema and mechanical ventilation are superior to anatomical parameters in predicting deterioration among patients with occult pneumothorax. Our findings support a selective management strategy and highlight the utility of predictive modelling to guide tube thoracostomy decisions. Prospective multicentre studies are warranted to validate these results.

INTRODUCTION

Advancements in imaging technologies have established whole-body computed tomography (CT) as a routine protocol in trauma centres for evaluating patients with blunt injuries. Compared with conventional chest radiographs, thoracic CT provides higher sensitivity in detecting thoracic injuries, including minor lesions that may not influence immediate clinical management. One such entity is occult pneumothorax, defined as a pneumothorax not suspected based on clinical examination or initial.1–3 Many of the false negatives of chest radiographs are attributed to the disadvantaged supine position of the trauma patient because of spinal precautions. Occult pneumothorax is incidentally detected in 5% of all trauma patients and in up to 15% of those undergoing thorax CT for traumatic injury.4 Recent studies have confirmed that subtle radiographic findings, such as subcutaneous emphysema, may serve as early indicators of occult pneumothorax and support the diagnostic value of early CT evaluation.5

With the increasing use of CT scans, the detection of occult pneumothoraces has become more frequent; however, the question of optimal management strategies for these patients remains unresolved. While many patients with occult pneumothorax, especially those receiving positive pressure ventilation, undergo prophylactic tube thoracostomy, a significant proportion of surgeons’ advocate for close observation without chest drainage. Although occult pneumothorax is increasingly recognized for its often-subtle clinical presentation, the risk of progression to tension pneumothorax—particularly in patients receiving positive pressure ventilation—continues to be the primary justification for early tube thoracostomy, given the potential for rapid deterioration and high associated mortality.6 While some clinicians advocate for prophylactic tube thoracostomy, particularly in patients requiring mechanical ventilation, others recommend conservative observation due to the significant risks associated with unnecessary chest tube insertion. Notably, tube thoracostomy carries a complication rate of up to 40%, including infection, prolonged hospitalization, and procedural morbidity.7 In light of these considerations, accurately identifying clinical and radiological predictors that necessitate tube thoracostomy is crucial to optimize management strategies and minimize avoidable interventions.

This study aimed to identify clinical and radiologic factors predictive of tube thoracostomy requirement in patients with occult pneumothorax and to assess the performance of a weighted logistic regression model in guiding management decisions.

METHODS

This retrospective, single-centre study was conducted at the University of Health Sciences, Antalya Training and Research Hospital, Thoracic Surgery Clinic. The study was approved by the institutional ethics committee (Approval No: 2023/046, Date: 16.02.2023, Decision No: 3/25) and conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Due to the retrospective nature of the study, the requirement for informed consent was waived.

A total of 1469 trauma patients requiring thoracic surgery consultation for pneumothorax between January 2015 and December 2023 were screened (Figure 1). Occult pneumothorax was defined as a pneumothorax ≤4 cm in maximal depth on CT imaging, consistent with prior literature such as the Wolfman classification of minimal occult pneumothorax (depth <1 cm; width <4 cm).8 The exclusion criteria for patient selection in this retrospective study are detailed below:

Based on these criteria, a total of 166 patients (11.3% of 1469 trauma patients screened) were included in the final analysis. Weighted logistic regression was applied using inverse probability weights to address substantial class imbalance (n = 17 events) and optimize model sensitivity.

For each patient, data were collected on age, sex, mechanism of injury, pneumothorax laterality and size, number of rib fractures, presence and volume of haemothorax and subcutaneous emphysema, mechanical ventilation, intensive care unit (ICU) admission and length of stay, hospital length of stay, and the need for tube thoracostomy.

A modified Thoracic Trauma Severity Score (mTTSS) was retrospectively computed for each patient using available CT and clinical data, including number of rib fractures, pleural involvement, pulmonary contusions, and patient age, as described in the original TTSS methodology.9 Due to incomplete availability of some physiological parameters, the score was restricted to anatomical and demographic components.

All patients diagnosed with occult pneumothorax were admitted for in-patient observation. No patients were followed up as outpatients.

Clinical deterioration prompting tube thoracostomy was defined as the occurrence of one or more of the following: (1) progressive respiratory distress, (2) haemodynamic instability, or (3) radiological evidence of pneumothorax enlargement on serial imaging. Alternative causes of deterioration, such as pulmonary contusions, infections, or haemothorax, were evaluated and excluded using clinical and radiological findings where applicable. All patients were initially managed conservatively without prophylactic chest tube insertion. Delayed tube thoracostomy was defined as chest tube insertion performed > 1 hour after the initial diagnosis of occult pneumothorax, following definitions used in recent literature.8 While no formal institutional protocol existed for the management of occult pneumothorax during the study period, management decisions were guided by clinician judgement informed by international guidelines such as the British Thoracic Society Pleural Disease Guideline 2010.10

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS software, version 22.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Continuous variables were summarized as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median with interquartile range (IQR), depending on data distribution. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages. The distribution of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test.

Between-group comparisons for continuous variables were performed using the independent-samples t-test for normally distributed data and the Mann-Whitney U test for non-normally distributed data. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate.

To identify independent predictors of tube thoracostomy in patients with occult pneumothorax, a multivariable logistic regression model was constructed. Variables with a P-value <.20 in univariate analyses were entered into the multivariable model. To address the substantial class imbalance—given the relatively low number of events (tube thoracostomy)—a weighted logistic regression approach was utilized to optimize classification performance, particularly sensitivity. This approach assigned higher weights to patients requiring tube thoracostomy to address the class imbalance inherent in the dataset and reduce bias towards the majority class. Class imbalance was addressed using inverse probability weighting. In SPSS, this was implemented using the “Weight Cases” function, with weights assigned as the inverse of class frequencies

Model discrimination was assessed using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis, and the area under the curve (AUC) was calculated. In addition, performance metrics including sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), overall accuracy, and F1-score were computed. All statistical tests were 2-sided, and a P-value <.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

A total of 166 patients with blunt trauma and radiologically confirmed occult pneumothorax were retrospectively analyzed. The mean age of our cohort was 44.4 ± 19.6 years old, and 77.7% of the cohort were male. Subcutaneous emphysema was present in 23.5% of cases, and 21.1% required positive pressure ventilation. Clinical deterioration requiring delayed tube thoracostomy occurred in 17 patients (10.2%), while the remaining 89.8% were managed conservatively. Baseline characteristics of the cohort and subgroup distributions are summarized in Table 1. A mTTSS was retrospectively calculated for the cohort. The mean mTTSS was 3.40 ± 1.64 (median 3; range 1-8). Patients who underwent tube thoracostomy (n = 17) had a mean mTTSS of 3.59 ± 1.46, while those managed conservatively (n = 149) had a mean score of 3.38 ± 1.66. The Mann-Whitney U test confirmed no significant difference between groups (P = .52). These findings indicate that mTTSS alone was not predictive of subsequent tube thoracostomy requirement. Although differences in variables such as pneumothorax size, haemothorax volume, subcutaneous emphysema, and mechanical ventilation appeared notable between patients requiring tube thoracostomy and those managed conservatively, no formal statistical comparisons were performed due to the highly imbalanced group sizes and the small number of events. These variables were further evaluated in multivariable logistic regression to identify independent predictors of intervention.

Multivariable logistic regression was performed to identify independent predictors associated with the need for tube thoracostomy. Covariates included age, sex, number of rib fractures, subcutaneous emphysema, pneumothorax size, mechanical ventilation, ICU length of stay, and haemothorax volume.

The analysis revealed that subcutaneous emphysema (odds ratio [OR] 20.10; 95% confidence interval [CI], 5.00-80.50; P = .001) and mechanical ventilation (OR 17.30; 95% CI, 4.50-66.40; P = .002) were the most significant independent predictors of tube thoracostomy. Haemothorax volume showed a mild but statistically significant association with increased risk (OR 1.06; 95% CI, 1.01-1.12; P = .045). Other variables, including pneumothorax size, age, sex, rib fractures, and ICU stay, were not significantly associated with intervention.

These findings emphasize the dominant role of physiological compromise (eg, need for mechanical ventilation or presence of air leakage into subcutaneous tissues) over purely anatomical factors in predicting clinical deterioration. The wide CIs for some predictors reflect the small number of events and underscore the need for further validation. Table 2 presents the ORs, CIs, and P-values for all included predictors.

Although the multivariable model performed well, attempts to generate ROC curves and derive cut-off values based solely on haemothorax volume or pneumothorax size were unsuccessful due to class imbalance; only a minority of patients (10.2%) experienced clinical deterioration. Efforts to match thoracostomy cases with controls based on injury severity were similarly inconclusive. The limited number of events precluded the development of reliable thresholds for individual predictors.

These results underscore the need for larger, prospective multicentre studies to validate these findings and to refine evidence-based criteria for early intervention in occult pneumothorax.

DISCUSSION

This study offers important insights into the clinical management of occult pneumothorax in the context of blunt thoracic trauma. Despite the increasing frequency of occult pneumothorax detection due to routine use of CT in trauma settings, the decision to intervene remains nuanced and highly dependent on individual patient risk factors.6,10–12 Our findings support a selective, risk-adapted approach to intervention, informed by a combination of clinical and radiologic parameters.

Subcutaneous emphysema and the need for mechanical ventilation emerged as the strongest independent predictors of clinical deterioration requiring tube thoracostomy. These factors are indicative of underlying physiological compromise rather than purely anatomical disruption. Subcutaneous emphysema reflects underlying air leakage from injured lung parenchyma, while mechanical ventilation increases intrathoracic pressures, both of which may exacerbate or unmask latent pneumothorax. Our findings align with prior literature emphasizing the prognostic relevance of these clinical variables in identifying high-risk patients.13–15 Similar findings were reported by Paplawski et al,15 who demonstrated that mechanical ventilation and moderate-sized pneumothoraces were significant predictors of chest tube placement in blunt trauma patients with occult pneumothorax. This is further supported by a recent large-scale retrospective study in blunt thoracic trauma patients, which identified subcutaneous emphysema and lung contusion on supine chest radiography as independent risk factors for occult pneumothorax (OR: 25.8 and 1.4, respectively).5

In our cohort, a retrospectively computed modified TTSS did not differ between groups and was not predictive of intervention, underscoring the stronger discriminative value of physiological indicators.

Our study adds value by quantifying these effects within an occult pneumothorax cohort and directly comparing their predictive strength against anatomical parameters, thereby clarifying their relative importance in clinical decision-making.

Our prediction of deterioration was based on multivariable logistic regression modelling, which demonstrated that subcutaneous emphysema and mechanical ventilation were independent predictors, whereas anatomical factors such as pneumothorax size and rib fractures were not significant.

Tube thoracostomy in this study was performed as a reactive intervention following deterioration rather than prophylactically. Although stabilization was frequently noted afterwards, the retrospective dataset did not permit systematic quantification of clinical improvement. Accordingly, the study focuses on predictors associated with the need for intervention rather than assessing its efficacy.

Importantly, 89.8% of patients in our cohort were managed conservatively without requiring chest tube placement, reinforcing that occult pneumothorax does not inherently necessitate invasive treatment. This finding supports previous studies that advocate for close observation rather than prophylactic drainage in selected patients.8,13,16 Similarly, Wilson et al12 discussed the necessity of tube thoracostomy in trauma patients with occult pneumothorax and suggested that conservative management may represent a safe alternative in selected cases. This finding aligns with our argument regarding the safety of an observation strategy and underscores the prognostic importance of physiological indicators in guiding clinical decision-making. Avoiding unnecessary tube thoracostomy is clinically meaningful, as it mitigates the risk of procedure-related complications such as infection, bleeding, tube malposition, and prolonged hospitalization—all of which contribute to patient morbidity and healthcare burden.7,17

Haemothorax volume was also associated with increased risk, albeit with a lower effect size, and should be interpreted in the context of overall injury severity. While the presence of haemothorax may be a marker of concurrent intrathoracic trauma, its volume alone may not be sufficient to justify intervention without additional clinical signs of compromise.

In contrast, variables traditionally assumed to influence clinical outcomes—such as pneumothorax size, rib fractures, age, sex, and ICU stay—did not demonstrate predictive value in multivariable analysis. These results support a growing body of evidence questioning the utility of radiographic size measurements as the primary determinant of management in occult pneumothorax. This finding is particularly relevant in settings where reliance on CT measurements might lead to overdiagnosis and overtreatment. It also underscores the importance of incorporating physiologic and clinical indicators into decision-making algorithms.

Our results mirror recent evidence highlighting the clinical relevance of functional indicators such as ventilatory status and subcutaneous emphysema in determining the need for intervention in occult pneumothorax. de Lesquen et al18 demonstrated that, in patients with blunt chest trauma, conservative management during the first 48 hours—even among those receiving mechanical ventilation—was associated with low rates of clinical deterioration. Similarly, Spaans et al19 reported that the type of analgesia used after thoracic injury significantly impacts postoperative outcomes, further underscoring the importance of individualized care planning.

In a separate investigation, Jiang et al. highlighted that rib fractures may lead to lung entrapment, which can contribute to persistent air leaks and delayed deterioration under positive pressure ventilation.20 This aligns with our observation that subcutaneous emphysema and mechanical ventilation were stronger predictors of intervention than pneumothorax size.

Furthermore, Yang et al21 provided evidence that omission of routine chest drain insertion following minimally invasive thoracic procedures may reduce morbidity without compromising safety, reinforcing the selective approach we advocate for patients with occult pneumothorax.

Our weighted logistic regression model achieved excellent discriminatory performance (AUC = 0.97), suggesting potential for application in clinical risk stratification, pending further validation. Such tools may assist trauma teams in identifying patients who would most benefit from early intervention while avoiding unnecessary procedures in low-risk individuals. However, we encountered notable limitations in attempting to derive specific cut-off thresholds for haemothorax and pneumothorax measurements due to class imbalance. Only a minority of patients (10.2%) required intervention, limiting statistical power for threshold analysis and highlighting the need for larger, multicentre datasets.

Our study has several limitations. The retrospective design introduces potential for selection and information biases. The single-centre setting may limit generalizability, particularly in institutions with different trauma management protocols or resource availability. Additionally, our regression model included multiple covariates despite a low number of events (n = 17), yielding an approximate event-per-variable (EPV) ratio of 2.1. Although weighted logistic regression was used to address class imbalance, the wide CIs reflect model instability and potential overfitting. No internal validation (eg, bootstrapping or cross-validation) was performed due to the small sample size. Multicollinearity was not formally assessed (eg, variance inflation factors or correlation matrix). Although the included variables were conceptually distinct and no extreme overlap was expected, the absence of formal testing may have affected coefficient stability and contributed to the wide CIs. Calibration of the predictive model (eg, by Hosmer-Lemeshow test or calibration plots) was not assessed due to the small number of events, which limits the reliability of such analyses. The absence of calibration testing may reduce understanding of the model’s reliability and underscores the need for future studies with larger cohorts to incorporate both discrimination and calibration metrics. These limitations should be considered when interpreting the results, and future multicentre, prospective studies with larger cohorts and alternative modelling approaches such as LASSO or ridge regression are recommended to validate these findings and derive robust thresholds for intervention. Machine learning approaches such as Random Forest and XGBoost, which may better address class imbalance and capture non-linear interactions, were not feasible in this small dataset but should be incorporated in larger future studies to complement conventional logistic regression. Furthermore, trauma severity scores such as the injury severity score (ISS) or TTSS were not systematically recorded during the study period, which may have limited our ability to adjust for overall injury burden. In this context, mechanical ventilation may also act as a surrogate marker for polytrauma; future studies should control for this confounding effect. All CT images were reviewed retrospectively by a single radiologist; interobserver variability was not assessed, which may have introduced a potential source of bias.

Point-of-care ultrasonography (POCUS), as part of the extended FAST (e-FAST) protocol, has demonstrated sensitivities of up to 100% for detecting small pneumothoraces in trauma patients. However, in supine trauma patients, its diagnostic performance can vary widely, with reported sensitivities ranging from 56% to 95%, depending on operator expertise and the distribution of intrathoracic air.22,23 Therefore, we elected to use CT as the reference standard for the diagnosis and measurement of occult pneumothorax in this study to ensure methodological consistency. This choice, while providing robust diagnostic accuracy, may limit the generalizability of our findings to resource-limited settings where POCUS is more commonly relied upon.

Another limitation of this study is the absence of a written institutional protocol for the management of occult pneumothorax. However, management decisions were guided by clinician judgement informed by international recommendations, including the BTS 2010 guideline for spontaneous pneumothorax and the updated BTS 2023 guideline extending similar conservative management principles to traumatic pneumothorax. This alignment with international guidance mitigates concerns regarding variability and enhances the reproducibility of our findings.10,22

In conclusion, our findings underscore the value of integrating clinical indicators—particularly subcutaneous emphysema and mechanical ventilation—into risk assessment frameworks for patients with occult pneumothorax. While radiologic extent remains a useful component of evaluation, it should not serve as the sole determinant of management. Future studies should focus on validating predictive models in broader patient populations and exploring the incorporation of real-time physiologic monitoring and machine learning algorithms to further refine risk stratification and guide evidence-based, individualized care.
