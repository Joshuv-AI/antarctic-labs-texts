Cumulative exposure and dynamic trajectories of the C-reactive protein-triglyceride-glucose index (CTI) versus the cholesterol, high-density lipoprotein, and glucose index (CHG) for incident hypertension prediction: a national cohort study
Authors: Jiaqing Dou, Shuya Zhang, Chaofan Ding, Haoquan Li, Pengfei Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe C-reactive protein-triglyceride-glucose index (CTI) and cholesterol-high-density lipoprotein-glucose index (CHG) are emerging composite biomarker indices, but their cumulative exposure and comparative value for predicting incident hypertension remain unclear.MethodsLeveraging a nationwide longitudinal cohort from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), the cumulative burden of CTI and CHG was modeled. These cumulative indices were defined as the average of the values measured at Wave 1 and Wave 3, multiplied by the time interval between these two assessments. Then, multivariable Cox proportional hazards models were used to assess associations and restricted cubic splines (RCS) for dose–response relationships. Group-based trajectory modeling (GBTM) was used to identify trajectory patterns. To gauge the predictive performance at 7 and 9 years, we analyzed time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, alongside the C-index, net reclassification improvement (NRI), and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI), and cuCTI was further compared with triglyceride-glucose (TyG). Finally, the findings were further subjected to subgroup and sensitivity analyses to test their robustness.ResultsOver a median follow-up of 9 years, 408 of 2673 participants (15.3%) developed hypertension. Both elevated cuCTI and cuCHG significantly increased hypertension risk. In the fully adjusted model, participants in the highest quartile had a higher risk of hypertension for both cuCTI (HR = 2.16; 95% CI 1.63–2.87) and cuCHG (HR = 1.63; 95% CI 1.24–2.15). Crucially, cuCTI demonstrated superior predictive accuracy in time-dependent ROC analysis (9 years DeLong P = 0.025). In the fully adjusted model, adding cuCTI improved the 7 years C-index from 0.572 to 0.609 (P = 0.014) and the 9 years C-index from 0.582 to 0.618 (P = 0.003), compared with 0.590 and 0.597 for cuCHG (P = 0.110 and 0.094, respectively). Furthermore, cuCTI showed stronger gains in NRI and IDI compared to cuCHG (NRI: 30.31% vs. 12.61% at 7 years and 35.47% versus 15.77% at 9 years; IDI: 0.86% versus 0.47% at 7 years and 1.32% vs. 0.71% at 9 years; all P < 0.05). Further comparison with TyG also suggested superior predictive performance of cuCTI. Subgroup and sensitivity analyses also showed consistent results.ConclusionBoth indices were independently associated with incident hypertension, and cuCTI showed better long-term predictive performance than cuCHG. These findings support the value of monitoring cumulative inflammatory-metabolic burden for early hypertension risk stratification.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12933-026-03175-3.

Introduction

Hypertension is increasingly prevalent and remains a major threat to global cardiovascular health, with recent estimates exceeding 1.4 billion affected individuals worldwide [1]. In China, 44.7% of adults aged 35–75 had hypertension in 2017 [2, 3]. Hypertension is a multifactorial disorder involving metabolic dysregulation, chronic inflammation, vascular dysfunction, and insulin resistance (IR) [4–6]. Biomarkers that effectively reflect the interactions of these disorders would provide add-on values for risk estimation of the incidence of hypertension beyond traditional clinical and lifestyle risk factors [7–9]. Among such biomarkers, the cholesterol–high-density lipoprotein–glucose (CHG) index and the C-reactive protein–triglyceride–glucose (CTI) index shall be pointed out. The CHG index was proposed as an alternative indicator of IR and was initially used for the diagnosis of type 2 diabetes [10]. Its relationship with the risk of cardiovascular disease (CVD) and hypertension has been reported recently [11–14]. The CTI index reflects both metabolic disorders and inflammation status and was initially proposed for prognostics in malignant tumors [15]. Although a few studies have reported its predictive value on the risk of cardiovascular events [16], its association with incident hypertension has rarely been systematically investigated.

Another important point is that hypertension may not become clinically apparent without cumulative exposure to multiple pathogenic factors. Previous studies on incident hypertension may have been limited because they largely relied on single baseline measurements of risk factors [17]. However, there is currently limited longitudinal evidence regarding the association of CTI and CHG with the incidence of hypertension among middle-aged and older adults in China. Thus, we calculated two indices (cuCTI and cuCHG) to reflect their cumulative effects. The value of these two indices as well as their dynamic trajectory patterns on the risk of incident hypertension was investigated in this study.

Methods

Study population

We used data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS). This is a nationwide cohort managed by Peking University to track aging trends in China. The 2011–2012 baseline survey used multi-stage probability sampling. It covered 150 counties and 450 villages in 28 provinces, including 17,708 participants from about 10,000 households. Follow-up surveys are done every two years, and data from five waves (2011、2013、2015、2018、2020) are currently available. The data includes sociodemographics, health status, biochemical markers, and 13 physical exam indicators. Trained staff collected the data through face-to-face interviews. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Peking University (No. 00001005–11,015), and all participants gave written informed consent. More details are provided by Zhao et al. [18] and on the official website (http://charls.pku.edu.cn/en).

We initially included 17,708 participants from Wave 1 (2011) and subsequently excluded participants based on the following criteria: (1) missing lab data in Wave 1 or Wave 3 (n = 10,227); (2) missing baseline demographics (n = 5); (3) non-fasting blood samples (n = 97); (4) age under 45 at baseline (n = 173); (5) Participants with CRP > 10 mg/L at Wave 1 or Wave 3, suggestive of acute inflammation (n = 128); (6) loss to follow-up in Waves 4 and 5 (n = 434); (7) hypertension diagnosed before Wave 4 (n = 3971). Assessment of cuCHG and cuCTI

The calculation of CHG and CTI indices involved five key biomarkers: total cholesterol (TC), fasting plasma glucose (FPG), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), C-reactive protein (CRP), and triglycerides (TG). These two indices were determined using the following formulas: CHG = ln[TC (mg/dL) × FPG (mg/dL) / (2 × HDL-C (mg/dL))] [10]. CTI = 0.412 × ln(CRP [mg/L]) + ln(TG [mg/dL] × FPG [mg/dL]/2) [15]. We derived cumulative indices by averaging values from 2012 and 2015, multiplied by the time elapsed between assessments: cumulative Index = (Index2012 + Index2015) / 2 × time interval (2015 − 2012). This method follows the approach for cumulative metabolic indices used in previous CHARLS studies by Yang [19] and Ma [20].

Ascertainment of outcomes

Hypertension was defined as the presence of at least one of the following indicators: (1) a self-reported history of a physician diagnosis; (2) current use of blood pressure-lowering medication; or (3) physical examination findings showing systolic blood pressure (SBP) ≥ 140 mmHg or diastolic blood pressure (DBP) ≥ 90 mmHg in two or more measurements. Participants who met any of these criteria during the 2018 or 2020 follow-up visits were classified as having incident hypertension [21, 22].

Study variables and definitions

Baseline covariates were gathered through structured interviews conducted by trained personnel. Sociodemographic and lifestyle variables included age, sex, urban or rural residence, education, marital status, and health behaviors (smoking/alcohol consumption). Anthropometric assessments followed standardized protocols to record height and weight. Medical history was ascertained via self-report, covering physician-diagnosed comorbidities such as diabetes and heart disease, as well as concurrent medication regimens. Finally, venous blood samples were analyzed for key biomarkers, including FPG, HbA1c, CRP, and lipid profiles (TC, TG, HDL-C, LDL-C). Diabetes was defined as a self-reported diagnosis, use of antidiabetic medication, or an FPG ≥ 7.0 mmol/L; other comorbidities relied on self-reported history or ongoing treatment. For subgroup analyses, participants were stratified according to baseline blood pressure status into a normal blood pressure group and an elevated blood pressure group. Elevated blood pressure was defined as SBP 120–139 mmHg or DBP 70–89 mmHg [23].

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics were summarized using standard descriptive statistics. Continuous variables were assessed for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test in combination with skewness and kurtosis, and visual inspection of histograms and Q-Q plots. Variables with normal distributions are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and group differences were analyzed using Student’s t-test. Continuous variables showing a skewed distribution are presented as the median with interquartile range (IQR) and were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables are reported as counts with percentages and were compared using Pearson’s chi-square test. All analyses were carried out in Python version 3.12 and R version 4.3.0, and two-sided p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Univariable and multivariable Cox proportional hazards models were used to assess how the cuCTI and cuCHG indices relate to hypertension risk, with findings expressed as hazard ratios (HRs) together with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Both indices were entered into the models in two ways, namely as continuous measures and after being treated as categorical variables. To assess multicollinearity, variance inflation factors (VIFs) were calculated separately for the fully adjusted cuCTI model and the fully adjusted cuCHG model. All VIFs remained below 5, indicating no evidence of multicollinearity (Table S1). Then, three nested models were fitted: Model 1 was unadjusted, Model 2 adjusted for age, sex, residence, education, marital status, smoking status, drinking status, and body mass index (BMI), and Model 3 further adjusted for heart disease, stroke, and kidney disease in addition to the covariates included in Model 2. The proportional hazards (PH) assumption for the Cox regression models was evaluated using the Schoenfeld residuals test. To keep the sample size as large as possible, missing values were handled by applying multiple imputation.

For the trajectory analysis, GBTM with a finite mixture of linear models was used to describe the longitudinal change patterns of CTI and CHG from Wave 1 to Wave 3. The optimal number of latent groups, ranging from 2 to 4, was determined based on multiple criteria. A candidate model was considered acceptable only when two requirements were met simultaneously: adequate classification quality, indicated by an average posterior probability (APP) > 0.70 in every subgroup, and at least 5% of the total analytical sample in each trajectory group to maintain clinical representativeness and statistical robustness. Ultimately, the qualified model with the lowest Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) was selected. The association between trajectory subgroups and incident hypertension was analyzed by fully adjusted Cox proportional hazards models, with the first group as the reference group, and the PH assumption was tested with Schoenfeld residuals.

To check whether the associations might deviate from linearity, restricted cubic splines (RCS) were fitted within the fully adjusted model. In light of the Bayesian information criterion, the preferred specification used four knots placed at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles, and evidence for non-linearity was tested using a likelihood ratio test.

To evaluate predictive performance, time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were generated for 7 years and 9 years follow-up, and areas under the curve (AUCs) were compared by applying the DeLong test. To further contrast the predictive ability of cuCTI versus cuCHG, the restricted Harrell’s C-index, net reclassification improvement (NRI) and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI) were also calculated. In addition, because the single-time-point TyG index is a widely used and simpler surrogate marker of metabolic dysfunction, we additionally included the baseline TyG index for comparison with cuCTI and cuCHG, in order to further assess the potential clinical utility of the cumulative CTI and CHG indices. The TyG index was derived as ln [TG (mg/dl) × FPG (mg/dl) / 2] [24]. Differences were assessed via bootstrap resampling within each imputed dataset, and estimates were then pooled using Rubin’s rules.

Finally, subgroup analyses were performed based on the fully adjusted model to examine potential heterogeneity, with stratification by sex, age, residence, BMI, lifestyle factors, history of diabetes and baseline blood pressure status.

Sensitivity analysis

To assess the robustness of the findings, five sensitivity analyses were conducted. First, participants with extreme cuCTI or cuCHG values, defined as outliers beyond median ± 2.5 × median absolute deviation (MAD) [25], were excluded and the analysis was repeated. Second, a complete-case analysis was performed by including only participants with full data. Third, to assess the robustness of the findings to the modeling approach, the associations were further examined using a logistic regression model. Then, with all-cause death treated as a competing event, we performed a sensitivity analysis using the Fine–Gray subdistribution hazards model to assess the associations between cuCTI and cuCHG and incident hypertension. Finally, the E-value was calculated to estimate the potential impact of unmeasured confounding: E = RR + √[RR × (RR − 1)] [26].

Results

Participant characteristics

Table 1 presents the baseline characteristics of the study population stratified by incident hypertension. Out of 2673 participants, 408 (15.3%) developed hypertension during follow-up. The mean age was 56.50 years (SD = 7.82), and females accounted for 55.9% of the sample. In comparison with the non-hypertension group, participants who developed hypertension were older (57.78 vs. 56.27 years, P < 0.001), and they were more likely to have no formal education (54.9% vs. 46.1%) and to live in rural areas (72.1% vs. 66.5%; all P < 0.05). Participants in this group also exhibited higher baseline blood pressure levels (systolic and diastolic) (all P < 0.001). Similarly, higher TG, BMI, FPG, HbA1c, CRP, LDL-C, and TC levels were observed (all P ≤ 0.05). The cuCTI was significantly higher among those who developed hypertension (14.28 ± 1.40 vs. 13.86 ± 1.34, P < 0.001), and a similar trend was observed for cuCHG (15.77 ± 1.04 vs. 15.56 ± 0.97, P < 0.001). Moreover, the prevalence of heart disease was higher among individuals who developed hypertension (10.5% vs. 7.4%, P = 0.042). Associations of CTI and CHG exposure trajectories with the risk of incident hypertension

Based on predefined model selection criteria, two trajectory patterns were identified for CTI (Group 1: low-stable and Group 2: moderate-to-high-stable) and three for CHG (Group 1: low-stable, Group 2: moderate-stable, and Group 3: high-stable; Table S4). The incidence of hypertension increased across CTI trajectories (from 11.7% to 19.1%) and similarly across CHG trajectories (12.2%, 15.1%, and 18.9%, respectively). In the fully adjusted models, the moderate-to-high-stable CTI group exhibited a significantly higher risk than the low-stable reference group (HR = 1.75, 95% CI 1.43–2.13, P < 0.001). Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analyses supported the robustness of the findings. Results remained consistent with the primary analysis after removing outliers (Table S8), restricting analyses to complete cases (Table S9), and using a fully adjusted logistic regression model (Table S10). Competing-risk analyses treating all-cause death as a competing event showed similar associations in fully adjusted Fine–Gray models (cuCTI: SHR = 1.31, 95% CI 1.20–1.43; cuCHG: SHR = 1.20, 95% CI 1.11–1.30; Table S11). E-values of 1.99 for cuCTI and 1.72 for cuCHG indicated that relatively strong unmeasured confounding would be required to fully explain these associations (Table S12).

Discussion

This study systematically evaluated the association and predictive value of cuCTI and cuCHG with incident hypertension in a middle-aged and elderly Chinese population. After adjustment for lifestyle-related variables and comorbidities, both cumulative indices remained independently associated with incident hypertension, and both showed a sustained positive dose–response relationship without a significant threshold effect. Trajectory analysis further showed that subjects with long-term sustained high CTI and CHG levels had the highest risk of hypertension. In addition, comparison of long-term predictive value for hypertension revealed that cuCTI outperformed both cuCHG and the widely used single-point measurement TyG index, and that all three provided incremental value beyond the fully adjusted model, suggesting that cuCTI may have higher application potential in primary prevention of hypertension.

Although the exact mechanisms underlying hypertension are not fully understood, current evidence suggests a multifactorial etiology involving the complex interplay of abnormal glucose and lipid metabolism, inflammation, and oxidative stress [27–30]. Composite indices have been put forward aiming to comprehensively reflect the above intertwined pathological processes. For example, TyG and metabolic score of insulin resistance (METS-IR) serving as surrogate indicators of insulin resistance, have been linked to the occurrence and prognosis of hypertension [31–34]. The states of glucolipid metabolic perturbation, as reflected by TyG, METS-IR, and CHG in this study, may partially contribute to elevated blood pressure by activating the sympathetic nervous system and the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system, increasing renal sodium and water retention, and impairing vasodilatory function [35, 36]. Dyslipidemia may also lead to impaired vascular endothelial function and increased arterial stiffness [37, 38]. Inflammation may further amplify the adverse effects of the above metabolic abnormalities [39], as evidenced in the present study that CTI held better predictive performance for hypertension against CHG or TyG. Some other inflammation-metabolic composite indices, such as monocyte-to-high-density lipoprotein cholesterol ratio (MHR) have also been observed to be closely related to the hypertension [40, 41].

Importantly, the value of cumulative exposure indices has been increasingly emphasized given the chronic nature of hypertension development and progression. Compared with a single baseline measurement, cumulative exposure indices may reduce regression dilution bias and capture long-term burden patterns more accurately [17]. Tai et al. conducted a study of 5695 patients with type 2 diabetes and examined the positive correlation between cumulative TyG (cuTyG) and major adverse cardiovascular events (MACEs). Also, cuTyG showed an incremental predictive value beyond traditional risk models [42]. Later studies also reported that the cuTyG was associated with an increased risk of heart failure [43] and stroke risk among individuals with stage 0–3 cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic syndrome [44]. Li et al. explored the association between cumulative exposure to TyG-BMI and incident CVD [45]. In addition to TyG-related indices, the cumulative effect of the atherogenic index of plasma (AIP) on the risk of myocardial infarction was reported by Liu et al. [46]. The associations of cumulative exposure to the emerging CTI and CHG indices with cardiovascular disease have also attracted increasing attention. Higher cuCTI has been linked to a higher risk of stroke [19]. Increased cuCTI and the continuous high-level trajectory were significantly correlated with the increase of new-onset CVD risk in a linear dose–response manner [20].

However, evidence on the association between cuCTI or cuCHG and incident hypertension remains limited. More recently, Zhang et al. reported an association between cuCHG and incident hypertension [17]. In accordance with their results, the present study also found that higher cumulative CHG exposure was significantly associated with an increased risk of incident hypertension, and the restricted cubic spline analyses in both studies supported a predominantly linear dose–response relationship. Beyond their reports, we further evaluated the incremental value of adding cuCHG to a fully adjusted traditional risk model and found that the addition of cuCHG improved both risk reclassification and model discrimination. Since direct comparisons of cuCTI and cuCHG for hypertension are scarce, the present study further deepens the understanding of the predictive value of the two indices in that cuCTI was superior to cuCHG or single-measure TyG in long-term prediction performance. These evidence highlighted that inflammation and metabolic disorders promote the occurrence of diseases gradually through the long-term cumulative exposure [20]. Moreover, in this study, the comparative analysis was stratified by baseline blood pressure status. The results showed that the effect sizes of cuCTI and cuCHG associations with incident hypertension were slightly higher in the normal blood pressure group. This suggests that inflammation and metabolic burden may already play a role in the very early stage of hypertension.

It is worth noting that the present study also has some inconsistent results. In diabetes patients and people with body mass index of 24–28 kg/m2, the associations of cuCHG and cuCTI with incident hypertension were not statistically significant, but there was no significant interaction between groups. Yang et al. also observed a similar phenomenon in the study on the association between CTI and stroke [19]. This finding may be partly attributable to insufficient statistical power resulting from the limited sample size, particularly within the diabetic subgroup (n = 86, including 18 incident cases of hypertension). Alternatively, it could be associated with pre-existing metabolic abnormalities among diabetic and overweight populations [47–50], or potential sampling bias. This reflects the complex relationship between cuCTI and cuCHG and hypertension risk.

This study also has some limitations. First, although incident hypertension was defined using a combination of office blood-pressure measurements, previous diagnoses, and medication information, it was difficult to completely avoid underdiagnosis. Second, this study is an observational study, and the influence of residual confounding factors such as salt intake and genetic susceptibility cannot be completely excluded, which may lead to bias in effect size estimation [51, 52]. Third, limited by the information in the database, we could not accurately identify secondary causes in patients with incident hypertension. Fourth, the longitudinal study design in the middle-aged and elderly population has the risk of healthy survivor bias, and the subjects finally included in the analysis may be a relatively healthy subset of the original population, which may lead to underestimation of the true association. Fifth, the current CHG calculation formula may have some mathematical redundancy: HDL-C is also involved in the denominator construction and indirectly enters the numerator as a component of total cholesterol, which might limit the independence of its parameter interpretation. In the future, the reconstruction of the formula based on non-high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (non-HDL-C) can be considered. Finally, the study sample was only Chinese adults older than 45 years, and the generalizability of the findings to other populations is uncertain.

Conclusion

Both cuCTI and cuCHG indices showed a clear linear link to incident hypertension risk in middle-aged and older Chinese adults. The trajectory analysis of both indices also suggested that individuals who maintain high index levels have the highest risk of incident hypertension. Since cuCTI outperformed cuCHG in predicting incident hypertension, long-term monitoring of CTI may help in the early prevention of hypertension.
