Facet-Resolved Electric Double Layer of Polycrystalline Gold
Authors: Ariba Adnan, Mark Aarts, Hui Wang, Núria Félez-Guerrero, Kasinath Ojha, Bert M. Weckhuysen, Marc T. M. Koper
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In electrochemistry, polycrystalline gold is widely used as a practical and model electrode because of its relative inertness, yet interpreting its electric double-layer (EDL) response remains nontrivial. In practice, this response is often reduced to a single feature, a capacitance minimum near the potential of zero charge (PZC) of Au(110), which has long been taken as evidence that polycrystalline Au is dominated by its least dense facet. Here we systematically demonstrate that this apparent dominance reflects a masking effect, not an intrinsic absence of other facet contributions. Using electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD)-characterized polycrystalline electrodes, single-crystal facets, and a cylindrical electrode with a basal Au(111) facet and polycrystalline sidewalls, we resolve two and even three distinct minima on the same electrode at potentials characteristic of the PZC of Au(111), Au(100), and Au(110). This provides direct evidence that, in conventional polycrystals, the (111) and (100) contributions, long hypothesized, are present but are typically hidden by the strongly potential-dependent (110) response. We further show that stepped surfaces, such as Au(554), cannot be captured by the simple additive model of the parent low-index facets, as terraces and step edges carry distinct local charge densities that are screened collectively rather than independently. Grain boundaries, by contrast, exert little measurable influence in our experiments, consistent with their low areal density. Together, these findings establish a broader principle: polycrystalline Au electrodes may appear dominated by a single facet, but the measured capacitance curve reflects the sum of contributions from multiple facets, whose visibility depends on facet domain size and Debye screening length.

Introduction

Gold is widely used in electrochemistry due to its exceptional stability, high conductivity, and resistance to oxidation and corrosion in air. These properties make it an ideal electrode material for applications ranging from sensors and biosensors to electrocatalysis. , Although more reactive and less expensive electrode metals often dominate industrial applications, gold provides a uniquely clean model platform for studying both non-Faradaic and Faradaic processes. Its well-defined surface chemistry, with a wide double-layer window, has made it a model system for probing electrode–electrolyte interactions. , In particular, Au(111) is unique among noble metal as it remains purely capacitive, having a clear potential of zero charge (PZC), also showing no (dissociative) adsorption of H2O, H2 or OH–, across an unusually wide double-layer window of nearly 1 V. It thus provides an ideal platform for disentangling cation and anion effects in double layer phenomena, which manifest as the so-called “camel-shaped” capacitance plots.

The conceptual framework for describing polycrystalline electrodes was introduced by Frumkin, who emphasized that crystallographic inhomogeneity shapes the capacitance response because different facets carry different local charges at the same potential. This led to the so-called “independent electrode” approach, in which the total capacitance of a polycrystalline electrode is treated as the weighted sum of its constituent lower-index facets.

The first quantitative demonstration came from Valette and Hamelin’s 1973 study of silver electrodes. Using macrographic etching, they confirmed that polycrystalline Ag consisted of many large grains and modeled its surface as a parallel combination of low-index facets, explicitly neglecting grain boundaries. Restricting the surface facets to Ag(111), Ag(100), and Ag(110), they assigned each facet a fractional coverage derived from stereographic projections of the FCC lattice. By summing the capacitances of these faces with their respective weights, they reproduced the measured polycrystalline capacitance curve. Even though the curves did not completely overlap with the polycrystalline bead electrode, both experiment and calculation placed the minimum near the PZC of Ag(110), the most negative PZC of the three low-index facets, establishing (110) as the dominant contributor to poly Ag and giving strong quantitative support to Frumkin’s original hypothesis.

Frumkin later formalized this idea in his seminal 1980 review on PZCs, explicitly suggesting that the capacitance of a polycrystalline electrode could be expressed as a weighted average of the low-index facets: 1 C=C(111)θ(111)+C(100)θ(100)+C(110)θ(110) where C(111), C(100) and C(110) are the capacitances of individual low-index single-crystal facets, and θ (111), θ (100) and θ (110) are the fractions of these facets on the surface of a polycrystalline electrode.

For gold, Hamelin and Lecoeur provided the key single-crystal data already in 1971. In neutral NaF solution, they reported distinct minima for Au(110), Au(100), and Au(111). They noted that for Au(100) (PZC, 0.38 V vs SHE) and Au(111) (PZC, 0.50 V vs SHE) the minima were shallow and easily masked by hydroxyl adsorption or faradaic currents. By contrast, Au(110) displayed a broad, well-defined minimum at 0.19 V vs SHE. The capacitance minimum of polycrystalline gold lay in the same potential range as the PZC of Au(110), reinforcing the assumption that for polycrystalline gold, the response is also dominated by (110)-like regions. Still, these conclusions rested entirely on electrochemical curves, without structural confirmation, leaving the precise contribution of the other two lower-index facets, Au(111) and Au(100), unresolved.

Subsequent theoretical studies tested the “weighted average model” (eq ) by explicitly accounting for crystallographic inhomogeneity. Independent-electrode models predicted that if facet domains were wide relative to the Debye length and their PZC values differed sufficiently, the diffuse layers could remain decoupled, producing multiple capacitance minima. − For smaller domains, the diffuse layers overlap, yielding only a single broadened minimum near the most negative (PZC). ,,, These models also anticipate that capacitance at the minimum should be higher on inhomogeneous surfaces.

Further insight came from studies of stepped and vicinal surfaces. Work on Au­(11n) and Ag­(11n) electrodes demonstrated that step edges and terraces carry different local charges: the effective Helmholtz capacitance at the (PZC) was reduced by up to 50% compared to flat surfaces, and the (PZC) shifted to more negative values in proportion to step density. − These effects were attributed to the altered polarizability of water molecules bound at step edges and to dipole moments associated with step atoms, indicating that steps cannot be described by a simple sum of low-index facets.

From the early double layer studies, ,, through later pH-dependent investigations, to more recent analyses of capacitance-based descriptors for surface area, it has become apparent that electrochemical measurements on polycrystalline electrodes weigh different surface sites unequally and may effectively mask others, complicating their interpretation in the absence of structural resolution. These findings have long supported the view that polycrystalline gold is “dominated” by the (110) facet. Whether the intrinsic contributions of Au(111) and Au(100) are merely masked by Au(110), however, has remained unresolved. Earlier studies speculated that such contributions should exist, but they were never explicitly resolved with structural confirmation. ,

In the present work, we address this question by combining Electron Backscatter Diffraction (EBSD)-characterized polycrystalline electrodes with single-crystals and cylindrical electrodes with a well-defined basal plane. This approach allows us to directly probe whether the hidden responses of different facets can be separated, and to test how grain boundaries and steps shape the double-layer capacitance of gold. Importantly, by using gold as a model system, we aim to clarify how these effects translate to polycrystalline electrodes more generally, addressing how facet identity and their domain size govern whether individual contributions are masked or revealed. Also, by carefully tuning the contribution of different facets, we are able (for the first time, to the best of our knowledge) to resolve three capacitance minima on a single electrode, at potentials characteristic of the PZCs of Au(111), Au(100), and Au(110).

Results and Discussion

To better understand the role of individual facets, we first examined the double-layer region of three well-defined low-index gold surfaces, namely Au(111), Au(100), and Au(110) (Figure a–c). Au(111) shows the characteristic camel-shaped response with a clear scan-direction difference; Au(100) exhibits a minor hysteresis; and Au(110) presents a single broad minimum with no detectable hysteresis near PZC.

Capacitance–potential curves for (a) Au(111), (b) Au(100), (c) Au(110), and (d) polycrystalline Au in Ar-saturated 1 mM HClO4, 20 mV s–1. Figures (a–c) show facet-specific double-layer characteristics and PZCs; figure (d) shows that poly-Au exhibits a single capacitance minimum denoted as potential of capacitance minima (PCM) near the PZC of Au(110). Two features near 0.8 and 1.0 V are consistent with Au(110) and Au(100), respectively.

For Au(111)/(100), the forward and backward scans probe different surface states: on the forward scan, the surface is thermally reconstructed from the electrode preparation by flame annealing. It undergoes lifting of the reconstruction with increasing potential, whereas on the backward scan, it begins as an unreconstructed surface and gradually reconstructs again as the potential becomes lower than the PZC. Because the interfacial structure differs in different directions at the same potential, the apparent PZC also varies between scan directions. By contrast, Au(110) is known to reconstruct to (1 × 2), yet the capacitance minima in the forward/backward traces coincide within our window. The absence of hysteresis here indicates that the interface remains in the same effective state (or in states with similar PZC) or interconverts rapidly on the voltammetric time scale.

The three facets exhibit clearly distinct PZCs, Au(111) is the most positive at 0.67 V vs RHE (0.50 V vs SHE); Au(100) is intermediate at 0.48 V vs RHE (0.30 V vs SHE); and Au(110) is the most negative at 0.37 V vs RHE (0.19 V vs SHE). Notably, the facet-specific PZCs do not simply follow the vacuum work functions: recommended values for clean Au(111), Au(100), and Au(110) are more similar (≈5.33, 5.22, and 5.16 eV, respectively), yet their PZCs differ by ∼ 0.3 V, emphasizing that interfacial water/ion structure, rather than the vacuum work function alone, codetermines the PZC. The greater shift in the PZC of Au(110) relative to Au(111), as compared to the work function difference between the two surfaces, has been rationalized by ab initio molecular dynamics (AIMD) simulations, which show that on the more open Au(110) surface, the first water layer approaches ≈1 Å closer and adopts a stronger net interfacial dipole, shifting the potential of zero charge to more negative values. The double-layer windows also differ: Au(111) remains purely capacitive over 0–1.0 V vs RHE, Au(100) over 0–0.8 V, and Au(110) over 0–0.6 V, before preoxidation features emerge. , This indicates that within this region, the response is dominated by double-layer charging, with no significant contribution from specific adsorption. The capacitance shown here is obtained from cyclic voltammetry. Additional comparison between cyclic voltammetry (CV) and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) capacitance responses for Au(111) is provided in the Supporting Information (Figure S1), showing similar capacitance in the double-layer region.

A first comparison of the lower-index facets (Figure a–c) with a polycrystalline Au electrode (Figure d) reveals a single capacitance minimum. The potential of the capacitance minimum (PCM) for polycrystalline Au is located at 0.38 V vs RHE (≈0.20 V vs SHE). Independent measurements on polycrystalline Au also place the PCM near 0.2 V on the SHE scale, despite some pH dependence. In the present work, the PCM consistently falls within the same potential range across variations in pH (Figure S2), potential window (Figure S3) and scan rate (Figure S4).

The PCM of polycrystalline Au at (0.20 V vs = . SHE) lies close to the PZC of the Au(110) lower-index facet, suggesting electrochemical dominance of Au(110)-like regions. In addition to the PCM, the polycrystalline trace exhibits a shoulder near 0.80 V characteristic of Au(110) and a feature near 1.00 V associated with Au(100). Despite the presence of these facet-dependent signatures, the PCM remains pinned close to the Au(110) response. Taken together, the polycrystalline electrode displays Au(110)- and Au(100)-type features, while a distinct Au(111) contribution cannot be resolved under these conditions.

Figure c displays the capacitance–potential traces of the two samples. Surprisingly, despite the different facets, the double-layer response is very similar. Both samples show a PCM close to the PZC of (110) facet, a shoulder around 0.80 V consistent with (110), and a subtle curvature change near 1.00 V consistent with (100). Oxidative/reductive and Hydrogen evolution reaction (HER) features differ more noticeably between Poly-A and Poly-B, aligning with their orientation maps. Still, these “faradaic differences” do not align with changes in the position of the double-layer minimum. This suggests that different electrochemical phenomena emphasize different forms of heterogeneity: whereas Faradaic processes respond to the population and reactivity of specific facets, the double-layer response appears to be governed instead by electrostatics, such that facet “activity” in the Faradaic sense does not translate into dominance in the EDL region. Highlighting that EDL and faradaic reactions probe different “projections” of the same surface.

Comparing the EBSD and capacitance plots, Figure shows that multiple facets are present, yet the PCM remains near the (110) reference, even when the orientation distributions differ significantly. The polycrystalline curves also show only weak signatures in the potential ranges where (111) and (100) are expected to stand out. This raises the question whether the (111)/(100) responses are missing or, if present, masked by the more substantial, potential-dependent contribution of (110).

To reveal how the (111)/(100) responses are masked by the dominant (110) signal, we eliminate two key sources of ambiguity inherent to conventional polycrystals: (i) unknown facet mixtures and grain-boundary contributions, and (ii) uncertainty in the relative area of the “minor” (111)/(100) facets compared to (110). We therefore employ geometry-constrained electrodes that fix which crystallographic orientations are exposed and in what proportions. Two complementary approaches are used. First, we construct a facet-assembled “supercrystal” electrode by electrically connecting Au(111), Au(100), and Au(110) single crystals, providing equal exposure of all three basal planes and establishing a clean additive baseline. Second, we use Au(111) and Au(100) disk electrodes in which the immersion depth is deliberately varied, thereby tuning the relative contribution of the basal plane and the cylindrical sidewall containing multiple orientations. Together, these platforms allow us to make the capacitance contributions of the (111) and (100) facets explicit and to test whether their PZC-related minima can be resolved alongside the (110)-like response of the sidewalls.

Figure presents the capacitance–potential curves of these controlled geometries and shows that the (111)/(100) minima can indeed be resolved when the geometry is constrained. In the facet-assembled “supercrystal”, the exposed areas of (111), (100), and (110) are each ∼ 0.33. The capacitance–potential trace shows a dominant minimum near (110), a very shallow feature at the (111) PZC, and no explicit (100) minimum, as the (100) region is subsumed by the stronger potential-dependent (110) capacitance response in this potential window.

It is important to note that the equal-area “supercrystal” is an idealized limitreal polycrystals inevitably present facets with steps. Approaching this limit experimentally is valuable because it separates pure low-index contributions from step effects that otherwise obscure interpretation. As illustrated in Figure S8, the experimentally measured “supercrystal” response reproduces the same facet-dependent features obtained from an equal-area additive reconstruction of independently measured Au(111), Au(100), and Au(110) capacitance responses.

We then tested a basal-facet cylinder (111|poly)a cylinder with a basal Au(111) disk facet and a polycrystalline sidewall; the meniscus was set to wet both surfaces, so the signal is an area-weighted sum. On the cylinder (111|poly) electrode (Figure b, positive-going scan), we resolve three minima: near the (111) PZC (∼0.68 V), the (100) PZC (∼0.53 V), and a poly/(110)-like minimum (∼0.27 V). Two additional cylinders (Figure S7)  (111|poly) and (100|poly)  each show two minima (basal-facet near-PZC + (110)-like), consistent with the sidewall contribution. The positions of these minima are largely independent of scan rate (Figure S9), with only minor variations in magnitude; larger differences are observed only at more positive potentials approaching the onset of preoxidation.

Because at any given potential including the PZC of a certain facet, other facets still contribute potential-dependent capacitance (corresponding to a local nonzero charge), we refer to capacitance minima rather than to assign PZCs to multifaceted surfaces. The increased broadness of the capacitance response near the minimum is consistent with the overlap of several nearby facet capacitance contributions, which averages into a broader minimum than a single-facet minimum.

To assess grain-boundary effects, Poly-A (EBSD in Figure d) is compared with a spherical single crystal bead electrode, containing all crystal facets but no grain boundaries (Figure S10). The traces are essentially indistinguishable, both showing a minimum near the (110) reference and carrying the same (100)/(110) fingerprints, indicating that grain boundaries make a negligible contribution to the double-layer capacitance under our conditions. Based on the EBSD-determined boundary densities of ∼ 372 cm–1 (Poly-A) and ∼ 20 cm–1 (Poly-B), and assuming a characteristic grain-boundary width of ∼ 1–2 nm, the estimated grain-boundary area fraction remains below 10–4. This further supports that grain-boundary contributions are negligible compared to the total surface area and cannot account for the observed capacitance features. In addition, Figure S5 shows the full oxidation–reduction cycle, demonstrating that oxide formation and reduction do not significantly influence the position or depth of the capacitance minimum. Figure S6 further shows that the onset of hydrogen evolution at negative potentials and hydrogen oxidation at positive potentials likewise does not affect the position of the capacitance minimum.

Together, the results in Figure show that the Au(111) and Au(100) responses are not absent on polycrystalline gold. Rather, they are masked by the stronger potential-dependent Au(110) contribution. Once a geometry is selected with a higher (111)/(100) fraction, their capacitive signatures become more pronounced, even to the extent that three separate capacitance minima can be observed.

Returning to the polycrystalline electrode, we have established that grain boundaries contribute negligibly, and, depending on area ratios, more than one capacitance minimum can be resolved, corresponding to the (local) PZC of their respective facets.

Real polycrystals do not consist solely of low-index terraces; they also include stepped vicinals, in which terraces and step edges carry different local charges. In that situation, additivity in the low-index sense (eq ) is not expected. To illustrate this well-established point, we compare the vicinal Au(554), Figure a and b, with its parent facets and with a trace calculated from a weighted sum of Au(111) and Au(110) (eq ). Au(554) exhibits a single, well-defined minimum. Still, both the minimum position and the line shape deviate from the calculated low-index sum, consistent with prior reports. − that step edges possess distinct dipoles and polarizabilities, rendering stepped surfaces intrinsically nonadditive. Our Au(554) data serve here as a concrete example within the present data set. To understand the behavior of steps in a broader context, we compiled from the literature, PZCs for low-index and vicinal Au (Figure c). Along 111 → 100 and 100 → 110, the lowest PZC occurs at intermediate vicinals (e.g., Au(511), Au(210)) rather than at the end points. In contrast, along 111→110 the PZC varies approximately linearly between (111) and (110). Slight separations near the (110) corner (e.g., ≈0.03 V between Au(331) and Au(110)) mean any intermediate-vicinal minimum could be present there, but for now remains unresolved, because the PZCs cluster tightly.

Taken together, these comparisons indicate that several vicinal surfaces have PZCs lower than either parent low-index face. Thus, stepped surfaces are not reproduced by a simple sum of (111)/(100)/(110) responses; step density and geometry impose their own electrostatic signature. Accordingly, stepped vicinals should be treated as independent surface types rather than decomposed into their parents. This picture has a number of important implications, which are perhaps not surprising, but which are nevertheless sometimes not expressed explicitly enough in the literature. A polycrystalline surface does not have a single PZC in the sense that the entire surface is uncharged at that potential. Quoting a PZC of a polycrystalline surface should take this into account. A minimum in the capacitance curve of a polycrystalline surface does not represent the potential of zero charge of that surface, but at best only the PZC of a specific facet. Relating the electrochemical or electrocatalytic activity of a polycrystalline surface with its PZC or capacitance minimum should therefore be done with great caution.

Conclusions

In this work, we revisited the classical view that polycrystalline gold is “Au(110)-dominated” because its double-layer capacitance minimum coincides with the Au(110) minimum. By combining facet-resolved benchmarks, EBSD-mapped polycrystals, and geometry-defined cylinder electrodes, we show that the real picture is more nuanced. The strong, potential-dependent response of Au(110) masks, but does not eliminate, the contributions of Au(111) and Au(100): when the geometry and facet fractions are controlled, their signatures emerge as additional capacitance minima on the very same macroscopic electrode. In this way, we were able to resolve three separate capacitance minima on a single gold electrode, attributable to three “local” facet-dependent potentials of zero charge.

Within the resolution of our experiment, grain boundaries are found to be electrostatically silent. On the other hand, stepped vicinal surfaces, such as Au(554), are intrinsically nonadditive: their capacitance curves and minima cannot be reconstructed from simple sums of parent low-index facets, reflecting distinct local charging at terraces and steps. Taken together, these results establish that the double layer of polycrystalline gold is best understood as the area-weighted sum of all electrochemically distinct surface domains, low-index terraces, vicinals, and steps, rather than as true (110) dominance.

On a more practical note, many mechanistic and catalytic studies are performed on polycrystalline electrodes and implicitly assume that “poly Au” is a well-defined, transferable reference. Our results show that this assumption is fragile. Even when two polycrystalline electrodes are nominally identical, unknown and uncontrolled facet distributions, and the masking of weaker responses by stronger ones, can shift which local environments actually dominate the measured signal.

This does not only apply to facet-dependent catalytic activity arising from different surface structures, but also to facet-dependent electrolyte or double-layer effects arising from different local PZC values. Unless the difference between two polycrystalline surfaces is very large, comparing their activity or “EDL effects” without structural context can therefore be misleading. What we demonstrate here for Au is that once facet composition and geometry are constrained, the underlying logic becomes simple: the macroscopic response is the weighted sum of distinct local surface states, some visible due to their electrostatic visibility, some masked. This framework can, in general, be directly extended to other metals and to Faradaic processes whose kinetics are governed by the same local interfacial structure.

Experimental Section

Chemicals and Materials

Electrolytes were prepared using high-purity reagents: HClO4 (60% Suprapur, Sigma-Aldrich), HClO4 (70% ROTIPURAN Ultra, Carl Roth), H2SO4 (Suprapur, Merck), KMnO4 (≥99%, Sigma-Aldrich), and H2O2 (30%, Sigma-Aldrich). All solutions were prepared with ultrapure water (Milli-Q Gradient, ≥ 18.2 MΩ cm, TOC < 5 ppb). Argon gas (Ar, 6.0 purity, Linde) was used to purge electrolytes. Gold wire (99.99% purity, Mateck) was used as the counter electrode

General Electrochemical Methods

The glassware was stored overnight in a 1 g/L solution of KMnO4 and H2SO4. Before use, the glassware was submerged in a mixture of H2SO4 and H2O2 to remove any trace of KMnO4 and MnO2. Then, the glassware was washed and boiled six times using ultrapure water. All electrochemical measurements were carried out using a Biologic VSP-300 potentiostat. The measurements were carried out in a homemade borosilicate glass cell, where the reference electrode was separated from the working compartment using a Luggin capillary. A shunt capacitor of 10 μF was connected with the RHE, and a Au wire, 99.99% purity, was used as a counter electrode. Before every experiment, the electrode was purged with Argon (Ar) gas for ∼ 20 min. A constant Ar gas blanket was maintained above the electrolyte throughout the measurements to prevent O2 from entering the cell.

Single crystals with a thickness of 2 mm and a diameter of 5 mm, 1 cm (purity 99.999%, Mateck GmbH) were used, along with two polycrystalline gold electrodes with diameters of 5 mm and 2 mm, respectively, and a thickness of 1 mm purity 99.999%). They were annealed and then cooled down before adding a droplet of degassed Milli-Q water. It is crucial to stress that the Au crystals should be annealed to a point where they glow, but the orange-glowing crystal should be cooled down before being brought into contact with water. The polycrystalline electrodes were only annealed, not polished.

Capacitance Plots

The Au(111) single crystal was brought into the hanging meniscus configuration under a controlled potential of 0.06 V vs RHE. Some of the measurements described below were performed at very low electrolyte concentration, with no additional supporting electrolyte. The distance between the working electrode and the Luggin capillary tip was kept at ∼ 1 cm. The electrolyte’s Ohmic drop was determined by electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) at 0.06 V (vs RHE; double-layer region), and 85% correction was applied during measurement. It is important to note that the current was extremely low in some measurements despite a high solution resistance; correcting it did not yield a significant shift in potential when the 85% ohmic drop correction was not applied. Potentials are reported vs RHE unless otherwise noted; conversions to SHE correspond to pH 0 (i.e., NHE). Sample Characterization

The surface facet distribution of the two polycrystalline samples was determined using a scanning electron microscope (SEM) (Zeiss Gemini 45) equipped with an Electron Backscatter Diffraction (EBSD) detector (Oxford Instruments, Symmetry S), at a voltage of 20 kV and a beam current of 2 nA.
