Integrative advances in biomarker-driven prostate cancer management from genomic discovery to precision oncology
Authors: Priyanka Ramesh, Leena Shree, Alex Stanley Balraj
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Prostate cancer (PCa) is the second most common malignancy in men worldwide, with rising mortality linked to late-stage diagnoses. While current diagnostic strategies rely heavily on biomarker detection, their limitations highlight the need for comprehensive and integrative biomarker discovery. This review consolidates recent advances in PCa biomarkers, encompassing genetic, proteomic, liquid biopsy, and imaging-based approaches. Genetic mutations inform disease prognosis and therapy selection, while proteomic biomarkers elucidate molecular mechanisms and therapeutic targets. Blood- and urine-based biomarkers, including exosome-derived markers, circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA), PCA3, and SelectMDx, enable minimally invasive risk stratification. Artificial intelligence–assisted analysis of multiparametric MRI and PSMA PET/CT has been explored to support lesion detection and staging, although most AI-based tools remain investigational. Integrating these biomarker-driven strategies into clinical workflows supports precision oncology and improves patient outcomes. Despite challenges in assay standardization, validation, cost, and ethical implementation, the convergence of genomics, liquid biopsy, and AI technologies marks a transformative step toward personalized prostate cancer management. To distinguish clinically validated tools from emerging candidates, biomarkers are discussed according to their level of evidence and clinical readiness.

Introduction

Cancer is characterized by uncontrolled cellular proliferation and spread, with prostate cancer (PCa) representing a major global health concern and one of the leading causes of cancer-related mortality in men. According to the World Health Organization (WHO, 2025) and GLOBOCAN 2022, nearly 20 million new cancer cases and 9.7 million deaths occur annually, accounting for approximately one in six global deaths [1]. In men, PCa ranks as the fourth most prevalent malignancy worldwide and a leading cause of cancer mortality. Data from the National Cancer Institute’s (NCI) Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) program indicate that the five-year survival rate exceeds 99% for localized or regional PCa but declines sharply to approximately 34% when metastasis occurs, underscoring the urgency of early detection and precision diagnostics [2].

Prostate cancer typically develops within the prostate gland and manifests through symptoms such as weak urine flow and increased frequency. Clinicians commonly evaluate disease progression using two staging systems: they use biomarkers and imaging for clinical staging, and they determine pathological staging after surgical intervention. They assess tumor aggressiveness using the Gleason score (GS), which ranges from 6 to 10 and reflects histological differentiation and prognosis. Established clinical parameters such as Gleason score, PSA level, and tumor stage remain the foundation of prostate cancer risk stratification and provide the clinical context within which biomarker-based approaches are applied.

The histopathological features used in prostate cancer grading, illustrating the progression from low to high - risk with increasing Gleason score and structural disorganization [3]. PCa primarily affects older men, with most diagnoses occurring after the age of 65 [4]. Lifestyle and dietary factors, including high consumption of red meat and dairy products and low intake of fruits and vegetables, have been linked to increased risk. Prostate-specific antigen (PSA) testing remains a cornerstone of prostate cancer screening and early detection [5]; However, its limited specificity and inability to reliably distinguish indolent from aggressive disease underscore the need for more accurate and clinically informative biomarkers. The relationship between PSA levels, tumor grade, and disease stage, including patterns of local progression and distant metastasis. Recent advances in genomics, proteomics, liquid biopsy technologies, and molecular imaging have further enhanced patient stratification and improved prognostic assessment, contributing to more personalized treatment strategies and better clinical outcomes. These staging and grading systems form the clinical framework within which biomarkers are applied for early detection, risk stratification, treatment selection, and disease monitoring.

When prostate cancer metastasizes, it becomes treatable but not curable. Treatment paradigms have evolved beyond chemotherapy to include androgen deprivation therapy (ADT), androgen receptor signaling inhibitors, targeted therapies such as PARP inhibitors, radioligand therapy, and immunotherapeutic—reflecting a shift toward personalized precision oncology [6].

This review provides an integrated overview of prostate cancer biomarkers. It also addresses their translational potential, clinical applicability, and ethical considerations.

Given the rapid expansion of proposed biomarkers, a major challenge is distinguishing clinically validated tests from exploratory discoveries. To address this, this review adopts a structured framework that categorizes biomarkers according to their level of clinical validation and readiness for routine use. This approach aims to provide a balanced interpretation, highlight translational gaps, and clarify which biomarkers currently influence patient management versus those that remain primarily research tools.

Search strategy and study selection

A literature review was conducted to identify relevant studies on prostate cancer biomarkers. Publications were retrieved from PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science, with emphasis on studies published between 2010 and 2025.

The search included a combination of keyword-based terms such as “prostate cancer biomarkers,” “genetic biomarkers,” “liquid biopsy,” and “precision oncology.” Additional references were identified through manual screening of reference lists from relevant articles.

Studies were selected based on their relevance to diagnostic, prognostic, or predictive biomarkers in prostate cancer. Clinical studies, cohort analyses, and key review articles were considered, with preference given to work that provided clinically interpretable findings.

While efforts were made to include clinically relevant and well-described studies, this review does not follow a formal systematic review protocol, and the selection process was not exhaustive. The included studies are intended to provide a representative overview of the current biomarker landscape.

Biomarker landscape in prostate cancer

Levels of evidence and clinical readiness of prostate cancer biomarkers

The landscape of prostate cancer biomarkers has expanded rapidly over the past decade, encompassing markers that range from long-established clinical tools to newly proposed experimental candidates. This diversity, while scientifically valuable, also presents a challenge: emerging biomarkers are sometimes discussed alongside validated tests despite major differences in their clinical reliability and real-world applicability. To support balanced interpretation and avoid overstating the translational relevance of early discoveries, this review adopts a structured, evidence-based framework for classifying biomarkers according to their level of validation and clinical readiness.

Biomarkers are grouped into three categories

Level I—clinically validated biomarkers

These markers are supported by robust evidence from large prospective clinical studies, demonstrate consistent performance across independent cohorts, and are incorporated into major international clinical practice guidelines (such as NCCN and EAU). They have a direct impact on patient care, contributing to diagnostic decision-making, risk stratification, prognosis, or treatment selection.

Level II—clinically available biomarkers under ongoing validation

This group includes biomarkers that are commercially available or frequently evaluated in clinical studies, but for which the evidence base remains moderate or is still evolving. They are commonly used as complementary tools alongside established clinical parameters yet are not universally accepted as independent determinants of clinical management.

Level III—experimental or exploratory biomarkers

These candidates are primarily supported by preclinical investigations or small-scale retrospective clinical studies. Although they often provide important biological insights and hold promise for future translation, their current level of validation is insufficient for routine clinical application.

This classification scheme is applied consistently throughout the review to establish a clear hierarchy of clinical utility, to highlight existing gaps between discovery and implementation, and to distinguish biomarkers that currently inform clinical practice from those that remain at the research stage.

Conceptual framework for biomarker integration in prostate cancer

Biomarker development in prostate cancer has progressed from single-analyte serum testing to multi-parameter molecular and imaging approaches. At present, clinical adoption has been uneven, and only a limited subset of biomarkers is routinely used in patient care. For clarity, the biomarkers discussed in this review are organised according to their principal clinical function: screening and early detection, (ii) risk stratification and prognosis, (iii) treatment selection, and (iv) disease monitoring.

Prostate-specific antigen (PSA) and its derivatives remain the primary tools for population-level screening and longitudinal surveillance, despite well-recognised limitations in specificity. Blood and urine-based assays are mainly used as adjuncts to refine biopsy decisions in selected patients rather than as independent diagnostic standards. Tissue-based genetic alterations, particularly defects in homologous recombination repair genes, are currently the most reliable molecular markers for guiding targeted therapy in advanced disease.

This framework is intended to distinguish biomarkers that presently influence routine clinical decisions from those for which evidence remains limited, and to provide a structured context for interpreting emerging technologies discussed in subsequent sections.

Although a wide range of biomarkers has been investigated, only a small number currently demonstrates sufficient clinical validity to influence patient management. Accordingly, this review emphasizes clinically actionable biomarkers (Level I) as primary determinants of decision-making, while emerging and exploratory biomarkers (Level II–III) are discussed with a focus on their potential rather than established utility. This distinction is intended to maintain a balanced translational perspective and avoid overinterpretation of early-stage findings.

Genetic biomarkers: key mutations and pathways in prostate cancer

Genetic alterations play a central role in prostate cancer initiation, progression, and therapeutic response. However, the clinical relevance of individual mutations varies substantially. While some genetic biomarkers are now essential for treatment selection, others remain primarily prognostic or exploratory. To reflect this heterogeneity, genetic biomarkers are discussed according to their level of clinical validation [7].

Clinically validated genetic biomarkers (level I)

BRCA1 and BRCA2

Among all genetic biomarkers in prostate cancer, alterations in BRCA1 and BRCA2 have the clearest and most direct impact on patient care. These genes play a central role in repairing damaged DNA. When they are mutated, cancer cells lose this repair ability and become highly sensitive to PARP-inhibitor therapy.

Large clinical studies in men with metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer have consistently demonstrated improved outcomes when PARP inhibitors are used in BRCA-mutant tumors, leading to regulatory approval and incorporation into international treatment guidelines. As a result, BRCA testing is now routinely recommended for patients with advanced or high-risk disease to guide therapeutic decisions [8, 9].

Ataxia-Telangiectasia mutated (ATM) mutations

Mutations in the ATM gene are also common in advanced prostate cancer and reflect impaired DNA damage signaling. Clinical evidence suggests that ATM-mutated tumors behave more aggressively than ATM-wild-type disease.

However, responses to PARP inhibitors in ATM-mutant patients are more variable than in BRCA-mutant cases. Most available data come from subgroup analyses and retrospective cohorts rather than dedicated randomized trials [10]. For this reason, ATM status is currently used mainly for risk stratification and clinical-trial selection rather than as a strong standalone predictor of treatment response. Evidence for ATM-directed therapy is derived primarily from subgroup analyses and retrospective cohort studies, with lower response rates compared to BRCA-mutant tumors [11, 12].

CDK12

CDK12 mutations define a distinct molecular subtype characterized by tandem duplications and high neoantigen burden. These tumors often display limited response to androgen receptor signaling inhibitors but may exhibit enhanced sensitivity to immune checkpoint inhibitors. CDK12 is therefore increasingly recognized as a predictive biomarker for immunotherapy response and is included in many clinical sequencing panels [13].

Collectively, HRR-related genes (BRCA1, BRCA2, ATM, and CDK12) represent the strongest genetic biomarkers currently available in prostate cancer, with direct therapeutic implications and high-level clinical evidence.

PCA3

PCA3 (Prostate Cancer Antigen 3) is a prostate cancer–specific long non-coding RNA detectable in urine after prostatic massage and is one of the most clinically validated genetic biomarkers for prostate cancer diagnosis, particularly in patients with elevated PSA levels and previous negative biopsies. Although PCA3 is a non-coding RNA biomarker, it is discussed here under genetic biomarkers due to its transcriptional origin and molecular specificity.

In men undergoing repeat saturation biopsy, Pepe and Aragona showed that the PCA3 score had significantly higher diagnostic specificity than the free/total PSA ratio, improving discrimination between malignant and benign disease and reducing unnecessary repeat biopsies. In a subsequent study, a PCA3 cut-off of 35 provided higher specificity, whereas a cut-off of 20 improved sensitivity for cancer detection [14, 15]. These findings support the use of PCA3 as an adjunct diagnostic tool in repeat biopsy decision-making.

Although PCA3 does not guide targeted therapy, its reproducible diagnostic performance and availability as a commercial assay support its classification as a clinically validated genetic biomarker.

Prognostic and emerging genetic biomarkers (Level II–III)

IDH1 mutations

Recurrent IDH1 mutations have been reported in a rare pathological subtype of prostate cancer associated with psammoma Tous calcifications. While these tumors display unique metabolic and epigenetic features, their clinical behavior and therapeutic vulnerabilities remain incompletely understood.

At present, evidence for IDH1 as a clinically useful biomarker is limited to small pathological series and retrospective genomic studies, and no treatment guidelines currently incorporate IDH1 testing [16].

WNT pathway alterations

Mutations in WNT signaling components (e.g., APC, RNF43, CTNNB1) have been linked to increased tumor invasiveness, epithelial–mesenchymal transition, and metastatic potential. Retrospective analyses suggest an association with poorer survival outcome [17]; At present, prospective validation is lacking, and no WNT-targeted therapies are currently approved for prostate cancer. As such, these alterations remain primarily prognostic and experimental. Current evidence is limited to retrospective genomic analyses and small pathological series, without prospective clinical validation [18].

ctDNA and CIN: genomic drivers of precision oncology in prostate cancer

Integrating protein biomarkers with electrochemical biosensors enhances the sensitivity and specificity of PCa detection, strengthening diagnostic capabilities [19]. Furthermore, detecting chromosomal instability in circulating DNA shows promise as a prognostic marker in metastatic hormone-sensitive PCa, aiding in refined patient risk stratification [20]. ctDNA is released into the bloodstream by tumor cells undergoing apoptosis or necrosis, while Chromosomal Instability (CIN) is detected using various NGS techniques [21]. Key markers include broad copy number alterations, chromosome arm-level gains/losses, and mutational signatures linked to chromosomal mis segregation [22]. High CIN levels in ctDNA are linked to poor outcomes, including a higher risk of metastasis and progression to lethal disease. Collectively, these advancements highlight the evolving landscape of PCa management, reinforcing the vital role of genetic testing and tailored therapies in improving patient outcomes and advancing precision medicine [23]. These findings remain exploratory and should be interpreted cautiously, as they do not currently influence routine clinical decision-making.

Critical synthesis and translational limitations

Although numerous genetic alterations have been described in prostate cancer, only a small number currently influence routine clinical care. HRR gene defects represent the only group of genetic biomarkers with robust predictive value for treatment response. Most other mutations provide prognostic information at best and remain insufficiently validated for therapeutic guidance.

Several challenges further limit the clinical impact of genetic biomarkers, including intratumoral heterogeneity, sampling bias from tissue biopsies, and variability in sequencing platforms. Liquid biopsy approaches offer partial solutions but are not yet fully standardized. Large multi-institutional studies and prospective biomarker-driven trials are essential to establish causality between specific genetic alterations and therapeutic benefit.

In summary, although numerous genetic alterations have been described in prostate cancer, only a small subset currently influences routine clinical practice. Defects in homologous recombination repair genes, particularly BRCA1, BRCA2, ATM, and CDK12 represent the only genetic biomarkers with consistent therapeutic relevance.

Most other mutations, including IDH1 and WNT-pathway alterations, remain biologically informative but clinically unvalidated. Their supporting evidence is largely retrospective or preclinical. Future multicentred trials and biomarker-driven treatment studies will be essential to determine whether these emerging candidates can move beyond prognostic research tools and into standard patient management.

Protein-based biomarkers in prostate cancer: clinical value and limitations

Protein biomarkers provide direct insight into tumor activity and cellular signaling and therefore offer a functional complement to genomic alterations. However, despite extensive investigation, very few protein biomarkers have achieved sufficient validation for routine clinical use. Most candidates remain investigational and serve primarily as indicators of tumor aggressiveness or as therapeutic targets rather than as decision-making tools.

To reflect this disparity, protein biomarkers are discussed according to their level of clinical evidence.

Most protein biomarkers are supported by preclinical experiments or retrospective observational studies, with a notable absence of randomized clinical trial validation.

Clinically validated protein biomarkers (level I)

Prostate-specific antigen (PSA)

PSA remains the only protein biomarker firmly established in routine clinical practice and is widely used for screening, monitoring treatment response, and detecting biochemical recurrence. Despite its clinical utility, PSA lacks cancer specificity and is frequently elevated in benign conditions, contributing to overdiagnosis and overtreatment. Nonetheless, no alternative protein biomarker has yet demonstrated comparable validation or acceptance [24].

To improve diagnostic specificity, several PSA derivatives have been introduced, particularly for patients with PSA values in the intermediate range (4–10 ng/mL). The ratio of free to total PSA (fPSA/tPSA) is the most widely applied of these markers and is commonly used to support biopsy decision-making.

In a large case-finding study of 14,453 men, Pepe and Aragona demonstrated that incorporation of the fPSA/tPSA ratio significantly reduced the detection of clinically insignificant prostate cancer compared with total PSA alone. Lower fPSA/tPSA values were associated with clinically relevant disease, whereas higher ratios were more frequently observed in benign conditions and low-risk tumors, indicating improved patient selection for biopsy and reduced overdiagnosis [25].

In a separate study involving men undergoing repeat saturation biopsy, the same authors reported that although the fPSA/tPSA ratio improves cancer detection compared with total PSA alone, its specificity remains limited in distinguishing clinically significant prostate cancer from benign disease. These findings confirm that PSA derivatives are useful adjunctive tools for diagnostic risk stratification but remain insufficient as standalone diagnostic markers, particularly in patients with previous negative biopsies.

Protein biomarkers with moderate or emerging clinical relevance (level II)

BET proteins & MVP

Proteins involved in transcriptional regulation, such as BET family members (particularly BRD4), play important roles in androgen-receptor signaling and therapy resistance. Early-phase studies suggest that BET inhibition may suppress tumor growth; In contrast, their role remains primarily therapeutic rather than diagnostic, and they are not currently used as biomarkers for patient stratification [26].

Similarly, major vault protein (MVP) overexpression correlates with poor prognosis and immune checkpoint expression in advanced prostate cancer. While consistently associated with aggressive disease, MVP has not yet demonstrated sufficient reproducibility or predictive value to justify clinical implementation [27].

Experimental protein biomarkers and therapeutic targets (Level III)

Rho GTPases

Rho GTPases regulate cytoskeletal remodeling, invasion, and metastasis. Although they represent attractive therapeutic targets, evidence supporting their utility as clinical biomarkers is currently limited to preclinical and small cohort studies [28].

PROS1 (protein S)

PROS1 contributes to androgen-independent tumor growth and immune modulation. Elevated expression has been observed in metastatic disease, but its clinical application remains restricted to experimental research contexts.

Protein tyrosine kinases (PTKs) and phosphatases (PTPs)

Dysregulated phosphorylation signaling via EGFR, HER2, and IGF-1R plays a key role in prostate cancer progression. While PTKs are actively targeted therapeutically, their expression patterns have not demonstrated reliable prognostic or predictive performance as standalone biomarkers [29].

HPV-related oncogenic proteins

Viral oncoproteins such as E6 and E7 have been detected in a subset of prostate tumors, but causality and clinical relevance remain controversial. Current evidence does not support routine testing.

Overall, these protein biomarkers remain primarily investigational and do not currently alter clinical management.

Critical synthesis

In contrast to genetic biomarkers, protein-based biomarkers in prostate cancer remain largely underdeveloped in clinical terms. Except for PSA, no protein biomarker has reached Level I evidence for guiding diagnosis or treatment selection. Many candidates demonstrate biological relevance but suffer from limited reproducibility, assay variability, and inconsistent performance across patient cohorts.

Most candidates are supported by preclinical experiments or retrospective observational studies, with a notable lack of randomized clinical trial evidence. Until standardized assays and prospective validation become available, protein biomarkers should be regarded as complementary research tools rather than determinants of clinical management.

Liquid biopsy and exosomal biomarkers in prostate cancer: evidence-based evaluation

Liquid biopsy approaches such as ctDNA and circulating tumor cells provide minimally invasive tools for disease monitoring. In prostate cancer, these approaches are primarily investigated as complementary tools for molecular characterization and longitudinal disease assessment, particularly when repeated tissue sampling is impractical [30]. However, as with other biomarker classes, their level of clinical maturity varies considerably.

Clinically informative liquid biopsy biomarkers (Level II)

ctDNA burden and chromosomal instability

Quantification of ctDNA and assessment of chromosomal instability provide prognostic information in metastatic and hormone-sensitive prostate cancer. Elevated ctDNA levels correlate with disease burden, treatment resistance, and reduced survival [31]. Although increasingly incorporated into clinical trials and translational studies, ctDNA testing has not yet been standardized sufficiently for universal routine application [32].

Circulating tumor cells

Circulating tumor cell counts show similar prognostic value but remain technically demanding and costly, limiting widespread clinical adoption.

Predictive liquid biopsy biomarkers (Level II–I)

Exosomal AR-V7

Detection of the androgen receptor splice variant AR-V7 in circulating exosomes represents the most clinically actionable liquid biopsy biomarker to date. AR-V7 positivity is associated with resistance to androgen receptor signaling inhibitors such as enzalutamide and abiraterone while retaining sensitivity to taxane chemotherapy. Although not yet universally mandated in guidelines, AR-V7 testing is increasingly recognized as a valuable predictive biomarker in advanced disease [33].

Experimental and emerging liquid biopsy biomarkers (Level III)

Exosomal microRNAs (miR-141, miR-375, miR-451a)

Several miRNAs demonstrate associations with tumor aggressiveness and metastatic progression. In contrast, inter-study variability and lack of standardized detection platforms limit their clinical utility [34].

Exosomal proteins (PSMA, CD9, CD63)

Protein cargo within exosomes reflects tumor activity and metastatic behavior, but current evidence remains preliminary [35].

Metabolomic signatures

Urinary and plasma metabolite profiles show preliminary evidence for early detection and risk stratification but remain confined to research settings [36]. Despite promising associations, these biomarkers have not yet reached the level of validation required for routine clinical application.

Critical synthesis

Liquid biopsy biomarkers represent one of the most promising advances in prostate cancer diagnostics due to their non-invasive nature and ability to capture dynamic tumor evolution. Nevertheless, only a small subset—most notably AR-V7—currently demonstrates predictive clinical value.

Most liquid biopsy markers remain prognostic rather than therapeutic decision tools. Major obstacles include pre-analytical variability, differences in isolation protocols, absence of universal cutoff values, and limited multicenter validation. Until these challenges are resolved, liquid biopsy biomarkers should be considered adjunctive technologies rather than replacements for established tissue-based or imaging diagnostics.

Clinical biomarker tests have become essential tools in prostate cancer evaluation by improving the accuracy of early detection and risk stratification. Clinical prioritization of prostate cancer biomarkers based on evidence and utility

To account for varying levels of clinical evidence, we have categorized these biomarkers by their validation status and their utility in guiding clinical interventions.

High-priority biomarkers (Level I–clinically actionable)

These biomarkers have strong evidence from large clinical studies and are incorporated into clinical guidelines. Prostate-specific antigen (PSA) remains the primary tool for screening and monitoring. Multiparametric MRI (mpMRI) and PSMA PET/CT are essential imaging biomarkers for diagnosis and staging. Genetic alterations in homologous recombination repair genes, particularly BRCA1 and BRCA2, directly guide the use of PARP inhibitors in advanced prostate cancer.

Moderate-priority biomarkers (Level II–clinically supportive)

These biomarkers support clinical decisions but are not used independently. Tests such as Prostate Health Index (PHI), 4Kscore, PCA3, and SelectMDx improve risk stratification and assist biopsy decisions. Liquid biopsy markers such as circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) and AR-V7 provide prognostic or predictive information, particularly in advanced disease settings.

Low-priority biomarkers (Level III–investigational)

Most emerging biomarkers, including exosomal microRNAs, metabolomic signatures, and several protein candidates, remain experimental. Although biologically informative, these markers lack prospective validation and are not currently recommended for routine clinical use.

From a clinical perspective, this prioritization highlights that only a limited subset of biomarkers directly influences patient management, while the majority remain research tools requiring further validation.

From a clinical standpoint, decision-making in prostate cancer remains predominantly guided by a small group of highly validated biomarkers, including PSA, mpMRI, PSMA PET/CT, and homologous recombination repair gene alterations. In contrast, most other biomarkers serve supportive or investigational roles and should not be interpreted as equivalent in clinical impact.

Evidence-based prioritization of prostate cancer biomarkers and their clinical applicability

Clinical application of biomarkers across the prostate cancer care pathway

Although a large number of biomarkers have been reported in prostate cancer, only a small number are currently integrated into routine clinical practice. Their utility becomes clearer when considered within specific stages of patient management.

Screening and early detection

Serum PSA remains the primary tool for initial screening and early detection. In men with borderline or mildly elevated PSA levels, adjunctive blood- and urine-based assays, including PHI, 4Kscore, PCA3, SelectMDx, and ExoDx Prostate, are used to refine risk assessment and support decisions regarding prostate biopsy. These tests improve specificity but do not replace histopathological confirmation.

Diagnosis and local staging

Following initial evaluation, imaging plays a central role in disease assessment. Multiparametric MRI, interpreted using PI-RADS criteria, is widely used for lesion localization and targeted biopsy. PSMA PET/CT has emerged as a valuable modality for detecting metastatic disease and evaluating biochemical recurrence, thereby contributing to more accurate staging.

Risk stratification and prognosis

After diagnosis, risk assessment is based on established clinical parameters, including Gleason score, PSA level, and TNM stage. Genomic classifiers and selected liquid biopsy approaches provide additional prognostic information, particularly in identifying patients at higher risk of disease progression. However, these markers are generally used as adjuncts rather than standalone decision-making tools.

Treatment selection and precision therapeutics

The most direct clinical application of biomarkers is observed in advanced disease. Alterations in homologous recombination repair genes, particularly BRCA1 and BRCA2, are used to guide the use of PARP inhibitors. In addition, biomarkers associated with treatment resistance, such as AR-V7, may inform therapeutic choice in selected clinical settings.

Disease monitoring and treatment response

PSA remains the standard biomarker for monitoring treatment response and disease progression. Imaging, including PSMA PET/CT, is increasingly used to assess recurrence and treatment outcomes. Emerging approaches such as circulating tumor DNA analysis show potential for dynamic disease monitoring but are not yet part of routine clinical practice.

Translational perspective

Despite the large number of proposed biomarkers in prostate cancer, routine clinical management relies on a limited set of well-validated tools applied at specific decision points. These include PSA for screening and monitoring, selected blood- and urine-based assays for biopsy decision-making, mpMRI and PSMA PET/CT for diagnosis and staging, and HRR gene testing and AR-V7 for treatment selection. Most other biomarkers remain investigational and are not yet incorporated into standard clinical practice, underscoring the gap between biomarker discovery and clinical implementation.

Imaging innovations: radiological biomarkers in focus

Following blood- and urine-based biomarker assays used for early detection and biopsy decision-making, imaging biomarkers represent the next level of clinical evaluation by providing anatomical localization and functional assessment of prostate cancer. Unlike molecular biomarkers, imaging techniques do not directly characterize genomic or proteomic alterations but contribute essential information for diagnosis, staging, and treatment planning [48–50].

Multiparametric MRI

Multiparametric magnetic resonance imaging (mpMRI) is currently the most widely used imaging biomarker in localized prostate cancer. By integrating T2-weighted, diffusion-weighted, and dynamic contrast enhancement imaging, mpMRI improves lesion detection and characterization compared with conventional ultrasound-guided approaches [51–54]. Standardized interpretation using the Prostate Imaging Reporting and Data System (PI-RADS) supports structured reporting and risk stratification and is routinely applied to guide targeted biopsy and assess local tumor extent.

Despite its clinical utility, mpMRI performance depends on image acquisition protocols, radiologist expertise, and interobserver variability. False-negative findings may occur in small or low-grade tumors, and inflammatory or benign prostatic conditions can reduce specificity [55–58]. Consequently, mpMRI is used as a complement to histopathology and molecular testing rather than as a standalone diagnostic tool [59, 60].

PSMA PET/CT

Prostate-specific membrane antigen positron emission tomography/computed tomography (PSMA PET/CT) has become an important imaging modality in advanced disease. It is primarily used to detect nodal and distant metastases, evaluate biochemical recurrence, and identify patients eligible for PSMA-targeted radioligand therapy [61].

Compared with conventional imaging, PSMA PET/CT demonstrates higher sensitivity at low PSA levels; however, uptake can vary among tumor subclones, and PSMA-negative lesions may be missed. Access, cost, and radiotracer availability also limit widespread implementation in some healthcare settings. As with mpMRI, PSMA PET/CT findings are interpreted alongside clinical parameters and molecular biomarkers to guide management decisions. Recent evidence also supports an emerging role for PSMA PET/CT in the primary evaluation and staging of clinically significant prostate cancer. In a prospective study of men enrolled in active surveillance, Pepe et al. reported that 68Ga-PSMA PET/CT detected focal uptake consistent with clinically significant disease in a subset of patients initially classified as low risk. Imaging findings led to risk reclassification and modification of clinical management in several cases, indicating that PSMA PET/CT may complement multiparametric MRI in identifying tumors with higher biological aggressiveness. Although these results are preliminary and derived from a limited cohort, they suggest that PSMA PET/CT has potential value beyond metastatic staging, particularly for detecting occult high-grade disease and refining initial risk stratification. Larger prospective studies are required before routine use can be recommended for primary diagnosis [62].

Clinical positioning of imaging biomarkers

In current practice, imaging biomarkers function mainly as supportive diagnostic and staging tools. They refine tumor localization, assess disease burden, and assist in treatment planning, but they do not reliably predict molecular treatment response or replace genomic testing in precision oncology. Emerging imaging approaches, including radiomics, artificial intelligence–assisted interpretation, and experimental molecular tracers, are discussed separately in the following section as investigational tools requiring further validation.

Different biomarker classes contribute distinct types of clinical information in prostate cancer management. As summarized in Table 3, genetic biomarkers provide the most direct guidance for treatment selection, particularly in tumors with defects in DNA repair pathways. Proteomic and liquid biopsy markers offer indirect information on tumor activity and disease dynamics, whereas imaging biomarkers remain primarily relevant for anatomical localization and staging. Together, these approaches provide complementary perspectives on disease status and support integrated clinical assessment.

Translational and clinical applications of biomarkers in prostate cancer

Biomarker integration with emerging diagnostic platforms

Recent studies have explored the integration of multiple biomarkers with advanced diagnostic platforms to improve the accuracy of prostate cancer detection. Multiplexed approaches, particularly electrochemical biosensors, enable the simultaneous detection of biomarkers such as PSA, PCA3, and kallikrein-related markers, addressing tumor heterogeneity and improving diagnostic sensitivity [60, 61].

Several experimental platforms have demonstrated enhanced analytical performance compared with single-marker assays. For example, graphene oxide–based biosensors have shown high sensitivity for PSA detection, while urine-based platforms targeting β2-microglobulin have been evaluated for distinguishing localized from advanced disease [63]. These approaches highlight the potential of combining multiple biomarkers within a single analytical framework to improve risk stratification and reduce unnecessary invasive procedures [19, 64, 65].

In addition, emerging biomarker platforms incorporating exosomal and circulating tumor–derived components provide insights into disease biology and treatment response. At present, these technologies remain at an early stage of development and have not yet been incorporated into routine clinical workflows [48, 66, 67].

Overall, while integrated biomarker platforms represent a promising direction for improving diagnostic precision, their clinical utility is currently limited by a lack of standardization, limited validation in small cohorts, and the absence of prospective clinical studies [68].

Biomarkers for treatment selection and precision therapeutics in prostate cancer

Biomarker-driven treatment selection represents one of the most clinically relevant applications of precision oncology in prostate cancer [59, 69]. However, only a limited subset of biomarkers currently demonstrates sufficient evidence to guide therapeutic decisions [70, 71].

Within the Level I framework, alterations in homologous recombination repair genes, particularly BRCA1 and BRCA2, are the most clinically actionable biomarkers. These alterations directly inform the use of PARP inhibitors in advanced prostate cancer and are incorporated into clinical guidelines [72, 73].

Additional biomarkers, including ATM and CDK12 mutations, provide supportive information for treatment stratification but show variable predictive value and are therefore considered Level I–II. Similarly, AR-V7 serves as a predictive biomarker for resistance to androgen receptor signaling inhibitors, supporting therapy selection in specific clinical contexts [74].

In contrast, most emerging therapeutic strategies, including nanotechnology-based drug delivery systems and experimental targeted therapies, are not yet linked to validated predictive biomarkers. In these approaches, evidence remains limited and fall within Level III, reflecting the current gap between therapeutic innovation and biomarker-guided patient stratification [75].

Overall, treatment selection in prostate cancer remains guided by a small number of validated molecular biomarkers, while the majority of proposed therapeutic strategies lack clinically actionable biomarker support. Further progress will depend on continued clinical validation and integration of biomarker-driven therapeutic strategies.

Prognostic and predictive biomarkers in advanced prostate cancer

Advanced prostate cancer is managed using multiple systemic therapies, including androgen receptor pathway inhibitors, taxane chemotherapy, PARP inhibitors, and PSMA-targeted radioligand therapy; however, treatment response remains heterogeneous, highlighting the need for reliable prognostic and predictive biomarkers.

Clinically applied prognostic and response markers

Several clinical parameters are routinely used to monitor therapeutic response and estimate disease progression. These include prostate-specific antigen decline following treatment initiation, PSA doubling time, baseline PSA level, and biochemical markers such as alkaline phosphatase and gamma-glutamyl transferase [76, 77]. A multi-institutional study by the Young Academic Urologists Working Group of the European Association of Urology reported significant associations between these variables and response to PSMA-targeted radioligand therapy as well as overall disease progression [78].

PSMA expression assessed by PSMA PET/CT is also used to determine eligibility for radioligand therapy. Nevertheless, intratumoral heterogeneity and the presence of PSMA-negative lesions limit its predictive accuracy [79, 80].

Molecular biomarkers

Among molecular markers, alterations in homologous recombination repair genes, particularly BRCA1, BRCA2, ATM, and CDK12, provide the strongest evidence for predicting benefit from PARP inhibitor therapy and selected immunotherapeutic approaches [81].

PSMA expression and PSA kinetics remain important markers for monitoring disease activity and therapeutic response, as summarized in Table 4. Several non-coding RNAs, including long non-coding RNAs and microRNAs, have been associated with tumor aggressiveness and recurrence in observational studies but lack sufficient prospective validation for routine clinical use. Circulating tumor DNA and RNA have also been linked to treatment resistance and disease progression, although methodological variability limits their standardization [82].

Computational models

Computational approaches integrating molecular and clinical data have been explored for prognostic stratification and outcome prediction. While these models demonstrate feasibility in retrospective datasets, their clinical reliability remains uncertain because of limited external validation and cohort heterogeneity. Their application is therefore currently restricted to research settings.

Critical synthesis

At present, prognostic and predictive assessment in prostate cancer relies primarily on clinical variables, PSA kinetics, homologous recombination repair gene status, and PSMA expression for radioligand therapy selection. Most additional molecular and computational biomarkers remain investigational and should be interpreted cautiously outside clinical trial settings.

Ongoing clinical trials and emerging biomarker-based technologies in prostate cancer

Several experimental technologies are being investigated to improve biomarker detection and molecular characterization in prostate cancer. Nanomaterial-based biosensors, particularly electrochemical platforms incorporating carbon nanotubes and gold nanoparticles, have demonstrated high analytical sensitivity for prostate-specific antigen detection in laboratory studies and may offer technical advantages over conventional immunoassays. Nevertheless, their diagnostic performance in large clinical cohorts and routine practice has not yet been established.

In parallel, multiple long non-coding RNAs, including PCA3, CTBP1-AS, HOTAIR, PCGEM1, MALAT-1, and PCAT-1, have been associated with tumor progression, androgen receptor signaling, and recurrence risk in transcriptomic analyses [83]. Although these markers provide insights into disease biology, their clinical applicability is limited by variability between studies, lack of standardized assays, and insufficient prospective validation [84, 85].

Ongoing clinical trials are also evaluating targeted immunotherapeutic agents and kinase inhibitors in combination with molecular profiling strategies [86]. At present, these approaches primarily reflect therapeutic development rather than validated biomarker-guided management. Overall, most emerging biomarker-based technologies remain exploratory and require rigorous clinical validation before integration into routine prostate cancer care.

Recent advances and multi-omics integration in prostate cancer

Multi-omics approaches integrating genomic, transcriptomic, and metabolomic data have been increasingly explored to improve biomarker discovery in prostate cancer. These strategies aim to capture tumor heterogeneity more comprehensively than single-marker approaches [87].

Several candidate biomarkers, including proliferative markers such as MKI67 and regulatory non-coding RNAs, have shown associations with tumor aggressiveness and disease progression in retrospective analyses [88–91]. Additional evidence supporting the clinical relevance of microRNAs has been provided by Salemi et al., who evaluated the expression of miR-132 and miR-212 in primary prostate tumors and metastatic lymph node tissue. However, these findings are derived from limited cohorts and lack consistent validation across independent studies.

Despite their biological relevance, integrated multi-omics signatures have not yet demonstrated reproducible clinical utility. Key limitations include variability in data integration methods, lack of standardized analytical pipelines, and absence of prospective validation studies [92]. These findings were validated using decision tree algorithms on independent datasets, reinforcing the potential of genetic biomarkers in PCa management.

Artificial intelligence in biomarker discovery and clinical prediction

Artificial intelligence (AI) has emerged as a promising tool for integrating complex datasets in prostate cancer research, particularly in biomarker discovery and imaging-based risk assessment. AI-based approaches have been applied to radiological data, histopathology, and multi-omics datasets to identify patterns associated with disease progression and treatment response [96, 97].

In clinical imaging, AI-assisted analysis of multiparametric MRI and PSMA PET/CT has shown potential in improving lesion detection, risk stratification, and prediction of clinically significant prostate cancer. Similarly, machine learning models have been explored for integrating genomic and transcriptomic data to identify candidate biomarkers associated with tumor aggressiveness [98].

However, several limitations restrict the clinical implementation of AI-based biomarkers. These include variability in data quality across datasets, lack of standardized analytical frameworks, limited external validation, and challenges in reproducibility across independent cohorts [99]. In addition, most studies remain retrospective, with insufficient prospective clinical validation.

Overall, AI currently serves as a supportive tool for biomarker discovery rather than a source of clinically validated biomarkers. Accordingly, its role in routine clinical decision-making remains limited.

Ethical and regulatory considerations in biomarker-driven prostate cancer care

The integration of biomarker-based strategies into prostate cancer management raises important ethical considerations related to informed consent, interpretation of results, psychological impact, data privacy, and equitable access to testing. Patients must be adequately informed about the clinical purpose, limitations, and potential implications of biomarker analyses, particularly when testing may reveal hereditary cancer risk or variants of uncertain significance [100]. Misinterpretation of results may lead to unnecessary anxiety, overtreatment, or inappropriate therapeutic decisions in low-risk disease [17].

Genetic testing introduces additional challenges, including emotional distress associated with germline findings and the need for structured pre- and post-test counseling. Data confidentiality is critical, as biomarker results represent sensitive personal health information with implications for both patients and their families. Furthermore, disparities in healthcare infrastructure, testing availability, and reimbursement policies limit access to advanced molecular diagnostics in resource-constrained settings, thereby exacerbating existing inequalities in cancer care.

Addressing these challenges requires standardized reporting frameworks, appropriate genetic counseling, robust data protection measures, and policies that promote equitable and affordable access to clinically validated biomarker technologies.

Regulatory considerations for biomarker implementation

Regulatory oversight is essential to ensure the analytical validity, clinical validity, and clinical utility of biomarker tests used in prostate cancer management. Agencies such as the U.S. Food and Drug Administration require evidence that biomarkers provide actionable information that improves patient outcomes or informs therapeutic decision-making [101].

However, variability in laboratory procedures, testing standards, and result interpretation across institutions remains a challenge. Emerging technologies, including multi-omics assays and AI-assisted diagnostic tools, further complicate regulatory evaluation [102]. Harmonization of validation protocols, standardization of testing methodologies, and clinician training are therefore necessary to support consistent and evidence-based implementation of biomarker-guided precision oncology [103].

Future directions in prostate cancer precision oncology

PCa management is poised for significant advancement as innovations in genomics, proteomics, imaging, and artificial intelligence continue to reshape clinical practice. Integrating these technologies into routine workflows will enable more accurate diagnosis, refined risk stratification, and improved monitoring of disease progression. The incorporation of AI-driven analytical models into multi-omics data interpretation is expected to deepen our understanding of tumor heterogeneity, treatment resistance, and rare PCa subtypes, strengthening decision-making in precision oncology. Progress will require large-scale, multi-ethnic validation studies, real-world data integration, and cost-effective biomarker deployment to ensure broad clinical utility. Interdisciplinary collaboration—bringing together molecular scientists, computational experts, and clinicians—will be essential for translating emerging biomarkers and predictive models into everyday practice. As data harmonization improves and access to advanced diagnostics expands, biomarker-guided approaches hold the potential to extend survival, reduce overtreatment, and meaningfully enhance the quality of life for patients with prostate cancer.

Future research should also focus on validating biomarker performance across multiethnic populations, as current evidence is heavily centered on Western cohorts. Cost-effectiveness studies are needed to determine whether advanced genomic tests, liquid biopsy assays, and PSMA-based imaging can be adopted in routine practice, especially in resource-limited settings. Integrating biomarker data into clinical decision-support systems will help clinicians use these tools more effectively. In addition, innovative trial designs such as basket, umbrella, and adaptive clinical trials are essential for accelerating the evaluation of targeted therapies guided by biomarker profiles.

Conclusion

Prostate cancer biomarker research has expanded substantially, but only a limited subset currently demonstrates clear clinical utility. Prostate-specific antigen, selected blood- and urine-based assays, imaging modalities such as multiparametric MRI and PSMA PET/CT, and homologous recombination repair gene alterations remain the most consistently applied tools in clinical practice.

In contrast, most emerging biomarkers, including multi-omics signatures and AI-based models, remain investigational and require further validation before routine implementation [104].

The key challenge in this field is not the identification of new biomarkers, but the translation of promising candidates into clinically actionable tools supported by robust evidence.

Future progress will depend on well-designed prospective studies, standardization of biomarker assays, and integration into clinical decision-making frameworks.
