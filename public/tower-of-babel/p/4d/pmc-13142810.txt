Alternating Right and Left Bundle Branch Block: Management in a Patient with Cardiac Amyloidosis
Authors: Avitasha Darshni, Mohammad Paymard
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Alternating bundle branch block (ABBB) is a phenomenon described by the presence of both right bundle branch block (RBBB) and left bundle branch block (LBBB) in the same individual at different points in time. We present the case of a 74-year-old male with known cardiac amyloid who was managed in hospital for an ABBB in the context of an accidental fall. Serial electrocardiograms (ECGs) revealed transient alternating RBBB and LBBB. The patient underwent dual-chamber permanent pacemaker implantation. This case reviews the management of alternating bundle brunch blocks and highlights the complexities of treating conduction disease in patients with cardiac amyloid.

INTRODUCTION

Alternating right bundle branch block (RBBB) and left bundle branch block (LBBB) is reflective of significant disease affecting the infra-Hisian conduction system, which has a high probability of progressing to complete heart block (CHB) and its associated complications, including syncope and heart failure.[1] The aetiology of this abnormality can be multifactorial, including structural heart disease, inflammation, scar, and fibrosis. In cardiac amyloidosis, arrhythmias occur due to the accumulation of insoluble amyloid fibrils in the myocardium, which distorts the structure of myocytes and conductive fibers, thereby affecting the transmission of electrical impulses through conduction pathways.[2]

Atrial fibrillation (AF) is the most common arrhythmia to affect patients with both light chain (AL) and transthyretin (ATTR) subtypes of cardiac amyloid.[2] In additon, approximately 50% of amyloid patients have wide QRS complexes on their baseline electrocardiograms (ECGs), followed by 1st degree heart block affecting another 40%–50% of this cohort.[3] The type of bundle branch block also has some correlation to the subtype of amyloid, for example, LBBB is more common in patients with ATTR amyloid. However, RBBB is equally prevalent amongst both ATTR and AL subtypes.[4] Approximately 10% of the ATTR amyloid population is affected by high-grade atrioventricular (AV) block that eventually requires management with pacemaker implantation.[3]

CASE PRESENTATION

A 74-year-old male presented to a regional emergency department (ED) with back pain radiating to his anterior chest. An hour prior, he had sustained a mechanical fall while walking down a set of stairs. There were no preceding presyncopal symptoms, head strike or loss of consciousness. The patient’s medical background was significant for ATTR cardiac amyloidosis diagnosed in 2020, managed with tafamidis 61 mg daily and vutrisiran subcutaneous injections every 3 months. In addition, he had ischemic heart disease with previous coronary artery bypass grafts; paroxysmal AF anticoagulated with apixaban and hypertension.

On examination, the patient was hemodynamically stable with an irregular pulse. Heart sounds were dual with nil murmurs, and chest auscultation revealed inspiratory crepitations up to the left mid zone and right lung base. There was no evidence of peripheral edema.

Initial ECG taken in ED demonstrated an atrial tachycardia with LBBB, most likely consistent with a cavotricuspid isthmus-dependent atrial flutter [Figure 1]. A subsequent ECG taken 5 min later showed a new RBBB, which later resolved [Figure 2]. Blood tests revealed haemoglobin of 141 g/L, creatinine 97 umol/L, and serial high sensitivity troponin I of 62 ng/L and 61 ng/L. In addition, the coagulation profile showed a prothrombin time of 12 s, activated partial thromboplastin time of 32 s, and an international normalized ratio of 1.0. A computed tomography (CT) aortogram demonstrated a large left pleural effusion concerning for haemothorax with no evidence of dissection or pulmonary embolism. Transthoracic echocardiography completed 12 months prior demonstrated moderate-to-severe left ventricular (LV) wall thickness with an infiltrative appearance. Ejection fraction (EF) was 50% with dyssynchronous ventricular activation. Global longitudinal strain was − 6.1% with relative apical sparing. Moderate tricuspid regurgitation was present with preserved right ventricular (RV) function.

A clear cause for the haemothorax was not identified, given there was no CT evidence of fractures affecting the ribs or vertebrae. However, given that the patient was anticoagulated with apixaban, his baseline risk of bleeding was higher. The patient subsequently underwent imaging-guided thoracocentesis to drain the haemothorax. Following this procedure, he underwent implantation of a dual-chamber pacemaker for long-term management of his conduction disease.

DISCUSSION

Alternating bundle branch block (ABBB) is a rare but serious arrhythmia which requires semiurgent medical attention. Given his background of ATTR amyloid, this patient already had an underlying mechanism to develop arrhythmias and conduction disease, reflected by the presence of pre-existing paroxysmal AF and LBBB. Fortunately, the patient had no prior history of dizziness or syncope. Although this episode of ABBB was transient, there was a high likelihood that this abnormality could reoccur or progress to a higher degree of AV block in the future. Nonetheless, guidelines provide a class 1 recommendation for permanent pacemaker insertion to manage ABBB even in the absence of symptoms such as syncope.[5]

Device selection in amyloid patients is challenging because management of both potential arrhythmias and possible LV dysfunction needs to be considered. Individuals with LV impairment, prolonged QRS complex duration, and a history of malignant rhythms such as ventricular tachycardia would qualify for cardiac resynchronisation therapy (CRT) devices with defibrillation capacity. On the other hand, patients with preserved EF and normal QRS complex duration are not candidates for resynchronisation therapy therefore, RV septal pacing is the standard practice for this cohort. In amyloidosis, standard RV pacing can cause dyssynchrony and pacing-induced cardiomyopathy, which can further exacerbate heart failure symptoms. Therefore, left bundle branch area pacing (LBBAP) in patients who are not eligible for biventricular pacing offers the best option to achieve physiological pacing while preventing worsening heart failure symptoms.

In this case, the patient had preserved LV function; therefore, there was no indication for biventricular pacing or an automated implantable cardioverter defibrillator. Given the background of paroxysmal AF, he qualified for a dual-chamber device to optimise function while in sinus rhythm. The procedure was commenced with the intention to perform LBBAP however, intraprocedurally, the patient experienced electrical instability with recurrence of ABBB and due to concerns for CHB, a decision was made to proceed with RV septal pacing instead. A Medtronic pacemaker was implanted, programmed to DDD mode 60–130 bpm. All lead parameters intra-operatively were satisfactory, and the procedure was uncomplicated (sensed R wave 16 mV, sensed P wave 1 mV). The patient will be closely monitored, and if LV function deteriorates in the future, his device would be upgraded from RV septal pacing to a CRT device or LBBAP at that point.

CONCLUSION

The above is an interesting case of ABBB occurring in a patient with known cardiac amyloidosis and highlights the importance of timely detection and management of this phenomenon. It also reinforces the challenges associated with device selection in these patients and the factors that need to be considered before proceeding with device implantation.
