Age-sex-specific risk and burden of fall injury incidence associated with ambient temperature in China: A case-crossover study with 4.19 million cases
Authors: Guanhao He, Yuliang Er, Weiquan Zeng, Jiamei Zeng, Yuan Wang, Xiao Deng, Tao Liu, Jianxiong Hu, Qijiong Zhu, Ye Jin, Cuirong Ji, Ziqiang Lin, Fengrui Jing, Leilei Duan, Pengpeng Ye, Wenjun Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryFall injury poses considerable disease burden. However, limited studies investigated the age-sex-specific risk and burden of fall injury associated with ambient temperature. A time-stratified case-crossover study with 4.19 million fall injury cases from the National Injury Surveillance System was conducted in China. We identified a U-shaped relationship between temperature and fall injury in the total population, with higher risk from heat than cold. There were significant age-sex-specific disparities in temperature-related fall injury risk, with diminished heat effects and increased cold effects as age rises, particularly among females. Ambient temperature exhibited a substantial burden on fall injuries, with much higher burden associated with heat than with cold across almost all age groups. Compared to the 2020s, our projections indicate a 1.22-fold increase in heat-related attributable fraction (AF) and a 0.38-fold decrease in cold-related AF under the SSP5-8.5 scenario. These findings can guide injury prevention and control strategies in the context of climate change.

Introduction

Injuries, as the fifth leading cause of death, are garnering increased attention. Falls rank as the second most common cause of unintentional injury fatalities globally, accounting for approximately 684,000 deaths annually.1 Moreover, an estimated 172 million falls result in short- or long-term disabilities each year in the world.2 In China, falls are also the primary cause of injuries and the second leading cause of injury mortality.3 According to the Global Burden of Disease (GBD), the incidence, prevalence, and mortality rates associated with fall injuries in China have surged since 2005 (GBD Data: https://vizhub.healthdata.org/gbd-results/), which indicates fall injuries to be a pressing public health concern.

The GBD 2021 findings also underscored a notable age disparity in fall-related injury that the age-specific incidence rate, age-specific death rate, and age-specific DALYs (disability-adjusted life years) rate of falls were all positively correlated with age globally.4 Regional study also indicated that falls were the major contributor to higher DALYs among the older populations in countries of the Association of Southeast Asian Nations (ASEANs).5 In China, the incidence rate of falls among people aged 60 and above is 3,799.40 per 100,000 population, with 39.20 deaths per 100,000 population attributed to falls6; and for children and adolescents, the incidence rate is 2,261.10 per 100,000 individuals (GBD Data: https://vizhub.healthdata.org/gbd-results/). GBD also reported a higher mortality rate of falls in male (12.27 per 100,000 people, 95% CI: 10.50–13.47) than in females (7.86 per 100,000 people, 95% CI: 6.18–8.88).4 Although males bear a greater fall-related burden in the overall population, this disparity exhibits age heterogeneity. Specifically, the incidence of falls is higher among young men compared to women, whereas the incidence of falls among women surpasses that of men after the age of 60.4 This age-sex disparity was also observed in specific region such as ASEAN.5 A recent study in China reported that children, adolescents, and the elderly represent a larger proportion of patients with a higher burden in young males and old females.7

According to the Conference of the Parties Declaration on Climate and Health, identifying and protecting vulnerable populations is a key strategy in addressing the health challenges posed by climate change.8 Therefore, understanding demographic patterns, such as age-sex-specific distribution of climate-related health impacts, is important, since it provides vital information to identify vulnerable age and gender groups for implementing targeted adaptation actions. Although previous studies have explored age-specific or sex-specific patterns in temperature-injury relationships separately,9,10,11,12,13 few have jointly examined age and sex subgroups. This integrated approach is critical for identifying the most vulnerable subpopulations with greater precision. For instance, while some evidence suggests males and children face higher heat-related injury risks overall,9,10 such broad categorizations may mask important within-group variation, such as differences between young girls and boys, or between older women and men. A recent study focused solely on temperature and fall mortality among the elderly, while mortality represents only the most severe outcome and fails to capture the full burden of fall injuries.11 Moreover, by restricting the study population to older adults, that analysis could not reveal how temperature-fall associations evolve across the entire life course.

To address these knowledge gaps, this study provides a comprehensive picture of age-sex-specific risk and burden of fall injury incidence associated with ambient temperature across the entire life course. Using 4.19 million fall injury cases from the National Injury Surveillance System (NISS) covering 31 Chinese provinces, we jointly estimated temperature-attributable risks for fine-grained age-sex subgroups. This approach allows us to identify potentially vulnerable subgroups that earlier studies could not resolve, for example, whether young males may be at higher risk during hot temperatures and older women during cold temperatures. Our findings may offer an evidence base for designing demographically targeted adaptation strategies to protect those most susceptible to temperature-related fall injuries.

Results

The characteristics of the study sample

During the period from 2006 to 2021, a total of 4.19 million fall-related injury cases were recorded in surveillance hospitals across 94 counties/districts. Of these, 2.49 million (59.39%) were males and 1.70 million (40.61%) were females. The age groups with the highest proportions of fall injuries were those aged 0–9 years (17.83%), 20–29 years (14.02%), and 30–39 years (13.84%). The proportion of fall injuries was higher among males than females in individuals below 60 years old, whereas the opposite was observed in individuals aged 60 years and above (Table 1). Age-specific and sex-specific association between temperature and fall injury

As depicted in Figure 1, the relationship between daily mean temperature and fall injuries among the total population was U-shaped, with greater risks at high temperatures than at low temperatures. Notably, the exposure-response associations significantly varied across different age groups. For children and adolescents under the age of 20, there existed a positive association between ambient temperature and fall injuries, peaking at 24.9°C for those aged 0–9 years and at 21.6°C for those aged 10–19 years. As age increased, the risk associated with low temperatures escalated, whereas the risk linked to high temperatures diminished. This trend manifested as a J-shaped curve for young adults aged 20–39 years, a U-shaped curve for individuals aged 30–59 years, and an inverse J-shaped curve for those over 60 years. Age-sex-specific burden of fall injury attributed to ambient temperature

We observed that 23.35% (95% CI: 22.84%–23.65%) of fall injuries could be attributed to non-optimal temperature in the total population (Figure 3; Table S2). Notably, high temperatures accounted for much higher attributable fractions (AFs = 22.62%) than cold temperatures (AF = 0.73%). Higher AFs were observed in children (45.76%, 95% CI: 45.16%–46.08%) and adolescents (48.13%, 95% CI: 47.51%–48.43%), with a marked decline as age increases, reaching a nadir in the 70–79 years old (3.67%, 95% CI: 3.42–3.87%). In terms of sex, males exhibited substantially higher AFs (28.62%, 95% CI: 28.02%–28.97%) compared to females (15.64%, 95% CI: 15.28–15.87%).Figure 3Attributable fraction (AF, %) of fall injury attributed to heat and cold(A) Age-specific AF of fall injury attributed to heat and cold; (B) Age-sex-specific AF of fall injury attributed to heat; (C) Age-sex-specific AF of fall injury attributed to cold

After conducting an age-sex-specific analysis, we found that boys and girls aged 0–19 years exhibited a similar burden of heat-related fall injuries. However, as individuals entered adulthood, the decline in heat-related fall injuries for females was more rapid, leading to a gradual increase in the sex disparity of heat-related fall injuries. For example, 48.03% and 48.38% of fall injuries in the 10–19 age group were respectively attributed to heat for males and females, whereas the corresponding percentages for males and females were 3.28% and 1.99% in the 70–79 age group, respectively. No heat-related fall injuries were observed among those aged 80–89. Additionally, no cold-related fall injuries were recorded among individuals within the 0–19 age group; as age increased, females generally exhibited higher cold-related fall injury than males aged 50 and above.

Future fall injury burden attributed to temperature

We observed a significant increasing trend of fall AF attributed to heat while a declining trend of fall AF due to cold in the future (Figure 4). For instance, compared to the 2020s, our projections indicate a 1.22-fold increase in heat-related AF (from 14.89% to 18.21%) and a 0.38-fold decrease in cold-related AF (from 0.87% to 0.33%) under the SSP5-8.5 scenario (Figure 4; Tables S3 and S4). Comparable trends are projected under the SSP3-7.0 scenario, with a 1.18-fold increase and a 0.45-fold decrease in heat- and cold-related AFs, respectively (Table S4). We also observed a similar trend across various age and sex groups (Tables S5–S7). Although children and adolescents accounted for the largest proportion of fall burden in the future, middle-aged and elderly people experience the most significant increase in fall burden due to global warming (Table S4). Specifically, under the SSP5-8.5 scenario, from the 2020s to the 2090s, individuals aged 70–79, 60–69, 50–59, 40–49, 30–39 and 20–29 are projected to experience 1.41-fold, 1.32-fold, 1.39-fold, 1.28-fold, 1.29-fold and 1.27 increase in heat-related AF, respectively (Table S4). Sensitive analysis

In the sensitivity analysis, we observed similar temperature-fall associations when changing the maximum lag days from 0 to 3 days. The results were also stable when adjusting various air pollutions including PM2.5, O3, CO, NO2, and SO2. After excluding the cases during 2020–2021, we also observed similar results (Figure S1). Additionally, we changed the degrees of freedom for temperature, relative humidity, and PM2.5 from 3 to 5, and the main heat- and cold-related effect estimates remained consistent with the primary analysis (Figure S2). Finally, we conducted sensitivity analyses using B-splines, natural cubic splines, or linear terms for relative humidity and PM2.5, and observed consistent results (Figure S3).

Discussion

This study represents the first large-scale investigation into the age-sex-specific fall risk associated with ambient temperature. We identified a U-shaped relationship between temperature and fall injury in the total population, with a greater risk for heat than for cold. There were significant age-sex-specific disparities in temperature-related fall injury risk, with diminished heat effects and increased cold effects as age rises, particularly among females. Ambient temperature exhibited a substantial burden on fall injuries, with much higher burden associated with heat than with cold across almost all age groups. Global warming is expected to increase heat-related fall injury and decrease cold-related injury in the future. These findings indicate that the risks and burdens related to cold- and heat-induced fall injuries vary significantly based on age and gender, necessitating age-sex-specific adaptation strategies.

Consistent with our results, a previous study based on NISS in Ma’anshan City of China also found a U-shaped association between temperature and fall injury incidence.14 However, research in South Korea showed that emergency visits for fall injury increased at high temperatures.15 In addition, previous studies found a monotonously positive association between temperature and fatal fall injury.9,16 The inconsistency among various studies may be due to the different climate patterns, study samples, socio-economy, and research design.

We observed that both males and females under the age of 20 were at a higher risk for fall injuries when exposed to elevated temperatures. This finding aligns with prior research indicating an increase in fall-related injuries among children during warmer seasons and sunny days.14,17,18 When exposed to high temperatures, children are susceptible to heatstroke and dehydration because they sweat slower and have weaker temperature regulation.19 This impacts their physical state and may cause coordination loss, raising their fall risk. A potential explanation is that high temperatures necessitate opening windows for ventilation,20 thereby increasing the likelihood of children falling from heights. Second, a previous study demonstrated an inverse U-shaped relationship between temperature and outdoor activities,21 mirroring the temperature-fall correlation observed in children and adolescents. These consistent findings suggest that moderate warmth encourages risky outdoor behavior, thereby increasing the likelihood of fall injuries. Conversely, extremely high temperatures may limit outdoor activities, subsequently slightly decreasing the risk of falls compared to moderate warmth. Additionally, rapid urbanization may further intensify this risk due to children living in high-rise apartments.

We found that individuals aged 60 years and over had a higher risk of falling in low temperatures compared to high temperatures. This observation is consistent with several previous studies.15,22,23 During cold periods, icy and snowy roads become slippery, increasing the likelihood of falls and injuries among the elderly.24 Additionally, low temperatures and insufficient sunlight often coexist, leading to vitamin D deficiency and osteoporosis, which further elevate the risk of falls.25,26 Cold weather, frequently accompanied by gusty winds, heightens the difficulty of maintaining balance, thereby enhancing the likelihood of falls. Moreover, low temperatures may trigger the onset or aggravation of chronic diseases,27,28 which increases the fall risk. Furthermore, cold weather can cause vasoconstriction in elderly people, resulting in muscle stiffness, poor coordination, and increased risk of spasms and rigidity,29 thereby boosting the chances of falls. Finally, thick clothing in cold weather can lead to restricted mobility and induce a fall. Our findings indicate that older females are particularly vulnerable to cold-related fall injuries. This elevated risk can be attributed to an interplay of physiological, functional, and behavioral factors. Physiologically, the notably higher prevalence of osteoporosis among older females in China not only increases the likelihood of fall injuries but may also impair thermoregulation and reduce cold tolerance, potentially accelerating body heat loss in cold environments.30 Functionally, the higher rates of vision and cognitive impairments in this demographic can hinder the perception of slippery or uneven terrain and slow reaction times, deficits that are exacerbated on cold days when walking surfaces may be icy or obscured.31,32 Furthermore, behavioral patterns may also contribute. Older women often constitute a larger proportion of the community-dwelling elderly and engage in more essential outdoor activities (e.g., grocery shopping, childcare) than their male counterparts,33,34,35 thereby increasing their exposure to cold conditions. The convergence of these factors—greater baseline risk of fracture, impaired sensory-motor function, and potentially higher exposure—explains the disproportionate burden of cold-related fall injuries borne by older women.

To accurately identify the populations susceptible to non-optimal temperature, this study calculated age-sex-specific AFs of fall injuries due to temperature. Our findings indicate that 23.35% of fall injuries can be linked to non-optimal temperatures, which underscores that the population-level burden of fall injuries attributable to non-optimal temperatures is substantial and warrants increased public health attention. Additionally, our results show that children and adolescents bear a higher heat-related burden of falls, whereas the elderly experience a greater cold-related AF. Conversely, when considering fatal injuries as an outcome, previous studies have highlighted a higher AF among the elderly.9 This discrepancy implies that non-optimal temperatures may predominantly cause non-fatal falls in younger individuals but fatal falls in older populations. Across most age groups, males exhibit a higher heat-induced fall burden, while females demonstrate increased cold-related AFs. Although prior studies have documented a higher injury burden due to elevated temperatures in males,9 greater cold-related burden in females has not been previously reported, and the underlying reasons remain unclear.

The future burden of injuries attributable to climate change is an emerging public health concern. Studies in the US and China both showed that global warming could increase excess injury mortality.9,16,36 However, no study was found to project fall injury incidence attributable to global warming in China. The current study first projected the total and age-sex-specific temperature-related fall injury incidence attributed to temperature. According to our results, global warming leads to an increase in the future disease burden of falls, with a rising burden due to heat and a declining burden due to cold. Additionally, the burden of falls attributed to future temperatures will intensify with a heightened greenhouse gas emissions scenario. Furthermore, children and adolescents will bear the largest fall burden in the future; middle-aged people will see the most significant increase due to global warming. Our findings indicate that climate change might evidently increase fall injury risk and pose a considerable threat to population health, especially for children, adolescents, and middle-aged people in the future in China.

Our findings extend current knowledge by quantifying the age-sex-specific risk patterns of fall injuries related to non-optimal temperatures. Crucially, we demonstrate a shift in vulnerability across the life course: heat is the predominant risk factor for children and adolescents, whereas cold exposure poses a greater risk to the elderly, particularly older women. This inversion of risk challenges a uniform approach and provides a valuable evidence base to inform stratified climate adaptation planning. Our projections further suggest a differential future increase in heat-related fall burden, with the most significant rise occurring among middle-aged and elderly populations. This highlights the compounding challenge posed by an aging population structure and global warming, suggesting the importance of lifecycle-oriented adaptation strategies that integrate fall prevention into public health preparedness for climate change.

These insights offer important implications for policy development. A dual-track, demographically informed intervention strategy may be warranted. For children and adolescents, who bear the highest heat-related burden, policies could focus on environmental and behavioral modifications during warm periods, including safer playground designs and public health education for caregivers. For the elderly, particularly women, interventions may need to prioritize mitigating cold-related risks through winter preparedness programs, community-based balance training, and integrated geriatric care. Implementing an enhanced early warning system that delivers population-tailored fall prevention advice during temperature extremes could facilitate a precision public health approach and help allocate resources effectively to protect the most vulnerable.

In conclusion, our study observed age-sex-specific differences in fall risk and burden related to ambient temperature. The risk and burden of falls associated with heat diminished with age increase, while those associated with cold escalated, notably among females. Overall, the burden of heat-related fall injuries was significantly higher than that of cold-related fall injuries, particularly for individuals aged 60 years and younger, whereas for people aged 60 years and over, the cold effect was more prominent than heat, especially among the oldest old. These findings provide crucial insights into age-sex-specific fall injury risks and burdens associated with temperature, which can guide injury prevention and control strategies in China in the context of climate change.

Limitations of the study

Our research has several strengths. Primarily, it is the first study to utilize a national database from China to explore the relationship between temperature and the incidence of fall injuries. Additionally, our large sample size enables us to perform age-sex-specific subpopulation analyses, identifying vulnerable populations to heat and cold. However, we must acknowledge certain limitations. Firstly, as our study depends on hospital-based injury data, there is a risk of underreporting fatal injuries, which could potentially bias our findings. Secondly, although the case-crossover study design inherently controls for time-invariant confounders, it does not adjust for the influence of time-varying confounders. Thirdly, due to the lack of future injury incidence data, projecting fall injury morbidity burden attributable to climate change was based on historical temperature-fall associations and assumed constant incidence of fall injuries, which overlooks socio-economic changes. As economic and technological progress continues, adaptive capacity to climate change is likely to enhance, potentially leading to reductions in both the incidence of fall injuries and temperature-related injury risks. Consequently, the projected impacts of climate change on injury burden may be overestimated. Fourthly, considering that the projected age-sex-specific population data are solely available at the provincial level, the estimation of AFs has been carried out at the provincial level, rather than at the individual level. This approach may potentially lead to an underestimation of AFs, as the temperature range considered at the provincial level may be narrower compared to the variations that could exist at the individual level. Fifthly, NISS may underreport fall-related injuries because minor injury cases and out-of-hospital deaths do not result in an emergency visit. Sixth, our decision to simplify the exposure-response function from a spline to a linear form was based on visual inspection (monotonic and nearly linear), supported by previous literature,9,37,38 and further evaluated by the statistical significance of the linear regression coefficients. Nonetheless, we acknowledge some subjectivity remains.

Method details

Study area

This study utilized data from the National Injury Surveillance System (NISS) in China. The NISS includes 243 surveillance hospitals in 94 counties/districts across 31 provincial administrations (refer to Figure S4). Detailed information on the NISS was shown in a previous study.39

Study design

In the present study, we employed a time-stratified case-crossover design. For each injury case, we compared the exposure to the daily mean temperature on the day of injury occurrence (case days) with that on corresponding days of the week within the same calendar month (control days). This method enabled each injury case to act as its own control, with each case being matched to 3 or 4 self-controls. By using this design, we effectively minimized potential confounding effects from various factors, such as long-term and seasonal trends, day of the week, sex, age, economic status, and lifestyle.9,16

Fall injury incidence data

Fall injury is defined as an event which results in a person coming to rest inadvertently on the ground or floor or other lower level.40 During the period from 2006 to 2021, data on fall-related injuries were gathered from the National Injury Surveillance System (NISS) in China. Within the NISS framework, a “fall injury case” is defined as an individual presenting for the first time at any emergency department of surveillance hospitals due to an injury event related to a fall. A standardized form was utilized to record the details of each fall injury case. This form consisted of three sections: the first section contained general information about the case, such as name, sex, age, identification number, residential address, educational background, and occupation; the second section detailed the specifics of the injury, including the precise time of the injury, the time when medical attention was sought, the mechanism causing the injury, the location and activity at the time of injury, and the intention behind the injury; the third section pertained to the clinical aspects of the injury, such as the nature of the injury, the body part affected, the severity of the injury, and the clinical diagnosis.39

Meteorological data

We collected data on hourly mean temperature (°C) and relative humidity (%) between 2006 and 2021 from the fifth-generation European ReAnalysis-Land (ERA5-Land) dataset, which is produced by the European Center for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts (ECMWF). The dataset provides data at a spatial resolution of 0.1 ° × 0.1 °, equivalent to 10 km × 10 km. The daily mean, minimum, maximum temperatures, and relative humidity data from ERA5-Land have been thoroughly validated against Chinese weather stations, showing good performance with R2 values of 0.95, 0.94, 0.93, and 0.84, respectively.13 We extracted grid data corresponding to each surveillance hospital to obtain hourly meteorological data.

To assess the future fall injury burden stemming from temperature in China, projected daily average temperatures spanning the period from 2020 to 2099 were utilized. These projections were sourced from a comprehensive database crafted by the Inter-Sectoral Impact Model Intercomparison Project (IMIP), leveraging the most recent advancements from the Coupled Model Intercomparison Project Phase 6 (CMIP6). CMIP6 incorporated various scenarios grounded in the shared socioeconomic pathways (SSPs), allowing for a nuanced understanding of how societal and economic developments might intersect with climate change to influence future temperatures and their associated health impacts.41 This study encompassed three distinct Shared Socioeconomic Pathways (SSPs): SSP1-2.6, representing a sustainable development trajectory with low greenhouse gas emissions; SSP3-7.0, depicting a medium-to-high development path accompanied by moderate-to-high greenhouse gas emissions; and SSP5-8.5, a fossil fuel-dominated development scenario characterized by high greenhouse gas emissions.42 Utilizing five General Circulation Models (GCMs) – GFDL-ESM4, IPSL-CM6A-LR, MPI-ESM-1-2-HR, MRI-ESM2-0, and UKESM1-0-LL – the study obtained projected daily mean temperatures under each SSP scenario. For each province in China, the daily mean temperatures from these five GCMs were extracted from the corresponding interpolated grids, taking into account the provinces’ specific latitudes and longitudes.

Air pollution data

In our study, we adjusted for air pollutant concentrations, specifically PM2.5 and O3, when estimating the temperature-injury association. The daily mean concentrations of PM2.5 and O3 were obtained from the National Earth System Science Data Center, which is part of China’s National Science & Technology Infrastructure (http://www.geodata.cn). The reliability of this data has been confirmed in a previous study.43

Statistical analysis

Estimation of the association between temperature and injury occurrence

A conditional logistic regression integrated with cross-basis functions of distributed lag non-linear model (DLNM) was applied to estimate the temperature-injury association as follow:(Equation 1)Log(P(Case=1))=cb(tem,lag)+ns(rh)+ns(PM2.5)+Holiday+Strata(ID)Log(P(Case = 1)) denotes the conditional probability of experiencing a fall injury incident. cb(tem,lag) denotes a cross-basis function to model the association on exposure-response dimension and lag-response dimension between temperature and fall injury. In constructing the cross-basis function, we applied a natural cubic spline function with 3 degrees of freedom (with three internal knots based on the 10th, 75th, and 90th percentiles of the temperature distributions) for the temperature dimension, consistent with prior studies.9,10 According to previous studies,9,37,38 if the association exhibited a monotonical and nearly linear pattern, we would further simplify the model by substituting the natural cubic spline function with a linear function. In our analysis, the exposure-response curve was visually inspected and found to be monotonic and approximately linear (Figures 1 and 2). We additionally employed linear regression to characterize the exposure-response association between temperature and fall injury, and all regression coefficients were statistically significant (Table S8), further supporting the use of a linear approximation. If the association showed “U” or “J” shape with minimum risk temperature (MRT), we would also conduct linearization below and above MRT, which were also informed by previously published studies.9,37,38 For the lag period selection, we conducted a preliminary analysis using hourly temperature and fall injury data to examine the lag structure of the association. We extended the maximum lag to 48 h to fully capture the temporal pattern (Figure S5). The results showed that the effect of temperature was most pronounced within the first 24 h and attenuated thereafter, consistent with previous studies reporting acute short-term effects of temperature on injuries.9,10 Based on this empirical evidence, we set the maximum lag to 1 day in the main analysis. Given the short lag duration, we modeled the lag-response relationship using a linear function, as a nonlinear function (e.g., spline) would require more lag points to be reliably estimated. Sensitivity analyses using alternative maximum lag periods (e.g., 2 days and 3 days) yielded consistent effect estimates, supporting the robustness of this choice (Figure S1). Consistent with previous studies,9,16 several potential confounders including relative humidity and PM2.5, were controlled in our model, and both were used natural cubic spline functions with 3 degrees of freedom and equidistant knots. Additionally, we considered the potential influence of holidays, represented as a binary variable indicating the public holidays in China. We estimated the associations of temperature with fall injury across different age and sex subgroups, and test their differences using Z test as follow:(Equation 2)Z=(b1−b2)/(SE12+SE22)Among them, b1 and b2 are the regression coefficients of the two groups, respectively; and SE1 and SE2 are the standard errors.

Assessing fall injury burden attributable to temperature

The attributable fractions (AFs) of fall injury attributed to temperature were assessed as follows:(Equation 3)AF(%)=∑(Freqage,sex,i×RRage,sex,i−1RRage,sex,i)∑Freqage,sex,i×100%where Freqage,sex,i refers to the age-sex specific number of fall injury at a specific temperature i, and RRage,sex,i represents the age-sex specific relative risk value associated with this specific temperature i. Utilizing this formula, we quantified the burden of fall injury attributable to non-optimal temperature. To derive the confidence interval for the AF, we employed Monte Carlo simulation (MCS), a robust method that generates random variables and assesses their inherent uncertainty by considering the unique characteristics of the underlying probability distribution.

Sensitivity analysis

To test the robustness of our findings, we conducted several sensitivity analyses. Firstly, we compared the results with and without the adjustment for PM2.5 and O3. Secondly, we changed the maximum lag period for temperature exposure from 0 to 3 days. Thirdly, given COVID-19 epidemic may influence the injury surveillance, we performed analysis without data of 2020–2021. Fourthly, we performed sensitivity analysis with different degrees of freedoms and spline functions.

All of the p values were two-sided. Type I errors were set at p < 0.05. The statistical analysis was conducted with R software (version 4.4.0, R Foundation for Statistical Computing). R packages of “dlnm” and “survival” were used for analysis.
