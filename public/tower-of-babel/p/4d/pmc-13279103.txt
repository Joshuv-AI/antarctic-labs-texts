From Caging to Uncaging With Bioadaptors: A Novel Paradigm in Coronary Revascularization
Authors: Placido Maria Mazzone, Marco Spagnolo, Simone Finocchiaro, Maria Sara Mauro, Giacinto Di Leo, Davide Capodanno
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The evolution of percutaneous coronary intervention has improved the outcomes of patients with coronary artery disease, but conventional drug‐eluting stents remain limited by late adverse events, reduced vessel compliance, and the inability to restore physiological function. The DynamX coronary bioadaptor aims to address these issues by combining the mechanical performance of drug‐eluting stents with a bioadaptive mechanism that restores natural vessel motion. The device features a cobalt–chromium scaffold connected by bioresorbable polymer elements that degrade 6 months after implantation. This process unlocks helical strands, enabling vessel pulsatility, compliance, and adaptive remodeling while maintaining lumen integrity. Preclinical studies demonstrated reduced vessel stress and restored physiological rotational motion. In the DYNAMIX (12‐Month Clinical and Imaging Outcomes of the Uncaging Coronary DynamX Bioadaptor System) study, patients exhibited increased vessel area without lumen loss, optimal strut apposition, and complete neointimal coverage at 12 months. The small BIOADAPTOR‐RCT (Randomized Controlled Trial of Sirolimus‐Eluting Bioadaptor Versus Zotarolimus‐Eluting Drug‐Eluting Stent) showed noninferiority to drug‐eluting stents with superior late lumen preservation and reduced neointimal hyperplasia. The larger INFINITY‐SWEDEHEART (Percutaneous Coronary Intervention With a Bioadaptor Compared to a Contemporary Drug‐Eluting Stent) trial confirmed low target‐lesion failure rates and a plateauing of adverse events after 6 months, highlighting the benefits of vessel motion restoration. The DynamX coronary bioadaptor may represent a significant advancement in percutaneous coronary intervention by providing early mechanical support and restoring vessel function. Further studies on complex anatomy and long‐term outcomes are needed to further define its role in interventional cardiology.

Over the past 4 decades, percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) has transformed the treatment of coronary artery disease, evolving from balloon angioplasty to bare‐metal stents and drug‐eluting stents (DESs). 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 While each innovation has incrementally improved the outcomes of PCI, stent‐related adverse events beyond 1 year remain a persistent challenge, with no evidence of a plateau in event rates. This issue is compounded by the permanent caging effect of metallic stents, which constrains vessel compliance, inhibits vasomotion and prevents positive adaptive remodeling of the artery.

Drug‐coated balloons (DCBs) offer an alternative approach to deliver antiproliferative drugs directly to the plaque, avoiding the need for permanent device implantation, which is attractive in selected anatomic settings, such as small‐vessel disease and in‐stent restenosis. 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 The development of bioresorbable scaffolds (BRSs) aimed to address these limitations by eliminating the permanent platform, allowing vessel pulsatility and reducing long‐term complications. 9 , 10 , 11 However, first‐generation BRSs were associated with thicker struts, higher thrombosis rates, and inferior acute performance compared with DESs, leading to limited adoption and the withdrawal of some devices from the market. 9 , 10 , 11 Additionally, first‐generation BRSs often failed to demonstrate long‐term benefits beyond the restoration of vessel integrity, as adverse events continued to accrue even after scaffold resorption. These shortcomings highlighted the need for a technology that not only minimizes adverse events but also preserves the acute mechanical support of DESs while restoring natural vessel function.

The DynamX bioadaptor (Elixir Medical Corp., Milpitas, CA) aims to address these unmet needs through a proprietary design that combines cobalt–chromium helical strands with biodegradable polymer elements. 12 This structure provides early radial strength and stability similarly to DESs, while allowing the device to unlock after 6 months as the polymer degrades. This process restores vessel pulsatility, compliance, and adaptive remodeling, mitigating nonplateauing adverse events associated with both DESs and BRSs. 12 Early evidence has demonstrated the potential of this novel device to improve long‐term outcomes by reducing the complications associated with traditional PCI technologies.

Brief History of Intravascular Metallic Devices: From Bare Metal Stents to Drug‐Eluting Stents

PCI has revolutionized the treatment of coronary artery disease (Figure 1). Initially, the introduction of balloon angioplasty was seen as a remarkable innovation but also presented several limitations and risks, including vessel elastic recoil, plaque dissection with acute vessel closure, and intimal denudation with medial tearing, which exposed the subendothelial matrix to blood and promoted platelet aggregation and thrombosis. 13 , 14 Additionally, restenosis rates were exceedingly high, often surpassing 50%, primarily due to neointimal hyperplasia. 14 , 15 This highlighted the need for a more durable solution, prompting the development of bare metal stents.

Bare metal stents provided structural support to the arterial wall, offering immediate mechanical stabilization to counteract elastic recoil and plaque dissection. 1 , 16 However, early clinical experiences with bare metal stents also revealed significant challenges. The metal platform posed a substantial risk of acute and subacute stent thrombosis, which was mitigated by adopting high‐pressure inflation for optimal stent expansion and implementing dual antiplatelet therapy. 17 , 18 Additionally, bare metal stents remained vulnerable to in‐stent restenosis, affecting 15% to 30% of lesions, typically emerging 6 to 9 months after implantation due to neointimal hyperplasia. 19

The advent of DESs marked a significant advance in PCI, overcoming many of the limitations of bare metal stents. In particular, the persistent challenge of restenosis, caused by neointimal hyperplasia, prompted the development of stents designed to deliver antiproliferative drugs locally, targeting the underlying pathological processes at the site of arterial injury. The first‐generation DESs were introduced in the early 2000s, combining a metal stent scaffold with a drug‐eluting polymer to enable the controlled, localized release of antiproliferative agents. 20 , 21 Numerous randomized controlled trials demonstrated a significant reduction in restenosis and target lesion/vessel revascularization with the use of such first‐generation DESs compared with bare metal stents. 20 , 21 , 22 , 23 , 24 , 25 , 26 Despite their initial success, first‐generation DESs were not without issues. The nonbiodegradable polymer coatings were associated with chronic inflammation and delayed endothelialization, contributing to an increased risk of late and very late stent thrombosis (Table 1). 27 , 28 , 29 , 30

The development of second‐generation DESs aimed to address these limitations, additionally, more biocompatible and biodegradable polymer coatings were introduced, improving tissue compatibility and minimizing long‐term inflammatory responses. 31 Another important advancement was the use of newer, more lipophilic antiproliferative agents like everolimus and zotarolimus, which allowed for deeper penetration into the vessel wall, leading to more effective inhibition of smooth muscle cell proliferation. 31

These advancements improved the mechanical and biological performance of second‐generation DESs, reducing stent thrombosis risk and maintaining low restenosis rates. 32 , 33 , 34 , 35 , 36 , 37 , 38 Despite significant technical improvements, concerns about long‐term safety remain. The occurrence of late adverse events and the need to reduce the duration of dual antiplatelet therapy have prompted the development of new devices and solutions, including metal‐free devices.

Metal‐Free PCI Device: Drug‐Coated Balloons and Bioresorbable Scaffolds

In recent years, new technologies designed to overcome the traditional limitations of metallic stents have gained increasing popularity in interventional cardiology. These innovations are based on the principle of “leaving nothing behind,” avoiding the implantation of a permanent metal scaffold in the coronary artery (Figure 1).

DCBs are semicompliant balloons made of polyurethane and nylon, coated with an antiproliferative drug, paclitaxel or sirolimus, embedded in a lipophilic and biocompatible polymer matrix (Table 1). This matrix enhances the drug’s solubility, facilitates its transfer to the vessel wall, and reduces its rapid release into the bloodstream. 39 The expansion of the DCB promotes homogeneous drug penetration into the vessel wall, facilitating vascular tissue healing and reducing the risk of excessive endothelialization, which underlies in‐stent restenosis. However, despite these advantages, PCI with DCB presents some complications, such as acute elastic recoil, flow‐limiting dissections, or the need for bailout stenting. Moreover, while these benefits are theoretically applicable to all settings of CAD, evidence for the use of DCBs is limited exclusively to small‐vessel disease and in‐stent restenosis, and a randomized clinical trial in the context of large‐vessel disease has shown disappointing results. 40 , 41

The BRS provides temporary mechanical support, preventing acute closure and recoil (Table 1). 42 Initial enthusiasm followed the first‐generation Absorb scaffold’s (Abbott Vascular, Santa Clara, CA) noninferiority to DES in target‐lesion failure (TLF) at 1 year. 11 However, early concerns arose with a multicenter registry reporting a 2.1% scaffold thrombosis rate at 6 months. 43 Subsequent trials and meta‐analyses confirmed a higher risk of scaffold thrombosis and target‐vessel myocardial infarction. 44 , 45 , 46 Thrombosis mechanisms include underexpansion, malapposition, and scaffold resorption, which may cause structural dislocation into the lumen between 6 months and 3 years. 47 Moreover, to enhance radial strength, BRS struts are thicker and wider than metallic stents, increasing blood stagnation and thrombosis risk. 47 These limitations notwithstanding, a 5‐year Absorb III analysis showed reduced thrombosis and TLF rates after 3 years, once resorption was complete. 10 A meta‐analysis of 5988 patients randomized to BRSs or DESs confirmed higher TLF rates with BRSs at 5 years, driven by early events. However, event rates between 3 and 5 years were comparable to DESs. 48

Unmet Needs in Coronary Revascularization

Despite significant advances in PCI, important challenges remain unaddressed. Stent‐related adverse events, such as in‐stent restenosis and late thrombosis, persist due to chronic inflammation and the permanent caging effect of metallic implants. 27 , 30 This caging restricts vessel compliance, inhibits vasomotion, and prevents the positive adaptive remodeling required to restore physiological vessel function. “Leave‐nothing‐behind” strategies were introduced to address these issues by eliminating permanent scaffolds and promoting vascular healing. However, as previously noted, DCBs have yet to convincingly replace DESs due to the lack of a structural platform, though ongoing trials may provide further insights. First‐generation BRSs, limited by thick struts, poor deliverability, and inferior acute performance, led to higher thrombosis and TLF rates. While newer BRSs show promise, long‐term data remain scarce, limiting clinical adoption.

These unmet needs underscore the need for a device that minimizes adverse events while restoring vessel function. The DynamX coronary bioadaptor addresses this challenge by combining DES‐like mechanical support with bioadaptive properties that restore vessel pulsatility, compliance, and remodeling after polymer resorption, presenting a potential alternative to DCBs, BRSs, and DESs for coronary revascularization.

Structural Features

The DynamX Sirolimus‐Eluting Coronary Bioadaptor System represents a recent advancement in coronary device technology, combining the acute performance characteristics of DES with the ability to restore natural vessel function (Table 1). Its structural design integrates a cobalt–chromium platform with novel bioresorbable polymer coatings that enable a unique adaptive mechanism following polymer degradation ≈6 months after implantation (Table 2).

Specifically, at its core, the DynamX bioadaptor features a 71‐μm thin (increased to 80 μm for diameters of 3.5 mm and 4.0 mm) cobalt–chromium alloy (L605) framework with 3 helical sinusoidal strands held together by a polylactide‐based bioresorbable polymer basecoat and topcoat (Figure 2). This system incorporates sirolimus as its active pharmaceutical ingredient and was developed on the basis of Elixir Medical’s predecessor DESs (DESyne and DESyne BD Novolimus‐Eluting Coronary Stent Systems) and BRSs (DESolve Novolimus‐Eluting Bioresorbable Coronary Scaffold System). 49 , 50 , 51 , 52

DynamX bioadaptor structural features.

The DynamX bioadaptor consists of 3 helical sinusoidal cobalt–chromium filaments (colored red, green, and blue) held together by a polylactide‐based bioresorbable polymer basecoat and topcoat. Over 6 months, the polymer coating gradually resorbs, enabling the circumferential release and controlled separation of the 3 helical filaments (as shown in the close‐up image).

The DynamX bioadaptor is coated with a proprietary polylactide‐based bioresorbable polymer. Sirolimus is delivered via the biodegradable polymer topcoat, with the majority of the drug being released over a sustained period of ≈4 weeks. The polymer itself undergoes complete degradation within 6 to 9 months, leaving only the bioadaptor framework in place. During this process, the polymer is metabolized into carbon dioxide and water, which are subsequently eliminated through the Krebs cycle. Sirolimus was chosen for the DynamX bioadaptor due to its established role as the parent compound of Novolimus, a sirolimus derivative extensively used in prior drug‐eluting devices from the same manufacturer. The nominal dosage of sirolimus applied to the bioadaptor is ≈7 μg per millimeter of device length.

Positive Adaptive Remodeling and Vessel Pulsatility

The DynamX bioadaptor unique design features a 6‐ or 8‐crown configuration incorporating multiple helical strands. Initially, these expansion segments remain locked, providing high radial strength and facilitating deliverability during implantation. Over ≈6 months, as the bioresorbable polymer coating is resorbed, the segments unlock and separate, transitioning the device into a dynamic support phase. This disengagement mechanism restores cyclic pulsatility, vessel compliance, and motion, while maintaining the structural integrity of the lumen (Figure 3). The DynamX bioadaptor’s unique mechanism of uncaging the vessel after polymer resorption allows it to regain its native adaptive remodeling capabilities, enhancing physiological function. The restoration of vasomotion not only mitigates the chronic mechanical constraints imposed by traditional stents but also promotes improved hemodynamic modulation, vascular compliance, and angulation restoration (Figure 3).

Specifically, positive adaptive remodeling is characterized by an increase in vessel diameter and compliance, maintaining lumen integrity while accommodating the natural biomechanical forces of the coronary arteries. The cyclic pulsatility restored by the bioadaptor enables the vessel to respond dynamically to the cardiac cycle, thereby improving coronary perfusion and reducing endothelial shear stress.

A mechanistic imaging study of 50 patients demonstrated that after 6 months, the unlocking process resulted in adaptive remodeling, characterized by an increase in vessel and device area, while the lumen area was maintained. Additional imaging findings confirmed improvements in arterial pulsatility, vasomotion, compliance, and restoration of coronary angulation, as well as enhanced coronary vasodilation in response to nitroglycerin. 53 , 54

Early Evidence

In a preclinical study, the bioadaptor’s ability to reduce coronary artery stress and restore natural vessel motion was evaluated using finite element analysis (eg, a computational technique to simulate and analyze physical stresses, deformations, and behaviors of complex structures under various conditions) and intravascular ultrasound (IVUS) imaging in 20 patients (eg, in vivo), comparing caged and uncaged configurations at implantation and 9‐ to 12‐month follow‐up. 55 The computational model showed that rotational uncaging of the bioadaptor reduces peak stress by 70% and, in vivo, the in‐bioadaptor segments resulted in significantly distorted vessels following implant compared with the native vessels. 55 However, at follow‐up, when the bioadaptor had uncaged, the vessel returned toward its equilibrium with a net‐effect rotational motion and no significant difference between the vessel segments. 55

The DYNAMIX (12‐Month Clinical and Imaging Outcomes of the Uncaging Coronary DynamX Bioadaptor System) prospective, single‐arm feasibility study was the first to assess the safety and performance of the novolimus‐eluting DynamX Bioadaptor System. 53 It was a multicenter study including 50 clinically stable patients with noncomplex coronary target lesions undergoing DynamX implantation. Most patients (87.0%) were on dual antiplatelet therapy at 12‐month follow‐up, and TLF (eg, the primary safety end point) occurred in 4% of patients (eg, 2 cases of cardiac death). In a paired analysis of 38 patients undergoing IVUS, from postprocedure to follow‐up, the mean bioadaptor area increased from 7.39±1.20 mm2 to 7.74±1.46 mm2 (Δ=5%, P=0.0005; primary imaging/efficacy end point), as well as the mean vessel area (Δ=3%, P=0.02), while the mean lumen area was maintained and the minimum lumen area decreased (Δ=–4%, P=0.02). In addition, an optical coherence tomography (OCT) pullback was performed in a subset of 28 patients where qualitative analysis confirmed disengagement of the uncaging elements in all imaged segments. Overall, the majority of struts (99.84±0.51%) were well apposed and fully covered by a thin, uniform neointimal layer (98.95±2.85% neointimal coverage). Interestingly, stationary coregistered OCT performed in a subset of 7 patients demonstrated pulsatility of the treated vessel segment during systole and diastole with a lumen area change of 11% and a device area change of 7.3%.

In the DYNAMIX follow‐up extension study, the device demonstrated optimal strut apposition by OCT at 24 months with mean device area increasing 20% at 24 months compared with postimplantation. 54 At 36 months, TLF grew to 8.7%, consisting of 1 clinically driven target lesion revascularization and 3 cardiac deaths (all site‐reported to be unrelated to the device or procedure). There were no additional target‐vessel revascularizations and no definite or probable scaffold thrombosis. 56 These encouraging results have allowed the device to obtain the CE mark in September 2019. Conversely, the device is not currently on sale in the United States.

However, in March 2024, the DynamX bioadaptor has received Breakthrough Device Designation from the US Food and Drug Administration for an indication to improve coronary luminal diameter, restore hemodynamic modulation, and reduce plaque progression in patients with symptomatic ischemic heart disease due to discrete de novo native coronary artery lesions. A regulatory pathway intended to facilitate the development and review of devices that may offer a more effective treatment for serious or irreversibly debilitating conditions. While no US–based clinical trials are ongoing at present, this designation may support future regulatory steps and the design of clinical studies aimed at evaluating the device in the US context.

Interestingly, a preclinical study in a porcine model analyzed smooth muscle cell gene expression in neointimal tissue, comparing the DynamX bioadaptor and Xience DES. 56 At 9 months, the DynamX bioadaptor demonstrated a 1.5‐ to 3.0‐fold increase in genes linked to smooth muscle cell contraction and a decrease in synthetic and proliferative markers. By 12 months, gene expression profiles of both devices aligned. 56 This suggests that the bioadaptor promotes faster vascular healing after uncaging compared with a caged DES, potentially contributing to the favorable outcomes observed in the DynamX mechanistic study. 56

Clinical Development

The first randomized comparison involving the DynamX coronary bioadaptor was the BIOADAPTOR‐RCT (Randomized Controlled Trial of Sirolimus‐Eluting Bioadaptor Versus Zotarolimus‐Eluting Drug‐Eluting Stent), comparing the device against the Resolute Onyx (Medtronic, Minneapolis, MN) zotarolimus‐eluting DES in 445 patients. 12 Baseline characteristics were similar between groups, with a mean age of 67 years, 22% of patients being women, 30% having diabetes, 39% with prior PCI, and up to two thirds presenting with stable angina. At 12 months, the primary end point of TLF was met with 1.8% event rate in the DynamX group, and 2.8% in the Onyx group (P<0.001 for noninferiority). No differences in any of the individual components of TLF emerged. 12 However, the observed TLF rate at 12 months was lower than expected, so the number of randomized patients was too small to ensure the study had adequate statistical power for the primary end point; furthermore, the operators were aware of the device they would be implanting, which could be a source of bias.

A subset of 100 patients enrolled in Japan was scheduled to receive additional angiographic and IVUS follow‐up at 12 months and 20 patients to receive additional OCT assessments. While no significant difference in acute gain was found at baseline between the 2 devices, superior late lumen loss at 12 months was observed in the DynamX group (0.09 mm) versus Onyx group (0.25 mm; P=0.038), consistent with greater preservation of vessel lumen area due to positive remodeling and more effective accommodation of neointimal hyperplasia (Figure 3). As such, IVUS showed that neointimal hyperplasia volume was inferior in the DynamX group (5.75 mm3 versus 9.67 mm3; P<0.001). Likewise, in the pull‐back OCT imaging cohort, neointimal hyperplasia area and neointimal hyperplasia volume were significantly lower in the DynamX group compared with the Onyx group at follow‐up. Despite the lower neointimal hyperplasia area of 0.71 mm2 versus 1.28 mm2 (P=0.009), OCT showed there was no difference in percentage strut coverage, with nearly all struts covered in both groups and no observed malapposition in either group. Stationary IVUS imaging was performed to demonstrate pulsatility of the treated vessel segment during systole and diastole. While there was no difference among the groups immediately following the procedure, at follow‐up, the DynamX sirolimus‐eluting bioadaptor group had superior in‐device lumen area and device area changes during the cardiac cycle compared with the DES group (mid in‐device lumen area change of 7.5% [95% CI, 6.1–8.9] versus 2.7% [95% CI, 1.4–4.0]; P<0.001). There was a significant difference between in‐device lumen area and the proximal and distal vessel segments in both groups after the procedure. While the difference remained significant for the DES group, the in‐device lumen area change for the bioadaptor at 12 months was similar to the proximal and distal vessel segment, representing restoration of device/vessel compliance in the DynamX group. Likewise, blood flow changes derived by lumen area changes measured by IVUS using the Hagen Poiseuille flow equation show that the percentage blood flow increase per heartbeat improved from 6.5% at baseline to 16.7% at 12 months (P<0.001) for the bioadaptor, while there was no difference between postprocedure and follow‐up for the DES group. The cyclic pulsatility observed by stationary IVUS was confirmed by stationary OCT. Moreover, significant differences in plaque volume were observed between the 2 groups. While plaque volume remained stable in the DynamX group, it increased in the DES group (plaque volume change from baseline to 12 months, 3% versus 12%; P=0.03). Excluding calcified plaques, the DynamX group even demonstrated plaque volume regression. This suggests that the restoration of the vessel’s functional integrity achieved with the bioadaptor, in synergy with lipid‐lowering therapy, could play a pivotal role in altering the progression of in‐stent atherosclerotic disease, effectively modifying its natural course. These findings demonstrated for the first time that the DynamX bioadaptor restores vessel compliance and pulsatility with significant improvements in lumen area changes and blood flow dynamics compared with DESs.

The INFINITY‐SWEDEHEART (Percutaneous Coronary Intervention With a Bioadaptor Compared to a Contemporary Drug‐Eluting Stent) trial sought to evaluate the long‐term efficacy and safety of the DynamX bioadaptor, again compared with the Resolute Onyx zotarolimus‐eluting stent in a larger cohort of patients. 57 A total of 1201 patients (1419 lesions) were randomly assigned to DynamX and 1198 patients (1431 lesions) were randomly assigned to Resolute Onyx. Key inclusion criteria included successful predilation of ≥1 target lesion(s), ≤3 target lesions requiring treatment during the index procedure, ≤2 target lesions and 1 nontarget lesion requiring treatment during the index procedure, distance between multiple lesions ensuring a gap of ≥10 mm between study devices, and target‐lesion vessel diameter and length amenable to treatment with both devices. 57 The mean age of patients was 68.2 years, most of the patients showed 1‐vessel disease (83.2%), more often in the left anterior descending (51.0%), the average lesion length was 24.6 mm, and the average reference vessel diameter 3.2 mm. Procedural and device success in the DynamX and Resolute Onyx groups were high (99.5% versus 99.8% and 99.6% versus 99.9%, respectively). 57

At 1 year, 2.35% of the patients assigned to DynamX and 2.77% of the patients assigned to Onyx experienced TLF (eg, primary end point) with an absolute risk difference that met the prespecified noninferiority margin (P <0.001 for noninferiority; Figure 3). 57 In the exploratory testing for superiority, the difference between the 2 treatment groups was not significant. At 1 year, no significant differences in the TLF individual components of cardiovascular death (0.59% versus 0.50%), target‐vessel myocardial infarction (1.27% versus 1.52%), and ischemia‐driven target‐lesion revascularization (1.27% versus 2.11%) were observed as well. 57 Consistently, target‐vessel failure (3.03% versus 3.52%) and ischemia‐driven target‐vessel revascularization (2.03% versus 2.86%) were not significantly different between groups. 57

A complementary 6‐month landmark analysis revealed a significant reduction in TLF in the DynamX group compared with the Onyx group, driven by lower rates of target‐vessel myocardial infarction and ischemia‐driven target lesion revascularization. These findings are noteworthy as they directly relate to the potential benefits of the bioadaptor’s uncaging mechanism. 57

The INFINITY‐SWEDEHEART findings should be interpreted cautiously, as the unexpectedly low 1‐year TLF incidence in the control group (2.77% versus an expected 7.5%) made the noninferiority margin of 4.2% too broad. 57 This allowed the DynamX group to potentially have twice the TLF rate while still meeting noninferiority criteria. Adjusting for the lower observed incidence would have required a sample size likely exceeding 5000 patients to robustly test noninferiority. 57 The low event rates observed, on the one hand, support the substantial advances of contemporary devices and PCI techniques, and on the other hand, may indicate that on average the patients included in the INFINITY‐SWEDEHEART trial had a low ischemic risk profile and noncomplex coronary artery disease. Critically, key anatomic and plaque characteristics, such as lesion calcification and angulation, were not detailed, necessitating further analysis in real‐world populations.

The most interesting findings from INFINITY‐SWEDEHEART probably come from the prespecified secondary analysis assessing the outcomes between groups after 6 months, when the DynamX bioadaptor helical strands unlock. 57 This analysis indicated low and plateauing adverse event rates in the DynamX group compared with the Resolute Onyx group as a plausible result of the vessel motion and function restoration. Nevertheless, the absolute risk difference between groups was 1.1%, and the available sample size cannot exclude a play of chance. For these reasons, further data at a more prolonged follow‐up are awaited to confirm whether the effects associated with the DynamX bioadaptor are maintained over time and can translate into tangible clinical benefits over conventional DESs.

Implications and Future Perspectives

The DynamX bioadaptor represents a potential new frontier in the treatment of coronary artery disease, positioned between DESs and “leave‐nothing‐behind” technologies. Its distinctive design provides dynamic radial support in the critical early months after implantation, while subsequently allowing the restoration of vasomotion and physiological vessel responses as the connectors dissolve. This dual capability addresses key limitations of existing technologies for de novo coronary lesions, including the permanent caging effect of DESs, and the suboptimal outcomes of DCBs and BRSs.

Despite these encouraging results, several important questions remain unanswered, necessitating further investigation. A key area of interest is whether the benefits observed in the first 6 months, particularly in reducing adverse events such as late thrombosis and the need for revascularization, persist over the long term. Additionally, while the study population of the INFINITY‐SWEDEHEART trial was relatively heterogeneous, complex lesions such as chronic total occlusions, severely calcified lesions, and tortuous vessels were not included. These challenging scenarios, where the risk of PCI failure is notably high, are the focus of the ongoing DYNAMITE (DynamX Bioadaptor Implantation for the Treatment of Complex Coronary Lesions; NCT05464147) study, which aims to assess the performance of the DynamX bioadaptor in complex coronary lesions. The objective of this study is to evaluate the acute and 9‐month postimplant performance by OCT as well as clinical outcomes at 9, 12, and 24 months after implantation, which could help expand the clinical indications for the bioadaptor, particularly in high‐risk patient populations where durable solutions are critically needed. While it is unlikely that the bioadaptor will completely replace current technologies, its optimal role may lie in specific clinical contexts where it could outperform DESs. Subgroup analyses from the INFINITY‐SWEDEHEART trial have highlighted potential advantages in certain scenarios, such as lesions in the left anterior descending artery, long lesions, and small vessels. These anatomic subsets are often associated with a higher risk of restenosis and impaired vessel function due to the rigidity imposed by traditional metallic stents. The ability of the bioadaptor to restore vessel pulsatility and compliance after polymer resorption could be particularly beneficial in these cases, potentially reducing late luminal loss and improving long‐term vascular adaptation. Another clinical setting in which the bioadaptor has shown favorable results in the INFINITY‐SWEDEHEART trial is acute coronary syndrome, where rapid endothelialization and restoration of vessel integrity are critical to minimize thrombotic risk. However, these findings from anatomic and clinical subsets should be interpreted with caution, as they are based on underpowered, nonrandomized data and require confirmation through dedicated randomized clinical trials.

Conclusions

The development of the DynamX bioadaptor reflects the ongoing progression of PCI technologies over the past 4 decades. Advancements from balloon angioplasty to bare metal stents and DESs have incrementally addressed key limitations while introducing new challenges. The bioadaptor builds on these innovations by combining the acute mechanical benefits of DESs with elements of vessel restoration, aiming to mitigate issues related to vessel caging and late adverse events. Positioned between traditional stents and “leave‐nothing‐behind” strategies, it represents a potential addition to the spectrum of options for PCI. Its ability to provide early mechanical support while allowing for some degree of vessel compliance restoration suggests a more physiological approach to coronary artery disease treatment. However, while initial data are encouraging, further studies and long‐term clinical follow‐up will be essential to clarify its role in routine practice.
