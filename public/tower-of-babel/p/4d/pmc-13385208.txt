Relationships of CSF biomarkers to cortical atrophy in early‐onset Alzheimer's disease
Authors: Alexandra Touroutoglou, Yuta Katsumi, Jennifer Hensel, Suzanne E Schindler, Laura Ibanez, Michael Brickhouse, Ani Eloyan, Ryan Eckbo, Alexander Zaitsev, Renaud La Joie, Maryanne Thangarajah, Alexander Taurone, Prashanthi Vemuri, Clifford R Jack, Dustin B Hammers, Tatiana Foroud, Paul Aisen, Laurel Beckett, Robert Koeppe, Walter A Kukull, Arthur Toga, Alireza Atri, David Clark, Gregory S Day, Ranjan Duara, Neill R Graff‐Radford, Ian M Grant, Lawrence S Honig, Erik C B Johnson, David T Jones, Joseph C Masdeu, Mario F Mendez, Erik Musiek, Chiadi U Onyike, Meghan Riddle, Emily Rogalski, Stephen Salloway, Sharon Sha, R Scott Turner, Thomas S Wingo

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONAlthough cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) biomarkers reflect neurodegeneration in Alzheimer's disease (AD), it remains unclear whether these biomarkers track neurodegeneration in early‐onset Alzheimer's disease (EOAD).METHODSIn 80 EOAD patients, we examined correlations between eight CSF biomarker levels and cortical thickness decreases within the EOAD cortical atrophy signature. Significant correlations were then entered into a multiple regression analysis. We also examined contributions of EOAD atrophy and CSF biomarkers to cognitive impairment.RESULTSThe levels of four CSF biomarkers correlated to EOAD signature atrophy. Stepwise regression analyses revealed that CSF neurofilament light chain (NfL) levels best predicted EOAD signature atrophy. Multiple regression showed EOAD signature atrophy combined with CSF NfL levels explained more variance in cognitive impairment than either factor alone.DISCUSSIONWithin EOAD patients, CSF NfL levels relate to the magnitude of cortical atrophy, and a combination of EOAD signature atrophy and CSF NfL levels most robustly predict cognitive impairment.Keywords: amyloid beta 42/40, chitinase‐3‐like protein, cerebrospinal fluid biomarkers, early‐onset Alzheimer's disease, EOAD signature, neurofilament light chain, phosphorylated tau, synaptosomal‐associated protein, visinin‐like protein 1

INTRODUCTION

Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) protein levels are valuable biomarkers used to characterize the pathological changes in Alzheimer's disease (AD). 1 , 2 For example, CSF levels of amyloid beta 42/40 (Aß42/40), phosphorylated tau 181 (p‐tau181), and total tau have high diagnostic sensitivity and specificity in AD 1 , 3 , 4 , 5 and have well‐established utility in the clinical diagnostic workflow. 6 Other CSF biomarkers include those that are not necessarily specific to the AD pathophysiologic cascade but that more generally index neuronal and synaptic injury and/or degeneration. These include neurofilament light chain protein (NfL, phosphoproteins that form cytoskeletal components of neuronal axons 7 , 8 , 9 ), synaptosomal‐associated protein (SNAP‐25, a protein involved in the fusion of synaptic vesicles to the pre‐synaptic membrane to facilitate neurotransmitter release 10 , 11 ), visinin‐like protein 1 (VILIP‐1, a neuronal calcium‐sensor protein also involved in synaptic plasticity 12 , 13 ), neurogranin (Ng, which post‐synaptically binds calmodulin and is thought to be involved in activity‐dependent synaptic plasticity 14 ), and chitinase‐3‐like protein 1 (YKL‐40, which is a marker of inflammatory neurotoxicity released by reactive astrocytes 15 , 16 ). Cross‐sectionally, the levels of all of these proteins are abnormal in AD compared to cognitively unimpaired controls. 3 , 5 , 7 , 8 , 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 , 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 Moreover, CSF levels of NfL correlate to the severity of cognitive impairment in AD patients. 23 , 24

Structural magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) can be used to quantify cortical atrophy and examine relationships with CSF biomarker levels. Much of the work on this topic has pooled participants across cognitively normal (CN), MCI, and AD dementia groups; and in some studies patients classified as having MCI or AD dementia did not have clear diagnostic biomarkers supporting AD pathology as the causative etiology for their cognitive deficits. 8 , 23 , 25 , 26 , 27 , 28 Furthermore, many such studies focused on typical, late‐onset AD (LOAD). Mounting evidence suggests that early‐onset Alzheimer's disease (EOAD) may have idiosyncratic clinicopathological features that distinguish it from LOAD, 29 , 30 , 31 , 32 , 33 , 34 and yet, whether CSF protein levels can track neurodegeneration in EOAD is less well understood. We recently described a cortical “EOAD signature” pattern of topographical atrophy, which encompasses the inferior parietal lobule, the posterior cingulate and ventral precuneus, and the caudal lateral temporal cortex, with relative sparing of the anterior and medial temporal lobe. 35 This signature can accurately differentiate EOAD patients from cognitively healthy controls, 35 from early‐onset non‐AD patients, 35 and from LOAD patients. 34 , 35 In addition to diagnostic utility, the EOAD signature demonstrated clinical validity against measures of symptom severity, such that greater atrophy in the EOAD signature was associated with greater cognitive impairment. 35

Here, we sought to test hypotheses regarding relationships between CSF biomarkers and EOAD signature cortical atrophy within a group of EOAD patients. Specifically, we examined relationships between CSF levels of Aß42/40, p‐tau181, total tau, NfL, SNAP‐25, Ng, VILIP‐1, and YKL‐40 and cortical thickness in a group of 80 EOAD patients from the Longitudinal Early‐Onset Alzheimer's Disease Study (LEADS). 36 We hypothesized that all eight CSF biomarkers would correlate with cortical atrophy within the EOAD signature but would do so to varying degrees. We further hypothesized that – within this sample of EOAD patients – the biomarkers shown to track neurodegenerative burden (total tau, NfL, SNAP‐25, Ng, and VILIP‐1) would best correlate with EOAD signature atrophy. We did not expect biomarkers used for diagnostic purposes (Aß42/40 and p‐tau181) to correlate with EOAD signature atrophy as all patients in our sample had elevations in these markers by definition and there was insufficient extant evidence that the degree of elevations in these markers correlated to neurodegeneration. We also predicted that CSF NfL levels would show the strongest correlation with EOAD signature atrophy, as the levels of this biomarker correlate most robustly with cognitive and functional impairment in EOAD. 24 Finally, we examined the hypothesis that a combination of EOAD signature atrophy and CSF biomarkers (such as NfL) would explain more variance in clinical outcome measures (e.g., the Mini‐Mental State Examination [MMSE] 37 and the Alzheimer's Disease Assessment‐Cognitive Subscale [ADAS‐Cog] 38 ) scores beyond that which is explained by EOAD signature atrophy alone.

METHODS

Participants

Data were obtained from the LEADS study 24 , 35 , 36 (www.leads‐study.org). There were 96 EOAD and 37 CN participants included in the CSF sample, 24 but only 80 EOAD and 30 CN participants were available for this analysis since a subset of the subjects did not have CSF SNAP‐25 and Ng biomarkers. The clinical and demographic characteristics of the subset of EOAD participants not included in the analyses are presented in Table S1. All participants received a standard clinical evaluation comprising a structured history obtained from both patient and informant and a patient examination to inform scoring of the global Clinical Dementia Rating score, 39 as well as a neuropsychological assessment. Clinical diagnostic formulation was performed through consensus conference by each site's team. All participants were between 40 and 64 years old at study enrollment, fluent in English, and in good general health, did not harbor other neurological or psychiatric disorders, and had a knowledgeable informant. Because LEADS focuses on sporadic early‐onset dementia, cognitively impaired individuals with genetic mutations in Amyloid Precursor Protein (APP), presenilin‐1 (PSEN1) or presenilin‐2 (PSEN2), microtubule‐associated protein tau (MAPT), chromosome 9 open reading frame 72 (C9ORF72), or progranulin (GRN) were excluded.

LEADS participants additionally underwent structural MRI neuroimaging as described in Touroutoglou et al. 35 CSF was collected and analyzed for Aß42/40, p‐tau181, total tau, NfL, SNAP‐25, Ng, YKL‐40, and VILIP‐1, as previously described. 24 Concentrations of Aβ40, Aβ42, total tau, and p‐tau181 were measured using the Lumipulse G1200 Chemiluminescent Enzyme Immunoassay platform; VILIP‐1, SNAP‐25, and Ng were measured by quantitative fluorescent two‐site immunoassays with Single Molecule Counting (SMC) technology; and NfL and YKL‐40 were measured with plate‐based commercial Enzyme‐Linked Immunosorbent Assays (ELISAs) manufactured by Uman Diagnostics and Quidel, respectively, and as previously described. 24 We tested the normality of data distribution for each CSF biomarker via a Shapiro‐Wilk test and log‐transformed any measure exhibiting evidence for a non‐normal distribution (p < 0.05).

Standard protocol approvals and registrations

Institutional Review Board (IRB) approval was obtained through a central IRB overseen by Indiana University.

Structural MRI data acquisition and analysis

MRI data were acquired with a 3.0 T scanner (14 sites used Siemens scanners, three used GE scanners, and one used a Phillips scanner) using ADNI‐3‐harmonized protocols after certification of each site employing phantom and human scans. Sequences acquired included a high‐resolution 3D T1‐weighted sequence (e.g., Siemens MPRAGE parameters are TR = 2300 ms, TE = 2.98 ms, flip angle = 9°, slice thickness = 1 mm, field of view = 240 × 256 mm, 208 sagittal slices, and 2x acceleration). A 3D FLAIR sequence was also included in part for the purpose of ruling out non‐degenerative neurological diseases, which may have contributed to patients’ clinical syndromes.

Each subject's 3D T1‐weighted MRI scan was analyzed using Freesurfer version 6.0 to estimate cortical thickness (http://surfer.nmr.mgh.harvard.edu). All MRI data were visually inspected for gross artifacts (e.g., subject motion) and evaluated on image quality prior to data processing, as previously described in Touroutoglou et al. 35 Once it passed quality control, each participant's 3D T1 data underwent intensity normalization, skull stripping, and automated segmentation of cerebral white matter to locate the gray matter/white matter boundary via FreeSurfer version 6.0, which is documented and freely available for download (http://surfer.nmr.mgh.harvard.edu). Defects in the surface topology were corrected, and the gray/white boundary was deformed outward using an algorithm designed to obtain an explicit representation of the pial surface. We visually inspected each participant's cortical surface reconstruction for technical accuracy. Whole‐brain maps of cortical thickness were registered to template surface space (fsaverage) and smoothed geodesically with full‐width at half‐maximum (FWHM) of 15 mm (see Touroutoglou et al. 35 ).

Next, we measured the magnitude of AD‐related cortical atrophy in EOAD within cortical regions identified in the EOAD signature of atrophy 35 (Figure S1). Using the spherical registration of each participant to fsaverage space, the 10 cortical regions of interest (ROIs) were mapped to individual participants’ native surface space. For each participant, mean cortical thickness within each ROI was calculated by deriving an average of all the thickness estimates from vertices that fell within the labeled ROI. We created a single composite EOAD‐signature ROI label by taking the composite of all individual ROIs as in Touroutoglou et al. 35 The EOAD‐signature ROI label was defined in a well‐characterized discovery sample from Massachusetts General Hospital (MGH) and replicated in a validation sample of participants with EOAD from the LEADS sample, including subjects of this study. Cortical thickness was then calculated by averaging thickness estimates of all vertices falling within this composite ROI. To account for global individual differences in cortical thickness, we converted each participant's cortical thickness estimates to W‐scores, which are analogous to Z‐scores adjusted for specific covariates of no interest, which in this study were age and sex (for more details see Touroutoglou et al. 35 ). Briefly, we first performed a multiple regression analysis using mean cortical thickness data obtained from CN participants, which resulted in beta coefficient values for age and sex as well as individual values of residuals. Because W‐scores in this study were calculated using cortical thickness, more negative values indicate greater cortical atrophy relative to what would be expected solely based on age and sex.

We tested our hypotheses by examining the relationships between the single EOAD‐signature measure derived from the ROI method described earlier. In post hoc analyses, we further constructed vertex‐wise general linear models in Freesurfer to examine the bivariate association between cortical atrophy and the CSF biomarkers. Statistical significance was assessed with a vertex‐wise threshold of p < 0.01 uncorrected for multiple comparisons.

Statistical analyses

We computed the Pearson's correlation coefficient between the magnitude of cortical atrophy (W scores) within the EOAD signature and each CSF biomarker. Upon identification of CSF biomarkers showing significant (p < 0.05) and trending (p < 0.10) associations with EOAD signature atrophy (SNAP‐25, Ng, NfL, and Aβ42/40), we constructed a multivariable regression model including these four variables as well as demographic and clinical covariates (age, sex, education, baseline CDR global scores) as predictors and EOAD signature atrophy as the outcome variable. We then performed stepwise regression analyses where we removed one variable at a time from this full model based on the highest p value. The best (most parsimonious) CSF model for predicting cortical atrophy in the EOAD signature regions was defined as the one with the fewest predictors and a change in the Akaike information criterion (ΔAIC) of less than 2 relative to the lowest AIC, which was considered most optimal with the best tradeoff between model fit and complexity. 40 , 41 As a sensitivity analysis, we also performed least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression with 10‐fold cross‐validation to assess the robustness of variable selection using the glmnet package (version 4.1‐10) run in R version 4.4.2. The optimal regularization parameter, λ, was determined using the minimum cross‐validation error criterion.

Next, we examined the independent and interactive contributions of cortical EOAD atrophy and CSF biomarkers on predicting cognitive impairment as measured by MMSE and ADAS‐Cog scores. To do so, we first constructed a series of simple regression models for each of the eight CSF biomarkers to predict MMSE and ADAS‐Cog scores. Similarly, we constructed simple regression models to use atrophy in the EOAD signature to predict MMSE and ADAS‐Cog scores. Then we entered CSF biomarkers with significant results from these bivariate associations into a multiple linear regression model to assess complementary contributions of CSF biomarkers and cortical thickness in the EOAD signature to predicting the severity of cognitive impairment. In this multiple regression analysis, we used EOAD signature cortical thickness and CSF levels as independent variables and MMSE scores as the dependent variable, while controlling for age and sex as covariates of no interest. We then ran the same analysis using ADAS‐Cog scores as the dependent variable.

Relationship between CSF biomarker levels and cortical thickness within EOAD signature

Figure 1 displays scatterplots of bivariate correlations between the CSF levels of SNAP‐25, Ng, NfL, and Aß 42/40 and W scores in the EOAD signature. Within this sample of EOAD patients, CSF levels of SNAP‐25 (r = 0.36, p < 0.001) correlated directly with cortical thickness in the EOAD signature (Figure 1, upper panel), whereas CSF levels of NfL (r = −0.29, p < 0.01) and Aß42/40 (r = −0.39, p < 0.001) correlated inversely with cortical thickness in the EOAD signature (Figure 1, lower panel). CSF levels of Ng showed a weak relationship (r = 0.20, p ≤ 0.074) with cortical thickness (Figure 1, upper panel). In addition, CSF levels of total tau (r = 0.06, p ≤ 0.60), p‐tau181 (r = 0.10, p ≤ 0.39), VILIP‐1 (r = 0.15, p ≤ 0.17), and YKL‐40 (r = 0.07, p ≤ 0.52) did not significantly correlate with atrophy in the EOAD signature (see Figure S2 for scatterplots showing correlations between cortical thickness and CSF biomarker levels of total tau, p‐tau181, VILIP‐1, and YKL‐40).

We then constructed a multivariable regression model including all CSF variables with significant and trending correlations (S1AP‐25, Ng, NfL, and Aβ42/40) as well as covariates of no interest. Next, we sequentially removed one variable at a time from the full model in a step‐wise fashion and refit the model. We found that the most parsimonious model – as defined by the one with the fewest predictors and a ΔAIC < 2 relative to the lowest AIC – included NfL, SNAP, and Aβ42/40 ratio as predictors, where NfL had the largest effect (Table 2). These findings support the robustness of the three CSF variables based on stepwise regression.

On a vertex‐wise basis, CSF NfL levels inversely correlated with cortical thickness in the precuneus and lateral temporoparietal and prefrontal regions of the EOAD signature (i.e., higher NfL values related to lower W‐score values, indicating greater atrophy). Relationship of CSF biomarker levels and cortical thickness within the EOAD signature with cognitive impairment

Relationships between cortical thickness in the EOAD signature, as well as levels of CSF biomarkers, and cognitive measures (MMSE and ADAS‐Cog scores) are shown in Table 3. We performed multiple regression analyses to determine whether imaging EOAD signature and CSF biomarkers contribute redundantly or synergistically to the prediction of symptom severity. Specifically, multiple regression analysis was applied to compare the relative contributions of cortical EOAD atrophy and the CSF levels of NfL and SNAP‐25 (both of which produced significant results in the bivariate associations) with MMSE and ADAS‐Cog scores. These results showed that cortical thickness in the EOAD signature and CSF levels of NfL and SNAP‐25 significantly added to the prediction of MMSE scores, while controlling for age and sex as covariates of no interest, F(5, 74) = 11.17, p < 0.001 (Table 4). The prediction model accounted for approximately 43% of the variance of MMSE scores (R 2 = 0.43) with cortical thickness in the EOAD signature having the strongest effect on the model's overall fit. This analysis further showed that cortical thickness in the EOAD signature and CSF NfL levels were the best predictors of the ADAS‐Cog score, while controlling for age and sex as covariates of no interest, F(5, 73) = 6.61, p < 0.001 (Table 4). The prediction model accounted for approximately 31% of the variance of the ADAS‐Cog scores (R 2 = 0.31). As with the MMSE scores, cortical thickness in the EOAD signature had the strongest effect on the model's overall fit for the ADAS‐Cog scores. The CSF levels of SNAP‐25, while negatively correlated with ADAS‐Cog scores, did not have a statistically significant contribution in this model to the prediction of the ADAS‐Cog scores (p > 0.05). In sum, those individuals that presented with the greatest abnormalities in cortical thickness in the EOAD signature and the highest levels of CSF NfL had the greatest cognitive impairment (lowest MMSE and the highest ADAS‐Cog scores; Figure 4).

DISCUSSION

We examined the relationship between atrophy within our recently described EOAD cortical signature and the levels of eight CSF biomarkers in 80 individuals with EOAD at the MCI or mild dementia stage. The CSF biomarkers NfL, Ng, SNAP‐25, and Aß42/40 demonstrated correlations with cortical thickness within the EOAD signature, while VILIP‐1, p‐tau181, and total tau did not. These findings did not fully concur with our hypotheses, as two CSF biomarkers that non‐specifically index neurodegeneration (VILIP‐1 and total tau) did not correlate with cortical thickness within the EOAD signature, while Aß42/40 – a marker of Aβ not typically associated with neurodegenerative burden – did. Consistent with our original hypothesis, a subsequent multiple regression analysis revealed that NfL accounted for the largest proportion of the variance in the relationship between EOAD signature atrophy and CSF biomarker levels. Moreover, multiple regression analyses also showed that cortical thickness in the EOAD signature predicted MMSE and ADAS‐Cog scores in EOAD patients, with the levels of CSF NfL explaining additional variance in cognition. Taken together, our results highlight the utility of CSF biomarkers as correlates of MRI‐derived measures of neurodegeneration in EOAD and clinical impairment, with particular emphasis on the importance of NfL.

A few other studies examined correlations between CSF biomarkers and cortical atrophy specifically within a group of EOAD patients. With respect to Aβ CSF levels, Falgas and colleagues found no correlation between CSF levels of Aß42 and atrophy within the AD cortical signature, which they defined in 64 EOAD patients, 42 and Ossenkopple et al. found a positive correlation between Aß42 CSF levels and cortical thickness measures in regions overlapping with our EOAD signature – the precuneus and posterior cingulate – that is, lower Aß42 CSF levels correlated with reduced thickness (or greater atrophy). 43 The discrepancies between these findings and our own may be due to the fact that these studies examined Aß42 CSF levels without adjusting for the levels of Aß40 CSF, as we did using a ratio of Aß 42/40. In a finding more broadly consistent with ours, Contador et al. showed that higher baseline CSF Aß42 levels predicted greater atrophy in the right precuneus 2 years later in EOAD patients. 44

Other studies also , probed the relationships between CSF tau levels and cortical atrophy in EOAD patients. In keeping with our results, Falgas et al. found no correlations between CSF total tau or p‐tau181 levels and cortical thickness measures in their EOAD cohort. Sluimer et al. found a modest association between increased p‐tau181 CSF levels and lower whole‐brain atrophy in AD patients. 28 However, several notable qualifications apply to these findings as the study did not involve EOAD subjects, the atrophy measure used was at the whole‐brain level, and the AD patient cohort did not have diagnostic biomarkers establishing plaque and tangle pathology as the probable causative process. In our study, CSF Ng levels exhibited zero‐order correlations with EOAD signature atrophy. This is again in contrast with the findings of Falgas et al., which revealed no such association. 42 A study by Tarawneh et al. found that CSF Ng levels negatively correlated with hippocampal, parahippocampal, entorhinal, and whole‐brain volumes in a group of 95 AD subjects, but again, these were not EOAD subjects, and their AD cohort pooled patients at the MCI and dementia clinical severity levels. 14 Portelius and colleagues found no association between CSF Ng levels and the rate of hippocampal atrophy in LOAD patients but did discover a correlation between these two variables in MCI patients who progressed to AD. 45

SNAP‐25, like synaptogamin and growth‐associated protein 43 (GAP‐43), is a measure of synaptic integrity and function. 46 SNAP‐25 levels can be elevated early in the disease course of AD, 25 , 46 , 47 in CN APOE 𝜺4 carriers, 48 and in amyloid‐positive, tau‐negative individuals. 49 SNAP‐25 levels are also associated with greater amyloid deposition in vulnerable cortical regions in AD. 49 With respect to associations between SNAP‐25 and cortical atrophy, one study used data from the Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI) to show that CSF SNAP‐25/Aß42 ratios correlated with the rate of hippocampal atrophy in non‐EOAD patients with progressive MCI due to AD pathology. Interestingly, unlike CSF levels of NfL and Aß42/40, CSF levels of SNAP‐25 correlated directly with cortical thickness within the EOAD signature in our sample of EOAD patients. In other words, across EOAD subjects, higher CSF levels of SNAP‐25 were related to thicker cortex (less atrophy) within the EOAD signature. This may be explained by the fact that while SNAP‐25 levels increase early in the pathophysiology of AD (possibly as a reflection of early synaptic loss), longitudinal studies in patients with AD dementia show that its levels decrease over time. 50 This hypothesis is partially supported by the post hoc analysis shown in Figure 3. EOAD patients with the least atrophy had the highest SNAP‐25 levels, while those with more prominent atrophy (i.e., with more advanced neurodegeneration) had lower levels. Thus, it is possible that earlier‐stage EOAD patients demonstrate higher SNAP‐25 CSF levels, which subsequently decline with disease progression. This hypothesis would admittedly be difficult to investigate, except possibly in patients harboring autosomal dominant mutations giving rise to EOAD.

Our results show that CSF levels of NfL were most robustly correlated with decreased cortical thickness (i.e., atrophy) within the EOAD signature. We predicted this result based largely on findings from Dage et al. 24 demonstrating that NfL levels showed the strongest correlations with most clinical measures in EOAD (i.e., MOCA, CDR sum of boxes, ADAS‐Cog). 24 Because NfL is a degenerative marker of large‐caliber axons, 27 CSF NfL levels are not specific indices of Aβ or paired helical filament tau pathology and are therefore not diagnostically specific for EOAD or for LOAD. 51 In fact, these levels are increased in normal aging, in multiple sclerosis, in frontotemporal dementia, in the presence of microvascular ischemic changes, and even in traumatic brain injury. 9 , 27 , 42 , 52 Still, CSF levels of NfL may hold a privileged role in neurodegenerative disorders, including in EOAD, representing the intensity of the neurodegenerative process. For example, Kern et al. found that CSF levels of NfL – and not those of total tau, p‐tau181, or Ng – were predictive of the longitudinal development of MCI in a cohort of cognitively unimpaired older individuals. 53 Also, Lleo et al. showed in a longitudinal study that CSF NfL levels, and not p‐tau181 or total tau levels, increased over time in AD patients. 54 Surprisingly, however, Falgas et al. did not find a relationship between NfL and cortical thickness in a sample of 64 EOAD patients, nor did Zetterberg et al., 27 but the MRI analysis in that study included only whole‐brain, hippocampal, and ventricular volumes. Since plasma NfL levels correspond to those observed in the CSF in AD patients, 55 it is worth mentioning that plasma NfL levels have themselves been found to correlate with gray matter volume loss in the posterior cingulate in AD and MCI patients who were amyloid positive, 56 and relatively rapid increases in plasma NfL levels have been shown to correlate with more aggressive atrophy rates in LOAD. 57 It is possible that plasma and CSF levels of NfL may contribute synergistically to the prediction of neurodegeneration and cognitive decline in EOAD.

Finally, the observation that the magnitude of EOAD signature atrophy and levels of CSF NfL contributed synergistically to the prediction of cognitive impairment in standardized measures used in clinical trials (MMSE and ADAS‐Cog) supports the clinical importance of multimodal diagnostic models combining imaging and CSF biomarkers in this patient population. This is broadly consistent with findings from other studies in LOAD. 58 , 59 For instance, Vemuri et al. demonstrated that both structural MRI metrics and baseline CSF biomarker (p‐tau181 and Aß1‐42) levels predicted the conversion from amnestic MCI to AD. In keeping with our findings, this study also showed that the combination of structural MRI metrics and CSF biomarker levels served as a better predictor for declines in MMSE scores than either factor alone, and that structural MRI was the slightly better prognosticator. 60 Regarding comparable findings in EOAD, Xie et al. found that structural MRI and plasma (not CSF) levels of NfL and p‐tau181 complementarily contributed to predictions of cognitive decline and longitudinal atrophy in EOAD patients. 61 Taken together, these findings suggest that clinical trials investigating novel disease‐modifying agents designed to slow neurodegeneration should continue to use multimodal biomarkers as study endpoints.

One limitation of this study is that it is cross‐sectional. Future investigations should examine longitudinal relationships to try to clarify the specific temporal ordering of distinct neurodegenerative biomarkers. For instance, it may be that longitudinal analyses would demonstrate that CSF levels of SNAP‐25 initially correlate inversely with thickness within the EOAD signature, while later correlating directly with cortical thickness. In this study, a subset of individuals with EOAD (n = 15) did not have all CSF biomarkers available and, thus, were not included in our analyses. Although the demographics and clinical characteristics did not differ among all participants in the study, results may have differed if biomarkers were available for everyone. Furthermore, this study did not examine the non‐linearity of reported associations, which should be explored in future studies with larger subject samples.

CONCLUSIONS

We observed that in EOAD, CSF levels of four biomarkers – NfL, neurogranin, SNAP‐25, Aß42/40 – correlated with cortical atrophy within our EOAD signature, with NfL accounting for the greatest amount of variance among the four. We further observed that CSF NfL levels and EOAD signature atrophy synergistically predicted cognitive and functional metrics in EOAD patients. These findings extend prior work showing that CSF biomarkers relate to structural neurodegeneration in AD by revealing the putative utility of such biomarkers in predicting neurodegeneration within a topographically relevant set of brain regions in EOAD. Therefore, CSF biomarkers, such as NfL, SNAP‐25, and others, may play complementary roles to MRI‐derived measures of neurodegeneration in monitoring as outcomes in clinical therapeutic trials with EOAD patients.
