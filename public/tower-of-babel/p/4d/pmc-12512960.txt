A rare case of Blepharophimosis-Ptosis-Epicanthus inversus syndrome (BPES) associated with keratoconus: a multidisciplinary approach to diagnosis and management
Authors: Abdulmajeed Al Khathami, Ali Hendi Al Ghamdi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBlepharophimosis-Ptosis-Epicanthus Inversus Syndrome (BPES) is a rare congenital condition typically associated with mutations in the FOXL2 gene. It is characterized by eyelid malformations and, in some cases, premature ovarian insufficiency. Clinical evaluation revealed features consistent with BPES, and corneal topography confirmed bilateral keratoconus. Introduction

Blepharophimosis-ptosis-epicanthus inversus syndrome (BPES) is a rare congenital condition characterized by four core features: blepharophimosis, ptosis, epicanthus inversus, and telecanthus [1]. Traditionally, BPES is attributed to mutations in the FOXL2 gene, a transcription factor involved in eyelid and ovarian development, and is inherited in an autosomal dominant pattern [2]. The syndrome is classified into two subtypes: Type I, which includes premature ovarian insufficiency (POI), and Type II, which affects only ocular structures [3].

Ocular complications in BPES commonly include astigmatism, amblyopia, and exposure keratopathy, often stemming from mechanical factors related to eyelid malformation [4]. However, an association between BPES and keratoconus, a progressive corneal ectasia characterized by thinning, steepening, and irregular astigmatism, has not been previously reported in the literature [5].

Keratoconus is a multifactorial disorder influenced by both genetic and environmental factors [6]. In this context, abnormal eyelid anatomy in BPES may contribute to biomechanical stress on the cornea, potentially acting as a predisposing factor for ectatic changes. This case adds to the evolving understanding of BPES by documenting a patient with classic phenotypic features and a pathogenic COLEC10 variant, rather than the more commonly implicated FOXL2 mutation.

Here, we report the first known case of BPES associated with keratoconus in a genetically confirmed Patient with a pathogenic COLEC10 variant. This case highlights the importance of corneal screening in syndromic eyelid disorders and calls for further research into the genetic and mechanical interactions contributing to corneal ectasia.

Case presentation

Patient background

A 41-year-old Saudi woman with a medical history of hypertension and asthma presented to the ophthalmology clinic with gradually worsening vision and lifelong eyelid abnormalities. She reported no history of ocular trauma or allergic conditions and had no additional systemic illnesses. She is a mother of two children: her son is affected with eyelid anomalies consistent with BPES, while her daughter is unaffected and developmentally normal.

Genetic and multidisciplinary assessment

Given the phenotypic features of blepharophimosis and ptosis, a genetic evaluation was initiated to investigate the underlying cause. Whole-exome sequencing identified a homozygous missense variant in COLEC10 (NM_006438.4:c.311G > T, p.(Gly104Val)), as reported by the CentoXome MOx 1.0 assay. According to the laboratory report, this variant has been previously observed in homozygosity in multiple patients with 3MC syndrome and is classified as pathogenic following ACMG/AMP/ClinGen guidelines. No mutations were detected in the FOXL2 gene. According to ACMG guidelines, the identified COLEC10 variant is classified as “likely pathogenic.” Genetic counseling was provided, and the autosomal recessive inheritance pattern of the COLEC10 mutation was explained to the patient, along with its potential implications for her children.

A multidisciplinary management plan was implemented and included the following:

The patient was clinically evaluated for features commonly associated with COLEC10-related 3MC syndrome, including craniofacial anomalies, cleft lip or palate, hearing loss, genitourinary abnormalities, and developmental delay. No dysmorphic facial features, ear anomalies, or oral cavity defects were observed. Auditory screening yielded normal results, and there was no evidence of developmental delay or systemic involvement. These findings indicate a predominantly ocular phenotype in this case, thereby contributing to the expanding phenotypic spectrum of COLEC10-related 3MC syndrome.

To further evaluate the inheritance pattern, a three-generation pedigree was constructed by a pediatric geneticist (Fig. 3). The patient was born to consanguineous parents. She has three brothers and one sister, all of whom are unaffected. This pedigree supports a likely autosomal recessive inheritance, consistent with the homozygous COLEC10 variant identified through genetic testing.

Although ptosis surgery is often indicated in BPES cases, our patient was evaluated by an oculoplastic consultant who recommended conservative management due to the absence of significant functional visual impairment at this stage. Ongoing follow-up was advised for future reassessment.

Discussion

This case report presents the first documented association between Blepharophimosis-Ptosis-Epicanthus Inversus Syndrome (BPES) and keratoconus, contributing a novel observation to the evolving phenotypic spectrum of BPES. Traditionally, BPES is described as an autosomal dominant syndrome caused by mutations in the FOXL2 gene, with hallmark ocular findings including blepharophimosis, ptosis, epicanthus inversus, and telecanthus [1]. While FOXL2 is well-established in eyelid and ovarian development [1], the current case deviates from this classic genotype–phenotype correlation, as FOXL2 testing was negative. Instead, genetic analysis revealed a homozygous pathogenic variant in the COLEC10 gene, implicating an alternative genetic etiology.

The COLEC10 gene, although not traditionally linked to BPES, has been associated with 3MC syndrome type 3, a recessively inherited disorder characterized by craniofacial and ocular abnormalities [7]. Its role in collagen-binding and lectin pathways suggests a potential influence on tissue morphogenesis, including ocular structures. The identification of a COLEC10 variant in this patient raises the possibility of a phenotypic overlap or misclassified syndrome. Given the consanguineous background and the presence of similarly affected siblings, the inheritance pattern appears autosomal recessive, supporting the pathogenicity of the COLEC10 mutation in this context.

Keratoconus, a progressive disorder marked by corneal thinning and irregular astigmatism, is commonly associated with genetic susceptibility, chronic mechanical stress, and environmental triggers such as atopy and eye rubbing [7]. In this patient, congenital eyelid malformations may have contributed to corneal microtrauma, exposure-related instability, or friction. The presence of Vogt striae, scissoring reflex on retinoscopy, and topographic findings confirmed bilateral keratoconus, classified as Amsler-Krumeich Grade II [1].

Although FOXL2 mutations are not directly implicated in keratoconus, previous research has highlighted their role in cellular regulation and connective tissue homeostasis [8, 9]. However, in this case, the absence of a FOXL2 mutation and the presence of a COLEC10 variant shift the discussion toward a possible new genotype-phenotype relationship. Further studies are warranted to investigate whether COLEC10-related pathways may contribute to corneal biomechanical instability or play a modifying role in syndromic ocular disorders.

While COLEC10 mutations have been classically associated with syndromic presentations under the 3MC umbrella, our case highlights an atypical, isolated ocular phenotype. This supports the growing evidence of phenotypic variability and reinforces the importance of detailed clinical assessment and broad genetic testing in patients with congenital ocular anomalies.

This case also broadens the clinical understanding of BPES-like phenotypes and reinforces the importance of comprehensive genetic testing in atypical presentations. The negative FOXL2 result underscores the need to move beyond targeted panels and adopt broader genomic approaches when classical features are present but standard mutations are absent. Previous studies have reported a high incidence of refractive errors and amblyopia in BPES patients [10], but keratoconus has not been previously documented. This makes corneal imaging an essential tool in the early assessment of patients with eyelid malformations, especially those reporting visual decline. Routine topographic screening in such cases may allow for early detection of corneal ectasia and timely intervention with therapies such as corneal cross-linking.

The management of this patient required a multidisciplinary approach, involving ophthalmology, oculoplastic surgery, genetics, and endocrinology. The identification of a homozygous pathogenic variant in the COLEC10 gene confirmed a diagnosis of 3MC syndrome, which has autosomal recessive inheritance. Genetic counseling was provided to the patient and her family, explaining that both parents are likely heterozygous carriers and that there is a 25% recurrence risk in future pregnancies. This information directly impacted reproductive planning, as it allowed the family to consider specific options such as carrier testing for potential future partners of the patient, prenatal diagnostic testing in future pregnancies, or preimplantation genetic testing (PGT-M) if assisted reproduction is pursued. This case highlights how a molecular diagnosis enabled personalized reproductive risk assessment and proactive family planning strategies. In terms of clinical management, surgical correction of ptosis is anticipated to reduce mechanical stress on the cornea and improve visual function. Rigid gas-permeable lenses and corneal cross-linking were discussed for keratoconus management. Evaluation by gynecology and endocrinology teams ruled out premature ovarian insufficiency, although continued monitoring is recommended.

Conclusion

This case highlights a novel association between BPES features and keratoconus in a patient with a homozygous pathogenic COLEC10 mutation and no FOXL2 involvement. The findings suggest a broader genetic spectrum for BPES-like phenotypes and raise the possibility of a link between COLEC10 and corneal ectasia. Comprehensive genetic testing and routine corneal imaging are essential in similar presentations. Multidisciplinary care, including ophthalmology, genetics, and endocrinology, supports optimal outcomes. Further research is needed to clarify the role of COLEC10 in ocular development and its potential contribution to keratoconus pathophysiology.
