Progress and challenges in pet management in disasters: recommended strategies for developing countries - a scoping review
Authors: Shandiz Moslehi, Asghar Tavan, Sajjad Narimani, Samira Soleimanpour
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe increasing trend of pet ownership presents a new set of challenges for disaster management. Many disaster programs currently focus on human populations but overlook the role of pets during emergencies. This research investigates the current progress and challenges surrounding pet management during disasters, aiming to inform forward-looking strategies in disaster management.MethodA scoping review was conducted to gather insights on advances and challenges of pet management in disasters. The researchers searched several major databases (Web of Science, Scopus, and PubMed) and identified articles that specifically focused on pets in disaster contexts. After screening articles for relevance, 57 articles met the inclusion criteria for the review.ResultFrom an initial 2,532 articles, the authors narrowed down their findings by removing duplicates, resulting in 694 unique studies. Further screening led to 111 articles for in-depth review, and finally, 57 articles were selected for inclusion. The findings were categorized into two main themes: “Challenges of pet management in disaster” including four sub-themes and “Recommendations for pet management in disasters” with four sub-themes.ConclusionsThe study’s conclusions center on psychological theories, specifically the Trans-theoretical Model and Protection Motivation Theory. These frameworks highlight the importance of understanding pet owners’ behaviors, particularly their stages of change (how they prepare for and respond to disasters) and perceived sensitivity toward their pets. Addressing these psychological factors could significantly improve the safety and evacuation of pets during emergencies.

Introduction

Disaster management is rapidly expanding to address all types of hazards [1]. Comprehensive Disaster Management (CDM) involves managing all hazards across the entire disaster management cycle, which includes mitigation/prevention, preparedness, response, and recovery phases [2].

Mitigation and preparedness steps occur in the pre-disaster phase, response during the disaster, and recovery immediately after the disaster. Mitigation activities aim to eliminate or reduce the probability of disaster occurrence or lessen the effects of unavoidable disasters. The preparedness phase seeks to respond satisfactorily to any emergency through measures that strengthen the technical and managerial capacity of governments, organizations, and communities. Emergency response provides immediate assistance to maintain life, improve health, and support the morale of the affected population. Recovery involves reconstruction and rehabilitation efforts that continue until all systems return to normal or better conditions [3]. The ultimate goal of these stages is to protect human life and property by minimizing disaster impacts [4].

Recently, pet ownership has become a growing trend worldwide [5]. Some studies have reported increased rates of animal abandonment during the COVID-19 pandemic, highlighting concerns about animal needs during disasters. Paradoxically, social restrictions during the pandemic also led to increased interactions between people and their pets [6]. Research on natural disasters such as tsunamis and hurricanes has shown that neglecting pet-related issues results in loss of public trust and reduced cooperation with authorities. Conversely, comprehensive pet management enhances the physical and psychological well-being of pet owners [7].

Most studies on pet management in disasters have been conducted in high-income countries, while many developing countries remain at the early stages of implementing such programs [8–11]. There is a critical need for research in developing countries to establish pet management programs to mitigate the stress caused by disasters for pet owners and relief workers [12]. Research on livestock keeping during crises in developing countries reveals that non-governmental organizations (NGOs) often neglect livestock keepers. The use of livestock emergency guidelines and standards (LEGS) has significantly improved conditions for affected populations and could be adapted to benefit pet owners during disasters [13].

Summarizing and reviewing findings from individual studies on pet management in disasters is essential to increase practitioners’ understanding of the challenges and progress in this field. This scoping review aims to identify past and future actions needed for effective pet management during disasters.

Scoping reviews serve to identify key factors related to a concept, examine how research is conducted or reported, and clarify concepts or definitions within a field. The findings can then be integrated with theoretical frameworks to highlight new research directions. This study interprets its results through the lens of the Transtheoretical Model (TTM) and Protection Motivation Theory (PMT) [14, 15].

The TTM examines changes in individual behaviors using four constructs: stages of change (SOC), processes of change, self-efficacy, and decisional balance. It describes how pet owners move from the pre-contemplation stage (no intention to protect pets) to other stages, including contemplation (considering pet protection within six months), preparation (ready to protect pets within a month), action (actively protecting pets), and maintenance (sustained protection for over six months) [16]. The processes of change are strategies that individuals use to progress through SOC. Processes are divided into:1) Experiential (cognitive) processes, which focus on thoughts, feelings, and cognitive experiences, typically occur in the early SOC, including increasing awareness, dramatic relief, self-re-evaluation, environmental re-evaluation, and social liberation. 2) Behavioral processes focus on behaviors and reinforcements that are particularly relevant during the action and maintenance stages, which include helping relationships, self-liberation, counterconditioning, stimulus control, and reinforcement management. Decisional balance refers to weighing the pros and cons of protecting pets, and self-efficacy relates to confidence in performing protective behaviors; both influence progression through the stages [16, 17].

The PMT explains and predicts how pet owners adopt preventive actions based on risk perception, focusing on threat appraisal and coping appraisal. Threat appraisal assesses the seriousness and intensity of the event, and coping appraisal assesses how an individual responds to a situation. The perceived susceptibility to a risk and its perceived severity constitutes the threat appraisal. Coping appraisal evaluates perceived response efficacy (belief that recommended actions will reduce risk) and self-efficacy (belief in one’s ability to execute those actions) [18].

This paper presents a scoping review of pet management practices in disasters worldwide. Preliminary research indicated the breadth of the topic and the presence of numerous non-academic documents, which justified conducting a scoping review. The review addresses three main objectives:1) to identify challenges faced by pet owners in disasters; 2) to determine the facilities and resources pet owners need for disaster preparedness; and 3) to identify coping strategies employed to manage and protect pets during disasters. The findings aim to assist developing countries in integrating pet management into their CDM. Ideally, pet management should be incorporated into all phases of the disaster cycle, including mitigation, preparedness, response, and recovery, so that local managers and institutions are clear on their responsibilities before, during, and after disasters. Based on the review, general strategies for managing pets in disaster contexts are proposed.

To address these questions, a scoping review methodology was purposefully chosen. Scoping reviews allow for a broader inclusion of studies compared to systematic reviews. They are beneficial for identifying key concepts, knowledge gaps, and types of evidence in a given field [19].

Unlike systematic reviews, which typically rely on a limited number of databases such as Web of Science, Scopus, and PubMed, our scoping review employed a more comprehensive search strategy. In addition to the aforementioned databases, we also included ProQuest and ScienceDirect to capture a broader range of relevant literature. The search was structured around the disaster management cycle, which includes the phases of mitigation, preparedness, response, and recovery. We sought to identify global research addressing pet management during disasters, with an emphasis on the needs, challenges, and coping strategies of pet owners across these phases.

Stage 2: Finding relevant studies

To ensure a comprehensive review, a multifaceted search strategy was employed. The search included electronic databases, reference lists, manual searches of useful journals, and additional resources such as relevant organizations’ websites, protocols, and conference proceedings. Initially, keywords related to the research questions were extracted and subjected to a peer-review process involving subject-matter experts. Following this, a library science expert was consulted to assist in developing a robust search strategy. This finalized strategy was then applied across the databases outlined in Stage 1. The literature search was last updated on 10 July 2023. The search strategy, applied to four primary databases (Web of Science, Scopus, PubMed, and ScienceDirect), used a combination of keywords and Boolean operators (see Table 1). Keyword searches were also performed in Google and Google Scholar. This database identified gray literature, such as guidelines and organizational protocols, for inclusion.

Core search terms were as follows: ‘Hazards OR Disasters’ AND ‘pet OR domestic dog OR domestic cat OR domestic bird OR other domestic animal’ AND ‘management OR mitigation OR prevention OR preparedness OR response OR recovery OR evacuation OR sheltering.’ Additionally, snowballing techniques were used to identify further relevant literature by reviewing the reference lists of key articles published within the last five to ten years.

Stage 3: Study selection

The study selection process followed the PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) flow diagram adapted for scoping reviews (see Fig. 1). The study inclusion criteria for article selection were as follows: 1) Articles focusing specifically on the management of pets during disasters; 2) Articles published in the English language; and 3) Articles addressing any measures related to pets in the context of hazards or emergencies. The study exclusion criteria were (1) articles primarily addressing farm or livestock animals and (2) articles focused on pets but unrelated to disasters.Fig. 1 PRISMA Flowchart of a scoping review on progress and challenges in pet management in disasters: recommended strategies for developing countries

PRISMA Flowchart of a scoping review on progress and challenges in pet management in disasters: recommended strategies for developing countries

The study selection proceeded in three stages. First, two authors screened the articles for relevance to the research topic. Second, the abstracts of potentially eligible articles were reviewed to determine suitability for inclusion. Finally, full-text reviews were conducted for all remaining articles to ensure alignment with the research objectives. The selection process yielded the following results: Initially, 2532 eligible and potentially relevant articles were identified for study. Of these, 694 articles were removed due to duplication. Then, 1670 articles were excluded after title and abstract screening due to irrelevance. Of the 168 articles selected for full-text review, 111 were excluded because they did not adequately address the research questions. Ultimately, 57 articles were included in the scoping review. The research team carefully studied all 57 remaining articles to identify the advances and challenges facing pet management in disasters.

Stage 4: Charting the data

In this phase, key information from the selected studies was systematically extracted and organized using a descriptive-analytical approach. This method applies a standardized analytical framework to all included studies, enabling consistent data collection and facilitating thematic synthesis. A data charting form was developed and implemented using Microsoft Excel. This form was designed to capture essential information from each article, including author(s), year of publication, study location, study type, study populations (e.g., pets, pet owners), timing of data collection, methodology, outcome measures, and key findings and results. This process enabled a structured and uniform review of the literature, ensuring that all relevant data points were considered across the included studies.

Stage 5: Collating, summarizing, and reporting the results

Unlike systematic reviews that focus on assessing the quality and effect size of evidence, the goal of this scoping review was to map and summarize the breadth of existing research. Thus, it is crucial to consider the best way to present this potentially substantial body of information. Given the volume and diversity of the studies, a thematic analysis was conducted to synthesize findings meaningfully. After the final selection of articles, the following steps were taken:1) Two researchers independently reviewed the results sections of the included studies to develop a comprehensive understanding; 2) All data relevant to pet management in disasters were extracted and coded; 3) The codes were reviewed, categorized, and named collaboratively by the research team; 4) The main themes were developed by grouping categories based on their relevance to the original research questions; and 5) The themes and categories were then independently reviewed and finalized by two additional researchers to ensure consistency and accuracy.

To ensure alignment with the research objectives, the findings were organized into two overarching themes: “Challenges of Pet Management in Disasters” and “Recommendations for Pet Management in Disasters.” Each theme was further elaborated through conceptual subcategories, which allowed the results to address the research questions posed in Stage 1 directly. Additionally, to explore the behavioral dimensions of pet owners’ disaster preparedness, two theoretical models from health education were applied: (1) TTM, used to assess the stages of behavioral change among pet owners; and (2) PMT, applied to identify motivational factors influencing preparedness actions for pet safety. These models gave explanations for both the barriers and motivators affecting pet owners’ disaster management behaviors, adding a valuable behavioral lens to the study’s findings.

Results

Descriptive statistics

The studies were conducted between 1996 and 2024. Two studies were from before the year 2000, and the majority of studies were from 2022. In terms of location, 30 are from the USA, 11 from Australia, three from Canada, and two each from Italy, New Zealand, and India. Additionally, there was one study from each of the following countries: Japan, Norway, Turkey, Ethiopia, Brazil, Portugal, and Bulgaria. Thirty of the studies focused exclusively on pets, 21 studies on all animals, and the remaining studies focused on companion animals. (See Table 2).

Challenges of pet management in disaster

Restrictions on pets in residential areas: Keeping pets in residential settings is increasingly common around the world. However, a significant challenge for pet owners arises when certain residential facilities, such as rental properties, nursing homes, and homeless shelters, prohibit animals. In such cases, pet owners face difficult decisions regarding the care and accommodation of their animals. These restrictions may force individuals to relinquish their pets or significantly alter their living arrangements and lifestyle. Additional burdens, such as the higher cost of pet-friendly housing or fees for pet al.lowances in rentals, often lead owners to rely on pet shelters during specific times of the day or night. Suppose a good shelter at a low cost can provide adequate services to the pet. However, the problem of finding a place for pets in such situations can potentially result in psychological stress and other mental health challenges for pet owners [4, 6, 20, 21].Ethical and legal challenges of chasing the pet in the response phase: Attempting to retrieve a pet during disasters, particularly when an animal enters private property, can create serious legal complications for rescue teams. It even causes the loss of the golden time of rescuing people from debris. However, choosing not to retrieve pets may lead to emotional distress, including post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), in pet owners. Furthermore, the transportation and logistical challenges associated with relocating pets during delayed evacuations are among the key reasons animal caregivers often risk their own lives during disasters. These complex challenges not only endanger the pets but also put their owners, caretakers, and emergency responders at serious risk [1, 22, 23].The lack of a comprehensive evacuation plan for pets: While some progress has been made in high-income countries regarding pet evacuation during disasters, many nations still lack a formal, integrated plan [24–30]. In some countries, legislation has been enacted to include pets in evacuation and sheltering procedures. However, the lack of a comprehensive and coordinated approach undermines the effectiveness of these fragmented efforts. Although in some cases, rules and guidelines for evacuating pets in natural disasters have been established based on lessons learned from previous disasters, these measures remain largely insufficient. During disasters, inadequate planning and a lack of awareness can lead to chaotic evacuations and significant losses. When pets are excluded from evacuation strategies, the consequences can be devastating, not just for the animals but also for their human companions [24–29, 31].Lack or shortage of suitable shelters for keeping pets: Many pet owners, especially older adults, wish to stay with their pets in humane shelters during emergencies. However, sheltering humans and animals together can lead to overcrowding, increasing the risk of zoonotic disease transmission. The emotional bond between elderly individuals and their pets is often intense, and separation from their pets can cause distress [32, 33]. During the COVID-19 pandemic, for instance, pets played a vital role in alleviating psychological stress during quarantine conditions [34]. Despite the recognized importance of this bond, various studies indicate a persistent lack of adequate shelter facilities in both developed and developing countries during emergencies. In some cases, due to insufficient space, lack of proper equipment, and inadequate resources, owners felt that shelters were not adequately equipped to care for their animals in an emergency [5, 7, 13, 20, 22, 25, 30, 32, 35–42].

Lack or shortage of suitable shelters for keeping pets: Many pet owners, especially older adults, wish to stay with their pets in humane shelters during emergencies. However, sheltering humans and animals together can lead to overcrowding, increasing the risk of zoonotic disease transmission. The emotional bond between elderly individuals and their pets is often intense, and separation from their pets can cause distress [32, 33]. During the COVID-19 pandemic, for instance, pets played a vital role in alleviating psychological stress during quarantine conditions [34]. Despite the recognized importance of this bond, various studies indicate a persistent lack of adequate shelter facilities in both developed and developing countries during emergencies. In some cases, due to insufficient space, lack of proper equipment, and inadequate resources, owners felt that shelters were not adequately equipped to care for their animals in an emergency [5, 7, 13, 20, 22, 25, 30, 32, 35–42].

Recommendations for pet management in disasters

Integrating pet management program into the CDM: Over the past decade, interest in human-animal relationships during disasters has increased, leading to the development of specialized pet management programs. These programs often include strategies such as pet registration, identification, reproductive control, educational initiatives, and community engagement. Research shows these initiatives benefit both human and animal health [3, 5, 7, 10, 22, 23, 33, 35–37, 41–52]. Since responsible pet ownership is crucial for the health of both individuals and communities, pet management strategies must be regularly reviewed and aligned with public health policies [53]. While many of these programs operate under normal conditions, they often remain disconnected from CDM. Despite the adoption of standard guidelines, such as the guidelines on disaster management and risk reduction for veterinary services introduced by the World Organization for Animal Health (WOAH) [54], many countries have yet to incorporate pet care into their official disaster response systems. Studies suggest integrating the pet management program into local and national CDM [3, 5, 7, 10, 22, 35–37, 41–52]. For example, in the aftermath of Hurricane Katrina, the city of New Orleans revised its evacuation plan to include pets, based on new guidelines and lessons learned. Developing countries can follow suit, tailoring integration strategies to their own geographic and cultural contexts [4, 24, 38, 51, 52, 55–63]. Veterinarians are responsible for providing essential care to animals housed in designated shelters and helping ensure the safety and well-being of both animals and people. Discussion

This study highlights both the challenges and advances in managing pets during disasters through an extensive literature review and a practical classification of findings. The insights gained apply to both developed and developing countries, offering pathways to improve pet management across various disaster phases.

The study identified several key challenges hindering effective pet management preparedness during disasters. These include the absence of a comprehensive pet evacuation plan, a lack of emergency kits and pet carriers, insufficient appropriate shelter for pets, low prioritization of the pet evacuation plan by local authorities, and the failure to integrate pets into CDM [12, 72]. To address these challenges, a comprehensive pet management plan in disasters should be designed for other countries, similar to those in developed countries. Such efforts require disaster management officials to develop appropriate risk perception regarding pets, as suggested by Protection Motivation Theory. Managers must accurately appraise the threat posed by neglecting pets in disasters, recognize the efficacy of their response actions, and incorporate pets within overall disaster management frameworks.

A study conducted by Travers et al. emphasizes that a pet management plan should be embedded within state and national disaster management plans. This integration should cover five key domains:1) Incorporating animals into emergency preparedness policies and practices; 2) Establishing pet-friendly shelters and associated laws; 3) Encouraging community participation in disaster planning; 4) Enhancing individual abilities through training and capacity-building initiatives; and 5) Shifting emergency and healthcare services toward a more inclusive, “more-than-human” approach [10]. In the pre-disaster phase (prevention and mitigation), several proactive measures are recommended:1) Pet owners should ensure their pets wear collars and tags with up-to-date contact information. 2) Microchipping pets is critical, as it is one of the fastest methods to reunite lost animals with their owners; owners must also activate and keep their chip registrations current. 3) Keeping carriers or leashes near exit points and maintaining vehicles equipped with pet travel supplies (carriers, harnesses, and pet seatbelts) is essential. Preparing a dedicated pet disaster kit, including medical records compiled with veterinary assistance, supports safe evacuation for the entire household [4, 6, 7].

In the preparedness phase, practicing these measures enables all family members to evacuate safely with their pets. Pets significantly influence owners’ emergency decisions, sometimes complicating rescue operations. Moreover, the loss of pets during disasters has a profound negative impact on human health and well-being. Therefore, developing and implementing clear pet evacuation protocols aligned with rescue organizations’ “human-first” principle can minimize harm to pets and reduce psychological trauma for owners. This alignment also enables rescuers to carry out their duties more effectively and with enhanced peace of mind [7–10]. Finally, applying behavioral models such as the Stages of Change and TTM can help assess and improve disaster relief teams’ preparedness to evacuate and care for pets. For an adequate response, the relief teams’ decisional balance should align rescuing pets with saving their owners, ensuring coordinated efforts that address both human and animal welfare.

During the preparation phase, it is highly recommended that pet owners regularly practice disaster scenarios with their family members and pets to ensure readiness when a disaster occurs. Training should include familiarizing pets with their carriers to make them comfortable and encourage voluntary entry and stay. Owners should also rehearse transporting their pets in a vehicle similar to the one used for emergency evacuations. It is important to be aware of potential hiding spots where pets may seek refuge and to practice safely rescuing them if necessary. For cat owners specifically, practicing how to safely remove cats from hiding places using carriers, pillowcases, or sturdy boxes is vital for quick evacuations. Families should conduct evacuation drills with their pets so that everyone knows what to bring, where to locate them, and where to gather during an emergency.

The Federal Emergency Management Agency (FEMA) emphasizes the importance of the Emergency Operations Plan (EOP) in emergencies. An Emergency Operations Plan (EOP) is a comprehensive document that describes how an organization will respond to various emergencies. EOPs clarify the procedures, responsibilities, and resources needed to effectively prevent, protect, mitigate, respond to, and recover from emergencies [73]. It is critical to include a suitable pet shelter in the operation plan, ideally located near pet owner shelters to facilitate their reunification [35]. Pets cannot be housed at general human shelters without specialized animal services. Therefore, owners should identify pet-friendly shelters, trusted friends or family members out of town who can care for their pets, boarding facilities, or nearby veterinary clinics. If owners are absent during a disaster, establishing a reliable network of neighbors or friends to assist with pet evacuation and care is essential. It is also important to keep veterinarians’ contact information in disaster kits and to identify local veterinarians or animal hospitals near evacuation shelters. Pets should never be left outside, chained, or unattended during disasters, as such behavior poses significant risks. Finally, routine veterinary visits are essential in the recovery phase to ensure pets’ health and well-being. Emergency kits specifically prepared for pets, including food, water, and healthcare supplies, are crucial for their proper nutrition and care following a disaster [36, 43].

Key findings and recommendations

One of the main issues identified in this scoping review is that pets continue to be insufficiently prioritized within CDM in some countries. The Sendai Framework explicitly emphasizes that governments must protect human assets and minimize psychological harm to affected populations [74]. In alignment with these obligations, the government should be encouraged to integrate the pet management program into its national disaster management strategies.

By combining the findings of this study with the TTM and PMT, disaster managers in developed countries can enhance their risk perception by fostering self-efficacy and supportive decision-making attitudes. Subsequently, through processes of change such as self-liberation, social liberation, stimulus control, and reinforcement management, pet owners can be supported as they progress from the passive stages (pre-contemplation and contemplation) to the active stages (action and maintenance).

Future research directions and adaptive strategies

Future studies should explore the characteristics of pet owners through the lenses of TTM and PMT and consider the guidance of the Sendai Framework in protecting pets in disasters. Recommended adaptive mechanisms to improve emergency preparedness include (1) investigating the link between animal abuse and psychological or personality disorders among pet owners, using Protection Motivation Theory to identify motivations for abuse and prevent associated problems, especially during disasters. (2) Encouraging all family members to practice disaster preparedness scenarios specifically focused on pet protection to avoid complications during evacuation. (3) Advising pet owners to inform trusted neighbors about necessary pet care measures, facilitating assistance if emergencies arise. (4) Considering the implementation of assessments and criteria for pet ownership, similar to those used for emotional support animals, to ensure responsible pet care. (5) Ultimately, fostering motivation, sensitivity, and understanding among individuals regarding pet protection during disasters will significantly improve the success of evacuation programs and enhance pet welfare [72, 75–77].

Limitations

This study provides a scoping review of advances and challenges in pet management during disasters, offering a foundation for policymaking and more detailed operational planning. However, the findings are generalized and adopt an all-hazard approach. Future research should examine pet management challenges specific to different types of disasters.

Additionally, this study focuses exclusively on the management of owned pets. It does not address free-roaming unowned dogs (FRDs), which represent a significant concern in both natural and man-made disasters. Numerous studies highlight the role of FRDs in the transmission of zoonotic diseases [78, 79].

In regions where strategies for controlling FRDs are lacking and disaster preparedness is inadequate, particularly in developing countries, further investigation is warranted [72, 80]. Future studies should explore the complex issue of housing FRDs alongside owned pets in shelters.

Conclusion

Integrating pet management programs into comp CDM worldwide, especially in developing nations, can ensure better protection of pets during disasters. The challenge of inadequate pet shelters, especially during disasters, should also be addressed. An underexplored area is the motivation of individuals to keep and care for pets—research on pet owners’ motivation before disasters is recommended to better understand their preparedness behaviors. Moreover, assessing the behavioral stages of pet owners regarding pet evacuation is crucial. This evaluation should determine if individuals are in the active stages of change regarding the protection of their pets and identify which factors improve these stages for better pet protection during disaster situations. There are two fundamental constructs for enhancing the protection of pets during disasters. Two theoretical frameworks hold particular promise for enhancing pet protection: The Protection Motivation Theory, which can help examine pet owners’ risk perceptions, particularly about the availability and proximity of suitable shelters. The Stages of Change Model aids in understanding and influencing owners’ preparedness behaviors. Together, these approaches can inform more effective disaster management strategies that safeguard pets and support their owners.
