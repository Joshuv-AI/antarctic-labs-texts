Emodin inhibits Newcastle disease virus replication via regulating the phosphorylation levels of eEF2K/eEF2 and targeting viral N protein to reduce viral protein production
Authors: Wenjie Wu, Rongjing Xia, Si Ma, Zenglei Hu, Zhiqiang Duan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Newcastle disease (ND) caused by Newcastle disease virus (NDV) infection is a highly contagious avian disease that causes substantial threat to the global poultry industry. Emodin (EMO), a member of the free anthraquinone compounds derived from traditional Chinese medicines, is known for its antibacterial, antiviral, antitumor, and antioxidant activity. However, the anti-NDV activity and potential mechanism of action of EMO remains unknown. In this study, EMO was found to exert a remarkable anti-NDV activity in a dose- and phase-dependent manner. In addition, EMO restricted NDV replication mainly by affecting the production of viral proteins, but not by inhibiting the viral RNA synthesis and transcription. Moreover, the EMO treatment-induced reduction of viral proteins was correlated well with the increased phosphorylation level of eukaryotic elongation factor 2 (eEF2) in NDV-infected cells, which was further confirmed by inhibition of eEF2 kinase (eEF2K) activity with pharmacological inhibitors NH125 and A-484954, respectively. Meanwhile, multiple signaling cascades, including p38 MAPK, mTORC1/p70 S6K, and ERK1/2/p90 RSK1, were found to regulate the phosphorylation levels and activities of eEF2K and eEF2 by which EMO reduced viral protein production and inhibited NDV replication. Furthermore, molecular docking combined with protein-protein interaction analysis showed that EMO could form stable hydrogen bond structures with the NDV N protein at the Lys32 and Leu225 residues, and attenuate its interaction with eIF4E to decrease the production of viral proteins. These findings reveal the anti-NDV effect and the underlying mechanism of EMO, offering a potential natural compound for the development of antiviral strategies against NDV.

Introduction

Newcastle disease (ND), caused by the infection of Newcastle disease virus (NDV), is a highly contagious disease of avian species that causes substantial threat to the global poultry industry (Chai and Hu, 2025). Although NDV has only one serotype, it can be categorized into two categories (Class I and Class II) and three pathogenic phenotypes (known as velogenic, mesogenic, and lentogenic) (Miller et al., 2010). Among these, Class I NDV strains (NDVs) contain one genotype and are largely isolated from aquatic birds and domestic poultry with a lentogenic pathotype (Jia et al., 2022; Kim et al., 2024); while Class II NDVs include 21 genotypes that can be isolated from different bird species with pathotypes from lentogenic to velogenic pathotypes (Afonso., 2021; Kondakova et al., 2025). Notably, recent studies have revealed that some Class I (genotype 1.1.2, lentogenic) and Class II (genotype III, mesogenic) NDVs are showing the increased virulence and pathogenicity in chickens (Sun et al., 2022; Lu et al., 2024), and several velogenic Class II NDVs, including sub-genotypes VII.1.1, VII.2, XIIb, XIII.1.1, and XIV.2, have exhibited the characteristic of immune evasion in vaccinated chickens or geese (Amoia et al., 2023; Ren et al., 2025; Mihiretu et al., 2025; Sajo et al., 2025; Nguyen et al., 2026). These findings raise researchers’ attention to the continuous genetic mutation and adaptation of variant NDVs in poultry flocks (Duan et al., 2025).

At present, rapid diagnosis and vaccine development used for ND control are priorities in the developing and less-developed countries, but they have sometimes failed to provide complete protection against virulent NDV infection or a poorly immunized poultry population (Dimitrov et al., 2017; Hu et al., 2022; Kondakova et al., 2025). Therefore, apart from vaccination, developing more NDV prevention and control strategies, including the development of effective anti-NDV drugs, to reduce mortality rates and minimize the economic losses suffered by the poultry industry is currently an important research direction.

Antiviral agents are a class of medications used for preventing and treating viral infections. Nowadays, compounds with anti-NDV properties mainly include nucleoside analogs, antiprotozoal agent, chitosan nanoparticles, and natural compounds (Antony et al., 2020; Alkhalefa et al., 2022; Dyary., 2025; Singh et al., 2026). Of which plant-derived natural compounds are widely studied in anti-NDV research due to their broad bioactivity and sources. For instance, fucoidan, flavonoid, and canthin-6-one analogs have been reported to efficiently inhibit NDV replication (Bhuvaneswar et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2024; Gamage et al., 2025). A natural anthraquinone derivative emodin (EMO), derived from Rhubarb (Rheum spp.), Giant Knotweed (Polygonum cuspidatum), Aloe (Aloe spp.), Fleeceflower Root (Polygonum multiflorum), and etc., possesses antibacterial, anti-inflammatory, antifibrotic, antitumor, and antioxidant activities, which has great potential for clinical application (Zhu et al., 2025). Additionally, EMO has also exhibited remarkable inhibitory effects against various human and animal viruses, thereby providing valuable reference for the development of targeted antiviral drugs against virus infections (Shao et al., 2022).

Increasing research has revealed that EMO exerts antiviral effects through multiple pathways. For example, EMO inhibits the interaction between the spike protein of severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus (SARS-CoV) and the host receptor protein ACE2, or EMO targets and binds to the fusion protein of respiratory syncytial virus (RSV), which blocks subsequent viral adsorption and entry pathways (Ho et al., 2007; Xiong et al., 2024). In addition, EMO treatment not only reduces the gene transcriptional levels of pseudorabies virus (PRV) but also inhibits viral protein expression, alleviating tissue and organ damage caused by PRV infection (Cai et al., 2023). Moreover, the addition of EMO enhances host antiviral immunity against Micropterus salmoides rhabdovirus (MSRV) by activating innate immune responses; thus, it may be an effective and practical antiviral agent in largemouth bass aquaculture (Wang et al., 2025). However, there are currently no reported literature about the use of EMO for ND treatment. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the anti-NDV activity of EMO and investigate its underlying mechanism of action, which will provide experimental evidence for its potential application against NDV infection.

Materials and methods

Cells and virus

Chicken embryo fibroblast (DF-1) and human cervical carcinoma (HeLa) cell lines were kind gifts from Prof. Xiufan Liu (Yangzhou University, Yangzhou, China). DF-1 or HeLa cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) (Gibco, California, USA) containing 15% or 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco, California, USA) at 37°C in an atmosphere with 5% CO2. The virulent recombinant NDV rSS1RFP (recombinant NDV expressing red fluorescent protein) derived from its parent NDV SS1 (Sheldrake duck/China/Guizhou/SS1/2014, GenBank no. KP742770.1) was preserved in our laboratory (Duan et al., 2016). The virus was propagated once in 10-day-old embryonated SPF chicken eggs and then stored at −80°C until use.

Chemical compounds and primary antibodies

EMO at a purity of 98% high performance liquid chromatography was purchased from Beyotime Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China) and dissolved in 1% dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) to prepare a stock solution of 10 mM. The chemical compounds NH125 and A-484954 were purchased from MedChemExpress (South Brunswick Township, New Jersey, USA). The rabbit anti-N and -HN polyclonal antibodies were prepared in our laboratory. The rabbit anti-eEF2 (AF7721), anti-p-eEF2(Thr57) (AF7221), anti-eEF2K (DF7323), anti-p-eEF2K(Ser359) (AF8269), anti-p-eEF2K(Ser366) (AF2340), anti-p-eEF2K(Ser491/Ser492) (AF3785), anti-Myc (AF5204), anti-p70 S6K(AF6226), anti-p-p70 S6K(Thr389) (AF3228), anti-p90 RSK1(AF4777), anti-p-p90 RSK1(Ser380) (AF3808), anti-ERK1/2 (AF0155), anti-p-ERK1/2(Thr202/Tyr204) (AF1015), anti-p38 MAPK (AF6456), anti-p-p38 MAPK(Thr180/Tyr182) (AF4001) polyclonal antibodies, and the mouse anti-GAPDH (T0004), anti-HA (T0008), anti-Myc (T0001) monoclonal antibodies were purchased from Affinity Biosciences (Cincinnati, USA). The rabbit anti-p-eEF2K(Ser78) (PA5-143686), anti-p-eEF2K(Ser398) (PA5-143689), anti-p-eEF2K(Ser445) (PA5-143690), anti-p-eEF2K(Ser500) (PA5-143691), anti-mTORC1 (PA5-34663), anti-p-mTORC1(Ser2448) (44-1125 G) polyclonal antibodies were purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc. (Massachusetts, USA).

Cell‑counting kit (CCK)-8 assay for cell viability

CCK-8 (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) assay was used to evaluate the effect of EMO on cell viability. Briefly, 1 × 105 DF-1 or HeLa cells/mL were plated into 96-well cell culture plates (Corning, New York, USA), incubated for 12 h until the cells reached confluency, and then treated with different concentrations of EMO (5, 10, 20, 40, 80, and 100 μM). After 48 h, the cells were incubated with 10 µL CCK-8 at 37°C for additional 1 h, and the optical density (OD) values of each well at 450 nm wavelength were determined using a microplate reader. The cell viability was indicated as a percentage of the cell viability of the control to evaluate the cytotoxicity of EMO.

Antiviral activity analysis of EMO

To evaluate the inhibitory effect of EMO on NDV replication, DF-1 or HeLa cells were grown in 96-well culture plates for 12 h, and then infected with 0.1 multiplicity of infection (MOI) rSS1RFP at 37 °C. Different concentrations of EMO (5, 10, and 20 μM) were added before (pre-treatment), during (co-treatment), or after (post-treatment) rSS1RFP infection as previously described (Chen et al., 2023). At 24, 48, and 72 h post-infection (hpi), the virus titres in the culture supernatants were examined using 50% tissue culture infective doses (TCID50) in cells. Meanwhile, the cytopathic effect (CPE) and red fluorescent protein (DsRed) expression were observed using an inverted fluorescent microscope (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan).

Quantification of viral RNA synthesis and transcription

Viral RNA (vRNA) synthesis and transcription levels in the post-treatment group were examined using quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) assay at 6, 12, and 24 hpi. Primers qDsRed-F (5′-ACGGCGGCATTATTCACGTTAA-3′) and qDsRed-R (5′-TCCCAGCCCATGGTCTTCTTCT-3′) were used to quantify the mRNA level of DsRed gene, and the other primers used to detect vRNA synthesis (viral N and P genes) and mRNA (viral M gene) level were as described previously (Qiu et al., 2016; Duan et al., 2019). qRT-PCR was performed using a SYBR Premix Ex Taq Kit (Takara, Dalian, China) in Bio-Rad CFX-96 real-time fluorescence quantitative PCR instrument (California, USA). The qRT-PCR mixture system and cycling parameters were performed as in previous studies (Zhang et al., 2025). The threshold cycle 2-∆∆CT method was used to calculate the relative abundance of vRNA and mRNA, and the relative expression levels were normalized to those of the GAPDH gene.

Cell fractionation and Western blot analysis

NDV-infected cells were treated with EMO or control vehicle, harvested at the indicated time points, and then lysed using 1 × RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China). A bicinchoninic acid kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) was used to standardize the protein contents. The separation, polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane transfer, and antibody incubation of protein samples were carried out as described previously (Hou et al., 2025). After that, the protein bands were observed using an ECL detection system (Bio-Rad, California, USA), and the signals were quantified using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, Maryland, USA). The expression of GAPDH protein in samples was used to normalize the difference of detected target protein levels.

Molecular docking of EMO and NDV N protein

The 3D chemical structure of EMO (PubChem CID: 3220) was downloaded from the PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/), and the crystal structure of NDV N protein (PDB ID: 6JC3) was downloaded from the RCSB Protein Data Bank (PDB) (https://www.rcsb.org/). The CB-Dock2 website (http://183.56.231.194:8001/cb-dock2/index.php), an improved server for protein-ligand blind docking, integrating cavity detection, docking and homologous template fitting (Liu et al., 2022), was used for molecular docking analysis of EMO and the NDV N protein. PyMOL 2.5.5 software (Schrödinger, San Diego, California, USA) was used to generate the structural annotations.

Protein-protein interaction analysis

The coding sequences of human eIF4E gene (GenBank no. NM_001968.5), NDV N gene (GenBank no. KP742770.1) and its mutant (N protein harboring K32A/L225A mutations) containing appropriate restriction sites were synthesized by Tsingke Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China), and then inserted into the pCMV-HA and pCMV-Myc to generate pCMV-HA-eIF4E, pCMV-Myc-N, and pCMV-Myc-N/K32A/L225A, respectively. All the constructed recombinant plasmids were confirmed by restriction enzyme digestion and DNA sequencing.

The indicated plasmids were co-transfected into HeLa cells by Lipofectamine™ 3000 transfection reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Massachusetts, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and the interaction between the N protein or its mutant protein N/K32A/L225A and eIF4E in EMO-treated or -untreated cells was verified by co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) and indirect immunofluorescence assay (IFA) assays, respectively, as described previously (Zhan et al., 2020). The protein fluorescence was observed with a laser confocal scanning microscope (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan), and the line intensity profile of proteins was analyzed using ImageJ software.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were repeated at least three independent times, and the data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). The statistical significance of the data was assessed by two-tailed independent Student’s t-test or a one-way analysis of variance with the GraphPad Prism 9.5 software (San Diego, California, USA). The differences are indicated by asterisk (*): * P< 0.05, ** P < 0.01, and *** P < 0.001; no asterisk indicates no significant difference.

EMO post-treatment reduces viral protein production

To investigate the potential mechanism of EMO post-treatment inhibiting NDV replication, the vRNA synthesis, transcription, and translation of NDV was first examined. Consistent with the above results, post-treatment of 20 µM EMO obviously suppressed NDV replication in DF-1 cells at 6, 12, and 24 hpi, when compared to that in the 5 µM EMO, 10 µM EMO, and DMSO post-treatment groups, and the CPE and DsRed expression in rSS1RFP-infected cells were also decreased in 20 µM EMO post-treatment group at 24 hpi (Fig. 3A). However, there was no difference in the vRNA synthesis and transcription levels of rSS1RFP among the four groups at different time points (Fig. 3B). By contrast, the viral N and HN protein expression levels were significantly reduced in 20 µM EMO post-treatment group in comparison to the other three groups (Fig. 3C). Meanwhile, the results of IFA showed that 20 µM EMO post-treatment decreased the expression level of viral N protein (Fig. 3D). EMO upregulates the NDV-induced phosphorylation level of eukaryotic elongation factor 2 (eEF2)

Studies have demonstrated that EMO inhibits viral protein production by either affecting the eEF2 kinase (eEF2K)/eEF2 signaling pathway or by specifically binding to viral proteins (Yiu et al., 2014; Zhang et al., 2016; Cai et al., 2023). So, the expression and phosphorylation levels of NDV-induced eEF2 after EMO treatment were first examined. Consistent with the previous results, post-treatment with 20 µM EMO increased the phosphorylation level of eEF2 (p-eEF2(Thr57)) in DF-1 cells, but it had no obvious effect on eEF2 expression (Fig. 4A). It has been reported that chemical compounds NH125 and A-484954 are the highly selective eEF2K inhibitors, which specifically increases or inhibits the p-eEF2(Thr57) levels, respectively (Chen et al., 2011). As expected, compared with the DMSO and control groups, the p-eEF2(Thr57) levels in 20 µM EMO- or 8 µM NH125-treated cells were increased while were decreased in 80 µM A-484954-treated cells at 24 h after NDV infection (Fig. 4B). Meanwhile, there was no difference in the expression of eEF2 between all the treatment groups and the control groups, but EMO and NH125 treatment-induced downregulation of viral N protein was correlated well with the increased p-eEF2(Thr57) levels during NDV infection (Fig. 4B). In addition, EMO or NH125 treatment significantly decreased the viral titres and reduced the CPE and DsRed expression in rSS1RFP-infected DF-1 cells, whereas A-484954 treatment obtained the opposite results (Fig. 4C). These findings indicated that EMO reduced viral protein production by upregulating the p-eEF2(Thr57) level, which was detrimental to the NDV replication.

In addition, the results of Co-IP assay showed that the NDV N protein could strongly bind to eIF4E, whereas their binding ability obviously decreased in EMO-treated cells (Fig. 6B). Meanwhile, the interaction between the mutant N protein (N/K32A/L225A) and eIF4E was not affected even in the case of EMO treatment (Fig. 6B). Moreover, the results of fluorescence co-localization indicated that the NDV N protein had obvious co-localization with eIF4E in the cytoplasm without EMO treatment, but their cytoplasmic co-localization was visibly reduced after EMO treatment (Fig. 6C). On the contrary, whether EMO existed or not, the co-localization of the mutant N protein (N/K32A/L225A) and eIF4E did not change in the cytoplasm (Fig. 6C). These results demonstrated that EMO could bind to the NDV N protein and attenuate its interaction with eIF4E, which might be responsible for the reduction in the production of viral proteins.

Discussion

EMO is a representative member of the free anthraquinone compounds derived from various plants and traditional Chinese medicines. It has been reported to exhibit significant inhibitory effect on multiple viral infections and broad-spectrum antiviral activity (Colin et al., 2024). However, the anti-NDV activity and action mechanism of EMO remains unclear. In this study, time-of-addition assay was first performed to evaluate the anti-NDV activity of EMO. We found that EMO exhibited a remarkable anti-NDV activity in a dose- and phase-dependent manner. At present, several studies have shown that EMO inhibits the replication of SARS-CoV, SARS-CoV-2, PRV, African swine fever virus, and Chinese rice-field eels rhabdovirus in a dose gradient-dependent manner (Ho et al., 2007; Xu et al., 2023; Cai et al., 2023; Guo et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2026). However, it is strange that EMO exerted the inhibition effect of NDV in a dose gradient-independent manner. This phenomenon seemed to be unrelated to the cytotoxicity of EMO, as evidenced by the fact that EMO with concentrations ranging from 5 to 20 μM did not affect the cell viability. Even so, only DF-1 or HeLa cells treated with 20 μM EMO showed obvious anti-NDV activity, indicating its higher dose being most effective. The reason for this lack of dose gradient dependency is unclear in this study; it is possible that this observation may have been missed owing to the limited EMO concentrations of our evaluations. Therefore, EMO with concentrations ranging from 20 to 40 μM might be necessary to determine whether its anti-NDV activity is in a dose gradient-dependent manner.

Currently, mechanistic insights into the antiviral activity of EMO are usually involved in the blockade of viral adsorption and entry, suppression of viral genome replication and transcription, inhibition of viral protein production, disruption of viral assembly and release, and regulation of cellular immunity (Shao et al., 2022). As for NDV, the successful adsorption and entry into host cells of infectious viral particles are the key factors to complete its subsequent replication and transmission (Deng et al., 2024). In the present study, the pre- and co-treatment of EMO did not affect NDV replication in DF-1 and HeLa cells, suggesting that EMO might not destroy the morphology or infectivity of the NDV virions, nor did it affect the interaction between the viral glycoproteins and host cell receptors. By contrast, the post-treatment of EMO obviously reduced the viral titres of NDV, indicating that EMO predominantly influenced the replication process of NDV within cells. These results were consistent with the previous findings that EMO mainly inhibits the intracellular replication of herpes simplex virus type 1 and 2 (Xiong et al., 2011), enterovirus 71 (Zhong et al., 2017), porcine reproductive and respiratory syndrome virus (Xu et al., 2021), and PRV (Cai et al., 2023). Consequently, these results demonstrated that EMO exerted anti-NDV effect in a phase-dependent manner, suggesting that EMO might have a favorable effect on the in vivo treatment of NDV infection.

To further explore the underlying anti-NDV mechanism of EMO, the effect of EMO on vRNA synthesis, transcription, and translation of NDV was then evaluated. We found that EMO treatment inhibited NDV replication mainly by reducing the viral protein production rather than affecting the vRNA synthesis and transcription, suggesting its role in impairing viral translational machinery. Previous studies have reported that eEF2 occurs phosphorylation at Thr57 sites by its single known kinase eEF2K, which leads to inactivation of eEF2 and inhibition of the elongation step as well as the subsequent protein synthesis rate (Shen et al., 2021; Piserchio et al., 2024). In addition, one related study has indicated that the inhibition of coxsakievirus B3 protein production by EMO is involved in upregulating the p-eEF2(Thr57) level (Zhang et al., 2016). Consistent with this finding, EMO post-treatment obviously increased the p-eEF2(Thr57) levels both in NDV-infected DF-1 and HeLa cells, but had no effect on eEF2 expression. Meanwhile, the reduced viral protein production and viral replication caused by EMO post-treatment were correlated well with the increased p-eEF2(Thr57) level during NDV infection, which was further confirmed by inhibition of eEF2K activity with pharmacological inhibitors NH125 and A-484954, respectively. Thus, these findings clearly indicated that the elevated level of p-eEF2(Thr57) caused by EMO post-treatment was an important factor contributing to the inhibition of NDV replication.

To date, increasing number of research has demonstrated that eEF2K activity is regulated by phosphorylation of its serine at different sites, mainly including Ser78, Ser359, Ser366, Ser445, Ser491/Ser492, and Ser500. Among these, Ca2+- and calmodulin (CaM)-dependent auto-phosphorylation of eEF2K at Ser500 increases the affinity of kinase to CaM and enhances its activity (Tavares et al., 2017); by contrast, mTOR-driven phosphorylation of Ser78, p38 MAPK-driven phosphorylation of Ser359, mTORC1/p70 S6K- and ERK1/2/p90 RSK1-driven phosphorylation of Ser366, and AMPK-driven phosphorylation of Ser491/Ser492 decrease the kinase activity of eEF2K (Zhdanov et al., 2022; Piserchio et al., 2024). Therefore, the activation of eEF2K leads to phosphorylation of eEF2 and decreases its activity, while inactivation of eEF2K has an opposite effect (Jia et al., 2024). Here, the expression level of eEF2K had no obvious change, while the expression of p-eEF2K(Ser359), and p-eEF2K(Ser366) showed markedly decreased levels in EMO-treated group. In addition, we also found that multiple signaling cascades (including p38 MAPK, mTORC1/p70 S6K, and ERK1/2/p90 RSK1) were merged into the eEF2K/eEF2 signaling pathway, which converged at the activation of eEF2K and inactivation of eEF2 that involved in the suppression of viral protein production during EMO treatment (Fig. 7). Evidence suggests that viral proteins expressed across the viral life cycle can be as targets of antiviral drugs. Up to now, the viral proteins that regulate NDV protein production are reported to be the N and M proteins, of which the N protein primarily promotes the cap-dependent translation of viral mRNAs by directly interacting with host eIF4E (Zhan et al., 2020), and the M protein enhances viral translation by indirectly upregulating the expression of various ribosomal proteins (Duan et al., 2022). In this study, the NDV N protein was selected to investigate the potential binding of EMO with N protein and the effect of EMO on the N-eIF4 interaction. It was found that EMO could form stable hydrogen bond structures with the NDV N protein at the Lys32 and Leu225 residues through molecular docking. Specially, the binding ability and cytoplasmic co-localization of viral N protein with eIF4E showed significant reductions in the case of EMO treatment, while its effect on the mutant N protein (N/K32A/L225A) and eIF4E was lost. This finding demonstrated that the attenuated interaction between the NDV N protein and eIF4E caused by EMO treatment was another reason for the reduction of viral protein production (Fig. 7). It is notable that the viral N protein is a pivotal component of the ribonucleoprotein (RNP) complex inside the NDV virions, which encapsidates the vRNA to form nucleocapsids and assists in vRNA genome replication and transcription (Song et al., 2019; Zhou et al., 2025). However, EMO treatment did not affect the vRNA synthesis and transcription of NDV. The possible reason maybe that EMO did not interfere with the viral RNP formation and the RNA polymerase activity. Therefore, the N protein mutant NDV can be rescued by reverse genetics to further evaluate the effect of EMO on its vRNA synthesis, transcription, and protein production in the future.

Conclusions

In summary, our results demonstrate that EMO exerts a remarkable anti-NDV activity in a dose- and phase-dependent manner, which inhibits NDV replication mainly by affecting the production of viral proteins. Mechanistic studies suggest that EMO reduces viral protein production via regulating eEF2K/eEF2 phosphorylation levels through multiple signaling cascades, as well as targeting the viral N protein to attenuate its interaction with eIF4E, ultimately leading to the suppressed replication of NDV (Fig. 7). These findings enrich the research content and theoretical basis of EMO’s antiviral activity and also provide a potential antiviral drug candidate for preventing and controlling NDV infection.
