Global prevalence and mortality of cardiovascular disease by sex and sociodemographic index in young adults (20–39 years): Global burden of disease database 1990–2023
Authors: Emily Littman, Robert B. Barrett, Yaa Adoma Kwapong, Andrew Broda, Theresa Boyer, Erin D. Michos, Elizabeth Selvin, Allison G. Hays, Seth S. Martin, Anum S. Minhas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
None - brief report

Introduction

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) is the leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide, yet the global epidemiology of CVD in young adults is not as well-studied compared to older adults. CVD is influenced by sociodemographic factors and sex [1]. We evaluated global CVD prevalence, mortality rates, and temporal trends among young (20–39 years) compared to middle aged-older adults (40+ years), stratified by sex and sociodemographic index (SDI).

Methods

Using the 2023 Global Burden of Disease (GBD) Study, we evaluated global CVD prevalence and mortality per 100,000 people. Data from 1990–2023 was stratified by age (young [20–39 years] versus middle aged-older adults [40+ years]), sex, and Socio-Demographic Index (SDI). SDI, defined as a unit-interval based on income per capita, mean years of schooling, and total fertility rate, is reported in quintiles [2].

We performed endpoint comparisons to evaluate differences in prevalence and mortality between 1990 and 2023 using Wald-type tests based on standard errors approximated from reported uncertainty intervals (UI). Temporal trends were assessed using log-linear regression models, with calendar year as the independent variable, allowing estimation of annual percent change (APC) in rates. Because yearly estimates from the GBD study are not independent and may exhibit temporal autocorrelation, robust standard errors were calculated using the Newey–West method. As a sensitivity analysis, weighted regression models were constructed using inverse-variance weights approximated from reported UIs. Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided p-value <0.05. All estimates were reported with 95% UI, defined as the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of 1000 draws.

This study utilized publicly available, de-identified data. Informed consent and ethics committee approval were not required.

Results

Among young adults, prevalence in 1990 was 2474 per 100,000 [95% UI 2145–2871] in men and 2369 [2056–2756] in women, increasing to 2688 [2328–3131] and 2551 [2199–3002], respectively, in 2023. However, endpoint comparisons between 1990 and 2023 were not statistically significant for either sex in young adults (women: difference 182 [−351 to 714], p = 0.50; men: 214 [−327 to 755], p = 0.44).

Despite the absence of significant endpoint differences, log-linear trend analyses demonstrated a significant increase in prevalence over time among young adults (females: annual percent change [APC] 0.15% [0.13–0.17], p < 0.001; males: 0.18% [0.13–0.24], p < 0.001).

Among middle aged–older adults, prevalence was markedly higher but endpoint differences were not statistically significant in either sex (p > 0.70 for both). Trend analyses demonstrated modest but statistically significant declines over time (females: APC −0.12% [−0.19 to −0.05], p = 0.002; males: −0.07% [−0.12 to −0.02], p = 0.006).

Prevalence was consistently higher in men than women across both age groups, and there was no significant difference in temporal trends by sex (interaction p > 0.05).

Cardiovascular disease mortality

CVD mortality was markedly higher among middle aged–older adults than young adults and declined over time in both groups (Fig. 1b). Among young adults, mortality decreased from 29 [21–36] to 23 [19–29] per 100,000 in men and from 21 [16–25] to 15 [12–19] in women between 1990 and 2023, though endpoint reductions were non-significant.

Log-linear trend analyses demonstrated significant declines in mortality among young adults (men: APC −0.78% [−0.88 to −0.68], p < 0.001; women: −0.75% [−0.85 to −0.64], p < 0.001), with a slightly greater decline observed in men (interaction p = 0.018).

Among middle aged–older adults, mortality also declined substantially from 1990 to 2023 (men: 905 [820–984] to 662 [603–736]; women: 882 [778–975] to 575 [471–657] per 100,000; p < 0.001 for both). These declines were corroborated by trend analyses (men: APC −0.90% [−0.98 to −0.82]; women: −1.02% [−1.12 to −0.91]; both p < 0.001), with no significant difference in trends between sexes (interaction p = 0.21).

In sensitivity analyses, results for CVD prevalence and mortality trends were consistent across modeling approaches, including inverse-variance weighted regression, Newey–West models accounting for temporal autocorrelation, and generalized least squares with AR(1) correlation structure.

Sociodemographic index

Across SDI quintiles, CVD prevalence and mortality remained substantially higher among middle aged–older adults compared with young adults (Figs. 1c–d). While endpoint comparisons showed modest but non-significant changes across SDI strata, in 2023 prevalence among young adults was highest in low SDI regions. Whereas among older adults it was highest in high SDI regions.

Despite variable endpoint differences, log-linear trend analyses demonstrated significant but modest temporal changes in prevalence across SDI strata. Among young adults, prevalence increased in most SDI regions, whereas among middle aged–older adults, prevalence declined, particularly in higher SDI settings. In contrast, CVD mortality declined across all SDI strata with a clear inverse gradient with SDI and no consistent differences in trends between sexes.

Findings were consistent in sensitivity analyses, including inverse-variance weighted regression and models accounting for temporal autocorrelation.

Discussion

Our findings highlight a divergence in global CVD patterns by age. Among young adults, trend analysis showed CVD prevalence increasing over time, while mortality declined. In contrast, among middle aged-older adults, both prevalence and mortality declined. Together, these findings suggest a shift toward earlier disease onset with improved survival, likely reflecting advances in detection and treatment alongside persistent or emerging upstream risk factors [3].

Sex differences were consistent across age groups, with higher CVD prevalence and mortality in men. However, temporal trends were similar between sexes, suggesting that population-level drivers of CVD are indiscriminately affecting men and women. Notably, unique sex-specific risk factors in women remain important contributors that may not be fully captured [4].

Marked differences were also observed across SDI strata. Among young adults, higher prevalence and mortality in low SDI regions suggest earlier exposure to cardiovascular risk in settings with limited preventive care. In contrast, among middle aged–older adults, higher prevalence but lower mortality in high SDI regions likely reflects improved survival due to greater access to screening and treatment. These findings underscore the duality of earlier disease onset in lower-resource settings and prolonged disease survival in higher-resource settings [5].

Prevention and treatment strategies may historically have targeted older populations, resulting in disproportionate increase of CVDin young adults with declining prevalence in older adults. The American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association primary CVD prevention guidelines emphasize risk identification and mitigation in early adulthood (20–39 years) [6]. Consideration of traditional and sex-specific risk factors, risk-enhancing conditions, access to care, and SDI patterns, should be incorporated in efforts to reduce global CVD burden.

This study has limitations. CVD classification within the GBD framework relies on diagnostic coding, case definitions, and data availability which may vary across regions and over time. GBD estimates are derived from multiple data sources and modeling approaches, including imputation, and may not fully reflect true population-level prevalence. Further, the country level composite of SDI may miss regional heterogeneity, introducing ecological bias. Additionally, trend analyses based on annual GBD point estimates may not capture the uncertainty embedded in the modeled GBD estimates; therefore, regression-based p-values should be interpreted cautiously.

Conclusion

CVD prevalence is rising in young adults globally, with important differences by sex and SDI. Future work should prioritize age-and sex-specific cardiovascular prevention, screening, and treatment across SDI strata to improve outcomes.
