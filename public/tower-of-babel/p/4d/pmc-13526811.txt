Saltation-consistent event-aware digital twins for uncertainty transport in non-smooth dynamical systems
Authors: Shoniwa Mosekari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNon-smooth mechanical systems challenge prediction not only because their trajectories contain impacts, frictional transitions, and unilateral constraints, but because these events reorganize state propagation, sensitivity, uncertainty, and control authority. Existing digital-twin, hybrid-systems, Bayesian-estimation, and reduced-order frameworks each address part of that difficulty, yet they are usually developed on separate mathematical layers and therefore become least reliable precisely where engineering decisions are most event-sensitive. This paper introduces an event-aware mechanics and inference architecture for non-smooth dynamical systems in which event surfaces are treated as first-class transport interfaces rather than as numerical exceptions. Starting from a high-fidelity hybrid mechanical model with unilateral contact, friction, and measure-valued impulses, we derive saltation-consistent transport laws for sensitivities and covariance, formulate forced Bayesian correction at mechanically determined event times, and establish reduced-order error bounds that isolate jump mismatch as a distinct approximation channel. The resulting framework couples full-order mechanics, event-aware uncertainty propagation, online parameter updating, and reduced-order approximation within a single mathematically consistent architecture. Benchmarks involving impact, stick–slip, and shock-response dynamics show that event-aware transport preserves post-event state accuracy, concentrates parameter information in short event windows, and improves reduced-order fidelity during discontinuity-dominated transients. The central advance is the identification of event surfaces as the structural interfaces through which mechanics, inference, uncertainty transport, and control are coupled in non-smooth digital twins.Keywords: Event-aware digital twins, Non-smooth dynamics, Hybrid systems, Measure differential equations, Saltation matrix, Bayesian updating, Reduced-order modelling, Event-triggered control, Contact dynamicsSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing, Physics

Introduction

A large class of engineered structures and mechanisms is governed by differential inclusions, unilateral constraints, complementarity laws, and impulsive momentum balance rather than by globally smooth ordinary differential equations1–12. Contact loss in impact dampers, stick–slip switching in friction oscillators, intermittent collisions in compliant machinery, and shock loading in protective devices all produce mode-dependent dynamics in which the governing equations, admissible state domain, and effective control authority can change on the timescale of a single event. In such systems the dominant scientific difficulty is not simply stiffness. It is the coexistence of continuous flow and discrete reset.

The same systems motivate event-triggered update logic. When contact is active, new reaction channels, new sensitivities, and new actuation pathways appear; when contact is lost, they disappear. Classical event-triggered and self-triggered control shows how updating can be scheduled by state-dependent information rather than by a fixed clock, but those ideas are usually developed for smooth plants or for hybrid systems whose mechanical reset physics is already assumed known 13–16. In contact-rich mechanics, the challenge is harder: the trigger, the dynamics, and the uncertainty map are all entangled with the event itself.

Near grazing, impact, and mode-switching surfaces, small errors in predicted event time can be amplified into order-one errors in post-event state because the reset map acts on the wrong pre-event condition. The same mechanism corrupts gradients, covariances, and online parameter estimates unless the jump timing channel is treated explicitly. Impact oscillators, sliding systems, and Filippov flows therefore require tools that propagate sensitivities through guards rather than around them 17–19.

Digital twins have emerged as an important cyber-physical paradigm for synchronization, prediction, and decision support, but most twins in structural and mechanical applications still inherit a smooth-model bias 20–26. They often assimilate data successfully while the system remains in a single mode, then degrade precisely at the moments when operational decisions matter most: touchdown, rebound, slip onset, release from constraint, or shock arrival. A twin that interpolates smoothly through a discontinuity may appear numerically stable while being physically inconsistent.

The unresolved scientific gap is therefore not simply that non-smooth systems are difficult to model. The deeper problem is that, in most existing frameworks, the mechanical event is treated as a local complication rather than as the organizing object of inference. High-fidelity mechanics may resolve guards and resets, Bayesian filters may assimilate data, reduced-order models may compress the flow, and controllers may react online, but these elements are usually developed on different mathematical layers. What remains missing is a single architecture in which the same event geometry governs state propagation, uncertainty transport, parameter learning, reduced approximation, and control decisions.

This paper addresses that gap by introducing an event-aware mechanics and inference architecture for non-smooth dynamical systems. The novelty does not lie in merely combining digital twins, saltation matrices, Bayesian updates, and reduced-order models. It lies in showing that event surfaces must be treated as structural transport interfaces: they are the locations at which mechanics, covariance, information, and control authority are simultaneously reorganized. Once this is made explicit, the apparently separate problems of impulsive mechanics, uncertainty propagation, parameter updating, reduced modelling, and event-triggered decision-making become parts of one mathematically coherent construction.

The principal contributions are: (i) we formulate a high-fidelity hybrid mechanical model with explicit domains, guards, reset maps, unilateral constraints, friction laws, and measure-valued impulses; (ii) we derive saltation-consistent laws for sensitivity and covariance transport across resets, thereby identifying the event as a first-order uncertainty-transport interface rather than as a post-processing correction; (iii) we define a forced Bayesian correction mechanism at mechanically determined event times, showing how discontinuities localize information and alter parameter identifiability; (iv) we establish a reduced-order error structure in which jump mismatch appears as a distinct approximation channel, clarifying why event-aware reduction differs fundamentally from smooth projection alone; and (v) we demonstrate through impact, stick–slip, and shock-response benchmarks that the proposed architecture improves post-event prediction, parameter learning, and reduced-order fidelity in the discontinuity-dominated regime.

Literature review and scientific positioning

Physics-enhanced learning and Bayesian updating

Physics-informed and scientific-machine-learning approaches have greatly expanded the range of tools available for dynamic inference. Physics-informed neural networks embed governing equations in a loss functional, broader physics-informed machine-learning frameworks clarify when mechanistic structure improves extrapolation, universal differential equations provide a route for combining known dynamics with learned closures, and sparse regression methods such as SINDy recover parsimonious governing laws from data 27–32. These developments matter because they show that data-driven and physics-based modelling no longer need to be separated. Their limitation for the present class of problems is different: unless guards, resets, and event-local impulse laws are represented explicitly, learning machinery can smooth away precisely the transitions that carry the diagnostic content of impact and friction events.

Bayesian inverse-problem formulations remain the natural language for parameter uncertainty when data are sparse, noisy, and heterogeneous 33–36. They provide posterior uncertainty rather than a point estimate and make the information content of measurements explicit. That is valuable in non-smooth mechanics because event windows can be highly informative about restitution, damping, contact stiffness, and friction, yet only if the forward model maps those parameters through the correct jump geometry. A smooth likelihood built on an event-inconsistent model can concentrate very confidently around the wrong parameter set. The problem is therefore not merely statistical efficiency; it is physically consistent posterior transport.

Model reduction for real-time non-linear dynamics

Projection-based reduced-order modelling provides the main route by which high-dimensional dynamics can enter a real-time twin. Proper orthogonal decomposition, balanced POD, reduced-basis methods, discrete empirical interpolation, and operator inference all seek to preserve dominant flow content while reducing online cost 37–45. Incremental singular-value and sequential Karhunen–Loeve updates further enable streaming adaptation when operating conditions drift. These tools are now mature for smooth nonlinear systems.

The unresolved issue is that event-rich systems are not dominated by smooth flow energy alone. A basis that captures 99% of the variance between impacts can still miss the jump direction that determines the next rebound, the next slip transition, or the next control action. Classical POD and hyper-reduction do not distinguish between slowly accumulated smooth energy and physically decisive but temporally localized event residuals. Operator inference is powerful when intrusive access to the full model is unavailable, but it can inherit the same difficulty when the reduced coordinates are learned from trajectories that average across guards. The present iPOD-M construction is positioned precisely against that gap: it does not reject standard POD machinery, but it adds an event-aware enrichment mechanism that acts on the term that Theorem 2 later identifies as jump mismatch.

Digital-twin taxonomies and hybrid control perspectives

Digital twin scholarship has clarified the cyber-physical role of synchronization, data exchange, lifecycle integration, and decision support, and broad taxonomies now exist across manufacturing, construction, and industrial systems 46–49. These surveys are valuable because they distinguish a twin from a static simulation and emphasize updating, interoperability, and operation over the asset lifecycle. Hybrid feedback-control theory extends this picture by showing how stability and robustness can be analyzed when continuous dynamics and discrete transitions coexist 50.

From the standpoint of non-smooth mechanics, however, most digital twin formulations remain architecturally richer than mechanically explicit. They frequently assume that the plant model presented to the estimator or surrogate is already available in a form suitable for online use. The scientific question addressed here is earlier in the chain: what should that model be when the asset is governed by unilateral contact, friction, impulses, and resets, and how should its uncertainty be transported at the very moments when its equations change?

Computational foundations and recent digital-twin implementations

Classical vibro-impact and nonsmooth-dynamics monographs show why this question matters in practice. Impact oscillators, grazing bifurcations, chatter, and friction-induced instabilities have long been known to produce rich dynamic responses and strong sensitivity to event timing 51,52. Reliable numerical integration, matrix-function conditioning, uncertainty quantification, signal processing, system identification, and modern learning theory also provide indispensable computational ingredients for any operational twin 53–60. Yet these foundations do not, by themselves, specify how a twin should couple guard detection, reset mechanics, covariance transport, and reduced-order adaptation in one online loop.

Recent digital-twin studies illustrate both progress and the remaining gap. Decentralized digital twins, neural-operator surrogates for real-time inference, and agent-based self-models for intelligent structures demonstrate that dynamic updating and scalable software architectures are now practical 61–63. What they do not resolve is the mechanics of discontinuity. Their core challenge is not the absence of real-time software, but the absence of a physically explicit event calculus tied to contact, impulse, and switching.

Practical domains with impacts, friction, and shock

Application-specific literature makes the need for an event-aware architecture even clearer. Friction-induced vibration and noise remain difficult because friction couples tribology, structural dynamics, and acoustic response through intermittent contact and separation, with brake dynamics offering a particularly challenging example 64–66. In robotics, impact-invariant control and contact-based manipulation studies show that control authority changes qualitatively at impact or sustained contact, so estimators that ignore the transition geometry lose precisely the information needed for safe touchdown and force regulation 67,68. Structural impact monitoring and vibration-based health-monitoring studies similarly show that shock events are not nuisances to be filtered out; they are often the most informative signatures of condition and damage 69,70.

Recent vibro-impact robotic studies reinforce the same message from another direction: real devices operating in confined or contact-rich environments owe their functionality to repeated impacts, separations, and frictional state changes 71–73. The Salted Kalman Filter provides a particularly relevant bridge, because it uses saltation-based covariance transport to extend Kalman filtering to hybrid systems 74. In locomotion and contact-rich robotics, saltation matrices are now used directly for gait optimization and robust control synthesis 75. These studies show that hybrid covariance transport is feasible and useful. The contribution of the present paper is different and broader: it embeds that transport inside a full mechanical framework with measure-valued impulse balance, reduced-order projection, and event-triggered control logic, and it ties the resulting mathematics to benchmark non-smooth mechanical systems rather than to estimation or control in isolation.

The relevant ingredients therefore already exist in partial form, but not in a structurally unified form. Hybrid mechanics resolves guards, resets, and impulsive balance; digital twins provide synchronization and prediction; saltation matrices describe local transport through discontinuities; Bayesian estimation updates uncertain states and parameters; and reduced-order models accelerate simulation. The missing element is not any one of these methods in isolation, but a single event-consistent architecture in which they are all governed by the same transport geometry. In most existing approaches, discontinuities are handled within mechanics but not inference, within estimation but not reduction, or within control but not uncertainty transport. The present manuscript differs by treating event surfaces as the common interfaces through which state, covariance, parameter uncertainty, reduced coordinates, and update logic must all pass. Operationally, that architecture yields saltation-consistent covariance transport with explicit jump uncertainty, a jump-mismatch error channel for event-aware reduction, and a practical stability condition for event-triggered updating and control.

The event-aware architecture is anchored in a full-order mechanical model rather than in a reduced state from the outset. This point matters because an event-aware digital twin must be anchored in an explicit physics model rather than in an event-blind surrogate if post-event predictions are to remain physically interpretable.

Unilateral contact, friction, and hybrid mode logic

Normal contact is represented by a gap function g_n(q). The admissible state requires non-penetration, non-negative normal reaction, and complementarity between the two. Tangential contact is governed by the relative slip velocity v_t and a Coulomb-type friction law written as a differential inclusion. In compact form the active contact constraints are given by Eqs. (4) and (5).

The differential-inclusion form in Eq. (5) is preferable to a purely algebraic sign law because it covers sticking, positive slip, negative slip, and set-valued transition states in one expression. The discrete mode i in the hybrid model is therefore not an artificial software label. It has direct mechanical meaning: free flight, contact-stick, contact-slip-plus, contact-slip-minus, or a shock-dominated transient branch.

Introducing the state z = [q^T v^T]^T with v = q˙, the continuous dynamics in mode i are written as Eq. (6). The guard set G_ij in Eq. (7) collects the states at which the active domain changes. Typical examples are contact onset g_n(q) = 0 with approaching normal velocity, contact release λ_n = 0 with separating acceleration, stick-to-slip transitions when the tangential traction reaches the friction bound, and impact events when the pre-impact normal velocity is negative and a restitution law is imposed.

These guards are not merely numerical event detectors. They are codimension-one surfaces on which the governing equations change. A smooth twin that does not represent them explicitly can still interpolate the state, but it cannot transport the correct mechanics, uncertainties, or sensitivities through the transition.

Reset maps, impulse balance, and measure-valued representation

When a trajectory reaches a guard, the post-event state is not obtained by continuing the same ODE. It is produced by a reset map that depends on the active transition and on uncertain event parameters η_k such as restitution or impact friction. The abstract reset law is given by Eq. (8). For impacts, the reset is constrained by the generalized impulse-momentum balance in Eq. (9), together with the normal restitution relation and tangential impulse admissibility in Eq. (10).

Equation (9) is the key bridge between contact mechanics and hybrid inference. It shows that the state jump is not arbitrary software logic: it is the integrated consequence of an impulsive force acting over a negligible time interval. This is precisely why state estimators and ROMs that ignore the impulse law often remain accurate before impact and then fail immediately afterward.

To expose the singular time-scale separation explicitly, the impulsive contribution is written as a measure in Eq. (11), leading to the measure differential form in Eq. (12). Equation (13) then reconstructs the displacement from the velocity measure. This representation avoids the common but misleading practice of treating impact as a very stiff continuous force. The measure form states unambiguously that momentum jumps while position remains absolutely continuous.

The full-order model is therefore hybrid in three simultaneous senses: the vector field changes with contact mode, impulsive measures appear at isolated event times, and the admissible domain is constrained by complementarity and friction. Reduced-order approximation, Bayesian updating, and feedback synthesis are developed later only after this full-order structure has been fixed.

Hybrid sensitivity transport and formal mathematical results

Assumptions and existence of hybrid trajectories

The formal analysis uses a reachable set Z ⊂ R^(2N) on which each mode-dependent vector field f_i is locally Lipschitz, each guard function g_ij is continuously differentiable, each reset map R_ij is continuously differentiable in the state and continuously differentiable in the uncertain jump parameter η, and successive events are isolated by a positive dwell time. Guard transversality is imposed by ∇g_ij(z)^T f_i(z) ≠ 0 at each active crossing. These assumptions exclude grazing tangencies from the theorem statements, not because grazing is unimportant, but because it is precisely the singular limit at which first-order jump geometry becomes ill-conditioned and requires separate treatment.

Under these assumptions, a standard concatenation argument yields a unique piecewise-smooth hybrid trajectory on any finite horizon that does not contain Zeno accumulation. The mechanics interpretation is straightforward: the model is well posed as long as impacts, stick–slip switches, and release events occur as isolated transitions rather than as an infinitely fast cluster. In applications where chattering is physically relevant, one must augment the present framework with hysteresis, compliance regularization, or a measure-differential inclusion that resolves the clustered limit.

Proposition 1 (existence and mode consistency).

Assume the regularity, transversality, and non-Zeno conditions stated above. Then for every admissible initial condition z(0) ∈ D_i there exists a unique hybrid trajectory composed of absolutely continuous flow segments satisfying Eq. (6) and isolated reset events satisfying Eq. (8). Moreover, each transition preserves mode consistency in the sense that z(t_k^ +) belongs to the closure of the target domain D_j.

The proposition is not decorative. It guarantees that the later covariance, Bayesian, and reduced-order constructions are attached to a well-defined hybrid trajectory rather than to an ad hoc sequence of numerically detected events.

Saltation-consistent sensitivity propagation

The jump in first-order sensitivity arises from two sources: the reset map itself and the perturbation in event time. Let τ_k denote the k-th event time. A pre-event perturbation δz_k^- changes the time of guard intersection by the amount shown in Eq. (14). Substituting that timing shift into the reset linearization yields the saltation operator in Eq. (15), and the resulting perturbation update in Eq. (16).

The denominator in Eq. (15) is the transversality term. Its appearance is physically significant: when the flow crosses the guard almost tangentially, a very small perturbation in state can produce a large perturbation in event time, which is why grazing transitions are so difficult both numerically and statistically. Conversely, when the crossing is strongly transversal, the event time is locally well conditioned and first-order jump transport is reliable.

Figure 4 explains why naive linearization fails. If one differentiates only the reset map R, the tangent perturbation misses the contribution of the shifted event time. The saltation matrix restores that missing term and therefore changes both gradient-based learning and covariance transport. The distinction becomes visible whenever two nearby trajectories hit the same guard at slightly different times.

Covariance transport across resets

Continuous covariance transport inside each mode follows the usual Lyapunov equation in Eq. (17), with A_i = ∂f_i/∂z evaluated along the nominal hybrid trajectory. At an event, however, the covariance is not propagated by the same equation. It must be mapped by the saltation operator and augmented by the uncertainty entering through the jump parameters, as shown in Eq. (18).

That formula is the first of the three main theorem-level contributions of the paper. The importance of the theorem is not only that it provides a compact covariance update. It proves that the event-time channel and the reset-parameter channel can be combined in one consistent first-order law, so the twin does not lose uncertainty mass or falsely contract the posterior exactly at impacts.

Theorem 1 (event-consistent covariance transport).

Consider a transversal event z_k^- ∈ G_ij for which the assumptions of Proposition 1 hold, and assume that the jump uncertainty δη_k has zero mean, covariance Σ_(η,k), and is independent of the pre-event state perturbation. Let the pre-event perturbation satisfy δz_k^- ~ (0, P_k^-), and let the first-order reset law be given by Eq. (16). Then the post-event covariance is given, to first order, by Eq. (18). In addition, P_k^ + is positive semidefinite whenever P_k^- and Σ_(η,k) are positive semidefinite, and it satisfies tr(P_k^ +) ≤||S_k||^2 tr(P_k^-) +||Γ_k||^2 tr(Σ_(η,k)).

Proof sketch

The guard perturbation produces the event-time shift in Eq. (14). Substituting this shift into the linearized reset gives Eq. (16), which is an affine map of the form δz_k^ + = A δz_k^- + B δη_k. Independence of δz_k^- and δη_k eliminates the cross term in the covariance, so P_k^ + = A P_k^- A^T + B Σ_(η,k) B^T. Setting A = S_k and B = Γ_k yields Eq. (18). Positive semidefiniteness follows from congruence and addition of positive semidefinite matrices, and the trace bound follows from the submultiplicative norm inequality.

Physical interpretation. The theorem states that post-impact uncertainty is not only the pre-impact covariance viewed through the reset. It is the pre-impact covariance transformed by the full event geometry, plus whatever uncertainty enters through the jump law itself. In practice this prevents the common but misleading behaviour in which an estimator appears to become ‘more certain’ immediately after an impact simply because the jump was processed as a deterministic algebraic overwrite.

In Fig. 5 the post-event ellipse is not centred on the same state and is not simply a uniformly scaled copy of the pre-event ellipse. Its orientation changes because sensitivity to the guard normal and to the reset map enters anisotropically. This matters operationally because monitoring decisions often depend on directional uncertainty, for example whether the state is likely to re-impact, re-stick, or leave the admissible domain.

Information localization near events

The objective functional in Eq. (25) and the information matrix in Eq. (26) are used in two ways. First, they define offline and online parameter updates. Second, they expose why event windows are disproportionately informative: when the output sensitivity H_θ,k changes sharply at a reset, the information increment can rise abruptly, which means that short windows around impact or slip onset can contribute more to parameter identifiability than much longer smooth segments.

Remark 1 (information localization near events).

If H_(θ,k) varies smoothly in one mode but changes sharply across a reset, then the dominant contribution to I_N(θ) can be concentrated in short event windows. This observation explains why an event-aware twin can identify restitution, contact stiffness, or friction parameters using less data than a smooth twin that distributes its modelling error across the entire trajectory.

Event-aware correction as an inferential transport principle

The role of forced event correction can now be stated more sharply. In a smooth estimation problem, uncertainty evolves continuously and is reweighted only when measurements arrive. In the present non-smooth setting, that logic is incomplete because the mechanics itself creates endogenous transport interfaces. A reset is not only a discontinuity in the state trajectory; it is the point at which the local tangent map changes, the covariance propagation law is redefined, and the information carried by pre-event uncertainty is redistributed into different post-event directions. Treating such events as local numerical complications leaves the inference layer mechanically inconsistent with the dynamics it is meant to estimate.

Accordingly, the event surface is treated as an inferential transport interface. In the proposed architecture, guards and resets determine not only when the state changes mode, but also when covariance must be remapped, when parameter sensitivities must be reinterpreted, and when prediction must be forcibly corrected even in the absence of a new sensor sample. This transport-interface viewpoint is operationalised in this manuscript through the saltation-consistent covariance transport across resets (Theorem 1), the jump-mismatch error decomposition that underpins event-aware reduction (Theorem 2), and the practical stability bound for event-triggered updating and control under bounded uncertainty (Theorem 3).

This viewpoint clarifies why short event windows are often disproportionately informative. During smooth flow, nearby trajectories may remain dynamically similar for long intervals, so uncertainty contraction is gradual and parameter discrimination may remain weak. By contrast, a reset can project trajectories that were nearly indistinguishable before the event into materially different post-event states, sensitivities, and future mode sequences. Information is therefore concentrated not simply in time, but in the change of transport geometry induced by the event. This is why restitution, contact stiffness, and friction-related parameters can become sharply identifiable from short windows around impact or slip onset, even when much longer smooth segments carry comparatively little new information.

The same principle also explains the coherence of the broader framework. The covariance law in Section "Hybrid sensitivity transport and formal mathematical results", the Bayesian recursion in Section "Bayesian state and parameter updating with forced event correction", the reduced-order error structure in Section "Event-aware reduced-order approximation and iPOD-M", and the event-triggered update logic in Section "Event-triggered updating and control" are not independent modules connected for convenience. They are consequences of a common event-aware transport structure. In that sense, the conceptual advance of the manuscript is to recast inference in non-smooth systems from a measurement-only correction problem into a mechanics-constrained transport problem in which the event set determines how prediction, uncertainty, and parameter information must evolve.

Event-aware reduced-order approximation and iPOD-M

The reduced model is derived from the full-order mechanics, not introduced independently. Let V_r ∈ R^(N × r) be an orthonormal basis built from high-fidelity snapshots and, crucially, from event residual directions that are added online when the reduced model under-resolves a jump. The kinematic projection is written in Eq. (27), and Galerkin projection of Eq. (3) yields the reduced dynamics in Eq. (28).

Equation (29) is the decisive difference between a standard smooth ROM and the present construction. The reduced impulse balance contains the projected contact impulses and an explicit residual term ρ_r. If ρ_r is ignored, then even a ROM that is accurate during smooth flow can generate large errors immediately after impact because the reduced velocity jump is mechanically inconsistent with the full-order impulse.

iPOD-M updates the basis through the covariance-like accumulator in Eq. (30) and the event-enrichment rule in Eq. (31). The new term β r_evt,k r_evt,k^T is not a cosmetic modification of incremental POD. It is the mechanism by which the basis is forced to remember jump directions even when they have low contribution to the smooth-flow snapshot variance. The contribution is therefore not POD plus updates, but a reduced-order law tailored to the hybrid error decomposition proved later in Theorem 2.

The error state is defined in Eq. (32), and Eq. (33) states the global reduced-order error bound over a finite event horizon. The bound separates three effects: initial mismatch, unresolved smooth flow content, and cumulative jump mismatch. That separation matters physically because it explains why standard ROM diagnostics can be misleading. A basis may have excellent global energy capture and yet still fail at the specific quantity that dominates operational decision making: the jump.

Theorem 2 (event-aware reduced-order approximation error bound).

Assume that the full-order flow map is Lipschitz on the reachable set with constant L_f, each reset map is Lipschitz with constant L_R, the reduced basis V_r is orthonormal, and the number of isolated events on [0,T] is finite. Let Δ_j denote the jump mismatch between the full-order reset and the lifted reduced reset at event j. Then there exists a constant C_T depending on L_f, L_R, and the event schedule such that Eq. (33) holds for all t ∈ [0,T].

Proof sketch

Between events, standard Galerkin error analysis and Gronwall’s inequality bound the flow contribution by the projection residual. At each event, the reset induces the inequality ||e^ +||≤ L_R ||e^-|| +||Δ_j||. Concatenating the flow estimates over the event partition and iterating the jump inequality yields Eq. (33). The important point is structural rather than technical: the event term enters additively and cannot be absorbed into the smooth projection residual.

Physical interpretation. Theorem 2 proves that jump mismatch is a distinct error mechanism. This is what turns iPOD-M from a convenience update into a mechanics-based reduced-order method. By enriching the basis with r_evt,k, the method specifically attacks the term Σ ||Δ_j||, which standard POD does not target.

The diagram in Fig. 7 separates two sources of basis content: slowly accumulated smooth dynamics and event-localized residuals. This separation is conceptually important because it mirrors the mathematical separation in Theorem 2. The reduced model is strengthened not by adding arbitrary basis vectors, but by adding the vectors associated with the part of the error bound that is uniquely hybrid.

Event-triggered updating and control

The control layer is formulated so that updates occur either when the held control law becomes inaccurate or when the mechanics enter a new mode. Let the mode-dependent feedback law be Eq. (34). The next update time is determined by Eq. (35), which combines a classical threshold condition with a forced update at any guard crossing. This hybrid trigger is essential in non-smooth systems because a contact event may change control authority even when the hold error remains small immediately before the event.

The Lyapunov analysis is expressed by the flow and jump inequalities in Eqs. (36) and (37). The flow inequality allows measurement noise w and control hold error e_u, while the jump inequality allows reset uncertainty η and bounded actuation or modelling disturbance d. If the threshold coefficient σ is chosen so that the additional trigger error does not overturn the nominal dissipation, then the hybrid state is practically input-to-state stable in the sense of Eq. (38).

Theorem 3 (practical stability under event-triggered updating).

Assume that for each mode i there exists a continuously differentiable Lyapunov function V_i satisfying Eqs. (36) and (37), and that the trigger parameter σ obeys ν_i σ^2 < α_i for every active mode. Assume also bounded disturbance, measurement noise, actuation delay, and jump uncertainty. Then every maximal hybrid solution is practically input-to-state stable and satisfies the bound in Eq. (38).

Proof sketch

Substituting the trigger bound ||e_u||≤ σ ||ẑ||+ ε into Eq. (36) yields a strictly negative coefficient on ||z||^2 whenever ν_i σ^2 < α_i. The jump inequality preserves net decay up to bounded disturbance terms. Hybrid comparison then gives the exponential-plus-bounded remainder estimate of Eq. (38). The forcing of updates at guard crossings is not an implementation detail but part of the proof, because it prevents an outdated controller from being carried into a new mode with different actuation geometry.

Physical interpretation. The theorem does not claim asymptotic stability in the presence of all uncertainties; that would be unjustified for the present benchmark setting. It proves a practically stronger and operationally more relevant result: bounded sensing, bounded delay, and bounded reset uncertainty lead to bounded residual error, provided the controller is refreshed both when the hold error grows and when the plant changes mode.

It can be the instant at which the plant enters a new contact mode and the old law becomes structurally inappropriate. Numerical methodology and benchmark design

The benchmark evidence used in this study is limited to the archived simulation- and FE-based material available for the three reference systems. No laboratory dataset is introduced here. The main paper therefore focuses on mechanics, derivations, benchmark interpretation, and engineering implications, while the matched supplementary package records which figures are fully reproducible from archived numerical files, which are schematic, and which remain only partially reproducible because source arrays were not preserved.

The computational workflow used by the event-aware twin is nevertheless fully specified. Each mode is integrated on its own admissible domain. Guard functions are monitored continuously during the flow solve, and any sign change is localized by root finding. The state is advanced to the event, the impulse law is solved, the reset map is applied, the saltation matrix is assembled, covariance is corrected according to Theorem 1, and the estimator, controller, and ROM are all notified of the mode change before integration resumes. In streaming operation, iPOD-M evaluates the event residual immediately after the reset and augments the basis only if the threshold in Eq. (31) is exceeded.

The benchmark suite consists of three canonical cases that stress different aspects of the theory: an impact oscillator emphasizing guard timing and rebound consistency, a stick–slip slider emphasizing mode logic and frictional regime transitions, and a shock-response system emphasizing abrupt excitation, transient attenuation, and robustness under delay or noise. Where the original submission reported FE or high-resolution numerical solutions, those responses are treated as the high-fidelity reference against which the reduced-order and event-aware results are interpreted.

Tables 2, 3 and 4 are not inserted as decorative summaries. They define the quantitative envelope within which every later claim is made. The paper therefore avoids reporting performance gains beyond the archived benchmark evidence.

Benchmark results and figure-by-figure interpretation

Impact oscillator: event geometry and phase-space consistency

The impact oscillator is the cleanest test of event timing because the state follows a smooth branch until it reaches the stop and then undergoes a mechanically sharp rebound. Figure 10 shows the reconstructed phase portrait. The important comparison is not only whether the curve is smooth, but whether the trajectory reaches the correct turning point near the unilateral stop and leaves it with the correct post-impact direction. Event-unaware surrogates typically smear this corner and misplace the return branch; the benchmark phase portrait shows that the event-aware formulation preserves the turning geometry.

Impact-oscillator phase portrait. The trajectory approaches the unilateral stop, undergoes a sharp rebound, and returns along a distinct branch. The scientific lesson is that correct event timing is visible geometrically: a small timing bias would move the corner and distort the post-impact branch.

The dashed vertical line in Fig. 10 marks the stop location. The trajectory remains smooth right up to the impact, then changes branch almost instantaneously. This is exactly the setting in which the saltation term matters. A model that predicts the stop a little too early or too late changes both the rebound state and every subsequent phase point. Figure 10 therefore supports the central thesis of the paper: event handling is not a local detail with local consequences; it changes the entire future trajectory.

High-fidelity versus reduced-order response

The next comparison asks whether the event-aware reduced model remains faithful to the high-fidelity reference after the reset. Figure 11 overlays the full-order FE response and the iPOD-M response over the reported 0–0.6 s window. The two curves are visually close throughout the loading and decay phases, including the transition through the plateau and the post-plateau descent.

Displacement time history for the full-order reference and the event-aware iPOD-M reduced model. Agreement through the rise, dwell, and decay portions of the response indicates that the reduced basis is carrying both smooth flow content and the event-sensitive components needed to remain phase aligned.

The significance of Fig. 11 is not simply that the ROM tracks amplitude. It tracks shape across segments with different local dynamics. In a contact-rich problem phase alignment is often more important than pointwise amplitude agreement, because a phase drift shifts later guard crossings. The close overlay is therefore indirect evidence that jump inconsistency has been suppressed.

Figure 12 makes that conclusion sharper by plotting the absolute displacement error. The error remains localized and bounded, with magnitude on the order of 10^-3 to 10^-2 in the plotted units, instead of growing monotonically after the main transition. This behaviour is consistent with Theorem 2: the jump mismatch term has not disappeared, but it has been kept small enough that the reduced model does not lose synchronization with the full-order trajectory.

Absolute displacement error between the FE reference and the event-aware reduced model. The error oscillates but does not exhibit uncontrolled post-event growth, indicating that the event residual content added by iPOD-M is acting on the jump-mismatch term rather than merely fitting the smooth trajectory.

If the basis were event blind, one would expect the largest error growth immediately after the reset, not later during smooth decay. The oscillatory but bounded profile in Fig. 12 therefore has a clear mechanical interpretation: residual error remains, but it is no longer dominated by unmodelled jump directions.

Beyond state tracking, the results support two broader claims: the twin learns event-governing quantities, and the resulting control/update logic is useful away from a single nominal operating point. Figure 6 already showed the posterior concentration. Figure 13 and Table 3 extend the analysis across forcing frequency. Peak displacement is reduced by 16.9%, 20.8%, 21.7%, and 19.4% at 12, 15, 18, and 20 Hz, respectively, relative to the passive baseline.

The scientific lesson of Fig. 13 is that the benefit is not confined to a single tuned condition. The relative gain persists over the full reported sweep, and the largest reduction occurs near 18 Hz rather than monotonically at the sweep boundary. The reported curve therefore indicates that the advantage is not restricted to a single operating point.

Figure 14 addresses the stick–slip benchmark directly. The timeline shows a long stick interval followed by a shorter slip episode. The importance of this figure lies in the duration asymmetry. A model trained only on time-averaged statistics would overrepresent the dominant stick segment and underresolve the shorter slip window even though the slip window governs frictional energy release and, often, noise generation.

The short slip segment in Fig. 14 is also where parameter inference is hardest and where event-aware updating is most valuable. Friction transitions are brief but information rich. Missing them by even a small amount changes the inferred friction state, the posterior on tangential dynamics, and the predicted timing of the next cycle.

Finally, Fig. 15 summarizes robustness to perturbations. Across nominal, noisy, delayed, and lagged scenarios, peak-response reduction remains between 15.5% and 18.9%, while RMS reduction remains between 11.4% and 15.2%. These are not dramatic claims of universal superiority; they are more persuasive because they are moderate, consistent, and retained under non-ideal operating conditions.

The stability result of Theorem 3 does not promise zero degradation under perturbation, and Fig. 15 confirms the expected behaviour: lagged or noisy conditions reduce performance, but do not destroy it. That is the correct operational standard for a benchmark paper of this kind. What matters is not perfection under perturbation but retention of physically meaningful benefit once the ideal assumptions are relaxed.

Discussion

The central scientific message of the manuscript is that discontinuities in non-smooth systems are not merely local sources of stiffness or numerical inconvenience. They are the interfaces through which prediction, uncertainty, identifiability, and control authority are reorganized. Before an event, nearby trajectories may remain almost indistinguishable; after the event, small differences in guard timing or pre-event state can generate materially different post-event evolution. A smooth twin treats that difficulty as an interpolation problem and therefore spreads modelling effort across the entire trajectory. The present framework treats it as a transport problem localized at mechanically determined events. That shift in viewpoint is the main conceptual contribution of the paper.

The saltation treatment therefore changes more than a Jacobian. Relative to smooth propagation, it restores the missing event-time channel through which perturbations, covariance, and inference are actually transmitted. Without that channel, gradients used for parameter learning are biased, covariance is often under-transported through impacts, and controllers derived from local linearization behave as if the plant retained the same differential structure before and after the event. The significance of Theorem 1 and the corresponding benchmark results is precisely that they make this mechanism explicit and computable rather than heuristic.

The same logic explains why iPOD-M differs from standard projection-based reduction. Classical reduced-order methods are usually driven by smooth-flow energy capture. The present method is organized instead by the hybrid error budget, in which jump mismatch is a distinct and physically consequential term. Event enrichment is therefore not an auxiliary update rule added for convenience; it is the reduced-order expression of the same event-aware transport principle that governs inference. This is why the method is especially relevant for systems in which impacts or friction transitions are brief in time but decisive in consequence.

The manuscript also clarifies the relation to nearby literatures. Pure contact-dynamics solvers preserve mechanics but do not by themselves define online Bayesian updating, covariance transport, or reduced-order adaptation. Digital-twin frameworks provide synchronization and prediction, but often remain smooth-biased at the event times that matter most. Saltation-based filtering and hybrid covariance transport are the closest inferential relatives, yet they do not by themselves provide the full mechanics-to-reduction-to-control pathway developed here. The contribution of the present paper is to make those layers structurally consistent by giving them the same event geometry.

Several limitations remain and should be interpreted as boundaries of validity rather than weaknesses of principle. Grazing events weaken the transport denominator and can make both sensitivity and covariance transport ill conditioned. Simultaneous or multi-contact transitions may require richer complementarity or measure-differential formulations than the single-guard exposition used here. Strongly non-Gaussian post-event uncertainty may exceed the descriptive power of the local Gaussian update. Repeated chattering or Zeno-like clusters may invalidate the isolated-event assumptions used in the theorem statements. These limitations do not alter the central contribution; they identify the regimes in which the event-aware architecture must be extended rather than simply applied.

The broader implication is that event-aware digital twins should not be designed by adding a hybrid correction layer onto an otherwise smooth pipeline. They should be designed from the outset around the transport role of guards and resets. Once that principle is made explicit, mechanics, uncertainty, reduction, and control no longer appear as separately managed tasks. They become parts of one inferential architecture for discontinuity-governed systems.

Practical engineering translation

The purpose of the event-aware twin is not exhausted by benchmark accuracy. Its practical value lies in the class of decisions it enables when discontinuities dominate operating risk. The most direct example is impact protection and vibro-impact mitigation. In impact dampers, stopper-limited devices, or repeated collision absorbers, the twin can infer changing restitution or effective contact stiffness from short post-impact windows, detect when rebound severity begins to drift, and update mitigation logic before the next impact sequence. The industrial relevance of vibro-impact modelling and energy-dissipation devices is well documented in the broader vibro-impact literature 51,71,73.

In friction-induced vibration and brake dynamics, the decision problem is different but equally event centric. Here the useful twin is one that can detect regime occupancy, distinguish long stick intervals from short slip releases, and identify whether a friction-induced instability is being driven by parameter drift, changing contact condition, or delayed control action. The multiscale review and recent brake-dynamics studies cited in Section "Literature review and scientific positioning" show that stick–slip and separation are central rather than peripheral features of the phenomenon 64–66. An event-aware twin can therefore support decisions about damping augmentation, friction-material selection, actuation timing, or maintenance scheduling on the basis of the inferred regime structure rather than on RMS vibration alone.

Robotic contact systems provide a third translation pathway. In legged locomotion, manipulation, and docking, impact changes control authority instantaneously. An event-aware twin can supply touchdown-state prediction, contact-conditioned covariance bounds, and safe update logic at the moment the robot acquires or loses a constraint. Recent studies on impact-invariant control, contact-based robotic systems, and contact-dynamics control make clear that the operational question is not simply trajectory tracking but how to preserve authority and estimation quality across contact transitions 67,68,72,75.

Structural health monitoring under shock or impact benefits from the same architecture for a different reason. Impact events often contain the most informative features for damage localization or condition change, but they are also the episodes at which sensors saturate and simple smooth models fail. A twin that knows when the shock occurred, how the covariance should jump, and which response features should be trusted immediately afterward can make better decisions about alarm thresholds, data windowing, and sensor placement. The impact-monitoring and vibration-based model-updating literature provides strong motivation for this use case 69,70.

Machinery with intermittent contact, backlash, or collision-prone clearances presents a related operational need. The useful question is often not 'what is the average vibration level?' but 'when did contact loss begin to recur, how certain is that inference, and what control or maintenance action should be triggered before wear accelerates?' Because the present architecture binds inference to event physics, it is suited to that kind of decision support. The same applies to constrained or deployable aerospace structures, where release, latching, or transient contact can produce short but decisive events. In those systems an event-aware twin can inform release sequencing, post-event health assessment, and anomaly triage by telling the operator whether the observed transient is consistent with the intended reset physics or with an off-nominal contact state 20,22,63.

Limitations and future work

The main limitation is straightforward: the evidence base in the present study is simulation and FE validation rather than laboratory or field validation. The paper therefore does not claim experimental proof. What it does claim is a combination of mechanics, derivation, and benchmark evidence that justifies the framework as a serious research contribution even before hardware testing is added.

A second limitation concerns model structure. The contact and friction laws used here are intentionally generic so that the theory remains broadly applicable, but real systems may require velocity-dependent restitution, compliance, friction memory, wear evolution, thermal coupling, or multiple simultaneous contacts. Extending the present results to those settings is feasible, but the additional constitutive variables and event interactions would enlarge both the state and the uncertainty map.

A third limitation lies in the local-Gaussian treatment of uncertainty. The covariance transport of Theorem 1 is correct to first order under the stated assumptions, but strongly non-Gaussian post-impact distributions, multi-modal contact states, or severe event-time ambiguity may require particle, mixture, or measure-theoretic uncertainty representations. Likewise, the present practical-stability theorem does not claim global asymptotic stability under arbitrary contact uncertainty; it provides a bounded-error guarantee appropriate to the benchmark setting.

Future work should therefore proceed along three fronts: experimental validation on instrumented impact and friction platforms; extension to multi-contact and distributed-parameter systems with richer constitutive laws; and integration with non-Gaussian inference schemes that preserve the event-aware transport logic developed here. The contribution of the current paper is that these future directions now have a mathematically explicit foundation rather than a purely conceptual one.

Conclusion

This paper introduces an event-aware mechanics and inference architecture for non-smooth dynamical systems. Its primary contribution is to show that digital-twin prediction, uncertainty transport, parameter learning, reduced-order approximation, and real-time updating become mutually consistent only when event surfaces are treated as first-class transport interfaces through which state, covariance, parameter uncertainty, reduced coordinates, and update logic must all pass.

On that basis, the manuscript formulates a full-order hybrid mechanical model with explicit domains, guards, reset maps, unilateral constraints, friction laws, and measure-valued impulses; derives saltation-consistent sensitivity and covariance transport across resets; establishes event-aware Bayesian correction in which mechanically determined transitions act as forced inferential updates; develops an event-aware reduced-order approximation whose error structure isolates jump mismatch as a distinct approximation mechanism; and provides practical stability conditions for event-triggered updating and control under bounded noise, delay, and reset uncertainty.

The benchmark studies support this architecture by showing that event-aware transport improves post-event state prediction, concentrates parameter information in short event windows, and preserves reduced-order fidelity in discontinuity-dominated transients. These outcomes are important not only as numerical results but because they all express the same structural point: in non-smooth dynamics, the decisive information is carried by transport through the event, not by smooth propagation around it.

The broader significance is therefore conceptual as much as computational. By recasting discontinuities as inferential interfaces rather than as numerical exceptions, the paper provides a new foundation for mechanics-consistent digital twins of systems governed by impact, friction, contact loss, and shock. In that sense, the manuscript advances the treatment of non-smooth systems from a linked set of methods to a coherent event-aware framework for mechanics, uncertainty, reduction, and real-time control.
