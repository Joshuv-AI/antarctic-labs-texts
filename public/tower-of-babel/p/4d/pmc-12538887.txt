HIV vaulnerability among people who inject drugs (PWID): findings from the Bangladesh integrated biological and behavioural surveillance (IBBS) study 2020
Authors: Jessica Srivastava, Md Nazmul Karim, Fariha Haseen, Md Hasan, Md Golam Rabbani, Farhana sultana, Md Shakawat Alam, Sibgha Fatima, Saif Ullah Munshi, The National AIDS/STD Programme (NASP)
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPeople who inject drugs (PWID) contribute significantly to the global HIV burden. Various individual and contextual factors exacerbate the risk of HIV among PWID, this problem is particularly acute in low- and middle-income countries, where resource constraints impede effective prevention and treatment efforts. Although Bangladesh is classified as a low HIV prevalence country, and despite national efforts, including surveillance through the Integrated Biobehavioural Survey (IBBS), gaps in evidence and actionable insights persist. This study aims to analyse the latest IBBS 2020 data to identify risk factors associated with HIV vulnerability among PWID in Bangladesh, informing culturally relevant and targeted harm reduction strategies to mitigate these risks.MethodThis study analysed data from IBBS, which used a two-stage cluster sampling and Time Location Sampling methods to recruit participants. Data were collected across four domains: (1) Sociodemographic characteristics, (2) Drug and Injection-Related Behaviours, (3) Sexual Behaviours, and (4) Co-infections, with blood samples collected for HIV, hepatitis-C, and syphilis serological testing. Sample weights were applied to adjust for the complex survey design. Descriptive statistics summarized participant characteristics and risk behaviours. Two binary logistic regression models were used to identify HIV risk factors: The Fully Adjusted Model, which included all plausible confounders and used the Wald backward elimination method to determine significant predictors, and the Partially Adjusted Model, which controlled for age, gender, and education to explore intermediate factors through adjusting confounding or mediation.ResultOverall, the prevalence of HIV among PWID was 4.1%. Social exclusion (AOR: 1.71, 95% CI 1.1, 2.7). Hepatitis C infection (AOR: 2.57, 95% CI 1.6, 4.0), drug use of more than 10 years (AOR: 3.74, 95% CI 1.3, 10.8), injecting once or more daily (AOR: 5.23, 95% CI 2.6, 10.7), having multiple injecting partners (AOR: 3.11, 95% CI 1.8, 5.3) sharing injecting accessories (AOR: 2.55, 95% CI 1.5, 4.4) and engaging with a commercial sex partner (AOR: 1.80, 95% CI 1.1, 2.9) emerged as significant predictors of HIV risk among PWID patients.ConclusionThis study reveals the heightened HIV risk among PWID in Bangladesh, driven by intertwined social, behavioural, biological, and structural factors. It underscores the urgent need for tailored, holistic interventions combining harm reduction, structural reforms, and biomedical strategies to address vulnerabilities, reduce high-risk behaviours, and improve healthcare access for this marginalized group.Keywords: HIV, IBBS, PWID, Bangladesh, High risk behaviour

Background

People who inject drugs (PWID) account for a substantial proportion of the global HIV burden, with a global median HIV prevalence of approximately 5.0% [1]. Literature from around the world highlights a range of factors contributing to the elevated risk of HIV acquisition and progression to severe disease outcomes, including acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS), within this population [2–4]. The blood-borne route of HIV transmission suggests that PWID are susceptible, particularly if engaging in certain high-risk behaviours [2, 3], that facilitate direct exposure to HIV through contaminated blood and significantly increase the risk of acquiring the disease [4–7]. Additionally, an array of social, economic, and environmental factors [2, 3, 8] are reported to exacerbate this vulnerability by creating barriers to accessing harm reduction and treatment services [2, 3, 9], further perpetuating the cycle of risk and poor health outcomes [9]. This situation is particularly concerning in low- and middle-income countries, where limited resources, inadequate harm reduction programs, and structural inequalities further exacerbate the risk and hinder effective prevention and treatment efforts [10, 11].

Despite Bangladesh being classified as a low HIV prevalence country (< 1%), the impact of HIV is exacerbated in this population due to limited healthcare access, often driven by stigma and discrimination [12]. Additionally, the lack of data-driven evidence and the absence of effective policies informed by such evidence present significant barriers to the implementation of HIV prevention programs, further hindering outreach efforts. The absence of comprehensive harm reduction programs, coupled with the criminalization of drug use, creates significant obstacles to addressing the HIV epidemic in PWID communities [2]. Identification of specific risk factors which contribute to the disproportionate vulnerability of HIV/AIDS among PWID in Bangladesh is essential to inform harm reduction strategies which are both effective and culturally appropriate as well as target interventions which aim to address underlying barriers including marginalisation and poverty [5, 8]. Recognizing these challenges, the Bangladesh government has demonstrated a commitment to curbing the spread of HIV through national surveillance systems, including the Integrated Biobehavioural Survey (IBBS) [5].

In Bangladesh, HIV prevalence among PWID has shown a concerning upward trend. In 2017 alone, 865 new cases were reported—accounting for 15.5% of the country’s 5,586 confirmed cases since 1989—with over one-third of these new infections occurring within the PWID community [14]. Literatures based on earlier IBBS rounds (2007–2015) estimated HIV prevalence among PWID between 5.5 and 7.3% in selected urban areas, mainly in and around the capital city, Dhaka, but these studies were limited by narrow geographic coverage and underrepresentation of female PWID, despite documenting high injecting and sexual risk behaviours.

The IBBS 2020 survey addressed many of these gaps by re-establishing integrated surveillance after 2015, expanding coverage to 12 districts, and deliberately including female PWID through targeted recruitment. Methodological enhancements included concurrent collection of biological and behavioural data, testing for HIV, HCV, and syphilis, and immediate on-site disclosure of results with referral to care. These innovations improved representativeness and strengthened the capacity of surveillance to guide inclusive and effective harm reduction strategies. This study analyses the IBBS 2020 data to identify risk factors for HIV vulnerability among PWID in Bangladesh, with the goal of informing culturally appropriate and targeted interventions to reduce these risks”.

Methods

Study population and sampling

This study is a secondary analysis of the data collected in 2020 Integrated Biological and Behavioural Surveillance (IBBS) conducted at major urban and peri-urban areas in Bangladesh during October 2020 and December 2021 [5]. IBBS is a nationally coordinated effort to monitor HIV prevalence and risk behaviours among key populations at higher risk of HIV infection. These populations included people who inject drugs (PWID), female sex workers (FSWs), men who have sex with men (MSM), and transgender individuals (hijra). The IBBS aimed to generate evidence to inform national HIV prevention strategies. Current study focused exclusively on the PWID subgroup (n = 2,376). PWID was defined as: Persons who injected drugs (Opioids or stimulants or a combination of both), for non-medical purposes in the past one month, including both males and females. Participants were eligible if they were 18 years or older, had lived in the survey area for at least three months, and were willing and able to provide informed consent. Individuals were excluded if they had already participated in the same round of the survey at the same or another site, or if they were unable to provide informed consent, including those under the influence of alcohol or drugs at the time of recruitment.

IBBS followed a community based cross-sectional survey design [5]. The survey employed a two-stage cluster sampling design to ensure representativeness. A Pre-Survey Assessment (PSA) was carried out in districts, Dhaka, Cumilla, and Sylhet to evaluate feasibility for PWID recruitment, focusing on size estimation, typologies, and community sensitization. Information was gathered through desk reviews, key informant interviews, and group discussions, which informed the choice of sampling strategies. In the cluster selection stage, the country was stratified into urban and peri-urban areas known for high PWID activity. Within these areas, clusters were selected based on a mapping exercise and input from local stakeholders and harm reduction service providers. In the high HIV prevalence districts (Dhaka, Narayanganj, Gazipur, Cumilla, Rajshahi, and Chapainawabganj), Time-Location Sampling (TLS) was applied after mapping PWID hotspots. In low prevalence districts (Barishal and Mymensingh), Respondent Driven Sampling (RDS) was used. Seeds from PWID networks-initiated peer-based recruitment using coded coupons, with each participant able to refer up to three peers. In Mymensingh, 6 seeds were engaged, while in Barishal, 9 seeds initiated the process.

Data collection

Data collection was carried out by trained field staff through face-to-face interviews using a structured, pre-tested questionnaire tailored for the key populations. Biological samples were collected at designated survey sites, with informed consent obtained from all participants. The IBBS followed standardized procedures developed in collaboration with UNAIDS, WHO, and the Global Fund [32]. These included SOPs for specimen collection and rapid testing (HIV, HCV, syphilis), structured training manuals for field teams, and tablet-based data collection systems with built-in quality checks.

The data were collected under four broad domains. The first domain, Demographic and Social Characteristics, included information on age, sex, education, living circumstances, HIV knowledge, experiences of family exclusion, and healthcare avoidance. The second domain, Drug and Injection-Related Behaviors, captured data on age at first drug use and injection, frequency of injection, use of pre-used or prefilled equipment, and sharing of injection paraphernalia. The third domain, Sexual Behaviours, included the number of sexual partners, condom use, engagement with casual or commercial partners, and MSM activity. The final domain, Coinfections, assessed symptoms of sexually transmitted infections (STIs) and included results from biological testing. HIV and HCV screening were conducted using rapid diagnostic tests (RDTs), followed by confirmation via enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays (ELISA). For syphilis, a two-step process was used: the Rapid Plasma Reagin (RPR) test for initial detection of active infection, and the Treponema Pallidum Hemagglutination Assay (TPHA) for confirmatory identification of treponemal antibodies.

HIV knowledge was assessed using the standardized UNAIDS five-question survey, which includes: (1) “Can people reduce their risk of HIV by using a condom correctly and consistently in any type of sex?” (Correct answer: ‘yes’); (2) “Can people reduce their risk of HIV by avoiding sex with multiple partners?” (Correct answer: ‘yes’); (3) “Can a person get HIV through a mosquito bite?” (Correct answer: ‘no’); (4) “Can a person get HIV by sharing a meal with someone who is HIV infected?” (Correct answer: ‘no’); and (5) “Can you tell by looking at someone whether they are infected with HIV?” (Correct answer: ‘no’). Participants answering ≥ 3 questions correctly were classified as having ‘sufficient knowledge’ of HIV, while those answering < 3 questions were classified as having ‘insufficient knowledge.’

The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee (IRN no-BSMMU/2020/8524). During IBBS, a process of informed consent was employed and all the participants were given simple and clear information regarding the risks and benefits of participating in the national IBBS.

Data analysis

Sample weighting was applied to adjust for the complex survey design and account for the multistage cluster sampling used in the IBBS survey including RDS weights and post-stratification adjustments. Descriptive statistics were generated. Independent risk factors significantly influencing the risk of HIV infection among the PWID were determined by univariate analyses. The analysis employed two multivariable binary logistic regression models to identify factors associated with HIV risk among people who inject drugs (PWID): Partially Adjusted Model: explored individual factors only adjusting for universal confounders (age, gender, and education), this model aimed to examine additional intermediate factors that may influence HIV risk. Fully Adjusted Model: This model included all plausible confounders (those appeared as significantly associated with HIV in univariate analysis. (P < 0.05 in the univariate analysis) and applied the Wald backward elimination method to identify the most significant predictors. Results were reported as adjusted odds ratios (AOR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). Results were presented as adjusted OR and 95% CI. All analyses were performed in Stata 17®SE (9–13).

Results

Demographic characteristics and HIV prevalence

A total of 2,391 participants were included in the study, with the majority being male (96.9%) (Table 1). Around half (48.4%) of the participants were aged < 40 years, while 34.7% were aged 40–49 years, and 16.9% were aged 50 years or older. Regarding educational attainment, 40.4% had no formal education, 49.0% completed primary or secondary education, and only 10.6% achieved higher secondary education or above. Most participants lived alone (77.3%), and around a third (32.6%) reported feeling of family exclusion. The prevalence of HIV/AIDS among the sampled PWID was 4.1%. HIV prevalence in participants below 40 years of age was 3.9%, and was 4.3% among participants aged 40–49 years, and 1.5% in those aged ≥ 50 years. Females had higher prevalence (6.8%) compared to males (4.0%). HIV prevalence also varied by educational attainment, being lower among those with higher secondary or above education (0.8%) in comparison to those with primary or secondary education (4.9%) and no formal schooling (3.9%). Those living alone had a HIV prevalence almost double (6.8%) than those living with family or friends (3.2%). Further, participants who reported feelings of family exclusion had a higher HIV prevalence (7.1%) compared to those who did not (2.6%).

Co-infection status, HIV knowledge level and HIV prevalence

Diagnosed cases of syphilis and hepatitis C were reported in 5.2% and 33.2% of participants, respectively, while approximately 6.4% experienced symptoms of sexually transmitted infections (STIs), such as genital discharge or ulcers (Table 1). Despite this, 64.8% of participants demonstrated sufficient HIV knowledge, 15.9% indicated avoiding health-seeking behaviours. Participants with diagnosed syphilis had a significantly higher HIV prevalence (11.3%) compared to those without syphilis (3.7%) (p < 0.001). Similarly, individuals diagnosed with hepatitis C showed a higher HIV prevalence (6.9%) compared to those without hepatitis C (2.6%) (p < 0.001). Additionally, participants reporting one or more STI symptoms had a greater HIV prevalence (7.2%) compared to those without symptoms (3.8%) (p = 0.040). Insufficient HIV knowledge was linked to a higher HIV prevalence (5.7%) compared to sufficient knowledge (3.2%) (p = 0.003). Those avoiding health-seeking behaviours also exhibited a higher HIV prevalence (6.1%) compared to individuals who sought care (3.7%) (p = 0.030).

Drug injecting behaviour and HIV prevalence

Around 43% of participants initiated illegal use of either Injecting drugs, non-injecting substances: or combination of both between the ages of 25 and 34 years, 24.1% at the aged between 35 to 44 years, around 23.6% under 25 years and 9.4% at 45 years or older (Table 2). In terms of the duration of substance use, 44.9% of participants reported using drugs for 20 years or more, while 39.5% had been using for 10–19 years, and 15.4% had used for less than 10 years. Regarding injecting duration, 59.9% of participants had been injecting drugs for five or more years, while 30.1% had been injected for less than five years. Daily injecting was reported by 60.9% of participants. A small proportion (4.2%) experienced difficulty accessing syringes. Nearly a third (29.6%) reported sometimes or always reusing syringes. Around 8.7% reported using of prefilled syringes. Around 29.6% of participants shared needles, 29.2% shared injecting accessories, and 19.6% reported having more than one injecting partner. The prevalence of HIV increased with longer duration substance use of any form, with 4.8% of those using for 20 or more years, compared to only 1.1% among those using for less than 10 years (p = 0.006). The prevalence was significantly higher (5.2%) among people who injected drug for ≥ 5 years compared to those who injected for < 5 years (2.2%) (p = 0.003). Daily injectors exhibited a higher HIV prevalence (6.0%) compared to non-daily injectors (1.0%, p < 0.001). HIV was more prevalent among individuals who sometimes or always reused syringes (9.2% vs. 1.9%, p < 0.001), reported difficulty accessing sterile syringes (10.0% vs. 3.8%, p = 0.002), and those who used prefilled syringes (6.7% vs. 3.8%, p = 0.043). Higher HIV prevalence was also found among people who share needles (9.2% vs. 1.9%, p < 0.001), injecting accessories (9.6% vs. 1.8%, p < 0.001), and having multiple injecting partners (14.7 vs. 2.0%, p < 0.001).

Sexual behaviour and HIV prevalence

Around 27.6% of the participants reported having ≥ 2 sexual partners in the past year (Table 2). Having casual sex partners were reported by 11.1% of participants, while 24.7% reported to have had engaged in sex with commercial partners. Nearly half of the participants who had sex in the last month (46.9%) reported not using condoms. Participants with ≥ 2 sexual partners in the past year had a significantly higher HIV prevalence (6.7%) compared to those with 0–1 partner (3.1%, p < 0.001). Those who had commercial sex partners exhibited a higher prevalence (7.6%) than those who did not (2.9%, p < 0.001). Those who had casual sex partners exhibited a HIV prevalence of 3.4%, whereas the prevalence was 4.1% among those who did not; having casual sex partners showed no significant difference in HIV prevalence (p = 0.564). Although MSM participants had a higher HIV prevalence (7.7%) compared to non-MSM participants (4.0%), this difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.132).

Table 3 presents the results of two sets of binary logistic models (Partially adjusted models and Fully adjusted Model) to paint a comprehensive picture of HIV vulnerability. The association of individual factors with HIV risk was explored through binary logistic regression analyses adjusting for age, gender, and education (Partially adjusted Models). These models aimed to examine how individual factors contribute to HIV risk in isolation or within specific demographic contexts. Among socio-demographic factors, living alone (AOR: 2.11, 95% CI 1.3, 3.2) and feeling excluded from the family (AOR: 2.77, 95% CI 1.8, 4.1) significantly increased the odds of HIV infection. Poor HIV knowledge was associated with elevated HIV risk (AOR: 1.83, 95% CI 1.2, 2.8). Among co-infections, hepatitis C (AOR: 3.16, 95% CI 2.1, 4.9) and Syphilis (AOR: 3.70, 95% CI 2.0, 6.8) were associated with HIV infection.

Regarding drug use history, prolonged drug use of more than 10 years (AOR: 5.05, 95% CI 1.9, 13.2) and a history of injecting drugs for more than five years (AOR: 2.52, 95% CI 1.4, 4.4) significantly increased the odds of HIV infection. Unsafe injecting behaviours were highly associated with HIV risk, including frequent (daily) injecting (AOR: 7.14, 95% CI 3.5, 14.4), having multiple injecting partners (AOR: 6.94, 95% CI 4.5, 10.6), difficulty accessing syringes (AOR: 2.95, 95% CI 1.5, 5.9), using pre-used syringes (AOR: 5.30, 95% CI 3.4, 8.2), sharing needles (AOR: 5.31, 95% CI 3.4, 8.2), and sharing injecting accessories (AOR: 5.82, 95% CI 3.7, 9.1). Additionally, sexual risk behaviours, such as having more than one sexual partner (AOR: 2.17, 95% CI 1.4, 3.3) and engaging with commercial sex partners (AOR: 2.65, 95% CI 1.7, 4.1), were significant predictors of HIV infection in after adjusted for Age, Gender and Education.

The multivariable binary logistic regression model (fully adjusted Model) was fitted using stepwise backward elimination to identify independent predictors of HIV risk, adjusting for all plausible confounders. The analysis refined the understanding of HIV risk, narrowing down significant predictors. Social exclusion, reflected in feelings of exclusion from the family, remained a significant factor (AOR: 1.71, 95% CI 1.1, 2.7). Hepatitis C infection also emerged as an independent predictor of HIV (AOR: 2.57, 95% CI 1.6, 4.0). Prolonged drug use of more than 10 years (AOR: 3.74, 95% CI 1.3, 10.8) and frequent injecting (once or more daily) (AOR: 5.23, 95% CI 2.6, 10.7) were strongly associated with HIV infection. Unsafe injecting behaviours, such as having multiple injecting partners (AOR: 3.11, 95% CI 1.8, 5.3) and sharing injecting accessories (AOR: 2.55, 95% CI 1.5, 4.4), remained significant after adjusting for all plausible confounders. Additionally, engaging with a commercial sex partner (AOR: 1.80, 95% CI 1.1, 2.9) persisted as a significant sexual risk behaviour contributing to HIV infection.

Discussion

This study found an HIV prevalence of 4.1% among PWID in Bangladesh, with significant associations observed for social exclusion, HCV co-infection, prolonged drug use, frequent injecting, multiple injecting partners, sharing injecting accessories, and engagement with commercial sex partners. These findings support the study’s aim to identify behavioural and structural predictors of HIV vulnerability among PWID, offering evidence to inform targeted harm reduction strategies in Bangladesh.

HIV prevalence among PWID in Bangladesh stands at 4.1%, underscoring a significant vulnerability in this subgroup, despite the low prevalence of HIV in the general population (< 1%) [5, 16]. This disparity highlights the need for targeted interventions to mitigate HIV risk among high-risk populations. Bangladesh is considered as a low-prevalence but high-risk setting for HIV [12], influenced by socio-economic determinants of health, limited access to effective harm reduction services, and pervasive stigma and discrimination against HIV and drug use, all of which hinder healthcare engagement.

This study identifies multiple drivers of HIV risk among PWID using two analytical models: an all-plausible-confounders-adjusted model and a partially adjusted model. These models provide complementary insights, with the former identifying key independent predictors and the latter highlighting intermediate pathways influencing HIV vulnerability. The fully adjusted model highlights a compact set of independent predictors, reflecting the complex interplay of socio-demographic factors (e.g., social exclusion), co-infections (e.g., hepatitis C), drug use behaviours (e.g., prolonged drug use > 10 years, frequent injecting), unsafe injecting practices (e.g., sharing injecting accessories, multiple injecting partners), and sexual risk behaviours (e.g., engaging with commercial sex partners). These predictors offer actionable priorities for public health strategies. The partially adjusted model (adjusted for age, gender, and education) reveals additional factors—such as living alone, poor knowledge, avoidance of health-seeking behaviours, syphilis co-infection, injecting drugs for > 5 years, difficulty accessing syringes, and multiple sex partners—that lose significance in the fully adjusted model. These intermediate factors may operate through confounding or mediation pathways and highlight the need for targeted outreach and education. Leveraging both models enable a nuanced understanding of HIV risk and informs multifaceted intervention strategies.

Injection-related behaviours emerged as strong predictors of HIV risk in this study. Prolonged drug use and frequent (daily) injecting were particularly significant, reflecting elevated HIV exposure associated with these practices. Unsafe injecting behaviours, including sharing injecting accessories and having multiple injecting partners, further amplified risk. Limited access to syringes and injecting for more than five years also increased HIV risk. These findings align with established literature [15–17], which underscores the direct role of injecting practices in HIV transmission via exposure to contaminated blood. High-frequency injection increases the likelihood of unsafe practices, particularly in settings with scarce clean injecting equipment. Hence duration and frequency of injecting have consistently been linked to elevated HIV risk in South Asian PWID cohorts as shown previously by Ray et al. [33].

Social and structural factors exacerbate risk associated with injecting behaviours. Barriers such as, stigma, criminalization, and fear of arrest compel PWID to share needles or reuse syringes. Dynamics within injecting networks, including trust, power imbalances, or financial constraints, further drive risky practices. Prolonged drug use also impairs judgment, reducing adherence to safer injecting methods. Addressing these structural challenges is essential for effective harm reduction. Social isolation significantly influences HIV risk. People who live alone or are excluded from family support are more vulnerable, likely due to psychological distress and maladaptive coping mechanisms [18, 19], Social isolation and marginalisation are also likely to hinder healthcare access, contributing to delayed testing, reduced treatment adherence, and higher engagement in risk behaviours [20–23]. The partially adjusted model also identified avoidance of health-seeking behaviours and poor HIV knowledge as significant factors, suggesting that stigma, discrimination, and mistrust in healthcare systems are critical barriers to effective interventions. A similar pattern of association of poor knowledge implies that while HIV knowledge is a key determinant of risk, its effect might be intertwined with other factors like unsafe injecting practices or social determinants (e.g., exclusion or isolation). Evidence suggests that education and advocacy across structural, public, and individual domains are among the most effective strategies for stigma and related reduction [22]. Enhancing social support is another critical avenue, as it has been shown to protect against substance use disorders and improve addiction treatment outcomes [24]. In Bangladesh, socio-economic and cultural factors, compounded by social stigma, significantly hinder access to healthcare for PWID [18]. Addressing social exclusion is essential to alleviate psychological distress, minimize high-risk behaviours, and enhance healthcare engagement within this population. While these factors lost significance in the fully adjusted model, their role as mediators underscores the need for targeted interventions to improve healthcare engagement and HIV literacy.

HCV and HIV are highly prevalent co-occurring infections, with an estimated 2.3 million HCV-HIV co-infections globally, more than half (1.3 million) of whom are PWID [7]. Hepatitis C co-infection is strongly associated with HIV risk in this study, reflecting shared transmission routes such as injecting drug use and sexual activity [25] in Bangladeshi PWID. Syphilis and other sexually transmitted co-infections may also share transmission pathways with HIV and indicate overlapping risk behaviours, such as unprotected sex and needle sharing. In Bangladesh, resource limitations and socio-economic challenges compound the burden of HCV-HIV co-infection. Comprehensive screening and treatment for co-infections should be integral to HIV prevention programs.

Sexual risk behaviours equally contribute to HIV vulnerability. Engaging with commercial sex partners and having multiple partners were significant predictors in this study, emphasizing the dual risk from overlapping sexual and injecting behaviours. Despite HIV being an STI, injecting behaviours in this study appeared as a greater contributor to HIV risk. This may partly result from under reporting of sexual behaviours due to stigma and social desirability bias, particularly in conservative settings like Bangladesh [16, 20–22]. Engaging with commercial sex partners and having multiple sex partners indeed significantly increased the odds of HIV infection in our study, underscoring the dual risk faced by PWID due to overlapping sexual and injecting behaviours. Sexual risk factors such as unprotected intercourse and multiple partners are well-documented among PWID [1, 5], the interplay between substance use and unsafe sexual practices compounds HIV risk, as impaired judgment from substance use increases the likelihood of high-risk sexual activity [26, 27]. Addressing these interconnected behaviours requires a holistic approach rather than treating them in isolation. The higher HIV prevalence among individuals engaged in commercial sex particularly highlights the urgency of integrating sexual health education with harm reduction strategies. Improving access to condoms and STI/HIV testing are critical to mitigating these overlapping risks and enhancing HIV prevention efforts among PWID [25] Integrated strategies addressing both sexual health and harm reduction are critical, including condom distribution, STI testing, and targeted sexual health education. Harm reduction strategies are vital for addressing HIV risk among PWID in Bangladesh. Proven interventions, such as needle and syringe exchange programs (NSPs) and opioid substitution therapy (OST), have demonstrated substantial efficacy, with NSPs reducing HIV transmission by 48% and OST by 54% [28, 29]. Despite this, harm reduction services in Bangladesh currently cover only about 60% of PWID [16]. Expanding these programs and addressing access barriers can significantly improve outcomes. Community-based campaigns to reduce stigma, enhance social support, and increase healthcare utilization should complement these efforts, as stigma serves as a barrier to healthcare access [30].

This study benefits from a large sample size (2376 participants), robust statistical methods, and a comprehensive dataset from the 2020 Integrated Biological and Behavioural Surveillance (IBBS) [31, 32]. The dual-model approach enhances the depth of analysis, identifying both independent predictors and context-specific influences. For PWID, the IBBS 2020 had several limitations. Only selected districts were included, so findings cannot be generalized nationally. TLS recruitment relied on accurate mapping of PWID venues; omission of sites may have caused selection bias, capturing mostly those linked to prevention programs. In non-intervention districts, RDS may have favored PWID already connected with NGOs or drop-in centers, while more isolated, upper-class, or female PWID were underrepresented. Those younger than 18, who may begin injecting early, were excluded. These factors should be considered when interpreting results and designing future HIV programs targeting PWID in Bangladesh. Also gender bias may have influenced recruitment and representation, as despite targeted seed selection, only a small proportion of female PWID were captured, reflecting network constraints and social invisibility in this subgroup. The reliance on self-reported data—particularly for sensitive behaviors such as injecting practices and sexual activity—introduces potential for recall bias and social desirability bias, which may have led to misreporting. Underreporting of HIV risk behaviors and service access is likely, especially in stigmatized settings where participants may fear disclosure. Lastly, the possibility of residual confounding remains, as unmeasured factors (e.g., mental health, structural violence, informal peer networks) may have contributed to the observed associations despite statistical adjustment. The cross-sectional design also limits causal inference, necessitating longitudinal studies to explore temporal relationships. Future studies may consider supplemental recruitment strategies to enhance female representation avoiding selection bias.

In conclusion the study highlights the disproportionately higher prevalence of HIV among PWID in Bangladesh, driven by a complex interplay of social, behavioural, biological, and structural factors. Key predictors include social exclusion, HCV co-infection, prolonged substance use, frequent and unsafe injecting practices, and engagement with commercial sex partners. These findings underscore the pressing need for targeted and holistic interventions tailored to the unique vulnerabilities of PWID. A coordinated approach that integrates harm reduction strategies, structural reforms, and biomedical interventions is essential to mitigate high-risk behaviours, enhance healthcare engagement, and curb the HIV epidemic within this marginalized population. Given reported prevalence of HIV and limited diagnostic reach, future IBBS should consider integrating RNA-based testing to confirm active infections, recruiting females to a representative level, coupled with expanded harm reduction services readily accessible, including opioid substitution therapy (methadone, buprenorphine), needle-syringe exchange, naloxone distribution, and condoms. Risk-reduction programs should emphasize consistent condom use. HCV treatment should begin in high-prevalence districts. HIV-positive PWID need improved treatment adherence through OST, counselling, and education. Employment opportunities are essential to address socio-economic marginalization. Affordable treatment for opioid and stimulant abuse should be scaled up, guided by mapping existing services. Operational research is needed to investigate widespread buprenorphine injection and its sources to inform targeted interventions and strengthen national harm reduction strategies.
