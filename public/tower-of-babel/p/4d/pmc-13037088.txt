Engineering a yeast cell factory for high-level production of unnatural ginsenoside 3β-O-Glc-DM
Authors: Xiao-Yan Sun, Yu Peng, Ting Gong, Jing-Jing Chen, Tian-Jiao Chen, Jin-Ling Yang, Ping Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe unnatural ginsenoside 3β-O-Glc-DM exhibits potent anti-colon cancer activity. An engineered yeast strain expressing the dammarenediol-II synthase (DS) and glycosyltransferase (PgUGT74AE2) genes from Panax ginseng was previously constructed to produce 3β-O-Glc-DM. However, the titer of 3β-O-Glc-DM was insufficient for industrial-scale production. Background

Panax ginseng has been widely recognized for its medicinal value in East Asian traditional medicine. Ginsenosides, the main bioactive components of P. ginseng, demonstrate a wide range of pharmacological effects, including antitumor [1], anti-aging [2], memory enhancement [3], immunomodulation [4], cardioprotection [5], hepatoprotection [6], glycemic control [7], and neuroprotection [8]. Dammarane-type ginsenosides are the major type and the main pharmacologically active components of ginsenosides, which are classified into protopanaxadiol (PPD) and protopanaxatriol (PPT) glycosides. The biosynthesis of these ginsenosides begins with dammarenediol-II (DM), a precursor containing hydroxyl groups at C3 and C20 positions. Hydroxylation at the C12 position of DM forms PPD, while further hydroxylation at C6 of PPD forms PPT. Subsequent glycosylation of PPD and PPT aglycones produces structurally diverse ginsenosides. Notably, to date, no naturally occurring ginsenosides with DM as the aglycone have been identified in Panax species [9].

In our previous study, we discovered that the glycosyltransferase PgUGT74AE2 from P. ginseng could glycosylate the C3-OH of DM to produce the unnatural ginsenoside 3β-O-Glc-DM, which exhibits potent anti-colon cancer activity [10]. We engineered a yeast strain via synthetic biology to produce 3β-O-Glc-DM at a titer of 2.4 g/L in a 3-L bioreactor [10]. However, the titer remained insufficient for industrial-scale production due to the limited catalytic efficiency of PgUGT74AE2 in 3β-O-Glc-DM biosynthesis.

Combining protein engineering with metabolic engineering has become a critical strategy for increasing target compound yields in engineered strains. Zhuang et al. [11] employed semi-rational protein engineering to improve the catalytic efficiency of UGT51 from Saccharomyces cerevisiae by approximately 1800-fold for converting PPD to ginsenoside Rh2. Following the introduction of the engineered UGT51 gene into a PPD-producing chassis, Rh2 production increased by more than 120-fold. Subsequent metabolic engineering, including preventing Rh2 degradation and augmenting UDP-glucose precursor supply, further elevated Rh2 production to over 900-fold that of the parent strain. Li et al. [12] improved the catalytic efficiency of the glycosyltransferase PjUGT10 towards 20S,24R-ocotillol by an unprecedented 6.16 × 103-fold through iterative semi-rational protein engineering. Coupled with metabolic engineering via co-expression of UDP-glucose synthesis genes in Escherichia coli, the engineered strain yielded approximately 500 mg/L of the rare OCT-type ginsenoside pseudoginsenoside Rt5. Zhou et al. [13] improved the catalytic efficiency of UGT109A1 towards PPD by 3.5-fold through semi-rational protein engineering. By integrating the genes of the engineered UGT and a high-efficiency β-glycosidase Bgy2 into a metabolically optimized PPD-producing yeast chassis, they developed an engineered strain that produced the unnatural ginsenoside 12β-O-Glc-PPD at a titer of 38.6 mg/L. These successful cases demonstrate the synergistic potential of combining protein engineering with metabolic engineering, providing a viable strategy to increase the production of target compounds in the engineered strains.

Beyond genetic modifications within the host, fermentation optimization is equally critical for enhancing the production of target compounds. Wang et al. [14] systematically optimized shake-flask parameters for Cordyceps sinensis fungus UM01 to maximize bioactive polysaccharide production. By employing orthogonal design to adjust temperature (15 °C), initial pH (6.0), and medium capacity (2/5 v/v), they achieved a polysaccharide titer of 486.16 mg/L, representing a 2.3-fold increase over baseline conditions. Zhang et al. [15] optimized fed-batch fermentation for sucrose isomerase production in Bacillus subtilis at the bioreactor level. Through dynamic control of carbon/nitrogen source ratios (glucose 30 g/L, yeast extract 3 g/L) and pH gradients (7.0-7.5) in a 3-L bioreactor, they achieved an enzyme activity of 862.86 U/mL — the highest reported titer in food-safe strains. The systematic implementation of such fermentation optimization strategies could effectively enhance the production of target compounds in industrial bioprocesses.

Therefore, to enhance the production of 3β-O-Glc-DM, we performed protein engineering on the glycosyltransferase PgUGT74AE2, generating a mutant with higher catalytic activity. Metabolic engineering strategies were then applied to boost precursor supply: overexpression of mevalonate (MVA) pathway enzymes, suppression of competitive metabolic pathways, and activation of the transcriptional regulator HAC1 [16] to increase 2,3-oxidosqualene availability. Using the CRISPR/Cas9 system, the genes of PgUGT74AE2-K276A and dammarenediol-II synthase (DS) were both introduced into the optimized chassis strain to generate the engineered strain with higher titer of 3β-O-Glc-DM (Fig. 1). In particular, the DS gene was fused with GFP at its C-terminus based on our prior finding that this fusion significantly improves the production of dammarenediol-II in S. cerevisiae compared with expressing DS alone [17]. Ultimately, the production of 3β-O-Glc-DM was significantly increased through optimized shake-flask culture conditions and fed-batch fermentation of the engineered strain, achieving a titer of 3.4 g/L in a 3-L bioreactor. This study significantly increased the titer of 3β-O-Glc-DM, laying a foundation for its industrial-scale production and the development of new anti-colon cancer drugs.

Materials and methods

Strains and media

E. coli Trans1-T1 and Transetta(DE3) (TransGen Biotech, Beijing, China) were used for plasmid amplification and protein expression, respectively. E. coli strains were cultured at 37 °C in LB medium supplemented with 100 µg/mL ampicillin. S. cerevisiae Y-ΔHXK2, in which the HXK2 gene was deleted to increase acetyl CoA flux, was used as the chassis strain [10]. The engineered yeasts were cultivated at 30 °C either in SD medium lacking leucine, uracil, tryptophan or histidine where appropriate for the selection of recombinants or in YPD medium for fermentation.

Homology modeling of PgUGT74AE2

The three-dimensional (3D) model of PgUGT74AE2 was built using SWISS-MODEL (https://swissmodel.expasy.org/) [18], and the overall structure was constructed based on the crystal structure of UGT74AC1 from Siraitia grosvenorii (PDB ID: 6L90). Following initial modeling, we refined the predicted PgUGT74AE2 structure using the energy minimization function within Discovery Studio 2016. The quality of the final model was evaluated using the PROCHECK program (https://saves.mbi.ucla.edu/) [19].

Docking experiments

The PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov) provided the 3D structure of DM (PubChem CID: 10895555). The protein structure was modified by removing water molecules and adding polar hydrogens in the docking process using AutoDock Tools (https://auto-dock.scripps.edu/resources/adt). Docking simulations between PgUGT74AE2 and DM were performed using AutoDock 4.2 [20]. We ran 10 independent docking trials and ranked the resulting conformations based on their calculated binding affinity scores. Visualization of the docking results and analysis of the protein-ligand interactions were carried out using PyMOL (https://www.pymol.org) and Discovery Studio 2016.

Plasmid construction

Plasmid pET32a-PgUGT74AE2 was constructed previously [10]. The alanine-scanning and semi-saturation mutations were all achieved by whole-plasmid amplification PCR. FastPfu Fly DNA Polymerase (TransGen Biotech, Beijing, China) was used for PCR.

Enzymatic characterization of PgUGT74AE2 and its mutants

The pET32a plasmids carrying the genes of wild-type PgUGT74AE2 and its mutants were transformed into E. coli Transetta(DE3). The PgUGT74AE2 protein and its mutants were expressed and purified using a previously established method [10]. To measure their enzyme activities, we quantified the total amount of 3β-O-Glc-DM produced over the reaction period. The activity of the wild-type PgUGT74AE2 was set as 100%, and the activities of all mutants were expressed as a percentage thereof.

Enzyme activity tests used 100 µL reaction mixtures, which contained 25 mM Tris-HCl buffer (pH 8.0), 0.5 mM DM, 5 mM UDPG, and 500 ng of the PgUGT74AE2 enzyme or its mutant. Reactions proceeded at 37 °C for 12 h. As a negative control, we used a reaction mixture containing the lysate from E. coli Transetta(DE3) cells carrying the empty pET32a plasmid. We stopped the reactions by adding 200 µL of methanol. After centrifugation and filtration, we directly analyzed the resulting target products by HPLC. Each experiment included three replicates. Specific HPLC conditions are listed in Table S1.

The kinetic parameters were determined towards DM in a concentration range of 0.1–2.4 mM. Reactions were conducted in 25 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0) containing 5 mM UDPG and 5 µg purified enzymes in a total volume of 100 µL at 30 °C and 37 °C for 30 min. The reactions were terminated by adding 200 µL of methanol. For each temperature condition, the initial reaction rate at each substrate concentration was measured in three independent experiments. The mean rate (± SD) from these replicates was used for kinetic analysis. Kinetic parameters (Km and Vmax) were determined by fitting initial reaction rates to the Michaelis-Menten equation using nonlinear regression (least-squares fit) in GraphPad Prism 9, with no weighting applied. The results are presented as the best-fit parameter value ± (SD) of the fit, as estimated by the software.

Construction of engineered yeasts

The gene expression cassettes of DS-GFP, tHMG1, HAC1, IDI1, ERG20, ERG9, ERG1, antisense ERG7 and PgUGT74AE2 were constructed in our previous work [10]. The gene expression cassette of mutant PgUGT74AE2-K276A was amplified from its wild-type cassette. To construct the engineered strain capable of high 2,3-oxidosqualene production, the integration modules containing the expression cassettes of tHMG1, IDI1, ERG20, ERG9, ERG1, antisense and LEU together with integration modules containing the expression cassettes of HAC1 and TRP were co-transformed into the δ4 and rDNA sites of Y-ΔHXK2, resulting in the strain Y13.

The two DNA integration modules are designated as DS-GFP, PgUGT74AE2, and HIS; DS-GFP, PgUGT74AE2-K276A, and HIS, respectively. The strains Y13-WT and Y13-K276A were also constructed by integrating these two DNA integration modules into the δ1 site of Y13 and co-transforming plasmid pESC-URA-Cas9-δ into the strain Y13, respectively. Fifteen single colonies were randomly picked from SD plates and were cultivated in YPD medium at 30 °C for 3 days to compare the titers of the target products. Cultivation condition optimization and fed-batch fermentation

To optimize culture conditions in shake flasks, we evaluated YPD media prepared with various brands and grades of yeast extract and peptone, assessing their impact on product titer. In this study, when comparing product titer among different transformants during preliminary screening, the yeast extract and peptone were both purchased from Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China). After comparison, we found that the YPD medium prepared using yeast extract and peptone bacteriological both from Oxoid Ltd. (Basingstoke, UK) led to the highest product titer. Subsequently, all YPD medium used in further experiments was prepared with these Oxoid components.

The engineered strains were first inoculated in 10 mL of YPD medium and grown at 30 °C, 220 rpm for 16 h, then transferred into 150 mL YPD medium in 500 mL shake flasks with an initial OD600 of 0.2 and grown at 30 °C, 220 rpm for 144 h. 1 mL feed solution [10] was added into the medium at 48 h, 72 h, 96 h and 120 h, respectively. Utilizing the single-factor control method, a sequential optimization of cultivation conditions was performed, including the optimization of culture volume, medium pH, the amount of single feed, cultivation temperature, and cultivation time.

The engineered strain Y13-A9 underwent fed-batch fermentation in a 3-L bioreactor following the previously described procedure [10]. A feedback control feeding method was employed to introduce the aforementioned feed solution into the bioreactor during the glucose fed-batch process. The concentration of glucose and ethanol was maintained below 5.0 g/L and 20.0 g/L. The fermentation broth was sampled and analyzed at 24-hour intervals. All shake-flask and bioreactor fermentations were conducted in three independent biological replicates.

Metabolite extraction and HPLC analysis

The cells were collected by centrifugation at 8000 g for 10 min. After centrifugation, the cell pellets were freeze-dried and their dry weight was recorded. To extract the compounds, the dried cells were subjected to three rounds of extraction using methanol with the aid of ultrasound. The combined methanol extracts were then evaporated under vacuum until completely dry. The resulting residue was dissolved in an appropriate volume of methanol to prepare the sample for analysis. Product yield was determined using HPLC on an Agilent 1260 Infinity II system.

Chromatographic separation was achieved using a Cosmosil 5C18-MS-II column (5 μm, 4.6 mm × 150 mm) maintained at 28 °C. The mobile phase consisted of water and ACN, delivered at a flow rate of 1 mL per minute. Detection was performed at a UV wavelength of 203 nm. Specific conditions for the analytical and semi-preparative HPLC methods used to analyze enzyme reaction mixtures and yeast fermentation extracts are detailed in Table S1. All reported data were presented as means ± SD of three independent replicates.

Results

Three-dimensional structure prediction of PgUGT74AE2

PgUGT74AE2 catalyzes the key glycosylation step in the 3β-O-Glc-DM biosynthetic pathway. The enzyme was previously introduced into the DM-producing engineered yeast strain to generate 3β-O-Glc-DM. Enhancing the activity of PgUGT74AE2 may increase 3β-O-Glc-DM production in engineered strain [21]. Since the 3D structure of PgUGT74AE2 was unavailable, we first generated a homology model with SWISS-MODEL and then performed semi-rational design to improve its catalytic efficiency towards DM.

The glycosyltransferase UGT74AC1 (PDB ID: 6L90) from S. grosvenorii shares 41.8% sequence identity with PgUGT74AE2 and also catalyzes the C3-OH glycosylation of DM [22, 23]. Both proteins belong to the GT1 superfamily of glycosyltransferases. The 3D structure of PgUGT74AE2 was obtained through homology modeling using the crystal structure of UGT74AC1 as a template. The derived 3D structure of PgUGT74AE2 is depicted in Fig. 2A. PgUGT74AE2 contains a GT-B fold composed of two distinct β/α/β Rossmann-like domains: the N-terminal domain and the C-terminal domain. The N-terminal domain is responsible for binding the acceptor substrate, while the C-terminal domain binds the donor substrate. The above structural characteristics are consistent with those of the template protein UGT74AC1. The predicted model underwent energy minimization in Discovery Studio 2016 and stereochemical validation using online tools. Ramachandran plot analysis indicated robust structural quality, with 92.5% of residues residing in the most favored regions and only 0.3% in disallowed regions (Fig. S1), confirming high stereochemical plausibility.

Identification of key residues of PgUGT74AE2 using alanine scanning

We docked the substrate DM with PgUGT74AE2 using Autodock 4.2, and 22 amino acid residues were identified within 5 Å of DM (Fig. 2B). The PSPG motif [24], a conserved 44-amino acid sequence at the C-terminus of plant glycosyltransferases (Fig. S2), was excluded from mutagenesis because it binds sugar donors [25]. H17 (Fig. S2), located in the catalytic center, was also excluded to avoid disrupting activity [26]. Based on the above, we first excluded nine residues located within the PSPG motif (H345, C346, G347, W348, N349, Q367, F368, D369 and Q370) and the conserved active-site residue H17. So we selected the 12 remaining residues for alanine scanning: F12, G16, M73, M77, L110, H131, L182, H185, L186, V275, K276 and N373.

In order to investigate the sites associated with the catalytic activity of PgUGT74AE2, these 12 “hot spot” residues were mutated to alanine (Fig. 2D). Mutating V275 to alanine completely abolished activity, confirming its essential role. Replacing M73 and M77 with alanine clearly lowered activity, while changing F12, G16, L110, H131, L182, L186 and N373 to alanine severely impaired activity, indicating their critical role. Compared with the wild type, the H185A mutant showed slightly higher activity, while K276A exhibited a 3.4-fold increase in catalytic activity, suggesting both sites are promising targets for further engineering to boost the catalytic activity of PgUGT74AE2.

Semi-saturation mutagenesis of K276 and H185 of PgUGT74AE2

Through semi-saturation mutagenesis of the H185 and K276 sites identified from alanine scanning, we observed distinct functional impacts correlated with their spatial positions in PgUGT74AE2. For K276, all mutants showed higher catalytic activity towards DM than the wild type (Fig. 2E). The most active mutant, K276V, exhibited 3.4-fold higher activity than the wild type, comparable to the K276A mutant. Molecular docking revealed that wild-type K276 occupies entrance space without direct substrate interaction (Fig. 2B, C).

In contrast, H185 mutants (positioned near the entrance but within a shallow cavity) showed divergent effects: 8 mutants (H185L/I/N/D/C/S/G/R) had reduced activity, while 3 mutants (H185F/V/Y) showed mild improvement. The best performer, H185V, reached 1.3-fold of wild-type activity, outperforming the prior H185A mutant. Smaller residues at H185 may slightly expand local space, but its deeper position limited the enhancement of catalytic activity compared with entrance-located K276 (Fig. 2F). Structural analysis further highlighted the dominance of entrance residues in activity modulation, whereas residues distant from the active pocket (outside the entrance region) showed negligible impacts. These findings underscore that strategic engineering of entrance-located residues offers the most effective route for glycosyltransferase optimization.

To explore additional engineering targets, we generated four homology-guided mutants (R27H, S46R, M73L, V106I) based on the engineered UGT74AC1-M7 (H47R/M76L/S15A/T79Y/R28H/L48M/L109I) [22, 23] (Fig. S2). While S46R and V106I marginally improved activity, R27H and M73L severely compromised function (Fig. S4), indicating long-range structural roles despite lacking direct substrate contact (> 5 Å). Subsequently, 11 combinatorial mutants merging S46R, V106I, H185A with K276A variants were constructed. None surpassed the activity of K276A or K276V (Fig. S4), suggesting additive perturbations from non-interacting residues (R27/S46) destabilized the catalytic architecture.

Molecular basis for the improved glycosylation activity of engineered PgUGT74AE2

Mutation at the K276 site of PgUGT74AE2 significantly increased its catalytic activity. To elucidate the underlying mechanism, we conducted molecular docking and enzyme kinetics studies on the wild-type enzyme and the three most active mutants K276A, K276V, and K276F.

Enzyme kinetics analyses revealed distinct improvements for the three most active mutants relative to wild type (Table 2). It is noteworthy that these kinetic analyses were conducted at 30 °C. Although K276A, K276V and K276F showed comparable efficiency at the initial screening temperature of 37 °C (Table S2), their parameters were clearly differentiated at 30 °C. This temperature was selected because it represents the cultivation temperature of the yeast chassis. The Km of PgUGT74AE2 towards DM (0.21 mM) was slightly higher than that of K276A towards DM (0.19 mM), while the turnover number (kcat) of K276A towards DM (0.23 s⁻¹) was 1.21-fold that of PgUGT74AE2 towards DM (0.19 s⁻¹). The catalytic efficiency (kcat/Km) of K276A towards DM (1.21 mM⁻¹ s⁻¹) was 1.34-fold that of PgUGT74AE2 towards DM (0.90 mM⁻¹ s⁻¹). In comparison, the Km of K276V towards DM (0.26 mM) was 1.24-fold that of PgUGT74AE2 towards DM, while its turnover number (0.27 s⁻¹) was 1.42-fold that of PgUGT74AE2, resulting in a catalytic efficiency (1.03 mM⁻¹ s⁻¹) that was 1.14-fold higher than the wild type. For K276F, the Km towards DM (0.23 mM) was slightly higher than that of PgUGT74AE2, with a turnover number (0.25 s⁻¹) 1.32-fold that of the wild type, leading to a catalytic efficiency (1.09 mM⁻¹ s⁻¹) 1.21-fold higher than PgUGT74AE2. Taken together, among the tested enzymes, K276A exhibited the highest catalytic efficiency towards DM, indicating it is the most active mutant.

Molecular docking revealed that wild-type K276, located at the entrance of the active pocket within 5 Å of the substrate, imposed steric hindrance and weak electrostatic interference, limiting substrate binding and orientation (Fig. 2C). For the K276A mutant, the alanine substitution (-CH3) eliminated steric bulk, widening the entrance and lowering substrate binding barriers (Km reduction). Concurrently, new alkyl interactions with residues F12 and A14 expanded the hydrophobic network. This network, which now comprises a total of 15 contacts, involves residues F368 (4 contacts), V275 (1), H185 (2), L182 (1), L110 (2), F12 (2), and A14 (3), with F368 contributing the most contacts (Fig. 3A, B). This restructured environment enhanced catalytic flexibility (kcat increase), further supported by an additional pi-sigma interaction via F12. Critically, the wild-type Q370-substrate hydrogen bond remained intact, preserving orientation stability.

Collectively, K276 mutations improve glycosyltransferase activity through synergistic strategies: alleviating steric hindrance at the entrance, expanding hydrophobic networks via intensified alkyl and Pi-sigma interactions, and reorganizing hydrogen bonds to stabilize catalytic transitions. These findings underscore how structural flexibility and precise molecular interactions can be harmonized to optimize enzymatic performance, offering a strategic framework for engineering biocatalysts in synthetic biology.

Optimization of the chassis cell for enhancing 2,3-oxidosqualene production

In our previous research, we demonstrated that the deletion of the HXK2 gene in yeast strain YPH499 increases the supply of acetyl CoA to the MVA pathway, ultimately enhancing the biosynthesis of heterologous ginsenosides [10]. Therefore, the strain Y-ΔHXK2 was used as a chassis for producing the target product. 2,3-Oxidosqualene is the precursor of ginsenosides, and its biosynthetic pathway is inherent to yeast hosts. To enhance the production of 3β-O-Glc-DM, we initially optimized the biosynthetic pathway of the precursor 2,3-oxidosqualene in the chassis cells. The strategies included increasing the accumulation of the target compound’s precursor by upregulating the expression of key enzymes (tHMG1, IDI1, ERG20, ERG9, ERG1) and downregulating the level of side-pathway metabolism (ERG7−), inducing high expression of related enzymes through the upregulation of the transcription factor HAC1 expression, and facilitating the multi-copy integration of foreign genes via the CRISPR/Cas9 system (Fig. 3C).

However, during product detection, it was found that 2,3-oxidosqualene could not be detected in any of the transformants or the chassis cell Y-ΔHXK2, but its precursor compound squalene and its metabolic product ergosterol could be detected (Fig. 3E). This may be due to the instability of 2,3-oxidosqualene during the extraction process, a phenomenon that has also been observed in previous studies [26]. We hypothesized that strains with high squalene content also exhibit elevated levels of 2,3-oxidosqualene. Therefore, strain Y13, which showed a significant increase in squalene accumulation (Fig. 3E), was selected as the chassis strain to engineer for 3β-O-Glc-DM production.

Construction of the engineered yeast to produce 3β-O-Glc-DM

The genes of PgUGT74AE2 and its K276A mutant were co-integrated with DS-GFP gene into the genomes of yeast strain Y13 (Fig. 3D), resulting in two types of recombinant strains: Y13-WT and Y13-A, respectively. Fifteen randomly selected transformants from each strain group were cultured in YPD medium at 30 °C for 3 days. Subsequently, they were analyzed for 3β-O-Glc-DM production. The result showed that the strains engineered with the K276A mutant of PgUGT74AE2 demonstrated higher median titer and overall production range of 3β-O-Glc-DM than those with the wild-type enzyme, confirming that the improved catalytic activity of the mutant was maintained in the yeast system (Fig. S6).

We compared the highest-producing transformants (Y13-WT4 and Y13-A9) with Y8CSH, which was constructed previously to produce 3β-O-Glc-DM [10]. The results showed that the 3β-O-Glc-DM titers in Y13-WT4 and Y13-A9 increased by 10.6% and 15.2% compared with Y8CSH, respectively, confirming that the optimization of the chassis cell was effective (Fig. 3F). Metabolic intermediate analysis showed a 33.5% reduction in DM peak area for Y13-A9 compared with Y8CSH (Fig. 3G), indicating that the K276A mutant effectively enhanced DM glycosylation, consistent with the result of the previous enzyme kinetic study.

Optimization of cultivation conditions for the engineered yeast producing 3β-O-Glc-DM

Compared with protein engineering and metabolic engineering, using fermentation environment variables to regulate the metabolism of the engineered strains is a flexible and simple method [27]. Under initial cultivation conditions, strain Y13-A9 showed relatively low 3β-O-Glc-DM production in shake flasks, which is not conducive to product accumulation and titer improvement during subsequent high-density fermentation.

During optimization of culture conditions at the shake-flask level, we first tested YPD media made with different brands and types of yeast extract and peptone. We found that YPD medium prepared with yeast extract and peptone bacteriological from Oxoid Ltd. (Basingstoke, UK) gave the highest product titer, reaching 230 mg/L. All subsequent experiments were carried out using this optimized YPD medium. Since the growth and metabolism of S. cerevisiae is an aerobic fermentation process, dissolved oxygen is an important factor that affects the growth of the microorganism and the expression level of their proteins [28]. Under shake-flask conditions, to ensure the highest dissolved oxygen content in the yeast fermentation broth, the volume of liquid in the shake flask should be as small as possible, keeping the liquid surface in a thinner state. When the culture volume decreased from 150 mL to 20 mL, the titer of 3β-O-Glc-DM significantly increased (Fig. 4A). However, the culture volumes of 50 mL and 20 mL had a negligible impact on titer (393 mg/L and 394 mg/L, respectively), possibly because the 50 mL and 20 mL culture liquids were placed in 250 mL and 100 mL conical flasks, respectively, with little difference in the thickness of the liquid surface. Interestingly, although the 100/500 mL condition maintained the same 1/5 volume ratio, its titer was lower than those of the other two conditions. Since all flasks used in this study shared the same geometry, this decrease was likely due to the lower oxygen mass transfer efficiency (kLa) in the larger flask, where the liquid film is relatively thicker during shaking compared with smaller flasks. When examining the optimal feed solution amount, we observed that the titer of 3β-O-Glc-DM initially increased and then decreased with the reduction of the feed volume. The highest titer of 3β-O-Glc-DM, 396 mg/L, was achieved with 1 mL feed solution amount at 48, 72, 96, 120 h (Fig. 4B). This could be attributed to the fact that an excessive one-time addition of glucose leads to ethanol production due to overflow metabolism, which disrupts the normal metabolism of the yeast.

Optimization of key fermentation parameters for increased 3β-O-Glc-DM titer. A The effect of cultivation volume on 3β-O-Glc-DM titer. B The effect of feed solution amount on 3β-O-Glc-DM titer. C The effect of cultivation temperature on 3β-O-Glc-DM titer. D The effect of cultivation time on 3β-O-Glc-DM titer. E Fed-batch fermentation of the engineered strain Y13-A9 in a 3-L bioreactor.

The ideal growth temperature for S. cerevisiae is 28–33 °C [29]. Previous studies typically used 30 °C, but Qin [30] discovered that lower temperatures (22 °C) can greatly boost terpenoid production in yeast, possibly due to upregulation of lipid homeostasis genes and cell wall maintenance genes. This study tested temperatures from 22 °C to 32 °C and found that 28 °C was optimal for maximizing 3β-O-Glc-DM titer in the engineered yeast (Fig. 4C), reaching a titer of 425 mg/L. Ultimately, we investigated the effect of cultivation duration on 3β-O-Glc-DM titer and determined that neither shortening nor extending the cultivation period increased the titer, indicating that a 144 h fermentation period is optimal for 3β-O-Glc-DM production (Fig. 4D).

By optimizing the culture conditions (50 mL/250 mL working volume, 1 mL feed solution, 28 °C and 144 h fermentation), the titer of 3β-O-Glc-DM was increased to 425 mg/L at the shake-flask level, laying the foundation for subsequent fed-batch fermentation.

Fed-batch fermentation for increasing the production of 3β-O-Glc-DM

In order to achieve higher production of 3β-O-Glc-DM, we chose the engineered strains Y13-A9 for fed-batch fermentation using a feedback-controlled feeding method which was performed in a 3-L bioreactor with the medium pH controlled at 5.5. In the fermentation broth, the concentrations of ethanol and glucose were maintained below 20.0 g/L and 5.0 g/L, respectively. The biomass of the engineered strain continued to increase until 120 h, after which it began to slightly decline (Fig. 4E). Between 144 and 192 h, there was a gradual increase in biomass, with the OD600 of the fermentation broth reaching the highest value of 1349 at the time of harvest at 192 h (Fig. 4E). The titer of 3β-O-Glc-DM in the fermentation broth initially increased with the extension of fermentation time and then slowly decreased, remaining at a relatively low level throughout, with a titer of 202.4 mg/L in the fermentation broth at the time of harvest. Despite the antifoam treatment, the foam produced prior to its application caused persistent accumulation of microbial clumps on the bioreactor wall.

At the time of harvest at 192 h, the total volume of the fermentation broth was 1.05 L. Dry cells attached to the fermenter wall were collected, weighing 23.5 g (dry cell weight, DCW). The intracellular content of 3β-O-Glc-DM in these attached cells was 142.6 mg/g DCW, which corresponded to a volumetric equivalent of 3191.5 mg/L based on the culture volume (Fig. 4E). During the fermentation process, only a small portion of the product can be detected within the yeast cells, while the majority of the product adheres to the wall of the fermentation tank. This may be due to the ability of saponins to cause perturbations in the cell membrane [31]. In this study, the large amount of saponin compound 3β-O-Glc-DM produced by the engineered yeast may form complexes with sterols and create pores on the membrane, leading to the efflux of 3β-O-Glc-DM [32].

Therefore, the total titer of 3β-O-Glc-DM reached approximately 3.4 g/L. This represents an 8-fold increase over the titer (425 mg/L) achieved in shake flasks. The productivity was 0.0177 g/L/h and the yield was 0.00325 g/g glucose.

Discussion

In this study, we aimed to enhance the production of 3β-O-Glc-DM in the engineered yeast, which exhibits potent anti-colon cancer activity. Previous titers were insufficient for industrial-scale production, mainly due to the low catalytic efficiency of the key glycosyltransferase in the biosynthetic pathway. In this study, we utilized a multi-level engineering strategy to overcome the limitations in 3β-O-Glc-DM production. First, at the enzyme level, we employed semi-rational design to evolve the bottleneck glycosyltransferase PgUGT74AE2, obtaining a key mutant (K276A) with enhanced activity. Second, at the cellular level, we reconfigured the host metabolism to amplify the precursor supply. Finally, at the process level, we optimized fermentation parameters to maximize the output of the engineered strain. This integrated approach ultimately enabled a final 3β-O-Glc-DM titer of 3.4 g/L.

To improve the catalytic efficiency of PgUGT74AE2, we applied structure-guided engineering to it by targeting residues near the substrate entrance tunnel, a method that has been effective for other UGTs [33]. Alanine scanning identified K276 as an important residue. Replacing it with smaller amino acids such as Ala or Val greatly improved enzyme activity. Kinetic analysis showed that the K276A mutant had both higher substrate affinity, indicated by a lower Km, and a higher turnover rate (kcat), resulting in a 1.34-fold increase in catalytic efficiency. Molecular docking indicated that replacing the lysine with a smaller residue reduces steric hindrance, helps the substrate enter the active site and promotes hydrophobic interactions, thereby optimizing the catalytic environment. Our results demonstrate that engineering residues that guide substrate access can improve glycosyltransferase function, which is consistent with previous findings [25, 34].

In addition to enzyme improvement, increasing metabolic flux in the precursor pathway was essential for increasing the yields of the target products in the engineered strains. We engineered the chassis strain Y13 to enhance 2,3-oxidosqualene production, which successfully served as a foundation for 3β-O-Glc-DM synthesis. In this study, we were unable to directly detect 2,3-oxidosqualene because it is a low-abundance and unstable intermediate [35], which is likely due to its rapid conversion to downstream sterols. Although methods like GC-MS or derivatization could improve sensitivity, the potential degradation during extraction makes squalene a more reliable indicator for monitoring upstream metabolic flux. The introduction of the high-activity K276A mutant into this chassis effectively pulled flux towards the target product. This conclusion is supported by the lower accumulation of the intermediate DM and the higher yield of 3β-O-Glc-DM in the strain Y13-A9, relative to the previous reference strain Y8CSH. This approach of combining chassis optimization with downstream pathway enhancement has been proven to be a successful strategy for other terpenoids [13, 36].

Scaling up from shake flasks to a controlled bioreactor was crucial for achieving industrial production. We optimized culture conditions such as medium composition, dissolved oxygen, feeding strategy, and temperature. We found that a lower temperature of 28 °C was optimal, which is consistent with previous reports that suboptimal temperatures can improve terpenoid production in yeast. The reasons may include reduced metabolic burden, better protein folding, and adjusted membrane fluidity, which help reduce cytotoxicity [30, 37]. During fermentation in the bioreactor, no distinct plateau or decline phase was observed. This is likely because the low feed volume and high aeration rate in the later stage caused significant water evaporation, which concentrated the broth and may have masked the actual trends in cell growth and product formation. Moreover, we also observed that most of the product adhered to the bioreactor wall. This is likely because saponins such as 3β-O-Glc-DM have surfactant properties. They can interact with membrane sterols and promote efflux [31, 32]. The adhesion of the product to the bioreactor wall presents a major challenge for industrial production, as it can significantly reduce yield and process stability. Addressing this problem will likely require fermentation process optimization or the development of specialized recovery methods for future commercial-scale applications [38].

In general, we have developed a high-titer microbial platform for producing 3β-O-Glc-DM, achieving an unprecedented titer of 3.4 g/L in a 3-L bioreactor. The engineered strain and strategies developed here not only enable sustainable production of 3β-O-Glc-DM for further pharmacological studies, but also provide a useful reference for synthesizing other valuable glycosylated natural products in microbial cell factories.

Conclusion

This study aimed to develop an engineered strain for high-titer production of the unnatural ginsenoside 3β-O-Glc-DM. By employing protein engineering to modify the glycosyltransferase PgUGT74AE2, mutant K276A with the highest catalytic activity was obtained. Subsequently, using synthetic biology techniques, the genes of the mutant K276A and DS were simultaneously introduced into the optimized chassis strain with enhanced 2,3-oxidosqualene production, yielding the strain Y13-A9 for efficient 3β-O-Glc-DM biosynthesis. Fermentation condition optimization of the strain Y13-A9 at the shake-flask level resulted in a 3β-O-Glc-DM titer of 425 mg/L. Fed-batch fermentation of the strain Y13-A9 in a 3-L bioreactor achieved a 3β-O-Glc-DM titer of 3.4 g/L, which is a 42% increase in 3β-O-Glc-DM production compared with the previously constructed engineered strain Y8CSH (2.4 g/L) [10]. This study significantly increased the production of 3β-O-Glc-DM to the highest titer reported to date, laying a foundation for its industrial-scale production and the development of new anti-colon cancer drugs.
