Bundgaard Syndrome as a Rare Cause of Diffuse ST-Segment Depression
Authors: Ferdi Ekinci, Metin Okşul, Yusuf Ziya Şener, Serdar Söner, Sacide Yekta Öztoprak, Hamza Polat, Erkan Baysal, Henning Bundgaard
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFamilial ST-segment depression syndrome (Bundgaard syndrome) is a recently defined, dominant inherited heart disease characterized by widespread nonischemic ST-segment depression and an increased risk of atrial and ventricular arrhythmias, heart failure, and sudden cardiac death (SCD).Case SummaryA 41-year-old male patient presented with chest pain and generalized ST-segment depression (with aVR elevation) and showed slowing of left anterior descending artery flow on angiography. Normal scintigraphy, family history of SCD, and stress test findings supported the diagnosis of Bundgaard syndrome. The patient was treated conservatively without implantable cardioverter-defibrillator implantation, and regular follow-up was planned.DiscussionEstablishment of the diagnosis and family screening is crucial to offer early treatments of arrhythmias and heart failure to improve outcomes and prevent SCD.Take-Home MessageBundgaard syndrome, which can be a precursor to malignant arrhythmias, should be kept in mind in patients with persistent widespread ST-segment depression without ischemia.

History of Presentation

A 41-year-old man presented to the emergency department with complaints of dizziness, fainting spells, and chest pain. A detailed history revealed that the chest pain was constricting and lasted for an hour. The patient could not pinpoint the exact location of the pain, describing it as radiating to a wider area and accompanied by fainting spells. Vital signs were within normal limits, and physical examination revealed no abnormalities.Take-Home Message•Bundgaard syndrome, which can be a precursor to malignant arrhythmias, should be kept in mind in patients with persistent widespread ST-segment depression without ischemia.

Past Medical History and Predispositions

His past medical history was unremarkable. Importantly, the patient did not have a prior history of diabetes mellitus, hypertension, or any other recognized cardiovascular risk factors that are typically associated with an increased predisposition to atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease. His family history revealed sudden cardiac death (SCD) in both his 70-year-old maternal grandmother and his 50-year-old paternal great-aunt. Furthermore, his 39-year-old sister, who had undergone supraventricular tachycardia ablation, exhibited similar ST-segment depression, whereas his 46-year-old sister presented with hydrocephalus, bradykinesia, and cognitive impairment alongside ST-segment depression. His father and 4-year-old daughter also exhibited mild ST-segment depression.

Investigations

Electrocardiography (ECG) demonstrated incomplete right bundle branch block, widespread concave ST-segment depression in leads I, II, III, aVF, and in V2-V6, and ST-segment elevation in aVR (Figure 1), with normal cardiac axis and sinus rhythm. Echocardiography revealed a left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) of 55%. Management

Given the presumptive diagnosis of unstable angina pectoris, coronary angiography was performed, revealing an atherosclerotic plaque in the proximal left anterior descending artery and slow coronary flow in the left anterior descending artery, without any significant stenosis (Figure 3). A working diagnosis of microvascular angina was established, and anti-ischemic pharmacotherapy was initiated, comprising diltiazem 90 mg once daily, acetylsalicylic acid 100 mg once daily, ranolazine 375 mg twice daily, and atorvastatin 20 mg once daily. The patient's ECG abnormalities persisted during the hospitalization. In light of the persistent ECG changes in the index case and the family history, Bundgaard syndrome was also considered. The patient was discharged with close follow-up.Figure 3Patient's Coronary Angiography Image(A) The plaque arrow sign in the proximal left anterior descending artery is shown. Outcomes and Follow-Up

At the 1-month follow-up, symptoms persisted despite full adherence to treatment. Myocardial perfusion scintigraphy, performed after a 1-week withdrawal period from anti-ischemic therapy, excluded ischemia and provided further support for the diagnosis of familial ST-segment depression syndrome (FSTD). Exercise stress testing revealed deepening of ST-segment depression and increased ST-segment elevation in lead aVR during exertion, consistent with Bundgaard syndrome (Figure 4). Cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) demonstrated diffuse but mild (including <10% of the heart) myocardial edema (predominantly in the septal and apical regions) and patchy late gadolinium enhancement (LGE) involving the septal wall, inferior right ventricular insertion, and basal inferolateral wall (Figure 5). Moreover, mitral annular disjunction (MAD) was also detected. CMR revealed a borderline preserved LVEF (54%), a reduced stroke volume (66.7 mL), and a borderline low cardiac index (2.7 L/dk/m2). Family Screening

His 39-year-old sister, who had undergone supraventricular tachycardia ablation, exhibited similar ST-segment depression, whereas his 46-year-old sister presented with hydrocephalus, bradykinesia, and cognitive impairment alongside ST-segment depression. His father and 4-year-old daughter also exhibited mild ST-segment depression (Figure 6). In addition, the patient's grandmother experienced SCD at the age of 72 years, and his grandmother's sister experienced SCD at the age of 50 years. CMR of the patient's father similarly revealed myocardial edema and patchy LGE. The patient's father, both sisters, and daughter at the age of 4 years fulfilled the diagnostic criteria for Bundgaard syndrome.Figure 6Pedigree of the FamilyThe pedigree is in agreement with dominant inheritance.

Diagnostic Summary and Further Management

A definitive diagnosis of Bundgaard syndrome was established according to the criteria of Bundgaard et al.1 Given the absence of syncope, low LGE burden (<10%), and unremarkable Holter findings, an implantable cardioverter-defibrillator (ICD) implantation was not considered. Diltiazem and ranolazine were discontinued, and the patient was scheduled for biannual follow-up to identify new symptoms and to detect arrhythmia and changes in myocardial function.

Discussion

FSTD, also referred to as Bundgaard syndrome, is a recently described autosomal dominant inherited cardiac disorder characterized by distinctive ECG features, including persistent diffuse concave ST-segment depression with concomitant ST-segment elevation in lead aVR, occurring in the absence of any underlying structural heart disease.2 The index case was reported in Denmark in 2018, with additional cases reported in the Netherlands and the United Kingdom. An analysis of the Danish nationwide ECG database, encompassing approximately 12 million ECG recordings, estimated the prevalence of FSTD to range between 3 and 9 cases per 100,000 individuals.3 FSTD has been linked to impaired systolic function and appears to confer an increased risk of both arrhythmic events and SCD.4 A set of diagnostic criteria for FSTD has recently been proposed. Atrial fibrillation and other supraventricular tachyarrhythmias represent the most frequently reported arrhythmias. Atrial fibrillation typically occurs at around 50 years of age and, unlike in other cardiac conditions, is usually not linked to left atrial enlargement or left ventricular systolic dysfunction. Therapeutic response to both pharmacological therapy and catheter ablation appears similar to that observed in the general population.4 The most common type of ventricular arrhythmia is polymorphic ventricular tachycardia. Interestingly, the QTc interval is not prolonged, and the electrophysiological studies suggest the basal segment of the left ventricle as the culprit arrhythmic substrate in FSTD.4 The coupling interval between the initiating PVC and polymorphic ventricular tachycardia has been reported to be relatively short, ranging between 300 and 360 milliseconds.1 The risk of SCD is increased in FSTD, and its prevalence has been reported to be 9% to 13%, with a predominance in male patients.1,5 In contrast to other inherited arrhythmia syndromes, such as Brugada syndrome or long QT syndrome, no distinct arrhythmic triggers have been identified in FSTD.4 SCD risk stratification and decision regarding ICD implantation remain challenging because of the absence of certain established criteria. Our patient did not experience any palpitations, and 24-hour Holter monitoring revealed a negligible PVC burden (217 [0.2%]). Echocardiography showed normal left atrial size, and both echocardiography and CMR confirmed that the LVEF was 55%. Despite a family history of SCD and the presence of MAD, ICD implantation was deferred because of the low LGE burden (<10%), the absence of syncope, documented malignant arrhythmia, and lack of significant arrhythmic events on Holter monitoring. Instead, a strategy of close clinical follow-up was adopted to monitor risk over time.

Left ventricular systolic dysfunction represents another manifestation of FSTD. A recent study revealed that the mean LVEF was approximately 50% in patients with FSTD, and 8% of the cases had LVEF values lower than 30%. Left ventricular systolic dysfunction was shown to be more pronounced in males compared with females.1 CMR serves as the reference method for LVEF quantification and further enables SCD risk stratification through assessment of LGE burden.6 LGE was reported in only 1 male patient with FSTD, and it was localized to the inferolateral wall.1 Therefore, CMR might be a useful tool in the assessment of both LVEF and SCD risk in FSTD. In our case, CMR revealed a borderline preserved LVEF, a reduced stroke volume, and a low cardiac index. Furthermore, the presence of LGE and myocardial edema in our patient, along with widespread patchy LGE observed in his father's CMR, supports the limited evidence available in the literature. Beyond LVEF measurement and LGE assessment, CMR may provide data about accompanying structural heart diseases in FSTD. For example, a secundum-type atrial septal defect was previously reported in 1 patient with FSTD.1 We also established the accompanying MAD without associated mitral valve prolapse in our case, which was not previously described in this syndrome. The recognition of MAD in this context is clinically relevant, given its established association with an increased risk of SCD.7

An autosomal dominant pattern of inheritance is established for FSTD. A recent genetic study conducted by Christensen et al8 revealed that a pathogenic variant of the KCNB1 (potassium voltage-gated channel subfamily B member 1) gene located on chromosome 20 formed a perfect cosegregation in 67 patients from 20 families. The promoter interaction of this gene was confirmed. Two different characteristics of the KCNB1 pathogenic variants, loss-of-function and gain-of-function (GOF), have been considered. The KCNB1 gene is one of the genes encoding voltage-dependent potassium channels.9 GOF variants of the KCNB1 gene can cause hyperactivity in potassium channels, thereby triggering abnormal repolarization processes.10 This mechanism provides a potential pathophysiological basis for FSTD, which is characterized by diffuse and nonischemic ST-segment depression. Some studies in the literature suggest that KCNB1 GOF variants are associated with hydrocephalus, neurodevelopmental delay, and Parkinsonism.9, 10, 11 In our case, the proband's father, 2 sisters, and daughter exhibited electrocardiograms fulfilling diagnostic criteria, reinforcing the autosomal dominant inheritance pattern. Furthermore, the presence of intellectual disability, hydrocephalus, Parkinsonism, and bradykinesia in the proband's 46-year-old sister, when considered alongside her diagnostic ECG findings, raises the possibility of a broader syndromic association between FSTD and KCNB1-related neurological phenotypes.

Conclusions

FSTD is a rare autosomal dominant inherited cardiac disorder characterized by persistent, diffuse ST-segment depression that mimics ECG changes seen in ischemic heart disease. Early recognition is crucial because the condition has been linked to left ventricular systolic dysfunction, arrhythmias, and an increased risk of SCD. Systematic family screening should be pursued to identify asymptomatic cases. Further genetic and clinical studies are warranted to elucidate the underlying pathogenic mechanism, refine SCD risk estimation, and establish evidence-based management strategies.
