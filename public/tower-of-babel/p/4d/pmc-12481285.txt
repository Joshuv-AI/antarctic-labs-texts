Ilimaquinone-induced lipophagy diminishes lipid accumulation via AMPK activation
Authors: Eun-Sun Yang, Se-Yun Cheon, Ji Yeong Park, Yeseul Park, Wonyoung Park, Snehal Chandrakant Meshram, Yunju Jo, Sung-Jin Bae, Dongryeol Ryu, Taekyung Kim, Ki-Tae Ha
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Lipid metabolism plays an important role in aging and longevity, and lipophagy—a specialized form of autophagy that targets lipid vesicles—regulates lipid homeostasis and alleviates metabolic diseases such as metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD). Ilimaquinone (IQ), a sesquiterpene extracted from the sea, is well-known for its various biological effects; however, its effects on lipid metabolism and longevity have not yet been elucidated. In this study, IQ acted in a dose-dependent manner, extending the lifespan of Caenorhabditis elegans (C. elegans) by up to 50%, causing transcriptional changes in 1,878 genes related to fatty acid degradation and longevity pathways. Additionally, IQ reduced lipid accumulation in C. elegans and mouse AML12 cells, as confirmed by Oil Red O staining. RNA sequencing and quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction validation showed that the expression of key lipid metabolism genes, such as lipl-4 in worms and Lipa in mammalian cells, increased with IQ treatment. Lipophagy has been identified as the key mechanism underlying the lipid-lowering effects of IQ. The inhibition of autophagy by Bafilomycin A1 reversed the reduction in lipid accumulation in both C. elegans and AML12 cells, indicating the involvement of autophagic flux. Western blot analysis demonstrated that IQ activates AMPK, a key regulator of autophagy and lipid metabolism, and inhibits mTOR. IQ increased the turnover of LC3-II and decreased p62 levels, confirming autophagosome formations and increased lysosomal degradation. These findings suggest that IQ promotes autophagy, alleviates lipid accumulation, and has a therapeutic potential for metabolic diseases. In addition, AMPK activation and mTOR inhibition pathways may have contributed to the extension of C. elegans lifespan. Future studies should investigate the potential of IQ in lipid metabolism regulation and lifespan extension.

INTRODUCTION

Lipid metabolism plays an important role in regulating the aging and longevity of various organisms (1, 2). Diverse genetic pathways regulating lifespan in model organisms are closely associated with lipid metabolism and signaling, with lipid metabolic changes and signaling molecules playing significant roles in aging and health span (2). The processes of breaking down lipids into free fatty acids and glycerol, lipolysis and lipophagy, are key steps in the metabolism of lipids, especially through β-oxidation, which oxidizes fatty acids to generate energy and reduce lipid levels in the body (3). Lipophagy, a specific form of autophagy of lipid droplets, is crucial for maintaining lipid balance and extending lifespan (4) and plays a role in various age-related diseases, such as neurodegenerative disorders, atherosclerosis, type II diabetes, and metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) (5, 6). Enhanced autophagy, particularly lipophagy, supports cellular maintenance and longevity by promoting lipid catabolism and mitigating oxidative stress and maintaining cellular homeostasis (6). Thus, autophagy-related lipolysis is considered a possible therapeutic target for several metabolic diseases including MASLD (7, 8).

Ilimaquinone (IQ), a marine sponge-derived sesquiterpene, possesses various biological actions, including antibacterial (9), anti-viral (10), anti-inflammatory (11), and anti-cancer properties (12). Phospholipase D was initially identified as an inhibitor of protein transport through the Golgi apparatus (13), and it has been reported to be required for Golgi membrane fragmentation (14). Research on the anticancer action of IQ has suggested molecular pathways such as expression of the ER stress-associated protein GADD153 (15), increased p53 (16), induction of mitochondrial-mediated apoptosis (12), and increased autophagy (17). In addition, a recent study has shown that IQ effectively reduces ocular neovascularization by targeting the p53 and Wnt/β-catenin pathways in age-related macular degeneration model (18). However, to date, there is no evidence that IQ modulates lipid metabolism or lifespan.

In this study, using the C. elegans model, we found that IQ plays a significant role in extending lifespan. RNA sequencing analysis performed to determine the key pathways-involved in prolonged longevity suggested that lipid metabolism contributes to lifespan extension. We determined whether IQ could inhibit lipid accumulation in C. elegans and mammalian hepatocytes and investigated the underlying molecular mechanisms.

RESULTS

RNA sequencing analysis reveals that IQ alters several genes in C. elegans

To investigate the effect of IQ (Fig. 1A) on aging in C. elegans, we assessed the lifespan of worms treated with 1-100 μM of IQ. The results demonstrated that IQ extended the lifespan of C. elegans in a dose-dependent manner, with approximately a 50% increase in the median lifespan observed at 100 μM (Fig. 1B). To elucidate the mechanism underlying IQ’s lifespan-prolonging effects, we analyzed the transcriptional response to 100 μM of IQ. RNA sequencing analysis revealed that 1,878 genes exhibited more than a 2-fold change in expression (Fig. 1C, D). GSEA and KEGG pathway analysis revealed that IQ influences gene sets involved in the longevity-regulating pathway and fatty acid degradation (Fig. 1E, F, and Supplementary Fig. 1C-F).

IQ reduces the lipid accumulation in C. elegans

To verify the RNA sequencing results, a heat map of the genes involved in lipid metabolism was constructed (Fig. 2A), and the expression of 12 genes with high statistical significance was confirmed using qRT-PCR. Among these, ilpl-8, ilpl-4, and cpt-4 were expressed at higher levels following with IQ treatment, whereas three genes (acox-1.2, rme-2, and fat-7) were expressed at lower levels (Fig. 2B). Meanwhile, in the case of ech1.1, egg-1, egg-2, and acdh-8, mRNA expression was increased by IQ treatment, unlike the heatmap analysis. For clec-121 and elo-7, no significant differences were detected (Supplementary Fig. 2). To investigate whether IQ affects lipid degradation, we treated worms with 100 μM of IQ and stained them with Oil Red O (Fig. 2C). The intensity of the Oil Red O staining was approximately 60% lower, indicating that IQ treatment reduced lipid accumulation in C. elegans (Fig. 2D). This result is consistent with the bioinformatics analysis of the RNA sequencing data, suggesting that IQ has a significant effect on lipid metabolism. In addition, these results showed that the reliability of transcriptome changes was strongly supported by validation experiments.

IQ reduces the lipid generation in AML12 cells

When a non-toxic dose of PA and IQ was applied to AML12 cells (Supplementary Fig. 3), IQ dose-dependently reduced the PA-induced accumulation of lipid droplets in Oil Red O staining (Fig. 3A, B). In experiments using a neutral lipid dye to stain the lipid droplets, IQ significantly reduced the increase in PA-induced lipid droplets (Fig. 3C, D). We also investigated whether IQ affected the expression of homologous genes related to lipid metabolism, as confirmed in a previous C. elegans experiment. The expression of Lipa and Cpt1b, which was reduced by PA treatment, was significantly increased by IQ (Fig. 3E). In addition, there was no significant change in the expression of Acox1, Lrp4, and Scd-1 genes by PA treatment, but there was a significant decrease after IQ treatment. PA treatment reduced the expression of Lipf, a gene encoding a key enzyme in cytoplasmic lipolysis, which was not significantly affected by IQ. These results indicate that IQ reduces lipid accumulation in mammalian cells.

Lipophagy is involved in the suppression of fat accumulation by IQ

Lipa, a homolog of lipl-4, is a lipase acting in the acidic conditions of the lysosome and has been reported to play a key role in longevity through lipophagy (19, 20). Therefore, we investigated whether lipophagy is involved in the reduction of lipid accumulation by IQ. When worms were treated with Bafilomycin A1, an inhibitor of vacuolar-type H＋-ATPase (V-ATPase), which blocks autophagic flux, the reduction in lipid accumulation observed in worms treated with IQ was reversed (Fig. 4A, B). We also confirmed whether autophagy was involved in the inhibition of lipid accumulation by IQ in mammalian cells. As a result, the lipid droplets inhibited by IQ were restored to a level comparable to that of PA alone after treatment with Bafilomycin A1 (Fig. 4C, D). This was confirmed using western blotting to determine whether IQ affected autophagic flux. The protein levels of LC3 and p62, which are representative markers of autophagy, accumulated by PA were reduced by IQ. When autophagic flux was inhibited by Bafilomycin A1 treatment, autophagosomes and p62 accumulated without degradation (Fig. 4E). These results suggest that the induction of lipolysis by IQ is mediated by autophagic flux.

The effects of IQ on AMPK and mTOR, major autophagy-related pathways, were confirmed by western blotting. IQ increased the phosphorylation of AMPK and decreased the phosphorylation of mTOR, even in the presence of PA (Fig. 4F). Time-dependent changes in protein levels after IQ treatment were examined. The results showed that IQ increased AMPK phosphorylation and decreased mTOR phosphorylation. Conversion of LC3-I to LC3-II increased in a time-dependent manner, whereas that of p62 decreased (Fig. 4G). To determine whether AMPK is an important mediator, we examined IQ-induced autophagic flux and lipid accumulation in the presence of compound C, an AMPK inhibitor. Treatment with compound C did not increase IQ-induced AMPK phosphorylation; LC3 and p62 levels, which were reduced by IQ, were restored (Fig. 4H). In addition, the lipid droplets inhibited by IQ were restored to a level comparable to that induced by PA alone after treatment with compound C (Fig. 4I, J and Supplementary Fig. 4). These results suggest that autophagy is regulated through the activation of AMPK following IQ treatment.

DISCUSSION

This study revealed that IQ treatment effectively inhibited lipid accumulation in C. elegans and mouse hepatocytes. Transcriptome analysis revealed that this effect was associated with the upregulation of Lipa expression, also known as lipl-4 in worms. Upregulation of Lipa, also known as lysosomal acid lipase, contributes to the reduction in lipid accumulation in various diseases, including MASLD (21, 22). Previous studies showed that Lipa plays an important role in lipid metabolism by hydrolyzing triglycerides and cholesterol esters into free fatty acids and cholesterol, which are essential for cellular lipid homeostasis (23). Lipa is also a lipase that works in the acidic environment of lysosomes and is closely related to lipophagy, which is a process of autophagy-related lipid degradation (24). Indeed, treatment with Bafilomycin A1, a well-known inhibitor of V-ATPase that prevents the fusion of autophagosomes with lysosomes and consequently inhibits autophagy (25), reversed the inhibitory effect of IQ on lipid accumulation in both C. elegans and mouse hepatocytes, suggesting that the autophagy pathway plays an important role in the underlying mechanism of IQ action. In contrast, the expression of Lipf, which is another homolog of worm lipl-4 and plays an important role in cytoplasmic lipolysis (20, 22), was not increased by IQ in murine hepatocytes. Therefore, lipophagy may be the key pathway underlying the suppression of fat accumulation by IQ.

The results of the effect on markers of autophagy showed that IQ treatment activates autophagy in hepatocytes, as confirmed by the effect on LC3 and p62 protein levels. PA treatment, which is known to cause lipid accumulation and autophagic flux inhibition (26), increased LC3-II and p62 levels. IQ treatment reversed the effects of PA by reducing the LC3-II and p62 protein levels. These observations are consistent with the findings of previous studies showing that PA inhibits autophagy and promotes the accumulation of autophagosomes and their carriers, including p62 (27). In addition, LC3-I conversion to LC3-II increased over time with IQ treatment alone, which is characteristic of autophagosome formation (28, 29). At the same time, p62 levels decreased, suggesting that IQ not only promotes autophagosome formation, but also autophagosome-lysosome fusion and subsequent cargo degradation (30). This indicates that IQ restores and enhances autophagic flow, thereby counteracting the inhibitory effect of palmitate on autophagy and inhibiting lipid accumulation. The role of autophagy in lipid metabolism is well known, and effective autophagy is essential for the removal of lipid droplets and maintenance of cellular homeostasis (20). Our data suggests that IQ promotes lipophagy by reducing p62 accumulation and increasing LC3-II turnover. This mechanism may underlie the reduction in lipid accumulation observed in hepatocytes following IQ treatment and may be critical in ameliorating MASLD caused by the accumulation of saturated fatty acids such as PA.

Previous studies have reported that IQ promotes autophagy in several cancers (16, 17). However, the precise molecular mechanism underlying this effect has not yet been elucidated. This study confirmed that IQ induced AMPK activation and mTOR inhibition. These two factors are important regulators of autophagy and lipid metabolism (31). AMPK is a key energy sensor that activates catabolic pathways, including autophagy, to restore the cellular energy balance. Moreover, AMPK activation upregulates Lipa expression (31). Our data show that IQ activates AMPK and suggest that AMPK may be an important upstream regulator of enhanced autophagy and Lipa upregulation mediated by IQ. In addition, this activation may promote the degradation of lipid droplets through lipophagy, which targets lipid droplets for lysosomal degradation. In addition to activating AMPK, IQ treatment inhibited mTOR, a well-known negative regulator of autophagy. Inhibition of mTOR leads to the activation of autophagy, which promotes the formation of autophagosomes and their fusion with lysosomes (32, 33). Therefore, in this study, we propose the combination of AMPK activation and mTOR inhibition as a key molecular pathway by which IQ promotes lipophagy in hepatocytes. Our findings provide a comprehensive understanding of the mechanisms by which IQ regulates lipid metabolism and highlight the potential therapeutic benefits of IQ in metabolic disorders characterized by lipid accumulation, such as MASLD. Future studies should aim to fully elucidate the signaling pathways involved by exploring the downstream targets of AMPK and mTOR in IQ treated cells.

Previous studies have found that lipophagy not only inhibits MASLD by regulating lipid metabolism but also promotes longevity independently by enhancing cellular homeostasis and reducing metabolic stress (2, 34). The present study revealed that IQ treatment significantly extended the lifespan of C. elegans. It appears that IQ promotes autophagy and lipophagy, which reduces lipid accumulation, but it is not yet clear whether these effects directly affect the lifespan extension observed. The relationship between lipid metabolism and longevity is complex and may involve several interconnected pathways, including energy balance, oxidative stress reduction, and protein homeostasis improvement (1, 35). However, since AMPK activation and mTOR inhibition is a well-known pathway that promotes longevity (36, 37), it is possible that it contributes to extending the lifespan of C. elegans. AMPK activation promotes longevity by enhancing autophagy and mitochondrial function, and mTOR inhibition is a well-known mechanism that extends the lifespan in various species (37). However, the role of lipid metabolism in this pathway and its contribution to the observed lifespan extension requires further study. Therefore, in the future, we plan to conduct further studies in C. elegans and mouse models to determine whether the effect of IQ on lifespan extension is mainly determined by lipophagy and lipid removal, or by other metabolic and signaling pathways. The use of genetic or pharmacological tools that specifically target genes related to lipophagy and lipid metabolism may help clarify the causal relationship between the life-extending and lipid-regulating effects of IQ.

In this study, we found that IQ activated AMPK and inhibited a downstream target mTOR, which regulates autophagy (36). This is the first report to suggest that IQ enhances autophagy by activating AMPK and inhibiting mTOR. However, it should be noted that the activation of AMPK is controlled by various upstream signaling pathways, and the exact mechanism by which IQ activates AMPK remains unclear. AMPK activation can be influenced by factors such as SIRT1, LKB1, or TSC2, which are involved in sensing the cellular energy status or stress response (38). Thus, these pathways may mediate AMPK activation in response to IQ therapy. Therefore, further studies focusing on the upstream pathways involved in AMPK activation are required to identify the exact target of IQ-activated AMPK. Understanding the molecular mechanism by which IQ activates AMPK will provide insights into the regulation of lipid metabolism and autophagy. Furthermore, exploring how IQ interacts with other metabolic and signaling pathways other than AMPK-mTOR is essential to fully understand its impact on lipid metabolism and its potential therapeutic applications. These findings may help to clarify the role of IQ in the treatment of metabolic and age-related diseases.

In summary, this study shows that IQ effectively reduces lipid accumulation in both C. elegans and mouse hepatocytes through lipophagy, a process involving lipolysis associated with autophagy. IQ treatment increases the expression of key lipid metabolism genes, such as Lipa, which breaks down neutral lipids and cholesterol by lipophagy in lysosomes. The involvement of lipophagy was confirmed by the reversal of the lipid-lowering effect of IQ following the inhibition of autophagy flux using bafilomycin A1, suggesting that autophagy is a central mechanism of IQ action. In addition, IQ promoted the formation of autophagosomes and lipid degradation by activating AMPK, a key regulator of autophagy and lipid metabolism, and by inhibiting mTOR. These findings highlight the potential of IQ as a treatment for metabolic diseases such as MASLD. IQ treatment significantly extended the lifespan of C. elegans, possibly owing to the combined effects of enhanced autophagy and reduced metabolic stress. Further research is needed to fully elucidate the relationship between the relevant signaling pathways, lipid metabolism, and longevity to better understand the therapeutic implications of IQ for metabolic diseases and aging.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Materials

PA was prepared by conjugation of sodium palmitate (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA, P9767) with fatty acid-free bovine serum albumin (BSA, Sigma, A7030) in a 6:1 molar ratio at 37°C. The stock solution of PA was composed of 2 mM palmitate and 0.34 mM BSA in 150 mM sodium chloride (pH 7.4) and was filtered, aliquoted, stored at −20°C, and further diluted to obtain the desired final concentrations. All other chemicals, including dexamethasone, dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS), Tween 20, 3-(4,5-dimethyl-thiazole-2-yl)2,5-diphenyl tetrazolium bromide (MTT) and Oil Red O were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Bafilomycin A1, an autophagy inhibitor, was purchased from Cayman Chemical (Ann Arbor, MI, USA). Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM)/F-12 medium, 1× phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; pH 7.4), and 0.25% trypsin-ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) were purchased from Gibco (Rockville, MD, USA). Fetal bovine serum (FBS), penicillin/streptomycin and insulin-transferrin-selenium (ITS) were purchased from Gibco. The cytoplasmic microtubule inhibitor IQ was obtained from Abcam Chemical (Cambridge, UK). BODIPY 493/503 was purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Waltham, MA, USA).

Nematode and cell cultures

C. elegans was maintained according to standard protocols. C. elegans N2 strain was maintained at 20°C on NGM plates seeded with E. coli OP50. Five L4-stage worms were picked on a 60-mm seeded NGM plate. After four days, the worms were collected and bleached in liquid for synchronization. Synchronized worms were maintained in glass tubes containing M9 buffer at 20°C until the L4 stage. At the L4 stage, 1 ml of complete S-Basal buffer containing 200 μM FUDR, 100 μg/ml ampicillin, and 100 μM IQ was added. Heat-shocked E. coli OP50 (50 μl) was added to each tube every 2-3 d.

AML12 cells, mouse normal hepatocytes, were purchased from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, CRL-2254). AML12 cells were maintained in DMEM/F-12 (1:1) medium supplemented with 10% FBS, 1% P/S, 40 ng/ml dexamethasone, and ITS supplement (final concentration: 0.01 mg/ml insulin, 0.0055 mg/ml transferrin, and 6.7 ng/ml selenium) in a 5% CO2 incubator at 37°C under 98% humidified conditions.

Lifespan analysis

C. elegans N2 strain was maintained at 20°C using standard nematode growth medium plated seeded on with E. coli OP50. Five L4 stage worms were picked onto a large-seeded NGM plate. After 4 d, the worms were collected and bleached in liquid for synchronization. Synchronized L4 stages worms were transferred to a 96-well plate, with 130 μl of complete S-Basal buffer containing 200 μM of FUDR, 100 μg/ml of ampicillin, and 1-100 μM of IQ. Heat-shocked OP50 (4 μl) was added to each well every 2-3 d. Five worms were added to each well and 60 worms were used per condition to monitor the survival rate. Survival was monitored daily by tapping worms with a platinum wire. The percentage of survival was plotted, with each data point representing the average of all experiments.

RNA sequencing

For extraction of total RNA and determination of RNA quality, control and 100 μM IQ-incubated C. elegans samples were washed with ice-cold phosphate buffered saline and sent to the ROKIT Genomics Inc. (Seoul, Korea). The total RNA concentration was determined using Quant-IT RiboGreen (Invitrogen, Grand Island, NY, USA, #R11490). To assess the integrity of the total RNA, the samples were run on a TapeStation RNA Screentape (Agilent, #5067-5576). For the construction of the RNA library, only high-quality RNA preparations with an RIN greater than 7.0 were used. RNA sequencing library was prepared independently for each sample using 1 ug of total RNA using the Illumina TruSeq Stranded mRNA Sample Prep Kit (Illumina, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA, #20020595). The first step in the workflow is to purify the poly-A-containing mRNA molecules using poly-T-attached magnetic beads. After purification, mRNA is fragmented into small pieces using divalent cations at an elevated temperature. The cleaved RNA fragments are copied into first strand cDNA using SuperScript II reverse transcriptase (Invitrogen, #18064014) and random primers. Then, DNA Polymerase I, RNase H and dUTP are used to synthesize the second strand of cDNA. These cDNA fragments undergo an end repair process, the addition of a single ‘A’ base and subsequent ligation of the adapters. To generate the final cDNA library, the products are purified and enriched by PCR. The libraries were quantified using KAPA Library Quantification kits for Illumina Sequencing platforms according to the qPCR Quantification Protocol Guide (KAPA Biosystems, #KK4854) and qualified using a TapeStation D1000 ScreenTape (Agilent Technologies, # 5067-5582). Libraries were sequenced using a NovaSeq6000 system (Illumina Inc., San Diego, CA, USA) and paired-end sequencing (2 × 100 bp) was performed by Macrogen Inc.. All raw RNA-Seq data generated in this study have been deposited in Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) under the accession number GSE292447.

Data processing analysis

The paired-end sequencing reads were generated using an Illumina’s NovaSeq6000 sequencing platform. Prior to the analysis, Trimmomatic v0.38 was used to remove adapter sequences and trimmed bases with poor base quality. The cleaned reads were aligned with C. elegans (WBcel235) using HISAT v2.1.0. Reference genome sequence and gene annotation data were downloaded from NCBI Genome Assembly and the NCBI RefSeq database, respectively. The aligned data (SAM file format) were sorted and indexed using SAM tools v 1.9. The transcripts were assembled and quantified using StringTie v2.1.3b. Gene and transcript quantification was calculated as raw reads, FPKM (Fragments Per Kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads) and TPM (Transcripts Per Million).

Differentially expressed genes (DEG) analysis

Statistical analysis of differential gene expression was conducted using DESeq2 v 1.38.3, with raw counts as input. Genes with statistically significant differences in expression between the IQ and control groups were identified. Genes with non-zero counts in all samples were selected for QC. The statistical significance of the differentially expressed genes was determined using the DESeq2 nbinom Wald test (Supplementary Fig. 1A, B). The fold change and p-value were extracted from the Wald test results. To control for the false discovery rate (FDR), all p-values were adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg algorithm. Significant gene list was filtered by |fold change| ≥ 2 & raw P-value < 0.05. A heatmap of DEGs was generated using hierarchical clustering analysis to visualize the gene expression patterns across different samples. Hierarchical clustering was performed on the log-transformed values of significant genes using several parameters (distance metric = Euclidean distance, linkage method = complete). The expression values between the comparison samples were expressed in a volcano plot. Functional annotation analysis of significant genes was performed using an in-house KEGG Viewer script against the KEGG pathway database. Adjusted P-values for the KEGG results were derived using a two-sided corrected Fisher’s exact test and corrected using the Benjamini-Hochberg method. All data analyses, including the visualization of differentially expressed genes, were performed using R 3.5.1. (www.r-project.org).

KEGG pathway mapping

KEGG mapping analysis was performed to visualize genes that were significantly altered in the DEG analysis. These include the longevity regulation and fatty acid degradation pathways, which were significantly altered in the KEGG analysis. The map was visualized using the KEGG mapper color tool (https://www.genome.jp/kegg/mapper/color.html) in accordance with the latest reports (39, 40).

qRT-PCR analysis

AML12 cells treated with IQ for 24 h were collected after washing with PBS. C. elegans treated with IQ for 11 d were collected after washing with PBS. Total mRNA was isolated from AML12 cells using the TRIZOL reagent (Invitrogen) and quantified using a Nanodrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Total mRNA from C. elegans was isolated using a homogenizer containing the TRIZOL solution. An equal amount of RNA (1 μg) from each sample was used to synthesize cDNA, which was reverse transcribed using RevertAid reverse transcriptase (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Real-time qPCR was performed using a StepOnePlus real-time PCR system (Applied Biosystems, Thermo Fisher Scientific) with a RealHelix qPCR kit (NanoHelix, DJ, Korea). Relative mRNA levels were normalized to the actin levels. Primers used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table 1.

Staining of lipid droplets

After bleaching C. elegans N2 worms, they were cultured in liquid medium with 100 μM IQ for 11 d. Six hours before the experiment, half of the IQ-treated worms were additionally exposed to 16 μM bafilomycin A1. To prepare worms for staining, they were washed from the plates with M9 buffer and collected in a 1.5 ml Eppendorf tube. After sedimentation, the supernatant was removed, and the worms were fixed in 1 ml of 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min. The fixed worms were washed three times with M9 buffer and incubated for 30 min. Samples were subsequently dehydrated in 60% isopropanol for 15 min and stained with 500 μl of 60% Oil Red O solution for 30 min. Following staining, the Oil Red O solution containing worms was dispensed onto a non-seeded NGM plate using a glass pipette, allowing it to be absorbed into the plate. The worms were then transferred to a drop of M9 buffer on a microscope slide and imaged using a Nikon Eclipse Ts2R-FL microscope.

AML12 cells were seeded in 24-well plates and treated with various concentration of PA for 24h. Cells were washed with PBS and fixed in 3.7% formaldehyde for 20 min, followed by three washes with PBS. After removing PBS, 60% isopropanol was added for 5 min. Oil Red O working solution (60% Oil Red O with 40% DW) was then added and incubated for 10 min with rotation at room temperature. The cells were washed thrice with DW and observed under a microscope (Carl Zeiss Co., Germany). Lipid droplets were measured by BODIPY 493/503 staining. AML12 cells were harvested, washed with PBS, and fixed in 3.7% formaldehyde for 20 min. Cells were washed thrice with PBS and then stained with 2 μM BODIPY 493/503 for 15 min at 37°C. Finally, the cells were washed thrice with PBS and observed under a fluorescence microscope. Lipid production was quantified from the fluorescence images of each group using the ImageJ software (www.image.net/ij).

Western blot analysis

Cells were harvested and lysed in ice-cold 1% NP40 lysis buffer containing 150 mM NaCl, 10 mM HEPES (pH 7.45), 5 mM sodium pyrophosphate, 5 mM sodium fluoride, 2 mM sodium orthovanadate, and a protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche Applied Science, Mannheim, Germany). Whole cell extracts were obtained from the supernatants after centrifugation of the cell lysate at 13,000 × g for 15 min at 4°C. Protein concentration was quantified using the Bradford assay. Proteins (50 μg) were separated via SDS-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE; 8-13%) and transferred onto 0.45 μM nitrocellulose membranes (Amersham Bioscience, Uppsala, Sweden). The membranes were blocked for 60 min at room temperature using 5% (w/v) BD Difco skim milk powder (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and immunoblotted with primary antibodies at 4°C overnight at optimal dilution. After washing thrice with Tris-buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween 20, the membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies at a dilution of 1:2,000 for 1 h at room temperature. Immunoreactivity was detected using the EZ-Western Lumi Pico Alpha kit (Dogenbio, Seoul, Korea) and digitized using Image Quant LAS 4000 (GE Healthcare, Chicago, IL, USA).

Statistical analysis

All data were obtained from at least three independent experiments and are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation. Statistical differences were calculated using one-way analysis of variance with Dunnett’s test or an unpaired Student’s t-test. GraphPad Prism software (version 8.0) was used for all statistical analyses, and P < 0.05 was considered a significant difference.
