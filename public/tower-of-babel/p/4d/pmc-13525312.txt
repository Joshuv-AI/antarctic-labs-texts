Coats disease: A narrative review of clinical spectrum, imaging features, and contemporary management
Authors: Fawaz Almutairi, Ali Esmaeil, Nouf Al-Farsi, Aseel AlKandari, Alaa AlAli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCoats disease is a rare idiopathic retinal vascular disorder characterized by telangiectatic and aneurysmal retinal vessels, intraretinal and subretinal lipid exudation, and variable progression to exudative retinal detachment, secondary glaucoma, and severe visual loss. This narrative review summarizes the clinical spectrum, diagnostic evaluation, imaging features, and contemporary management of Coats disease, with emphasis on areas of diagnostic uncertainty and treatment controversy. The disease most commonly affects young males and is classically unilateral; however, rare bilateral presentations and Leber miliary aneurysms should be recognized within the broader spectrum of idiopathic retinal telangiectatic disease. Modern multimodal imaging, including wide-field photography, fluorescein angiography, optical coherence tomography, and optical coherence tomography angiography, has improved detection of peripheral nonperfusion, macular edema, subfoveal lipid, photoreceptor damage, and vascular abnormalities with prognostic implications. Management remains stage-dependent. Laser photocoagulation and cryotherapy remain the foundation of treatment, while anti-vascular endothelial growth factor therapy, corticosteroids, photodynamic therapy, and surgery have selective roles as adjunctive or advanced-stage interventions. The evidence base is limited by heterogeneous retrospective series, small case reports, and scarce comparative prospective data. Further multicenter studies are needed to define treatment algorithms, imaging biomarkers, long-term safety, and functional outcomes.Keywords: coats disease, leber miliary aneurysms, retinal telangiectasia, exudative retinopathy, optical coherence tomography angiography, anti-VEGF, cryotherapy, laser photocoagulation

Introduction

Coats disease is an idiopathic retinal vascular disorder characterized by retinal telangiectasia, aneurysmal vascular dilatation, and intraretinal or subretinal lipid-rich exudation in the absence of primary vitreoretinal traction. 1 George Coats first described the condition in 1908 as a unilateral retinal vascular abnormality associated with massive exudation, most often affecting young males. 2 Leber later described multiple retinal miliary aneurysms with retinal degeneration, and subsequent work by Reese clarified that Leber miliary aneurysms and Coats disease represent related manifestations along a spectrum of idiopathic retinal telangiectatic disease.3,4 The Shields classification, derived from a large clinical series, remains the most widely used staging framework and provides a practical link between anatomic severity, prognosis, and treatment selection.1,5

The estimated incidence of Coats disease is low, approximately 0.09 per 100,000 population, and most large clinical series demonstrate strong male predominance and unilateral presentation.6,7 Nevertheless, bilateral cases and bilateral Leber miliary aneurysms have been reported, emphasizing that bilateral disease should not automatically exclude Coats-spectrum disease, although it should prompt careful assessment for hereditary, syndromic, inflammatory, or neoplastic mimics.8–11 Current literature does not establish a consistent racial predilection; however, available data are influenced by referral patterns, publication bias, and limited population-based studies.6,7,12,13

Although diagnostic and therapeutic techniques have advanced, Coats disease remains challenging because the disease can range from subtle peripheral telangiectasia to advanced leukocoria, total exudative retinal detachment, and painful neovascular glaucoma. In children, the most important diagnostic challenge is differentiation from retinoblastoma, whereas in older children and adults the differential diagnosis includes familial exudative vitreoretinopathy, retinopathy of prematurity, ocular toxocariasis, persistent fetal vasculature, von Hippel-Lindau-related retinal capillary hemangioblastoma, inflammatory retinal disease, and Coats-like retinopathy associated with inherited retinal degeneration.14–18

This article is a narrative review of Coats disease. Its aim is to synthesize current knowledge on the clinical spectrum, pathophysiology, imaging features, differential diagnosis, staging, natural history, and contemporary management of Coats disease, while highlighting the limitations of the evidence base, current controversies, and future research priorities, while incorporating recent literature on advances in diagnosis and treatment. 12

Methods

This article was designed as a narrative review to provide a clinically oriented synthesis rather than a systematic review or meta-analysis.

A literature search was performed using PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, and Google Scholar. Search terms included combinations of: “Coats disease”, “Coats’ disease”, “Leber miliary aneurysms”, “retinal telangiectasia”, “exudative retinopathy”, “exudative retinal detachment”, “leukocoria”, “retinoblastoma differential diagnosis”, “wide-field fluorescein angiography”, “optical coherence tomography”, “optical coherence tomography angiography”, “anti-VEGF”, “bevacizumab”, “ranibizumab”, “corticosteroids”, “cryotherapy”, “laser photocoagulation”, “photodynamic therapy”, “vitrectomy”, and “Coats plus syndrome”. Searches included literature from database inception to April 2026, with emphasis on peer-reviewed English-language articles and recent reviews, cohort studies, retrospective series, systematic reviews, and clinically important case reports relevant to rare presentations or diagnostic dilemmas.

Articles were selected according to relevance to epidemiology, clinical presentation, imaging, differential diagnosis, management, outcomes, and complications. Because Coats disease is rare and the available literature is dominated by retrospective series, small observational cohorts, case series, and case reports, formal quantitative pooling was not performed. Instead, the evidence was synthesized narratively, with attention to the strength and limitations of available data, areas of disagreement, and gaps requiring further research.

Pathophysiology and vascular abnormalities

The pathophysiology of Coats disease remains incompletely understood. The dominant concept is a primary retinal vascular disorder in which abnormal retinal vascular endothelium and pericyte dysfunction compromise the blood-retinal barrier, leading to telangiectasia, aneurysmal vascular dilatation, plasma leakage, and lipid-rich exudation.19,20 Histopathological examination demonstrates subretinal exudation, mononuclear inflammatory cell infiltration, cholesterol crystals, lipid-laden macrophages, and ghost cells within retinal layers, with progressive architectural distortion in advanced disease. 20

Peripheral retinal nonperfusion is an important but underemphasized feature. Fluorescein angiography frequently demonstrates areas of capillary dropout adjacent to telangiectatic and aneurysmal vessels, supporting the concept that vascular incompetence and ischemia coexist in the disease process.21,22 Nonperfusion may contribute to increased vascular permeability, exudation, and, in advanced eyes, secondary neovascular complications. However, unlike classic proliferative ischemic retinopathies, Coats disease is primarily an exudative telangiectatic vasculopathy, and the relationship between ischemia, VEGF expression, inflammation, and structural fibrosis remains incompletely defined.

Genetic associations have been proposed, including abnormalities involving NDP, CRB1, ABCA4, and TERC, but isolated Coats disease is generally considered sporadic and non-familial.23–25 Somatic mosaicism affecting retinal vascular development has been suggested as a plausible mechanism, but conclusive causative evidence remains lacking. The presence of bilateral disease, systemic abnormalities, or very early severe presentation should raise suspicion for syndromic or hereditary Coats-like retinopathy rather than isolated idiopathic Coats disease.

Leber miliary aneurysms and the coats disease spectrum

Leber miliary aneurysms are important to place in the correct clinical context. Historically, Leber described multiple aneurysmal retinal vascular abnormalities with associated retinal degeneration, and Reese later proposed that Leber miliary aneurysms and Coats disease represent related entities within the same pathological spectrum.3,4 In contemporary clinical use, Leber miliary aneurysms are often considered a localized, milder, or earlier phenotype of idiopathic retinal telangiectatic and aneurysmal disease, whereas classic Coats disease usually refers to more extensive vascular abnormalities with prominent lipid exudation and potential progression to exudative retinal detachment.

Recognizing this spectrum is clinically relevant because excluding Leber miliary aneurysms from the Coats disease continuum may underestimate the breadth of disease expression. Localized aneurysmal lesions may remain indolent, present later in life, or respond well to targeted laser therapy, while more diffuse telangiectatic disease with macular exudation or retinal detachment carries a poorer prognosis. Bilateral Leber miliary aneurysms have also been reported, including in female patients, reinforcing the need for balanced discussion of laterality and sex distribution rather than presenting Coats disease as exclusively unilateral and male.

Clinical manifestations and macular involvement

Coats disease is most often recognized in the first or second decade of life, but it can present at any age.7,9,13,26,27 Early disease may be asymptomatic or associated with subtle visual disturbance, whereas more advanced cases may present with reduced visual acuity, strabismus, xanthocoria, leukocoria, nystagmus, ocular pain, or secondary glaucoma.20,28,29 Xanthocoria is particularly important in children because lipid-rich exudation and exudative retinal detachment may produce an abnormal yellow-orange pupillary reflex that can be confused with the white reflex of retinoblastoma.

Macular involvement is a major determinant of visual outcome and should be explicitly assessed. Foveal or subfoveal lipid exudation, cystoid macular edema, subretinal fluid, subretinal hyperreflective material, and chronic photoreceptor disruption can substantially reduce visual acuity even when peripheral disease appears limited. In chronic or previously active disease, resorption of exudate may leave retinal pigment epithelial disturbance, pigmentary dispersion, macular or retinal atrophy, subfoveal nodules, fibrosis, and permanent loss of outer retinal architecture. These changes explain why anatomical control of leakage does not always translate into functional visual recovery.

Anterior segment findings occur mainly in advanced disease and include iris neovascularization, elevated intraocular pressure, cataract, corneal edema, and anterior chamber cholesterolosis. 28 Fundus examination typically demonstrates dilated irregular telangiectatic vessels, aneurysmal “light bulb” dilatations, intraretinal and subretinal lipid exudation, peripheral nonperfusion, and, in more advanced stages, subtotal or total exudative retinal detachment21,29 (Figure 1). Screening for complications is essential because chronic exudation and retinal detachment can lead to epiretinal membrane formation, vitreoretinal fibrosis, retinal macrocysts, vitreous hemorrhage, vasoproliferative tumors, neovascular glaucoma, phthisis bulbi, and eventual enucleation in blind painful eyes.5,28

Rarely, the disease may enter a self-limited or “burnt-out” phase in which aneurysmal vascular bulbs become thrombosed, leakage decreases, and exudation gradually resorbs. This should not be mistaken for complete recovery: the residual eye may show retinal atrophy, pigmentary disturbance, macular damage, and poor visual function despite absence of active leakage or rubeosis. Long-term follow-up remains necessary because recurrent exudation can occur after apparent quiescence.

Classification and staging

The Shields classification remains the most widely used clinical staging system for Coats disease. It is useful because it links structural severity with treatment planning and prognosis. Stage 1 consists of retinal telangiectasia without exudation. Stage 2 includes telangiectasia with exudation and is subdivided according to whether exudation is extrafoveal or foveal. Stage 3 is characterized by exudative retinal detachment and is subdivided into subtotal and total detachment. Stage 4 includes total retinal detachment with secondary glaucoma, while Stage 5 represents advanced end-stage disease with a blind, often painful eye. 5 Accurate staging should be supplemented by documentation of macular involvement, peripheral nonperfusion, degree of exudation, presence of fibrosis or traction, and anterior segment complications (Tables 1–3).

Diagnostic evaluation and imaging

Diagnosis is primarily clinical and is based on dilated fundus examination demonstrating typical peripheral telangiectasia, aneurysmal vascular dilatation, and lipid-rich exudation. However, modern imaging is essential for identifying subtle disease, documenting macular involvement, excluding mimics, guiding treatment, and monitoring recurrence.

Wide-field fundus photography and wide-field fluorescein angiography are particularly valuable because Coats disease commonly affects the peripheral retina. Fluorescein angiography identifies active telangiectasia, “light bulb” aneurysmal dilatations, vascular leakage, capillary nonperfusion, and areas requiring targeted laser photocoagulation or cryotherapy.12,21,22,30,31 In some eyes, angiographic abnormalities may be more extensive than suggested by clinical examination alone, including subtle changes in the contralateral eye despite an apparently normal fundus. 22

Optical coherence tomography is central to macular evaluation. OCT can identify cystoid macular edema, subretinal fluid, intraretinal exudates, subretinal hyperreflective material, hyperreflective foci corresponding to lipid deposits, epiretinal membrane, macular scarring, outer retinal disruption, ellipsoid zone loss, and photoreceptor damage12,26,30 (Figure 2). These findings have prognostic value because chronic foveal exudation and outer retinal loss are associated with limited visual recovery even after exudation improves.

Optical coherence tomography angiography provides non-invasive assessment of the retinal microvasculature and may demonstrate abnormal vascular networks, capillary rarefaction, flow impairment, or changes after treatment.12,30,31 OCTA should be interpreted as complementary rather than a replacement for fluorescein angiography because peripheral lesions and leakage activity may be better detected using wide-field angiography. Nevertheless, OCTA can provide useful structural-vascular correlation at the macula and may help assess foveomacular perfusion and aneurysmal lesions in cooperative patients.

B-scan ultrasonography is useful in advanced disease with media opacity, dense exudative retinal detachment, or poor fundus view. It can assess the extent of detachment and help evaluate for intraocular mass lesions. When retinoblastoma is a diagnostic concern, MRI is generally preferred over CT because it provides excellent soft-tissue contrast without ionizing radiation; CT may demonstrate calcification but should be used cautiously in children due to radiation exposure concerns.18,32 Functional testing such as central visual field mapping and microperimetry may be helpful in older cooperative patients to correlate structural macular damage with functional impairment.

Differential diagnosis and diagnostic challenges

The differential diagnosis is broad because Coats disease may present with leukocoria, xanthocoria, retinal exudation, and exudative retinal detachment. Retinoblastoma is the most important diagnosis to exclude in children because delayed diagnosis can be life-threatening, while unnecessary invasive procedures may risk tumor dissemination. Other mimics include retinopathy of prematurity, familial exudative vitreoretinopathy, persistent fetal vasculature, ocular toxocariasis, pars planitis, incontinentia pigmenti, von Hippel-Lindau-related retinal capillary hemangioblastoma, Norrie disease, Coats plus syndrome, and retinitis pigmentosa with Coats-like exudative vitreoretinopathy.14–18,29,32–35

Aqueous lactate dehydrogenase has historically been described as a potential biochemical marker that may be elevated in retinoblastoma compared with non-neoplastic conditions such as Coats disease. 36 However, invasive aqueous or subretinal fluid sampling is not routinely appropriate when retinoblastoma is suspected because of the risk of extraocular tumor spread. Therefore, biochemical testing should not replace careful clinical examination and appropriate imaging by ocular oncology or pediatric retina specialists.

Bilateral disease, female sex, adult presentation, or association with systemic features should be interpreted carefully. These features do not exclude Coats-spectrum disease, but they should lower the threshold for investigating hereditary vitreoretinopathy, inflammatory disease, syndromic Coats-like disease, or retinal tumor mimics. Diagnostic accuracy depends on integrating clinical phenotype, laterality, family history, systemic features, multimodal retinal imaging, and cross-sectional imaging when indicated.

Coats-associated syndromes and coats-like retinopathy

Although isolated Coats disease is usually sporadic and unilateral, Coats-like exudative retinopathy can occur as part of systemic or genetic disease. Coats plus syndrome, also known as cerebroretinal microangiopathy with calcifications and cysts, is an autosomal recessive multisystem disorder associated with CTC1 mutations. It may present with bilateral retinal telangiectasia and exudation, intracranial calcifications, leukodystrophy, brain cysts, growth restriction, skeletal abnormalities, gastrointestinal vascular malformations, and hematological complications.32,33

Norrie disease is an X-linked recessive vitreoretinopathy associated with NDP mutations. It typically presents with bilateral congenital or early-infantile vitreoretinal dysplasia, fibrovascular changes, tractional or exudative retinal detachment, and extraocular features such as progressive sensorineural hearing loss, developmental delay, cognitive impairment, seizures, and behavioral disturbance.34,35 Recognition of syndromic or hereditary Coats-like disease is important because it changes counseling, systemic evaluation, genetic testing, and long-term surveillance.

Contemporary management

Management is stage-dependent and individualized. The main goals are closure or ablation of abnormal telangiectatic vessels, reduction of exudation, preservation of macular structure, reattachment of the retina when possible, prevention of neovascular glaucoma, and maintenance of a comfortable eye.5,12,13,27,35 Treatment decisions depend on disease stage, location and extent of telangiectasia, degree of macular involvement, chronicity of exudation, presence of retinal detachment, vitreoretinal fibrosis, media opacity, and patient age.

The evidence supporting treatment is limited by the rarity of the disease. Most recommendations are based on retrospective series, pooled case analyses, and expert practice rather than randomized trials.12,13,27,37 Therefore, treatment should be framed as evidence-informed rather than evidence-definitive. Early-stage disease with peripheral telangiectasia and no foveal-threatening exudation may be observed with close follow-up, but active treatment is generally recommended once exudation approaches or involves the macula, because chronic foveal lipid and photoreceptor injury are often irreversible.12,13,26,27,38

Laser photocoagulation and cryotherapy remain the foundation of treatment. Laser photocoagulation is most suitable when telangiectatic vessels are visible and the retina is sufficiently attached to allow effective treatment.5,12,13,27,39–41 The target is the abnormal leaking telangiectatic and aneurysmal vasculature, with the goal of vascular closure and reduction of exudation. Limitations include difficulty treating peripheral lesions hidden by subretinal fluid, the need for repeated sessions, and potential collateral retinal damage.

Cryotherapy is useful for peripheral lesions, areas obscured by exudative detachment, and disease not amenable to laser.12,13,27,35 It induces vascular closure through cold-mediated endothelial injury and thrombosis. However, excessive or extensive cryotherapy may transiently worsen exudation, increase inflammation, or contribute to vitreoretinal traction and proliferative vitreoretinopathy. For this reason, treatment is often staged and limited by quadrant.

Anti-VEGF therapy has an increasingly important but controversial role. Elevated intraocular VEGF levels and vascular permeability provide a biological rationale for anti-VEGF use in eyes with macular edema, subretinal fluid, and extensive exudation.12,13,27,37,42 Reports describe both adjunctive and, in selected cases, standalone anti-VEGF therapy. However, because anti-VEGF treatment does not directly ablate telangiectatic vessels, many authors consider it most appropriate as an adjunct that reduces exudation and facilitates subsequent laser or cryotherapy rather than as definitive monotherapy.12,13,26,27,37,42–44 Evidence remains heterogeneous, and concerns persist regarding vitreoretinal fibrosis and tractional retinal detachment, particularly in advanced pediatric disease, eyes with pretreatment fibrosis, or when anti-VEGF is combined with cryotherapy.37,42,44

Intravitreal corticosteroids may reduce inflammatory mediators and vascular permeability and have been used for chronic exudation or macular edema, often in combination with ablative therapy.12,27,45,46 Their limitations include cataract progression, steroid-induced ocular hypertension, and the practical challenges of repeated intravitreal treatment in children. Photodynamic therapy has been reported in selected cases, especially for posterior telangiectatic or aneurysmal lesions close to the macula or optic disc where conventional laser or cryotherapy may be impractical. 47 PDT is intended to induce selective vascular occlusion of leaking aneurysmal or telangiectatic lesions, but evidence is limited to case reports and small series12,27,47; it should therefore be considered experimental or highly selective.

Surgical management is reserved for advanced or refractory disease. External drainage of subretinal fluid may facilitate retinal reattachment and allow subsequent ablative therapy in bullous exudative detachment.12,48 Pars plana vitrectomy may be considered when vitreous opacities, inflammatory bands, epiretinal membrane, vitreoretinal traction, or poor visualization prevent adequate treatment of the pathological retina, or when internal drainage and endolaser are required.12,38,49 PPV may improve anatomical outcomes in selected advanced cases, but it is associated with risks including cataract, recurrent detachment, proliferative vitreoretinopathy, and limited functional recovery when macular damage is longstanding. Enucleation is reserved for blind painful eyes, uncontrolled neovascular glaucoma, phthisis, or situations in which malignancy cannot be safely excluded. 28

Prognosis and outcomes

The prognosis of Coats disease is highly variable and is determined mainly by stage at presentation, age, extent of exudation, degree of retinal detachment, macular involvement, and secondary complications.5,12,13,26,27,30,50 Eyes diagnosed before foveal damage or total exudative retinal detachment generally have better anatomical and functional outcomes after timely ablative treatment. Conversely, chronic macular exudation, subfoveal lipid, photoreceptor loss, macular fibrosis, total retinal detachment, and neovascular glaucoma are associated with poor visual prognosis and higher risk of globe loss.

Anatomical improvement does not always translate into visual recovery because irreversible outer retinal and macular structural damage may persist after exudation resolves. Recurrence or reactivation may occur years after initial control, particularly in pediatric patients. Long-term surveillance is therefore essential to detect recurrent telangiectasia, new leakage, progressive exudation, glaucoma, fibrosis, or late retinal detachment.12,13,26,27

Current controversies, evidence gaps, and future directions

The principal limitation of the Coats disease literature is the lack of high-quality comparative evidence.12,27,37 Most treatment data are derived from retrospective series, small cohorts, pooled case reports, or expert experience. A 2023 systematic review of anti-VEGF therapy identified 24 case series involving 378 patients but no randomized, case-control, or cohort studies eligible for analysis. 37 This makes it difficult to determine whether favorable outcomes reflect treatment effect, earlier stage at diagnosis, selection bias, or natural disease variability. The role of anti-VEGF therapy is a major example: although many reports show reduced exudation and improved anatomical control, uncertainty remains regarding optimal timing, dose, injection frequency, role as monotherapy, and risk of fibrosis or tractional retinal detachment.

Future research should prioritize multicenter prospective registries with standardized staging, imaging protocols, treatment definitions, and outcome measures. Important endpoints should include not only retinal reattachment and exudation control, but also visual acuity, OCT-based outer retinal integrity, microperimetry where feasible, recurrence, treatment burden, adverse events, anesthesia exposure in children, and quality of life. Emerging imaging technologies, including ultra-wide-field angiography, OCTA, adaptive optics, and quantitative OCT biomarkers, may help identify early progression, treatment response, and long-term visual prognosis. Greater attention should also be given to the Coats disease spectrum, including Leber miliary aneurysms, bilateral presentations, adult-onset disease, and syndromic Coats-like retinopathies.12,13,26,27,30,31

Limitations of this review

This review has limitations. It is a narrative review rather than a systematic review or meta-analysis, and article selection was based on clinical relevance rather than formal risk-of-bias assessment. The available literature on Coats disease is heterogeneous and consists largely of retrospective series, case series, and case reports.12,13,27,37 Therefore, conclusions regarding comparative treatment efficacy, optimal sequencing of therapy, and long-term safety should be interpreted cautiously. In addition, rare presentations such as bilateral disease, Leber miliary aneurysms, and syndromic Coats-like retinopathy are often represented by case reports, limiting the ability to draw firm epidemiological conclusions.

Conclusion

Coats disease is a rare but potentially vision-threatening retinal vascular disorder with a broad clinical spectrum. Early recognition, careful exclusion of mimicking conditions, and detailed imaging assessment are essential to preserve vision and prevent end-stage complications. Laser photocoagulation and cryotherapy remain the foundation of treatment, while anti-VEGF therapy, corticosteroids, photodynamic therapy, and surgical intervention have selective roles depending on disease severity and anatomical features. Current management remains limited by the absence of robust prospective comparative evidence. Future multicenter studies using standardized imaging and outcome measures are needed to refine treatment algorithms and improve both anatomical and functional outcomes.
