Idiopathic Multicentric Castleman's Disease Diagnosed by Pulmonary Histopathology: A Case Report
Authors: Yuki Kurihara, Yuta Kishikawa, Mizuki Nanri, Arisa Ibi, Yuki Kuwahara, Tetsuro Haraguchi, Natsuko Komiya, Shinsuke Ogusu, Hiroki Tashiro, Chiho Nakashima, Seiichi Kato, Koichiro Takahashi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTA 41‐year‐old woman who presented with lower back pain, stiff shoulders and fatigue was found to have inflammatory laboratory findings and bilateral ground‐glass opacities on chest x‐ray. High‐resolution computed tomography (HRCT) revealed consolidation, a nodular shadow, multiple mediastinal and hilar lymphadenopathy, liver enlargement, and splenomegaly. Lung biopsy showed lymphoid follicles, plasma cells and lymphocyte infiltration along bronchovascular bundles and interlobular septa, and plasma cells infiltrating the interfollicular area. Lymph node pathology revealed regressed germinal centres and an extended interfollicular area with plasma cell infiltration, as well as capillary proliferation with endothelial hyperplasia. There were no specific findings of lymphoma or immunoglobulin G4 (IgG4)‐related disease and she was diagnosed with idiopathic multicentric Castleman's disease (iMCD). According to consensus diagnostic criteria, lymph node pathology is required for diagnosing iMCD, but this case demonstrates that lung lesions may also provide diagnostic supportive value.Keywords: high‐resolution computed tomography (HRCT), idiopathic multicentric Castleman's disease (iMCD), lymphadenopathy, tocilizumab, video‐assisted thoracoscopic lung biopsy

Introduction

Idiopathic multicentric Castleman's disease (iMCD) is a lymphoproliferative disorder with systemic inflammatory features for which the aetiology remains mostly unknown. Although iMCD with pulmonary involvement is not rare [1], studies of pulmonary pathological findings are limited, and there are no pathological criteria for lung findings in the consensus diagnostic criteria for iMCD proposed by the Castleman Disease Collaborative Network [2]. Lymph node findings are necessary to diagnose iMCD based on the criteria, but in some cases, lung biopsy may be less invasive than lymph node sampling. This case demonstrates that pulmonary pathological findings can be helpful for diagnosis of iMCD.

Case Report

A 41‐year‐old woman had experienced lower back pain, stiff shoulders and fatigue for several years. Chest x‐ray showed diffuse ground‐glass opacities in both lungs and cardiomegaly (Figure 1A). High‐resolution computed tomography (HRCT) showed bilateral ground‐glass opacities, consolidation, and an 18‐mm nodular shadow in the right middle lobe (Figure 1B). HRCT also showed multiple mediastinal and hilar lymphadenopathy (Figure 1C), liver enlargement, and splenomegaly (Figure 1D). No abnormal findings in other organs or additional lymph node enlargement were observed. Inflammatory laboratory results included white blood cells 7400/μL, haemoglobin 12.2 g/dL, platelets 44.5 × 104/μL, C‐reactive protein 5.80 mg/dL, erythrocyte sedimentation rate 88 mm/h, immunoglobulin G (IgG) 3283 mg/dL, immunoglobulin G4 (IgG4) 328.0 mg/dL, and interleukin 6 (IL‐6) 20.8 pg/mL. Serum autoantibodies and angiotensin‐converting enzyme were negative. Human immunodeficiency virus antigen and antibody which were negative, which suggests that the patient is negative for human herpesvirus‐8. Video‐assisted thoracoscopic lung biopsy of the right middle lobe and lower paratracheal lymph node sampling were performed. Photomicrographs of the lung lesion showed plasma cell and lymphocyte infiltration along bronchovascular bundles and interlobular septa with lymphoid follicles (Figure 2A–G). Plasma cells infiltrated the interfollicular areas. Hyalinizing fibrosis in the interlobular septa and hyalinized vessels were partially observed. Pathological examination of the lymph node biopsy revealed that germinal centres were markedly regressed and the extended interfollicular area showed plasmacytosis (Figure 2H–N). There was capillary proliferation with endothelial hyperplasia. Some IgG4‐positive cells were observed in the lung and the lymph node, but the IgG4/IgG‐positive cell ratio was lower than 40%, and there was no storiform fibrosis or obliterative vasculitis. Lymphoma was ruled out by immunohistochemistry, flow cytometry and Southern blot hybridization analysis. The free kappa/lambda ratio was normal, and no rearrangements of the T‐cell receptor gene and immunoglobulin gene were detected. Infection‐associated diseases, defined autoimmune diseases, and malignant or lymphoproliferative diseases were excluded by laboratory and histological findings. Thus, the patient was diagnosed with idiopathic multicentric Castleman's disease‐not otherwise specified (iMCD‐NOS). As the patient had a Barthel Index score of 100, ECOG performance status of 0 and no renal or respiratory dysfunction, fluid overload, or anaemia, we diagnosed mild iMCD based on the severity scoring of iMCD [2]. Treatment was initiated with prednisolone 15 mg/day, but there was no improvement in symptoms, inflammatory findings or chest x‐ray findings. Tocilizumab was then added, after which there was improvement in symptoms, inflammatory findings and chest x‐ray findings.

Discussion

iMCD is a lymphoproliferative disorder with systemic inflammatory features, but the aetiology remains unknown. The initial lung HRCT findings of iMCD typically show tiny and vague centrilobular nodules that gradually increase in size. Some of these nodules develop air spaces and progress into irregularly walled cysts [3]. Histopathological examination is required to differentiate iMCD from infection‐related diseases, IgG4‐related disease, blood diseases and autoimmune diseases. There are no pathological criteria for lung findings included in the consensus diagnostic criteria for iMCD proposed by the Castleman Disease Collaborative Network; accordingly, lymph node findings are necessary to diagnose iMCD based on the criteria [2]. There are two main reasons why lung histopathological findings of iMCD have not been adequately investigated. First, there are limited reports of iMCD with pulmonary involvement. According to a systematic review and meta‐analysis, the average percentage of pulmonary findings identified in patients with iMCD using a random effects meta‐analysis model was 31.4% (95% CI: 21.2%–49.8%) [4]. A previous report showed that 42.7% (146/342) of Japanese iMCD patients had pulmonary involvement [1]. Second, most iMCD patients have enlarged multiple lymph nodes that are easy to biopsy, such as in the neck, axillary, and inguinal regions. According to a report on the distribution of enlarged lymph nodes (n = 217), 70.5% of patients had enlarged lymph nodes in the head or neck area, 70.1% in the right axilla, 67.7% in the left axilla, 70.1% in the right inguinal area, and 67.7% in the left inguinal area [5]. In some cases, there is no lymphadenopathy easily accessible for biopsy, making lung biopsy a less invasive alternative. Pulmonary involvement in iMCD has been previously described. Nishimura et al. reported clinicopathological features of lung lesions in plasma cell‐type iMCD and emphasised that these findings should be interpreted as part of a systemic inflammatory disorder rather than as an independent diagnostic site [6].

The present case was treated with tocilizumab. This monoclonal antibody targeting the IL‐6 receptor was approved for iMCD therapy in Japan in 2005. In Japan, corticosteroids are typically used first for mild iMCD, with tocilizumab added if the response is inadequate. In contrast, international guidelines recommend siltuximab, an anti‐IL‐6 monoclonal antibody, as first‐line therapy. Reported treatment failure rates are 54% for corticosteroids and 32% for anti‐IL‐6 therapy based on a review of 344 cases. Further studies are needed to clarify whether racial or regional differences affect treatment outcomes. The novelty of our case lies in the comprehensive correlation of pulmonary histopathology, lymph node pathology and imaging findings, together with the clinical course and therapeutic response to tocilizumab. While previous studies have described pulmonary lesions in iMCD, few reports have provided detailed histopathological images alongside systemic features and treatment outcomes.

Future diagnostic criteria could consider incorporating pulmonary pathological findings, as lung biopsy can provide supportive diagnostic information when accessible lymphadenopathy is absent. This case demonstrates that pulmonary lesions may play a supportive role as lymph node lesions in confirming iMCD, highlighting the need for further research to establish standardised lung pathology criteria.
