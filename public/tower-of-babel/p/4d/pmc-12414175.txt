Does online information about hormone replacement therapy (or menopause hormone therapy) reflect indications from the British National Formulary and guidance from the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence: a cross-sectional study of UK media
Authors: Margaret McCartney, Rosa Morgan-Young, Frank Sullivan, Deirdre J Murphy, Jahan Albulushi, James Larkin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesTo describe: (1) the most visible information (from individuals or organisations) on UK social media regarding hormone replacement therapy (HRT)/menopause hormone treatment for menopause; (2) claims made by these sources for HRT and testosterone outwith the indications specified by the British National Formulary (BNF) and the National Institute of Health and Care Excellence (NICE) (ie, vasomotor instability, vaginal dryness, low mood associated with the menopause and, for testosterone, low libido after treatment with HRT) and for use for the prevention of future ill health and (3) conflicts of interest of commentators.DesignCross-sectional study.SettingOnline references to HRT, for use in menopause, in UK online media, comprising Facebook, Google, Instagram, TikTok and YouTube, 30 top ranked hits between 1 January 2022 and 1 June 2023 and Twitter (X) up to 1 May 2024.MethodsIdentification of the most visible information was performed via online searching with the term ‘HRT’ using incognito searches within each modality. Statements making claims were identified and analysed as to whether they were congruent with BNF and NICE advice on indications for use. Declarations of interest were extracted from the source or searched for if not apparent using a standardised search strategy. Data were entered into an Excel spreadsheet. Summary and descriptive statistics were used to summarise the results, including description of origin and types of claims, percentage of claims in agreement with NICE/BNF indications, relationship to financial interests and readership data, where available.Results180 recommendations and/or claims for HRT were examined (30 from each of six platforms), made by professional individuals (53.4%), laypeople (41.7%) and patient, media and professional organisations (4.9%) completing the total. Overall, 67.2% of claims were outside of BNF/NICE recommendations. 139 (77.2%) were associated with a conflict of interest. In 117 cases, this was a conflict either directly or indirectly related to menopause, through provision of private practice, pharmaceutical industry funding or retail products marketed at the menopause.ConclusionsSocial media commonly contains claims for HRT outside BNF/NICE guidance. Conflicts of interest by commentators are also common, directly or indirectly related to menopause. Less than a quarter of media contained no commercial conflict. Policymakers should consider means to ensure that non-conflicted, evidence-based information is visible to professionals, patients and the public.Trial registration numberOpen Science Framework (https://osf.io/r7e5c/).Keywords: Sex steroids & HRT, eHealth, Community gynaecology, Patients, Prescriptions

Introduction

In 2021/2022, 7.8 million items for hormone replacement therapy (HRT) were prescribed, a rise of 35% compared with the 12 months before.1 Google Trends show an increase in popularity in searches for ‘HRT’ in the UK over the last 5 years, with a peak at 1–7 May 20222 coinciding with a Channel 4 documentary on the menopause.3 A search on Instagram in April 2022 found half a million posts referencing #HRT, with the top 10 posts on TikTok referencing HRT being viewed over 2 million times in total.

Concern has previously been raised about the quality of information on social and lay media about healthcare interventions, particularly when it is financially conflicted.4,7 It is not known whether popular information about the indications for HRT is congruent with recommendations from the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) or the British National Formulary (BNF), or whether the features of ‘delicate diagnosis’ (where commercial influences can impact diagnosis and treatment rates), including unsettled science and conflicts of interest, contribute to information quality.8

84.4% of the adult UK population uses social media. 85% of UK women use created content sites (ie, excluding personal communication platforms like WhatsApp and Snapchat): YouTube (63%), Instagram (56%), Twitter (37%) and TikTok (12%).9 Menopause is frequently discussed on social media with patient advocates, advocacy groups, medical professionals and academics most visible.10 11 Patient advocates praise the ability of social media for ‘building community’, ‘activism’ and ‘education’ for both patients and doctors on menopause, as well as ‘research’, including attracting participants. However, there is potential for poor quality, biased information and ‘commercial gain’.12 The pattern of misinformation on social media has been raised as an area of concern to public health.13 Television programmes and traditional news media are also important sources of health information; however, these are also noted to be frequently unreliable, with a tendency not to disclose conflicts of interest.14 The number of articles about menopause in newspapers has quadrupled in the last decade.15 Over a third of postmenopausal women seek information on menopause from social media, including YouTube.16

The risks and benefits of HRT have been differently promoted and contested over time. In the ‘60s, popular discourse depicted great benefits and little risks of HRT, including prevention of future illness, particularly cardiovascular disease and dementia. However, conflicts of interest were later noted: one best-selling book claiming multiple benefits for HRT was subsequently revealed to be funded by manufacturers of the medication.17 In 2002, the Women’s Health Initiative Study reported an increased risk of breast cancer and heart disease related to taking HRT.18 Subsequently, prescribing in the UK fell by 50%.19 Reanalysis and further follow-up data suggested ‘a complex pattern of risks and benefits’, making HRT suitable for symptom control but not disease prevention.20 21 In 2023, in the context of the highest yet recorded use of HRT in the UK, concerns were raised that women were being prescribed doses beyond product licences.22

During this study, NICE recommended that HRT is prescribed at ‘the lowest dose for the shortest possible duration’.23 Licensing, for women at a normal menopausal age, does not include prevention of disease but symptomatic control, namely vasomotor and vaginal symptoms. During this study, while the BNF recognised that HRT can reduce the risk of osteoporosis, ‘alternatives to HRT should be considered if osteoporosis is the main concern’. Oestrogen must be given with progestogen in women who have a uterus in order to reduce the risk of unopposed oestrogen, namely, endometrial cancer. Side effects listed in the BNF include the risk of thrombosis, stroke, endometrial cancer, breast and ovarian cancer, with an increased risk of coronary heart disease in women starting HRT after 10 years of menopause.24 Excess thrombosis risk relates to oral, rather than transdermal, delivery of oestrogen. NICE stated, ‘Consider HRT to alleviate low mood that arises as a result of the menopause’ and ‘Consider testosterone supplementation for menopausal women with low sexual desire if HRT alone is not effective’. While NICE said that the risk of breast cancer varies according to background risks, ‘HRT with oestrogen and progestogen can be associated with an increase in the risk of breast cancer’ and ‘any increase in the risk of breast cancer is related to treatment duration and reduces after stopping HRT’. Risk tables produced by the Medicines and Healthcare Products Regulatory Agency (MHRA) show how the risks of combined HRT are greater than oestrogen alone: 20 more breast cancers are produced via using HRT for up to 5 years before the age of 69 years and 40 after 10 years of use, per 1000 women.25

In the UK, several organisations have promoted an increase in use of HRT. When NICE updated their guidance on HRT in 2015, the accompanying press release quoted a doctor on the guideline committee: “For the last decade, some GPs have been worried about prescribing HRT, and women worried about taking it. […] For health professionals, the guideline should boost their confidence in prescribing HRT, having fully discussed the woman’s individual circumstances with her”.26 The All Party Parliamentary Group on Menopause called for a ‘#menopauserevolution’ in 2023 saying, ‘We do recognise the evidence which shows a link between some types of HRT and breast cancer, yet this has been over-exaggerated and stated out of context. For many women, the benefits considerably outweigh the risks of HRT, and poor lifestyle choices are proven to pose a much greater threat to developing cancers, including breast cancer’.27

Within this contemporary narrative and the associated sharp rise in prescribing, it is uncertain whether popular online information reflects the indications described by the NICE and the BNF for HRT. In order to understand the quality of advice and recommendations on social media, analysis of highly ranked online sources of information was undertaken.

Methods

An observational study was conducted to assess the alignment of social media posts about HRT with guidance and prescribing indications from NICE/BNF.

Best practice guidance

The NICE and the MHRA via the BNF was searched for guidance applicable during the study period (1 January 2022 to 1 May 2024) for: (1) indications for HRT, including for prevention of future ill health; (2) adverse effects of HRT; (3) indications for testosterone for menopause. This information was extracted and was considered as the standard to compare social media posts against.

The NICE and BNF indications for HRT were recorded as: vasomotor symptoms, mood disorders and urogenital symptoms, with testosterone recommended ‘for altered sexual function, seek specialist advice on the appropriateness of testosterone supplementation (off-label indication)’.28

The BNF also noted that ‘oestrogen, given systemically in the perimenopausal and postmenopausal period…also diminishes postmenopausal osteoporosis but other drugs are preferred’. They also noted ‘The MHRA advises that HRT should only be prescribed to relieve post-menopausal symptoms that are adversely affecting quality of life and treatment should be reviewed regularly to ensure the minimum effective dose is used for the shortest duration.’29

Online posts

The online platforms analysed were Facebook, Google, TikTok, Twitter (X) and YouTube. These were chosen as the most highly accessed media platforms freely available online during the study period.9 Although ‘MHT’ is more accurate compared with ‘HRT’ (as menopause is physiological and ‘replacement’ implies it is not), ‘HRT’ was used for searches as it is the more commonly used term in the UK, and scoping searches indicated that MHT was hardly visible in lay media (we located only one mention of MHT in reference to menopause on a website for medical professionals in the first 10 pages of a Google search; this webpage also used ‘HRT’). Scoping searches also found that ‘HRT’ was not commonly used. Searches were completed using the term ‘HRT’ between 1 January 2022 and 1 May 2024.

Traditional print media was not separately analysed but relevant sources were included where located on Google searching.

Online posts were included if they concerned any recommendation for HRT or testosterone use during the perimenopause or menopause and were accessible by the general public using standard search methods. This included adverts or paid-for promotions, to replicate real world use. Data were excluded if they contained no recommendation, for example, concerning supply shortages or prescription costs. Recommendations for premature menopause, acute surgical menopause or gender dysphoria were excluded. Media aimed at healthcare professionals was excluded if a paywall was present or readers had to verify a professional status. Private social media accounts were not used, that is, if open to subscribers or followers only. Online search strategies were set to ‘incognito’ to minimise the use of algorithms in promoting particular content to researchers based on their previous search history.

The ‘top 30’ highest viewed or ranked media were analysed (online supplemental appendix 1), for each of Google, Instagram, TikTok, (X) and YouTube, based on previous media research.7 We were not able to determine the top 30 highest viewed or ranked media for Facebook and Twitter (X) (first ranked results). For Facebook and Twitter (X), we included the first 30 posts found from the search that during the time period had >1000 views. For Facebook posts, if viewership information was not available, then the inclusion criteria were >50 interactions. Adjustments were made for Facebook and Twitter data to ensure that the most visible data sources were analysed.

A structured pro forma was developed in Excel to facilitate data extraction. The pro forma included the following headings:

Origin of statements: (professional, layperson, or patient, charity or professional organisation).

Type of commentator (lay, professional, patient organisation, professional organisation).

Viewership and date of publication if available.

Whether information is signalling an advert, sponsored or promoted information.

Recommendations for use of HRT other than that identified from NICE guidance and BNF indications.

Conflicts of interest of information-givers and whether or not this was published directly on the website, classed by type and whether this was directly related to menopause or not and secondary conflicts of interest, for example, pharmaceutical funding received by Trustees of charitable organisations.

A screenshot was stored for non-video sources. Data on videos additionally included the time when claims were made and transcript if available.

Two researchers used the pro forma to independently extract data from each media item. Disagreements were recorded and discussed. A third researcher arbitrated in cases of non-resolution. If the sample of 30 included a duplicate source, one was excluded and the next in line was included.

Categories for origin of statement and type of commentator were developed using conventional (ie, inductive) content analysis.30

The origin of the statement was considered to be the type of website or social media platform where the comment on HRT was hosted and included. The following categories were developed: lay (personal social media accounts), retail (including supplement, fitness or beauty companies), professional (websites owned or hosted by healthcare professionals), patient associations (organisations run primarily by and for patients), professional organisations (organisations run primarily for and by professionals, eg, Royal Colleges) and media organisations (online magazines and news sites).

Types of commentators were assessed and coded as professionals if they stated they were a doctor or professor or described themselves currently as, for example, doctor, psychologist, dietician, nurse, sports scientist or physiotherapist. ‘Nutritionist’ is not a protected title in the UK, and so, this was classed as ‘lay’ as was ‘fitness expert’ and ‘menopause coach’ and similar terms. Healthcare students were classed as lay. Where organisations provided information authored by a named individual, the individual was coded as the source of the comment. If the organisation did not supply an individual name as the author, the information was coded as from the organisation.

Codes of recommendations for HRT and testosterone were inductively developed and iterated over the first 60 data extractions in discussion with the research team. These included claims for skin or hair (eg, a ‘glow’ or reduction in wrinkles), well-being (eg, a feeling of ‘vitality’), improvement of musculoskeletal conditions, improved cognitive function, better motivation, avoidance of weight gain or weight reduction and prevention of future illness, which included cardiovascular conditions and dementia. For testosterone, this also included sleep. Websites located using Google were searched for claims for HRT and testosterone, as the immediate page could act as a landing site with little or no detailed information. These were then categorised as agreement or disagreement with NICE/BNF guidelines/indications.

Publication of a conflict of interest included the presence of a statement of interests (eg, ‘acted as consultant to Bayer’) or the indication of the presence of a financial interest where readership could be expected to increase sales to the benefit of the author (eg, affiliate or paid promotional relationships, book sales or retail products, private practice). Where no such statement was published, a standardised process to search for declarations and conflicts of interest was used7 (online supplemental appendix 2).

Direct and indirect conflicts located during analysis of data were recorded, including conflicts introduced by work roles (eg, spokesperson for medical device company, private practice). After a declaration or one conflict was found, the search on other platforms was stopped, but all conflicts on the index site were recorded. Disagreements were resolved with a third researcher. A quote documenting each interest and a screenshot was stored in an online depository. First, conflicts of interest were categorised, as related to private practice (medical, physiotherapy), retail products (non-prescription supplements, clothing), media products (eg, books) or the pharmaceutical industry, regardless of whether the conflict of interest was menopause related or not menopause related. Examples of conflicts of interest that were not menopause related were general interest books, cosmetic products or beauty services not directly advertised with respect to the menopause. Then, among conflicts of interest that were menopause related, they were further categorised by the type of menopause-related conflict of interest, using the following four categories:

Direct conflict via paid-for medical products (eg, private healthcare clinics, including those specifically advertising menopause consultations).

Direct conflict via paid-for medical products (eg, private healthcare clinics, including those specifically advertising menopause consultations).

Direct conflict via retail products (advertised services to specifically menopausal women, for example, dietary packages, cosmetics, health and food supplements, exercise classes, coaching).

Direct funding from a pharmaceutical company (eg, payments by a manufacturer of interventions for menopause).

Analysis

The data were analysed using percentages and frequencies. A logit regression was used to model the relationship between menopause-related conflicts of interest (independent variable) and agreement with NICE/BNF advice (dependent variable). Covariates included in the model were platform and commentator type. To operationalise the platform variable, Facebook was used as the reference category as it is the most used of the included platforms among UK adult women.9 For commentator type, media commentator, professional and patient organisation were collapsed into a single variable due to the low number of professionals and patient organisations and because they all have a professional status when compared with lay-people/citizens. Complete-case analysis was used. An exploratory analysis was also conducted: univariate logit models with the same independent variables and agreement of the post with NICE/BNF advice as the dependent variable.

Patient and public involvement

Patients were not involved directly in this study. During this study, we read a wide cross-section of patient views about menopause and MRT/HRT, which we reflected on as we wrote the conclusion.

Results

Data were extracted from 180 sources, with 30 from each of the six platforms. Of the 180 commentators who posted, 53.3% (n=96) were professional, 41.7% (n=75) were lay-people/citizens, 3.3% (n=6) were media and 1.7% (n=3) were patient organisations (online supplemental file). Overall, 67.2% (n=121) of posts disagreed with NICE guidance or BNF indications (figure 1).

On Facebook, 15 lay commentators, 14 professional commentators and one media broadcast commentator were identified in 30 data extractions. 33.3% (n=10) published information enabling direct identification of potential conflicts. For 93.3% (n=28) of sources, a direct or indirect conflict of interest was identified. Of these 64.3% (n=18) related to retail products, 42.9% (n=12) related to private practice and 7.1% (n=2) related to media (eg, books); 14.3% (n=4) of sources with a conflict of interest had two identified conflicts. For 63.3% (n=19) of sources, a menopause-related conflict of interest was identified. Details of the types of menopause-related conflicts of interest for Facebook sources and sources from other platforms are in table 2.

On Instagram, 14 lay, 14 professional and two patient organisations were identified as commentators in 30 data extractions. 73.3% (n=22) contained information enabling direct identification of potential conflicts. 80.0% (n=25) of sources were judged to have a direct or indirect conflict of interest. Of these, 64.0% (n=16) related to private practice, 12.0% (n=3) related to the pharmaceutical industry and 36.0% (n=9) related to retail products; 12.0% (n=3) of sources with a conflict of interest had two identified conflicts. For 80.0% (n=24) of sources, a menopause-related conflict of interest was identified.

On Google websites included healthcare organisations (17) (including NHS, private practice and healthcare insurance providers), charities/patient organisations (including patient-facing educational resources created by professional charitable organisations) (8), media organisations (3) and retail companies (2). 46.7% (n=14) contained information on the source document enabling direct identification of potential conflicts. 16.7% (n=5) of sources did not contain enough information on identity to make a reasonable assessment of conflicts of interest. Direct or indirect conflicts were identified in 92.0% (n=23) of sources. Of these, 60.9% (n=14) related to private practice, 39.1% (n=9) related to the pharmaceutical industry and 4.3% (n=1) related to retail products; 4.3% (n=1) of sources with a conflict of interest had two identified conflicts. For 92.0% (n=23) of sources, a menopause-related conflict of interest was identified.

On TikTok, 25 lay and five professional sources were analysed. 76.7% (n=23) published information on the source post enabling direct identification of potential conflicts. Direct or indirect conflicts of interest were identified in 86.7% (n=26) of sources. Of these, 19.2% (n=5) were related to private practice and 80.8% (n=21) related to retail products; 0.0% (n=0) of sources with a conflict of interest had two identified conflicts. For 63.3% (n=19) of sources, a menopause-related conflict of interest was identified.

On Twitter (X), the time frame for searching was adapted. This was because a change of ownership of the platform incurred a change to, then loss of, academic access ability for searching the site. 30 tweets with a median number of views of 6732 (IQR=3403–29 025) at the time of capture were analysed. 11 lay and 18 professional and one media commentator posts were examined. 66.6% (n=20) contained information on the source document enabling direct identification of potential conflicts. Eight sources did not contain enough information on identity to make a reasonable assessment of conflicts of interest, 81.8% (n=18) were assessed as having a direct or indirect conflict of interest. Of these, (54.6%, n=12) related to private practice, 13.6% (n=3) related to media products and 13.6% (n=3) related to retail products. For 68.2% (n=15) of sources, a menopause-related conflict of interest was identified.

On YouTube, 30 videos with a median number of views of 33 263 (IQR=10 024–91 915) at the time of capture were analysed. Nine were comments from laypeople, 19 were comments from professionals, and two were from media organisations (one news report with a journalist commentating, the other from a media organisation with scripted content and no identified individual source). Five videos were clips of television programmes, and seven were made by commercial companies or private healthcare clinics. 30.0% (n=9) contained information on the source document enabling direct identification of potential conflicts. Direct or indirect conflicts were identified in 73.3% (n=22) of sources. Of these, 59.1% (n=13) related to private practice, 31.8% related to retail products, 9.1% (n=2) related to the pharmaceutical industry and 13.6% (n=3) related to media products; 13.6% (n=3) of sources with a conflict of interest had two identified conflicts. For 63.3% (n=19) of sources, a menopause-related conflict of interest was identified.

Conflicts were tabulated and analysed in each of the 180 identified sources. Of these sources, 92.8% (n=167) contained sufficient information to enable a search for conflict of interest information. Analysis of the 167 found that 142 (85.0%) had a conflict of interest and 71.3% (n=119) had a menopause-related conflict of interest. Items that were deemed as conflicts not related to the menopause included: individuals or companies selling fitness classes, beauty or lifestyle products with advertisements not specifically targeting menopausal women. Conflicts related to the menopause included products marketed specifically at menopausal women (eg, ‘for menopausal skin’) and retail products (for example, health food supplements) and private medical practice advertising services to menopausal women. Menopause-related conflicts of interest were most prevalent on Google (92.0%, n=23) and least on Facebook (63.3%, n=19), TikTok (63.3%, n=19) and YouTube (63.3%, n=19). Discussion

This study found that over two thirds of the most popular online information analysed make HRT claims that are inconsistent with NICE and BNF indications. Additionally, the majority of information located could not be considered independent because of conflicts of interest. Most were related to the menopause (eg, private practice advertised for menopause, relationships with pharmaceutical companies whose products include drugs licensed or in development for menopause or supplements, cosmetics and skin products advertised for menopause). More general conflicts were unrelated to the menopause (eg,provision of generalist private practice or retail affiliate links).

The study has several weaknesses. First, searches for conflicts were stopped after one was found and will therefore have underestimated the variety held by commentators. Further, there is no consensus around how long conflicts are impactful for and should be declared or deemed relevant.31 We noted some sources which advertised that they had no connection to the pharmaceutical industry but where individuals associated with it had, in the past, declared financial relationships to the pharmaceutical industry. Disclosure UK, a website operated by the Association of the British Pharmaceutical Industry, publishes financial transactions but only with consent.32 This means that some conflicts may have been missed. Some sources of information were unable to be assessed for conflicts of interest; for example, Wikipedia allows community editing but identity of editors and their conflicts of interest are not required either to be declared or managed. Some platforms were easier to identify conflicts by design, for example, fewer conflicts of interest were located on Twitter, as relative anonymity is common, compared with Instagram and Facebook, where authors tend to be much more identifiable as individuals. The impact of the information on women’s decision making was not measured, and it is therefore not possible, from this work, to identify whether readers trusted or used the recommendations analysed in social media. Additionally, although we used incognito searches, it is unlikely that our search results will be exactly replicated by others, given the algorithms used by social media companies to increase or decrease visibility of data; thus, these findings should be taken as an indicative search only. Readers may have used information about obviously presented conflicts in their own interpretation of recommendations. Additionally, the classification of professional commentators may not reflect perceptions of readers: for example, some people may not understand the distinction between ‘dieticians’ and ‘nutritionists’, or that ‘menopause’ coach or ‘fitness instructor’ are not regulated titles and do not infer any particular training.33 The pragmatic nature of sampling sizes used means that important other sources which may have been reliable and without significant conflicts may have been missed. View numbers were captured over several weeks, meaning that they are not directly comparable. Additionally, this research concerned recommendations for hormonal interventions only. Some information sources were noted, while agreeing with NICE/BNF indications for HRT, to also recommend interventions which they recommended against, such as blood testing for Follicle Stimulating Hormone levels when in the menopausal age group.34 Concordance with NICE/BNF advice in one area should not lead to assumptions about concordance with other parts of the menopause guidelines. Benefits of HRT/MHT may exist in addition to those indications given by NICE/BNF. A lack of research into the effects of testosterone, for example, has been noted. Additionally, the small sample sizes mean that the study was underpowered to detect relationships between conflicts and recommendations, which cannot be excluded.

Strengths of the study include the checking of sources by two researchers, the wide range of media sources studied and the time frame of searches, undertaken during a period of significant change in volume of HRT prescribing in the UK, as well as the searches for conflicts of interest.

The study has found that information which was both popular and independent was uncommon; only 16.2% of sources had no apparent conflict of interest, and 33.8% of sources did not have a menopause-related conflict of interest. Therefore, given the volume of conflicts identified, it may be difficult for individuals to avoid conflicted content when searching for information on HRT on these social media platforms. It was notable that while NHS websites did appear on Google searches, they were not highly viewed on other platforms in this study.

Previous research completed in 1995, when the volume of HRT prescribing was lower, suggests a ‘substantial minority of women’ found media information unhelpful or felt it to be incorrect.35 Medical guidelines now emphasise the importance of ‘shared decision making’, whereby patients are given information and assisted to make the best choice to match their values and preferences.36 While individuals are entitled to their own views on what is modifiable by HRT, it may be difficult for women to distinguish between opinion and NICE/BNF recommendations. In general, patients and professionals overestimate the benefits and underestimate the harms of healthcare interventions, and this study is in keeping with that finding.37 38

It is therefore crucial that women are able to base their decisions on high-quality evidence. However, previous research has identified ‘tension […] in the informed patient discourse which itself obscures the potential conflict between lay and expert/medical knowledges in the clinical encounter’.39 This is further complicated by the variety of views held by professionals on the effectiveness of HRT for wider issues. A systematic review and critical appraisal of guidelines for the management of menopause found consistently stated indications being vasomotor symptoms and menopause-related mood disturbance and none in support of use for cognitive symptoms or prevention of disease.40 However, we located information from a variety of commentators, including lay people with special interest in the menopause and healthcare professionals suggesting that HRT and testosterone can be useful in multiple other issues, for example, weight management, skin and hair appearance, energy levels, mental acuity, as well as prevention of cardiovascular disease and dementia. There may not just be disagreement between patients and professionals about what benefits may be attained, but also between professionals and between patients. Professional societies and leaders may need to balance this, but attempts to redress are unlikely to reach the same audience who viewed initial messaging.41 42

After the analysis was complete, NICE updated their guidance on menopause (2024).43 This included the statements, ‘consider HRT to alleviate depressive symptoms (not meeting the criteria for a diagnosis of depression) with onset around the same time as other symptoms associated with menopause’ and ‘consider menopause-specific cognitive behavioural therapy as an option for people who have sleep problems (such as night-time awakening) in association with vasomotor symptoms’.

It may be difficult for readers to identify when conflicts are present, as was noted particularly on YouTube. The format and type of information presented may lend itself to natural statements of interests or, for example, the ability to be anonymous. However, given the limited knowledge on how individuals interpret this information, statements of interest cannot be relied on to mitigate the impact of conflicts. The lack of truly independent advice online was noted, that is, that which had no commercial alignment or affiliation. This is important because such information does exist (eg, that provided through NICE) but was not, in our study, prominent on searched websites. Poor quality information on social media may cause NHS workload to increase without benefiting patients, via use of appointments and prescriptions which cannot meet hopes. Policy work could consider working with social media providers to make high quality independent advice appear more prominently on key health topics, and advice that is not aligned with best practice less prominent or not visible.

Further work could include the analysis of risks and side effects on social media, qualitative work to understand how information about HRT on social media is judged and used by women considering their options with regards to claims and apparent conflicts between health professionals and conflicts of interest. Further work should also explore ways to ensure that women get high value out of appointments and prescriptions related to menopause via use of independent and accurate information.

Conclusion

A wide range of claims is made for HRT (MHT) for menopause. Many of these are outwith the recommendations in the BNF/NICE, which may lead to unrealised hopes for their effect. Conflicts of interest are common but may not be highly visible. Information free of commercial interests is relatively uncommon. Means to improve the ranking of high quality and independent information on social media platforms should be considered.
