Virome analysis uncovers the tomato virosphere and a novel recombinant begomovirus–alphasatellite complex causing leaf curl disease in India
Authors: Mohana Pradeep R K, Kavi Sidharthan V, Nagendran Krishnan, Sivagnanapazham K, Vignesh P, Boopathi N, Raveendran M, Varanavasiappan S, Senthil Raja G, Indurani C, Karthikeyan G
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBegomoviruses are circular single-stranded DNA viruses transmitted by the whitefly Bemisia tabaci and are major causal agents of leaf curl disease in tomato (Solanum lycopersicum L.). The present study aimed to comprehensively characterize the virome associated with symptomatic tomato plants collected from major agro-ecological regions of Tamil Nadu, India, using high-throughput sequencing (HTS).ResultsHTS based virome analysis of tomato plants exhibiting severe leaf curl symptoms revealed a complex viral community. The virosphere includes multiple begomoviruses, namely tomato leaf curl New Delhi virus (ToLCNDV), tomato leaf curl Bangalore virus (ToLCBaV), tomato leaf curl Karnataka virus, and chilli leaf curl virus (ChiLCV), along with associated alpha- and betasatellites. In addition, RNA viruses including pepper virus A (PepVA), chilli veinal mottle virus (ChiVMoV), and tomato chlorosis virus (ToCV) were also identified, indicating frequent occurrence of mixed infections. Full-length or coding-complete genome sequences were recovered for most viral components, whereas partial contigs were obtained for Southern tomato virus (STV) and tobacco vein distorting virus (TVDV). Notably, a novel recombinant monopartite begomovirus, tentatively named tomato leaf curl Dindigul virus (ToLCDiV), showed < 89.82% nucleotide identity with known begomovirus sequences. In addition, a distinct alphasatellite, tomato leaf curl Madurai alphasatellite (ToLCMdA) showed 86.74% nucleotide identity with known alphasatellites, supporting its distinctiveness based on ICTV species demarcation criteria. Recombination analysis suggested that ToLCDiV may have originated from parental lineages related to ToLCBaV and tomato leaf curl Gujarat virus (ToLCGV), with recombination breakpoints in the AC1, AC4, and intergenic regions.ConclusionThis study revealed a complex tomato virome associated with leaf curl disease in Tamil Nadu, driven by mixed viral infections and recombination. The detection of the putative novel recombinant begomovirus ToLCDiV and distinct alphasatellite ToLCMdA highlights the emergence of new virus–satellite components and emphasizes the need for continued virome surveillance and integrated disease management in tomato production systems.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12870-026-09443-4.Keywords: Tomato, Virome, Begomovirus, High throughput sequencing (HTS), Recombination

Introduction

Tomato (Solanum lycopersicum L.) is one of the most economically important vegetable crops cultivated widely in tropical and subtropical regions. Globally, tomato production continues to expand due to its high nutritional value and market demand. According to recent FAO estimates, global tomato production reached 188.5 million metric tonnes with a harvested area coverage of 5.12 million hectares in 2024 [1]. In India, total growing area of tomato is about 0.81 million ha with a production of 20.6 million metric tonnes/ha. Tamil Nadu ranks 10th position in total tomato growing areas ~ 38.13 thousand hectares with an annual production of around 1.2 million tonnes [2].

Despite its economic significance, tomato productivity is severely constrained by a wide range of biotic stresses, among which viral diseases are particularly destructive. To date, more than 180 virus species have been reported to infect tomato, causing diverse disease symptoms and significant yield losses [3]. In recent decades, there has been a marked increase in the emergence and spread of tomato-infecting viruses, particularly those belonging to the genera Begomovirus, Orthotospovirus, Tobamovirus, Potyvirus and Crinivirus [4–9]. Among these, begomoviruses (family Geminiviridae) represent the most dominant and economically important group, primarily responsible for tomato leaf curl disease (ToLCD), a major constraint to tomato cultivation in the Indian subcontinent [10, 11].

Tomato leaf curl disease was first reported in India by Vasudeva and Sam Raj [12] and has since evolved into a complex disease system involving multiple begomovirus species, strains, and variants. Both bipartite and monopartite begomoviruses have been implicated in disease development. For instance, tomato leaf curl New Delhi virus (ToLCNDV), a bipartite begomovirus, requires both DNA-A and DNA-B components for systemic infection [13, 14]. In contrast, monopartite begomoviruses such as tomato leaf curl Bangalore virus (ToLCBaV) and tomato leaf curl Karnataka virus (ToLCKV) have been widely reported in southern India [15, 16]. Beyond begomoviruses, several RNA viruses have increasingly been reported infecting tomato, including pepper virus A (PepVA; genus Carlavirus), tomato chlorosis virus (ToCV; genus Crinivirus) and chilli veinal mottle virus (ChiVMoV; genus Potyvirus) [17–19]. The concurrent occurrence of DNA and RNA viruses in the same host system highlights the growing complexity of viral disease etiology in tomato.

Although substantial research has been conducted on tomato-infecting viruses in India, most studies have primarily focused on begomoviruses, with limited emphasis on the broader virome, particularly RNA viruses and their interactions within mixed infections. This narrow focus restricts a comprehensive understanding of the tomato virosphere under natural field conditions. In recent years, high-throughput sequencing (HTS)-based virome analysis has emerged as a powerful, unbiased approach for simultaneous detection and characterization of diverse viral populations, including novel and low-abundance viruses, thereby providing deeper insights into viral diversity, evolution, and epidemiology. In this context, the present study aims to investigate and profile the viral communities associated with leaf curl disease of tomato using HTS based virome analysis across major tomato growing regions of Tamil Nadu. By integrating molecular validation with genome level analyses, this study aims to profile the diversity of DNA and RNA viruses associated with symptomatic tomato plants, identify and characterize novel and recombinant viral entities, and elucidate their evolutionary relationships and potential epidemiological significance. The findings are expected to enhance our understanding of the tomato virosphere and provide valuable insights for developing effective disease management strategies.

Materials and methods

Symptomatology and sample collection

A systematic roving survey was conducted across major tomato-growing agro-ecological zones of Tamil Nadu, including Coimbatore (TCB), Dharmapuri (TDH), Krishnagiri (TKH), Dindigul (TDG), and Madurai (TMD), during the 2025–2026 cropping season and the surveyed locations are shown in Supplementary Figure S1. Field observations were recorded at approximately 60 days after sowing (DAS), corresponding to the active vegetative stage. Tomato plants exhibiting typical virus-like symptoms, such as upward and downward leaf curling, puckering, leaf twisting, mosaic mottling, interveinal chlorosis, vein thickening, reduced leaf size, shortened internodes, and stunted growth were selected for sampling. A total of 15 symptomatic leaves were collected from five locations. At each location, three symptomatic leaves were collected from a single representative plant showing diverse symptom expression. The three leaves from each plant were pooled and considered as one location-specific composite sample, resulting in five composite samples. The leaf samples were gently rinsed with sterile distilled water to remove surface contaminants, immediately immersed in RNAlater™ Stabilization Solution (Sigma-Aldrich, USA, Cat. No. R0901), transported under cold conditions, and stored at − 80 °C until further analysis.

RNA extraction, library preparation and high-throughput sequencing

Total RNA was extracted from symptomatic leaf tissues using TRIzol (Sigma-Aldrich, USA, Cat. No. T9424) following the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA was further purified using the MN Nucleospin® RNA Cleanup Kit (Macherey-Nagel, Germany, Cat. No. 740948). The concentration and purity of RNA were assessed using a NanoDrop™ 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and integrity was evaluated by agarose gel electrophoresis (Lonza, Belgium). For high-throughput sequencing, RNA extracted from each location-specific composite sample was used to prepare one library. Thus, five location-specific composite libraries corresponding to TCB, TDH, TKH, TDG and TMD were constructed and sequenced individually. Ribosomal RNA (rRNA) depletion was performed using the QIAseq FastSelect rRNA Plant Kit (QIAGEN, Cat. No. 334315) to enrich viral RNA. Sequencing libraries were prepared using the KAPA HyperPrep Kit (Roche, Cat. No. KK8544) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Library concentration was quantified using Qubit 4.0 fluorometer (Thermo Fischer Scientific, Cat. No. Q33231) with the Qubit 1X dsDNA high sensitivity assay kit, and fragment size distribution was assessed using an Aligent2100 Bioanalyzer with DNA High-Sensitivity chips (Aligent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). The prepared libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (San Diego, CA, USA) using paired-end chemistry (2 × 150 bp) with the S4 Reagent Kit v1.5 at M/s. Nucleome Informatics Private Limited, Hyderabad, India.

Bioinformatic analysis and virus identification

Raw sequencing reads from five location-specific composite libraries were processed using the Galaxy platform (v26.0.rc1). Sequencing depth was recorded separately for each composite library (Table 1). Quality assessment was performed using FastQC (v0.12.1), and adapter sequences along with low-quality bases (Phred quality score ≤ 30) were trimmed using Trimmomatic v0.39 [20]. High-quality reads from each library were assembled independently using MEGAHIT v1.2.9 [21] and rnaSPAdes v4.2.0 [22] to generate contigs. Assembled contigs were subjected to BLAST+ v2.16.1 [23] using BLASTx (e-value cutoff: 1 × 10⁻⁵) against the NCBI viral protein sequences. Virus identification was performed separately for each location-specific library, and mixed viral infections were inferred when multiple virus-specific contigs were detected within the same composite library. Therefore, mixed infections were interpreted at the pooled library/location level [24]. Putative viral contigs were further analyzed for open reading frames (ORFs) using the NCBI ORF finder (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/orffinder/). Functional annotation was performed using BLASTp against the non-redundant protein database, Pfam for conserved domain identification (https://www.genome.jp/tools/motif/) and Expasy tools for prediction of molecular weight and isoelectric point (https://web.expasy.org/compute_pi/).

Genome amplification and cloning of novel viral components

Based on HTS-derived sequences, specific primers were designed to amplify the full-length genome of the putative novel begomovirus, tomato leaf curl Dindigul virus (ToLCDiV; ~2.7 kb), and its associated alphasatellite, tomato leaf curl Madurai alphasatellite (ToLCMdA; ~1.3 kb) (Table 2). Total genomic DNA was extracted from symptomatic samples collected from TDG and TMD using the CTAB method. PCR amplification was performed in 10 µL reaction volumes containing 5× Red Dye PCR Master Mix (Amplicon, Denmark), 0.5 µM of each forward and reverse primer, and 50 ng of template DNA. The thermal cycling conditions consisted of an initial denaturation at 94 °C for 5 min, followed by 35 cycles of 95 °C for 1 min, primer-specific annealing for 1 min, and extension at 72 °C for 1 min per kb, with a final extension at 72 °C for 10 min. Amplified products were resolved on 1% agarose gels, and DNA fragments of the expected size were purified using the GeneAll Expin™ Combo GP Miniprep Kit (GeneAll Biotechnology Co., Ltd., South Korea) and cloned into the pGEM-T Easy vector (Promega, Madison, WI, USA). Recombinant plasmids were transformed into Escherichia coli DH5α competent cells, screened using M13 universal primers, and confirmed by colony PCR. Positive clones were sequenced bidirectionally using Sanger sequencing (Barcode Biosciences Pvt. Ltd., Bengaluru, India).

Experimental validation of viral components

To validate HTS results, PCR and reverse transcription PCR (RT-PCR) assays were performed using virus-specific primers. Genomic DNA and total RNA were extracted from stored samples using the CTAB [25] and TRIzol [26] methods, respectively. Detection of DNA viruses was carried out by PCR targeting DNA-A (ToLCNDV, ToLCBaV, ToLCKV, ChiLCV), DNA-B (ToLCNDV), alphasatellites, and betasatellites (Table 3). RNA viruses (ChiVMoV, ToCV, and PepVA) were detected by RT-PCR targeting conserved regions such as the polyprotein and RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (RdRp) genes (Table 3). The HTS-based detection of multiple viral contigs within the same location-wise pooled library was further supported by PCR/RT-PCR validation using virus-specific primers. The validated full-length and coding-complete viral sequences recovered from HTS analysis were deposited in NCBI GenBank, and the corresponding accession numbers are provided in Table 4.

Phylogenetic analysis and sequence identity

Full-length and coding-complete viral genome sequences obtained in this study were aligned with reference sequences retrieved from the NCBI GenBank database using MUSCLE. Phylogenetic trees were constructed using the Maximum Likelihood method implemented in MEGA version 12, with the Tamura 3-parameter substitution model and 1,000 bootstrap replicates to assess branch support [27]. Pairwise nucleotide sequence identity was calculated using the Sequence Demarcation Tool (SDT v1.3) based on ClustalW alignments [28]. Recombination analysis was performed using Recombination Detection Program (RDP) v4.101 with the default settings [29]. Nine recombination detection methods implemented in the program, namely RDP, GENECONV, BootScan, MaxChi, Chimaera, SiScan, LARD, PhylPro and 3SEQ, were used for the analysis. To avoid dependence on a single detection method, only recombination events supported by at least four out of the nine methods were considered for final interpretation (4/9 principle). Events detected by fewer than four methods were excluded from the reported results. The P-values were calculated using Bonferroni correction within the program, and recombination events were considered statistically significant at P < 0.05 [30].

Results

Field survey, symptomatology and sample collection

A systematic roving survey conducted across major tomato-growing regions of Tamil Nadu during the 2025–2026 season revealed the widespread occurrence of virus-like symptoms in field-grown tomato plants, with disease severity ranging from 43% to 65% across different surveyed locations. Symptomatic plants were observed predominantly at the active vegetative stage (60 DAS), when disease severity was highest. Affected plants exhibited characteristic symptoms including upward and downward leaf curling, puckering, leaf twisting, mosaic mottling, vein thickening, reduced leaf lamina, shortened internodes, stunting, and overall loss of vigour (Fig. 1A–E). Symptomatic leaf tissues representing the range of observed disease phenotypes were collected using RNAlater from five locations and preserved for molecular analyses.

Virome profiling, and identification of viruses from symptomatic tomato samples

Ribosomal RNA depleted libraries prepared from symptomatic tomato samples were subjected to high throughput virome sequencing followed by de novo assembly and similarity-based annotation. Sequencing output, assembled contigs, read depth/genome coverage and virus-mapped read proportions for each location-specific composite library are summarized in Table 1. Virome analysis revealed a complex viral community comprising both DNA and RNA viruses, indicating frequent mixed infections in field samples. Among DNA viruses, multiple begomoviruses (family Geminiviridae) were identified, including complete or coding-complete genomes of tomato leaf curl New Delhi virus (ToLCNDV; DNA-A and DNA-B), tomato leaf curl Bangalore virus (ToLCBaV; DNA-A), chilli leaf curl virus (ChiLCV; DNA-A), and tomato leaf curl Karnataka virus (ToLCKV; DNA-A). In addition, three associated betasatellites viz., chilli leaf curl betasatellite (ChiLCB), papaya leaf curl betasatellite (PaLCuB), and tomato leaf curl Bangalore betasatellite (ToLCBaB) along with one alphasatellite, chilli leaf curl India alphasatellite (ChiLCIA), were detected. The RNA virome consisted of pepper virus A (PepVA; genus Carlavirus), tomato chlorosis virus (ToCV; genus Crinivirus), and chilli veinal mottle virus (ChiVMoV; genus Potyvirus). Furthermore, partial contigs corresponding to southern tomato virus (STV; genus Amalgavirus) and tobacco vein distorting virus (TVDV; genus Polerovirus) were also recovered. Notably, a previously uncharacterized begomovirus genome, designated tomato leaf curl Dindigul virus (ToLCDiV), together with a distinct alphasatellite, tomato leaf curl Madurai alphasatellite (ToLCMdA), were identified from symptomatic samples.

Molecular validation of identified viruses

The HTS-derived virome results were validated using targeted PCR and RT-PCR assays on symptomatic samples. Specific amplification of begomovirus genomic components confirmed the presence of ToLCNDV, ToLCBaV, ToLCKV, and ChiLCV, together with associated DNA-B, alpha, and betasatellite molecules. Similarly, RT-PCR amplification using gene-specific primers confirmed the presence of ChiVMoV, ToCV and PepVA in the corresponding samples. For the newly identified viral components, three overlapping primer pairs successfully amplified the complete genome of ToLCDiV (~ 2.7 kb), while a specific primer pair amplified the full-length ToLCMdA (~ 1.3 kb). These results independently validated the sequencing data and confirmed the occurrence of complex mixed infections, including novel viral entities, in symptomatic tomato plants.

Genetic diversity and phylogenetic analysis of known begomoviruses and associated satellites

Comparative sequence analysis of begomoviruses and associated satellites recovered from the tomato virome showed high nucleotide identity with previously reported isolates from the Indian subcontinent. The DNA-A component of ToLCBaV isolates (TKH, TDG, TDH and TCB) shared up to 97.8% nucleotide identity, clustered with the reference isolate PQ587364 and showed complete conservation of coat protein (CP) region. ToLCNDV isolates (TDG and TMD) shared > 95% sequence identity, clustered with the isolate PQ568245.1 and 99.2% amino acid identity in the coat protein. The ChiLCV isolate (TMD) from Madurai shared > 98% nucleotide identity and clustered with HM007104 isolate and complete CP conservation at the amino acid level. The coding-complete ToLCKV isolate shared 98.0% nucleotide identity with KF612317 and 99.6% amino acid identity in the coat protein (CP) region with AHB20289.1 (Fig. 2A; Supplementary Figure S3A). The DNA-B components of ToLCNDV isolates from TMD and TDG exhibited 96.71% nucleotide identity and clustered with PQ587365.1, while the nuclear shuttle protein (NSP) region showed 98.58% amino acid identity (Fig. 2D; Supplementary Figure S3D. Among the satellite molecules, ToLCBaB, PaLCuB and ChiLCB shared 93.9%, 93.2% and 98.10% nucleotide identity with PQ614113, JX040472 and PQ524043, respectively (Fig. 2B; Supplementary Figure S3B). ChiLCIA shared 91.09% nucleotide identity with PP951072.1 and clustered with the same isolate in phylogenetic analysis (Fig. 2C; Supplementary Figure S3C). Recombination analysis revealed interspecific recombination events in ToLCBaV isolates involving the AC1 and AC4 regions, with tomato leaf curl Bangalore virus (Z48182) as the major parent and tomato leaf curl Gujarat virus (AY190290) as the minor parent. Similar interspecific recombination patterns were detected in the ToLCKV isolate, with breakpoints spanning the AC1, AC4 and intergenic regions. The event was supported by five RDP methods, with tomato leaf curl Iran virus (AY297924) identified as the putative major parent and tomato leaf curl Gujarat virus (AY190290) as the putative minor parent (Table 5). The graphical representation of the recombination breakpoint and parental region is provided in Supplementary Figure S4 and C.

Characterization of the novel begomovirus ToLCDiV and associated alphasatellite ToLCMdA

Coding-complete genomes of ToLCDiV (from Dindigul) and ToLCMdA (from Madurai) were recovered and analyzed in detail (Fig. 3). The ToLCDiV genome shared ≤ 90.5% nucleotide identity with currently available begomovirus sequences in GenBank, supporting its status as a distinct species based on current taxonomic criteria. Likewise, ToLCMdA exhibited only 86.74% identity with the closest known alphasatellite sequence, indicating substantial divergence. Recombination analysis detected strong and consistent signals across seven RDP methods in the ToLCDiV genome, with breakpoints located in the AC1, AC4, and intergenic regions. Tomato leaf curl Bangalore virus (Z48182) was identified as the major parent and tomato leaf curl Gujarat virus (AY190290) as the minor parent, with highly significant p-values ranging from 2.88 × 10⁻³³ to 1.01 × 10⁻⁴⁴. However, no recombination events were detected in ToLCMdA (Table 5). Graphical representation of the recombination breakpoints in ToLCDiV is provided in Supplementary Figure S4A. Phylogenetic analysis placed ToLCDiV within the broader ToLCBaV lineage, but it formed a distinct branch, supporting its recombinant origin followed by sequence divergence (Supplementary Figure S2A). Pairwise identity analysis further supported its distinctiveness from known begomoviruses (Supplementary Figure S3E). In contrast, ToLCMdA clustered separately from reported alphasatellites from India, Pakistan, Brazil and Venezuela, indicating a distinct evolutionary lineage (Supplementary Figure S2B). This divergence was also supported by pairwise sequence identity analysis (Supplementary Figure S3F).

Discussion

Viral diseases of tomato cause considerable economic losses worldwide and remain a major constraint to sustainable production. Accurate identification of the viruses associated with disease outbreaks is therefore essential for understanding pathogen diversity, tracking viral evolution and developing effective management strategies. Recent HTS-based tomato virome studies have shown that tomato viral diseases involve diverse viral communities rather than single-virus infections. Zisi et al. [31]. detected 43 eukaryotic viral species from tomato samples collected across 13 countries, while Kwak et al. [24]. reported seven tomato-associated viruses in Korea, including the newly reported olive latent virus 1 (OLV1) and tobacco necrosis virus A (TNVA). Similarly, Lett et al. [32]. through virome analysis, identified a novel Bemisia tabaci transmitted polerovirus, tomato necrotic yellowing virus (ToNYV), while Ma et al. [33]. demonstrated the movement of potato virus Y (PVY), broad bean wilt virus 1 (BBWV1) and a novel Ilarvirus, Solanum nigrum ilarvirus 1, between tomato and the neighbouring weed host Solanum nigrum. These studies highlight the importance of HTS based virome approaches in detecting novel viruses, understanding virus diversity and tracing possible virus movement between crop plants and associated weed hosts. However, such comprehensive virome level studies on tomato leaf curl disease remain limited in India. Therefore, our present study provides new insights into the complex association of DNA viruses, RNA viruses and satellite molecules in symptomatic tomato plants from Tamil Nadu.

The present study identified ToLCNDV, ToLCBaV, ToLCKV, ChiLCV, PepVA, ToCV, ChiVMoV, and several associated alpha and betasatellites from symptomatic tomato plants. In addition, a distinct de novo begomovirus, ToLCDiV, and a novel alphasatellite, ToLCMdA, were detected and characterized. The concurrent occurrence of these viral agents demonstrates that tomato leaf curl disease is not caused by a single pathogen but rather represents a dynamic and complex pathosystem shaped by mixed infections and continuous viral evolution.

ToLCNDV is a bipartite begomovirus that poses a serious threat not only to tomato but also to several economically important crops belonging to the families Solanaceae, Cucurbitaceae, Malvaceae, Fabaceae, and Euphorbiaceae [34–39]. Infection is commonly associated with severe leaf curling, crinkling, mosaic mottling, vein clearing, and plant stunting, often resulting in substantial yield losses [13, 40, 41]. Since its first report in India, ToLCNDV has expanded rapidly across South and Southeast Asia and into the Mediterranean basin, including Pakistan, Bangladesh, Sri Lanka, Indonesia, Malaysia, Taiwan, Thailand, Iran, Italy, Spain, and Tunisia [35, 42]. Its detection in the present study confirms its continued prevalence and epidemiological importance in southern India.

In contrast to bipartite begomoviruses, monopartite viruses such as ToLCBaV and ToLCKV have emerged as major constraints to tomato cultivation, particularly in southern and south-western India [15, 16, 43]. These viruses were also detected in the present investigation, indicating their persistent circulation in tomato ecosystems. Furthermore, recombinant strains of ChiLCV have previously been reported to cause enation leaf curl disease in tomato [44]. The detection of ChiLCV in the present study further emphasizes the expanding host range and adaptive potential of begomoviruses. The widespread occurrence of both bipartite and monopartite begomoviruses, together with frequent recombination events, reflects their remarkable evolutionary plasticity, broad host adaptability, and efficient transmission by the whitefly vector Bemisia tabaci [36, 45, 46].

Sequence identity and phylogenetic analyses showed that the full-length DNA-A components of ToLCNDV, ToLCBaV, and ToLCKV shared high nucleotide identity (> 95%) with previously reported tomato-infecting isolates from India, suggesting the continued dominance of established viral lineages in the region. Similarly, the ChiLCV isolate shared > 98% identity with a chilli derived isolate, indicating host-range expansion. Phylogenetic clustering of the study isolates with tomato and chilli infecting reference isolates further supported their close evolutionary relationships. These observations suggest that neighbouring cultivation of solanaceous hosts such as chilli and tomato, together with the movement of shared whitefly vector populations, may facilitate cross-host transmission and maintenance of viral reservoirs across cropping systems.

A major outcome of this study was the detection of several RNA viruses, namely PepVA, ToCV, and ChiVMoV, in symptomatic tomato plants. Earlier reports of these viruses in India were limited to specific regions [17–19], whereas their detection in Tamil Nadu suggests a wider geographical distribution than previously recognized. The simultaneous occurrence of DNA and RNA viruses in the same host system significantly increases disease complexity. Mixed infections are known to influence symptom severity, alter virus accumulation, modify vector transmission efficiency, and complicate symptom-based diagnosis. Therefore, the presence of multiple viral taxa in the same plant underscores the limitations of single-target diagnostic approaches and highlights the necessity for broad-spectrum surveillance tools such as HTS.

Mixed viral infections may have significant biological and epidemiological consequences because interactions among unrelated viruses can modify symptom expression, tissue tropism, virus accumulation and vector transmission. In begomovirus complexes, recombination and pseudorecombination during mixed infections are major sources of genetic variability [47, 48]. In addition, heteroencapsidation has been shown to facilitate whitefly transmission of pseudorecombinants, as reported for tomato leaf curl Palampur virus and tomato leaf curl New Delhi virus [49]. Mixed infections may also alter host responses and expand tissue invasion; for example, tomato rugose mosaic virus, which is normally phloem-limited, was reported to invade mesophyll tissues during co-infection with tomato yellow spot virus [50, 51]. Similarly, synergistic interactions between tomato chlorosis virus and tomato spotted wilt virus in tomato can intensify disease severity and may even result in plant death in susceptible hosts [52]. Therefore, the simultaneous detection of DNA and RNA viruses in the present study suggests that mixed infections may have an important impact on tomato leaf curl disease severity, symptom development, virus spread and disease management under field conditions. In the present study, recombination analysis revealed interspecific recombination events in ToLCBaV and ToLCKV, mainly involving the AC1, AC4, and intergenic regions. These genomic regions are functionally important, as AC1 encodes the replication-associated protein (Rep), AC4 is linked to pathogenicity and suppression of host defence responses, and the intergenic region contains essential regulatory elements for replication and transcription. Recombination within these regions may generate variants with improved fitness, altered pathogenicity, enhanced host adaptation, or increased epidemiological success. Similar evolutionary processes have been reported in other begomoviruses associated with severe disease outbreaks [10, 40].

The identification of ToLCDiV represents one of the major outcomes of the present investigation. Its genome organization showed typical begomovirus features, including predicted ORFs and an intergenic region (Fig. 3). Pairwise nucleotide identity analysis showed that ToLCDiV shared ≤ 90.5% identity with currently reported begomoviruses, which is below the ICTV species demarcation threshold of 91% for begomoviruses [53] (Supplementary Figure S3E). This, together with its distinct phylogenetic placement within the broader ToLCBaV lineage (Supplementary Figure S2A) and its recombination profile, supports its designation as a putative novel begomovirus species. Multiple recombination detection methods consistently indicated that ToLCDiV likely originated from parental lineages related to ToLCBaV and tomato leaf curl Gujarat virus, with breakpoints in the AC1, AC4 and intergenic regions. Thus, the combined evidence from genome organization, pairwise identity matrix, phylogenetic analysis and recombination analysis strengthens the taxonomic assignment of ToLCDiV. The co-localization of recombination events within these adaptive regions further highlights their possible role in generating host-adapted and potentially more virulent begomovirus variants [54–58].

The associated alphasatellite, ToLCMdA, also exhibited substantial sequence divergence. Its genome organization showed characteristic alphasatellite-like features (Fig. 3), and pairwise identity analysis showed only 86.74% nucleotide identity with the closest known alphasatellite sequence (Supplementary Figure S3F). This value is below the 88% species demarcation threshold used for alphasatellites, supporting its distinct status [59]. Phylogenetic analysis further placed ToLCMdA in a separate lineage from reported alphasatellites from India, Pakistan, Brazil and Venezuela (Supplementary Figure S2B). In contrast to ToLCDiV, no recombination events were detected in ToLCMdA, suggesting that it has evolved through a different trajectory. Although alphasatellites rely on helper begomoviruses for movement, encapsidation, and vector transmission, their biological role remains variable and incompletely understood. Previous studies have suggested that alphasatellites may modulate symptom expression, influence helper virus accumulation, or alter host defence responses in a context-dependent manner. Therefore, further biological characterization of ToLCMdA, particularly in association with ToLCDiV and other helper viruses, will be necessary to clarify its contribution to pathogenicity, host adaptation, and disease ecology.

The identification of ToLCDiV and ToLCMdA further expands the known diversity within the genus Begomovirus and its associated satellite molecules, reinforcing their prominence in the global plant virosphere. The concurrent presence of these newly identified viral entities together with ToLCNDV, ToLCBaV, ToLCKV, ChiLCV, PepVA, ToCV, and ChiVMoV reveals a highly dynamic viral ecosystem maintained through mixed infections, host shifts, and recombination-driven evolution. Such complexity presents serious challenges for disease diagnosis, resistance breeding, and vector management.

From an applied perspective, the present findings underscore the urgent need for continuous surveillance programs integrating HTS, targeted molecular diagnostics, infectious clone-based biological validation, vector monitoring and studies on interactions among different viral components to enable early detection and characterization of emerging viral variants. Although the present study provides HTS-based genome recovery, phylogenetic and recombination analyses, and PCR/RT-PCR-based molecular validation of the detected viral components, further infectious clone-based assays are required to confirm the biological infectivity, pathogenic association, viral component interactions and host range of the putative novel begomovirus and associated satellite molecules. Disease management strategies should focus on the use of virus-free seedlings, strict weed and alternate host management, effective suppression of whitefly populations, and deployment of broad-spectrum or durable host resistance. Given the rapid evolutionary potential of begomoviruses, resistance breeding programs should evaluate germplasm against diverse and mixed viral populations rather than against single representative isolates.

Conclusion

This study revealed a complex tomato virosphere associated with leaf curl disease in southern India, comprising DNA viruses, RNA viruses, and satellite molecules. The detection of the putative novel recombinant begomovirus ToLCDiV and the distinct alphasatellite ToLCMdA emphasizes the continuing emergence of new viral entities in tomato agroecosystems. These findings emphasize the need for continued virome surveillance, molecular diagnostics and integrated disease management approaches to safeguard tomato production and ensure long-term agricultural sustainability.
