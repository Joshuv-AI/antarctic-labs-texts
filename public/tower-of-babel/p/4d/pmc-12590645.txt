Evaluating the impact of video-only, video with group discussion, and collaborative 360VR on abdominal examination training for first-semester medical students and medicine with industrial specialization
Authors: Rameshnath Krishnasamy, Malene Plejdrup Hansen, Louise Thomsen Schmidt Arenholt, Jacob Gorm Davidsen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundImmersive technologies are increasingly explored in medical education to supplement traditional teaching methods. Instructional videos provide consistent demonstrations but often lack opportunities for interaction and reflection. We compared video-only instruction, video instruction combined with facilitated peer discussion, and a collaborative 360° virtual reality (VR) co-viewing environment for teaching abdominal examination to first-semester medical students.MethodsIn this three-arm comparative pilot study (non-randomized), 30 students were allocated to video alone (n = 11), video plus discussion (n = 10), or collaborative 360° VR (n = 9). All groups viewed the same professionally produced 10-minute instructional video. The discussion group then engaged in a 20-minute guided conversation; VR participants co-viewed the same video in a shared 360° environment with teleportation between camera angles and annotation tools. Performance was assessed using a 16-item checklist scored by blinded examiners (0–16 points). Learner perceptions were measured using the UEQ-S (8 items; six scales) plus open-ended questions. Between-group differences in checklist scores were tested with two-sided Mann–Whitney U tests.ResultsMedian checklist scores were 14.0 (interquartile range [IQR] 13.0–15.0) for video, 16.0 (IQR 14.5–16.0) for video + discussion, and 12.0 (IQR 12.0–13.0) for VR. Pairwise Mann–Whitney tests showed that video + discussion outperformed VR (U = 82.5, p = 0.0019) and video outperformed VR (U = 79.5, p = 0.0228), while video did not differ significantly from video + discussion (U = 32.0, p = 0.101). Questionnaires indicated high satisfaction with video and discussion. VR participants reported strong engagement and motivation but lower ratings for navigation and self-perceived preparedness.ConclusionSupplementing instructional videos with structured peer discussion produced the highest immediate competency, supporting social-constructivist approaches to skills training. Collaborative VR did not improve short-term performance but enhanced engagement and may serve as an adjunct when combined with clear learning objectives and user training. Larger studies are needed to explore long-term retention and optimize integration of immersive technologies into medical curricula.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-025-08128-0.

Background

Medical education has been transformed by successive waves of technological innovation. Traditional instructional methods centered on lectures, textbooks and live demonstrations, but critiques argue that “all that glitters is not gold” in modern medical education [4], because these approaches often lack interactivity and feedback. Advances in digital learning environments - such as virtual reality (VR) and augmented reality (AR) - offer opportunities to address these shortcomings [23]. As Falah et al. [8] note, VR can provide safe, realistic environments for anatomy education, and augmented reality applications have been shown to improve students’ experiences and learning outcomes [7].

Despite their advantages, video-based resources remain largely passive; they demonstrate procedures but do not ensure active practice or collaborative learning. Educators have therefore paired instructional videos with group discussion to foster reflection and peer learning. This approach aligns with problem-based learning (PBL), which emphasizes social interaction and contextual understanding [20]. Helle and Säljö [9] similarly argue that collaborating with digital tools and peers can enhance learning in medical education. Moreover, recent work by Kosko et al. [16] suggests that preservice teachers attending to 360° video notice different pedagogical elements than those watching standard video, indicating that immersion may affect professional noticing.

Educational theory further underscores the need to align outcomes, activities and assessments. Biggs’s [1] concept of constructive alignment requires defining learning outcomes in advance and designing teaching and assessment to support those outcomes [1, 2]. In the context of abdominal examination, the desired outcome is the ability to perform inspection, palpation, percussion and auscultation competently; teaching should therefore engage learners in activities (observation, discussion, hands-on practice) that support these skills. Experiential learning models [15] highlight the importance of concrete experience and reflective observation, while social constructivism emphasizes learning through interaction. These frameworks justify exploring whether collaborative VR - an immersive, interactive environment - can enhance skill acquisition beyond video-based modalities.

A growing body of evidence supports the pedagogical benefits of VR. Boubakri et al. [3] compared a VR classroom (V-Class) with a videoconferencing platform and found that VR participants reported significantly higher presence (median 3.25 vs. 2.33) and better performance, collaboration and engagement. Kadri et al. [11] developed the Immersive Virtual Anatomy Laboratory (IVAL); students rated it highly for engagement and learning enhancement. In a follow-up comparative study, students using IVAL demonstrated a 55.95% increase in vocabulary scores and an 18.75% reduction in task-completion times relative to traditional learning [12]. Longitudinal research on the Collaborative IVAL (C-IVAL) showed that mean knowledge scores increased from 2.48 to 3.94 and that collaborative VR yielded higher perceived usefulness and intention to use than non-collaborative VR [13]. These studies align with earlier work on 360VR in problem-based learning contexts [5, 6] and with findings that VR-based education can enhance spatial understanding and student engagement [7, 9].

Against this backdrop, we critically appraise three instructional modalities - video-only instruction, video with group discussion and collaborative 360° VR - for teaching abdominal examination to first-semester medical students and medicine-with-industrial-specialization students. Abdominal examination is a core clinical skill requiring both procedural knowledge and contextual judgment; it is traditionally taught through direct patient contact, which became challenging during the COVID-19 pandemic. By explicitly linking our instructional modalities to learning theories and situating them within current VR research, we aim to provide a robust conceptual framework for evaluating how collaboration and immersion contribute to clinical skills training. Our study examines whether the immersive and interactive qualities of collaborative VR can enhance engagement and potentially support long-term skill retention compared with more traditional instructional approaches.

Methods

Study design & allocation

This was an exploratory three-arm comparative pilot study. Thirty first-semester students volunteered and were scheduled into one of three session types (video only; video + facilitated discussion; collaborative 360° VR) based on timetable availability and session capacity. We aimed for roughly even distribution; final group sizes were 11, 10 and 9 due to scheduling and one VR technical dropout.

The study followed ethical guidelines and received approval from Aalborg University’s Contract Unit; informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Participants and sample size

Students had no prior clinical training in abdominal examination and were assigned to the three study arms according to scheduling and session capacity (i.e., no formal randomization). Thirty students volunteered to participate, permitting us to assess feasibility and generate preliminary effect estimates. Similar VR pilot studies in medical education have enrolled comparable sample sizes [7]; Falah et al., [8]. While power calculations were not performed a priori, the sample size aligns with recommendations for early-phase educational interventions.

Intervention procedures

Participants received a brief verbal introduction, then watched the professionally produced instructional video used in all groups, demonstrating a complete abdominal examination (inspection, palpation, percussion and auscultation) with explanatory commentary; the video lasted approximately 10 min. After completing the scored test, participants completed a questionnaire capturing demographic information and prior VR experience.

Outcome measures

Competency assessment

The primary outcome was proficiency in abdominal examination, measured by a 16-item checklist developed by two experienced clinicians based on established clinical guidelines and informed by the literature [4, 23]. The checklist assessed critical steps such as patient positioning, inspection of abdominal contours, systematic palpation of quadrants and appropriate use of percussion and auscultation. Content validity was established through expert review by an additional clinician and an educationalist. Two independent evaluators, blinded to group assignment, scored each participant’s performance immediately following the intervention. Inter-rater reliability was assessed using Cohen’s κ (0.78–0.85), and internal consistency (Cronbach’s α) was 0.83. Scores ranged from 0 to 16, with higher scores indicating greater competency.

User experience assessment

To capture secondary outcomes related to engagement and satisfaction, we administered the UEQ-S (8-item short form), which covers six dimensions (attractiveness, perspicuity, efficiency, dependability, stimulation, novelty) [10, 19]. Items were rated on a 7-point semantic differential. Scale scores were computed per the UEQ-S manual and contextualized using the official UEQ benchmark dataset [18]. Participants in the VR group additionally completed open-ended questions on usability, presence and collaborative features.

Data analysis

The competency score (0–16) was summarized as median with interquartile range (IQR) and compared between groups using two-sided Mann–Whitney U tests. Each checklist item/learning objective was evaluated as fulfilled or not and reported as n (%); groups were compared using Chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests as appropriate. For all tests, p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Questionnaire items (UEQ-S) were summarized descriptively (medians, IQRs) without inferential testing. UEQ-S scale means were mapped to benchmark categories using the UEQ benchmark tool [10, 19].

Results

Participant characteristics

Thirty first-semester medical or medicine-with-industrial-specialization students with no prior abdominal-examination training completed the study (video = 11; video + discussion = 10; 360° VR = 9; VR questionnaire n = 10). Baseline demographics were similar across groups: gender was predominantly female (video 8/11; video + discussion 9/10; VR 6/10, plus 3/10 male and 1/10 non-binary). Median age (years) was 21.0 [21.0–22.5] for video, 20.0 [20.0–20.0] for video + discussion, and 21.0 [20.0–22.0] for VR (age available for 9/10 after data cleaning). Prior VR experience (VR-only) was 5/10.

Qualitative feedback

Open-ended comments contextualized these findings. VR participants described the session as immersive and appreciated co-presence. They valued switching camera angles and annotating to share insights, which aided spatial understanding. Several noted that learning the interface was challenging and at times distracting, consistent with lower perspicuity/efficiency ratings; suggestions included more pre-training or simplified controls. Students in the video + discussion group highlighted that peer conversation clarified procedural steps and built confidence.

Discussion

This study compared three instructional modalities - video only, video combined with facilitated discussion and collaborative 360° VR - for teaching abdominal examination skills to novice medical students. The principal finding is that students who viewed the instructional video and engaged in a structured group discussion achieved the highest checklist scores, with a median of 16/16 and narrow interquartile range. Students who viewed the video alone performed moderately well (median 14/16), while those trained in the collaborative VR environment obtained lower immediate scores (median 12/16) despite reporting greater engagement and enjoyment. These results warrant careful interpretation within contemporary learning theory and the evolving literature on immersive technologies.

From a cognitive perspective, first-semester students have limited schemas for abdominal examination, so extraneous load from navigating VR (learning the interface, selecting perspectives) can consume working memory that would otherwise support germane load (schema construction). In our 360° co-viewing, unconstrained perspective switching likely introduced split attention and search costs (Which node? Which angle?), whereas video + discussion reduced processing demands via signaling, elaboration, and scaffolding, consistent with cognitive load theory and multimedia learning principles. For novices, design choices such as pretraining, segmenting, and guided viewpoints may mitigate extraneous load and avoid the expertise-reversal problem when learners must self-direct navigation [14, 17, 21, 22].

Relationship to learning theories and constructive alignment

The superiority of video supplemented by discussion aligns with principles of problem-based and social constructivist learning. Collaborative dialogue allows students to co-construct meaning, ask questions and clarify procedural steps, thus promoting deeper understanding and better retention of complex skills. Biggs’s [1] concept of constructive alignment emphasizes that teaching activities and assessments should be designed to support intended learning outcomes. In our study, the group discussion likely acted as a formative assessment, encouraging reflective observation and conceptualization in Kolb’s [15] experiential learning cycle. In contrast, the VR condition provided an immersive 360° video experience but lacked explicit scaffolding; students navigated the VR environment independently without instructor guidance, which may have limited integration of procedural knowledge with conceptual understanding. When interpreting performance differences, note that the 360° VR co-viewing design allowed unconstrained perspective switching; novices may not consistently select vantage points that expose critical cues, which could increase cognitive load and partly explain lower immediate checklist performance relative to video plus discussion.

Comparison with existing VR literature

Our findings contrast with recent studies reporting improved performance or strong perceived effectiveness in VR environments. For example, Boubakri et al. [3] found that a VR classroom outperformed videoconferencing in measures of presence, collaboration and engagement, while Kadri et al.’s [12] Immersive Virtual Anatomy Laboratory (IVAL) achieved high satisfaction and better learning outcomes compared with traditional methods. The C-IVAL project further demonstrated that collaborative VR could enhance long-term knowledge retention and technology acceptance [13]. Several factors may explain the discrepancy. First, the clinical skill assessed in our study requires nuanced tactile and cognitive integration; current VR technology may not fully replicate haptic cues, possibly limiting skill acquisition. Second, participants received only minimal VR orientation and had no prior experience, whereas earlier VR studies often incorporate comprehensive training sessions. The low ratings for perspicuity and efficiency indicate that navigation and interface issues consumed cognitive resources that could otherwise have been devoted to learning. Finally, our VR environment delivered identical content to the video but without adaptive feedback or instructor facilitation, whereas other VR programs incorporate real-time guidance and interactive assessments.

Participants were first-semester novices oriented toward knowledge acquisition rather than integrated psychomotor-cognitive performance. Our VR condition was a non-interactive 360° co-viewing tool (perspective switching, annotation), not a hands-on simulator. Much of the VR literature involves more advanced learners, interactive platforms, or different outcomes (e.g., knowledge tests, presence, long-term retention), limiting direct comparison with our immediate checklist scores. Our findings therefore reflect constraints of early-stage learners and a non-interactive design. Future work should test staged scaffolding, extended familiarization, guided viewpoints, and interactive/haptic VR with follow-up retention and transfer.

Nevertheless, the collaborative VR group exhibited high hedonic qualities (stimulation, novelty) and strong willingness to use VR in future training. Students reported feeling more active and engaged than when watching conventional videos. These observations mirror previous research demonstrating that immersive VR fosters greater presence and motivation, factors known to enhance long-term learning and retention. Thus, while VR did not improve immediate checklist performance in our study, it may offer complementary benefits when used alongside structured pedagogy and adequate training.

Implications for curriculum design

The data suggest that adding facilitated discussion to instructional videos markedly enhances learners’ immediate procedural competence. For educators constrained by time or resources, combining high-quality video demonstrations with group discussion may be a cost-effective strategy for early skills training. Collaborative VR should not replace traditional instruction at this stage but could serve as an engaging adjunct to support motivation, presence and possibly long-term retention. To harness its potential, VR sessions should include clear learning objectives, structured guidance and sufficient time for learners to familiarize themselves with the interface. Future VR platforms should also strive to improve usability (perspicuity, efficiency and dependability) to reduce cognitive load.

Strengths and limitations

A major strength of this study is the three-arm comparative pilot design (non-randomized) with clearly defined modalities and an anonymized checklist dataset. Incorporating qualitative feedback enriched interpretation of user experience. However, several limitations must be acknowledged. The sample was small and drawn from a single institution, limiting generalizability. The assessment instrument, although reviewed by experts, has not been extensively validated beyond this study. The VR platform lacked haptic feedback and was unfamiliar to participants; both factors may have depressed performance. Finally, we measured only immediate competency; long-term retention and transfer to clinical settings were not assessed.

Future research

Given the rapid evolution of immersive technologies and pedagogical frameworks, further research should explore hybrid models that integrate VR with structured facilitation and feedback. Studies with larger, more diverse cohorts and longitudinal follow-up could determine whether the motivational benefits observed in VR translate into durable skill acquisition. Comparative trials examining different levels of VR training, presence of haptic devices and integration with other distance-learning tools (e.g., augmented reality, mixed reality) would inform optimal curricular design. Additionally, developing validated, context-specific user-experience instruments could help educators better assess the pragmatic and hedonic qualities of emerging educational technologies.

Conclusion

This three-arm comparative pilot study (non-randomized) compared the effectiveness of three modalities for teaching abdominal examination skills to novice medical students: instructional video alone, instructional video combined with facilitated peer discussion and a collaborative 360° virtual-reality environment. The principal finding is that supplementing video instruction with a structured group discussion yields the highest immediate competency scores, suggesting that the combination of visual demonstration and collaborative reflection supports effective skills acquisition. Video alone produced moderate proficiency and high student satisfaction, while the collaborative VR experience, despite eliciting strong engagement and motivation, did not translate into higher checklist performance under the conditions tested.

The results underscore the enduring value of social constructivist learning approaches and constructive alignment: activities that encourage dialogue and reflection appear to bridge the gap between passive observation and active skill performance. VR, in its current form, should be considered an adjunct rather than a replacement for established instructional strategies; its motivational benefits and immersive qualities may enhance engagement and long-term learning when coupled with adequate training and pedagogical scaffolding. Future research with larger, more diverse cohorts and longitudinal follow-up is needed to determine whether the initial enthusiasm for collaborative VR can be harnessed to sustain knowledge retention and clinical competence. Continued development of user-friendly VR platforms and integration of real-time feedback mechanisms may help realize the full potential of immersive technologies in medical education. Future studies should evaluate guided viewpoint selection, onboarding, and interactive VR to determine effects on retention and transfer.
