Plant-based protein foods are less sensitive to price changes than animal-based ones, with differences across income and education levels
Authors: Cameron McRae, Hannu Saarijärvi, Jaakko Nevalainen, Mikael Fogelholm, Yu Ma, Laurette Dubé
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Shifting demand toward plant-based proteins is an important sustainability transition, yet adoption remains limited. Here, we used longitudinal loyalty data from 29,131 Finnish consumers to estimate differences in price elasticity between plant- and animal-based proteins in Study 1. Study 2a examined socioeconomic status as a moderator of price elasticity, while Studies 2b and 2c assessed income and education separately. Study 3 compared estimates when measured at the individual versus the neighborhood level. A robustness check replicated analyses in Canada. Two-level mixed-effects regression models revealed that consumers were less price elastic to plant proteins than animal proteins. Socioeconomic status moderated both, but disparities were over three times larger for animal proteins. Income moderated price elasticity, while education’s effect was stronger for animal proteins. Neighborhood-level measures yielded similar patterns. Results were comparable in Canada. Differences in price sensitivity by socioeconomic status are central to the protein transition and should be incorporated into future sustainability interventions.

Introduction

Despite overwhelming evidence and several calls for consumers to decrease their meat consumption in favor of more plant-based diets1–4, many struggle with gaps between their attitudes, intentions, and purchasing behaviors5. Compared to proteins derived from animal sources (abbr. animal proteins), proteins derived from plants (abbr. plant proteins) require less land, cause less biodiversity loss, and reduce water pollution6. Overall, the production of plant proteins emits nearly half the carbon emissions of animal proteins7, although the measure can vary depending on the category8. Even a partial substitution of red and processed meat with plant proteins can reduce greenhouse gas emissions by 25%9; eating smaller portions of meat is also effective10. Yet, despite the availability of plant-based products increasing, the consumption of meat continues to rise11 while the adoption of plant protein lags12.

Our focus on animal- and plant-based food categories in this article reflects their central role in ecological sustainability rather than nutrient-level protein adequacy. Environmental frameworks, including the EAT–Lancet Planetary Health Diet1,4, consistently show that animal-based foods carry substantially higher greenhouse-gas emissions and overall environmental footprints than plant-based products. Although amino-acid composition differs across protein sources (e.g., lower methionine in legumes and lower lysine in grains), population-level protein adequacy is generally maintained across plant-forward diets except in specific clinical groups13. Moreover, evidence indicates minimal trade-offs between environmental sustainability and dietary healthfulness: healthier diets tend to be lower-impact14, and higher adherence to the Planetary Health Diet Index is associated with lower mortality risk15. Accordingly, the indicators used in this study are designed to capture consumer behavior relevant to sustainability transitions—specifically, shifts between animal- and plant-based food categories—rather than nutritional physiology.

To facilitate a sustainable transition toward plant proteins, an understanding of how prices influence changes in consumption behavior is essential. Price is a commonly cited barrier to adopting sustainable products5. Price sensitivity is a latent construct referring to the extent to which consumers change their consumption of a product as a function of its price changes16. Similarly, price elasticity is the econometric measure of the percentage change in demand resulting from a one-percent change in price, holding all else constant17. Little research has systematically compared price sensitivities between plant and animal-based foods beyond experiments or surveys that have had limited scope or consumer samples (for a review, see Supplement A). In addition, other factors like poor plant-based product availability in terms of variety and quality, as well as loyalty to animal-based brands, act as barriers18. The categorization of plant-based products poses a challenge since studies overlook sub-categories like legumes and group them with vegetables19. At the same time, animal-based diets are already expensive and, once whole diet costs are considered, may not necessarily be more expensive than plant-based diets20. Pricing research has not been published on emerging plant-based categories like yogurts, beverages, cheeses, and simulated meats, and warrants further investigation.

Prices, particularly for essential products like food, are intimately woven with equity concerns21, as outlined by the Sustainable Development Goals Agenda 203022. Much research on equity has concentrated on socioeconomic status (SES) because SES is a critical factor influencing access to resources and opportunities23. SES reflects a combination of social and economic factors like income, educational attainment, occupation, place of residence, and, in some societies or parts of society, ethnic origin or religious background24. Prior research suggests that consumers with low SES are more price-sensitive than those with high SES due to the scarcity of their resources23,25,26. When disposable income increases, consumers may feel less motivated to search for lower-priced alternatives as search costs begin to outweigh the expected benefits of better prices27. However, contextual factors moderate the SES–price sensitivity relationship28. In food markets, meat demand has often been inelastic, especially among higher-income consumers29. Field experiments reinforce this: small cafeteria price increases barely reduced meat sales30, willingness-to-pay for sustainable beef remained inelastic31, and portion reductions were more acceptable than substitution32. Loyalty card data from Finland further showed red meat’s dominance, with dietary shifts occurring slowly through poultry and fish; price constrained fish purchases but not plant-based foods, which were generally cheaper than red meat33.

By contrast, plant-based alternatives exhibit far more variable responsiveness. Some studies found them highly price-elastic34, while others observed positive elasticity, reflecting willingness to pay premiums for newer, higher-quality options35. Evidence from away-from-home contexts showed elastic demand for plant-based meat alternatives but little effect on overall meat consumption due to inelastic supply and weak cross-price substitution36. Household and survey data suggested early adopters were disproportionately higher-income and better educated37–39, while variety expansion and promotions, more than relative price alone, drove demand in retail settings40,41. SES shaped these dynamics: education consistently predicted reduced meat and greater plant-based uptake33,38,39, whereas lower-education groups often substituted meat with less healthy alternatives42. Some other studies found price sensitivity linked to education and family size, but not income43, while others reported small income effects44. Taken together, the literature indicates that meat is largely price-inelastic, plant-based products alternate between elastic, penalized, or premium depending on context, and SES, especially education, is a key determinant of these outcomes. Yet few studies have directly examined how price sensitivity and SES interact across plant versus animal proteins, as well as the diverse and expansive portfolio of product categories that consumers face in-store, leaving a critical gap that this research aims to address.

Advancing this line of inquiry requires careful consideration of how SES is operationalized in empirical research. Measuring SES at the individual level provides direct insights into consumer behavior but requires surveys, which are costly, prone to non-response bias, and typically not available to policy makers in a timely manner45. Questions arise as to whether neighborhood-level SES, inferred from census data at a given geospatial granularity, offers a valid, reliable, and scalable alternative for policymakers designing area-based interventions like subsidies or zoning regulations. While prior research shows they are correlated but better for urban neighborhoods46, neighborhood SES also reflects local food environments and collective purchasing behaviors. Moreover, SES is often measured through income and education separately, yet these dimensions capture distinct but complementary facets of consumer resources and capabilities47; linking them to a composite SES measure enables systematic comparison across operationalizations. By comparing both approaches, this study assesses whether policy-relevant insights can still be drawn when individual data are unavailable, informing strategies to improve equitable access to protein-rich foods.

Considering both the societal and theoretical importance, we proposed four research questions:Does price sensitivity differ for plant proteins compared to animal proteins?Does SES moderate price sensitivity differently for plant proteins compared to animal proteins?How does the moderation of price sensitivity by income alone or education alone compare to the composite SES measure?How do estimates of price sensitivity differ when the SES measure is operationalized at the neighborhood level using census data instead of at the individual level using survey data?

To address the research questions, we analyzed longitudinal consumer loyalty card data on food purchases from Finland. Using these novel data, we conducted three studies, each corresponding to a research question, focusing on seven plant-based and 14 animal-based protein subcategories grouped based on nutritionist-informed classifications48. The conceptual frameworks are presented in Fig. 1. Study 1 examined whether consumers differ in price sensitivity between plant and animal proteins (RQ1). Using individual-level data from 29,131 Finnish consumers, we applied two-level mixed-effects regression models for each protein category and conducted a meta-analysis across categories. Study 2 compared the moderating role of SES when operationalized in alternative ways: Study 2a tested whether the composite measure of SES moderated price sensitivity differently across plant- and animal-based proteins (RQ2), compared to income alone (Study 2b) and education alone (Study 2c) to address RQ3. Finally, to evaluate whether results depend on data source, Study 3 compared individual-level survey-based SES measures to neighborhood-level census-based SES proxies (RQ4). For a robustness check, using the same methodology as Study 3 at the neighborhood level, we analyzed data from 58,267 Canadian consumers who participated in a grocery loyalty program and compared the regression coefficients to those from Study 2a to evaluate country-level differences and generalizability.Fig. 1Conceptual frameworks for Studies 1–3.

Results

Clustering Results

A total of 29,131 loyalty program cardholders were included in the Finnish dataset. The dataset included only consenting consumers who had completed a questionnaire, had a valid postal code on file, and indicated they purchased over 40% of their groceries at the retailer’s stores. The results of both clustering methods, k-Modes and k-Means, at the individual (Supplement B1) and neighborhood (Supplement B2) levels, respectively, suggested three SES clusters using the Scree plot and Elbow methods. At the individual level, the results of the clustering analysis followed a near-symmetrical distribution with 5641 cardholders in the high SES cluster, 13,845 in the middle cluster, and 9645 in the lowest SES cluster. However, the neighborhood-level results suggest a left-skewed distribution with 15,071 cardholders in the first high SES cluster, 11,572 in the middle SES cluster, and only 2488 in the lowest SES cluster. Supplement C provides more details and a geospatial distribution of the clusters.

Studies 1 and 2 used individual-level data for clustering from a survey with ordinal responses. Table 1 presents descriptive statistics for the SES clusters using these data. The high SES cluster had higher household income, with 89% (n = 5009) of the cluster reporting income over EUR 4500 per month. Accordingly, the high SES cluster also self-reported greater ease with which their income covered their expenses (perceived income adequacy); 89% (n = 5013) reported getting along quite nicely or excellently. The high SES cluster also reported the highest educational attainment, with 83% (n = 4706) of the cluster having a higher university education. Most of the low SES cluster (n = 7188; 75%) reported household incomes under EUR 4500 per month. Most consumers in the low SES cluster were frugal shoppers who “make do” (n = 5889; 61%), with another 25% of the cluster (n = 1578) making compromises when they shopped. 82% of cardholders in the low SES cluster (n = 7988) stopped their educational training before or at the level of secondary education. Descriptive results for consumers in the middle SES cluster fell between the high and low extremes. Study 3 used neighborhood-level data from the Finnish census for clustering. As mentioned earlier, the results showed that the number of cardholders was skewed toward neighborhoods with high SES. 15,071 cardholders were in the high SES cluster, 11,572 in the middle SES cluster, and 2488 in the low SES cluster. Overall, the high SES cluster had the highest average annual household income of EUR 45,201 (SD = 12,286), the middle SES cluster was EUR 37,570 (SD = 5289), and the low SES cluster was EUR 33,355 (SD = 4005). The proportion of the population with no secondary or vocational training was highest in low SES neighborhoods at 34.74% of the adult population. The proportion without higher education in neighborhoods with high SES was 17.14%. Again, values for the middle SES cluster lay between the two extremes of the high and low SES clusters for the household and educational attainment measures. Purchasing trends

Across individual-level SES clusters, Finnish consumers derived most of their protein from animal sources, with animal-based milk alone making up over 30% of purchased volume. Consumers with high SES spent the most on animal proteins (€3.29/day) but purchased the lowest absolute volume (733.99 g/day), compared with consumers with middle SES (739.74 g/day). For plant proteins, consumers with high SES spent the most (€0.27/day) and purchased the greatest volume (48.17 g/day), while consumers with low SES spent the least (€0.18/day; 36.12 g/day). A stepwise SES pattern was also observed in plant protein nutrient intake, with consumers with high SES purchasing the most energy (296 kJ) and protein (2.6 g) from plants. On average, animal proteins contributed ~30% of daily energy and plants ~15%, with consumers with high SES obtaining the least fat (48.5 g) and carbohydrates (30.5% of energy) from animal protein purchases relative to the other groups. Further details on average volumes, expenditures, and subcategories are provided in Supplement D.

Canadian purchasing patterns showed that consumers with low SES, on average, spent the most on total proteins (€5.04/day) and purchased the highest volume (991.76 g/day). However, consumers with high SES tended to buy more expensive animal proteins (€4.77/day; 857.51 g/day) and the highest volume of plant proteins (55.76 g/day). Consumers with middle SES spent slightly less on both animal (€4.62/day) and plant proteins (€0.23/day; 51.01 g/day), while consumers with low SES purchased the most animal proteins by volume (939.03 g/day) but fewer plants (52.74 g/day). Overall, SES differences in Canada reflected greater total protein purchasing among consumers with low SES but higher plant protein uptake among consumers with high SES. A more detailed breakdown is available in Supplement D.

Analytical results

In the following analyses, we report price elasticities as the formal econometric estimates for price coefficients in each model and interpret these coefficients in terms of price sensitivity—the broader behavioral construct reflecting consumers’ responsiveness to prices49. Study 1 aimed to assess how the composite measure of SES influenced price sensitivity across categories of plant and animal proteins. The results of the mixed effects regression models are provided in Supplement E1 for plant proteins and E2 for animal proteins. Estimates for individual categories are reported in Supplement F. Overall, we observed a difference (p < 0.0001) when comparing the price coefficients for plant (β = −3.95, p < 0.001) and animal proteins (β = −5.27, p < 0.001), with consumers being less price elastic to plant proteins.

Study 2a assessed how SES influenced price sensitivity across plant and animal protein categories. Differences emerged upon looking at the behavior of different SES segments. Figure 2 presents the 63 β-coefficients for each SES cluster across the 21 categories. These estimates are also reported in Supplement F. Green bars represent plant proteins and red bars represent animal ones. We observed, as expected, a laddered pattern with the price coefficients being the smallest in magnitude for the high SES cluster and the largest for the low SES cluster. However, this visualization also illustrates the heterogeneity of consumers and the diverse impacts of SES on price elasticity. All consumers were price elastic. Among plant-based categories, we observed a difference (p < 0.001) in price elasticity when comparing price coefficients across the three SES clusters, indicating differences in price sensitivity across the three SES clusters. Consumers with low SES were most price elastic to plant proteins (β = −4.05, p < 0.001), followed by middle SES (β = −4.02, p < 0.001) and high SES (β = −3.95, p < 0.001). For animal proteins, differences (p < 0.001) were also observed across the three clusters. Consumers with low SES (β = −5.58, p < 0.001) were most price elastic, followed by middle SES (β = −5.44, p < 0.001), and high SES being the least (β = −5.26, p < 0.001). Although SES moderated price elasticity for both plant-based and animal protein purchases, the magnitude of this moderation was substantially greater for animal proteins. Specifically, the gap in price elasticity between consumers with low- and high-SES was over three times larger for animal proteins (Δβ = 0.32) than for plant-based proteins (Δβ = 0.10). Study 2b focused on evaluating the individual influence of income as a moderator of price sensitivity for plant and animal proteins. The results are reported in Supplement E1 and D2. When disaggregated, the moderation effect for plant proteins was driven primarily by income, as both consumers with middle- (β = −4.01, p < 0.001) and low-income (β = −4.06, p < 0.001) exhibited stronger price elasticity compared to consumers with high-income (β = −3.97, p < 0.001). Income also explained part of the moderation of price elasticity for animal proteins, with consumers with middle (β = −5.52, p < 0.001) and low (β = −5.55, p < 0.001) income being more price sensitive than the high-income group (β = −5.27, p <0.001).

Meanwhile, Study 2c focused on how education moderated price sensitivity. Education alone explained little of the moderation for plant proteins, as the price interaction terms were significant for middle education (β = −0.04, p = 0.01) but not low education (p = 0.70), with the magnitiude of differences being relatively small. These results suggest that income is the dominant component underlying the composite SES moderation of price elasticity for plant proteins, although the magnitude of the effect is small. In contrast to animal proteins, consumers with middle (β = −5.53, p < 0.001) and low (β = −5.60, p < 0.001) education showed substantial increases in price elasticity for animal proteins relative to the high-income group, highlighting the differential roles that income and education played in moderating price elasticity for animal proteins. Together, these results indicate that socioeconomic disparities in responsiveness to protein prices differ for plant and animal protein categories and, again, suggest that socioeconomic disparities are more pronounced in responses to animal protein prices than in plant-based categories.

For plant proteins, the moderation of price elasticity by SES was broadly consistent across individual- and neighborhood-level specifications (Supplement G compared to Supplement E), though the magnitudes differed. Using individual survey data, composite SES clusters significantly moderated price elasticity, with lower SES consumers more responsive to price changes. Income also showed clear moderation, while education exerted only a modest influence as previously reported. At the neighborhood level, the coefficients generally moved in the same direction, but the effects of composite SES and education were no longer significant, and the income interactions, though still significant, were smaller in magnitude for consumers with middle income (β = −5.52, p < 0.001) and low income (−5.50, p < 0.001). Overall, the results suggest that while both individual- and neighborhood-based SES measures point toward greater price elasticity among lower-SES consumers, individual-level data capture sharper gradients, whereas neighborhood-level measures attenuate these differences.

For animal proteins, the neighborhood-level specifications showed moderation patterns consistent with the individual data but with smaller magnitudes. Price elasticity for composite SES clusters remained significant, though attenuated at the neighborhood level (middle SES cluster: β = –5.52, p < 0.001; low SES cluster: β = –5.56, p < 0.001). Similarly, education continued to moderate price elasticity, with effects reduced compared to individual-level estimates (middle education: β = –5.52, p < 0.001; low education: β = –5.56, p < 0.001). Income interactions were also significant but diminished (middle income: β = –5.52, p < 0.001; low income: β = –5.50, p < 0.001). Thus, while neighborhood-level SES measures pointed in the same direction as individual-level SES, they consistently underestimated the magnitude of socioeconomic disparities in responsiveness to animal protein prices.

Lastly, the objective of the robustness check was to assess the generalizability of the results observed in Finland to another country. The full results for the Canadian regression models are reported in Supplement I. In a meta-analysis, we observed that price sensitivities to plant proteins (β = −3.32; p < 0.001) were less (p = 0.02) than animal (β = −4.78; p < 0.001) ones overall, which was consistent with that observed in Finland. No difference was observed when comparing differences across SES clusters for plant proteins (p = 0.82); although, it was observed for animal proteins (p < 0.001). We also used meta-analysis to compare price coefficients between Finland and Canada using SES clustering methods at the neighborhood level. In doing so, a small difference (p = 0.07) was observed with Finnish consumers (β = −5.14, p < 0.001) being slightly more price-sensitive than Canadians (β = −4.34, p < 0.001) when comparing across all protein categories.

Discussion

Food systems are deeply intertwined with some of the most pressing global challenges, including climate change. Unsustainable production and consumption patterns have intensified the need for multidisciplinary solutions that drive sustainability transitions4. A key aspect of these transitions is the broader adoption of plant-based foods1,2. Over the past decade, research has underscored the importance of consumers incorporating more sustainable plant-based alternatives alongside, or completely replacing, animal products1,2,50. However, for this shift to be effective, it must be accessible to all consumers, regardless of socioeconomic position51.

Toward this end, this article investigated the role of price in driving the adoption of plant proteins by studying the purchasing behavior of over 87,000 cardholders who participated in loyalty programs in Finland and Canada. Study 1 revealed that estimated price elasticities were smaller in magnitude for plant-based proteins, indicating lower price sensitivity compared to animal proteins. Study 2a showed that SES moderated these effects. For animal-based categories, the estimated price elasticities were largest in magnitude for low SES consumers, indicating greater price sensitivity than those with high SES, consistent with prior research on resource constraints and food purchasing behavior25. By contrast, SES differences for plant-based proteins were relatively minor despite being significant, suggesting that the choice to purchase plant-based foods may be shaped less by price considerations and more by other intrinsic motivations—such as ethical commitments, sustainability values, or dietary preferences52.

Studies 2b and 2c disentangled the composite SES measure by examining income and education, respectively. Results showed that income accounted for much of the moderation of price sensitivity, in line with traditional economic theory that resource scarcity increases price responsiveness25, and with prior retail studies showing significant, albeit modest, income effects on price sensitivity44. By contrast, education effects were smaller in magnitude but more pronounced for animal-based categories, consistent with evidence that education shapes food choices through nutritional awareness and values rather than immediate budget constraints33,38,42. These findings resonate with earlier work showing that higher education is associated with more sustainable protein substitutions, even when income alone is not predictive33,38. Together, Study 2b and 2c highlighted that while income directly constrains consumers’ ability to absorb price changes, education plays a complementary role by shaping preferences, an important nuance often obscured when SES is treated only as a composite construct.

Study 3 extended this analysis by comparing individual- and neighborhood-level SES measures. While the results were broadly consistent across both approaches, neighborhood-level clustering slightly overrepresented high SES groups and may underrepresent within-neighborhood variation in mixed-income areas. Still, the similarity in price sensitivity estimates supports the use of neighborhood SES as a practical and scalable proxy in contexts where individual-level data are unavailable. A key strength of this study is the use of loyalty card data, which tracks actual purchases over time, captures price variations across stores and seasons, and includes detailed SES data. Unlike traditional dietary research45, which relies on self-reports or previous loyalty card studies that often lack SES background details, our approach provides a more precise and behaviorally grounded view of price sensitivity. Importantly, the comparability of results between individual- and neighborhood-level SES also supports the potential for multi-country comparisons, especially in contexts where only census or area-level data are available. This point is further supported by our robustness check using loyalty card data from Canada, where we observed similar but attenuated SES effects when measured at the neighborhood level. Together, these findings open pathways for global monitoring of dietary transitions, particularly in countries where individual-level SES data are scarce or difficult to collect.

Our results show that although SES differences were less pronounced for plant-based proteins, consumers across all groups remained price sensitive, highlighting that affordability remains central. One explanation is that plant-based categories in Finland and Canada are less mature, with fewer brands and limited price tiers, leaving consumers with little opportunity to “trade down” within the category. Vegan milk, for example, must still be purchased at a fixed premium if alternatives are scarce. Market analyses confirm that cost is consistently cited as a barrier in Canada53, while Finnish surveys show a large “no-change” segment sustaining meat demand despite growing flexitarian interest54. Crucially, evidence from European retailers demonstrates that temporary price-parity trials, where plant-based products were sold at the same price as their animal counterparts, produced immediate and measurable gains in sales55. This aligns with our findings: price matters for all consumers, but SES amplifies disparities much more in entrenched animal categories. As such, interventions that reduce or eliminate plant-based premiums through price parity, subsidies, or other discounts are likely to accelerate uptake broadly across SES groups while ensuring that dietary transitions are equitable.

This research contributes to food and sustainability science while intersecting with related marketing, nutrition, and public health disciplines. This work contributes to research on food prices in public health and nutrition sciences. In this article, we provided new estimates of price sensitivity for plant protein products that had not yet been studied, while also evaluating the impact of SES on price sensitivity across seven plant and 14 animal protein subcategories. Until now, little research has investigated the impact of SES on food price sensitivity using loyalty program data in the public health and nutrition disciplines17. We found that products in all plant-based categories had elastic demand, except for plant-based cheeses. This finding helps clarify a fragmented literature in which plant-based alternatives have been described as ranging from highly elastic34 to inelastic or even positively elastic depending on the product and setting35,36. By contrast, meat demand has often been characterized as inelastic, with only certain categories such as pork showing stronger responsiveness (Andreyeva et al.17; Lusk & Tonsor29) (Lusk & Tonsor; Andreyeva et al.). In our study, however, meat products were more elastic than prior evidence suggests. These differences in elasticity, consistently observed across both Finnish and Canadian datasets, likely reflect differences in measurement: whereas past studies often relied on surveys, experiments, or aggregated data, we used loyalty card data that capture revealed purchasing behavior in grams per day at the individual cardholder level across finely defined subcategories and multiple years. This choice of measurement avoids distortions that arise when elasticities are expressed in units sold, reduces recall and intention biases, and better reflects nutritional outcomes, helping explain why our estimates consistently showed negative own-price elasticities—consumers buy less when prices rise—across nearly all categories.

These findings offer actionable insights for food system actors navigating sustainability transitions. Price remains a central barrier to plant-based adoption, particularly among consumers with low SES, who in our study were the most price-sensitive. This sensitivity means that well-designed price interventions, such as coupons, discounts, mobile app promotions, or subsidies, could have a disproportionately positive effect by boosting consumption among those who currently purchase the least. In this way, targeted measures could help close equity gaps by enabling low SES consumers to access plant-based products at levels closer to their higher SES counterparts. For retailers, especially discount-focused chains, this underscores the importance of ensuring affordable plant-based assortments and testing personalized promotions through loyalty card programs. For policymakers, understanding how price drives consumption across SES groups is essential for designing effective measures, with affordability-focused interventions being especially critical56. Embedding targeted price supports for plant-based foods within government assistance programs could further enhance access, while governments can also catalyze innovation by funding research, coordinating sector efforts, and mobilizing financial resources57. Together, such approaches can both accelerate the protein transition and ensure it proceeds equitably.

Some limitations merit discussion. First, it is difficult to isolate the effect of SES on price sensitivity, as SES is connected to other social factors like gender, race, and ethnicity58. Further research would benefit from including other demographic variables to study consumer behavior. Second, humans have consumed meat for thousands of years, whereas plant and cell-based alternatives are much more recent8. Thus, it is likely that protein consumption is affected by other factors not studied here, like culture, traditions, values, norms, and other institutional patterns that merit further research. Third, the cross-sectional nature of our study hinders our ability to establish causation. Although we can observe associations between variables and changes over time, unaccounted confounding variables or reverse causation may still influence the observed patterns. Fourth, purchases reflected part of total food intake, as data came from only one retailer, though we only included cardholders who purchased more than 40% of their food there. Eating out is also excluded, but we previously found that purchase data correlated well with self-reported diets, similar to food diary comparisons33. Fifth, because we measure quantity in grams rather than unit counts, our elasticities likely exceed magnitudes reported in studies using expenditure or units, as grams capture downsizing and pack-size switching that unit-based measures may miss; our conclusions therefore emphasize comparative patterns across protein types and SES rather than absolute magnitudes. Sixth, large beta coefficients for some categories may suggest data issues. These coefficients, however, are within the extremes found in a review paper on price elasticity estimates16. We ran Tobit regression models with and without random effects, yielding similar results. A possible contributing factor is our use of volume in grams to measure consumption, unlike prior studies that used unit counts59. Seventh, while our analysis spans two high-income countries, the broader sustainability transition is global. Our indicators and retail-focused categories may not capture supply-side factors, cultural contexts, or nutrition-system dynamics in other regions. As such, our findings should be interpreted as demand-side evidence specific to Finland and Canada, and not necessarily as generalizable to all markets or dietary transitions worldwide. In addition, because our data come from 2016–2017, results may differ in today’s more dynamic plant-based market, and updated datasets would be valuable to assess whether these patterns have shifted over time. Finally, our indicators reflect consumer choices between meat- and plant-based food categories rather than amino-acid profiles or protein quality, which were beyond our scope and could be the focus of future research.

Overall, advancing sustainability transitions requires an integrated understanding of the economic and social drivers that shape consumer behavior. As one of the most pressing sectors contributing to climate change, the agri-food system offers a critical entry point for change. In this article, we examined how price and socioeconomic status influence the adoption of plant-based products, shedding light on the structural conditions needed to support more sustainable consumption. Our findings highlight the importance of equity-focused interventions and data-driven approaches that extend beyond traditional disciplinary boundaries. While rooted in the food sector, the implications of this work extend to other domains where affordability, accessibility, and behavior intersect to constrain sustainability transitions. Unlocking these transitions will require sustained collaboration across fields, methods, and policy frameworks.

Conclusion

In sum, this study showed that consumers were less price sensitive to plant proteins than to animal proteins and that socioeconomic disparities in responsiveness were substantially larger for animal proteins. Disentangling SES revealed that income is the primary driver of price sensitivity, while education contributes more strongly for animal proteins, highlighting distinct pathways through which resources and knowledge shape food choices. Neighborhood-level measures produced broadly consistent, though attenuated, results compared to individual-level data, suggesting their value as scalable proxies. Together with the Canadian robustness check, these findings demonstrate that equitable sustainability transitions toward plant-based proteins will depend on policies and retail strategies that lower price barriers while ensuring accessibility across all socioeconomic groups.

Methods

To answer the research questions, data were obtained from loyalty card programs operated by two grocery retailers in Finland and Canada. Ethics approval for the Finnish dataset was provided by the University of Tampere and University of Helsinki; ethics approval was exempted by Simon Fraser University for the Canadian dataset due to the anonymized nature of the data. The Finnish dataset contained transactions for food purchases of consenting cardholders made in stores run by the country’s largest grocery retailer between January 1, 2017, and December 31, 2018. The dataset contained all products purchased under the seven plant and 14 animal subcategories of interest, including expenditure and volume, as well as variables capturing time and location. This dataset included all purchases made at stores across six retail banners, geographically distributed across the whole country, which were differentiated by status (discount to premium) and convenience. The retailer, however, did not disclose marketing information like the product brand or promotional details. Typically, the retailer followed an everyday low pricing strategy60. A Finnish research group previously ran a survey among loyalty program members when they collected consent for the use of their data in academic research45, which facilitated access to individual-level data that measured SES and also contained their postal code. Thus, the dataset allowed us to dissect the research questions relating to price sensitivity across SES segments, as well as to compare clustering methods at the individual and neighborhood levels as a methodological contribution.

The Canadian dataset was used to address the robustness check. This dataset contained transactions for food purchases made by loyalty card members at one of the country’s largest grocery retailers between February 1, 2015, and December 31, 2016, in the province of Quebec, Canada. The dataset contained the same variables as in Finland, but consumer data was limited to that of the neighborhood based on the postal code of their home address. No survey had been run among Quebecois consumers to be able to tie other individual-level data to loyalty cards at the time. This dataset contained purchases across stores with only two retail banners that were mainly differentiated by the size of the store and the variety of their assortment.

Cardholder segmentation by SES

Clustering was used for methodological reasons and to ease interpretation for readers. First, SES, as defined previously, is a composite measure to reflect income, educational attainment, and employment. Following a protocol previously published41,61, clustering allowed us to incorporate such variables into a composite measure. Second, clustering into low, middle, and high SES allowed for comparability across the two international datasets used. At the individual level in Studies 1 and 2a, measures from the survey were collected in a categorical format, which limited our ability to analyze their direct effects. The categorical clustering approach also allowed us to incorporate the measure of perceived income adequacy that relates an individual’s income to their expenses, as expenses can vary by household and neighborhood62. For Study 3 and the robustness check at the neighborhood level, we used multiple measures of income and educational attainment (note that employment was dropped due to collinearity) to build a composite measure for segmenting consumers. This step is also important because measures of SES are often difficult for researchers to collect, whether they are in academia, industry, or the civil sector, and even harder to combine with accurate data on consumer behaviors. For their collection, cardholders need to be contacted for surveys, consent to participate, and finish various questionnaires that can have low response rates45. On the other hand, researchers can combine the postal addresses of cardholders, which are usually collected during registration in loyalty programs, to link to neighborhood-level demographic data via a postal code that can partially overcome the data availability issue. Last, for marketers and policymakers, the segmentation into the high, middle, and low SES eases the translation of findings into actionable insights for industry and government actors. While we acknowledge that SES variables like income and educational attainment have been used before in examining demand or price response with Nielsen data in the US, for example, we believe that clustering is overall better than just allowing these variables to have direct effects due to the methodological and translational advantages mentioned.

For Studies 1 and 2, we leveraged cross-sectional data collected through an online survey administered to the members of a Finnish retailer’s loyalty card program45. In total, 36,621 cardholders completed the survey and consented for their loyalty card data to be used for academic research45. For the present study, we excluded cardholders who either skipped survey questions about SES or who did not have a valid postal code available to link to neighborhood-level data. Additionally, we removed cardholders who reported that they purchased less than 40% of their overall groceries at the retailer’s stores to select only frequent shoppers. From the survey, we selected three variables that captured dimensions of SES: (1) the highest level of education achieved, (2) household gross monthly income, and (3) perceived income adequacy. All three questions were designed as ordinal, with individuals having selected the best answer from a list of five to eight options. Answers from each completed survey were anonymously linked back to purchase transactions from the loyalty program dataset using a unique identifier.

For Study 1 and 2a, we implemented a k-Modes algorithm using Python 3 in Google Collaboratory to cluster cardholders using the ordinal survey data63. The K-Modes algorithm is an unsupervised machine learning technique that calculates dissimilarities between data points using modal data. It updates the modes for each data point in each cluster as it iterates through the dataset. The user must specify the number of clusters (k) before final implementation. We employed the Elbow method to determine the optimal k by plotting the k-modes cost (total within-cluster dissimilarity) and identifying the inflection point where additional clusters provided only marginal reductions64. Once the optimal k was established, K-Modes were used to predict and assign each cardholder to an SES cluster, which served as categorical groupings for our regression modeling.

We then stratified consumers by income (Study 2b) and educational attainment (Study 2c) at the individual level. Household income was collected in categorical monthly bands (<€1500; €1500–2999; €3000–4,499; €4500–5999; €6000–7499; €7500–8999; ≥€9000; not disclosed). Respondents who selected “not disclosed” were excluded. For analysis, income was grouped into three categories: Low (<€3000/month; bands 1–2), Middle (€3000–5999/month; bands 3–4), and High (≥€6000/month; bands 5–7). This classification roughly aligns with the Finnish income distribution at the time of data collection, where the national average was €3465 and the national median was €3079/month65. Education was reported as the highest level attained (other, primary, secondary, tertiary, university) and grouped into Low (primary or other), Middle (secondary), and High (tertiary or university).

For Study 3, neighborhood-level data were used to cluster cardholders based on SES. Postal codes for the households of each consenting cardholder were linked to public data available through Paavo, an open demographic database developed by Statistics Finland that contains the national census information66. Matching the years of the loyalty program dataset, we collected two SES variables for each of the 2814 postal codes in the country in 2018: (i) average household income, and (ii) proportion of the population without general upper secondary or vocational education. We then applied a k-means algorithm using Python 3 in Google Collaboratory to cluster neighborhoods based on SES. k-Means is an unsupervised machine learning algorithm that uses continuous data to iteratively assign data points to their nearest centroid based on the squared Euclidean distance. Like the k-Modes approach, we used the Elbow method and Scree plot to determine the optimal number of k clusters based on the sum of squared error for varying numbers of k clusters. After establishing the optimal k clusters, we predicted and assigned each postal code to one SES cluster. The neighborhood SES clusters were then matched to each loyalty cardholder’s postal code, which was then used as a factor in subsequent regression models. Also, to compare results at the postal-code level, we used census-based measures of median household income and the proportion of the population without general upper secondary or vocational education to compare with individual-level results. Both variables were split into tertiles to generate balanced groups across the list of loyalty program members, given the continuous nature of the measures. To maintain consistency with the individual measures, values were re-coded so that 1 = High SES, 2 = Middle SES, and 3 = Low SES (i.e., higher incomes or higher levels of educational attainment mapped to High SES; lower incomes or lower levels of educational attainment mapped to Low SES). Using tertiles at the area level allowed meaningful comparisons with the individual-level SES measures.

For the robustness check, neighborhood-level demographic data were obtained from the 2016 Canadian census. The Canadian census contains sociodemographic data at the dissemination area level, which is the smallest unit of the census block corresponding to groups of 400 to 700 households. Access to the 2016 census data was provided via the Canadian Socio-Economic Information Management System. We collected two SES variables for each of the 13,308 postal codes in the province of Quebec at that time: (i) the median total income of households, and (ii) the proportion of the population without a high school education. The Postal Code Conversion File from Canada Post was used to convert dissemination area geographic units to postal codes, which were then linked to the postal codes for loyalty program members. Note that where multiple dissemination areas overlapped with one postal code, we took an average among the overlapping ones. The same method of k-means clustering was applied to the Canadian dataset as was done for Finland at the neighborhood level. Before statistical analysis, prices measured in Canadian dollars were converted to Euros using the retrospective average monthly exchange rate corresponding to the month and year of each observation.

Loyalty Program Data

Plant- and animal-based protein products were selected based on the categories defined by a panel of nutrition experts who developed the Healthy Eating Food Index (HEFI-2019) in Canada48. HEFI-2019 provides a healthiness score based on the conformance of an individual’s diet to nutritionist-based recommendations contained in national nutritional guidelines. Because the objective of this study is not to assess dietary intake, we only took the HEFI-2019 to guide the categorization of products for our analysis. The categorization process resulted in seven subcategories of plant-based proteins: legumes, nuts and seeds, plant-based beverages, fortified plant-based cheeses, plant-based yogurts, simulated meats, and other wheat and fungal proteins. The animal-based protein category contained 14 subcategories: milk, yogurts and kefir, cheeses, other milk-based foods, eggs, fish, seafood, beef, pork, poultry and wild birds, wild game, lamb, mixed red meats, and organ meats. Note that infant formula and breast milk substitutes were excluded from this study. Unique Product Code (UPC) level data were not provided by the retailer; however, we were able to select and recategorize 84 lower-level subgroups to those needed for this study. Back-of-the-label nutrition facts (protein, energy, fat, carbohydrates) were linked to product information for the Finnish dataset. For the Canadian dataset, we manually coded 6809 UPCs according to 20 of the HEFI-2019 subcategories, except for other wheat and fungal proteins, which were not available.

Knowing the target lists of products, we queried the retailers’ transactional database to collect purchases of all target products by consenting cardholders within the two-year study time frame. We then linked the transactional data to consumer and store tables. The store data allowed us to map each transaction to the retail banners. All data variables were cleaned to remove spaces, letters, misspellings, and null values. Overall, the unit of analysis is at the individual cardholder level, and all data were aggregated at the monthly level. Observations are added for each cardholder, by category, by month, and by store banner. Volume was measured as the total volume in grams of food purchased. For the regression modeling, we added a small constant (0.001) to the volume before making a log transformation due to a high frequency of observations with zero volume. The volume of this constant, 0.001 grams, is about the size of a few grains of salt and is not nutritionally or economically relevant given the monthly time horizon. Price was measured as the average price paid in euros per gram by each customer over all their purchase transactions throughout the month, segmented by category and store banner. In the Results, we report the price coefficient as price elasticity, the econometric measure of the percentage change in demand relative to a percentage change in price, and interpret it in terms of price sensitivity, the broader behavioral construct of consumers’ responsiveness to prices49. For Price, if no purchases were made in the category that month, we replaced the missing value with the store-level average price for the category across all customer transactions for the category in that month. To control for differences across product types, we included category fixed effects (7 categories for plant-based proteins, 14 for animal-based proteins), using legumes as the baseline for plant-based and milk as the baseline for animal-based. For the regression models, we also made a log transformation to price. Overall, the same operations were applied to both the Finnish and Canadian datasets. Statistical modeling

Loyalty program data may not represent a complete view of all food purchases made across competitive retailers67. For the analyses, we weighted all observations by a previously developed post-stratification weighting to control for non-response in comparison to Finland’s general adult population45. In Canada, we employed a recency frequency and monetary (RFM) technique68 to identify frequent consumers who shopped in every month of the dataset and who had average monthly expenditures, aggregated across all categories, over the 50th percentile (over CAD 83.89 per month), while also excluding consumers who had maximum monthly expenditures greater than the 95th percentile (over CAD 589.15). The sampling technique for the Canadian dataset ensures that we have selected consumers who shop at the retailer regularly and who purchase most of their groceries from them. In Quebec, the average household spent CAD 679.50 per month at grocery stores in 201569, and given that we are only interested in 20 categories of plant and animal proteins and not all categories, the sampling procedure allows us to capture cardholders who have a sizeable degree of loyalty to the retailer.

Post-estimation Wald tests were implemented for each mixed-effects model to determine if the interaction terms for Price*Cluster for clusters two and three (middle and low SES) were different than the first cluster (high SES). To assess whether coefficients differed significantly across models (e.g., between plant- and animal-based proteins or between SES specifications), we conducted difference-of-differences tests by computing z-statistics based on the difference between coefficients and the square root of the summed variances. Last, for the robustness check, we conducted meta-analyses using random-effect restricted maximum likelihood models to summarize the results estimated for the individual categories in Finland and Canada. Specifically, we conducted a meta-analysis on the price coefficients, which were estimated separately for the three SES clusters and 21 subcategories (coefficients from full models in Supplement H compared to Supplement I).
