Management of asymptomatic superior sagittal sinus compression in depressed frontal fracture: A rare case report
Authors: Yamama Tawashi, Aya Obaidin, Mulham Alkassem, Ebrahim Hamsho
Published: 2025

INTRODUCTION

Open depressed skull fractures are common injuries and some are emergencies.[8] This fracture can be divided into two types: open fracture (compound) which is characterized by wounds on the scalp and closed (simple) if there is no damage to the scalp.[2] The incidence of venous sinus involvement in skull fractures is 11.5% and is represented by stenosis or thrombosis.[2] The significance of this type of injury stems from the potential for substantial blood loss during trauma or emergency surgery, which can lead to a high mortality rate in the perioperative period.[4] Despite its critical nature, such injuries are only diagnosed preoperatively in approximately 50% of the cases, placing considerable demands on the neurosurgeon responsible for treatment.[4] The venous sinus is at risk of injury from adjacent skull fractures due to the presence of only a single layer of dura mater separating the bone from the sinus lumen.[2] Depressed fractures located over the venous sinuses can lead to increased intracranial pressure (ICP) as a result of venous sinus narrowing and subsequent venous hypertension or due to sinus thrombosis and reduced cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) absorption. This increase in ICP may manifest immediately following trauma or develop later.[3] We report a case of compound depressed frontal fracture with brain matter protrusion and a bone fragment compressing the anterior superior sagittal sinus (SSS).

CASE PRESENTATION

An 18-year-old man presented to the emergency department after falling from a 3-m-high staircase. He complained of headaches and vomiting once. The patient was aware and responsive. The vital signs were normal with the Glasgow Coma Scale at 15\15. Physical examination revealed a significant bruise on the head and a cut in the frontal area, with brain matter protruding from the laceration, but there were no signs of CSF leakage. Pupils were 5 mm and briskly reactive to light and symmetrical, and the rest of his cranial nerve function was intact. Examination of the body was unremarkable. He had no medical, surgical, allergic, drug, or familial history. All the laboratory investigations were within normal without hemoglobin (10.5 g/dL). The computed tomography (CT) showed bruising on the right frontal side of the brain and a compressed fracture with bone fragment compressing the anterior SSS [Figure 1] but the patient showed no signs of sinus thrombosis or obstruction. The surgical excision was indicated and written consent from the patient was taken. Medical consultations before surgery were taken. Under general anesthesia, a bifrontal incision was made, the bone fragment compressing the sinus was removed and the bleeding was stopped. The ruptured meninges were not sutured to prevent damage to the SSS and need to tie it and to relieve symptoms of ICP. One unit of blood was transfused to improve the hemoglobin. After surgery, the patient was placed in a semi-fowler position and was given three liters of physiological serum, prophylaxis antibiotics (intravenously for 5 days and orally for 9 days), analgesics, phenytoin, and nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs. The laboratory investigations were normal and the daily examination was normal. The patient was discharged after 5 days. The patient was followed up for 3 months physically and radiologically without any signs of recurrence.

DISCUSSION

Depressed skull fractures are caused by head trauma and are found in nearly 3% of skull injuries that come to the emergency department.[1] When the depressed fractures are overlying a venous sinus, the neurosurgeons must be alert to the possibility of bad consequences such as a laceration of the vessels.[5,8] Venous sinuses are situated between two layers of dura mater: a thin periosteal layer that is in contact with the bone and a meningeal layer, which is more susceptible to injury from fractured bone.[3] Given the mortality and morbidity rates around the preoperative time, knowing the locations of the venous sinus injuries is essential.[4] Injuries to the SSS were reported to be common in the anterior part of it, followed by the middle part.[3-5] In our case, the injury was in the anterior third of SSS. A depressed fracture in the anterior third of SSS may not present any symptoms. However, the literature indicates that a depressed fracture in the middle third of the SSS is often linked to signs of ICP, such as blurred vision, headaches, recurrent vomiting, and strabismus (sixth nerve palsy), along with altered consciousness in severe cases. In addition, paresis, particularly in the lower limbs, may occur. In contrast, a depressed fracture in the posterior third of the SSS typically results in visual disturbances alongside symptoms of increased ICP.[3] Seizures can also arise from this type of fracture.[3] In our case, the patient did not suffer from any symptoms of sagittal sinus compression such as obstruction, thrombosis, or hemorrhage, but he had symptoms of increased ICP. CT imaging is the cornerstone for establishing a diagnosis, allowing for immediate assessment of midline shift, parenchymal contusion, and intracerebral hemorrhage.[7] The use of bone windowing facilitates the accurate identification and localization of impression fractures. While magnetic resonance venography is preferred for assessing the patency of a compressed SSS, CT venography offers comparable specificity and sensitivity.[3] Although contrast injection is necessary, CT venography is often more suitable for trauma patients. Recently, some experts have suggested that angiographic evaluation of the venous sinuses should be performed prior to surgery.[5] However, in our case, the patient’s critical condition did not permit preoperative angiography. Due to sufficient collateral venous drainage that allows for tolerance of sinus injury or ligation, mortality rates are lower in patients with anterior SSS injuries while 17% of patients with injuries in the anterior third of the SSS succumbed, compared to 50% mortality in those with middle third injuries. All patients with posterior third injuries died. Open depressed skull fractures are typically surgically repaired, with the dura being addressed promptly to reduce infection risk.[4] In contrast, for closed depressed skull fractures, many experts suggest surgery only under one of three conditions: to evacuate a hematoma, to repair visibly lacerated dura (such as when large bone fragments are evident on CT), or to correct a significant cosmetic deformity.[4] Surgical intervention for open depressed skull fractures involving the SSS carries considerable risk.[8] Salunke et al.[6] noted that patients who treated by ligation anterior third of SSS experienced significant bilateral cerebral edema accompanied by venous infarction during the postoperative period. Clinically, these patients presented with a range of symptoms, including recent memory loss, urinary incontinence, intractable seizures, and personality changes.[6] It is reasonable to conclude that obstruction of the entire anterior segment of the SSS can lead to extensive venous infarction in the frontal lobe, depending on the drainage patterns involved.[6] In our study, we did not suture the meninges to prevent damage to the SSS and the potential complications associated with bleeding and ligation. Clinical manifestations and comprehensive imaging findings can assist neurosurgeons in assessing the complexity of a surgical procedure and in making appropriate preparations for hemostasis.[8] Ictal events that occur shortly after penetrating head trauma may lead to posttraumatic epilepsy, warranting the initiation of antiepileptic medications (such as phenytoin) for 7 days.[7] While comparing mortality rates associated with traumatic dural sinus injuries is challenging due to limited data from case records and varying time intervals between injury and surgery, existing literature indicates that mortality rates for patients with dural sinus injuries range from 7% to 12% in those without brainstem lesions. However, these rates can escalate significantly, reaching as high as 23–60% in patients with brainstem lesions.[4]

CONCLUSION

Dealing with a depressed skull fracture overlying the SSS presents unique challenges due to the risk of significant vascular injury and potential complications. Managing a depressed skull fracture overlying the SSS requires careful planning, surgical technique, and postoperative care due to its complexity and associated risks. Collaboration with other specialties can enhance outcomes and mitigate risks associated with this challenging scenario. Attention should also be paid to ICP and planning for its management well.
