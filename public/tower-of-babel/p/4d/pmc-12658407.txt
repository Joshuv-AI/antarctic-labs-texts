Developing New Drugs for the COVID‐19 Emergency: Anatomy of the U.S. Response
Authors: Thomas J. Moore, Mariana P. Socal, Gerard Anderson
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTContextTo meet the need for effective treatments during the COVID‐19 Public Health Emergency, the U.S. government sought to accelerate the discovery and development of new antiviral treatments—a process that normally took 4–12 years. The government changed many features of the established system, selecting the investigational drugs, sponsoring or conducting the clinical testing, and purchasing and managing the distribution of the successful products.MethodsWe focused on novel therapeutic agents for COVID‐19 that were funded, clinically tested, and/or received Emergency Use Authorization during the Public Health Emergency from January 2020 to May 2023. The primary sources were the public records of the National Institutes of Health, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration, and the Biomedical Advanced Research and Development Authority. Excluded were vaccines, devices, diagnostic tests, and new indications for approved drugs.ResultsIn less than 24 months, the emergency program developed, tested, approved, and distributed eight new therapeutic products, including six monoclonal antibodies and two new oral antivirals. In addition, 11 other investigational agents were funded or tested under the emergency program but did not receive Emergency Use Authorization. More than 30 million courses of treatment were distributed at a cost of $29 billion or $881 per patient. By the end of the emergency, viral mutations and rapidly growing population immunity rendered the new products ineffective in almost all patients.ConclusionsThe emergency program was dramatically effective in finding and testing new drug treatments using a variety of clinically relevant endpoints and serving varied patient populations. Planning for future pandemics should include a global network of clinical testing centers that were key to a rapid response. Research is needed to discover more durable antiviral treatments, especially in settings where mutation and population immunity are subject to rapid change.

Introduction

In 2019, in the city of Wuhan, China, a virus emerged that would be named SARS‐CoV‐2. It would cause a global pandemic under the name COVID‐19. Millions around the world would contract this novel coronavirus, and there were no known treatments.

The Challenge of Developing a New Drug

Over the past century, physicians, scientists, and government regulators had learned important lessons about developing a new drug [1]. Patients faced with serious illness expect to quickly receive some form of drug treatment. However, developing a new treatment with real benefits and manageable risks has traditionally required time‐consuming, complex, and expensive experiments first in animals and then in human subjects. Investigators had to know, in biological terms, how the treatment worked and test the novel drug in animals for toxic and beneficial effects. The next step is to establish a dose for humans and then, most importantly, compare it with the measurable effects of a placebo or an existing drug known to be effective. The problem is that the process is slow and expensive. For example, a full drug development program for a new anti‐infective drug could cost from $266 million to $2.1 billion [2] and take from 4 to 12 years [3]. It was clear that this traditional approach was not going to be fast enough to respond to the global COVID‐19 pandemic.

Published Reviews of COVID‐19 Treatments

Several papers have examined the properties of drugs used to treat COVID‐19. In June 2023, Li et al. reviewed the mechanism of action, microbiological characteristics of the viral target, and pharmacological properties of 23 therapeutics that were developed, repurposed, or studied as treatments for COVID‐19 [4]. The paper identified 13 organizational entities worldwide involved in issuing treatment recommendations based on the emerging evidence. The paper concluded that, “Effective treatment strategies that can be inexpensively and conveniently applied are still needed to tackle COVID‐19….”

A May 2023 perspective written by a research team at Pfizer assessed the clinical advantages and disadvantages of 11 novel and repurposed treatments with a major focus on Pfizer's own oral antiviral drug, nirmatrelvir co‐packaged with ritonavir (Paxlovid). The study concluded, “We need to find ways to further accelerate small molecule development…” [5].

In September 2024, researchers at the University of California, San Francisco, published a systematic review of the clinical trials for two antiviral drugs, nirmatrelvir and molnupiravir. It concluded that while the earliest studies found some evidence of effectiveness, a pooled analysis of 23 clinical trials showed no significant benefits overall. It recommended re‐evaluating the use of these treatments in the current population because of increasing immunity and new circulating viral strains [6].

In this paper, we focus on the health policy implications, costs, risks, and results when the U.S. decided to create a rapid response to the COVID‐19 pandemic and created a new paradigm to expedite the journey from a novel molecule to a treatment distributed to millions in a very short time. It would change not only how the U.S. Food and Drug Administration reviewed and approved new drugs but also involve the White House and other federal agencies.

Methods

This review focuses on the novel therapeutic agents to treat COVID‐19 that were funded, tested, and/or approved as part of the U.S. pandemic response. A special set of laws, regulations, spending authority, and other expanded government powers are triggered with a declaration of a Public Health Emergency. This can occur to deal with a disease pandemic, a biological warfare attack, or a disease outbreak from a national disaster [7]. One key provision–Emergency Use Authorization (EUA)–provides a more flexible but lenient set of evidence standards for FDA drug approval [8]. This analysis focuses only on newly developed drug therapies and excludes vaccines, diagnostic tests, already approved drugs, devices, and new COVID‐related indications for drugs approved for other disorders. The review examines the government actions taken under the authorities of the COVID‐19 Public Health Emergency, which began in January 2020 and expired in May 2023.

The source data for this review came from public, online disclosures from U.S. government agencies, pharmaceutical companies, and the peer‐reviewed scientific literature. For details of the U.S. government programs, pharmaceutical partnerships, and grants, the primary sources were the following:

The Department of Health and Human Services Administration for Preparedness and Response provided overall management [9]; the Biomedical Advanced Research and Development Authority (BARDA) managed grants for therapeutic products [10]; the Joint Program Executive Office for Chemical, Biological, Radiological and Nuclear Defense (JPEO‐CBRND) disclosed additional drug development grants [11]; the Department of Health and Human Services Electronic Reading Room provided contracts and specific grant detail [12]. Additional documentation about the grants, number of treatments, and other features was obtained from online press releases from the pharmaceutical grant recipients, which included Pfizer, AstraZeneca, Merck, Eli Lilly, GlaxoSmithKline, Novartis, and Genentech.

The primary source for the characteristics, testing, and evaluation of specific drugs approved was two FDA websites: (A) Emergency Use Authorizations for Drugs and Non‐Vaccine Biological Products [13] and (B) the Center for Drug Evaluation and Research (CDER) Scientific Review Documents Supporting Emergency Use Authorizations for Drugs and Biological Therapeutic Products | COVID‐19 [13]. Additional supporting data for drugs tested but not approved were obtained from ClinicalTrials.gov, trials sponsored by the National Institute of Allergy and Infectious Diseases (NIAID), and from published peer‐reviewed reports.

Each of these primary source websites included numerous links to other public sources with more detail. The specific Uniform Resource Locator (URL) for the facts in this paper is included in the citation and was initially collected in June, July, and August 2024. Additional detail about key online resources is provided in Appendix A.

Results

A New Policy Framework

The process of developing, testing, and manufacturing a new therapeutic drug is normally the primary responsibility of the pharmaceutical industry. When COVID‐19 appeared in 2019–2020, the concern was that the current system would be unable to produce new drugs in a very short time, and a new system for developing therapeutic products for the COVID‐19 emergency was necessary. The U.S. government managed the entire process in an active partnership with the pharmaceutical industry. It selected the most promising drugs, paid for most of the clinical testing, subsidized manufacturing capability, and used a new set of standards to judge whether the drugs were sufficiently safe and effective to administer to the public. The U.S. government also accepted liability should the new drugs cause serious injury, negotiated a purchase price with manufacturers, and managed, through states, the distribution of drug products without charge to providers or patients.

The clinical trials to test investigational products were designed and conducted by two basic sponsors: (1) Trials conducted by pharmaceutical companies of their own investigational agents—often with government funding or subsidy and (2) a new form of clinical trials with multiple investigative agents initiated by the National Institute of Allergy and Infectious Diseases (NIAID).

While clinical trials for most new drugs initially focus on a single disorder and specific patient population, it was recognized that COVID‐19 treatments had to be tailored to a wide range of severity of illness and varied specific medical needs. The populations and endpoints are shown in Box 1. Given the need for speed, the alternative to specific clinical endpoints was finding agents with measurable antiviral activity and offering them widely without further clinical testing. This new approach was based on an understanding that it was unlikely that any single treatment would be optimal for the different population needs.

The ACTIV Platform Trials

To speed the testing of multiple investigational agents, NIAID deployed novel standardized designs called platform trials, adapted from its earlier trials on Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV). The platform trial series was code‐named ACTIV, or Accelerating COVID‐19 Therapeutic Interventions and Vaccines [14].

In standard drug development, each clinical trial is an elaborately designed separate experiment, customized to the drug, disorder, and scientific evidence sought (Box 2). Platform trials were designed to test multiple investigational drugs and, if possible, push them through the testing phases as rapidly as possible. For example, a drug product could receive basic Phase 2 efficacy testing, and if successful, be immediately promoted to Phase 3.

Although nine NIAID platform trials were ultimately conducted, the key initiatives began in mid‐2020. ACTIV‐1 did not examine new drugs; rather, it tested new indications for existing approved drugs. ACTIV‐2, the first major trial of new investigative agents, enrolled infected outpatients. It set a primary endpoint as the time‐to‐resolution for at least 2 weeks of 14 specific symptoms. None of the eight investigational drugs tested in ACTIV‐2 successfully speeded the resolution of COVID‐19 symptoms [15]. Three of these drugs were monoclonal antibody products that subsequently received FDA emergency approval based on other trials and different endpoints discussed below. While we could not identify any published systematic analysis of the reasons for failure of the investigational drugs to reduce time to symptom relief, we suggest that four likely factors should be considered to explain this result: (1) By the time the treatments were administered to infected patients, substantial tissue damage had already occurred, and an antiviral product could not be expected to promote self‐repair; (2) The effects on viral load were modest; (3) The antiviral treatment period was brief: 1–5 days; (4) The symptom‐tracking technology (patient electronic diaries) was suboptimal in some trials.

The ACTIV‐3 platform trial tested six investigational drugs for the treatment of hospitalized patients with a primary endpoint of reducing the time to recovery and discharge. No findings of clinical benefit were reported, with a hint that treatment could be harmful in some subgroups [16].

Thus, ACTIV‐2 and ACTIV‐3 showed that none of the tested investigative agents had an identifiable beneficial impact on hospitalized patients and did not provide any evidence that any of the investigational drugs achieved earlier resolution of symptoms in outpatients. Nevertheless, having a database of accurate scientific information showing the minimal impact likely prevented massive amounts of inappropriate and possibly harmful use.

While the two major ACTIV trials of investigational new drugs were not successful, another series of clinical trials conducted by the drug manufacturers and mostly funded by BARDA produced results that led to the emergency approval of the biological and antiviral products developed during the Public Health Emergency. Notably, these trials largely focused on a different endpoint–the reduction of the risk of hospitalization or death in infected outpatients.

Therapeutic Products Approved

Seven products receiving EUA were approved for symptomatic, high‐risk outpatients to prevent progression to hospitalization or death. Tixagevimab‐cilgavimab was indicated to prevent infection among healthy caregivers or the immunocompromised. The antiviral molnupiravir received a second‐line indication, meaning it should be used only if other antiviral treatments were not available or were clinically inappropriate. Notably, no investigational drug tested was approved for use in hospitalized patients or to shorten the time to symptom resolution.

The successful, company‐conducted efficacy trials for the EUA products shared design features. They enrolled hundreds to thousands of patients in 100 or more clinical centers worldwide—but observed each patient for only a short treatment period—approximately 1 month. Because of rolling enrollment of patients, the trials would take months to complete but were still much faster than conventional clinical trials.

The successful efficacy clinical trials included another design feature intended to speed completion. Most of these trials provided for interim evaluations while the trial was still ongoing—often when approximately 50% of patients had reached 29 days of observation. Notably, both oral antivirals (nirmatrelvir, molnupiravir) halted enrollment early based on interim analysis results showing large reductions in hospitalizations/deaths (89%, 50%). There were other strategies designed to reduce the time for clinical trials. The ACTIV platform trials quickly promoted successful agents from phase to phase and allowed multiple investigational agents to share a placebo group. In both the ACTIV platform trials and the sponsor‐designed trials, the use of interim analyses resulted in early stopping of several trials—for failure to prove benefit or for futility.

Endpoints in the Successful Company Trials

The first two products to receive EUA—the monoclonals bamlanivimab and casirivimab‐imdevimab—were approved in November 2020 based on a primary endpoint of change in viral load. But the FDA emergency authorization reviews also highlighted the death or hospitalization endpoint data in each, which showed favorable trends at an early date, suggesting this clinically relevant endpoint was a major regulatory interest.

In May 2021, the monoclonal sotrovimab received EUA, mainly because of interim results for 583 patients in an ongoing clinical trial. It reported that 1% of the treated patients were hospitalized compared to 7% of the placebo group [13].

In December 2021, the FDA granted EUA for two oral antiviral products–nirmatrelvir and molnupiravir–based on substantial reductions in hospitalizations and deaths in high‐risk outpatients. In the early interim results of ongoing clinical trials, nirmatrelvir‐treated patients showed an 89% reduction in progression to hospitalization/death compared to placebo; molnupiravir, a 50% reduction. The FDA reviews noted these two antiviral products had the additional advantage of being orally administered and therefore could be manufactured and then administered in millions of doses without direct interaction with medical providers for an IV infusion or injection.

That same month, the FDA granted EUA to a new monoclonal combination for two new indications related to prevention rather than treatment—among the immunocompromised and for prevention of infection in exposed caregivers. The product was a combination monoclonal tixagevimab‐cilgavimab.

By the end of 2021, the FDA had approved five monoclonal antibody products and two oral antivirals to treat three key patient populations: symptomatic outpatients, the immunocompromised, and to prevent infection in exposed caregivers.

Development Time Speed

A major objective of the emergency program was to shorten clinical testing times. In normal times, clinical testing programs for new antiviral products took from 4 to 12 years [3]. One measure of development time is the number of days from the first patient receiving the investigational drug to the FDA granting EUA. The shortest time to EUA was achieved for bamlanivimab, which was approved just 145 days or 5 months after the first patient received an IV infusion. A second, most rapidly developed drug was casirivimab‐imdevimab, which received EUA in 165 days (or nearly 6 months). Overall group time to EUA was a median of 293 days or about 10 months. As Table 1 shows, the first group of products received EUA in November 2020, just 10 months after the COVID‐19 Public Health Emergency was declared. The two oral antivirals received EUA more than a year later, in December 2021.

The Purchase of the EUA Products

Under the emergency program, the U.S. spent $29 billion purchasing the 8 EUA drugs. (Table 2) The largest share, $12.5 billion, was for purchase of nirmatrelvir, the easily administered oral antiviral with largest reported reduction in hospitalization and deaths. With nearly 24 million courses of treatment purchased, nirmatrelvir instantly became one of the most widely available prescription drugs in the U.S.

The government negotiated prices for each product, paying from $530 to $2293 per course of treatment (1–5 days for each patient). Details are shown in Table 2. The purchase contracts had another unusual feature. To speed manufacture, the purchase contracts were negotiated prior to the FDA completing its review of the available interim data—with a provision that the money would be returned if the product failed to win approval. In one instance, the sponsor of bamlanivimab returned $435 million to the government when it was succeeded by the bamlanivimab‐etesevimab combination. At the end of the Public Health Emergency, 7 million courses of nirmatrelvir were returned to the manufacturer under an agreement to further distribute them to the uninsured in future months. Data on the clinical uptake of the other six EUA‐granted products were not available.

The Loss of Efficacy Over Time

While the emergency drug program had made available with unprecedented speed a portfolio of clinical‐trial‐proven new drugs to treat COVID‐19, the patient population and the virus itself were rapidly changing. Through the massive vaccination program–and/or through exposure–the U.S. population was developing herd immunity–especially for the most severe outcomes of progression to hospitalization or death. In addition, the SARS‐CoV‐2 virus mutated, continuing to be highly transmissible but less virulent.

By the spring of 2025, none of the EUA products were still effective in treating COVID‐19 for approximately 95% of those needing treatment. The emergency authorizations for all six monoclonal antibody products had been variously restricted, suspended, or revoked by the end of the Public Health Emergency in May 2023. Molnupiravir, the oral antiviral, had been withdrawn in Europe for lack of clinical benefit [17], but remained available in the United States under its EUA. Nirmatrelvir was the only product to receive full FDA approval. However, in the months and years after EUA in 2021, additional manufacturer clinical trials of nirmatrelvir had failed or were canceled in standard risk patients, hospitalized patients, for prevention in exposed caregivers, among the immunocompromised, and in long‐covid.

The Fate of the Monoclonals: Diminishing Effectiveness

Bamlanivimab, the first monoclonal to receive EUA, was rapidly developed from the convalescent plasma of a single patient [18]. It was also the first to be withdrawn, in April 2021, just 5 months after approval. The FDA noted that SARS‐Cov‐2 variants resistant to bamlanivimab had reached 20% the previous month and had been increasing rapidly. It remained as part of combination, bamlanivimab‐etesevimab, which also was suspended in January 2022 when omicron variants were the most prevalent. In that same month, the FDA launched a new policy of selective restriction for sotrovimab to exclude certain geographic regions (mostly Northeastern states) where resistant‐specific omicron variants were expected to be most prevalent. But in April 2022, sotrovimab use was discontinued nationwide when resistant variants reached 50%.

The last monoclonal to be withdrawn, tixagevimab‐cilgavimab, was different in being the only product authorized for prevention in the immunocompromised. It was withdrawn in January 2023, when the FDA concluded that more than 90% of the circulating variants were estimated to be resistant to the circulating strain of SARS‐CoV‐2.

An additional feature contributed to the limited effectiveness life of the monoclonal products. All targeted epitope regions of the SARS‐CoV‐2 spike protein that also proved most vulnerable to mutation as it evolved. In 2024, investigational drugs targeting better conserved regions of the spike protein were in early development [19, 20].

The Fate of the Oral Antivirals

Unlike the monoclonals, the two oral antivirals–nirmatrelvir and molnupiravir–did not appear to lose effectiveness as the SARS‐CoV‐2 virus mutated from the delta to omicron and later variants [21]. Nevertheless, the impressive early interim results for both oral antivirals were not sustained as additional scientific data accumulated and treated patient populations developed immunity. Analysis of each product provides new insights into the risks and benefits of rapid testing and approval during an evolving public health emergency and the need to do continual monitoring as the virus mutated, and immunities developed.

Molnupiravir's Declining Trial Efficacy

Molnupiravir (Lagevrio) was the first oral antiviral to report positive clinical trial results. It inhibited SARS‐CoV‐2 replication by inducing non‐functional genetic mutations, mostly to the spike protein [22]. The drug was invented at Emory University and moved to early clinical testing at Ridgeback Pharmaceuticals. The developer was quickly partnered with Merck for larger scale development and manufacture. The Phase 2–3 clinical trial (code‐named MOVe‐OUT) that ultimately resulted in EUA was launched in October 2020 in 173 planned locations worldwide [23]. The primary endpoint, as noted, was hospitalizations/deaths among infected outpatients. In June 2021, while the Phase 3 trial was still ongoing, the U.S. government and Merck agreed on the purchase of 1.7 million courses of treatment at a contract cost $1.2 billion, or $767 per 5‐day course of treatment. The contract would be voided if the drug did not receive EUA [24]. A few months later, on October 1, 2021, Merck announced that the Data Monitoring Board (DMB) had halted the trial enrollment after an interim analysis of 50% of planned enrollment (n = 775) had shown benefit in the primary endpoint of reducing deaths and hospitalizations by Day 29 [25]. The endpoint of hospitalization/death was reduced by 50%. It occurred among 14.1% of placebo patients versus 7.3% taking molnupiravir, for a total of 81 outcome events (p < 0.001).

However, the promising interim result was not sustained in the full trial population already enrolled when the trial was stopped. The decision to halt enrollment effectively created two patient populations: (1) The 762 outpatients who had already reached Day 29 and were included in the interim report of a 50% risk reduction; (2) another 646 patients who had been enrolled but had not yet reached Day 29 of observation.

Not only was no treatment benefit observed in the second group of 646 patients, but the hospitalization/death events for this group (6.2% treated vs. 4.7% placebo) also suggested an unfavorable trend toward harm, although the difference was not statistically significant. In addition to the diminished results in the outpatient trial, a companion trial in hospitalized inpatients showed no treatment effect on time to sustained recovery, the primary endpoint, and an unfavorable trend in patient deaths [27].

These data and other concerns about the trial results led to a split FDA advisory committee vote, 13–10 [28], and FDA restrictions on the molnupiravir EUA. It was given a second‐line indication, meaning it should not be used if other treatments were available. In addition, the FDA later barred pharmacists from prescribing molnupiravir. To speed uptake the FDA had allowed pharmacists–rather than physicians or other licensed providers–to prescribe nirmatrelvir.

New questions about the clinical benefit of molnupiravir were raised in late 2022 by the results of a large, unblinded clinical trial (n = 26,411) in the United Kingdom conducted in fully vaccinated patients. It was code‐named PANORAMIC [29]. It found no difference in the incidence of hospitalization/death between the treated and untreated patients. Notably, a key reason for failure was that hospitalization/death was rare in both treated and untreated patients, less than 1%. These results led the European Medicines Agency to refuse full marketing approval of molnupiravir in early 2023 for lack of clinical benefit. The sponsors, Merck and Ridgeback Pharmaceuticals, initially appealed the decision, but in June 2023 withdrew the application in the European Union [17]. As of late 2025, molnupiravir remained on the market in the U.S. under its EUA. No public record was identified indicating whether full approval was proposed, considered, or denied.

Nirmatrelvir Benefits Not Replicated

The clinical trial finding that publicly defined the apparent treatment value of nirmatrelvir was the widely cited conclusion that “nirmatrelvir plus ritonavir resulted in a risk of progression to severe COVID‐19 that was 89% lower than the risk of placebo, without evident safety concerns.” [30, 31] The trial was code‐named EPIC‐HR, for high risk, and was conducted by Pfizer.

In October 2021, a month before the available interim trial results had been submitted to the FDA, the government contracted with the sponsors to purchase 10 million courses of treatment at a cost of $5.3 billion [32]. EUA followed in late November, and the trial results were published in early 2022.

As with molnupiravir, both the complete initial trial results and later clinical trials show that these widely publicized results overstated the benefits of nirmatrelvir in most patients.

The notable limitation of the EPIC‐HR trial was that its measured benefits occurred in a highly selected patient population [13, 30]. The study patients were unvaccinated, treated within just 3 days of confirmed infection, and at high risk of hospitalization, and most were exposed to the more severe early delta variant of COVID‐19. In addition, 88% of the hospitalization/death events occurred in a subgroup that had never been exposed to any form of the virus, technically called seronegative. Among the seropositive patients—those with antibodies indicating any form of exposure to SARS‐CoV‐19 (except vaccination)—only 9 outcome events had occurred [30].

Nirmatrelvir was concurrently being tested in a “standard risk” population more typical of the U.S. outpatients who might then need treatment [33]. Although the study results were not published until 2 years later [34], in this population, nirmatrelvir failed to demonstrate a benefit, measured by time‐to‐alleviation of symptoms. A later extension intended to show a reduction in hospitalization/death was canceled for futility [35].

Fueled in part by government purchases of nirmatrelvir that ultimately totaled $12.6 billion, Pfizer sponsored an extensive series of additional clinical trials that tested nirmatrelvir in other patient groups, and for other primary outcomes. It was tested to prevent infection in caregivers and other exposed persons. The trial failed. It was tested in hospitalized patients to see if it could speed recovery. That trial was designed, treatment centers recruited and then canceled before enrolling patients. Nirmatrelvir was also tested in long‐covid and the immunocompromised, again with negative results. The rate of change in population immunity is further illustrated in Box 3 and helps explain why the interim oral antiviral trial results for both antivirals were not replicated. As early as December 2022, the risk of hospitalization or death–the only endpoint in which the two drugs had shown benefit–had occurred too rarely to be measured even in a large clinical trials.

These investigational products were exceptionally diverse in multiple respects. In terms of government investment or grants, spending ranged from zero for lenzilumab, which received only technical assistance, to $355 million for MK‐7110. One agent, ensitrelvir, was approved in Japan but not the U.S. based on the same Phase 3 clinical trial results. Mechanism of action for the unsuccessful products included a recombinant fusion protein (MK‐7110), an ankyrin repeat protein (ensovibep), a polyclonal antibody (SAB‐185), and an immunosuppressant of granulocyte‐macrophage colony‐stimulating factor (lenzilumab).

The reasons are unclear why each of these investigative agents was unsuccessful. The FDA did not publicly disclose rejection of EUA applications, and agents that failed pivotal trials or developed other disadvantages may have never advanced to an application. However, three unsuccessful agents failed to establish benefits in the ACTIV‐3 platform trial in hospitalized patients, and three more agents did not produce benefits in the ACTIV‐2 trial for sustained alleviation of symptoms. One agent, an antibody cocktail, IMM‐BCP‐01, did not advance through its Phase 1 trial. Another, BRII‐196/BRI‐198, was withdrawn from development because of an FDA clinical hold.

Discussion

The U.S. government partnered with the pharmaceutical industry to conduct or fund a major series of randomized clinical trials and obtain results in a much shorter time than conventional trials would require while still testing novel drugs in hundreds of clinical centers worldwide. To further expedite availability, the government subsidized expansion of the manufacturing facilities and contracted for purchase of drugs before even interim trial results were fully available and independently evaluated. Starting within months of FDA emergency approval, millions of courses of treatment were purchased and distributed for treatment without charge. All this was accomplished in a fraction of the time it normally takes to develop, test, manufacture, and distribute a new drug.

While some of the clinical trials did not demonstrate benefit, having a broad array of valid clinical trials data likely prevented vast amounts of inappropriate and possibly harmful use given the need for a rapid response. Despite effects on the surrogate measure of viral load, none of the tested agents proved useful to reduce symptoms more rapidly or in hospitalized patients to shorten the time to recovery.

Nevertheless, because of mutations and the development of herd immunity, this study confirms published reports and FDA actions cited above to conclude that in mid‐2025 none of the novel drug products developed now provide effective treatments for COVID‐19–for prevention or for use in any infected outpatient or inpatient population.

Why did this massive experiment in speeding drug innovation fail to produce new treatments with lasting value? We contend that several factors contributed to loss of or limited efficacy: The patient population rapidly developed herd immunity through vaccination and exposure. Limitations

This review does not examine repurposed drugs with previous FDA approval. It excludes vaccines and diagnostic tests evaluated and funded in similar programs. Data for this study were assembled from online public disclosures as reported in the Methods section. Over time (and in a few instances in this study) these public data may be revised or withdrawn. The primary focus of this review was benefit and effectiveness of new treatments developed during the COVID‐19 pandemic rather than the safety of the treatments. No EUA products were withdrawn or restricted for new safety concerns, but this analysis does not rule out the possibility that new adverse effects may yet be documented, or that known adverse effects might have been underestimated.

Conclusions: Looking Ahead to Future Pandemics

While it is nearly certain that future pandemics will occur, the timing, onset, severity, and transmissibility may be different from the COVID‐19 experience. A government‐directed and ‐funded partnership with pharmaceutical partners enabled rapid testing, manufacture, and distribution of millions of courses of treatment free of charge to the U.S. patient population. Maintaining a global infrastructure capable of mounting platform trials rapidly is a critical element in preparedness.

This review focused on only one aspect of the inordinately complex challenge of responding to a novel disease pandemic. The designers of a program to prepare for the next pandemic need to consider that a new threat may be different in multiple dimensions: the nature and origin of the viral invader; the portfolio of existing and potential treatments; and the technology and trained personnel to monitor the virus nationally and worldwide.

Three capabilities developed in the COVID‐19 response may be important to a future pandemic response: (1) The ability to mount clinical trials rapidly, testing multiple agents simultaneously; (2) the capacity to manufacture large quantities of newly proven drug treatments rapidly; and (3) the need to continually monitor viral evolution at the community and point‐of‐care patient levels. Among these capabilities that need improvement are a stronger program to monitor the prevalence and genetic character of a circulating pandemic virus.
