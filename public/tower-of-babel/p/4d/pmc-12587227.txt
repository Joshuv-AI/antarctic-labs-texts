Tryptophan derivatives as non‐invasive diagnostic indicators for obesity‐related MASLD in children and adolescents
Authors: Shumin Zhan, Xingyun Wang, Chen Wang, Bowen Zhu, Jianfang Gao, Zhou Peng, Rui Wang, Yun Yang, Liang Zhang, Tengfei Wang, Jiaoxiang Wu, Wei Wu, Ke Huang, Guanping Dong, Qiannan Ren, Shan Wang, Senjie Wang, Xuelian Zhou, Liling Xu, Junfen Fu, Xirong Guo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsMetabolic dysfunction‐associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) represents a major health burden in children and adolescents with obesity. This study aimed to characterize metabolic alterations associated with obesity‐related MASLD and to evaluate the potential of tryptophan (TRP)‐derived metabolites as non‐invasive biomarkers for early diagnosis.Materials and MethodsA total of 30 normal‐weight individuals and 80 patients diagnosed with obesity were included in the discovery cohort. Circulating metabolites were quantified and compared among normal‐weight individuals, patients with obesity, and those with obesity‐related MASLD to identify metabolic changes associated with disease status. In vitro experiments using mouse hepatocytes and liver organoids were conducted to assess the effects of TRP‐derived metabolites on intracellular lipid accumulation. Finally, a non‐invasive diagnostic model was developed using machine learning techniques and validated in an independent cohort of 112 individuals.ResultsCirculating TRP metabolism was markedly elevated in children and adolescents with obesity‐related MASLD compared with both normal‐weight controls and individuals with simple obesity. TRP‐derived metabolites significantly promoted intracellular lipid accumulation in mouse hepatocytes and liver organoids via the induction of oxidative stress. The non‐invasive diagnostic model based on TRP‐derived metabolites demonstrated robust performance in differentiating obesity‐related MASLD from simple obesity in both the discovery and validation cohorts.DiscussionThese findings highlight the pivotal role of TRP metabolism in the pathogenesis of MASLD in children and adolescents with obesity. Elevated TRP‐derived metabolites may contribute to hepatic lipid accumulation through oxidative stress and serve as promising non‐invasive biomarkers for the early diagnosis of obesity‐related MASLD. The machine learning–based diagnostic model offers a practical and less invasive alternative to current diagnostic approaches such as liver biopsy.Keywords: biomarkers, children obesity, liver organoids, metabolic dysfunction‐associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), tryptophan metabolism

INTRODUCTION

Children's obesity has emerged as a major global public health concern, with its prevalence steadily increasing over the past five decades. Paediatric obesity markedly increases the risk of metabolic complications, such as insulin resistance, dyslipidemia, and metabolic‐associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD). 1 MASLD, one of the most prevalent types of chronic liver disease in children, is characterised by excessive hepatic lipid accumulation. In obese populations, MASLD can progress to more severe conditions, including metabolic dysfunction‐associated steatohepatitis (MASH) and advanced liver fibrosis. 2 While liver biopsy remains the gold standard for diagnosing MASLD and its advanced forms, its invasive nature makes it unsuitable for routine use in child patients. Alternative diagnostic techniques, such as magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and transient elastography (FibroScan), provide non‐invasive options; however, they are often limited by technical requirements, specific usage scenarios, and high costs. 3 Therefore, there is an urgent need to develop affordable, convenient, and trustworthy diagnostic techniques for MASLD in children and adolescent populations. 4 , 5 , 6

Recent advancements in metabolomics have prompted the microscale detection of abnormal metabolomic profiles associated with obesity and its complications, showing promise for predicting and monitoring disease outcomes. 7 , 8 For instance, specific metabolome signatures involving 650 metabolites have been shown to effectively track metabolic changes in obesity and predict health outcomes as well as the risks of associated complications. 9 Furthermore, lipid profiling has revealed specific circulating lipids, especially triacylglycerols, free fatty acids, and ceramides, as indicators of cardiometabolic risk in children and adolescents with obesity. 10 , 11 In addition, a non‐invasive blood‐based panel measuring MASLD‐associated proteins has proven effective for diagnosing MASLD in adults. 12 Despite these advancements, there remains a gap in research specially aimed at identifying non‐invasive metabolic indicators for MASLD in children and adolescents. 13

In this study, we revealed distinct metabolic profiles for MASLD in children and adolescents with obesity. Tryptophan metabolism was significantly elevated in obesity statuses compared to normal‐weight individuals, with TRP‐derived metabolites significantly enhancing intracellular lipid accumulation in mouse hepatocytes and liver organoids. Through machine learning techniques, we further developed a diagnostic model leveraging these TRP‐derived metabolites to non‐invasively discriminate obesity‐related MASLD in children and adolescents.

RESULTS

Metabolomic profiling reveals distinct metabolic changes in children and adolescents with obesity

The study population consists of children and adolescents classified as normal weight (n = 30) or obesity (n = 80). Children and adolescents with overweight or obesity presented a body mass index (BMI) above 1 standard deviation for their age 14 were further categorised into three subgroups: healthy obesity without signs of steatotic liver (n = 30), obesity with metabolic‐associated steatotic liver (MASL) (n = 30), and obesity with MASH (n = 20) (Figure 1A). The average age across all groups was comparable (11.65 ± 2.20 years), although obesity was more prevalent in males compared to females. In addition, in comparison to normal weight or healthy obesity groups, serum levels of liver and metabolic function markers, such as γ‐glutamyl transferase (GGT), alanine transaminase (ALT), aspartate transaminase (AST), and uric acid (UA), were markedly elevated in obese people with MASL or MASH (Table S1). Notably, ALT and GGT levels were progressively elevated from healthy obesity to MASL and MASH, reflecting the severity of liver dysfunction in these groups.

To identify metabolic indicators for MASLD in children and adolescents with obesity, we employed untargeted metabolomics to track plasma metabolic perturbations across normal weight individuals and three obese statuses (healthy obesity, obesity with MASL, and obesity with MASH). We identified a total of 731 metabolites, predominantly lipids (45.48%) and amino acids (25.21%) (Figure S1a). Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed significant separation between normal weight individuals and three obesity categories, indicating distinct metabolic profiles in child obesity (Figure 1B,C). Additional PCA was conducted to exhibit separation based on age and weight stratifications (Figure S1b–e). Consistently, liver and metabolic function markers, such as ALT, GGT, and UA, showed significant elevations as weight gain (Table S2). We further conducted orthogonal partial least squares‐discriminant analysis (OPLS‐DA) and found that the groups with normal weight and those with obesity had notable differences in metabolites. Notable differences were also observed between the MASL and MASH groups within the obese cohort, whereas no substantial differences were identified between healthy obesity and MASLD groups (Figure 1D–F).

Adult MASLD patients showed dramatic circulating metabolome alterations, such as the lipid metabolomes. 15 Indeed, in this cohort, there were notable elevations in levels of traditional lipids such as phosphatidylserine (PS) and CER, as well as amino acids such as inosinic acid and serotonin in the obesity group compared to the normal weight group (Figure 1G,H). Consistently, metabolite set enrichment analysis indicated significant enrichment in pathways related to beta‐oxidation of very long‐chain fatty acids, bile acid biosynthesis, and alanine metabolism (Figure S1f, g). In comparison of obesity‐related MASLD individuals to those with healthy obesity, we noticed significant changes in amino acid profiles, particularly increased levels of N‐lactoylleucine, N‐acetyltryptophan (AcTRP), and L‐tryptophan (TRP) (Figure 1I,J). These altered metabolites were mainly enriched in pathways associated with alanine metabolism, carnitine synthesis, nicotinate and nicotinamide metabolism, and tryptophan metabolism, along with the de novo triacylglycerol biosynthesis pathway (Figure S1h, i). As MASL progressed to MASH in children and adolescents with obesity, pronounced changes in lipid and amino acid profiles were found. These changes were associated with pathways involved in fatty acid biosynthesis, carnitine synthesis, and tryptophan metabolism (Figure 1K,L, S1j, k). Overall, these findings highlight the metabolic distinctions associated with obesity and its complications, particularly MASLD, in children and adolescents.

Elevated tryptophan metabolism correlates with obese‐related MASLD progression in child and adolescent populations

To categorise altered metabolites across normal weight and three obese statuses, we performed Fuzzy C‐Means Clustering Algorithm (mfuzz) analysis, which identified six distinct clusters of metabolites associated with the progression of obesity (Figure 2A and S2a). Clusters 1 and 2 exhibited persistently elevated levels of metabolites primarily involved in nicotinate and nicotinamide metabolism, primary bile acid biosynthesis, and tryptophan metabolism (Figure 2B). In contrast, clusters 4 and 5 showed a consistent decline in metabolites predominantly linked to glycine, serine, threonine, glyoxylate, dicarboxylate, and tyrosine metabolism (Figure S2b).

Canonical tryptophan metabolic pathways, involving kynurenine, serotonin, and indole‐3‐pyruvate (I3P) pathways, are closely linked to the progression of MASLD in adults. 16 To pinpoint the specific pathways altered in paediatric obesity with MASL or MASH, we examined three canonical tryptophan metabolism pathways. Our findings revealed increased metabolic activity in both the kynurenine and serotonin pathways for child obesity with MASLD (Figure 2C). Despite indoleamine 2,3‐dioxygenase 1 (IDO1) activity remaining unchanged (Figure S2c), key metabolites such as kynurenine (KY), kynurenic acid (KYNA), xanthurenic acid (XA), and quinolinic acid (QA) showed significant increases (Figure 2C). Conversely, a slight reduction of metabolic activity in the I3P pathway was observed, indicating distinct and complex regulatory mechanisms of tryptophan metabolism during the progression of obesity‐related MASLD in children and adolescents (Figure 2C).

To correlate tryptophan metabolism to clinical outcomes, we examined ten TRP‐derived metabolites for their associations with clinical parameters (Figures 2D, S2d). TRP, KY, XA, and QA demonstrated positive correlations with BMI z‐score, systolic blood pressure, ALT, AST, GGT, and UA. In addition, metabolites such as TRP and AcTRP were significantly associated with insulin and C‐peptide levels, indicating a potential link of tryptophan metabolism to insulin resistance in paediatric obesity populations with MASLD.

To evaluate whether TRP‐derived metabolites predict a high risk of obesity‐related MASLD, we calculated the odds ratios (ORs) for 10 TRP‐derived metabolites using logistic regression (LR) analysis. All metabolites adhered to a linear model (p‐value for linearity <0.05), with six of them (TRP, 5‐HIAA, IAA, QA, AcTRP, and KY) showing high risks of obesity‐related MASLD in children and adolescents (OR>1; p‐value <0.05) (Figures 2E, S2e). Together, these findings underscore the crucial role of tryptophan metabolism in the progression of obesity‐related MASLD in children and adolescents and highlight its potential as a biomarker for disease risk.

Lipid accumulation was elevated in response to TRP‐derived metabolites

To investigate the functional role of tryptophan metabolism in obesity‐related MASLD in paediatric populations, we first investigated lipid accumulation in mouse hepatocytes treated with TRP‐derived metabolites in the presence of palmitic acid (PA) and oleic acid (OA). Notably, 7 administrations of TRP‐derived metabolites, particularly AcTRP, KY, and QA, significantly enhanced lipid droplet formation stimulated by PA and OA (Figure S3a,b). Consistently, biochemical analyses revealed that treatment with AcTRP, KY, and QA substantially increased TG levels (Figure S3c), while elevated total cholesterol (TC) levels were observed exclusively with QA treatment (Figure S3d). In addition, TRP or AcTRP treatments exhibited elevated malondialdehyde (MDA) levels (Figure S3e), while treatment with KY and KYNA significantly decreased levels of reduced glutathione (GSH) (Figure S3f), indicating increased oxidative stress induced by TRP‐derived metabolites. Interestingly, secretory ALT and AST levels were not significantly altered following treatment with TRP‐derived metabolites in mouse hepatocytes, suggesting that the impact of tryptophan metabolism on liver function may depend on the tissue microenvironment (Figure S3g, h). To further investigate the lipid metabolism induced by TRP‐derived metabolites in a physiological environment, we utilised liver organoids as models to replicate physiological functions. 17

The mouse ductal organoids were initially generated following established protocols and further differentiated into liver organoids upon stimulation (Figure 3A). Establishment of sufficient liver organoids was detected through morphological examination and increased expression of liver‐specific markers, including albumin and cytochrome P450 2E1 (Cyp2e1) (Figure 3B). Strikingly, treatment of AcTRP, KY, and QA significantly enhanced intracellular lipid levels in the liver organoids, as evidenced by Oil Red O and BODIPY staining (Figure 3C–E, S3l).

To elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying lipid accumulation induced by TRP‐derived metabolites, we further analysed the expression of genes associated with lipid biosynthesis. Consistently, mRNA levels of Perilipin 2 (Plin2) and Fatty acid synthase (Fasn) were significantly upregulated after administration of TRP‐derived metabolites, such as TRP, AcTRP, and QA (Figure S3i). In parallel, treatment with TRP‐derived metabolites also led to a significant increase in the expression of inflammation‐related molecules, including tumour necrosis factor (Tnf), interleukin‐6 (Il6), and interleukin‐1 beta (Il1β) in hepatocyte cultures. These results suggest that elevated TRP‐derived metabolites may not only promote lipid droplet formation but also enhance the inflammatory response in hepatocytes, potentially contributing to the pathogenesis of MASLD. In addition, upregulated protein levels of PLIN2 were also observed with treatment of QA in hepatocytes (Figure S3j, k). Likewise, immunofluorescence staining showed increased intensities of proteins associated with lipid biosynthesis in response to AcTRP and QA treatment (Figures 3F,G, S3m), indicating the activation of lipid synthesis pathways induced by TRP‐derived metabolites. Together, these data suggest an elevated lipid accumulation induced by TRP‐derived metabolites in mouse hepatocytes and liver organoids.

Txnip‐mediated oxidative stress contributes to TRP‐induced hepatic lipid accumulation

To further elucidate the molecular mechanisms by which TRP‐derived metabolites contribute to hepatic lipid accumulation, we explored the potential involvement of oxidative stress. Treatment of mouse hepatocytes with AcTRP and QA markedly increased intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels, as measured by DCFH‐DA fluorescence assays (Figure 4A,B). Transcriptomic profiling revealed that both AcTRP and QA treatments significantly enriched gene sets related to ‘response to oxidative stress’, alongside pathways involved in fatty acid metabolic processes (Figure 4C,E). Among the differentially expressed genes, Txnip (thioredoxin‐interacting protein), a key regulator of oxidative stress and redox homeostasis, was significantly upregulated in response to AcTRP and QA (Figure 4D,F).

To validate the functional role of Txnip in mediating lipid accumulation, we employed Txnip‐in, a selective inhibitor of the TXNIP–thioredoxin complex that has been shown to suppress Txnip mRNA expression in various cell types. Pharmacological inhibition of Txnip markedly attenuated lipid droplet formation following AcTRP or QA stimulation in hepatocytes (Figure 4G,H). Moreover, Txnip‐in treatment reversed the upregulation of downstream oxidative stress‐responsive genes Cyp1a1 and Fmo2, and suppressed the expression of lipid uptake and biosynthesis‐related genes including Cd36, Angptl4, and Srebp1 (Figure 4I). These findings indicate that TRP‐derived metabolites promote oxidative stress via Txnip induction, which in turn activates lipid metabolic pathways in hepatocytes.

Collectively, our data suggest that TRP‐derived metabolites, particularly AcTRP and QA, induce ROS accumulation and upregulate Txnip expression, thereby initiating a positive feedback loop that exacerbates lipid dysregulation in hepatocytes (Figure 4J).

TRP‐derived metabolic biomarkers for detection of MASLD in children and adolescents with obesity

To evaluate the potential of TRP‐derived metabolites as predictive biomarkers for obesity‐related MASLD in children and adolescents, we developed a diagnostic model by leveraging machine learning techniques (Figure 5A). The model was trained based on a discovery set that incorporated clinical parameters and differential metabolites associated with steatotic liver across normal weight and three obese statuses (Figure 5A,B). Notably, the elevated abundance of TRP‐derived metabolites across three obese statuses was further confirmed in an external validation set of 112 children and adolescents with obesity (37 with healthy obesity, 56 with obesity‐related MASL, and 19 with obesity‐related MASH) (Figure 5B).

We used receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis to assess the diagnostic model's performance by comparing its discriminative efficacy between TRP‐derived metabolites and clinical indicators. In the discovery set, the diagnostic model effectively differentiates MASL (n = 30) and healthy obesity (n = 30) (Table S4). Specifically, TRP, AcTRP, and QA achieved AUCs of 0.750, 0.792, and 0.693, which were comparable to those of ALT (0.745), BMI (0.752), and UA (0.746) (Figure 5C,D). Notably, combining TRP, AcTRP, and QA using LR significantly improved the AUC to 0.874, with a sensitivity of 86.7%, specificity of 67.7%, and accuracy of 76.7% (Table S5, Figure 5E). We applied this combination to differentiate MASLD in another group of obese populations. As expected, these findings were replicated in the validation set (Figure 5F).

We further explored the diagnostic accuracy for differentiating of obesity‐related MASH (n = 20) and MASL (n = 30) using the model. ALT was excluded because it is a recognised diagnostic criterion for MASH. As anticipated, AST and GGT showed high AUCs of 0.944 and 0.938, respectively (Figure 5G). Although most of TRP‐derived metabolites showed limited diagnostic capability (AUCs <0.7), TRP displayed an AUC of 0.743 (Figure 5H). We further enhanced the model by combining five metabolites using neural networks to improve diagnostic capability. This combination (a joint of metabolites including N‐acetyl‐L‐methionine, oxoglutaric acid, taurocholic acid (TCA), cysteine‐glutathione disulphide, and glycocholic acid) resulted in a superior AUC of 0.847, with sensitivity of 75.0%, specificity of 76.7%, and accuracy of 76% (Figure 5I, Table S5). Similarly, the combined metabolites maintained diagnostic performance in the validation set (Figure 4J).

To evaluate how TRP‐derived metabolites contribute to the prediction performance of the model, we conducted Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) analysis. TRP‐derived metabolites, such as AcTRP, TRP, and QA, played major roles in affecting the diagnostic ability of the model (Figure 5K,L). Collectively, these results underscore the potential of TRP‐derived metabolites as diagnostic biomarkers for obesity‐related MASLD in children and adolescents.

DISCUSSION

Although comprehensive blood metabolic profiles have been characterised in childhood obesity, with a majority of changes in amino acids, carnitines, lipids, and steroids, 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 limited studies have specifically linked metabolomics to MASLD in young obese populations. Key challenges include the lack of extensive metabolic network analyses and insufficient functional assays to elucidate the biological roles of these metabolites in disease progression. In this study, we identified metabolic changes associated with obesity‐related MASLD in children and adolescents. Through functional analyses, we confirmed that TRP‐derived metabolites were significantly associated with the progression of MASLD in obesity. Our research thus addresses these gaps by providing evidence of the metabolic reprogramming underlying MASLD in children and adolescents' obesity.

Untargeted metabolomics facilitates the identification of novel biomarkers, which markedly enhances our understanding of metabolic changes in physiological or pathological contexts. 23 , 24 One notable metabolic change in young populations with obesity‐related MASLD is the dysregulation of lipid profiles, particularly the increase in PS levels. It has been reported that levels of phosphatidylserine (PS) in the plasma membrane significantly influence hepatic lipoprotein metabolism. 25 , 26 However, in the context of obesity in children and adolescents, elevated PS levels may represent a protective mechanism of the body. Furthermore, we saw significant alterations in amino acids, particularly rises in TRP‐derived metabolites and branched‐chain amino acids (BCAAs). BCAAs demonstrated elevated levels in high‐fat diet mouse models and predict future hepatic fat accumulation in obese adolescents. 7 , 27 Moreover, levels of TCA and glycocholic acid (GCA) significantly increase as MASL progresses to MASH within our obesity cohort, which were previously connected to the severity of liver fibrosis and inflammation in adults. 28 Overall, these findings suggest that young patients with obesity‐related MASLD exhibit complex regulatory mechanisms as disease progression and may share similar metabolic alterations with adult patients.

Dietary protein is a critical nutritional risk factor for MASLD. Tryptophan, the only amino acid with an indole ring obtained from food, plays a fundamental role in human health. Factors such as increased tryptophan uptake, alterations in gut microbiota, and heightened enzymatic activity of IDO1 due to obesity progression may enhance tryptophan metabolism. 29 , 30 Although our study did not assess these external factors directly, we acknowledge that they may introduce variability in circulating metabolite levels. Nevertheless, the use of standardised fasting protocols and multi‐centre recruitment may have minimised potential biases introduced by these confounders. However, since no significant changes in IDO1 activity were observed in this study, it is unlikely to be the primary driver of the disrupted tryptophan metabolism seen in obesity‐related MASLD of youth. Further investigations of how tryptophan metabolism is regulated in such populations are required. Although the 5‐HT/5‐HIAA branch of tryptophan metabolism was not the primary focus of our functional validation in this study, we acknowledge its potential relevance and the intriguing clinical associations observed.

Liver organoids are valuable tools for modelling liver diseases due to their resemblance to the functional features of the human liver. In our study, we utilised mouse liver organoids to investigate how TRP‐derived metabolites influenced on lipid metabolism. Results revealed a significant increase in lipid accumulation induced by TRP‐derived metabolites. KY can activate the aryl hydrocarbon receptor signalling pathway, and mice fed with KY show weight gain and fatty liver. 31 However, additional studies are required to clarify the pathways through which particular metabolites, such as AcTRP, KY, and QA, contribute to lipid dysregulation and the aetiology of MASLD in childhood obesity.

Notably, in addition to increased flux through the tryptophan metabolic pathway, impaired downstream catabolism of TRP‐derived metabolites may also contribute to their accumulation. Enzymes such as quinolinate phosphoribosyltransferase (QPRT) and kynureninase (KYNU), which regulate the clearance of QA and KY, respectively, have been shown to be dysregulated in metabolic and inflammatory liver diseases. 32 , 33 Such dysregulation could lead to altered metabolite turnover and sustained exposure of hepatocytes to bioactive intermediates. Further investigation into these downstream pathways is warranted to fully elucidate the metabolic dynamics of TRP‐derived compounds in MASLD.

While lipid droplets may act as protective organelles under normal physiological conditions, our findings suggest that in the presence of elevated TRP‐derived metabolites, they may serve as reservoirs for inflammatory signals, contributing to the exacerbation of hepatocyte inflammation and injury. This underscores the need for further investigation into how TRP metabolites modulate both lipid storage and inflammatory signalling pathways in hepatocytes.

This study identified a combination of TRP‐derived metabolites as potential biomarkers with strong diagnostic accuracy for differentiating obesity‐related MASLD from healthy obesity in children and adolescents. The diagnostic performance of this combination outperforms most serological biomarkers. 34 , 35 Compared to existing non‐invasive tools such as FibroScan and the enhanced liver fibrosis (ELF) score, our TRP‐derived metabolite panel demonstrates comparable or superior discriminative ability, particularly in early‐stage MASLD, where traditional imaging or fibrosis‐based markers may lack sensitivity. While promising, clinical translation requires further consideration. These metabolites are quantifiable via standard Liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) platforms already used in clinical labs. Targeted, cost‐effective assays (~$30–50/sample) for key metabolites (e.g., KY, QA) can be implemented in tertiary care settings. Notably, markers like the KYN:TRP ratio have prior clinical use in cancer and neuroinflammation, 36 supporting regulatory potential.

While the findings are promising for early diagnosis and clinical intervention, the relatively modest sample size of both the discovery (n = 110) and validation (n = 112) cohorts may limit generalisability. This limitation reflects the challenge of acquiring well‐characterised paediatric liver disease samples and underscores the need for future validation in larger, multiethnic, and geographically diverse populations. Moreover, our cross‐sectional study design precludes direct inference on the temporal progression of MASLD. The lack of longitudinal follow‐up limits our ability to assess how TRP‐derived metabolite levels change over time or predict disease advancement. Future prospective studies are necessary to address these gaps and evaluate the dynamic nature of these biomarkers across the MASLD spectrum. Such studies may inform integration of TRP‐metabolite panels into early MASLD screening workflows, risk stratification models, or clinical decision‐making in paediatric populations.

In conclusion, we provide an overview of the metabolic alterations in clinical studies to identify tryptophan and its derivatives associated with obesity‐related MASLD. In addition, our study provides a valuable diagnostic tool by leveraging circulating TRP‐derived metabolites as potential biomarkers for MASLD in children and adolescents with obesity. This non‐invasive diagnostic model holds great promise for medical care and clinical management of children and adolescents with obesity‐related MASLD.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Patients and groups

This study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Children's Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine (No. 2021‐IRB‐301). Written informed consent was acquired from all child participants and their families before the commencement of the study. This consent included approval for sample collection, usage, and data analysis.

A total of 30 individuals with normal weight and 80 patients diagnosed with obesity were included in the discovery set. These individuals were recruited during their hospital visits. The validation set consisted of 112 participants with obesity, who were randomly selected in a stratified manner from a pool of 336 obese children at two primary schools and one middle school.

At enrollment, participants' height, weight, waist circumference, hip circumference, and BMI were recorded. The diagnostic criteria for obesity followed the Chinese Standard for Overweight and Obesity among School‐Age Children and Adolescents7. Participants were excluded if they: (1) were unable or unwilling to provide informed consent; (2) had a history or current diagnosis of type 1 diabetes mellitus; (3) had any known chronic liver disease other than MASLD; (4) had used, or were currently using, medications known to induce hepatic steatosis in the past 12 months; or (5) were considered unsuitable by the investigators for any clinical reason.

To assess the impact of obesity severity on metabolic profiles, participants were stratified based on BMI Z‐score into three categories: overweight (1 ≤BMI Z‐score <2), mild obesity (2 ≤BMI Z‐score <3), and severe obesity (BMI Z‐score ≥3). This classification aligns with widely accepted paediatric criteria.

The diagnosis of hepatic steatosis was established using either Proton Magnetic Resonance Spectroscopy (1H‐MRS), FibroScan, or liver ultrasound. MASH was defined using a non‐invasive composite approach: (1) imaging‐confirmed hepatic steatosis; and (2) persistent elevation of serum ALT levels above 60 U/L for more than 3 months, in the absence of identifiable secondary causes. 37 This definition is consistent with clinical consensus in paediatric populations and is appropriate given the ethical constraints on liver biopsy in children.

At enrollment, additional assessments were conducted, which included measuring blood pressure, analysing body composition, and evaluating bone density. Biochemical indices, lipid profiles, levels of inflammatory cytokines, and glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c) were also assessed. Blood samples were taken to assess glucose, insulin, and C‐peptide levels at 0, 30, 60, 90, and 120 min as part of an oral glucose tolerance test. The AUC for glucose, insulin, and C‐peptide was subsequently calculated across these time points.

Blood sample collection and storage

Each participant had at least 8 h to fast overnight before 1 mL of blood was drawn in the morning using tubes coated with EDTA to stop coagulation. Following centrifugation at a low temperature, the plasma was aliquoted under sterile conditions and immediately stored at −80°C for preservation.

Untargeted metabolomic study using four UPLC‐MS/MS

Methanol was used to extract the samples (1:4), which were then shaken and centrifuged at 4000 g for 10 min at room temperature. After being collected, the supernatant was dried under a nitrogen stream and then redissolved.

The test was carried out using a Q Exactive hybrid Quadrupole‐Orbitrap mass spectrometer (Thermo) and an ACQUITY 2D UPLC (Waters), scanning from 70 to 1000 m/z and operating at a mass resolution of 35,000. Positive electrospray ionisation (ESI) was used in the first UPLC‐MS/MS method on a C18 reverse‐phase UPLC column (Waters). The mobile phases were methanol and water, each of which contained 0.1% formic acid (FA) and 0.05% perfluoroacetic acid (PFPA). Using the same C18 column with water and methanol treated with 6.5 mM ammonium bicarbonate at pH 8, the second approach used negative ESI mode. The third approach used the C18 column with positive ESI once more. The mobile phases consisted of water and a methanol/acetonitrile/water combination that contained 0.01% FA and 0.05% PFPA. Using an HILIC UPLC column (Waters) and mobile phases of water and acetonitrile containing 10 mM ammonium formate, the fourth technique transitioned to negative ESI mode.

Ion peak extraction was performed utilising proprietary in‐house hardware and software after pre‐processing and quality control of the raw data. By applying criteria like narrow retention index, accurate mass variation of less than 10 ppm, and high matching scores for MS/MS spectra, the experimental data was compared to an internal library created from publicly acquired reference standards for the purpose of identifying metabolites.

Using the AUC method, the peak area for every metabolite was determined. To account for technical variations across runs, the raw peak areas of each metabolite were first log2‐transformed. Then, each value was divided by the median of the log2‐transformed values across all samples from the same batch, so that the batch median was normalised to 1. Missing values were imputed using the minimum detected value for each metabolite across all samples. This procedure helped reduce skewness and approximated normal distribution for downstream statistical analysis.

Metabolites with a variable importance in projection (VIP) greater than 1 were retained for additional analysis using the OPLS‐DA model. MetaboAnalyst 6.0 was used to conduct pathway and enrichment studies (https://www.metaboanalyst.ca/).

Targeted metabolomic study using UPLC‐MS/MS

A sample of plasma (20 μL) was put into a 96‐well plate and processed. Each sample was vortexed and centrifuged after receiving 120 μL of ice‐cold methanol with partial internal standards. Derivatization reagents of the same volume were applied after the supernatant had been transferred to a new plate. Samples were sealed and then derivatised for 60 min at 30°C. The samples were then diluted with 50% methanol. These were centrifuged one more time and kept at −20°C.

ACQUITY UPLC‐Xevo TQ‐S (Waters) and SCIEX Triple Quad™ 6500 plus QTRAP (SCIEX) were used to quantify the targeted metabolites. Steraloids, TRC Chemicals, and Sigma‐Aldrich provided the calibration standards. Quality control samples were injected at regular intervals, and analytical deviations were tracked using internal standards. The calibration technique included seven concentrations covering the expected metabolite range, a blank sample, and a zero sample. For peak integration and quantification, TMBQ software (Metabo‐Profile, v1.0) was used to process the raw data. Similar to the untargeted metabolomics data, peak area values were log2‐transformed, followed by median normalisation within each batch, and missing values were imputed using the minimum detected value. In addition, StandardScaler normalisation was applied during model development to reduce scale differences among variables and enhance comparability between features.

Cell culture and cell viability assay

The American Type Culture Collection provided the AML12 cell line. When cells reached about 90% confluence, they were passaged. The cells were treated with OA (MCE, HY‐N1446) at 200 μM and PA (MCE, HY‐N0830) at 100 μM for 48 h after the confluence reached roughly 70%. Additionally, selected metabolites were added during the treatment. The Cell Counting Kit‐8 (APExBIO, K1018) was applied to track cell viability, and the concentrations of the metabolites utilised were chosen to be the greatest levels that did not negatively impact it.

The metabolites and inhibitors applied in the cell experiments included Tryptophan (MCE, HY‐N0623), N‐acetyltryptophan (MCE, HY‐W011978), Kynurenine (MCE, HY‐104026), Kynurenic acid (MCE, HY‐100806), Xanthurenic acid (MCE, HY‐W014666), Quinolinic acid (MCE, HY‐100807), and Txnip inhibitor (MCE, HY‐115688). Depending on solubility, compounds were dissolved in either DMSO (MCE, HY‐Y0320) or sterile PBS, aliquoted, and stored at −80°C until use.

Cells were gathered following the intervention. The EnzyChrom™ Triglyceride Assay Kit (BioAssay Systems, ETGA‐200) was used to quantify the total triglyceride concentration. The TC Assay Kit (Nanjing Jiancheng, A111‐1‐1) was used to measure the TC concentration. The ALT Kit (Nanjing Jiancheng, C009‐2‐1) and the AST Assay Kit (Nanjing Jiancheng, C010‐2‐1) were used to measure the levels of ALT and AST, respectively. In addition, Assay Kits (Solarbio, BC0020, BC1175) were used to measure the levels of reduced glutathione (GSH) and malondialdehyde (MDA).

Liver organoids isolation and culture

Primary ductal organoids were isolated from mouse liver tissue following established protocols. 38 Ethical approval for the animal research project was obtained from the Laboratory Animal Welfare and Ethics Committee of Zhejiang University (ZJU20210313). Liver tissue was minced into small fragments and digested in a solution containing collagenase II (Sigma, C6885), DNase I (Coolabor, CD4871), and Dispase II (Coolabor, CD4691) at 37°C for 90 min. The supernatant was moved into a fresh centrifuge tube and subjected to two rounds of washing once ductal structures were visible. After that, ductal stem cells were hand‐picked to enrich them. Following their isolation, the cells were cultivated in an isolation medium and embedded in a Matrigel matrix (BD, 356231). 10 μM Rho kinase inhibitor (MCE, HY‐10583), 100 ng/mL Wnt3a (MCE, HY‐P70453B), and 25 ng/mL recombinant human Noggin (Peprotech, 120‐10C) were added to an expansion medium.

Three to four days after isolation, the isolation medium was removed and replaced with an expansion medium to support ductal cell expansion. 1:50 B27 (Gibco, 12587010) was added to Advanced DMEM/F12 medium (Gibco, 12 634 010) to create the expansion medium. 50 ng/mL mouse EGF (Thermo, PMG8045), 500 ng/mL R‐Spondin‐1 (PeproTech, 20–38), 100 ng/mL human FGF10 (PeproTech, 100–26), and 50 ng/mL human HGF (PeproTech, 100–39) were also included.

The cultures were moved to a differentiation medium that contained human gastrin I (MCE, HY‐P2671), 50 ng/mL recombinant mouse EGF, 100 ng/mL recombinant human FGF10, 50 nM A83‐01 (MCE, HY‐10432), and 10 μM DAPT (MCE, HY‐13027) in order to induce differentiation into liver organoids. From days 13 to 15, the culture medium was supplemented with an extra 3 μM dexamethasone (MCE, HY‐14648). During the final 3 days of the differentiation process, to assess lipid droplet formation, organoids were incubated with OA (200 μM) and PA (100 μM), along with specified metabolites.

Reverse transcription quantitative real‐time PCR

The SreadPure RNA Extraction Kit (Accurate Biotechnology, AG21024) was used to extract total RNA. The RT Mix Kit with gDNA Clean (Accurate Biotechnology, AG11728) was utilised to reverse transcribe one microgram of the isolated RNA into cDNA. The SYBR Green qPCR Kit (Accurate Biotechnology, AG11718) was used for RNA amplification and quantification on QuantStudio™ 5 (Thermo). Table S7 contains the primer sequences used in this investigation.

Western blot

A 1× protease inhibitor cocktail (FUDE, FD1001) was added to RIPA buffer, which was used to lyse the cells after they had been cleaned with cold phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS). The BCA Assay Kit (Coolaber, SK1070) was used to measure the protein concentration. A FuturePAGE® gel with a protein content of 4%–20% (ACE Biotechnology, ET10420) was then loaded with equal amounts of protein. The membrane was then incubated with the proper primary and secondary antibodies after being transferred to a PVDF membrane. Protein bands were subsequently visualised using the FDbio‐Dura ECL Western solution (FUDE, FD8020).

The following antibodies were utilised in this study, all sourced from Proteintech: anti‐GAPDH (HRP‐60004), anti‐PERILIPIN‐2 (15294‐1‐AP), anti‐FASN (10624‐2‐AP), anti‐SREBP1 (14088‐1‐AP), anti‐TNF‐alpha (60291‐1‐Ig), anti‐AHR (67785‐1‐lg), as well as anti‐Mouse (SA00001‐1) and anti‐Rabbit (SA00001‐2) antibodies.

Visualisation of lipid droplets

The intact liver organoids in Matrigel were centrifuged at 200 g for 30 s after being incubated for an hour at 4°C in Recovery Solution (Corning, 354253). Following a 30‐min fixation in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA), the liver organoids underwent two 5‐min washes. Following fixation, the liver organoids were stained for 15 min at 37°C using 5 mM BODIPY493 (Sigma‐Aldrich, 790389). Prior to imaging, the organoids underwent three rounds of washing and DAPI staining (Vector, H‐1200‐10).

Organoid paraffin sectioning and tyramide signal amplification (TSA) multiplex Staining

Liver organoids were gathered, preserved, and then embedded in paraffin as previously mentioned. The implanted tissues were then placed on glass slides after being sliced into 5 μm thick slices. The slides were sectioned, then deparaffinised in xylene and rehydrated with a graded series of ethanol. The portions were finally rinsed with PBS to get them ready for further examination.

Slides were heated in a microwave for 15 min when being submerged in Tris‐EDTA buffer (Coolabor, SL1861) for TSA multiplex staining. The slides were blocked with bovine serum albumin (Coolabor, SL1333) for 20 min after they had cooled to room temperature. The sections were coated with optimally diluted primary antibodies, which were then incubated at 4°C for the entire night. The sections were incubated with HRP‐conjugated secondary antibodies for half an hour at room temperature. The TSA reagent (RunnerBio, Bry‐Try488, Bry‐TryCy3, Bry‐TryCy5) was then applied. After a 10‐min incubation, the reaction was stopped by rinsing thoroughly with PBS. Finally, the slides were counterstained with DAPI or an appropriate nuclear stain and mounted using an appropriate mounting medium.

The antibodies used included the following: anti‐PERILIPIN‐2 (15294‐1‐AP), anti‐FASN (10624‐2‐AP), and anti‐SREBP1 (14088‐1‐AP).

Oil red O staining

Following PBS cleaning, the AML12 cells were fixed with 4% PFA for 30 min. After 30 min of incubation in the Oil Red O solution (OriCell, OILR‐10001), the cells were washed three times. Finally, 2 mL of PBS was added, and lipid staining was observed under a microscope. Every step was carried out at room temperature.

For the organoids, staining was performed using Oil Red O, followed by decolorisation with 60% isopropanol. The organoids were then counterstained with Haematoxylin Stain Solution (Coolabor, SL7050), rinsed, and covered with a coverslip for observation.

ROS detection by DCFH‐DA staining

Following PBS washing, the AML12 cells were incubated with a 10 μM solution of DCFH‐DA, prepared according to the instructions of the ROS Assay Kit (Beyotime, S0033M), at 37°C for 20 min. After incubation, the cells were washed three times with PBS to remove any excess probe that had not entered the cells. The intracellular ROS levels were then observed directly under a laser scanning confocal microscope using FITC channel settings. All procedures were carried out at room temperature unless otherwise specified.

Utilising metabolomic biomarkers and machine learning for diagnostic applications

The diagnostic value of individual clinical indicators and metabolites was first assessed using ROC analysis, in which thresholding methods were applied to compute optimal cutoff values by maximising sensitivity and specificity. For the diagnostic model utilising combinations of metabolites, 80% of the samples from each group were selected through stratified sampling to create the training set, which was used to develop predictive models, with a five‐fold stratified cross‐validation scheme applied so that the stability of the results could be verified, while the remaining 20% constituted the test set for evaluation. This procedure was repeated five times so that every sample was tested once by a model that had not seen it before. The predictions from all five rounds were then pooled together, and accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, negative predictive value, and confusion matrix counts were calculated on this combined set. The area under the ROC curve was also reported, with 95% confidence intervals estimated by bootstrap resampling. For models that produced probabilities, the probability cutoff for classifying a case as positive was not fixed at 0.5 but chosen at the point on the ROC curve where sensitivity and specificity achieved the best overall balance. For single‐indicator analyses, the cutoff was chosen by directly maximising the sum of sensitivity and specificity.

Five machine learning techniques were employed for MASL and MASH prediction based on metabolomic biomarker features. LR 39 was used as a linear classifier with L2 regularisation, and the penalty strength was tuned across candidate values including 0.1, 1.0, and 10. Support vector machine (SVM) 40 was trained with a linear kernel and probability outputs enabled, with the penalty parameter compared across values of 0.1, 1, and 10. Gradient boosting (GB) 41 sequentially built decision trees to reduce residual errors, with the number of boosting stages tested at 50 and 100 and the learning rate adjusted between 0.01 and 0.2. Random forest (RF) 42 combined multiple decision trees; the number of trees was varied between 50 and 200, the maximum depth was adjusted between 10 and 20 or left unrestricted, and the minimum number of samples required for a split was tuned between 2 and 10. Neural network (NN) 43 was implemented as a multilayer perceptron with up to 5222 training iterations; the hidden layer structure was compared between a single layer of 100 units and two layers of 50 units, while the regularisation parameter alpha was tuned between 0.0001 and 0.001.

Finally, SHAP analysis 44 was applied to all classifiers to interpret model predictions and to evaluate the relative contribution of individual features. For RF and GB, SHAP values were computed using TreeExplainer, which directly exploits the tree structure of these models without requiring additional parameters. For LR, SVM, and NN, SHAP values were obtained using KernelExplainer, a model‐agnostic approach that estimates contributions by sampling around each prediction. All SHAP values were summarised as mean absolute contributions, which served as the measure of global feature importance.

In each of the five rounds of cross‐validation, SHAP values were calculated only on the test samples, thereby ensuring that the interpretation relied exclusively on data unseen during training. The SHAP results from all folds were then concatenated, and the combined values were used to generate global importance rankings. This approach provided both stability and robustness in the evaluation of feature effects. In addition to global rankings, SHAP also offered individual‐level explanations, showing how each feature increased or decreased the predicted probability for specific subjects. The results were visualised through summary plots, which display both the strength and direction of feature influences, thereby clarifying the diagnostic role of specific metabolites and clinical indicators in model predictions.

STATISTICAL ANALYSIS

To identify significantly altered metabolites between groups, both parametric and non‐parametric statistical tests were employed. One‐way ANOVA and the Student's t‐test were used as parametric tests, with post‐hoc comparisons conducted using Tukey's Honestly Significant Difference Test to identify specific group differences. Prior to applying parametric tests, data normality and variance homogeneity were assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test and Levene's test, respectively. Effect sizes (e.g., fold‐change, FC) were calculated for each metabolite and reported alongside p‐values to provide an estimate of the magnitude of the differences between groups. The Kruskal–Wallis rank sum test and the Wilcoxon rank‐sum test were applied as non‐parametric tests, with post‐hoc comparisons conducted using Dunn's test to determine which specific groups were significantly different. For comparisons between groups of categorical data, we used the Fisher's exact test when expected frequencies were <5; otherwise, we used the Chi‐squared test. MSTATA software (https://www.mstata.com/) was used to assess patient characteristics; p‐values <0.05 were deemed statistically significant; all statistical tests were two‐tailed, and p‐values <0.05 were deemed statistically significant.

Pearson correlation coefficients were computed between circulating levels of TRP metabolites and clinical or lipidomic phenotypes. Bonferroni correction was applied for multiple comparisons in correlation analyses between metabolite–phenotype pairs, while false discovery rate (FDR) correction was used for multiple testing adjustments in untargeted metabolomic analyses. A significance threshold was set at adjusted p‐values <0.05.

We used a combination of statistical significance (adjusted p <0.05, FDR‐corrected) and VIP scores from partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS‐DA) to prioritise metabolites. Metabolites were ranked by their VIP scores, and those consistently selected across multiple resampling iterations were retained. We further validated the selection using random forest‐based feature importance metrics to ensure robustness. The top 10 metabolites included in the diagnostic model were those with the highest predictive power and stability across methods.

For data visualisation, graphical representations were largely generated using R software (version 4.4.1) and RStudio (version 2024.09.0 + 375), as well as GraphPad Prism (version 10.3.0). Visualisation techniques included volcano plots, scatter plots for PCA and OPLS‐DA, mountain plots, and circular layouts, 45 allowing for a comprehensive depiction of the results.
