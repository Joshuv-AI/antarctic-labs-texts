Experimental study on the seismic performance of thin-walled CFST superimposed shear walls with different column widths
Authors: Mengqi Gao, Shibo Wang, Chunliang Zhang, Chenyu Lv, Hetao Hou, Pengying Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Prefabricated concrete-filled steel tube shear walls leverage the advantages of both concrete and steel and have found applications in high-rise buildings and regions with high seismic activity. This paper proposes a type of thin-walled concrete-filled steel tube with superimposed shear walls (CFST-SSWs) that can be conveniently installed on-site to improve construction efficiency and quality. Four full-scale specimens were experimentally tested under constant axial forces and lateral cyclic loads to examine their seismic performance. The two main variables during tests were the end column type and the CFST column width. The X-shaped crack in the CFST-SSWs specimen only extended to the edge of the superimposed wall, while that of the cast-in-place concrete column composite shear wall specimen connected to a crack in the end column. As the widths of the CFST column increased, the ductility increased to 13.47%, and the bearing capacity increased by an average of 14.5%. The average ductility coefficients of the specimens ranged from 2.23 to 3.95, and the CFST-SSWs showed moderate ductility. Increasing the width of the CFST column had little effect on its overall energy dissipation capacity in the early stage, but helped slow the stiffness degradation and intensity degradation of CFST-SSWs. It also significantly increased the ultimate inter-story displacement angle. A calculation model was developed to predict the CFST-SSWs’ cracking load, and experimental data were used to validate the model’s accuracy.

Introduction

Prefabricated concrete-filled steel tube shear walls are innovative structural systems that leverage the advantages of both concrete and steel1, which endows them with superior load-bearing capacity and outstanding seismic performance2–4. Consequently, they have found extensive applications in high-rise buildings and regions with high seismic activity. Early research on concrete-filled steel tube columns focused on circular and square cross-sections5–9, which was succeeded by rectangular CFST columns10,11. The prevailing forms of contemporary steel-concrete composite shear walls are steel plate composite walls12,13, concrete-filled steel tube composite shear walls14, and steel fiber reinforced concrete composite shear walls15–17. Yang et al.18 showed that the limiting width-to-thickness ratio of rectangular CFST columns was about 50*(235/ f y)1/2. Liwei et al. studied the seismic performance of a novel type of prefabricated built-in round steel tube concrete composite shear wall19. Their results showed that its elastic-plastic inter-story displacement angle reached 2.5%, which was 25% higher than that of an ordinary reinforced concrete shear wall. Zhang et al.20–22 proposed a novel concrete-filled wide rectangular steel tube and used it as a critical load-bearing component in a real concealed steel frame system. Hou et al. designed a wall with composite shear walls as the carrier and steel-reinforced concrete as the edge constraint23,24. CFST composite shear walls have broad application prospects but suffer from high steel consumption and costs. Given a constant shear wall thickness, the width of the CFST columns is a key factor affecting the system’s cost-effectiveness, but this issue has rarely been investigated.

Based on our previous research, this paper proposes a new type of thin-walled concrete-filled steel tube with superimposed shear walls (CFST-SSWs), which consist of rectangular steel pipes filled with concrete at both ends as the edge constraint components and a three-part composite shear wall panel in the middle. The two sides are precast concrete panels, which are connected by a planar steel truss. The cavity in the middle is filled with concrete on-site, and the steel pipe concrete column is connected to the composite shear wall with shear connectors, as shown in Fig. 1. The steel tube columns are integrated with the central composite shear wall by welded shear studs to their inner faces, enabling the transfer of shear stress. The columns of the adjacent stories are welded together for vertical continuity. The columns pass through the reinforcement cage of the transverse beam at the beam-column joints, and the beam’s longitudinal bars are threaded through openings in the steel tubes. Finally, a 60 cm high cast-in-place closure strip is located at the base of the wall segments. This strip contains vertical starter bars that are lap-spliced to the reinforcement of the lower floor system, completing the floor-to-floor connection.

Fig. 1Schematic of the CFST-SSWs.

Schematic of the CFST-SSWs.

All components of CFST-SSWs are prefabricated in a factory and then transported to the construction site and hoisted into place. The inner and outer prefabricated concrete wall panels and the steel tube columns on both sides serve as the framework and temporary support. Self-compacting concrete is poured into the interior of the steel tube columns and the cavity in the composite shear wall, preventing blockages between the rebar and insufficient compaction that may occur during the on-site casting of concrete, and ensuring high construction quality.

In engineering practice, steel tubes are considerably more expensive than concrete. This economic consideration makes the optimization of the steel column width a crucial strategy for enhancing the cost-effectiveness of CFST-SSWs, provided that adequate load-bearing capacity and seismic performance are maintained. To investigate variations in seismic performance across different end column types and widths of the CFST, this paper presents the design of three full-scale CFST-SSW specimens and one full-scale cast-in-place column composite shear wall specimen. The intention is to examine the hysteresis and failure patterns of these specimens through quasi-static loading tests. Due to space constraints, this discussion will only analyze key seismic performance indicators for each specimen, including their hysteresis curves, skeleton curves, stiffness and strength degradation curves, and ductility. This research provides a theoretical basis and technical support for the research and development, design, and engineering application of CFST-SSWs.

Test loading plan

All specimens were loaded using vertical axial compression and horizontal low-cycle cyclic loading. Before applying a horizontal load, a vertical axial pressure was applied to the specimen in ten stages, while ensuring that the vertical axial pressure remained constant during the horizontal loading period. The ratio of the horizontal displacement of the loading point to the net height of the specimen was defined as the inter-story displacement angle. The horizontal loading adopted the interlayer displacement angle control method, with two loading cycles for each interlayer displacement angle per level. The lateral loading protocol is shown in Fig. 3. The main loading equipment for this proposed static test was a 1000 t large-scale multifunctional structural testing machine system, as shown in Fig. 4.

Results and discussion

Specimen failure

The failure modes of the test specimens are shown in Fig. 6, which shows that the crack development and failure modes of the SW specimens were similar but different from those of the CW specimen.

SW-1

For the SW-1 specimen, at a 0.25% inter-story displacement angle, the first crack appeared at a height of 500 mm at the connection between the superimposed shear wall and the steel tube end column. The horizontal crack extended along a 45o diagonal towards the bottom of the wall and gradually developed into a symmetrical funnel-shaped distribution of diagonal cracks that extended upwards to a height of 1200 mm and increased in width.

At an inter-story displacement angle of 0.42%, there was slight bulging at the bottom of the steel tube column. After removing the horizontal force, bulging became nearly invisible. Vertical cracks appeared where the internal steel bar truss was located, i.e., 100 mm, 300 mm, and 500 mm away from the inner edge of the shear wall and the west end column. The shape of the hysteresis curve also showed a serious twisting trend, and the specimen gradually entered the yielding stage.

When the inter-story displacement angle was 0.69%, the concrete at the corner of the middle superimposed shear wall began to peel off from the crack. A large number of cracks appeared in the middle wall panel and continuously extended upwards and developed from 45o diagonal cracks to cross-cracks, showing an X-shaped distribution. Concrete also peeled off at the upper corner.

When the inter-story displacement angle was 0.75%, the specimen reached a peak strength of 1297.2 kN. The concrete in the post-pouring area fell off, the steel bars were exposed, and the bottom of the steel tube column bulged more severely. When the inter-story displacement angle was 2%, the bearing capacity of the specimen decreased to 981.9 kN, which was 75.7% of the peak bearing capacity. At this point, the specimen was severely damaged, so the test was terminated.

SW-2

For the SW-2 specimen, when the inter-story displacement angle reached 0.28%, the first horizontal crack appeared at a height of 600 mm at the connection between the superimposed shear wall and the steel tube column and extended diagonally towards the bottom of the wall. When loaded to a 0.5% inter-story displacement angle, the specimen reached a peak load of 1127 kN in the opposite direction. When loaded to a 0.75% inter-story displacement angle, the specimen reached a peak load of 1287 kN. When loaded to a 0.77% inter-story displacement angle, the concrete at the bottom of the superimposed wall started to fall off, and many cracks appeared and continuously extended upwards to form an X-shaped distribution. When loaded to a 1% inter-story displacement angle, the bearing capacity of specimen SW-2 decreased to below 80% of its peak bearing capacity, and the test was ended.

SW-3

For the SW-3 specimen, when the inter-story displacement angle reached 0.15%, the first horizontal crack appeared at a height of 700 mm at the connection between the superimposed shear wall and the steel tube end column. The crack extended diagonally toward the bottom of the wall at a 45o angle and continuously expanded to form a funnel-shaped distribution of diagonal cracks. When loaded to a 0.39% inter-story displacement angle, there was a slight bulging at the bottom of the steel tube column, and the specimen gradually entered the yield stage. When the inter-story displacement angle reached 0.75%, the concrete at the bottom of the superimposed wall began to fall off, and many cracks appeared and formed an X-shaped distribution. The peak load in the positive direction was 1488 kN, and the peak load in the opposite direction was 1283 kN. When the inter-story displacement angle reached 1% in the negative direction, the strength of the specimen decreased to below 80% of the peak strength, and the test ended.

CW-1

For the CW-1 specimen, when the inter-story displacement angle reached 0.22%, the first horizontal crack in the specimen appeared at a height of 700 mm at the connection between the superimposed shear wall and the end column. The crack extended towards the bottom of the wall and gradually developed into a diagonal crack. Subsequently, the cracks continued to expand, with most being funnel-shaped oblique cracks that gradually developed upwards to a height of 1200 mm on the wall and became wider. When loaded to a displacement angle of 0.72% between floors, concrete crushing occurred at the bottom of the wall and on both sides of the middle superimposed shear wall. At this point, cracks had spread throughout almost the entire wall. Unlike CFST-SSWs, the X-shaped crack in the middle composite shear wall was connected to the crack in the cast-in-place column. When loaded to an inter-story displacement angle of 1.5%, the specimen suffered severe damage, and the hysteresis curve significantly decreased. When the inter-story displacement angle reached 1.5% of the peak value at the same level, the strength decreased to below 80% of the peak strength, and the test ended.

Comparison of SW-1 and CW-1 failure phenomena

A quantitative analysis of the failure phenomena of specimens SW-1 and CW-1 revealed distinct differences in their performance. Initial cracking in SW-1 was observed at an inter-story drift angle of 0.25%, whereas the first crack appeared earlier in CW-1 at 0.22%, indicating that SW-1 possessed a higher initial stiffness. In terms of peak performance, CW-1 demonstrated a superior load-bearing capacity, achieving a peak load of 1499.5 kN at a drift angle of 1.5%, compared to 1297.2 kN at 0.75% for SW-1. Regarding the ultimate state, SW-1 failed at a significantly larger drift angle of 2.0%, at which point its load dropped to 981.9 kN (75.7% of its peak). In contrast, CW-1 reached its failure point at a drift angle of 1.5% as its load fell below 80% of the peak. Consequently, the ultimate drift angle of SW-1 was 33.3% greater than that of CW-1, although CW-1 showed better load retention post-peak. This superior deformability of SW-1 was quantified by its ductility coefficient of 2.67, which was substantially higher than the 1.00 recorded for CW-1, confirming its excellent deformation capacity.

The damage to the middle wall limb of CFST-SSWs was not worse than that of the cast-in-place end column composite shear wall. The hysteresis curve indicated bending, and cracks in the cast-in-place concrete end column composite shear wall spread throughout almost the entire wall. The X-shaped crack in the middle composite shear wall was connected to the crack in the cast-in-place column. The concrete at the bottom of the specimen was crushed and fell off. The CFST-SSWs exhibited more obvious yield characteristics, with bulging at the bottom of the steel tube column and less severe cracking. After reaching the peak strength in the hysteresis curve, the bottom concrete of the cast-in-place concrete end column composite shear wall collapsed, and its bearing capacity rapidly decreased. The steel bars at the corners of the column and wall were exposed. However, due to the action of the steel tube column, the concrete in the post-pouring area of the composite wall gradually fell off, and the cracks widened and increased in number. However, the cracks did not spread throughout the entire wall during the experiment. The ductility and energy dissipation capacity of cast-in-place concrete end column composite shear walls were worse than those of CFST-SSWs, and the crack development was more severe.

Hysteresis curves

The hysteresis curves of shear wall specimens are shown in Fig. 7. During the early loading stages, the hysteresis loop area was very small, and the hysteresis curve was almost linear. As the interlayer displacement angle increased, cracks began to appear in the superimposed shear wall specimens. The area of the hysteresis loop gradually increased, and the hysteresis curve tended to bend downward. As the specimen entered the elastic-plastic transition, the shape of the hysteresis loop changed from spindle-shaped to bow-shaped, and pinching became increasingly prominent. After reaching the peak strength, the area of the hysteresis loop for the interlayer displacement angles loaded in the second cycle was significantly smaller than that during the first cycle. This indicated that the load-bearing capacity of the specimen decreased rapidly. When the load-bearing capacity dropped below 80% of the peak load capacity, the rate of decline significantly accelerated.

Fig. 7Hysteresis curve of each specimen.

Hysteresis curve of each specimen.

Comparing the hysteresis curves of specimens SW-1 and CW-1 shows that during the initial stage of loading, specimens exhibited similar performance and had roughly the same stiffness. During the continued loading process, the peak load of CW-1 exceeded that of SW-1, indicating a greater bearing capacity, which was related to the high reinforcement ratio of CW-1 specimens in the cast-in-place columns. After reaching the peak load, the strength degradation of the CW-1 specimen significantly accelerated, and its strength significantly decreased during the second loading cycle at the same peak inter-story displacement angle. This is also consistent with the detachment of a large amount of concrete and crushing at the bottom of the cast-in-place column after the peak load. Its plasticity and ductility were worse than those of the SW-1 specimen.

A comparison of the SW-1, SW-2, and SW-3 specimens showed that as the column-to-wall ratio increased, the ductility of the specimens improved. The inter-story displacement angle corresponding to the peak load of the specimens remained the same, but after exceeding the peak, the stiffness and strength degradation rate significantly slowed. This was because the ductility of the steel-concrete columns was better than that of cast-in-place column composite shear walls.

Skeleton curves

The skeleton curve of each specimen followed nearly the same trend, in which they exhibited a linear state before reaching the yield point. As the number of cracks within the concrete of the specimens increased, the skeleton curves bent until they reached their peak load. Due to severe damage to the specimens, the decline of the skeleton curves became more significant, as did the rate of decline.

The skeleton curves of SW-1 and CW-1 are shown in Fig. 8a, wherein SW-1 exhibited a higher initial stiffness than CW-1, while CW-1 showed a higher peak loading-bearing capacity. However, during the failure stage, the bearing capacity of CW-1 decreased more significantly and rapidly, indicating poorer elastic-plasticity and lower ductility. A comprehensive comparison showed that steel-tube reinforced concrete columns demonstrated performance comparable to that of cast-in-place concrete columns, but with a much higher ductility.

Fig. 8Comparison of the skeleton curves.

Comparison of the skeleton curves.

The skeleton curves of SW-1, SW-2, and SW-3 are shown in Fig. 8b. As the column-to-wall ratio increased, the ductility of the wall improved, and the strength declined more slowly after reaching the peak strength. This was attributed to the presence of the wider end column, which enhanced the relative contribution of the steel-tube-confined concrete column to the overall wall structure. Steel-tube-confined concrete columns are known for their high load-bearing capacity and excellent ductility, and increasing the column-to-wall ratio should improve the wall’s ductility. Within the parameters of our experiment, the width of the steel-tube column significantly influenced the compressive strength of the confined concrete within the tube under triaxial stress. A narrower cross-sectional width of the steel-tube column exerted a stronger confining effect on the internal concrete, which led to a higher strength enhancement coefficient for the concrete confined within the tube. Consequently, the peak stress of the specimen increased. For example, the SW-3 specimen had the narrowest steel-tube column width, resulting in the greatest enhancement of compressive strength for the confined concrete under triaxial stress. This clarifies the observed discrepancy between a high column-to-wall ratio and a low peak bearing capacity. This was exemplified by the SW-1 specimen, which, despite having a high column-to-wall ratio, exhibited a lower peak load-bearing capacity than the SW-3 specimen.

Stiffness degradation curves

The stiffness degradation curves for SW-1 and CW-1 depicted in Fig. 9. SW-1 has a higher peak stiffness, approximately 11.1% higher than that of CW-1. Its stiffness degradation rate is significantly higher, about 2.4 times that of CW-1.They shared an identical stiffness degradation trend and similar degradation rates. This confirms that the CFST-SSWs possessed a lateral stiffness comparable to that of cast-in-place concrete column composite shear walls.

Fig. 9Comparison of the stiffness degradation curves.

The stiffness degradation curves of SW-1, SW-2, and SW-3 are shown in Fig. 9b. SW-3 has the highest peak stiffness (169.0 kN/mm), which is 33.3% higher than that of SW-1. It also exhibits the highest stiffness degradation rate,, approximately 4 times that of SW-1. Under the same experimental parameters and overall shear wall width, a larger column-to-wall ratio slowed the stiffness degradation rate of CFST-SSWs. It also resulted in a greater stiffness of the steel-concrete column, which significantly increased the stiffness of the shear wall and increased the lateral stiffness of the entire shear wall specimen.

Strength degradation curves

The phenomenon in which the strength continuously decreased under the same inter-layer displacement angle as the number of loading cycles increased is referred to as strength degradation. As the inter-layer displacement angle increased, the strength degradation of the specimen became increasingly worse, as shown in Fig. 10.

The strength degradation curve of some specimens exhibited significant fluctuations. Before reaching yield, the strength degradation coefficient of the specimens was consistently above 90%. At the ultimate displacement, the coefficient is greater than 80%. A comparison of the graphs showed that SW-1 was more uniform and less severe than that of CW-1, and increasing the column-wall ratio and aspect ratio slowed the strength degradation rate and reduced its magnitude.

Ductility analysis

The ductility is assessed by the ductility factor (µ), which is defined as the ratio of the ultimate inter-story drift ratio to the yield inter-story drift ratio of the specimen, as expressed in Eq. The ultimate point was defined as the point on the yield points where the load-carrying capacity has degraded to 80% of its peak value, and the corresponding inter-story drift ratio is taken as θu. Therefore, this method was adopted in this study to determine the yield point of the CFST-SSWs.

Compared with specimen SW-1, specimen CW-1 exhibited a 43% higher yield inter-story drift angle, a 23% higher peak load, and a 124% higher peak inter-story drift angle. In contrast, its ultimate inter-story drift angle was 2% lower, and its ductility coefficient was 31% lower. This confirms that CFST columns offer superior ductility enhancement compared with cast-in-place concrete columns.

Comparing SW-1, SW-2, and SW-3, when the widths of the concrete-filled steel tube (CFST) columns of specimens SW-4 and SW-5 were reduced by 100 mm and 200 mm relative to that of SW-1, their ductility coefficients decreased by 34% and 39%, respectively. The ductility coefficient of the specimens decreased as the width of the CFST column decreased, confirming the significant role of the CFST columns in enhancing the ductility of the CFST-SSWs.

According to FEMA 356, a shear wall specimen is defined as having moderate ductility if its ductility coefficient falls within the range of 2–4. In this study, the average ductility coefficients of specimens, considering both positive and negative loading directions, ranged from 2.23 to 3.95. Therefore, the CFST-SSWs satisfy the requirements for moderate ductility and are suitable for applications in structural systems where such performance is required (Fig. 11).

Energy dissipation analysis

The energy dissipation capacity of cast-in-place concrete column composite shear walls and CFST-SSWs was measured by the area enclosed by the hysteresis curve at each inter-story displacement angle. The cumulative energy dissipation curves under various parameters in Fig. 12a show that SW-1 and CW-1 consumed similar amounts of energy, and their cumulative energy dissipation curves overlapped before reaching a 1% inter-story displacement angle. At an inter-story displacement angle of 1.5%, the energy dissipation of CW-1 was 13.66% higher than that of SW-1, but SW-1 achieved a 2% inter-story displacement angle, and the difference between the two was not significant. This indicates that the steel tube edge columns showed significantly greater energy dissipation capacity than concrete composite shear walls.

Displacement and strain analysis

According to the displacement sensor readings at each inter-story displacement angle, the sliding displacement between the CFST columns and the superimposed wall was almost zero before concrete cracking. It showed a linear increase during the yielding stage. At the peak displacement, the sliding displacement of the specimen was less than 5 mm, while at the ultimate load or during severe damage, the sliding displacement did not exceed 6 mm.

Before reaching the peak inter-story displacement angle, the normal strain distribution of the cross-section of the SW-1 specimen 100 mm from the bottom of the wall is shown in Fig. 13. Within a relatively high range of interlayer displacement angles, the strain distribution was approximately linear. From the horizontal line where the normal strain was zero, under compression, the neutral axis gradually moved towards the edge of the side wall and converged at an inter-story displacement angle range exceeding 0.182%. This indicates that the CFST-SSWs conformed to the planar section assumption. The neutral axis did not move as the lateral displacement was increased, which indicates plastic deformation within the specimen.

Calculation of cracking load

The quasi-static tests revealed that the failure mode of the CFST-SSWs was primarily a flexural-shear failure, dominated by flexural behavior. Conclusions

The seismic performance of superimposed shear walls was evaluated using quasi-static experimental testing and analysis. The study focused on the impact of the different end column types and widths of CFST columns on the superimposed shear wall performance. Key findings from this study are summarized below.

Under a vertical axial pressure and horizontal low-cycle cyclic loading, the specimen underwent bending failure. The X-shaped crack in the CFST-SSWs specimen only extended to the edge of the superimposed wall, while that of the cast-in-place concrete column composite shear wall specimen connected to a crack in the end column. Throughout the testing phase, all specimens demonstrated flexural failure, characterized by concrete cracking under compression and the yielding of longitudinal bars and steel tubes under tension. The tests also confirmed that CFST-SSWs are capable of providing significant axial and lateral resistances while effectively dissipating hysteretic energy.The SW specimen possessed a higher initial stiffness. In terms of peak performance, the CW specimen demonstrated a superior load-bearing capacity. Regarding the ultimate state, the ultimate drift angle of the SW specimen was 33.3% greater than that of the CW specimen, but CW-1 showed better post-peak load retention. As the widths of the CFST column increased, the ductility increased by 13.47%, and the bearing capacity increased by approximately 14.5% on average. This confirms that the CFST columns enhanced the ductility of the CFST-SSWs. The average ductility coefficients of specimens ranged from 2.23 to 3.95, making them suitable for applications in structural systems that require such performance. A wider CFST column slowed the stiffness degradation and intensity degradation of CFST-SSWs. Increasing the width of the CFST column hardly affected its overall energy dissipation capacity in the early stage, but significantly increased the ultimate inter-story displacement angle. A calculation model was developed to predict the CFST-SSWs cracking load, and experimental data were used to validate the model’s accuracy, which showed an error of less than 11%.
