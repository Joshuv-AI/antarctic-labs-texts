Indigenous Status and Outcomes in Deep Neck Space Infections: An ICU Cohort Study
Authors: Jonathan Daniel, Joshua Kovoor, Krishnaswamy Sundararajan, Jake Jervis‐Bardy, Giri Krishnan, Brandon Stretton, Suzanne Edwards, Stephen Bacchi, Paul Sambrook, Andrew Foreman, Kelvin Kong, John‐Charles Hodge, Gerry O'Callaghan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundDeep neck space infections (DNSIs) present ongoing challenges to all clinicians, from physicians to surgeons. While several risk factors for adverse outcomes have been well established, to our knowledge, this is the first study to link Indigenous status directly to DNSI outcomes. We performed a retrospective cohort analysis of 351 adult patients with DNSIs who were admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU) at a South Australian quaternary centre between 2018 and 2024. We assessed outcomes such as hospital length of stay (LOS), 30‐day readmission rates, and returns to the theater. We had a particular emphasis on Indigenous status and socioeconomic status.MethodsMultivariable linear regressions were performed to analyse the primary outcome of Hospital LOS identifying several associations with adverse DNSI outcomes. Indigenous status was self‐identified by patients to be Aboriginal and/or Torres Strait Islander. Socioeconomic disparities were characterized using the Socioeconomic index for areas (SEIFA) deciles.ResultsAboriginal and Torres Strait Islander patients had markedly longer hospital stays (adjusted mean ratio, 1.41; 95% CI, 1.10–1.81; p = 0.0073) and a more than five‐fold increase in readmission rates (OR, 5.59; 95% CI, 1.55–20.19; p = 0.0086) compared to nonindigenous patients. Each 10‐year increase in age was associated with a 6% rise in the mean LOS (p = 0.0038), and each single unit increase in glucose level correlated with a 3% increase in LOS (p = 0.0077).ConclusionThese findings underscore the importance of incorporating demographic variables such as Indigenous status, alongside clinical parameters, in DNSI risk stratification and management. The literature addressing the influence of Indigenous status in surgical outcomes, particularly in DNSIs, remains limited. We advocate for further prospective observational studies and the development of care models co‐designed with Indigenous patients and healthcare professionals as key stakeholders, with the goal of narrowing persistent disparities across Australian healthcare.Keywords: indigenous, infection, intensive care, risk factors

Introduction

Deep neck space infections (DNSIs) are potentially lethal pathologies with mortality rates persisting between 1% and 25% despite the many advancements of modern medicine [1, 2, 3]. While clinicians are well‐versed in traditional risk factors such as advanced age, diabetes, and delayed presentation, the influence of factors such as socioeconomic status and particularly Indigenous background on DNSI severity and presentations is often overlooked [4, 5, 6]. The health disparities faced by Indigenous populations globally are stark [5]. In Australia, there is a life expectancy gap of approximately 8 years between Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander populations and non‐Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people [7, 8]. Such statistics, even in light of advances in modern medicine, continue to serve as a grim reminder of the complex historical and cultural factors that shape health outcomes [4, 5, 9, 10]. Despite a significant amount of DNSI research, there is a scarcity of studies focusing on these more nontraditional risk factors [5]. Our study aims to broaden the understanding of DNSIs and aid in their management by informing targeted interventions and enhancing care for all DNSI patients, particularly those from vulnerable populations.

Methods

Study Design and Participants

We conducted a retrospective cohort study at The Royal Adelaide Hospital, a quaternary centre in Adelaide, South Australia. Cases from January 1, 2018, to June 1, 2024, were examined to include adult patients (aged 18 years or older) who had a diagnosis of DNSI based on clinical presentation and confirmed with imaging, computed tomography (CT) being the most common modality. We excluded patients with incomplete records or those transferred to other facilities. Indigenous status was determined by patient self‐identification as Aboriginal and/or Torres Strait Islander, as recorded in medical records; no other Indigenous populations were represented in the cohort.

A DNSI was defined as a clinically evident infection of one or more deep cervical fascial spaces confirmed on contrast‐enhanced CT, requiring intravenous antibiotics and/or surgical drainage. Included entities comprised parapharyngeal, retropharyngeal, submandibular, buccal, masticator, and parotid space abscesses, Ludwig's angina, and suppurative sialadenitis.

Data Collection

A team of trained research assistants extracted data from electronic medical records. We collected information on demographics, age, sex, Indigenous status, socioeconomic status using Socioeconomic index for areas (SEIFA) deciles, and clinical parameters such as glucose levels, hospital transfers, admission timing, length of stay (LOS), 30‐day readmission, and return to theater. “Glucose high” was defined as the highest recorded blood glucose level during a patient's admission. A glucose high > 7 mmol/L was considered elevated, irrespective of diabetes status. The highest recorded BGL was utilized instead of HbA1C because it was more consistently available across the retrospective cohort. While single‐point glucose levels can be elevated in the context of steroid use common in DNSI management, these peak levels remain a clinically relevant indicator of acute metabolic stress [11]. Furthermore, specific bacteriology data, such as the prevalence of Streptococcus milleri, were not systematically extracted due to inconsistent microbiological reporting in electronic records during the study period; this is addressed further in the discussion.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics with continuous variables are presented as mean (SD) or median (IQR), depending on their distribution. Categorical variables were expressed as numbers and percentages. Multivariable linear regressions were performed for Hospital LOS being our primary outcome against: Indigenous status, SEIFA deciles, age, sex, year of admission, admission source (home vs. other), and diabetes (Type 1, Type 2 or not previously diagnosed) (one model) or glucose high (second model). Multivariable linear regressions were performed for the secondary outcomes: ICU and Ward LOS. Assumptions of a linear model, including normality of residuals and homoscedasticity, were found to be upheld throughout by inspection of scatter plots and histograms of predicted values and residuals only when a logarithm of the outcome was used.

Logistic regressions were performed for secondary outcomes, including readmission and hospital outcome (home vs. other), with predictors of Indigenous status and SEIFA deciles.

The statistical software used was SAS On Demand for Academics (SAS Institute Inc. 2021). A p value ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethical Considerations

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Central Adelaide Health Network Ethics Committee (approval number: 16860).

Results

351 patients with DNSIs were admitted to the Intensive Care Unit (ICU) in a quaternary hospital in South Australia over a 6‐year period. As identified in Figure 1, 70.1% of these infections originated from an odontogenic etiology (247/349), with the remainder secondary to either peritonsillar (10.1%) or parapharyngeal (4.5%) space abscesses and suppurative sialadenitis (6.3%). The mortality rate in our cohort was 0.56% (2/351).

Etiology of deep neck space infection cases (n = 351).

Demographically, as depicted in Table 1, Aboriginal or Torres Strait Islanders comprised 7.1% (n = 25/351) of the cohort. The median age of all patients was 42 years, with a male predominance of 59%. There was a statistically significant association between Hospital LOS and Indigenous status (p = 0.0073). Patients who were Aboriginal or Torres Strait Islander had mean Hospital LOS 1.41 times greater than those who were nonindigenous (mean ratio = 1.41, 95% CI: 1.10, 1.81). A total of 16 patients were readmitted, of whom 4 (25.0%) were Indigenous. There was a statistically significant association between readmission rates and Aboriginal or Torres Strait Islander status (p = 0.0086), with Indigenous patients having odds of readmission 5.59 times higher than nonindigenous patients (Odds Ratio = 5.59, 95% CI: 1.55, 20.19). A total of 56.3% of cases (9/16) were readmitted without the need for further surgery. Of the remaining 43.7% (7/16) that required surgical intervention, operative records were retrievable for 6 of 7 patients; of these, 2 (33.0%) were of Aboriginal or Torres Strait Islander background. Documentation did not reliably identify instances of early self‐discharge among readmitted patients, which has been noted as a potential contributor and a target for future data collection and culturally safe discharge planning.

Out of 351 patients, 40 (11.4%) had diabetes mellitus Type 1 or 2 (DMT1/2), 9 (2.5%) had DMT1, 31 (8.8%) had DMT2. Indigenous patients had a higher overall incidence of diabetes (6/25, 24.0%) compared to nonindigenous patients (34/326, 10.4%); Indigenous status remained independently associated with longer LOS after adjusting for age and diabetes status. There was a statistically significant association between Hospital LOS and Glucose High (p = 0.0077). For every 1 unit increase in Glucose High, the mean Hospital LOS increased by 3% (mean ratio = 1.03, 95% CI: 1.01, 1.06) (Table 2). Clinically, this corresponds to an approximate increase of 0.26 days in mean hospital LOS for every 1 mmol/L increase in BGL.

Table 1 summarizes frequency data across the cohort including the breakdown of each of the SEIFA deciles. Used as a proxy for socioeconomic disadvantage, the SEIFA scores did not confer significance in outcomes such as hospital or ICU LOS. However, they were associated with longer exit block hours—defined as the time elapsed between medical discharge readiness and physical departure from the ward or ICU—with a mean ratio of 1.07 (p = 0.0030).

Discussion

Several risk factors are associated with poor outcomes in patients with DNSIs. Consistent with the existing literature, we found that advanced age and elevated glucose levels significantly impacted hospital LOS [1, 2]. The observed relationship between high glucose levels and prolonged recovery is consistently supported by literature demonstrating that acute hyperglycaemia impairs immune function and wound healing [12]. Similarly, Huang et al. [2, 13] reported that age over 50 years old independently predicted complications in DNSIs, while diabetes mellitus has been independently associated with prolonged hospitalization in this setting. While traditional risk factors have been extensively studied, our findings highlight the crucial role of Aboriginal or Torres Strait Islander status as an independent predictor of adverse outcomes in DNSIs. It is important to note that socioeconomic status (SEIFA decile) did not significantly predict hospital LOS in our results (p = 0.9147), suggesting that cultural barriers and systemic health inequities may play a more critical role than economic disadvantage alone.

The link between Indigenous status and poor DNSI outcomes adds to the growing evidence of health disparities faced by Indigenous populations worldwide. While research specifically on DNSIs in Aboriginal or Torres Strait Islander patients is limited, broader studies have consistently shown worse surgical outcomes for Indigenous people across various specialties and countries [5, 14]. A systematic review and meta‐analysis in particular identified that Indigenous patients across North America and Oceania experience higher rates of postoperative complications and increased hospital stays across multiple surgical disciplines [14]. Our findings resemble these results with increased LOS and significant readmission rates among Indigenous DNSI patients. Specifically, 25% (4/16) of readmitted patients identified as Indigenous, which is disproportionately higher than their representation in the total cohort (7.1%), although numbers are overall low.

As aforementioned, the influence of socioeconomic status was explored using SEIFA (socioeconomic Indexes for Areas) deciles, a measure that ranks areas in Australia based on relative socioeconomic advantage and disadvantage created by the Australian Bureau of Statistics [15]. Although not associated with overall LOS, it was, however, associated with longer “exit block hours” (mean ratio: 1.07, p = 0.0030). This parameter describes the additional time spent in the hospital after being medically fit for discharge. This suggests that such patients face delays in transitioning from acute care to other care settings, which may be indicative of a system that is not equipped to provide the necessary supports for certain populations in our society. This finding may reflect higher healthcare resource utilization and warrants further study. Addressing exit block delays would likely require system‐wide improvements, such as more effective care coordination and case management, access to culturally safe post‐acute care pathways, and the implementation of telehealth services to facilitate follow‐up care.

Our results regarding the impact of age on hospital LOS are consistent with previous DNSI literature; we build on this by quantifying the incremental impact of age on LOS and highlighting the vulnerability of older patients who may experience slower recovery due to comorbidities and decreased physiological reserve. Tailored interventions, such as early mobilization programs and enhanced discharge planning for older patients, could help mitigate these challenges.

The readmission rate for patients with DNSIs in our cohort was 4.6% (16/351), with 25% (4/16) of readmissions involving Aboriginal or Torres Strait Islander patients. This disproportionate representation—relative to their 7.1% share of the total cohort—underscores the vulnerability of this population to adverse post‐discharge outcomes and suggests that current discharge and follow‐up pathways may not adequately meet their needs.

Given the predominance of odontogenic infections in this cohort, disparities in access to preventive and urgent dental care likely also contribute to delayed presentation and severity. Australia and particularly South Australia's geographical barriers not only include cost, but also remoteness and rurality with limited culturally safe services; addressing these upstream determinants is integral to closing the outcome gap [6, 16].

A study by Kerrigan et al. [17] suggests that systemic inequities in access to primary care and late presentation often exacerbate outcomes in marginalized populations. Factors such as effective communication with consistent use of interpreter services significantly improved satisfaction and reduced self‐discharge and readmission rates [17]. Furthermore, patient family involvement and increasing the number of Aboriginal healthcare workers have enhanced cultural safety and improved patient experiences [17, 18]. This further supports our observation that Indigenous status remains a significant predictor of readmission and prolonged stay, likely for reasons outlined above.

A promising Australian intervention is the “Aboriginal Wellbeing Hospital Discharge Booklet,” developed by the Mid North Coast Local Health District in Australia [19]. This culturally appropriate resource aimed to improve the discharge journey for Aboriginal patients by providing information on Aboriginal hospital liaison officers, outlining the 48‐h follow‐up process after discharge, detailing medication information and changes, listing follow‐up appointments in the community, and explaining available home support services. Implementation of this booklet led to improved coordination between healthcare services, resulting in a reduced hospital LOS and a lower risk of readmission within 28 days [19]. More specifically, a retrospective cohort study of approximately 18 000 patients showed that receipt of a 48 h follow‐up by either phone or in‐person review was associated with both a reduction in emergency department presentations and at least one adverse event within 28 days of discharge [20].

Another effective approach was the cardiology department at Princess Alexandra Hospital in Brisbane, Australia, which published a model of care developed in partnership with Indigenous communities [21]. This model resulted in a significant reduction in unplanned cardiac readmissions for Indigenous patients, from 32% to 18% (p = 0.0060). A multidisciplinary team, comprising clinical nurse consultants, an Indigenous Hospital Liaison Officer, a medical officer, a pharmacist, and an administrative officer, provided personalized support, education, and care coordination for Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander patients with acute coronary syndrome. This approach also produced a significant improvement in the primary composite outcome at 90 days (34% vs. 20%, p = 0.012), effectively eliminating the disparity in outcomes between Indigenous and nonindigenous patients [21].

The observed disparities in studies like ours and those discussed clearly display the multifaceted and complex issues that encompass not only medical, social, and systemic factors but have been in play for many years. Our findings have significant implications for clinical practice and health care policy. By stating objective measures and defining disparities, they serve to highlight the true nature of the issue and provide a benchmark for future medical progress. Moreover, they suggest that Indigenous status should be considered a key factor held in a similar regard to more traditional risk factors such as age and glucose levels in the risk stratification and management decisions for DNSI patients. Reviewing the literature, the interventions that have been effective in improving healthcare in these settings involve having key individuals with lived experiences who can serve as both consumer advocates and designers in models of care [21]. A carefully designed prospective observational study, in partnership with Indigenous patients and advocates, would help identify key factors that could lead to the co‐design of a model of care tailored to meet the healthcare outcomes and cultural requirements of this consumer cohort.

Strengths and Limitations

Despite this study's relatively large sample size and the use of multivariate analysis, several key limitations should be considered. The retrospective, single‐center aspects of the study, as well as the difficulty in accounting for all potential confounding factors, such as patient comorbidities or the severity of infection as indicated by blood markers at presentation, all contribute to its limited applicability. Furthermore, detailed bacteriology of these infections is a critical consideration. While specific data on heavily implicated groups such as Streptococcus milleri prevalence, which is already well established in the literature, was not collected for this retrospective review—existing literature suggests that this, along with other key pathogens, are key considerations in DNSIs [22, 23]. Additionally, while SEIFA deciles provide a measure of area‐level socioeconomic status, they may only partially capture the extent of the impact on the individual's socioeconomic factors.

Conclusion

This retrospective analysis identifies Indigenous status in an Australian retrospective cohort study as a significant and independent predictor of adverse outcomes in DNSI alongside established risk factors such as advanced age and elevated glucose levels. These findings contribute meaningfully to the limited literature on DNSIs in Indigenous populations and highlight an important area for future research. These future efforts should focus on prospective, multicentre studies with the aim of developing improved shared models of care to ultimately optimize the management of this complex surgical pathology.
