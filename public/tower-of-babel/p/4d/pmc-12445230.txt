Ex vivo expansion of corneal endothelial cells enabled by small molecule inhibitors of LATS kinase
Authors: Natsuki Abe-Fukasawa, Ryuhei Hayashi, Mio Morita, Shohei Azuma, Takumi Iwawaki, Kenta Kagaya, Taito Nishino, Kohji Nishida
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionTransplantation of expanded corneal endothelial cells (CECs) has been regarded as a promising approach for treating corneal diseases caused by CEC damage or dysfunction. However, an efficient method for expanding CECs remains inadequately established.MethodsWe examined whether small molecule inhibitors of large tumor suppressor kinase (LATS) promote the proliferation of CECs. We also evaluated the expression of functional markers in CECs treated with the inhibitors.ResultsWe found that LATS kinase inhibitors enhance the cell density of bovine CECs ex vivo. CECs that were expanded in the presence of these inhibitors exhibited increased nuclear translocation of yes-associated protein (YAP) and upregulated expression of YAP-regulated genes. Furthermore, we observed that YAP was essential for promoting cell proliferation. Notably, the inhibitors also increased the density of primary human CECs. Expanded human CECs expressed CEC functional markers, including Na+/K+-transporting ATPase subunit alpha-1 (ATP1A1), Zonula occludens-1 (ZO-1), and N-cadherin; they showed upregulated expression of YAP-regulated genes.ConclusionsCollectively, these findings support the development of efficient culture techniques for CEC expansion and may facilitate the advancement of therapeutic strategies for CEC-associated diseases.

Introduction

The cornea is a transparent, avascular tissue located in the anterior segment of the eye. The corneal endothelium constitutes a single layer of corneal endothelial cells (CECs) that line the entire posterior surface of the cornea. CECs are essential for maintaining corneal transparency through their pump and barrier functions, which reduce aqueous humor influx into the corneal stroma [1,2]. Adult human CECs do not regenerate in vivo; they are arrested in the G1 phase of the cell cycle and possess severely limited proliferative capacity [3,4]. Consequently, a reduction in cell number or function—due to Fuchs endothelial corneal dystrophies, glaucoma, aging, injury, or surgical trauma—leads to corneal overhydration (i.e., corneal edema) [5]. When CEC density, typically 2500–3000 cells/mm2 in healthy individuals, falls below 1000 cells/mm2, endothelial function becomes deficient, potentially resulting in irreversible blindness [6,7]. The definitive and conventional treatment for this dysfunction is corneal transplantation, or keratoplasty, using healthy cadaveric donor tissue [8,9].

Penetrating keratoplasty was historically the most frequently employed procedure for managing blindness caused by corneal endothelial diseases or injuries. Recently, innovative surgical techniques categorized under endothelial keratoplasty have been developed. Endothelial keratoplasty includes Descemet's stripping automated endothelial keratoplasty and Descemet's membrane endothelial keratoplasty. Compared with conventional penetrating keratoplasty, endothelial keratoplasty offers several advantages, such as significantly reduced risk of graft rejection, fewer post-transplantation complications, and shorter durations of corticosteroid therapy [5,8,9]. These techniques, which selectively transplant the corneal endothelium, decrease the amount of allogenic tissue transplanted and thus reduce the risk of immune response. Despite the superior therapeutic outcomes of endothelial keratoplasty, the global supply of cadaveric donor tissue remains insufficient to meet the demand for keratoplasty. A growing global shortage of donor corneal tissue has been reported; worldwide, 12.7 million patients await corneal transplantation [8,10]. The development of alternative therapeutic strategies that reduce the reliance on donor tissues is needed to address this unmet clinical demand.

Accordingly, cell-based therapies are under active investigation and early clinical development. In these approaches, CECs derived from donor corneas are transplanted into the anterior chamber of the eye, either as a suspension of cells or as an intact cell sheet [[11], [12], [13]]. Recently, Kinoshita et al. demonstrated that intracameral injection of donor CECs in combination with a Rho-associated protein kinase (ROCK) inhibitor ameliorated corneal edema in patients with bullous keratopathy [14]. The primary advantage of cell therapy lies in the possibility of expanding CECs ex vivo from a single donor, thereby increasing the number of patients who can receive treatment. Numerous efforts have been made to culture and expand human CECs for greater availability of transplantable cells [[15], [16], [17], [18]]. Multiple factors contribute to the maintenance of CECs in a quiescent state, including contact inhibition, cell cycle regulation, and growth-related signaling pathways (e.g., those involving transforming growth factor-β) [[19], [20], [21]]. However, efficient expansion of CECs in vitro remains challenging due to limited knowledge of the signals that can elicit their full proliferative potential. Furthermore, practical obstacles persist in the development of cell culture systems that can consistently produce high-quality CECs while preserving their characteristic morphology and functional properties [[15], [16], [17], [18]].

In this study, we hypothesized that inhibition of the Hippo signaling pathway could selectively activate pathways that promote CEC proliferation ex vivo. Large tumor suppressor kinases (LATS) 1 and 2 are core components of the Hippo pathway [[22], [23], [24]]. Our previous study showed that LATS inhibitors GA-002 and GA-017, which we originally identified, promoted the proliferation of various animal cells, particularly under three-dimensional (3D) culture conditions [25]. GA-002 and GA-017 possess potent and selective inhibitory activity against LATS1/2 kinases, although they exhibit distinct chemical structures (Fig. 1) [25]. The 50 % inhibition concentration (IC50) values of GA-017 against LATS1 and 2 were 4.10 ± 0.79 and 3.92 ± 0.42 nM, respectively and those of GA-002 were 3.93 ± 0.73 and 3.87 ± 0.08 nM, respectively [25]. Notably, GA-017 substantially enhanced the growth of human umbilical vein endothelial cells, which exhibit simple squamous morphology identical to CECs. Based on this observation, we suspected that these inhibitors also stimulate CEC proliferation. Materials and methods

Isolation of human primary CECs

Human primary CECs were isolated from donor corneoscleral rims purchased from CorneaGen (Seattle, WA, USA). Donor ages ranged from 35 to 69 years. Descemet's membranes containing human primary CECs were peeled from the corneal stroma and digested with 1 mL of 0.1 % collagenase (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) at 37 °C for 7–12 h. After digestion, 7 mL of 2 % B27 supplement (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) was added. The isolated cells were centrifuged, resuspended in primary culture medium, and plated into 48-well plates coated with iMatrix-511 silk (Nippi, Tokyo, Japan). After 1 or 2 weeks, the culture medium was changed to maintenance medium. Upon reaching confluency, the medium was replaced with serum medium lacking basic fibroblast growth factor. The primary culture medium consisted of Dulbecco's modified Eagle medium (DMEM)/F12 supplemented with 2 % B27 (Thermo Fisher Scientific), 5 mg/mL Albumax II Lipid-Rich BSA (Thermo Fisher Scientific), 4 ng/mL basic fibroblast growth factor (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical, Osaka, Japan), 0.1 % benzylpenicillin potassium (Meiji Seika Pharma, Tokyo, Japan), and 0.1 % streptomycin sulfate (Meiji Seika Pharma). The maintenance medium was composed of the primary culture medium supplemented with 20 μg/mL ascorbic acid magnesium (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical) and 10 μM Y-27632 (Selleck Chemicals, Houston, TX, USA). The serum medium included DMEM (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with 10 % fetal bovine serum (FBS), 2 mM l-glutamine (Thermo Fisher Scientific), 0.1 % benzylpenicillin potassium, and 0.1 % streptomycin sulfate.

Isolation of rabbit primary CECs

Male New Zealand White rabbits (2.0–2.5 kg) were purchased from Oriental Yeast (Tokyo, Japan) and housed under conventional conditions at Nissan Chemical (Saitama, Japan). All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the guidelines of the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the Biological Research Laboratory, Nissan Chemical (Approval No. A003021). Rabbit primary CECs were isolated from Descemet's membranes. The membranes were peeled from the corneal stroma and digested with 1 mL of Trypsin/EDTA (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical) at 37 °C for 3–5 min. Digestion was halted by adding low-glucose DMEM containing 10 % FBS, 1 % penicillin-streptomycin (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and 2 ng/mL basic fibroblast growth factor (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical). The cells were centrifuged, resuspended in fresh medium, and plated in six-well plates coated with FNC coating mix (Athena Environmental Sciences, Baltimore, MD, USA).

Cell culture

BCE C/D-1b cells were maintained in DMEM supplemented with 10 % FBS. The culture medium for human primary CECs and rabbit primary CECs was refreshed every 3–4 days. For passaging or experimental assays, human primary CECs were detached using Accutase (Nacalai Tesque, Kyoto, Japan) and reseeded at a 1:2 ratio onto iMatrix-511–coated plates. BCE C/D-1b cells and human primary CECs were cultured at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5 % CO2. Rabbit primary CECs were maintained under the same temperature and humidity conditions but with 7 % CO2.

Reagents

GA-002 and GA-017 were synthesized using standard chemical procedures as previously described [25]. All other small-molecule compounds used in this study are listed in Table S1. All compounds were dissolved in DMSO and stored at −20 °C.

Cell proliferation assay

To evaluate cell proliferation, WST-8 solution (Dojindo Laboratories, Kumamoto, Japan) was added to each well at one-tenth of the culture volume and incubated at 37 °C for 100 min. Viable cells were quantified by measuring absorbance at 450 nm using a SpectraMax® 190 absorbance spectrometer (Molecular Devices, San Jose, CA, USA). For the adenosine triphosphate (ATP) assay, which quantifies cell number based on ATP detection, an equal volume of ATP reagent (CellTiter-Glo® Luminescent Cell Viability Assay; Promega, Madison, WI, USA) was added to the culture medium, and luminescence intensity (relative light units) was measured using an EnSpire system (PerkinElmer, Waltham, MA, USA). For image-based analysis, cell number was determined using the Operetta CLS™ system (PerkinElmer) by counting Hoechst 33342–stained nuclei.

Monolayer culture

Cells were seeded at densities ranging from 7 × 103 to 190 × 103 cells/mL in the appropriate medium and plated in 96-well flat-bottom tissue culture–treated plates (Corning Incorporated, Corning, NY, USA) or 96-well cyclo olefin polymer plates (PerkinElmer), at 100 μL per well. For compound treatment, the culture medium was replaced with medium containing the test compounds after overnight incubation. Cells were maintained for 1–29 days at 37 °C. Cell numbers were assessed using either the ATP assay or imaging analysis.

Cell aggregate culture on U-bottom microplates

Cells were seeded in the appropriate medium and plated in 96-well U-bottom low-attachment plates (Corning Incorporated) at 700 cells per 90-μL/well. For compound treatment, 10 μL of medium containing a 10-fold concentration of the test compound were added. Cultures were incubated for 1–7 days at 37 °C. Cell numbers were evaluated using the ATP assay.

HTRF assay

BCE C/D-1b cells or human primary CECs were suspended in DMEM (phenol red–free) supplemented with 10 % FBS and seeded at a density of 1 × 105 cells/mL (90 μL/well) in 96-well plates. Cells were incubated at 37 °C for 4 h and subsequently treated with DMSO, GA-002, or GA-017 for 1 h at 37 °C. Total YAP and phosphorylated YAP (Ser127) levels were measured using an HTRF assay kit (PerkinElmer). Fluorescence resonance energy transfer signals were read using an EnVision plate reader (PerkinElmer) at 615 nm (donor) and 665 nm (acceptor) for BCE C/D-1b cells and Infinite M1000 PRO (Tecan, Männedorf, Switzerland) at 620 nm (donor) and 655 nm (acceptor) for human primary CECs. The HTRF ratio, which is positively correlated with the level of total or phosphorylated YAP, was calculated using the formula: HTRF ratio = (signal at 665 or 655 nm/signal at 615 or 620 nm) × 104.

Real-time polymerase chain reaction

Real-time polymerase chain reaction assays were carried out using an ABI PRISM® 7700 Sequence Detector (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with 40–45 amplification cycles (each cycle: 15 s at 95 °C and 1 min at 60 °C). All TaqMan primers and probes used in this study are listed in Table S1.

Nuclear translocation assay

Cells treated with GA-002, GA-017, or DMSO were fixed in 4 % paraformaldehyde in PBS. After fixation, cells were blocked and incubated with primary antibodies, Hoechst 33342 (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and the appropriate secondary antibodies. Fluorescence imaging was performed using the Operetta CLS™ system (PerkinElmer). Image analysis algorithms in Operetta were used to define the boundaries of cells and nuclei. The ratio of signal intensity in the nucleus to that in the cytoplasm was calculated. YAP was considered nuclear-translocated when this ratio exceeded 1.4 for BCE C/D-1b cells or 1.3 for human primary CECs. A ratio below 1.0 indicated cytoplasmic localization. All antibodies used are described in Table S1.

Plasmid

The pGL4.20-GTIIC reporter vector was constructed by inserting the following 350-bp synthetic DNA fragment (optimized and synthesized by Eurofins Japan [Kyoto, Japan]) between the 5′ NheI and 3′ NcoI sites of the pGL4.20 [luc2/Puro] vector (Promega): GCTAGCCCGGCCAGTGCCAAGTTGAGACACATTCCACACATTCCACTGCAAGCTTGAGACACATTCCACACATTCCACTGCAAGCTTCTAGCCCGGCCAGTGCCAAGTTGAGACACATTCCACACATTCCACTGCAAGCTTGAGACACATTCCACACATTCCACTGCAAGCTTCTAGAGATCTGCAGGTCGAGGTCGACGGTATCGATAAGCTTGGGGGTGGGCGCCGGGGGGACCTTAAAGCCTCTGCCCCCCAAGGAGCCCTTCCCAGACAGCCGCCGGCACCCACCGCTCCGTGGGACGATCCCCCAAGAGCTTGGCATTCCGGTACTGTTGGTAAAGCCACCATGG.

Transfection

One microgram of the pGL4.20-GTIIC reporter vector was transfected into BCE C/D-1b cells (2.4 × 105 cells/0.5 mL per well in a 24-well plate) using Lipofectamine™ 2000 Transfection Reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific). After transfection, cells were selected in culture medium containing 1 μg/mL puromycin (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Reporter assay

BCE C/D-1b reporter cells were seeded in 96-well flat-bottom tissue culture–treated plates (Corning Incorporated) at 4 × 104 cells/90 μL/well, with or without 1 μM Verteporfin, and incubated overnight at 37 °C. For compound treatment, 10 μL of medium containing a 10-fold concentration of the test compound were added. After 20 h of incubation at 37 °C, cell viability was assessed using the WST assay. An equal volume of ONE-Glo™ reagent (Promega) was subsequently added to each well, and luminescence intensity was measured using the EnSpire system (PerkinElmer). Luminescence values were normalized to those obtained from the WST assay.

Statistical analysis of experimental data

All results are presented as means ± SD. Statistical significance was assessed using Dunnett's or Tukey's test, as indicated in the figure legends, and calculated using EXSAS version 10.0.3 (Arm Systex, Osaka, Japan). P-values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Unless otherwise specified, comparisons were made relative to the DMSO control group.

Results

LATS inhibitors increase cell number among CECs in vitro

To investigate the effects of LATS inhibitors on CECs, we first utilized BCE C/D-1b cells, a bovine immortal CEC line selected for its availability and robust proliferative capacity in vitro [26]. We initially evaluated the activities of GA-002 and GA-017 in promoting BCE C/D-1b cell proliferation under 3D culture conditions, based on our prior findings concerning preferential growth promotion by LATS inhibitors in 3D environments [25]. GA-002 and GA-017 visibly increased both cell aggregate size and total cell number when the cells were cultured for up to 7 days in U-bottom wells of low-attachment culture plates (Fig. 2A and B). Furthermore, these compounds promoted BCE C/D-1b cell proliferation in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 2C). Additionally, GA-002 and GA-017 increased cell density in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 3C). However, GA-002 and GA-017 did not increase cell density or promote proliferation when the cells were sparsely seeded at less than 20 % confluence (Fig. S1), suggesting that cell-cell contact is essential for the growth-promoting effects of these compounds. Consistent with the 3D culture results, Y-27632 did not affect cell density or proliferation under 2D conditions (Fig. 3A and B). LATS inhibitors promote yes-associated protein (YAP) activation and nuclear translocation in a CEC line

Subsequently, we examined the effects of GA-002 and GA-017 on the Hippo signaling pathway in CECs. Under 2D conditions, treatment with these compounds significantly induced the expression of Hippo pathway–regulated genes, including cyr61, ctgf, ankrd1, and ccnd1 (Fig. 4A). To assess the activation state of YAP, a key transcriptional co-activator in the Hippo pathway [30,31], we performed a quantitative assay using the homogeneous time-resolved fluorescence (HTRF) method, which enables cell-based quantitative detection of total and phosphorylated YAP. GA-002 and GA-017 reduced YAP phosphorylation under 2D culture conditions (Fig. 4B). We next evaluated the effects of GA-002 and GA-017 on nuclear translocation of YAP. Consistent with the reduced phosphorylation observed in the previous assay, immunofluorescence microscopy demonstrated that GA-002 and GA-017 induced prominent nuclear localization of YAP (Fig. 3C and D, and S3). Additionally, Verteporfin, a potent inhibitor of the interaction between YAP and transcriptional enhanced associated domain (TEAD) transcription factors—mediated by Verteporfin-induced conformation changes in YAP [32]—reduced the growth-promoting effects of GA-002 and GA-017 in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 4E). Reporter gene assays confirmed that Verteporfin inhibited the expression of YAP-regulated genes induced by GA-002 and GA-017 in BCE C/D-1b cells (Fig. 4F). Similarly, Verteporfin suppressed GA-002- and GA-017–mediated induction of the Hippo pathway–regulated genes ankrd1, ctgf, cyr61, and ccnd1 (Fig. S4). LATS inhibitors increase the density of human primary CECs in culture

We investigated whether GA-002 and GA-017 could promote the proliferation of human primary CECs, which reportedly possess little regenerative capacity [3,4]. Based on the results of BCE C/D-1b cell proliferation (Fig. 3C) and our previous report [25], we set the concentrations of GA-002 and GA-017 to 10 μM each for experiments involving human primary CECs. GA-002 and GA-017 substantially increased cell density in human primary CECs compared with vehicle (DMSO)-treated controls over a 29-day culture period, without altering cell morphology (Fig. 5A and B). Moreover, GA-017 increased the cell density of rabbit primary CECs, and treated cells expressed ZO-1 protein (Fig. S5). Human CECs treated with GA-002 and GA-017 showed similar or higher expression of ATP1A1, ZO-1, and N-cadherin proteins compared with controls (Fig. 5C). ATP1A1, localized in the CEC membrane, plays a critical role in maintaining the pump function of the corneal endothelium [1,18,33]. ZO-1 is essential for the tight junctions between CECs, which inhibit aqueous humor penetration into the stroma [18,33]. N-cadherin serves as a specific marker for CECs and facilitates intercellular adhesion through adherens junction [18,34]. Furthermore, after 29 days of culture with GA-002 and GA-017, human primary CECs retained their canonical morphology with regular polygonal patterning and did not exhibit fibroblastic phenotypes associated with endothelial-to-mesenchymal transition (Fig. 5C). LATS inhibitors promote YAP activation and nuclear translocation in human primary CECs

Finally, we investigated the effects of GA-002 and GA-017 on YAP nuclear localization and activation in human primary CECs. Immunofluorescence microscopy revealed that GA-002– and GA-017–treated cells exhibited increased nuclear localization of YAP compared with DMSO-treated controls on day 29 (Fig. 6A). Consistent with these findings, the expression levels of YAP-regulated genes—CYR61, ANKRD1, CTGF, and CCND1—were more strongly upregulated in GA-002– and GA-017–treated cells than in controls (Fig. 6B). Furthermore, GA-002 and GA-017 promoted YAP nuclear translocation more effectively at high cell density (>90 % confluence) than at low cell density (<20 % confluence) (Fig. 6C). Discussion

Here, we report a cell culture approach for the expansion of human CECs through YAP activation by inhibition of LATS kinase. Our findings indicate that small-molecule LATS inhibitors sufficiently expand human CECs while preserving key functional characteristics, including the expression of established CEC markers—ATP1A1, ZO-1, and N-cadherin—and maintenance of a polygonal cell morphology. These functional characteristics are essential in cell grafts intended for the treatment of corneal endothelial disorders. In recent years, various strategies have been explored for ex vivo expansion of human CECs. These include optimization of culture media [35], as well as treatments with l-ascorbic acid 2-phosphate [36], basic fibroblast growth factor [36,37], and the selective ROCK inhibitor Y-27632 [27,28]. Other efforts involve extracellular matrix coatings, such as collagen, fibronectin, and laminin [38], or genetic modification using tools such as SV40 T-antigen [39,40]. Despite these efforts, it remains challenging to consistently expand CECs without compromising morphology and function. For example, CECs treated with basic fibroblast growth factor often undergo epithelial-mesenchymal transition, characterized by fibroblastic transformation and the loss of tight junctions and barrier function [37,41]. In contrast, our approach using LATS inhibitor–mediated YAP activation enables efficient expansion of human CECs while preserving their healthy morphology and functional phenotype. This result is consistent with the previous finding that exogenous YAP expression enhances human CEC proliferation while maintaining hexagonal morphology without induction of epithelial-mesenchymal transition [42].

YAP plays pivotal roles in regulating cytoskeletal organization, cell polarity, and contact-mediated inhibition of cell proliferation in mammalian cells [30,31]. We have previously demonstrated that LATS inhibitors stimulate mammalian cell proliferation, particularly in 3D cultures, by activating YAP [25]. However, the effects of LATS inhibitors on endothelial cells under 2D conditions had not been fully explored. In the present study, we showed that 10.13039/501100017248LATS inhibitors promote CEC proliferation in both 2D and 3D cultures, suggesting that YAP activation supports the ex vivo expansion of multiple endothelial cell types across different culture platforms. Among the YAP-regulated processes, contact inhibition appears particularly important for CEC proliferation. In support of this speculation, our data showed that 10.13039/501100017248LATS inhibitors failed to increase cell number under low-density culture conditions, where contact inhibition is minimal. Although suppression of contact inhibition presumably underlies the YAP-mediated enhancement of CEC proliferation, further investigation is necessary to reveal the precise mechanisms involved.

Ex vivo expansion and transplantation of CECs are expected to increase the availability of transplantable grafts for the treatment of corneal endothelial disorders, including bullous keratopathy [18]. Thus far, the primary source of CECs for ex vivo expansion has been cadaveric donor corneal endothelium. However, the identification of alternative sources could substantially improve cell availability for transplantation. Intriguingly, skin-derived precursors [43,44] and mesenchymal stem cells [45] have been successfully differentiated into CECs. Furthermore, induced pluripotent stem cell technology represents an innovative and versatile approach for generating CECs, with potential applicability across all corneal cell types [[46], [47], [48], [49], [50], [51]]. LATS inhibitors may prove useful in improving the efficiency of CEC derivation from diverse cell sources, enabling large-scale production of functional CECs to be explored in future studies.

Currently, there is no approved pharmaceutical agent for treating corneal endothelial dysfunction. Available options are limited to symptomatic management and corneal transplantation. Although effective, transplantation requires long-term corticosteroid therapy to prevent graft rejection and is therefore reserved for patients with advanced visual impairment. Recently, the ROCK inhibitor Y-27632 has emerged as a promising candidate for treating corneal endothelial dysfunction [52]. Other small molecules, such as nicotinamide, have been reported to facilitate corneal endothelial wound healing by promoting proliferation, indicating potential for drug development [53]. Additionally, fibroblast growth factor 1 derivatives have shown efficacy in inducing CEC proliferation; one such derivative, TTHX1114, is currently in proof-of-concept clinical trials for the treatment of endothelial dysfunction [54]. Consistent with these therapeutic approaches, small-molecule modulators of the Hippo pathway, including LATS inhibitors, may be developed as corneal endothelium-targeting regenerative agents. This approach may be advantageous because Hippo pathway modulators clearly promote CEC proliferation, as demonstrated in this study.

Conclusions

Our findings demonstrate that small-molecule–mediated activation of YAP significantly enhances the expansion of human CECs in 2D culture. This technology provides a promising platform to supply CECs for cell-based therapies and regenerative medicine, as well as a method to develop pharmaceutical agents for corneal endothelial dysfunction. Although LATS inhibitors exhibit beneficial effects on human CEC expansion, their efficacy on corneal endothelium in vivo has not yet been validated. It is necessary to confirm their safety and efficacy in large mammals before proceeding with clinical trials.
