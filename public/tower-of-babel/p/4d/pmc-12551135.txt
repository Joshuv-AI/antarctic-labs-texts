Bridging the preparedness gap: a systematic review of recommended stockpile items for radiological and nuclear emergencies
Authors: Matteo Nocci, Michelangelo Bortolin, Attila J Hertelendy, Eman Al Shaikh, Terri Davis, Marco Serale, Luca Ragazzoni, Fadi Issa, Stefano Romagnoli, David DiGregorio, Jeffrey M Franc, Janice Y Kung, Amalia Voskanyan, Gregory Ciottone
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWhile rare, radiological and nuclear (RN) emergencies pose complex challenges that require tailored preparedness strategies. A key aspect is the strategic stockpiling of medical countermeasures (MCMs), yet existing literature offers limited and fragmented recommendations regarding their appropriate composition.MethodsThis systematic review, conducted following PRISMA guidelines, aims to consolidate and critically appraise available evidence on stockpiling for RN emergencies. Seven databases and selected grey literature sources were screened from 2011 onward. Studies were included if they provided direct or indirect recommendations on stockpiling items. Data extraction was conducted in two steps by independent pairs of reviewers, and identified items were categorized as therapeutics, medical devices, personal protective equipment (PPE), or RN-specific equipment. Items were further analyzed for regulatory status, administration route, shelf-life, and storage requirements.ResultsThirty-two articles met the inclusion criteria. Therapeutics dominated the findings, with 50 distinct agents identified, most frequently potassium iodide, Prussian Blue, Ca-/Zn-DTPA, and hematopoietic growth factors such as filgrastim. In contrast, medical devices were not supported by a sufficient level of stockpiling recommendation, while PPE (6 of 32 articles) and RN equipment (14 of 32 articles) were cited less often. Pediatric considerations were rarely addressed, with 2 studies explicitly focused on this population. Gaps in operational guidance, regulatory harmonization, and standardized planning emerged across the literature.ConclusionsThis review highlights that stockpiling recommendations for RN emergencies remain limited, with greater guidance concentrated on a few therapeutics and little direction for other items. The findings provide an informative evidence base to support more consistent policy and preparedness planning.Keywords: Medical stockpiling, Nuclear threats, Radiation exposure, Radiological emergencies

Background

While infrequent, radiological and nuclear (RN) emergencies pose a significant threat to public health and emergency response systems due to their complexity and widespread consequences, which require specialized preparedness.

These events can stem from unintentional incidents like nuclear power plant (NPP) accidents or radiation overexposures in nuclear medicine. More concerning are intentional threats associated with terrorism, including radiological exposure devices (REDs), radiological dispersal devices (RDDs or “dirty bombs”), and improvised nuclear devices (INDs), each capable of causing large-scale disruption. The risks of nuclear proliferation and misuse of radioactive materials further highlight the existing threats [1–4].

The hazards associated with exposure and overexposure to ionizing radiation include immediate and long-term health effects and can impact many individuals quickly. Among the acute consequences, acute radiation syndrome (ARS) [5, 6] is a primary clinical concern, particularly due to its significant impact on the hematopoietic system. Additionally, delayed effects may also arise [7]. Both scenarios can severely strain healthcare systems, even the most advanced ones.

Preparedness for such events, therefore, represents a critical component in mitigating the consequences of RN emergencies. The complexity inherent in these scenarios requires a comprehensive preparedness strategy, integrating rigorous risk assessment and advanced operational readiness of healthcare systems [8, 9].

As with many other disaster scenarios, the strategic stockpiling of medical countermeasures (MCMs) is a central pillar of RN emergency preparedness [10–14]. Stockpiles serve dual purposes: providing quick access to essential resources during surge events and ensuring the availability of specialized items often missing from standard clinical practice. This is crucial in RN emergencies, where many countermeasures are highly specific and not routinely used in conventional healthcare. Stockpile compositions include four categories: therapeutics, medical devices, personal protective equipment (PPE), and RN threat-specific response equipment [15–17].

Despite the critical role of strategic stockpiles in radiological and nuclear emergency preparedness, ongoing challenges in their building, maintenance, and optimization might leave states and healthcare organizations without adequate support during such crises. Notably, limited evidence exists on which items should be included: a key insight for policymakers and stakeholders, enhancing preparedness events [17–20].

The aim of this study was to systematically review the literature, including grey literature, to identify items directly or indirectly recommended for stockpiling in RN preparedness.

Methods

Study design

The Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) Statement standards guided this systematic review’s reporting.

Participants

The systematic review is promoted and coordinated by a team of experts affiliated with leading university centers in disaster medicine, specifically CRIMEDIM - Interdepartmental Research and Training Center in Disaster Medicine, Humanitarian Aid, and Global Health located in Novara, Italy (hereinafter referred to as Center 1) and the BIDMC Fellowship in Disaster Medicine - Boston, USA (hereinafter referred to as Center 2).

Search strategy

In collaboration with the research team, the medical librarian developed and executed comprehensive searches in Ovid MEDLINE, Ovid Embase, APA PsycInfo (Ovid), CINAHL, Scopus, Web of Science Core Collection, and Cochrane Library (via Wiley) in May 2024. Relevant keywords and controlled vocabulary were carefully selected to capture all relevant literature about stockpiling supplies for radiological and nuclear events.

In addition to subscription databases, the research team reviewed the first 200 results from Google Scholar for inclusion, which has been demonstrated to be a reasonable number of results to screen since there is a high overlap between Web of Science and Google Scholar [21]. Bibliographies from the included studies were also reviewed. The searches were restricted to English-language publications and those published from 2011 onward. After the extraction phase, duplicates were removed, and the unique results were entered into a web-based tool (Covidence, http://www.covidence.org) for the initial title and abstract screening. For complete search strategies, refer to Appendix 1.

Selection process

The title, abstract, and full-text screening phases were conducted by different reviewers using Covidence. Titles and abstracts were reviewed based on eligibility criteria for in-depth analysis if (1) both Centre 2 reviewers agreed or (2) the abstract lacked sufficient information. Conflicts among Centre 2 reviewers were resolved by a third reviewer from Centre 1, who decided on publication inclusion. The final selection required at least one item or category to be mentioned for acceptance. Identified studies (included and excluded) were summarized in a PRISMA flow diagram, and data extracted from the selected studies were organized into a database.

Data collection and extraction

Two authors from Center 1 independently gathered and organized relevant data into a structured database implemented as an Excel (Microsoft Corp., Redmond, WA, USA) table (hereinafter “database”). Afterward, experts from Center 2 reviewed the database and addressed any discrepancies. An Excel spreadsheet was created to extract and categorize key characteristics from the articles. Data synthesis and reporting

Because of the heterogeneity in study designs, populations, interventions, and outcomes, most articles being narrative research, data were not pooled for meta-analysis, and no statistical analyses were done. Instead, a qualitative content analysis addressed the study objectives. A formal quality or risk of bias assessment was deemed not suitable given the nature of the included records, which mainly comprised policy documents, technical reports, and narrative reviews.

Results

Systematic review process

Database searches identified 16,090 records. After duplicate removal and title/abstract screening, 165 articles (1.0%) underwent full-text review. Of these, 32 studies (0.20%) met the eligibility criteria and were included in the final database [1–4, 6, 9, 12, 15, 17, 19, 23–43]. The review process is summarized in Fig. 1, and the included studies are detailed in Appendix 2.

General characteristics of selected articles

Systematic reviews/review articles (including narrative ones) were the most frequent (14 articles; 43.8%), followed by original research (6; 18.8%), policy advice (4; 12.5%), and opinion articles/commentaries (4; 12.5%). Comparative analyses and technical reports accounted for 1 article (3.1%), while advisory documents represented 1 article (3.1%).

Regarding the target population, 30 articles (93.8%) addressed a generic population without specifying whether the recommendations targeted adults or pediatrics, while two studies (6.2%) focused explicitly on pediatric populations. Most studies occurred in civilian settings (27 articles; 84.4%), with 5 (15.6%) adopting a civil-military perspective.

All 32 articles (100%) included stockpiling recommendations, with 15 (46.9%) offering direct and 17 (53.1%) indirect indications. Location was determined based on where the study was conducted or the research team’s institutional affiliation relevant to the work’s scope. Studies were classified under global or non-specific contexts without explicit geographic references. Accordingly, 16 articles (50.0%) were associated with the USA, followed by Asia (6; 18.8%), Europe (3; 9.4%), and global/non-specific contexts (7; 21.9%) (Table 1).

Medical devices

The analysis did not identify any medical devices with a sufficient level of specificity to be considered for stockpiling. References found in the literature were limited to very generic items, such as hospital beds or supplies for burn and blast injuries, and were not accompanied by either direct or indirect recommendations for their inclusion in stockpiles. For this reason, no table is presented for this category.

Discussion

This systematic review identified 32 articles out of a total of 16,090 records. Of these, more than 50% include only indirect recommendations, and even when direct recommendations are present, not all items mentioned in the article are explicitly endorsed for stockpiling. Indirect recommendations are typically inferred from identified preparedness gaps rather than clearly stated inclusion criteria [24]. This highlights a certain scarcity of literature explicitly addressing this topic and a fragmented and non-standardized approach across studies. Indeed, while some reviews have investigated medical countermeasures for radiological and nuclear emergencies, they have predominantly focused on identifying relevant substances or treatments, with limited attention to stockpiling strategies.

The frequency of stockpiling recommendations appears influenced by the reference scenario (e.g., potassium iodide in nuclear power plant accidents, Prussian Blue in dispersal device scenarios), introducing a potential bias that contributes to the heterogeneity of findings.

To date, our literature search has found no comparable peer-reviewed studies. Relevant insights on this topic can instead be found in grey literature sources [16, 17].

The predominance of studies from the United States appears closely linked to the country’s robust institutional framework for emergency preparedness, including stockpiling strategies. Agencies such as the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services (HHS) and the Biomedical Advanced Research and Development Authority (BARDA), established in 2006 within the Office of the Assistant Secretary for Preparedness and Response (ASPR), have played a pivotal role in advancing stockpiling capabilities for radiological events. Legislative instruments such as the Project BioShield Act (2004) have supported these efforts [1, 42–44]. Articles from other parts of the world remain relatively limited, potentially due to the more recent introduction of stockpiling strategies at the international level, as in the case of the European Commission’s rescEU program in Europe [45, 46], or because of their confinement to the national level.

From a functional perspective, the analysis confirmed the centrality of therapeutics in RN preparedness. These were the most frequently represented items, underscoring a critical role in the clinical management of radiation exposure [35]. In contrast, medical devices and PPE were cited far less frequently and seldom with direct stockpiling recommendations, suggesting gaps in the literature or a lower degree of integration into preparedness frameworks. Most articles referred to a generic or adult population, with a relatively small proportion explicitly addressing the pediatric population. This may indicate a gap and highlight the need to ensure equitable access to MCMs across population groups during radiological emergencies [17].

Stockpiling rationales like prioritization criteria, stockpiling level, and logistical elements are rarely addressed systematically. This limits the application of these recommendations and highlights a gap between scientific evidence and public health planning.

Therapeutics

Therapeutics stood out as the most commonly referenced category in the literature reviewed, appearing 182 times across 28 of the 32 articles (87.5%). The frequency of citations and the existence of direct stockpiling recommendations for specific agents bolster their inclusion in medical reserves (Table 2). However, around 50% of therapeutic items were mentioned only once, and none received clear stockpiling recommendations. This indicates a landscape driven more by exploratory or speculative interest than solid operational guidance. Such a trend may stem from the diverse nature of the sources, most of which lack a policy focus and the strict inclusion criteria applied in this review. Furthermore, limited or changing evidence for specific agents might hinder their formal recommendation for stockpiling.

Thyroid blocking agents

Potassium iodide (KI), the most frequently referenced therapeutic agent, is widely acknowledged for its efficacy in saturating the thyroid gland with stable iodine, thereby mitigating the risk of thyroid cancer following radioactive iodine exposure [47]. KI is particularly prioritized for administration in infants, children, pregnant or breastfeeding women, and adults under 40 [37, 48]. Stockpiling is strongly recommended in pediatric (65 mg) and adult (135 mg) dosages [37, 40, 49]. Nonetheless, the reviewed literature provides limited evidence regarding predistribution strategies or threshold-based release criteria [23]. While several guidelines emphasize the importance of ensuring local availability in high-risk areas [25, 29], national implementation strategies remain inconsistent. These recommendations align with other key publications [16, 50], although they predominantly focus on nuclear power plant accident scenarios and rarely address incidents involving limited iodine-131 release [2, 4, 23, 25, 26, 40, 51]. For this item, recommendations for inclusion in stockpiles are explicit, although a lower degree of evidence supports the level at which stockpiling should occur.

Decorporation agents

Chelating agents such as calcium-DTPA and zinc-DTPA are primarily employed to manage internal contamination by transuranic elements and rare earth metals. These compounds are typically reserved for post-exposure administration following confirmed internal contamination [3], and they exhibit no efficacy in uranium decorporation. Intravenous administration may hinder their usability in field conditions for mass exposures; however, alternative routes, including oral formulations, are currently under investigation [52]. Their inclusion in national stockpiles is frequently justified based on scenario-based planning for radiological dispersal devices (RDDs) and the lack of viable alternatives during acute response phases [16, 17, 42, 51, 53]. The literature review does not reveal significant differences between calcium-DTPA and zinc-DTPA, though the latter may be preferentially indicated for pediatric use.

Prussian Blue is a decorporation agent that promotes fecal excretion of cesium-137 and thallium. Its stockpile inclusion is strongly supported in scenarios involving RDDs and nuclear fallout with cesium contamination [42, 53]. A key advantage of Prussian Blue is its potential for administration before dosimetric confirmation, thereby enabling early intervention, an approach generally recommended for all agents within the decorporation category. The recommendation is underpinned by their widespread regulatory authorization and the absence of special storage requirements.

Hematopoietic growth factors and cytokines

These agents are used in the management of Acute Radiation Syndrome (ARS). Filgrastim (G-CSF) was the most frequently cited, followed by Pegfilgrastim, Sargramostim (GM-CSF), and Romiplostim. Their early administration significantly enhances survival outcomes and accelerates hematologic recovery, even when precise dosimetry is unavailable [54–57]. These agents are routinely used in healthcare, supporting their incorporation into medical stockpiles. However, cold-chain storage and subcutaneous administration may pose challenges.

Agents such as IL-3, IL-6, and IL-11 were mentioned less frequently and lacked robust clinical data, thus limiting their strategic relevance. The selection of cytokines or therapeutic agents often relies on pre-clinical studies rather than high-quality clinical evidence, highlighting a gap in empirical validation [35]. Infrequent citations and limited evidence strength provide weak support for the stockpiling of interleukin.

Erythropoiesis-stimulating agents such as EPO were marginally cited and are only weakly recommended in radiological settings [17, 27, 54].

Radioprotectors

Among radioprotectors, Amifostine has been cited in four different articles. Although generally approved for radiation-induced xerostomia (e.g., FDA), it is not authorized for the treatment of Acute Radiation Syndrome (ARS). Its mechanism of action is based on prophylactic DNA protection [58]. In addition to the absence of extensive recommendations, logistical constraints, such as the requirement for refrigeration and intravenous administration, limit its applicability in field settings [28, 53, 59, 60].

N-acetylcysteine (NAC) and other antioxidant agents were infrequently mentioned. Despite promising animal data [12, 61], their safety profiles and regulatory status remain uncertain [19, 62, 63], making their inclusion in national stockpiles unlikely. Similarly, tocotrienols, including Gamma-Tocotrienol (GT3) and tocopherols, showed preclinical promise [28, 64–66], but their high-dose toxicity, poor bioavailability, and lack of approval prevent their inclusion in structured stockpiling policies.

Other chelating agents such as dimercaprol, succimer, and CaNa₂EDTA are primarily indicated for treating heavy metal poisoning. The reviewed literature reports only a minimal number of articles mentioning these agents, and in all cases, their relevance to radiological stockpiling is indirect or absent [2, 9, 19, 49, 67].

Supportive care agents, mainly categorized under “Other supportive care/generic” in this review, were cited less frequently but remain essential for the symptomatic management of Acute Radiation Syndrome (ARS) and radiation-induced complications [17, 37, 40, 42]. The reviewed studies highlight various agents, including antiemetics (ondansetron, granisetron), fluid and electrolyte support, and symptom-directed treatments such as loperamide, pyridoxine, diazepam, and dextrose 50% [9, 17, 19, 32]. Antiemetics are the most common subcategory reported in multiple sources, warranting consideration for stockpile inclusion. Integrating them into strategic stockpiles might be indicated within a broader preparedness framework.

The review also identified references to blood components in two articles [35, 37], primarily for treating complications from ionizing radiation exposure. These products may fall within the scope of broader stockpiling strategies but require specific considerations due to their nature, particularly concerning regulatory requirements, storage conditions, short shelf life, and administration protocols. Unlike stable therapeutics, blood components cannot be stockpiled in the conventional sense; preparedness strategies instead rely on mechanisms such as rotational use within clinical services, regional blood banks, and surge agreements to ensure availability in emergencies. This review does not examine these aspects in detail.

Finally, the Experimental/Under Investigation Drugs category includes many items cited only once [27, 28, 35]. For example, Entolimod, ON01210, Ex-RAD, and MPCs show potential for radioprotection or regeneration but lack clinical approval and have limited data on efficacy and scalability. Due to low citation frequency, lack of regulatory requirements, and limited evidence, valid stockpiling recommendations cannot be made. Although not examined in detail, some may eventually gain sufficient evidence or regulatory approval to justify inclusion in stockpiles.

Medical devices

No medical devices with sufficient specificity were identified in the included studies. References were limited to generic items, such as hospital beds and supplies for the treatment of trauma-related injuries, including burns and blast wounds, as well as unspecified dressings, and were not accompanied by direct or indirect recommendations for inclusion in stockpiles. The scope of the search strategy may partially explain this limited representation. However, it likely underestimates the clinical relevance of managing complex injury patterns in the aftermath of a nuclear detonation. In such scenarios, radiation-induced harm often coexists with other severe injuries, including those from blunt trauma, thermal burns, and blast waves. Notably, ionizing radiation represents only a fraction of the total energy released, estimated at 15%, whereas mechanical and thermal energy account for the majority, approximately 50 and 35%, respectively [75].

Given this, immediate medical care should prioritize the stabilization of life-threatening injuries, particularly those caused by trauma or burns, which are often the first and most critical conditions requiring intervention [76]. Radiation exposure injuries are inherently complex, and when associated with a nuclear detonation, they become even more challenging due to the presence of concurrent trauma, thermal, and blast-related damage [43]. It is evident that radiological emergencies will necessitate increased access to basic medical devices to expand surge capacity; however, this point is not reflected in the present review. In addition, international recommendations explicitly highlight the need to stock supplies for sample collection and bioassays, such as cotton-tipped applicators for nasal swabs, containers for urine or feces samples, gauze for blood samples from wounds, and shielding devices for radioactive shrapnel, which are essential to radiological triage and internal contamination assessment [16].

Personal protective equipment

Although the systematic review revealed limited recommendations regarding stockpiling personal protective equipment (PPE), with only one evidence-based systematic review offering explicit guidance [15], PPE is significantly addressed in both peer-reviewed and grey literature. Multiple bodies, including the IAEA and the U.S. Nuclear Regulatory Commission, have long emphasized the critical role of PPE in radiological emergency preparedness [15, 77].

The items most frequently cited across the reviewed articles were protective suits and coveralls, followed by gloves, respiratory protection, and foot coverings. Protective suits appeared in several sources [2, 15, 35], explicitly mentioning external shielding materials such as lead and tungsten [33, 78]. Gloves were reported in various forms, including surgical and multi-type gloves [15, 32, 41]. Respiratory protection was cited consistently, including masks and respirators such as N95 types [2, 15, 17, 24, 42, 43]. Foot protection, including boots and shoe covers, was also described in multiple articles [2, 15, 43]. Although cited less frequently, additional items included head, ear, and eye protection [2, 15, 26].

PPE categories reported in the literature align with IAEA recommendations: body (suits, aprons), hand (gloves), foot (boots, covers), respiratory (various masks), eye/face (goggles, shields), and personal dosimeters. Some studies also mention undergarments, vests, and alarm-equipped whole-body dosimeters, reflecting a broad view of barrier protection in radiological response frameworks [15, 33, 79, 80].

Although most indications for PPE stockpiling were indirect, the review did highlight specific scenarios where increased quantities were necessary, thus providing indirect justification for stockpiling. This was particularly evident in settings involving large numbers of responders or healthcare personnel. For instance, Millard et al. [81] reported protocols requiring up to four pairs of gloves for workers at higher risk of exposure. Additional recommendations emphasized frequently replacing gloves, gowns, and masks to minimize radioactive particle retention and prevent patient cross-contamination [2, 82].

Considerations surrounding PPE stockpiling extend beyond material characteristics. Crucially, the usability and familiarity of these items among healthcare workers significantly influence operational effectiveness. A lack of confidence in using PPE or fear of exposure may reduce healthcare personnel’s willingness to provide care during radiological emergencies. In response to these challenges, recent innovations have introduced garments that offer enhanced radiation protection with substantially lighter materials. However, these products are not yet widely available in hospitals and emergency settings and may require adequate training to be considered for stockpiling [15]. Raising awareness about decontamination protocols and ensuring the availability of these garments may help reduce psychological barriers and promote responder engagement [2].

The findings of this review support the inclusion of PPE among stockpiled countermeasures, and the categories identified appear consistent with those reported in Table 3.

Radiological and nuclear equipment

Only a few direct recommendations regarding the stockpiling of radiation-specific devices were identified. Nonetheless, several studies indirectly support strategic preparedness, highlighting the importance of early identification of exposed individuals to enable timely decorporation therapy and medical triage, preferably within 24–48 hours of intake [41, 83, 84].

Radiation detection instruments were frequently cited, with handheld devices such as Geiger counters, Personal Radiation Detectors (PRDs), Radioisotope Identification Devices (RIIDs), Radiation Portal Monitors (RPMs), and other survey meters valued for their portability, cost-efficiency, and usability in the field. These tools support rapid triage and targeted medical interventions, potentially reducing the demand for pharmaceutical countermeasures [2–4, 9, 25, 34, 41, 42]. Recent advancements include advanced personal isotope identification devices, which detect and identify specific isotopes, expediting treatment decisions [85]. Their presence near incident sites is critical in beta/gamma scenarios [40], and international guidelines endorse specific types, including Geiger–Müller, sodium iodide meters, and radionuclide spectrometers [16].

However, these tools lack the precision for dose estimation, prompting the need for advanced systems such as shielded gamma spectrometers, whole-body counters, and confirmatory laboratory methods [25, 41–43, 43]. Despite their relevance, such systems face logistical barriers and are not linked to explicit or indirect stockpiling recommendations.

Biodosimetry techniques were also prominent. Point-of-care devices, like blood analyzers for Neutrophil-to-Lymphocyte Ratio (NLR) and biomarker platforms (PCR, immunoassays), enable dose-based triage without requiring highly specialized personnel [42, 43]. While laboratory-based high-throughput assays offer greater accuracy, their stockpiling remains unaddressed. Nonetheless, ease of use and field adaptability support their potential inclusion [1, 15, 17, 42].

Decontamination equipment, though less frequently discussed, was identified as critical for early-phase response. Systems such as showers, mobile units, and personal decontamination facilities help prevent internal contamination and cross-exposure [9, 31, 32, 39]. International sources recommend additional materials (e.g., soap, saline, sponges, and clean clothing) for effective deployment [16].

Although explicit stockpiling guidance remains scarce, the evidence supports prioritizing rapidly deployable, field-compatible equipment. Operational needs, available training, and the anticipated emergency scale should guide their inclusion. Stockpiling strategies must also consider practical challenges, including the risk of data misinterpretation, unit confusion, and the need for user training [1].

Stockpiling level recommendations

Recommendations regarding appropriate stockpiling levels (e.g., local, national, or international) were generally scarce or absent. However, according to several studies, indirect insights into stock location can be inferred from the necessity of ensuring rapid distribution, as in the case of potassium iodide, which must be delivered within a critical timeframe ranging from two to twenty-four hours post-exposure [39, 82]. This time sensitivity requires local or pre-positioned stockpiles, with inventory levels aligned to contextual risks and operational needs. Determinants include proximity to nuclear plants, site-specific risk assessments, and hospital designation as ARS treatment centers. These cases demand targeted pharmaceutical stocks and trained personnel, highlighting that stockpiling necessitates parallel expertise across pharmacy, logistics, regulation, and emergency response to ensure effective management and deployment.

Item selection

Multiple studies provide insights into choosing items, emphasizing that medical countermeasures should possess broad applicability, an extended shelf life (preferably at room temperature), and manageable storage and cold chain requirements, especially in inconsistent electricity or refrigeration risk. The fragility of supply chains and the potential for shortages also require organized risk assessments [3, 40].

Preferred administration routes, rather than intravenous, require minimal clinical oversight: oral, subcutaneous, transdermal, inhaled, or intramuscular. For pediatric or elderly populations, liquid formulations or compounding instructions may be needed when available [1]. Administration routes and radionuclide specificity may limit the applicability of some agents in mass casualty scenarios [1, 86]. Therefore, stockpiling strategies often prioritize formulations optimized for rapid and simplified deployment, including oral, transdermal, and inhaled products.

The definition of the stockpile must be risk-based, considering the likelihood of high-radiation exposure scenarios that trigger acute symptoms and necessitate accelerated therapeutic intervention [56], such as near NPPs. While immediate needs (e.g., ARS) should be prioritized [5, 6], both qualitative and quantitative planning must also address Delayed Effects of Acute Radiation Exposure (DEARE)—a condition marked by progressive multi-organ dysfunction manifesting weeks to months post-insult. DEARE includes pulmonary, renal, cardiovascular, and gastrointestinal damage requiring prolonged care [1, 7, 87].

Stockpile size

This review did not identify direct recommendations on stockpile size but highlighted key guiding elements. According to WHO, “the national and regional risk profile, potential scenarios, and the scale of a potential emergency define the scope and the size of the national stockpile” [17]. Additionally, stockpile dimensions are shaped by operational frameworks: the estimated number of casualties, injury types requiring urgent versus prolonged treatment, dosing schedules (e.g., potassium iodide and DTPA require ongoing administration), and expected treatment duration [17, 30, 37].

Treatment duration guidance is not uniformly available for all agents; WHO (2007) generally recommends 10–12 days, whereas therapy may extend up to 30 days [3] in cases like Prussian Blue. The extent of stockpiles should also reflect potential exposure to high levels of radionuclides, which prompt faster onset of symptoms and an increased need for rapid therapeutic intervention [88], particularly near NPPs.

The quantity of various stockpiled items is interrelated; in the case of therapeutics, limited measurement capabilities (e.g., biodosimetry devices) may create bottlenecks and necessitate increased empirical drug administration.

Pediatric considerations

Special considerations must be dedicated to pediatric populations, particularly regarding dosage and formulation [37, 49, 89–91]. Children exhibit heightened vulnerability to radiation due to smaller body mass relative to organ size, higher surface area-to-volume ratios, elevated minute ventilation, and more rapid cellular division and growth rate factors that increase the risk of radiation-induced injury and skin burns [49, 92].

Despite this, pediatric-specific stockpiling considerations remain limited in most of the literature. Several articles highlight the need for adapted dosing and formulation [49], and this review confirms the existence of significant gaps in pediatric preparedness strategies.

Drug authorization considerations

A major challenge in defining stockpiles for radiological and nuclear emergencies lies in the heterogeneity of regulatory systems. While centralized frameworks exist in the United States and the European Union, many countries rely on national regulations, causing inconsistencies. Nevertheless, potassium iodide, Prussian Blue, and Ca/Zn-DTPA are generally authorized across the countries examined. [28, 65], indicating a link between regulatory alignment and inclusion in national stockpiles.

Regulatory status can shape stockpile composition more than clinical necessity. Absence of approval may hinder integration into preparedness frameworks, particularly under FDA or EU regulation, where unlicensed or restricted agents are unlikely to be stockpiled, even with proven utility in radiological emergencies [38, 42, 43].

Moreover, the role of off-label medications, such as hematopoietic growth factors (e.g., filgrastim, sargramostim, romiplostim), is emerging, as these are approved for other clinical uses but may be repurposed for radiation injury in emergency stockpiles. Despite minimal real-world use, some agents have been included primarily based on regulatory status, suggesting that regulatory frameworks sometimes override operational data in preparedness planning [35].

Findings indicate that dedicated regulatory pathways, such as emergency use authorizations, could facilitate timely access to countermeasures. Enhanced collaboration with disaster medicine experts may improve preparedness planning [26]. Furthermore, proactively addressing regulatory requirements during early-stage research may accelerate the translational readiness of medical countermeasures [1], facilitating their integration into national preparedness strategies.

Limitations

First, while inclusion criteria were based on both direct and indirect recommendations for stockpiling, the identification of indirect recommendations may have introduced a degree of subjectivity. Although predefined definitions and the involvement of a third reviewer in case of disagreement minimized this limitation, a residual risk of interpretive bias cannot be excluded. Second, the nature of this topic inherently lends itself to the inclusion of technical documents, some of which may not have been fully captured despite deliberate efforts to incorporate grey literature. Third, this review did not include a formal risk of bias assessment, which may represent a limitation.

Conclusions

This systematic review provided a thorough and targeted analysis of medical countermeasures stockpiling strategies for radiological and nuclear emergencies, focusing specifically on item selection. It highlights a consistent pattern: direct recommendations were scarce and mainly limited to a few therapeutics, while guidance for most other items was minimal or implied. Key factors affecting stockpiling decisions include availability, regulatory approval, and the robustness of scientific evidence. By organizing findings around these operational criteria, this study delivers a practical framework that can strengthen decision-making and improve the design of strategic stockpiles across a broad range of preparedness and response systems.
