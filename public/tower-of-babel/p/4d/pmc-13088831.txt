Prevalence of hypertension among adults in Nigeria: a systematic review and meta-analysis

Published: 2026

Background

Hypertension is one of the leading global risk factors for cardiovascular disease (CVD), stroke, myocardial infarction, heart failure, renal impairment, and premature mortality. In 2019, an estimated 1.3 billion adults worldwide were living with hypertension, nearly half of whom were unaware of their condition, and fewer than one in five achieved adequate control [1, 2]. Although once considered a predominantly high-income country condition, hypertension now disproportionately affects low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), which account for more than two-thirds of hypertension-related deaths [3]. Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) bears a substantial share of this burden, with prevalence estimates ranging from 20% to 40%, and Nigeria – Africa’s most populous nation—contributes markedly to the regional total [4–8].

Hypertension and its complications impose substantial economic burdens on individuals, families, health systems, and national economies. Affected individuals face direct medical expenses and other economic loss, often during their most productive years, which can push families into poverty. Managing complications such as myocardial infarction and strokes caused by uncontrolled hypertension is costly for health care systems. At the national level, economies suffer from reduced productivity, loss of tax revenue, higher health care expenditures, and greater social support demands for adults disabled by cardiovascular events and children left without caregivers. Estimates suggest that the economic returns from strengthening hypertension control programs outweigh their costs by approximately18 to 1 [1].

In Nigeria, the burden of hypertension has risen steadily over the past three decades, paralleling rapid urbanization, dietary shifts, and declining physical activity [9–11]. Hypertension is no longer confined to urban populations; recent evidence indicates comparable occurrence in rural communities, diminishing the historical rural advantage linked to traditional lifestyles and lower salt intake [12, 13]. However, reported prevalence estimates in Nigeria vary widely – from approximately 10% in earlier studies to over 50% in more recent surveys – reflecting substantial methodological heterogeneity [4, 14]. Differences in diagnostic thresholds, age distributions, sampling frames, blood pressure measurement protocols, and study settings have contributed to this variability. This fragmentation limits comparability across studies and impedes the derivation of a reliable national estimate to guide policy and resource allocation.

Several systematic reviews have previously attempted to synthesize evidence on the prevalence of hypertension in Nigeria [4, 15]. Nevertheless, these reviews are constrained by earlier timeframes, limited subgroup analyses, and insufficient exploration of between-study heterogeneity. Since their publication, Nigeria has undergone rapid epidemiological and sociodemographic transitions, including rising obesity prevalence [16], increasing life expectancy [17], and expanding urbanization [18], all of which may have altered the national hypertension profile. An updated synthesis incorporating recent evidence and enhanced analytical approaches is therefore warranted.

Moreover, prior reviews have provided limited disaggregation by sex, residential setting, and geopolitical zones. Emerging evidence suggests evolving sex differentials potentially driven by shared lifestyle exposures and healthcare access patterns [4, 10, 11]. Regional disparities across Nigeria’s geopolitical zones – likely influenced by differences in urbanization, socioeconomic conditions, dietary practices, and healthcare infrastructure – also remain insufficiently characterized [4, 19, 20]. A comprehensive analysis that systematically evaluates these subgroup patterns is essential for more context-sensitive interpretation of the burden.

Therefore, this systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to estimate the pooled prevalence of hypertension among adults in Nigeria using standardized diagnostic criteria and to quantify between-study heterogeneity. We further examined subgroup differences by sex, residential setting, and geopolitical zone to assess potential sources of variation. By synthesizing evidence published between January 2015 and September 2025 using random-effects modelling and robust heterogeneity metrics (I², τ², and 95% prediction intervals), this study provides an updated and methodologically rigorous national estimate of hypertension burden in Nigeria.

Methods

The study was designed, conducted, and reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 statement [21]. It applies the Population–Intervention–Comparison–Outcomes (PICO) framework to systematically evaluate studies on hypertension among adults in Nigeria (Fig. 1). This review was not prospectively registered. However, all objectives, eligibility criteria, and analytical procedures were defined a priori before data extraction and analysis.

Data sources and search strategy

A comprehensive search of the literature was conducted across seven major electronic databases – MEDLINE (via Ovid), EMBASE, Web of Science, Scopus, CINAHL, PsychInfo, and African Journals Online (AJOL) – from January, 2015 to September 30, 2025. Studies published between this timeframe were included to provide a contemporary estimate of hypertension prevalence reflecting recent epidemiological and sociodemographic transitions in Nigeria. This temporal restriction also minimizes overlap with earlier systematic reviews and enhances comparability under consistent diagnostic thresholds. The search strategy combined controlled vocabulary (MeSH terms) and free-text keywords related to hypertension (e.g., “hypertension,” “high blood pressure”, HTN), Nigeria, and epidemiological study designs (e.g., “survey,” “cross-sectional,” “cohort”) (see Supplementary file 1). Boolean operators “AND” and “OR” were used to combine terms appropriately. Grey literature sources, including Google Scholar, ResearchGate, Google, conference proceedings, dissertations, and preprints from medRxiv, were also reviewed to minimize publication bias. The reference lists of all included studies and relevant reviews were manually screened for additional eligible studies.

Study selection and data extraction

All identified records from databases were imported into EndNote 21 for deduplication, and the unique records were uploaded into Rayyan for screening and data management. Titles and abstracts were screened independently by two reviewers (NM and MW), followed by full-text assessment of potentially relevant studies. Any disagreements during selection were resolved by discussion or adjudication by a third reviewer. A standardized data extraction template was developed in Microsoft Excel and pilot-tested to ensure consistency. Extracted data included the first author’s name, year of publication, study design, sampling method, geopolitical zone, setting (urban or rural), sample size, age range or mean age, sex distribution, hypertension definition, blood pressure measurement method, number of hypertensive cases, and total participants. When relevant data were missing, attempts were made to contact corresponding authors for clarification.

Eligibility criteria

Studies were included if they involved adult participants aged 18 years and above residing in Nigeria, adopted either a cross-sectional or cohort design, and reported measured blood pressure or hypertension status based on standard diagnostic thresholds. Eligible studies were those that provided sufficient data to compute the burden of hypertension. Hypertension was defined as systolic blood pressure (SBP) ≥ 140 mmHg and/or diastolic blood pressure (DBP) ≥ 90 mmHg or self-reported use of antihypertensive medication, in accordance with WHO guidelines [22]. For studies applying alternative diagnostic thresholds (e.g., ≥ 130/80 mmHg following the 2017 ACC/AHA guideline) [23], prevalence estimates corresponding to the ≥ 140/90 mmHg definition were extracted where available. Where recalculation was not feasible, such studies were included but examined in sensitivity analyses to assess their influence on pooled prevalence estimates. Articles published in English were eligible, while those restricted to children, clinical populations, or special subgroups, as well as reviews, commentaries, and studies without extractable data, were excluded. In cases where multiple publications reported on the same population, the most comprehensive or recent version was used to avoid duplication.

Quality assessment of included studies

The methodological quality of the included studies was evaluated using a validated cross-sectional adaptation of the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS) [14, 24]. This tool assesses three domains: selection of participants, comparability of study groups, and outcome assessment. Each study that fulfilled a criterion was awarded one point, while no point was given if the criterion was not met. Each study was scored out of a maximum of nine points, with scores of 7–9 considered low risk of bias, 5–6 moderate risk, and 0–4 high risk. Studies were classified as high quality if they scored at least 7 out of 9 points, moderate quality if they scored 5–6 points, and low quality if they scored below 5 points (Table 1). The quality rating did not determine inclusion in our systematic review or meta-analysis but served to assess the robustness of available evidence. Overall, 14 of the included studies were of moderate quality while 18 were rated high quality, with a mean NOS score of 7.78 ± 0.79. Common strengths across studies included representative sampling, use of standardized blood pressure measurement protocols, and clear diagnostic criteria. However, several studies did not provide adequate details on non-response rates, calibration of instruments, or adjustment for potential confounders such as age, sex, and body mass index. These limitations may contribute to variability in reported estimates but may not substantively affect the overall pooled estimates.

Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analyses were performed to assess the robustness of the pooled estimates and to evaluate potential publication/small study bias. A leave-one-out sensitivity analysis was conducted by sequentially excluding one study at a time to determine the influence of individual studies on the overall pooled estimate. The Classic fail-safe N test was used to estimate non-significant studies that are missing from our analysis, that if added, would substantially nullify the pooled estimate [25]. Potential publication bias was further examined using Begg and Mazumdar’s rank correlation test to evaluate the expected inverse correlation between study size and observed effect size [26, 27] and the intercept of Egger’s regression line was used to assess bias [27]. In the presence of funnel plot asymmetry, the Duval and Tweedie trim-and-fill method [28] was applied to look for missing studies to the left-hand of the mean effect and to impute these potential missing studies in the overall effect.

Data analysis

A random-effects model was specified a priori due to anticipated heterogeneity across studies in terms of population characteristics, geographic location, sampling methodology, and study design. This approach assumes that the true prevalence varies between studies rather than estimating a single common effect. To stabilize variance and address the bounded nature of prevalence data, proportions were logit-transformed prior to pooling. Pooled estimates and corresponding 95% confidence intervals were subsequently back-transformed to the original prevalence scale for presentation. Where studies reported zero events or extreme prevalence values, a continuity correction was applied within the transformation procedure to enable computation of standard errors and inclusion in the meta-analysis. The quantitative synthesis was performed using a random-effects meta-analysis model to calculate the pooled prevalence of hypertension among Nigerian adults. The random-effects model was employed because it accounts for variance in study-specific effects through weighted estimation, quantifies between-study variability, and makes the findings from this study as a basis for comparison in future studies in Nigeria. The effect size was prevalence proportion from each study, and the pooled estimate is presented as a mean prevalence with a 95% confidence interval (CI). The Z-test was used to test the null hypothesis that the mean effect size is zero for individual studies and the overall prevalence. Heterogeneity among the study effects was assessed using several measures. The Q-statistic tests the null hypothesis that all studies share a common true effect size [29] and for testing the null hypothesis that subgroups share a common effect size. The I² index quantifies the proportion of observed variance that reflects variance in true effects rather than sampling error. The between-study variance, tau-square (τ²) estimates the variance of the true effect sizes, and τ (the square root of τ²) is the standard deviation of true effects. The prediction interval (PI) was computed (assuming normal distribution) to estimate the range within which 95% of the true effect sizes of future studies are expected to fall, thereby providing a measure of the dispersion of true effects across studies beyond the summary pooled estimate. Subgroup analyses were conducted using a mixed-effects model to explore potential sources of heterogeneity. All hypothesis tests were two-tailed, and statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. For the test of heterogeneity, an alpha level of 0.10 was applied. All analyses were performed using MetaXL (version 5.3; EpiGear International Pty Ltd., Sunrise Beach, Queensland, Australia).

Results

Literature search

The process of study identification, screening, and inclusion is summarized in Fig. 2. We identified 5,744 studies in our search; prior to screening, 3,922 duplicate records were removed, along with 978 records automatically flagged as ineligible and 611 excluded for other reasons. Consequently, 233 records were retained for title and abstract screening. Following this step, 71 records were excluded for irrelevance to the review objectives. Of the remaining 162 reports retrieved for full-text review, 68 could not be obtained due to unavailability of full texts or incomplete data. The remaining 94 reports were assessed for eligibility. After detailed evaluation, 62 reports were excluded for the following reasons: irrelevant study design (n = 37), irretrievable documents (n = 5), duplicate publications (n = 4), and irrelevant outcomes (n = 16). In total, 32 studies satisfied the inclusion criteria and were retained for the final qualitative and/or quantitative synthesis [9, 11, 30–59]. A manual search of reference lists within these included studies did not identify any additional eligible records. The core characteristics of the included studies are summarized in Table 1. The quantitative synthesis was based on 32 population-based studies comprising 49,788 participants drawn from diverse Nigerian populations across the six geopolitical zones. Each study contributed an effect size representing the proportion of individuals diagnosed with hypertension according to standardized diagnostic criteria. The effect size index used in the meta-analysis was the prevalence of hypertension, expressed as a proportion of affected individuals in each study population.

Overall pooled prevalence of hypertension in Nigeria

The quantitative synthesis was based on 32 population-based studies comprising 49,788 participants drawn from diverse Nigerian populations across the six geopolitical zones (Fig. 1). A random-effects model was employed to pool the effect sizes across studies. This model assumes that the included studies represent a random sample from a larger universe of potential studies that could be conducted in Nigeria. Under this assumption, our goal is to make inferences not only about the observed studies but also about the broader distribution of true effects across comparable populations in Nigeria. The random-effects meta-analysis is particularly appropriate given the known diversity in sampling frames, age structures, and measurement protocols across included studies. Figure 3 depicts the forest plot of individual study estimates and the pooled prevalence derived from a random-effects model. The pooled prevalence of hypertension was 34.2% (95% CI: 29.6, 39.1%), indicating that approximately one in three adults in Nigeria is hypertensive. The Z-statistic was used to test the null hypothesis that the prevalence of hypertension is zero. The overall effect was statistically significant (Z = − 6.06; p < 0.001). We reject the null hypothesis and conclude that the prevalence of hypertension in Nigeria is not zero. Between-study heterogeneity was substantial (Q = 3,600.33, df = 31, p < 0.001; I² = 99.1%; τ² = 0.367), confirming that the observed variability among studies largely reflected true differences in underlying population prevalences rather than sampling error. The 95% prediction interval ranged from 13 to 65%, indicating that the true prevalence of hypertension in any comparable Nigerian population could plausibly fall anywhere within this range.

The meta-analysis was based on 32 population-based studies comprising 49,788 participants sampled from diverse populations across Nigeria’s six geopolitical zones (Fig. 1). Individual study estimates varied considerably, with reported prevalences ranging from 10% to 57.5%. The distribution of these estimates is presented in the forest plot (Fig. 3). The pooled prevalence of hypertension was 34.2% (95% CI: 29.6, 39.1%). The overall effect estimate was statistically significant (Z = − 6.06; p < 0.001). Between-study heterogeneity was high (Q = 3,600.33; df = 31; p < 0.001; I² = 99.1%; τ² = 0.367), indicating substantial variability in prevalence estimates across studies. The 95% prediction interval ranged from 13% to 65%, reflecting wide dispersion in the underlying study-specific estimates.

Rural-urban prevalence of hypertension

Figure 4 presents the subgroup analysis of hypertension prevalence by place of residence. A total of 22 studies reported separate prevalence estimates for rural and urban populations. The pooled prevalence in rural populations was 34.4% (95% CI: 23.4, 47.3%), while the pooled prevalence in urban populations was 34.0% (95% CI: 25.7, 43.5%). The difference between rural and urban subgroups did not reach statistical significance (Q = 1.84; p = 0.175). Heterogeneity within subgroups remained extremely high (rural: I² = 98.3%, τ² = 0.021; urban: I² = 97.8%, τ² = 0.019), indicating substantial variation in prevalence estimates across studies. The 95% prediction intervals further illustrate the dispersion of true effect sizes: 16% to 58% for rural populations and 8% to 75% for urban populations.

Sex-based comparison

Figure 5 presents the subgroup analysis comparing pooled hypertension prevalence between male and female participants. Sex-specific estimates were available for the majority of included studies.

Forest plot of subgroup analysis showing hypertension prevalence by sex among Nigerian adults using a random-effects model. Squares represent individual study estimates with 95% confidence intervals, and the diamond represents the pooled estimate

The pooled prevalence among males was 41.6% (95% CI: 33.9, 49.8%), while the pooled prevalence among females was 32.3% (95% CI: 25.1, 40.5%). The difference between subgroups was not statistically significant (Q = 2.613; p = 0.106). Between-study heterogeneity remained high within both subgroups (males: I² = 99%, τ² = 0.629; females: I² = 96.5%, τ² = 0.207), indicating substantial variability in reported prevalence estimates. The 95% prediction intervals were wide, ranging from 11% to 80% among males and from 14% to 58% among females.

Hypertension across geopolitical zones

Figure 6 presents the subgroup analysis of hypertension prevalence across Nigeria’s geopolitical zones. Subgroup analyses were restricted to zones represented by three or more studies to ensure stable variance estimation. Considerable variation in pooled prevalence estimates was observed across zones. The South-West recorded the highest pooled prevalence at 41.7% (95% CI: 33.8, 50.1%), followed by the North-Central zone at 38.1% (95% CI: 26.5, 51.3%) and the South-South at 32.8% (95% CI: 24.6, 42.3%). The South-East showed a comparatively lower pooled prevalence of 28.7% (95% CI: 21.6, 37.1%). The test for subgroup differences was not statistically significant (Q = 5.353; p = 0.148). Heterogeneity within subgroups remained very high, with I² values ranging from 95.2% to 99.1%.

Sensitivity analysis

The robustness of the pooled estimates and the potential for publication/small study bias were assessed using multiple complementary approaches. The Begg and Mazumdar rank correlation test indicated no significant evidence of publication bias, with Kendall’s tau = − 0.10 (z = 0.81, p = 0.42) without continuity correction and tau = − 0.099 (z = 0.79, p = 0.43) with continuity correction. Similarly, the Egger’s regression asymmetry test yielded an intercept of − 4.06 (95% CI: − 11.93 to 3.08; t = 1.05; df = 30; p = 0.30), confirming the absence of small-study effects. In addition, the Classic fail-safe N test estimated that 3,502 additional studies with null results would be required to bring the combined p-value above the 0.05 threshold (observed Z = − 53.15; p < 0.001). This exceptionally high fail-safe number suggests that the overall findings are statistically robust and unlikely to be overturned by unpublished or missing studies. Taken together, these sensitivity analyses consistently demonstrate that the meta-analysis results are stable, reliable, and not overly influenced by publication bias. The symmetry of the funnel plot (Fig. 7) further supports this conclusion.

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis synthesized evidence from 32 population-based studies comprising 49,788 participants across Nigeria’s six geopolitical zones. The pooled prevalence of hypertension was 34.2% (95% CI: 29.6, 39.1%), indicating that approximately one in every three Nigerian adults is hypertensive. No statistically significant differences were observed across rural and urban populations, between males and females, or across geopolitical zones. Heterogeneity was consistently very high across analyses.

Compared with previous pooled prevalence estimates [4, 15, 60], the higher estimate obtained in this review underscores a continued upward trajectory in the national burden over the past decade. This increasing trend is consistent with the global epidemiological transition from infectious to chronic diseases and mirrors the rapid urbanization, population aging, and proliferation of lifestyle-related risk factors now characterizing Nigeria’s health landscape [3, 11]. Specific drivers include the increased consumption of processed and high-sodium foods, physical inactivity, harmful alcohol use, and rising obesity rates, and high cost of antihypertensive medications, all of which are becoming entrenched in both urban and rural settings [6, 61]. Others are economic hardship and psychosocial stress, high cost of living and lifestyle modification, healthcare workforce emigration (Japa syndrome) in search for better opportunities. The wide prediction interval (13–65%) observed in this study signifies substantial variability in the true population prevalence across different Nigerian contexts, reflecting profound disparities in demographic composition, socioeconomic status, cultural practices, and measurement methodologies [62, 63].

The pooled prevalence of 34.2% situates Nigeria among the sub-Saharan African (SSA) countries with the highest hypertension burdens, where rates typically range between 30% and 40% [4, 12, 60, 64]. This estimate exceeds the global age-standardized prevalence of 31% reported in the 2021 Global Burden of Disease study [5] and is substantially higher than the 27% average for the WHO African region, highlighting Nigeria’s disproportionate contribution to the regional CVD burden [65]. This high prevalence is a critical concern given the strong, graded, and etiological association between hypertension and catastrophic health expenditures from stroke, heart failure, and chronic kidney disease in SSA [1, 66, 67].

The lack of a statistically significant difference (Q = 0.002, p = 0.966) between rural (34.4%) and urban (34.0%) populations represents a notable epidemiological shift from earlier reports that consistently demonstrated urban predominance [12, 13]. This convergence likely reflects the rapid diffusion of urban lifestyle risks into rural areas through changing dietary habits (increased availability of processed foods), migration, motorized transportation, and declining agricultural physical activity [68]. As rural communities increasingly adopt obesogenic environments and sedentary patterns, the traditional rural–urban dichotomy in hypertension risk appears to be diminishing, a phenomenon also observed in other LMICs undergoing similar transitions [69, 70].

The sex-based analysis revealed a higher pooled prevalence among males (41.6%) than females (32.3%), though the difference was not statistically significant (Q = 2.613, p = 0.106). While earlier evidence from Nigeria and other SSA settings reported male predominance [11, 60, 71], the overlapping prediction intervals for both sexes in the present study suggest that hypertension is driven by shared risk exposures rather than biological sex alone. Factors such as occupational stress, differential health-seeking behaviors (with men often presenting later), and lifestyle patterns–particularly higher rates of alcohol and tobacco use among males–may partly explain the observed numerical difference [72, 73]. However, the rising prevalence among women, potentially linked to obesity and changing social roles, is narrowing this gap [74].

Geopolitically, this review revealed numerical but not statistically significant variations (Q = 5.353, p = 0.148), with the South-West (41.7%) and North-Central (38.1%) zones recording higher pooled estimates than the South-South (32.8%) and South-East (28.7%). This gradient aligns with prior evidence showing regional differences in hypertension and related metabolic risks [75]. The disparities likely reflect complex interactions among varying degrees of urbanization, dietary patterns (e.g., high salt-based condiments in the North-Central), genetic susceptibility, healthcare access, and socioeconomic status [57]. States within the South-West zone, being more urbanized and industrialized, experience greater exposure to fast foods, psychosocial stress, and sedentary occupations, whereas some rural zones may still retain protective traditional lifestyle elements [4, 14, 76].

Limitations of the evidence

Despite its comprehensiveness, several limitations inherent in the primary studies should be acknowledged. All included studies were cross-sectional, precluding causal inference and limiting interpretation of temporal relationships. Methodological inconsistencies were evident, including variability in blood pressure measurement protocols, number of readings, instruments used (mercury vs. digital), and observer training–all of which influence prevalence estimates. Many studies did not adjust for key potential confounders such as body mass index, socioeconomic status, salt intake, or comorbidities, introducing residual confounding and potentially biasing prevalence estimates upwards. Poor reporting of non-response rates and inadequate calibration of sphygmomanometers raise the possibility of measurement error and selection bias. Although subgroup analyses were performed to explore potential sources of heterogeneity, formal meta-regression was not conducted. Future studies with a larger number of comparable datasets and more detailed study-level covariates may further elucidate drivers of between-study heterogeneity.

While sensitivity analyses and the Duval and Tweedie trim-and-fill method were applied to adjust for potential bias, the possibility of residual asymmetry remains.

Limitations of the review process

Certain limitations of the review process itself also merit consideration. Although extensive searches were conducted across multiple databases and grey literature sources following PRISMA 2020 guidelines [21], publication bias cannot be entirely excluded. Restriction to English-language publications may have led to omission of studies published in local or non-indexed outlets, potentially inflating the pooled prevalence [26, 27]. Although sensitivity analyses and the Duval and Tweedie trim-and-fill method were applied to adjust for potential bias [28], the possibility of residual asymmetry remains. The exclusion of zones represented by fewer than three studies from subgroup analyses, a necessary statistical precaution, limited the completeness of the geographical comparison. Furthermore, as this review was based on study-level (aggregate) data rather than individual participant data (IPD), it was not possible to examine complex interactions or adjust for individual-level confounders. The combined impact of these limitations may have influenced the precision of estimates and reduced the ability to identify determinants of heterogeneity. Nevertheless, the adoption of advanced random-effects modeling, heterogeneity metrics (I², τ², and Q), and the inclusion of prediction intervals enhance the robustness, transparency, and interpretability of the findings.

Implications for practice and policy

The high and pervasive burden of hypertension revealed in this analysis has significant implications for Nigeria’s public health system and national development. With one-third of adults affected, hypertension represents a substantial population-attributable risk for stroke, ischemic heart disease, and renal failure [5, 77]. Urgent policy attention is therefore warranted. First, the integration of hypertension screening, prevention, and management into existing primary healthcare frameworks is imperative. Community health workers can be trained under a task-shifting approach to conduct regular blood pressure assessments and provide lifestyle counseling, thereby expanding coverage at minimal cost. Second, health promotion initiatives should move beyond urban centers to address the emerging epidemic in rural communities. Culturally adapted public education campaigns emphasizing salt reduction, healthy diets, physical activity, and weight control are essential. Third, ensuring affordable and sustained access to antihypertensive medications through the National Health Insurance Scheme (NHIS) scheme could improve control rates and reduce complications. Finally, the establishment of a national NCD surveillance system with standardized data collection protocols would enable continuous monitoring of hypertension trends, control rates, and intervention outcomes.

Recommendations for future research

Future research should address the identified knowledge gaps by implementing longitudinal, population-based cohort studies to assess hypertension incidence, treatment, and control trends across Nigeria. Researchers should adopt standardized blood pressure measurement protocols consistent with WHO and JNC guidelines to improve comparability. Expanding research coverage to underrepresented northern zones is critical for achieving true national representation. Further, individual participant data meta-analyses are recommended to explore the independent and combined effects of demographic, behavioral, and genetic risk factors. Studies employing geospatial mapping could identify regional hotspots for targeted intervention, while mixed-methods research would help elucidate sociocultural barriers to diagnosis, treatment adherence, and control.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this systematic review and meta-analysis demonstrate that hypertension affects approximately one in three adults in Nigeria, confirming a substantial and widely distributed public health burden. The absence of statistically significant differences across rural–urban residence, sex, and geopolitical zones suggests that hypertension represents a generalized population-level challenge rather than a condition confined to specific subgroups. Although marked heterogeneity and a wide prediction interval indicate considerable contextual variability, the consistently high prevalence across diverse settings underscores the magnitude of the burden. As a descriptive synthesis of cross-sectional data, this study quantifies the extent and distribution of hypertension but does not establish causal determinants. Nevertheless, the findings highlight the need to strengthen national surveillance, standardize measurement practices, and expand access to early detection and management services to address this pervasive cardiovascular risk.
