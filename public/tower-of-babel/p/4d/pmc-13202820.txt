Predicting prosthesis use and mobility needs in lower limb amputees: a machine learning approach using clinical and actigraphy data
Authors: Sara Nataletti, Shusuke Okita, Jacob Sindorf, Megan K O’Brien, Rachel Maronati, Matthew McGuire, Shenan Hoppe-Ludwig, Anushua Banerjee, Amber Wacek, Juan Cave, John M Looft, Brad D Hendershot, Arun Jayaraman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDespite advances in prosthetic technology, many individuals with lower limb amputation (LLA) fall short of achieving their full functional potential. The Medicare Functional Classification Level (K-level) system remains a standard for guiding prosthetic prescriptions; however, it has historically reflected anticipated mobility potential rather than actual performance in daily life. Similarly, standardized clinical assessments provide only episodic snapshots and are limited in their ability to determine whether individuals will meet their real-world mobility goals. Consequently, there is a pressing need for objective, scalable methods to capture prosthesis use and activity in the community.ObjectiveThis study investigated the potential of machine learning (ML) and passively collected community activity data to predict two key outcomes in prosthetic users: (1) reassessed K-level based on real-world mobility data and expert panel review, and (2) attainment of self-defined mobility goals.MethodsFifty-three adults with unilateral or bilateral LLA participated in three months of passive community monitoring using ActiGraph accelerometers. Participants also completed standardized performance-based, patient-reported outcome measures, and a semi-structured mobility goal interview. A multidisciplinary expert panel reviewed all data to reassess the K-level and review whether participants met their personal mobility goals. These served as ground truth for model evaluation. Gradient-boosted classifiers were trained using combinations of demographic, performance-based, patient-reported, and sensor-derived (ActiGraph) features across various community monitoring durations (3, 7, 30, and ≥ 90 days).ResultsModels predicting reassessed K-levels demonstrated modest recall (range: 30.6–60.4%), with the best performance (60.4%) achieved using demographic, performance-based, patient-reported and 30 days of community ActiGraph data. Longer monitoring durations (≥ 90 days) reduced performance. In contrast, models predicting personal goal attainment achieved consistently high recall (Range: 77.0–89.9%), with the best-performing model using demographic, performance-based, patient-reported, and ActiGraph data. Notably, removing the performance-based outcomes had minimal impact (recall ≥ 82.1% with just 3 days of monitoring).ConclusionsML models predicting personal goal attainment outperformed those targeting K-level classification, particularly when using passively collected community activity data. These findings support the value of real-world passive sensing as scalable, low-burden strategy to remotely identify individuals at risk of suboptimal outcomes and enable timely, goal-oriented rehabilitation.Trial registration This work includes data from a registered clinical trial (NCT03930199).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12984-026-01954-3.Keywords: Lower limb amputation, Prosthesis use, Community activity, Actigraphy, Personal goals, K-level classification, Machine learning

Introduction

Restoring independent community activity is a central goal of rehabilitation following lower limb amputation (LLA) [1]. Despite substantial advances in prosthetic technology and rehabilitation methods, many individuals with LLA exhibit low prosthetic use and sedentary lifestyles [2–5], contributing to reduced physical activity and limited community engagement. This not only undermines rehabilitation goals, but also significantly lowers quality of life and increases risk of secondary health complications such as cardiovascular disease, obesity, and musculoskeletal ailments [6].

Currently in the US, clinical decision-making in prosthetic rehabilitation is tied to the Medicare Functional Classification Level [7] (MFCL), commonly known as K-level. This classification system was designed to represent an individual’s anticipated potential to use a prosthesis rather than measure actual daily-life performance. While this forward-looking approach is useful for initial prosthetic prescriptions and insurance reimbursements, in practice it is often based on subjective clinical assessments and revisited only when specific triggers occur, such as the need for a new prosthesis or a clinically concerning change in functional status. Outside of these situations, K-levels are updated infrequently due to logistical barriers such as variations in clinical follow-up protocols, scheduling difficulties, clinician availability and training constraints, and lack of space or equipment [8]. Consequently, the K-level may become outdated over time, potentially leading to a disconnect between the original prosthetic prescriptions and the patients’ real-world capabilities and evolving priorities. In contrast, patient-defined goals—such as returning to work, visiting a local store, or navigating uneven terrain—offer a complementary, patient-centered perspective on real-world mobility [9, 10]. However, both K-level classifications and patient-defined goals lack standardized, objective criteria, leading to inconsistent interpretations across providers and care settings [11–15].

Clinic-based assessments, including both patient-reported and performance-based tools such as the Amputee Mobility Predictor (AMP) [16], Comprehensive High-Level Activity Mobility Predictor [17] and mobility-related questionnaires, offer a more objective basis for decision-making. However, these tools are typically conducted under controlled conditions, offering only infrequent and unrepresentative snapshots of functional capacity and often fail to differentiate between K-level categories [14, 16, 18, 19]. The use of these assessments during routine practice is further limited by practical barriers, including time limitations, inadequate space or equipment, clinician workload, and insufficient infrastructure for standardized implementation [20]. These limitations hinder early detection of functional decline and delay timely, targeted interventions.

Wearable sensors and smartphone technologies offer a promising alternative by enabling passive, continuous monitoring of real-world activity [21]. When combined with performance-based assessments and patient-reported outcomes, these technologies can generate a multifactorial, objective view of prosthesis use and the physical, psychological, and social factors influencing daily mobility. This approach can support multidisciplinary teams (e.g., physicians, prosthetists, physical therapists, psychologists, and rehabilitation researchers) to reassess K-level based on real-world mobility and evaluate progress toward individual goal attainment [22–26]. In the context of this study, such information was reviewed by a multidisciplinary expert panel to establish a comprehensive ground truth for functional mobility. However, such a level of coordinated review is highly resource-intensive and often impractical outside of specialized centers. Additionally, the volume and complexity of these data can be overwhelming in clinical practice, posing significant challenges for real-time interpretation and use in decision-making.

To address these gaps, we developed machine learning (ML) models capable of synthesizing diverse data inputs, including demographic, performance-based, patient-reported, and community-derived accelerometer data, to approximate expert clinical decision-making. Specifically, we aimed to predict: (1) whether an individual’s community activity aligned with the reassessed K-level derived from real-world data and expert panel review, and (2) whether they were achieving their personal mobility goals. We also investigated whether passive sensor data alone could serve as a reliable, low-burden alternative to in-clinic assessments, and identified the minimum monitoring duration needed for accurate prediction.

By leveraging ML with real-world, multimodal dataset, this study sought to establish a scalable, data-driven approach for evaluating mobility outcomes in individuals with LLA. Reliable prediction from shorter monitoring periods or limited data inputs could reduce the need for in-person evaluations, support remote assessments, and facilitate timely, targeted interventions. Ultimately, this approach promotes a shift toward individualized, goal-oriented rehabilitation and lays the foundation for future deployment of clinically actionable digital tools.

Methods

Participants

We recruited individuals with LLA between 2020 and 2022 from research registries and prosthetic clinics associated with three medical research institutions, including the Shirley Ryan AbilityLab (SRAlab; Chicago, IL; Civilian), Minneapolis Veterans Affairs Health Care System (MVAHCS; Minneapolis, MN; Veteran), and Walter Reed National Military Medical Center (WRNMMC; Bethesda, MD; Service Member), as part of a larger clinical trial (ClinicalTrials.gov Identifier: NCT03930199). The study received Institutional Review Board (IRB) approval from all participating sites (SRAlab: STU00208186; MVAHCS: VAM-18–00362; WRNMMC: WRNMMC-2019–0226), and each participant provided written informed consent before enrollment.

All participants were 18 years or older with unilateral or bilateral transtibial or transfemoral amputation, including knee disarticulations. To be eligible, individuals must have been using a definitive prosthesis for at least six months, defined as a fully constructed, non-temporary prosthetic limb with a finalized socket and componentry not expected to change. Additionally, participants were required to have a prescribed Medicare Functional Classification Level (K-level) between K2 and K4, indicating at least household ambulation ability [27]. Individuals were excluded if they had co-morbidities that significantly impacted prosthetic use, such as a history of stroke or severe brain injury.

Study design

Participants completed two study visits approximately three months apart (Fig. 1). At the enrollment visit (T-1), baseline demographic and medical history data were collected, and participants received an ActiGraph wGT3X-BT (ActiGraph LLC; Pensacola, FL, USA) tri-axial accelerometer, placed on their primary prosthesis, to passively monitor community activity over the three-month period. Because the ActiGraph was mounted directly on the prosthesis, it did not require daily handling or donning/doffing by the participant. As a result, periods of no recorded activity primarily reflect non-wear of the prosthesis rather than non-adherence to ActiGraph device wear. To preserve battery life and ensure continuous data collection, devices were exchanged approximately every 30 days. At the follow-up visit (T0), participants returned to complete a series of standardized performance-based and patient-reported outcome measures. In addition, a semi-structured interview, developed specifically for this study using motivational interviewing (MI) techniques, was conducted to explore participants’ personal mobility goals, barriers to prosthesis use, and contextual factors influencing activity participation [28–30]. The interview utilized open-ended questions with guided prompts, ensuring consistency across sites while allowing flexibility to capture individualized experiences, challenges, and feasibility for rehabilitation engagement.

Participants without stated goals were considered to be already meeting their desired mobility. These determinations served as the reference standards for training and evaluating the machine learning (ML) models developed in this study

Following T0, an expert panel reviewed all participant data to determine whether their prescribed K-level differed from the expert panel reassessed classification and evaluate the presence, feasibility, and attainment of personal mobility goals. Participants without stated goals were considered to be already meeting their desired mobility. The panel included multidisciplinary representation from all three study sites, with at least one prosthetist, physical therapist, health provider trained in MI and researcher with expertise in LLA and prosthetic rehabilitation [31–33]. Each participant’s case was collaboratively reviewed, and consensus was reached through structured discussion and majority voting. The evaluation considered performance-based outcomes, patient-reported outcomes, accelerometer-derived activity metrics, and individualized insights from participant interviews. As with any expert consensus process, the relative weight assigned to each data source was not fixed a priority and likely varied across panel members, reflecting individual clinical judgment and experience. Although accelerometer-derived data are not routinely incorporated into clinical reassessment, we intentionally provided the panel with high-level summaries (e.g., weekly average steps) to ensure that their decisions reflected a complete picture of real-world prosthesis use. These summaries were descriptive in nature and differed substantially from the engineered features used as inputs to the machine-learning models, which are described in detail in the following section. This approach aligns with research priorities identified by the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS) Workgroup, which recommends incorporating real-world (e.g., daily prosthesis use, time spent ambulating, ability to navigate ramps, and stair) and clinical outcome measures to better evaluate functional impact and guide prosthetic decision-making [26]. For each participant, the panel’s final consensus established the reference standards (ground truth) for evaluating the predictive performance of the ML models in two areas (1) reassessed K-level classification (K2, K3, K4), and (2) achievement of personal mobility goals (met vs. not met). Participants in this study then have three unique K-levels for comparison: the reassessed K-level, based on real-world mobility data and expert panel review used as ground truth, the prescribed K-level, and the K-level estimated from AMP scores using standardized cutoffs (K1: 15–26, K2: 27–36, K3: 37–42, K4: 43–47).

Data collected and features

Data from all sources were processed into features for the ML models. Single time point features were derived from demographic and medical history data collected at T-1, and from standardized performance-based assessments and patient-reported outcomes collected at T0. Multi-time point features were derived from the ActiGraph data collected continuously from T-1 to T0.

Raw accelerometry data was trimmed to each participant’s average most active continuous 12-h window, calculated across all valid days based on acceleration peaks, to capture representative daily activity profiles. ActiGraph features were computed across multiple time intervals (3 days, 7 days, 30 days, and ≥ 90 days [full T-1 to T0 period]), with each interval starting from the day closest to T-1, as shown in Fig. 2. Features were organized according to the following categories: (1) Demographic, (2) Performance-Based Outcome, (3) Patient-Reported Outcome, and (4) ActiGraph:

Demographic (Dem.) Features: Demographics and medical history were collected from participant’s electronic health records or questionnaires including: sex, age, height, weight, body mass index (BMI), cause of limb loss, use of a medical assistive device, years since limb loss (from enrollment), level of amputation (transtibial, transfemoral, knee disarticulation, etc.), laterality (unilateral or bilateral), and prescribed K-level. The level of amputation variable was represented using multiple indicator variables to capture clinically relevant distinctions, including side-specific information and alternative categorizations (e.g., knee disarticulation modeled separately or grouped with transfemoral amputations) (14 features total).

Step count features. Daily step counts were calculated using ActiGraph’s proprietary regression-based (REG) step detection algorithm [34]. Daily metrics included mean, standard deviation (SD), first quartile (Q1), third quartile (Q3), interquartile range (IQR), maximum, minimum, range, root mean square (RMS), skewness, kurtosis, slope, slope intercept, sample entropy, median, mean absolute difference (MAD), coefficient of variation (CV), mean day-to-day difference, SD of day-to-day difference, lag-1 autocorrelation, and Theil-Sen slope. The mean of each value was calculated (21 features total).

ML framework. The ML models integrate ActiGraph sensor data computed across multiple time intervals (3 days, 7 days, 30 days, and ≥ 90 days [full T-1 to T0 period], along with additional outcomes to predict a participant’s reassessed K-level or achievement mobility goals. For outer loop, LOSOCV was employed. In inner loop, five-fold CV was adopted for hyperparameter tuning of the XGBoost classifier using Optuna. The preprocessing pipeline includes StandardScaler, Variance Threshold (0.01), and RFECV for feature selection

Activity count features. Activity counts were computed based on the vector magnitude of accelerations collected each day. Metrics per day included total counts, mean, SD, maximum, Q1, Q3, median, percent of active time, and percentages in low, moderate, and high-intensity activity ranges (11 daily metrics). Across the 11 daily metrics, mean, SD, median, and IQR were calculated (44 features total).

Frequency features. Power Spectral Density (PSD) was computed using Welch’s method, implemented in SciPy (version 1.15.2) [35, 36] and applied to the vector magnitude acceleration sampled at 1 Hz. A segment length of 3600 seconds (one hour) was selected to balance frequency resolution with stability of daily PSD estimates. Daily PSD metrics included mean, SD, skewness, kurtosis, maximum PSD value, frequency at maximum power (peak frequency), and median frequency (7 daily metrics). Across the 7 daily metrics, mean, SD, median, and IQR were calculated (28 features total).

We first trained models using the full feature set to match the comprehensive information available to the expert panel, which served as our ground truth. This provided a benchmark for comparison. We then systematically removed feature groups to assess how much predictive performance could be maintained with lower-burden inputs. Several models relied only on demographic variables and ActiGraph features, allowing us to quantify the added value of each feature category and to determine whether passive activity monitoring alone could support accurate prediction.

Lastly, activity changes per participant were examined to assess whether longer monitoring periods might be influenced by seasonal or month-to-month fluctuations in activity. Using ActiGraph data spanning at least 30 days, monthly mean activity counts and step counts were Z-normalized for each participant to account for between-participant differences in overall activity level. This approach enabled comparison of month-to-month changes across participants independent of baseline mobility capacity.

Machine learning

We implemented a predictive modeling pipeline to classify (1) reassessed K-level, and (2) achievement of personal mobility goals using a gradient-boosted decision tree classifier (XGBoost, version 2.1.4). This classifier was selected for its robustness in managing high-dimensional, heterogeneous datasets [36]. Model validation and hyperparameter tuning were conducted using a nested cross-validation (CV). The outer loop used Leave-One-Subject-Out Cross-Validation (LOSOCV) to simulate independent testing across participants, while the inner loop applied fivefold CV for feature selection and hyperparameter tuning (Fig. 2). The outer LOSOCV framework iteratively held out each participant as an independent test set, training the model on data from all remaining participants and repeating this process until every participant had served once as the test set. All features were standardized using a standard scaler prior to analysis.

Initial dimensionality reduction was achieved by removing features with variance below 0.01, with variance threshold. Subsequently, supervised Recursive Feature Elimination with Cross-Validation (RFECV) was performed using a Random Forest classifier (number of estimators = 50, max depth = 3) with a fivefold internal CV and elimination step size of 10%. Due to the variation in the number of input features per model and RFECV optimization per fold, the number of retained features differed across the 19 model configurations. To facilitate comparison of the top performing features, the top 10 (when applicable) features retained across all models were recorded. Feature retention was based on how frequently a model used a specific feature across all LOSOCV folds, allowing features to be ranked by frequency of use across all folds. Hyperparameters tuning for the XGBoost model was conducted using Bayesian optimization implemented via Optuna (version 4.2.1) [37], with recall set as the objective function to prioritize correct identification of participants who did or did not meet mobility goals. The hyperparameter search space is shown in Fig. 2.

Model performance was primarily evaluated using recall, with additional metrics including accuracy, specificity, precision, and the area under the Precision-Recall curve (AUC-PRC) to provide a comprehensive evaluation of predictive accuracy. To quantify uncertainty around these estimates, 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were computed for applicable metrics using the Wilson score interval method. For K-level classification, macro recall was used to account for performance across all class labels. Given the unequal distribution of K-levels in our cohort (predominantly K3), standard accuracy can be misleadingly high if the model simply predicts the majority class. To address this, we reported balanced accuracy, calculated as the unweighted arithmetic mean of the recall for each class, ensuring that the model’s performance is evaluated equally across K2, K3, and K4 classifications, regardless of their sample size. The current AI-reporting guidelines stress setting task-specific performance targets and using multiple complementary metrics, rather than relying on a single numerical cut-off [38, 39]. Because there is no one-size-fits-all threshold, we treat recall ≥ 0.80 as generally acceptable for our study, whereas life-critical diagnostics such as cancer detection typically aim for recall ≥ 0.90 to minimize the number of false negatives [40–42].

To assess the relative influence of hyperparameters on model performance, we used functional Analysis of Variance (fANOVA) [43]. After conducting multiple Bayesian optimization trials with Optuna, we applied Optuna’s get_param_importances function to estimate each hyperparameter's contribution to performance variance using a random forest surrogate model. Finally, feature importance for the best-performing models was derived using the 'Gain' metric, which represents the average improvement in accuracy brought by a feature across all trees in the ensemble.

Results

A total of 66 participants were initially recruited for the study. Of these, one participant withdrew prior to the initial visit due to time constraints, and seven additional participants withdrew before completing the T−1 visit, citing reasons such as busy schedules and medical complications unrelated to the study. As a result, 58 participants completed the T−1 visit and entered the three-month monitoring phase. One additional participant was withdrawn before the T0 visit due to behavioral concerns unrelated to study procedures that precluded continued participation, leaving 57 who completed the full monitoring period from T−1 to T0 and were subsequently evaluated for further study progression. Four participants were excluded from analyses due to insufficient ActiGraph data (< 8 h/day across all monitoring periods: 3, 7, 30, and ≥ 90 days), either due to device malfunction or low prosthesis wear. This resulted in a final sample of 53 participants. Demographics data for these 53 participants are summarized in Table 1.

When comparing reassessed K-level with prescribed and AMP estimated K-levels, a total of 79% of participants had prescribed K-levels that matched their reassessed level, and 51% matched the AMP-based K-level. Among the 21% whose reassessed K-level differed from the prescribed one, most changes reflected a lower functional classification. Nine participants had been prescribed a higher functional level than what the expert panel determined based on real-world mobility, suggesting that initial prescriptions tended to overestimate functional capacity. Upward adjustments occurred for only two participants, indicating that underestimation of functional mobility was far less common in this cohort. Disagreement with AMP-based classifications was more common, as nearly half of the participants (49%) received an AMP-based classification that did not match the reassessed level. As with the prescribed K-levels, most discrepancies (20 participants) required a downward adjustment, indicating that AMP cutoffs frequently overestimated functional mobility compared to the expert panel evaluation. Upward adjustments occurred for six participants. Together, these findings reinforce the limited ability of AMP scores and, prescribed classifications to reflect day-to-day mobility, supporting the use of the reassessed K-level as the ground-truth target for model development. Most participants (87%) were prescribed a K3 level, while only 36% were determined as K3 or higher based on AMP scores. The balanced accuracy and recall for predicting the reassessed K-level was 61%, 0.61 using prescribed K-levels and 64%, 0.48 using AMP-based classifications. While match percentages reflect how often the labels were the same, balanced accuracy takes into account how well each K-level was identified, even if some levels were less common.

For the 19 models evaluated, each feature is listed by the number of times it appears in models that use the feature set containing it. For K-level prediction, the features that entered the majority of models were younger age, longer 6MWT distance, higher CHAMP scores, faster 10MWT-FV, and a higher prescribed K-level, all of which were positively associated with assignment to a higher K-level. For goal-attainment prediction, the most ubiquitous predictors were better performance scores (AMP, faster 10MWT-SSV) and higher patient-reported ratings (OPUS, PEQ mobility domains); larger values on each were linked to a greater likelihood of meeting personal goals. Among ActiGraph metrics, a more positive Theil–Sen slope (indicating increasing daily step counts over the monitoring period) and a larger day-to-day standard deviation of activity counts both tracked with higher K-levels and with successful goal attainment.

Lastly, analysis of seasonal variation showed that both activity counts and step counts followed a clear seasonal pattern, with community activity peaking during the summer months, from June until September, and reaching the lowest levels in the winter months of January and February (Supplemental Figure SF2).

Discussion

This study evaluated the potential of ML models to predict two clinically relevant outcomes in individuals with LLA: (1) alignment between their expected and true functional profile based on their prescribed (prospective) and reassessed (retrospective) K-level classifications, and (2) achievement of personal mobility goals. Models were trained using various combinations of demographics, performance-based outcomes, patient-reported outcomes, and community-derived accelerometer data (ActiGraph) across different monitoring durations (3 days, 7 days, 30 days, or ≥ 90 days). Our findings provide important insights into the predictive performance of various data sources and the minimum data duration required to maintain model recall.

The top-performing model when predicting reassessed K-levels, which incorporated all available data, including 30 days of ActiGraph activity monitoring, outperformed the AMP-based classification but was comparable to the prescribed K-level, both with a recall near 0.60. The low recall across all models highlights the complexity in predicting reassessed K-levels. This could be due to both the broad and diverse range of abilities within each K-level classification, and the high proportion of participants with a K3 classification. This reflects a common bias in prosthetics research, where community ambulator participants are disproportionately represented due to greater independence, eligibility, and ability to complete study protocols. In contrast, K2 participants often face barriers related to limited function or community accessibility, while K4 individuals, who are typically high-functioning, may be underrepresented because they are less likely to seek care or perceive a need for further intervention [44–47]. Another important consideration is the resolution of community-derived data used in these models. While accelerometer-based activity counts offer a valuable proxy for general mobility, they may not sufficiently capture the complexity and variability of real-world activities. Distinctions between walking on uneven terrain or navigating stairs could be missed by low-resolution activity metrics, potentially limiting model accuracy in predicting nuanced real-world function.

The top features selected by the models were mainly demographic and performance-based outcomes, and predictions improved when community-derived actigraphy features were also included in the model. Greater improvements were achieved by increasing the number of community monitoring days up to 30 days. This pattern may partially reflect the stabilization of behavioral reactivity after an initial period of activity monitoring, an effect commonly reported when individuals first begin wearing sensors. Although participants were not undergoing any intervention during monitoring, simply knowing they were being tracked may have temporarily influenced their routine. As this effect faded, a 30-day period provided a more stable representation of typical daily behavior. However, extending the community monitoring duration beyond 30 days (e.g., ≥ 90 days) slightly reduced model performance. We believe this is most likely due to seasonal variability in activity patterns, including changes in weather and environmental conditions that can occur with extended monitoring periods. Most participants in this study resided in Chicago, IL, or the surrounding communities, where increased activity during summer and reduced activity in winter would align with weather fluctuations that are inherent to this location. For example, snow and ice are expected to limit community activity, especially in K2 and K3 LLA populations. Future work should further consider the impact of season and other environmental conditions on community performance, including on K-level reassessment and goal attainment. At the same time, the fact that shorter periods (3–7 days) still yielded strong performance suggests that brief, periodic snapshot of mobility status may be sufficient for adaptive, real-time monitoring. This would allow clinicians to assess mobility at different points throughout the year, detect meaningful changes in function, and adjust care as needed without the burden of continuous long-term data collection.

Our findings are consistent with prior research showing that K-level classification, while useful in estimating clinical potential and guiding prosthetic provision, do not reliably reflect the variability and complexity of the real-world mobility. Multiple studies leveraging wearable devices, including actigraphy and Global Positioning System (GPS) data, have reported inconsistencies between prescribed K-level and actual activity levels between individuals with different K-levels, particularly among K3 and K4 groups (Hordacre et al. [48] and Mellema and Gjøvaag [49]). Similarly, Knezevic et al. [50] demonstrated that models using real-world activity data outperformed K-level classifications in predicting walking ability. These results further emphasize the limitations of the K-level system as a predictive target: its lack of objective grounding, coarse granularity, and reliance on anticipated rather than actual performance make it difficult to model effectively. In summary, while the K-level framework continues to play a central role in clinical decision-making, our findings underscore its limitations in the context of data-driven approaches. Even with expert-informed classifications, predictive performance remains constrained.

In contrast to K-level classification, we found that models predicting personal goal attainment performed consistently well across all feature sets and monitoring durations. Our best-performing model used all the features and 30 days of ActiGraph data, reaching a recall near 0.90. Similar performance was achieved using only performance-based outcomes (recall: 0.89) or as little as 3 days of ActiGraph data (recall: 0.82). These results suggest that both in-clinic assessments and brief at-home monitoring can effectively predict personal goal attainment, supporting scalable, robust solutions suitable for clinical or remote settings, which would be particularly valuable for individuals with limited access to in-person care. Importantly, the burden associated with the predictive approach should be distinguished from that of the full research protocol. Most participant attrition occurred before or early in the monitoring phase and was largely driven by the number of required in-person visits in the parent clinical trial, rather than difficulty with passive ActiGraph monitoring, which required minimal effort among participants who initiated it. From a clinical perspective, knowing whether a patient is on track to meet a personal mobility goal is valuable, as it can prompt timely adjustments for those at risk while supporting continuation of current strategies for those progressing as expected.

Together, our findings support the idea that goal attainment reflects real-world functional outcomes more directly than K-level assignment. Personal goals capture actual behavior, perceived success, and unmet needs, each of which are highly relevant to rehabilitation planning [51, 52]. This finding aligns with recent efforts in rehabilitation science to shift from prescriptive, classification-based models toward individualized, outcome-driven care pathways [53, 54]. Notably, our results from models relying on brief periods of passive activity monitoring show that meaningful insights into functional status can be obtained with minimal patient input and short monitoring periods, highlighting the promise of low-burden, remote strategies for early intervention and precision rehabilitation, especially in underserved populations. Performance-based assessments were not required to obtain accurate predictions of personal goal attainment, indicating that such assessments may not be required for follow-up monitoring in certain clinical scenarios.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. The relatively small sample size comprising mostly K3 participants may not fully represent the diversity of individuals with LLA, limiting generalizability to lower-functioning or highly active prosthesis users. To maximize the amount of data available for training while avoiding data leakage, LOSOCV was used to evaluate model performance; this is an established internal validation approach for individual-level prediction models. However, performance may be optimistic compared to real-world deployment in an independent external cohort [55]. Future studies with larger, more balanced cohorts across K-level classifications are needed to further validate and refine the prediction models. Additionally, although the expert panel format provided a structured and multifactorial approach to assign reassessed K-levels, their determination still involves some degree of subjectivity. Finally, while ActiGraph data provided objective insights into real-world activity, it did not account for contextual factors, such as terrain or environmental barriers that influence mobility.

A key direction for future development would be developing a smartphone-based monitoring tool, using built-in accelerometers, gyroscopes, and GPS on these devices to capture equivalent metrics. Leveraging personal smartphones could enhance accessibility, scalability, and user adherence in both clinical and community settings. Beyond activity levels and step counts, future studies should explore more granular activities such as stair climbing, uneven surface navigation, and cadence variability, which may offer deeper insights into functional performance and safety. Finally, integrating goal-based predictions into clinical workflows and evaluating real-time feedback across diverse cohorts will be essential for supporting personalized rehabilitation strategies.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that while predicting reassessed K-level classification after LLA remains complex, predicting personal goal attainment is feasible using minimal in-person or community-derived metrics. Leveraging wearable sensors and machine learning models to evaluate whether individuals with LLA are meeting their personal goals based on their community activity offers a practical, low-burden strategy to support personalized rehabilitation and improve post-amputation care.
