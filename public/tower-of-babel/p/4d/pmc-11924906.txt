The burden of cancer in metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease
Authors: Bianca Florentina Pîrvu, Diana Clenciu, Luiza Andreea Beldie, Cristina Camelia Dica, Marilena Alexandra Burticală, Tiberiu Ştefăniţă Ţenea-Cojan, Adina Mitrea, Anca Maria Amzolini, Ion Cristian Efrem, Gabriel Florin Răzvan Mogoş, Ionela Mihaela Vladu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) is the most prevalent chronic liver disease worldwide and has become a major public health problem. MASLD frequently progresses to cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma, but recent studies also show a frequent association with extrahepatic cancers. One of the mechanisms involved in both locations is insulin resistance and hyperinsulinemia. The aim of this narrative review was to present the main etiopathogenic mechanisms involved in cancer development in patients with MASLD.

Introduction

Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), formerly known as non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), has emerged as a primary risk factor for cancer, particularly hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) and given its strong and complex relationship with metabolic syndrome that impacts hepatic and extrahepatic tissues, its influence extends beyond the liver, creating a systemic environment conductive to carcinogenesis in extrahepatic tissues as well [1, 2]. Recent studies have shown the global prevalence of MASLD ranges between 30–33% [3]. As the prevalence of MASLD has increased, so has the incidence of MASLD-related HCC [4]. Significant long-term risks are associated with MASLD progression, as individuals with MASLD can develop cirrhosis and HCC [5]. HCC represents a significant health issue, with a high mortality rate and representing up to 80% of primary liver cancers [6]. Furthermore, this surge in MASLD-associated HCC is of particular concern, because of its effects on broader demographics. For example, in Western nations, the growing rise and burden of metabolic disorders impacts heavily on the growing incidence of HCC [7]. Studies project an increase rate in all countries, with an alarming 130% increase in USA between 2016–2030 underscoring the urgent need for public health strategies to address the underlaying metabolic risk factors [8].

MASLD and metabolic syndrome are deeply intertwined, excessive fat accumulation in liver cells leads to progressive liver damage, fibrosis and heightens the risk of HCC [1, 9]. Changes in the metabolic environment can lead to alterations in the normal activity of liver, which has a unique role in regulating metabolism through its dual blood supply, from both portal and systemic circulation [9]. The pathological process within MASLD, contributes to a persistent state of systemic inflammation, hyperinsulinemia and metabolic dysregulation that exerts systemic effects on gastrointestinal (GI), renal, heart and muscle tissues and alters the normal activation of various signaling cascades [3]. Large scale studies underscore the systemic impact of metabolic dysfunction that contributes to carcinogenesis in extrahepatic tissues such as gastric, colorectal, pancreatic, bile duct, thyroid, urinary, breast, skin and female genital [10]. Metabolic dysregulation creates a disturbed cellular environment with inhibited apoptotic response and unrestrained cellular growth that further reconfigures the epigenetic landscape, leading to deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) damage and mutations, thus laying a foundation for tumor development [11]. The transition from metabolic regulation to proliferative signaling becomes more pronounced in MASLD. A key factor in this process is the role of insulin receptor (IR) isoforms, whose expression imbalance is instrumental in both liver and systemic carcinogenesis and highlights the broader oncogenic potential of metabolic dysregulation [12].

Exploring the interplay between insulin resistance, metabolic syndrome and MASLD-associated cancer can offer a better understanding of this complex pathology and bring to fruition new therapeutic approaches and public health strategies.

MASLD progression and pathogenesis

MASLD has gained significant attention, as it encompasses a broad spectrum of conditions driven by metabolic dysfunction and insulin resistance and has dramatic effects on the entire organism. Given the correlation between metabolic syndrome and steatohepatitis, the terminology has shifted from NAFLD to MASLD, as it better conveys their connection and the heterogeneity of its pathogenesis and clinical manifestations [13, 14, 15, 16, 17].

The emergence and progression of MASLD is deeply interconnected with genetic risk factors, as well as numerous lifestyle factors, which are major contributors to insulin resistance and obesity [13]. Dyslipidemia is commonly found among these patients and leads to steatosis which is a fundamental trait of MASLD. In this context, the liver capacity to metabolize nutrients is overwhelmed, free fatty acids (FFAs) are excessively delivered to the hepatocyte, either through diet, lipolysis or de novo lipogenesis, and as insulin resistance impairs the liver capacity to process, FFAs are converted into triglycerides (TGs) and stored into liver cells [18]. This lipid overload creates a toxic environment characterized by mitochondrial dysfunction, inflammation and oxidative stress that triggers numerous pathways, creating a cycle of injury which further increases circulating FFAs and causes repeated liver cell injury [19]. Lipotoxicity induces endoplasmic reticulum stress, that disrupts organelle’s function and given the altered ecosystem, the unfolded protein response is engulfed, thus resulting misfolded and unfolded proteins [20]. Moreover, this environment causes mitochondrial dysfunction, which leads to impaired fatty acid oxidation, elevated reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, initiation of cellular apoptosis and reduction of adenosine triphosphate (ATP) output, creating a feedback loop of cellular stress that contributes to progression from simple steatosis to fibrosis (Figures 1, 2) [21].

Continuous activation of inflammatory pathways stimulates the release of proinflammatory cytokines, which activates hepatic stellate cells, that lose vitamin A cytoplasmic droplets and undergo transformation into myofibroblasts [18]. This transformation is a key event in fibrogenesis, as it leads to increased production of extracellular matrix (ECM) proteins, such as collagen, fibronectin and elastin, which contribute to tissue remodeling and fibrosis, their excessive activation leading to progressive liver dysfunction [22]. Nuclear factor-kappa B (NF-κB), transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β)/suppressor of mothers against decapentaplegic (SMAD), phosphatidylinositol-3-kinase (PI3K)/protein kinase B (Akt)/mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) pathways are over-activated and create a feedback loop that sustains and stimulates continuous alterations in hepatic ecosystem. As fibrosis develops and ROS are generated, the liver’s architecture is disrupted and this continuous hepatocyte damage and inflammation promotes DNA damage, uncontrolled cell proliferation, angiogenesis and reduced apoptosis, compromising the genome integrity and creating a favorable environment for oncogenic mutations, thus contributing to the progression to HCC [23].

Morpho-pathological, MAFLD-associated HCC presents as macroscopic features on liver biopsies a golden-yellow color and increased firmness, alongside microscopic patterns, including large droplet steatosis, inflammation, ballooning, Mallory–Denk bodies and fibrosis (trabecular or pericellular patterns) [24].

The mechanism of progression form liver inflammation, steatosis and HCC is awaiting to be clarified. Researches propose many theories, for instance alterations in gene expression, up-regulation of angiogenesis gene and ECM and downregulation of Wnt signaling pathway [25]. Progression from simple steatosis to fibrosis and eventually HCC is a complex multifactorial process. Chronic inflammation, insulin resistance and oxidative stress foster a tumor promoting environment by switching on key oncogenic pathways and causing disruptions in normal cellular mechanisms. These disruptions include mutations in key oncogenes, mutations in tumor suppressor genes, inactivation of p53 tumor suppressor and altered telomere maintenance that can induce genomic instability [26].

Hepatic cancer and MASLD

Metabolic syndrome as a driver of hepatic cancer

The core of metabolic syndrome resides in the assemblage of multiple risk factors such as increased waist circumference, blood pressure, plasma glucose and TGs, as well as decreased high-density lipoprotein (HDL)-cholesterol, that support a state of systemic inflammation characterized by augmented ROS production and oxidative stress [27]. Besides its value as a risk marker for cardiovascular disease, its complex and multifactorial pathogenesis is linked to the onset of various types of cancers including HCC [28].

Obesity and type 2 diabetes mellitus promote a state of chronic inflammation and insulin resistance, which leads to the development of chronic diabetes complications [29, 30] as well as at the level of the liver and adipose tissue [31]. Being a pivotal pathogenic component in metabolic syndrome, insulin resistance is a pathological state characterized by reduced responsiveness of insulin-targeted tissues, that leads to impaired glucose response and hyper-insulinemia [32]. Insulin resistance induces hepatocyte injury, which generates inflammation and fibrosis in hepatic ecosystem and alters homeostatic mechanisms [33]. Moreover, in adipose tissue, decreased insulin sensitivity impairs the inhibition of lipolysis, resulting in an enhanced release of FFAs into the bloodstream that provides the framework for further metabolic dysfunctions [34]. Alterations in lipoprotein metabolism, known as dyslipidemia, include excessive hepatic TG synthesis, which further causes liver steatosis [35]. All these elements are encompassed into a final common pathway of inflammation, that activates signaling cascades and triggers tissue fibrosis [34]. Numerous inflammation markers serum levels, such as tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), interleukin-6 (IL-6), fibrinogen and C-reactive protein (CRP) are elevated in this condition, but it is hard to state whether these play a causative role or not [34].

Metabolic syndrome elements may be connected to carcinogenesis through multiple cellular and molecular mechanisms including increased estrogen, insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF-1), insulin, adipokine that provide an energy-rich environment and produce ROS and oxidative stress. This complex interplay promotes angiogenesis, cell transformation, migration and proliferation as well as inhibition of apoptosis [27]. High level of IGF-1 and dysregulation of TNF-α and IL-6, which are commonly found among these patients, had been shown to also increase cell turnover and inhibit apoptosis, creating a bidirectional link between metabolic syndrome and the risk of cancer development [36, 37].

The pathogenic mechanism of metabolic syndrome is complex and not yet fully elucidated, but its individual components act synergically and additively, triggering multiple pathogenic pathways that are setting the stage for liver carcinogenesis. These mechanisms are wide and extensive, having unlimited potential for further research in this area.

The role of insulin resistance and hyperinsulinemia in liver tumorigenesis

In order for insulin to exert its effects, it must bind to the extracellular domain of its receptor, known as insulin receptor tyrosine kinase (IRTK), initiate a conformational change and activate its intrinsic activity to undergo auto-phosphorylation on specific tyrosine residues [38]. This initiates a coordinated cellular response with recruitment and activation of various intracellular signaling molecules, including substrates of the IR, PI3K pathway and Ras/mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathway [38]. PI3K pathway phosphorylates the serine/threonine residue of Akt, facilitating the translocation of glucose transporter 4 (GLUT4) to cell surface, promotes glycogen synthesis by inhibiting glycogen synthase kinase 3 (GSK3) activity and induces protein synthesis through the mTOR pathway [39]. An insulin resistant state diminishes the ability of insulin to initiate these phosphorylation cascades and overstimulates β-cells, which leads to metabolic exhaustion of the β-cell [38, 40].

The inflammatory status, oxidative stress, hyper-insulinemia and dyslipidemia inhibit insulin receptor substrate-1 (IRS-1) and promote insulin resistance [41]. Moreover, insulin resistant subjects had been shown to have lower expression of IR and GLUT4, thus further magnifying the issue [39, 42]. Disruptions in inositol pathway that occur in insulin resistance and hyperglycemia alter the regulation of Akt pathway, which has an abundance of crucial functions in cell metabolism including regulation of glucose metabolism, cell proliferation, apoptosis and survival [43]. Insulin resistance induces enhanced Akt-dependent processes, closely linked to carcinogenesis, hence converging to continuous hepatocyte proliferation on the basis of a pro-carcinogenic state and creates a promising environment to tumorigenesis [43].

mTOR is activated by growth factors (GFs), including insulin, its activation generating two distinct complexes, mTOR complex 1 (mTORC1), respectively mTOR complex 2 (mTORC2), that are also involved in cellular growth, metabolism and proliferation [44]. Excessive body weight as well as excessive nutrient intake leads to insulin resistance and upregulation of mTORC1 signaling cascade [45]. With glucose being the primary source of energy, decreased glucose uptake resulting from the insulin resistant state along with the cumulative environmental stressors factors (hypoxia, lack of nutrient availability, oxidative stress, inflammation), leads to DNA damage, since mTORC1 is known to influence DNA sequence [45]. Impaired insulin signaling causes systemic inflammation, resulting in alterations in activating the MAPK cascade, whose effects are crucial for regulating cell growth, differentiation and survival [46]. The persistent activation of effector molecules in MAPK pathway, leads to abnormal cell behavior through the assembly of an autocrine/paracrine loop and had been shown to have close connections with HCC [47]. Regulation of epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition genes, by means of which tumor cells lose epithelial characteristics and acquire mesenchymal traits that enhance their ability to detach and invade surrounding tissues, also takes place through the upregulation of these signaling cascades, underlaying the pivotal role of metabolic imbalance in tumorigenesis [48].

Furthermore, IRs exist in two primary isoforms, IR-A and IR-B, with distinct roles in cell growth and metabolism [49]. With IR-A being associated with mitogenic effects and IR-B being primarily involved in metabolic regulation, and given the unfortunate effects of insulin dysfunction, a shift in their expression can drive mitogenic signaling in liver cells, promoting hepatocyte proliferation, favoring the development of liver cancer [49, 50].

These mutations and alterations, along with countless others join and support chronic inflammation, a hallmark of insulin resistance, hence supporting an environment that increases the likelihood of cancer development.

The impact of insulin receptor isoforms in hepatocellular carcinoma development

Both IR isoforms are encoded by the INSR gene through the process of alternative splicing and can be distinguished by their presence or absence of exon 11, resulting in differences in length and function [12]. IR-A is primarily found in fetal tissues and certain tumors, while IR-B is predominantly expressed in well-differentiated adult tissues [49]. IR isoforms are co-expressed in many cells. In cells that also express insulin growth factor-like receptor (IGFLR), they can heterodimerize and form hybrid receptors [49]. While IR-B has as primary role metabolic regulation and has a strong affinity to insulin, IR-A is heavily involved in mitogenic signaling, promoting cell growth and proliferation and has strong affinity for both insulin and insulin-like growth factor-2 (IGF-2) [49, 51]. By those means, an impressive feature is included in the biological traits of cancer cells, that is the ability to respond to circulating insulin, particularly when it’s elevated beyond normal levels, giving cancer cells a significant advantage over normal cells in metabolic syndrome [51].

Highter insulin levels and chronic inflammation have been correlated with higher levels GFs and IGF-1 as well as IGF-2, whose overexpression is associated with tumor development [52]. In context of MASLD, alterations of the hepatic microenvironment support their upregulation leading to increased susceptibility of developing HCC. Elevated levels of IGF-1 activate IGF-1 receptor (IGF-1R) on hepatocytes [53]. Unlike IGF-1, IGF-2 has the ability to bind to both IGF-1R and IR-A, enabling it to trigger mitogenic signaling even in the absence of IGF-1R. This interaction facilitates a more rapid progression of tumor cells [54, 55]. Through its affinity to IGF-2, IR-A plays a crucial role in the initiation and progression of HCC as its attachment to IGF-2 triggers autophosphorylation and activates signaling cascades that promote cell proliferation [51]. Following its activation, several keys signaling cascades such as PI3K/Akt, MAPK pathway are stimulated, further enhancing proliferation in liver cells [52].

In healthy individuals, regulation of insulin metabolism occurs primarily through IR-B which supports proper growth and differentiation [56]. On the other hand, in MASLD, there is often in isoform expression imbalance with a shift towards IR-A, that favors proliferative signaling over metabolic regulation, increasing the likelihood of tumorigenesis [13, 52]. Moreover, these signaling cascades are already dysregulated as a result of the metabolic dysfunction that is the central element in the development of MASLD, additionally contributing to progression from liver inflammation to HCC. The significant crosstalk between PI3K/Akt, MAPK, mTOR pathways as well as the specific molecular changes that happen in MASLD, intricate a network of signaling cascades that creates a favorable environment for tumor development [57, 58].

Bearing in mind that MASLD creates an abnormal hepatic ecosystem, bedsides the consequences of metabolic dysregulation, specific molecular changes generate alterations in normal function of key signaling pathways, that promote genomic instability [3]. On top of that, the interplay between IR isoforms and these pathways reinforces the complexity of these mechanisms and reveals how these interactions are crucial in developing HCC as well as the necessity of understanding them in order to develop specific targeted therapies.

Current research offers promising insights into how IR isoform expression pattern in MASLD could be used as a risk stratification tool for developing HCC [49, 59]. The shift towards IR-A dominance in MASLD may indicate a higher likelihood of carcinogenesis, suggesting that the IR-A/IR-B ratio could reflect the degree of disease progression from MASLD to HCC [58]. Future strategies aiming to incorporate this ratio into clinical practice will need now to be considered as potential new approaches for effective diagnostic and management of HCC in patients with MASLD [60]. Therapies targeting IR-A/IGF-2 loop activation may bring novel perspectives in treatment of liver cancer, but further studies in this matter are needed [55, 61].

Extrahepatic localizations of cancers in MASLD

Metabolic syndrome and MASLD are closely inter-connected and influence each other’s progression. Although MASLD was considered a liver disease, with the passage of time its systemic effects were discovered, in particular the risk of developing malignancies in different extrahepatic tissues, which seem to be influenced by the components of the metabolic syndrome, particularly insulin resistance that produces a pro-oncogenic environment.

MASLD is correlated with the development of several types of GI cancers (Figure 3), among which are those that impact several target tissues as those in the esophagus, stomach, pancreas and colon. Emerging data indicates that MASLD also increases the risk of cancers outside the digestive tract including thyroid, breast, lung, kidney, bladder, gynecological and prostate cancer [62].

Intricate relationships among various metabolic pathways, particularly insulin resistance, chronic inflammation, dysregulated fat tissue and gut dysbiosis, supports the development of extrahepatic cancers [63].

Insulin resistance leads to increased levels of IGF-1, which activates the PI3K/Akt and Ras/MAPK pathways that regulate the cell cycle, leading to an increased risk of malignancy in insulin-sensitive tissues [64, 65]. Trying to compensate for the reduced sensitivity to insulin, the pancreas synthesizes more insulin which further sustains cell proliferation and suppresses apoptosis, thus contributing to the process of carcinogenesis in different tissues [66]. It has been evidenced that elevated levels of insulin act on IRs from extrahepatic tissues namely colon, kidney, breast and prostate and through mechanisms resembling the ones in hepatic cells and enhance the risk of developing cancer [67, 68]. What is more, elevated insulin levels can interact with the IGF-1R, which has approximately 80% resemblance with the IR but possesses an enhanced capacity to promote cellular proliferation and differentiation. In addition, insulin increases levels of IGF-1 by reducing IGF-binding proteins like IGF-BP1 and IGF-BP2 [69].

Extrahepatic localization of cancers in MASLD

As expressed above, hyperinsulinemia and MASLD create a distorted liver environment, but given their systemic effects, many other tissues are influenced. In numerous extrahepatic cancers, the IR is more abundantly expressed prioritizing IR-A isoform, which favors mitogenic activity over metabolic responses [70, 71]. Therefore, insulin binding to the IR-A isoform accelerates cancer progression and leads to significant malignancies by stimulating the growth of small and undetectable tumors [72]. Hyperinsulinemia has proved to be a key contributor to carcinogenesis in extrahepatic tissues, through the activation of MAPK pathway, which can be often found in cancers like breast and colorectal [73, 74].

Inflammation functions as a fundamental component in cancer development by fostering the formation of new blood vessels (neo-angiogenesis), cellular proliferation, loss of differentiation and the progression of metastasis. Numerous essential signaling pathways, including IL-6/Janus kinase (JAK)/signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3), MAPK, Wnt/β-catenin, mediate these mechanisms [75]. Furthermore, chronic inflammation stimulates the substantial release of proinflammatory cytokines namely IL-6, that had been associated with gastric [76], colorectal [77, 78] and renal carcinoma [79] and TNF-α, which is related to several types of cancer among them is colorectal cancer [80].

Disturbances in fat tissue contribute significantly not only to the development of several metabolic diseases, but also to cancer pathogenesis, through an inflammatory environment that facilitates tumor formation and adipokine release [63]. Reduced levels of adiponectin accompanied by enhanced levels of leptin and resistin, sustain chronic inflammation and promote neoplastic cells development [81]. Adiponectin possesses anti-inflammatory, insulin-sensitizing and anti-tumorigenic effects and the capacity to suppress colon cancer cell proliferation by means of adenosine monophosphate (AMP)-activated protein kinase (AMPK). This may represent a possible connection between MASLD and extrahepatic cancer, given the fact that MASLD patients exhibit diminished levels of adiponectin [63]. Moreover, leptin is recognized for being elevated in obesity as well as in MASLD and enhances tumor proliferation in several extrahepatic locations, including breast cancer, esophageal adenocarcinoma, colon cancer and colorectal cancer [82, 83]. Similarly, in patients with obesity and insulin resistance, increased concentrations of resistin had been identified. Resistin activates various proinflammatory pathways, such as NF-κB pathway and stimulates the secretion of other proinflammatory cytokines that further contribute to the formation of an environment conductive to carcinogenesis [84]. The susceptibility of extrahepatic tissues to cancer increases in patients with MASLD, as they present insulin resistance which is associated with increased levels of resistin. Therefore, resistin may be considered to play an important role in GI, non-small cell lung and breast cancer [85, 86, 87].

There is also a close connection between MASLD, alteration of the intestinal microbiome and the occurrence of extrahepatic cancer through the systemic inflammation and metabolic dysfunction produced by dysbiosis, which contributes to the carcinogenesis associated with MASLD. Research has shown that the modification of the microbial composition creates a systemic inflammatory environment favorable to the development of cancers by increasing the production of harmful metabolites and proinflammatory cytokines. The production of cytokines, namely IL-6 and TNF-α, is stimulated through activation of toll-like receptors (TLRs) by microbial components known as microbe-associated molecular patterns (MAMPs) [88]. These contribute to chronic inflammation that promotes tumor proliferation in extrahepatic tissues, for instance, the colon. Increased intestinal permeability, that appears through alterations in regulation of tight junctions such as zonulin-1 and occludin, by TLR2 in the ileum, allows the passage of microbial metabolites into the systemic circulation, increasing inflammation and leading to tumor proliferation in different distant places [89].

Conclusions

Given the growing public health concern that represents MASLD, which is now recognized as one of the most common causes of chronic liver disease globally, the added risk of cancers in MASLD implies a need for integrated surveillance strategies, as well as a multidisciplinary approach that includes screening and preventive strategies to manage the metabolic and oncological complications of MASLD. The association of MASLD with extrahepatic cancers, that occur dur to overlapping risk factors and the systemic inflammatory environment that MASLD and metabolic syndrome create, adds complexity to the healthcare management of these patients underlining once more the necessity of a holistic approach with personalized care plans and patient centered strategies in order to optimize both liver and overall health as well as the quality of life and long-term outcomes.
