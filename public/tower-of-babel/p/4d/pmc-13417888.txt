Strategies for Identifying Molecules of Interest in Large Chemical Spaces
Authors: Raphael Klein, Sascha Jung, Alexander Neumann, Mehran Jalaie, Alan M. Mathiowetz, Markus Boehm
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In recent years make-on-demand compound libraries (so-called Chemical Spaces) have gained more and more interest in pharmaceutical industry. Compound vendors promise cheap compounds, fast delivery, high synthetical accessibility and a large pool of novel chemical matter, fulfilling the requirements of fast Design-Make-Test cycles. Searching in ultralarge Chemical Spaces with known 2D similarity metrics, like fingerprint-based Tanimoto, substructure, or pharmacophore similarity searches, contains pitfalls due to the representation of molecules as synthons with connectivity rules. Applied to a set of almost 3000 drug-relevant queries we analyzed the ability of similarity search methods to retrieve analog compounds from Chemical Spaces, and how to best approach typical use cases in early phase drug discovery. Distinct characteristics of each similarity metric suggest orthogonal complementarity, enabling a versatile framework to diverse challenges present in hit discovery and lead expansion campaigns. Our investigations resulted in formulating practical considerations and guidelines for interpreting the scores including recommendations for thresholds for each similarity metric.

Introduction

Ultralarge virtual libraries of synthon-based make-on-demand compounds (nowadays referred to as Chemical Spaces) have increasingly gained interest in drug discovery and drug design campaigns in recent years. They provide access to novel molecules beyond established and protected scaffolds, yet with high synthetic feasibility, low cost and fast delivery. − As a consequence, the number of commercial providers of Chemical Spaces with make-on-demand compounds has steadily increased in recent years. This indicates a trend in medicinal chemistry to purchase make-on-demand compounds, especially in early phase drug discovery for hit identification and SAR-by-space exploration, supplementing traditional in-house synthesis. Thus, companies can focus their in-house synthesis resources on lead optimization efforts and later-stage drug design aspects. Since the launch of Enamine’s REAL Space in 2016, additional Chemical Spaces from other vendors were released and are updated on a regular basis (Table ). Altogether, commercial vendors have outlined a tangible chemical space that spans over 10 trillion virtual molecules. ,− Recently, the academic SAVI Database from the NIH was converted from an enumerated library into a Chemical Space format containing 7.5 billion virtual compounds. Many pharmaceutical companies assembled even much bigger, ultralarge Chemical Spaces using their own proprietary parallel medicinal chemistry (PMC) knowledge (Figure ). ,−

Guidelines for Interpreting the MCS Similarity Score

The massive increase in size of the tangible chemical space has been enabled by the resource-efficient representation of virtual molecules as sets of synthons (preprocessed monomers or building blocks) and rules specifying how they are connected together, hence avoiding a full enumeration which would be computationally challenging for trillions or more molecules. However, since molecular similarity is conventionally calculated on fully enumerated molecules rather than on their synthon representation, new or adapted search algorithms are needed to navigate through Chemical Spaces. To cover the most commonly used similarity metrices in medicinal chemistry, a number of distinct search algorithms have been developed over time: FTrees, SpaceLight, and SpaceMACS.

For more than two decades, FTrees (Feature Trees) was the only algorithm available to run similarity searches in Chemical Spaces. FTrees uses an algorithm that describes molecules as a molecular graph, thus still considering the overall topology but neglecting the exact connectivity. The molecular graph can be disconnected into nodes with steric and chemical features, representing substructures of the molecule. Chemical features define the pharmacophore profile of the nodes (aromatic, hydrophobic, H-bond donor and acceptor). The matching of feature trees between the query and a virtual molecule in Chemical Space allows for a certain degree of fuzziness. , Therefore, FTrees tends to retrieve compounds from a Chemical Space that are related to the query but often have a nonobvious chemical similarity if no exact match exists. This makes FTrees searches suitable for finding similar molecules with a different scaffold or chemotype, advantageous in lead-hopping exercises.

With the SpaceLight algorithm, the ability to search Chemical Spaces on the basis of more traditional fingerprints closed the gap to one of the most common similarity metric used in medicinal chemistry. SpaceLight uses either the well-known extended connectivity fingerprint (ECFP) or the newly developed connected subgraph fingerprint (CSFP), retrieving compounds from Chemical Space that have a more recognizable structural similarity to the query.

Within the toolbox of medicinal chemists, substructure searching is a fundamental task, especially with the increase in synthon-based screening approaches in recent years. SpaceMACS has been developed to enable exact structure, substructure, and Maximum Common Substructure (MCS) searches and was successfully used in a bottom-up systematic approach.

A wide range of relevant use cases in medicinal chemistry can be covered with either one of the three search algorithms. Due to the only recent introduction of fingerprint similarity (SpaceLight) and substructure (SpaceMACS) searches in Chemical Spaces, the majority of published success stories were performed with FTrees. , While the applicability domains of each similarity metric have been described in literature in general terms, searches in Chemical Spaces require additional considerations due to the nature of virtual molecules encoded as synthons together with their connectivity rules. This raises questions like: How do results of each search algorithm and their similarity scores compare to each other? What similarity thresholds are suitable to consider two molecules as being similar to each other? Are there any pitfalls using classical fingerprints like ECFP4 in Chemical Space searches?

In this work we highlight the algorithmic details of each search method with focus on their application in Chemical Space screening. We used a benchmark set of diverse bioactive compounds to identify similar compounds from three different Chemical Spaces (REAL Space, eXplore Space, and PfizerVL) and analyzed the resulting lists of compounds toward a number of aspects relevant for drug design. We anticipate that our observations, practical considerations, and concluding guidelines will assist medicinal and computational chemists in understanding the behavior of the different search methods, avoiding unexpected outcomes, and effectively identify close analogs in Chemical Spaces.

Results and Discussion

A previously published benchmark set of 2917 diverse bioactive compounds from CHEMBL (Set S) was used as search queries to retrieve similar compounds from the selected Chemical Spaces. The Enamine REAL Space (70 billion, version 2024–09) was chosen because of its broad usage in the pharmaceutical industry, the eXplore Space (5 trillion, version 2023–11) due to its larger size, and the PfizerVL (75 trillion, version 2024) as a representative Space from pharma. Using three Chemical Spaces each different in scope ensures that the observed outcomes are not biased due to the specific architectural setup of any single one. All three search algorithms, SpaceMACS, SpaceLight, and FTrees were applied to retrieve the top 100 most similar molecules for each of the 2,917 queries.

First, to assess the retrieval performance of SpaceLight and FTrees, we searched for exact substructure matches of the query set across the three examined Chemical Spaces. Furthermore, we extended the analysis via MCS matching of SpaceMACS to highlight its ability to identify analogs with similar scaffolds compared to the conventional usage of measuring only the size of an MCS.

Next, we analyzed fingerprint-based Tanimoto similarity searches in Chemical Spaces. To determine the most suitable option for SAR-by-Space approaches, the performance of the traditional ECFP fingerprints was compared to the newly developed fCSFP fingerprints. In addition to retrieval rates, we evaluated how well each fingerprint method captures diverse regions of chemical space, revealing clear differences in their ability to prioritize meaningful close-in analogs.

Lastly, we revisited the well-established Feature Trees (FTrees) algorithm measuring pharmacophore similarity and demonstrated how it can be used in scaffold hopping approaches.

At the end, we recommend thresholds for all similarity search algorithms to classify result molecules into categories of “very similar”, “similar”, and “dissimilar”. For each group we show exemplar query-result molecule pairs to highlight how simple structure modifications can influence different similarity metrics. As query we chose imatinib as representative of a typical drug-like molecule.

Identifying Exact Substructure Matches

In the first analysis we were interested to see how many of the 2,917 queries exist as identical matches in each of the three Chemical Spaces. The most suitable algorithm for this task is the exact substructure search option of SpaceMACS. 334 (12%, REAL Space), 380 (13%, eXplore), and 374 (13%, PfizerVL) identical matches were found for the 2,917 queries. Of these, 193 (7%, REAL Space), 174 (6%, eXplore), and 176 (6%, PfizerVL) molecules showed an MCS similarity of 1.0 and were therefore identical to one of the queries (for score calculation see eq ).

Consequently, for the majority of queries no identical match could be identified in the investigated Chemical Spaces. The general design principle of Chemical Spaces typically considers only robust PMC reactions with high success rates using two to three building blocks, inherently limiting the chemical complexity of the product structures. This helps to explain why most of the 334 bioactives identified in REAL Space are fragment- or lead-like, with a mean molecular weight (MW) of 303 (as noted by Neumann & Klein). In contrast, the mean MW of the entire set of bioactives is 422. Given that Chemical Space search campaigns usually aim to find novel matter adjacent to known actives, tractability should therefore be based on the density of close-in analogs similar to the query, not solely on substructure or exact match occurrences.

Identifying Close-In Matches Based on MCS Similarity

For this assessment, the top 100 scoring molecules for each of the 2917 queries were retrieved from the three Chemical Spaces using the MCS search option of SpaceMACS. Traditionally, MCS searches rank molecules by the number of heavy atoms of the MCS (defined as MCS size). SpaceMACS also offers a similarity metric that is based on the Tanimoto equation (defined as MCS similarity). It computes a relationship between MCS size (#M), size of the query (#Q), and size of the matching result molecule (#R)­ 1 MCSsimilarity=#M#Q+#R−#M

We analyzed in more detail the heavy atom ratio between query and MCS (defined as query coverage), and inversely, the heavy atom ratio between result and MCS (defined as result coverage). When sorting by the MCS size metric, result molecules tend to be larger (more heavy atoms) than the query. As a consequence, the query coverage is higher, whereas the result coverage is lower. In contrast, using the MCS similarity metric, the query coverage is lower and the result coverage is higher, which leads to a more similar size between query and result molecule ().

To illustrate this in an example, the most similar molecule for query 1 based on MCS size (1a) and MCS similarity (1b) is shown in Figure . When ranked by MCS size, the query coverage is 96% and the result coverage is 69%, indicating that the result molecule is substantially larger than the query. Using the MCS similarity ranking, the query and result molecules are equal in size with a more balanced query and result coverage of 85% and 96%, respectively.

Most similar molecule obtained for query 1 based on ranking by MCS size (left) and MCS similarity (right). Green: matching MCS atoms, red: nonmatching atoms.

Since the underlying MCS search algorithm is identical for both MCS size and MCS similarity metrics, the only difference is the ranking of the result molecules. Despite this seemingly minor distinction, the impact on the retrieved results was substantial. For 67, 277, and 250 queries (2–9%) across REAL Space, eXplore and PfizerVL, the two ranking metrics delivered nonoverlapping result lists. Moreover, for 850, 1027, and 1104 queries (29–38%), the top 100 result rankings differed by more than 50%. In most cases the MCS size changed by only one to three heavy atoms; the largest shift, observed in eXplore, was eight heavy atoms. While such a numerical difference might seem minor, it can have a profound impact. Result compounds with the same MCS size often originate from a single chemical reaction. Given the combinatorial nature of Chemical Spaces, this can translate to millions of compounds with identical MCS sizes. Exhaustively enumerating them would be computationally challenging without having any significant analytical benefit. Thus, the two distinct ranking strategies (MCS size and MCS similarity) allow to efficiently enumerate only a small number of relevant compounds from a pool of billions or trillions of molecules.

Identifying Close-In Analogs Based on Scaffold Matches

A key purpose of performing MCS searches is to understand if a molecule’s scaffold is adequately represented within the investigated Chemical Space. In medicinal chemistry, a scaffold is defined as the molecular core bearing variable substituents. In the commonly applied Bemis Murcko scaffold interpretation, only ring systems and the linkers connecting them are retained, while peripheral substituents are removed. On top of that, the more generalized Bemis Murcko skeleton representation converts all atoms to carbon type and all bonds are set to single bonds. Both representations, Bemis Murcko scaffolds and skeletons, have limitations of being either too strict or too general.

Our aim was to use both definitions and decide at what MCS similarity score a scaffold or skeleton match can be expected. Additionally, we wanted to address which ranking option, MCS size or MCS similarity, is most suitable, considering the aforementioned outcome differences (i.e., query vs result coverage). Therefore, we used both Bemis Murcko definitions, grouped the results based on their respective similarity score, and analyzed whether queries and results have identical Bemis Murcko scaffolds and/or skeletons. It should be noted that SpaceMACS enumerates results starting from the smallest number of heavy atoms. Results on the first ranks therefore have the lowest number of scaffold decorations.

Within the highest MCS similarity score range of 0.9–1.0, 74–83% of the result molecules matched the same Bemis Murcko scaffold as the corresponding query if MCS size option was used, and 84–88% if MCS similarity option was used across all three investigated Spaces (Figure left, ). Within the similarity score range of 0.8–0.9, many more molecules were retrieved but only 40–43% (MCS size) and 45–48% (MCS similarity) matched the same scaffold as the query. For the more general Bemis Murcko skeleton definition, 95–97% of the results within the similarity score range of 0.9–1.0 matched and 75–77% within the similarity score range 0.8–0.9, no matter whether the MCS size or MCS similarity option was used (Figure right, ). In general, across all three investigated Chemical Spaces the MCS similarity search option performed better finding identical scaffolds.

Analysis of Bemis Murcko scaffold and skeleton results extracted from PfizerVL. The results from MCS similarity delivered a slightly higher number of molecules with identical scaffolds or skeletons.

SpaceMACS defines the MCS as a continuous substructure that preserves element identity and bond characteristics (bond order, aromaticity, and ring/chain status). Atoms in exo position to this core are ignored, for example the CO double bond of an amide group. As a consequence, a basic amine nitrogen can be matched to an amide nitrogen, regardless of differences in geometry (tetrahedral vs planar) and charge. Pairs of similar molecules may share the same skeleton but not the same scaffold for two main reasons: (i) heteroatoms within rings (Figure , query 2) and (ii) double-bonded exo atoms excluded from the MCS (Figure , query 3). Beyond the physicochemical property aspect, it should be taken into account that an amide bond formation is one of the most often used reactions in medicinal chemistry and also widely present in Chemical Spaces. Matching amide to basic amine groups and vice versa increases the chance of finding structurally related compounds. However, case-by-case assessments are required whether such molecular property changes are acceptable to retain or alter the biological activity of the compound.

Guidelines Identifying Close-In Analogs Using MCS Similarity Search

Our collective findings using MCS similarity led us to formulate guidelines on how to best interpret the MCS similarity score, as summarized based on our example query imatinib in Table . These limits refer to the data set we examined, which consists predominantly of drug-related molecules. However, these thresholds must be viewed in relative terms, as they may differ for much smaller (fragments, building blocks) or larger structures (e.g., peptides, lipids, macrocycles), because score changes are molecular size dependent.

We selected imatinib (query 4) as an example query because, in addition to common ring systems and functionalities, it has a relatively undecorated scaffold that can be modified to generate analog structures. This made it a suitable representative of a typical drug-like structure.

In general, our observations indicate that an MCS similarity score of 1.0 always results in completely identical matches. The most practically relevant high-scoring similarity range (0.8–0.999) was characterized by three patterns: (i) small modifications, typically adding or removing only a few atoms (Figure , 4a/4b); (ii) ring topology shifts (e.g., aligning monocyclic aliphatic rings with bridged frameworks or spiro systems with fused frameworks), which generally keep the MCS intact (Figure , 4c/4d); and (iii) changes in peripheral decorations, such as a methyl group walk (Figure , 4h) or the contraction of the isopropyl substituent of query 1 (Figure , 1a). Molecules with similarity scores 0.5–0.799 are more dissimilar but still share a significant common substructure with the query. Shifting the ring nitrogen of imatinib’s pyrimidine ring (Figure , 4f) reduces the MCS by one heavy atom and has only a minor effect on the similarity score. By contrast, shifting the other nitrogen of the pyrimidine leads to a disruption of the MCS and can trigger a pronounced drop in similarity score (Figure , 4g). Other common modifications within the MCS similarity range of 0.5 to 0.799 include the ortho/meta/para interconversion of substituents (Figure , 4e) and the addition or loss of a terminal ring (Figure , 4i). Both modifications truncate the MCS in the same way. For compounds with similarity scores below 0.5 the common substructure to the query covers only a smaller fraction of the molecule. However, results with MCS similarity score below 0.5 can still be relevant in specific use cases such as fragment growing, where a small fragment binder serves as the starting query. In these cases, the low MCS similarity should be paired with a high query coverage (90–100%).

Overview of molecular modifications of imatinib and their effects on MCS similarity scores. Modified regions are highlighted in gray.

As described in Figure , the shifting of heteroatom positions within aromatic rings, a common medicinal chemistry modification in lead optimization efforts, may lead to unexpected similarity results, which we would like to discuss in more detail: The behavior is observed for query 5 (Figure ), where two fused rings are connected by a carbon–nitrogen bond. The highest-ranking MCS found in REAL Space comprises 22 heavy atoms (5a). Permitting the replacement of nitrogen with carbon in the five-membered ring (query 5 (SMARTS)) resulted in a larger MCS of 27 heavy atoms (5b). In this case, the change of atom elements results in an inversion so that the remaining nitrogen of the five-membered ring turns from an H-bond acceptor to an H-bond donor. This can influence a compound’s ability to bind to a target and alter its pharmacological activity. Other modifications like the ‘nitrogen walk’ from sildenafil to vardenafil do not have an effect on the physicochemical properties of the compound but lead to a significant drop in MCS similarity of 0.595 (Figure , query 6 vs 6a). The atom element switch between carbon and nitrogen disrupts the MCS, resulting in an MCS size of 25 atoms. Allowing either a nitrogen or carbon atom at the two relevant positions of sildenafil increases the MCS size to 33 atoms and results in a similarity score of 0.914 (Figure , query 6 (SMARTS) vs 6b). This example highlights that finding a molecule scaffold of interest in Chemical Spaces depends on an accurate query definition, including an indication which heteroatoms in a scaffold are considered essential or not.

Influence of minor modifications in the center of a scaffold on the MCS size and MCS similarity score.

In summary, our analysis revealed that the MCS similarity option is better suited to identify molecules with identical scaffolds or skeletons compared to the traditional MCS size option. Result molecules in the MCS similarity range of 0.8–1.0 are likely to match the scaffold or skeleton of the query. Since ring heteroatom shifts can disrupt the MCS, a careful query specification is crucial for identifying relevant result molecules from Chemical Spaces.

Identifying Close-In Analogs Using Fingerprint Similarity

In addition to finding compounds with identical scaffolds in a Chemical Space search, a common goal in medicinal chemistry is to screen databases for similar compounds using traditional molecular fingerprints. Search results contain molecules that share similar functional groups in a similar arrangement but may slightly differ in their underlying scaffold structure. The similarity comparison is typically performed using the Tanimoto coefficient based on circular extended connectivity fingerprint (ECFP) as the fingerprint of choice. − This fingerprint was designed for use in structure–activity relationship (SAR) modeling and comes in variants of increasing circular diameters (0, 2, 4, 6). Since fingerprint similarity results are sometimes less intuitive to interpret compared to results from scaffold-based MCS similarity, we explain similarity searching in Chemical Spaces in more detail. In a circular fingerprint like ECFP the molecule is described by features which are small groups of atoms. Depending on the selected diameter, either only single atoms (ECFP0), groups of atoms within a distance of two bonds (ECFP2), four bonds (ECFP4) or six bonds (ECFP6) are considered. The larger the diameter and thus the groups of atoms (feature set) are, the more precise a molecule’s circular environment is captured. To illustrate, all ECFP4 features for query 6 are listed in Figure . The molecule is described by seven features with diameter 0, nine features with diameter 2 and seven features with diameter 4, resulting in 23 features total describing the molecule. As mentioned before, molecules in Chemical Spaces are assembled from synthons and their connectivity rules. Thus, the information about the full circular environment for each synthon is likely incomplete and might vary depending on the environment of the other synthons attached to it in the assembled product molecule. This in turn affects the collection of features in the ECFP4 fingerprint. In the query 6 example, the product molecule is formed by an amide bond from a basic amine and carboxylic acid synthon, both attached with a connectivity linker. The information content from all ECFP4 features that include the newly formed amide bond in the product molecule is unknown in the corresponding synthons (Figure , left). In the example query 6 using ECFP4, 17 fingerprint features are generated to describe the molecule in synthon space, compared to the 23 features of the assembled molecule.

Fingerprint features for query 6 using ECFP4 (top) and fCSFP4 (bottom) fingerprint types. All features that can be generated at the synthon level in a Chemical Space (left) and at the product level (right) are listed.

To potentially overcome the disconnect of molecule fingerprint features in synthon space versus product molecule, a new fingerprint type was developed specifically for similarity searches in Chemical Spaces, the synthon- or fragment-based Connected Subgraph Fingerprint (fCSFP). In contrast to ECFP, the fCSFP fingerprint is not of circular nature. In SpaceLight, fCSFP is available in five variants, with molecules described by 1-atom features (fCSFP1), up to 2-atom features (fCSFP2), up to 3-atom features (fCSFP3), and so on. All 31 fCSFP4 features of query 6 are summarized in Figure . In its representation as two synthons in a Chemical Space only 22 features are generated, with 9 features lost. Interestingly, both fingerprint types ECFP and fCSFP appear to face the same problem of feature information loss at the boundary where the synthons are connected to form the product molecule. Nevertheless, the results indicate that, when applied to Chemical Spaces, fCSFP is able to extract relevant structures despite the reduced number of features (see Section “Coverage of Chemical Space from Similarity Searches Using Different Fingerprint Variants”).

Comparing the Performance of ECFP versus fCSFP Fingerprint Similarity

Based on the above findings, we investigated how the incomplete encoding of the molecular environment at the synthon level in Chemical Space affects the retrieval rate of result molecules identical to one of the queries as previously identified with SpaceMACS for each of the three investigated Chemical Spaces. For each query, the top 100 solutions using different ECFP and fCSFP variants were retrieved. Our expectation was that structurally identical molecules in the Chemical Spaces must be retrieved with a similarity of 1.0, and nonidentical molecules have similarity values below 1.0. Retrieval rates were higher for ECFP2 (REAL Space: 95%, eXplore: 91%, PfizerVL: 81%) than for ECFP6 (REAL Space: 94%, eXplore: 84%, PfizerVL: 73%) (Figures and ). However, while 100% of the result molecules with similarity of 1.0 obtained by ECFP6 were structurally identical to the query, only 47%/31%/32% of the results with similarity of 1.0 (REAL Space/eXplore/PfizerVL, respectively) were identical to the query for ECFP2 fingerprints. This is due to the fact that larger fingerprint diameters (larger feature groups) can better distinguish between identical and very similar molecules, whereas ECFP variants with smaller diameters cannot discern certain molecular differences, for instance, 5-, 6-, or 7-membered rings.

Result molecules identified with similarity 1.0 (blue) and the proportion of structurally identical molecules (green) for SpaceMACS (MCS size, MCS similarity, Substructure), FTrees, and SpaceLight (fCSFP1–5 and ECFP0–6) when searching the PfizerVL Chemical Space.

Using the ECFP0 variant with only 1-atom features and the least feature environment information, a very large number of molecules with similarity of 1.0 were obtained (REAL Space: 104,271, eXplore: 108,926, PfizerVL: 126,693). However, the retrieval rates were drastically decreased (REAL Space: 30%, eXplore: 18%, PfizerVL: 15%). Fingerprints store information about occurring groups of atoms (feature sets), but they do not capture the connectivity information between these groups. Thus, while result molecules contain the same chemical features as in the query, they can be completely rearranged for small diameter variants such as ECFP0 fingerprints ().

Subsequently, we performed the same analysis for the results obtained with the fCSFP variants (Figure ). The retrieval rates ranged between 98%/97%/90% for fCSFP2 and 100%/100%/94% for fCSFP3–5 (REAL Space, eXplore, and PfizerVL, respectively). For fCSFP2 a large number of molecules were retrieved with similarity of 1.0, of which the majority was not structurally identical to the query. The results for fCSFP1, the fingerprint variant with the smallest size, were similar to the findings for ECFP0 described above. A large portion of molecules (43%/48%/59% of all results) resulted in a similarity of 1.0, but only 36%/23%/17% of structurally identical molecules could be retrieved (REAL Space, eXplore, and PfizerVL, respectively).

Taken together, both ECFP0 and fCSFP1 were not able to sufficiently differentiate between similar and identical molecules and thus are not suitable for finding structurally identical molecules. Moreover, our analysis showed that ECFP-based similarity searching is not able to retrieve all theoretically possible molecules contained in a Chemical Space. However, the ECFP2–6 variants with retrieval rates of ∼95% are still a solid choice. Overall, the fCSFP fingerprint describes molecules with more and smaller features compared to the corresponding ECFP variant. Although more features are lost at the synthon boundaries with fCSFP, the number of remaining features is still higher compared to the corresponding ECFP features (Figure ). For that reason, fCSFP seems to be the best suited fingerprint for Chemical Space searches to not miss any identical or similar molecules. Concurrently, this raises two questions: (i) beyond exact matches, do the ranked lists still separate similar from dissimilar molecules, and (ii) do any of the fCSFP variants enumerate lists that represent approximately the same chemical space compared to those enumerated with the more established ECFP4.

Selecting the Optimal fCSFP Variant to Separate Similar from Dissimilar Results

To address whether a separation between identical, similar and dissimilar molecules for fCSFP variants is possible, we excluded fCSFP1 due to its low performance similar to ECFP0, both failing to separate similar from dissimilar molecules. While the fCSFP2 variant shows a high retrieval rate for identical molecules, it still assigns similarity values of 1.0 to many nonidentical molecules. The fCSFP3–5 variants recover 100% of identical molecules from REAL Space and eXplore and 94% from PfizerVL. To meet our additional requirement for an intuitive scoring where nonidentical molecules should not be scored with a similarity of 1.0, we determined excess rates of nonidentical molecules with “incorrectly” assigned similarity scores of 1.0 for the three Chemical Spaces. These false assignments arise when the size of the covered substructure in the fingerprint features is smaller than the substructure itself (e.g., aliphatic chain length or ring size without substitution), limiting both ECFP and fCSFP variants. As expected, the calculated excess rates decreased for higher fCSFP variants with larger feature sizes, from 60 to 75% for fCSFP3 and 32–44% for fCSFP4 to 14–24% for fCSFP5.

Plots of similarity distributions for fCSFP1–5 and ECFP0–6 () show that larger feature sizes sharpen the separation between identical (similarity = 1.0) and partially similar (similarity <1.0) molecules with an approximate Gaussian shape for all variants except ECFP0 and fCSFP1. Based on our observations, distribution peaks closer to similarity of 1.0 reduce the separation of identical vs similar molecules, whereas peaks closer to similarity of 0 reduce separation of similar vs dissimilar molecules. For fCSFP variants the distribution peaks are at similarity ranges of 0.6–0.7 for fCSFP3, 0.5–0.6 for fCSFP4, and 0.3–0.4 for fCSFP5. Although fCSFP5 has the lowest excess rate of nonidentical molecules with similarity of 1.0, it also decreases similarity scores for near identical molecules, which in turn helps to distinguish (almost) identical from distant analogs, but weakens separation of similar vs dissimilar molecules. On the other hand, fCSFP4 introduces some fuzziness for similarity searches given its restriction to smaller feature sets.

Taken together, we conclude that the fCSFP4 variant has the best balance of discriminating between identical, near-identical, similar, and dissimilar molecules and should be used as default fingerprint variant for similarity searches in Chemical Spaces.

Coverage of Chemical Space from Similarity Searches Using Different Fingerprint Variants

Lastly, we wanted to elucidate whether molecules extracted from Chemical Spaces using either ECFP4 or fCSFP variants have a similar chemical space coverage. A UMAP analysis of the results from 12 example queries revealed that both fCSFP4 and fCSFP5 cover similar regions compared to those extracted by ECFP4 (Figures , and ). The mean distance to ECFP4 similarities increases from molecules extracted with fCSFP5 to those identified by fCSFP1 (Figure , top).

Although ECFP4 and fCSFP variants encode features differently, they retrieve a modest overlap in identical hits from 20 to 25% (ECFP4 vs fCSFP4) to 22–28% (ECFP4 vs fCSFP5) across the three Chemical Spaces. For a sizable subset of queries, the fingerprint variants shared no identical results (REAL Space ∼7%, eXplore ∼9%, PfizerVL 13–14%). A cross-fingerprint rescoring was done for results from three groups of similarity ranges (very similar, similar, and dissimilar results). The thresholds for “very similar”, “similar”, and “dissimilar” are derived from the final assessment (see Section “Conclusion”). We observed that for the group of very similar results each method outperforms the other for a small number of queries (fCSFP4 > ECFP4 for 4–6% of analogs; ECFP4 > fCSFP4 for 1–5% of analogs; Figures , and ). For the group of similar results, ECFP4 missed more higher-similarity analogs (18–20%) than fCSFP4 (3–6%). For the group of dissimilar molecules, ECFP4 missed 30–34% of better ranking results from fCSFP4 (comparable patterns were observed with fCSFP5, ).

Cross-fingerprint rescoring of results and analysis of missed analogs due to loss of information at synthon boundaries. The list of 100 analogs extracted from PfizerVL with ECFP4 and fCSFP4 were rescored using the respective other fingerprint variant.

While similarity searches with either ECFP4 or fCSFP4 cannot consistently retrieve all results, our analysis revealed that the information loss due to the synthon representation of molecules leads to a higher number of missed molecules for ECFP4 compared to fCSFP4, the latter being specifically developed to minimize this problem of information loss. The more distant result molecules are compared to the query, the more analogs are missed for both fingerprint variants, with a significantly higher fraction of missed analogs for ECFP4 (Figure left).

Interpreting Fingerprint Similarity Scores

Our preceding analysis of fingerprint-based similarity searches in Chemical Spaces revealed that the fCSFP4 and fCSFP5 variants are best suited for identifying molecules of interest with optimal differentiation between identical, near-identical, similar and dissimilar analogs. To correctly interpret the resulting similarity scores, it is important to understand how a single structural modification affects the score. To illustrate this in more detail, we used the example query imatinib and applied 14 types of molecular transformations commonly used in medicinal chemistry lead transformation and optimization campaigns. For the resulting set of 178 derivatives of imatinib, the ECFP4, fCSFP4 and fCSFP5 similarity scores to imatinib were calculated (Figure and ). In general, the average decrease of similarity scores across the 178 derivatives was 0.266 for ECFP4, 0.245 for fCSFP5, and 0.190 for fCSFP4. The merging of ring systems (category 13) showed the largest score decrease. This effect was less pronounced for ring decompositions (category 14) but still had a stronger impact on the score than for other modifications. In both cases, the decrease can be attributed to the size of the affected moieties and the number of altered heavy atoms, which is greater than for the smaller transformations. Another category with significant similarity value decreases was the replacement of a linker atom (category 5) between two aromatic ring systems. This is due to the nature of structural fingerprints where changes to central atoms affect more features (see Figure ) resulting in a larger effect on the similarity score compared to modifications of terminal atoms. The next two categories with significant similarity value decreases were bioisosteric ring replacements (category 12) and the addition of a trifluoromethyl group (category 9). Although the latter is often considered bioisosteric to methyl groups with similar physicochemical properties, the associated fingerprints interpret both groups differently. In a direct comparison between adding a methyl group (category 8) and a trifluoromethyl group (category 9), the latter results in a more pronounced score decrease. Many other well-known transformations are covered by category 11 (amide modification). The flipping of an amide group shows only minor effects on the score across all fingerprints, whereas replacing it with a bioisoster such as a sulfonamide or an isoxazole group leads to significantly larger drops (see , structures S75, S77 and S84).

These findings indicate that certain similarity score thresholds may filter out medicinal chemistry relevant results containing functional groups that are scored as dissimilar because of additional decorations (e.g., fluorinated groups for metabolic protection). To analyze the sensitivity of each fingerprint on the applied modifications, we normalized the scores by computing the relative decrease taking the lowest score as the baseline (). The findings can be summarized as follows: (i) Changes to elements in aromatic ring systems, ortho-, meta-, and para-repositioning of ring systems, as well as extension and introduction of double bonds in the aliphatic side chain affect fCSFP4 less than ECFP4 (>10% difference in relative decrease). (ii) Similar relative score decreases were observed for replacement of a linker atom, linker shrinking, bioisoster replacement, addition of methyl and trifluoromethyl groups (as a proxy for introducing larger substituents). Thus, for a number of molecular transformations fCSFP4 is less sensitive than ECFP4, which can be advantageous in drug discovery scenarios.

Best Practices in Fingerprint-Based Similarity Searches

Above, we outlined the disadvantages using the established ECFP4 for similarity searching in Chemical Spaces due to the loss of information at the synthon boundaries. These limitations cannot be fully eliminated but minimized by applying the newly developed fCSFP4 specifically designed for searching in combinatorial synthon spaces. We applied common medicinal chemistry-relevant modifications to an exemplar query (imatinib) and analyzed how similarity scores are affected. From long-term experience interpreting similarity results using ECFP4 fingerprints, typical similarity score thresholds to classify compounds are identical (1.0), very similar (0.7–1.0), similar (0.5–0.699), and dissimilar (<0.5). Similarly, for fCSFP4 we defined the following similarity score thresholds based on our collective findings presented in this work (Table ): identical (1.0), very similar (0.8–1.0), similar (0.6–0.799), and dissimilar (<0.6). We believe that these threshold recommendations follow the intuitive similarity interpretation of a medicinal chemist and will help to select relevant molecules from Chemical Space search results.

Comparison to FTrees-Based Pharmacophore Similarity

In addition to analyzing the performance of the recently developed SpaceMACS and SpaceLight methods optimized for Chemical Space searching, we wanted to compare these methods to the more established FTrees algorithm. FTrees calculates similarity based on feature trees, where molecules are represented by connected nodes (tree) with distinct pharmacophore properties (features) (Figure ). Analogous to the previous analyses, the result lists for all queries from the three Chemical Spaces were extracted. FTrees was able to retrieve 192 of 193 (99%) identical molecules in REAL Space, 173 of 174 (99%) in eXplore, and 170 of 176 (97%) in PfizerVL. However, more than 90% of the result molecules with similarity of 1.0 were not structurally identical to the query (REAL Space: 2033; eXplore: 3248, PfizerVL: 3587; Figures , and ). The underlying reason is that ring systems are condensed into a single node by FTrees, which makes the method nondiscriminating with respect to heavy atom positions. As a result, it is not possible to distinguish between certain constitutional isomers. This is reflected by similarity scores of 1.0 for results featuring modifications like ortho-, meta-, or para-substitution flips, as well as nitrogen walks or any other heavy atom ring repositioning. Based on the score trends for the medicinal chemistry transformations described above, FTrees scores are in general higher than those from SpaceMACS or SpaceLight searches. This relationship is particularly important when interpreting results based on the similarity scores, since each algorithm operates in a different dynamic range. For example, a score of 0.9 using ECFP4 corresponds to a structure that is very closely related to the query, whereas the same score using FTrees indicates a less similar compound. In this context, a chemist should develop an awareness of how the score evolves as structural relatedness decreases, in order to avoid overestimating similarity.

A particular characteristic of FTrees is that structurally distinct moieties can map to nodes with features of similar size and pharmacophore profile. As a consequence, the retrieved result molecules may contain different molecular frameworks, which in turn broadens the applicability of FTrees for scaffold-hopping approaches. In the examples, this is illustrated by the merging of two separate ring systems into a single fused ring (S105) or by bioisosteric ring replacement (S108). However, it should be emphasized that the bioisosteric ring replacement produced the largest score decrease among the cases examined. While replacement with chemically similar rings (e.g., aliphatic for aliphatic rings, or aromatic for aromatic rings) was well tolerated with scores in the range of 0.975–1.0, the score can drop below 0.9 for aromatic to aliphatic ring changes (S100 and S104 in ).

A direct comparison to the corresponding ECFP4 and fCSFP4 similarity scores shows that in particular bioisosteric replacements, ring fusions, and ring decompositions are penalized for fingerprint-based descriptors, displaying substantially lower values (0.3–0.5 for some of the examples). From a medicinal chemistry perspective, however, bioisosteric replacement is a very common strategy in drug design, and there is considerable interest in screening large molecular sets specifically for compounds with such bioisosteric modifications. , Therefore, FTrees can be regarded as an orthogonal method to fingerprint-based searches, opening up additional regions of chemical diversity for similarity-based screening. The ability of FTrees to achieve scaffold hops was reported by de Freitas et al. 2021 to overcome a synthetic dead end of a lead series. An SAR-by-catalog approach delivered a hit compound, for which no synthetic limitations prohibited further exploration. The usage of FTrees delivered a hit designated as break-through compound. As mentioned, a property of each node is the volume, which allows FTrees to differentiate between halogen atoms as well as between oxygen and sulfur atoms. In both cases, fingerprint similarities do not explicitly distinguish between them because the main group atoms share similar properties. This additional aspect of FTrees can be particularly relevant for Chemical Space searches where the combinatorial design often leads to the use of building blocks that differ only in the contained halogen within the same reaction. In this context, FTrees similarity favors molecules with smaller halogens, which are commonly preferred in drug discovery.

Assessing the Orthogonality of Chemical Space Search Methods

All three search tools discussed in this work (SpaceMACS, SpaceLight, and FTrees) were able to retrieve identical molecules from Chemical Spaces. Identical molecules were typically accompanied by a large number of similar compounds. Once a molecular scaffold is covered by an encoded reaction within the Chemical Space, additional analog building blocks are available and due to their combinatorial nature populate the neighborhood around the identical hit. This is reflected in the scores of the top-ranking structures which exhibit comparatively higher values and therefore fall within the range of very similar compounds (FTrees, MCS similarity) or very similar to similar compounds (ECFP4, fCSFP4). Conclusions

Based on the collective findings of the preceding analyses, we suggest the following recommendations and observations for application of the investigated search algorithms (SpaceMACS, SpaceLight, FTrees) on synthon-based Chemical Spaces:

The MCS-based search perspective, here performed with SpaceMACS, covers use cases such as identifying exact or similar scaffolds or skeletons with different decorations. It is the only algorithm known to us that can reliably identify identical molecules with a score of 1.0 in Chemical Spaces. The MCS-based Tanimoto similarity slightly outperforms the traditional MCS approach (84–88% vs 74–83%, respectively). The limitations of its search capabilities arise primarily from the incomplete capture of the query’s chemical parameters. For example, the method ignores exo double bonds, so that an amine may be matched to an amide. In such cases, differences in 3D shape and chemical properties can occur. Furthermore, MCS is particularly susceptible to replacements of heteroatoms in ring systems, e.g., nitrogen walk, because these interrupt the continuous structure of the MCS. This should be considered during the query specification (for example through a precise yet inclusive SMARTS definition) in order to retrieve relevant molecules. We propose a MCS similarity score threshold of ≥0.8 to define very similar compounds and ≥0.5 to define similar compounds.

The fingerprint-based Tanimoto similarity, here performed with SpaceLight, can be employed to search for structurally related analogs to a compound of interest for the elucidation of SARs. For fingerprint-based methods, and in particular ECFP4, representing the molecule as synthons with connectivity rules leads to a loss of features at the synthon boundaries, resulting in the algorithm being unable to retrieve relevant chemistry in certain cases. The fCSFP was developed for use in combinatorial Chemical Spaces and outperforms the established ECFP4 in terms of retrieving all close analogs when using its fCSFP4 or fCSFP5 variants (90–100% vs 70–95%), although minor failures remain possible due to the reduced connectivity features of the synthons. When considering the impact of common medicinal chemistry modifications, the trend suggests that two modifications cause a result to no longer be categorized as very similar, while three modifications can be sufficient for it to be classified as dissimilar. This observation can usefully be employed by the medicinal or computational chemist as a rule of thumb to adjust expectations regarding the similarity quality of the results. In direct comparison, fCSFP4 is less susceptible to certain molecular modifications compared to ECFP4. Aromatic N-to-C replacements as well as double and triple modifications lead to smaller relative score decreases, making fCSFP4 a more reliable choice for queries where no close analogs are present in the Chemical Space, which is arguably the more common scenario in drug research. In contrast, ECFP4 is less affected by certain point modifications, such as changes to the linking atom, linker contraction, or introduction of (trifluoro)­methyl groups. Both fingerprints, however, are particularly sensitive to common bioisosteric transformations (amide and ring modifications), reflected by significant similarity score drops. Thus, if insights into key molecular features are already available, it can be useful to prioritize one fingerprint based on these characteristics. In the context of the SAR-by-Space approach, sets of derivatives with structural modifications in regions with design flexibility should therefore be used in order to obtain a comprehensive picture of the chemical landscape. We recommend using score thresholds of ≥0.7 (very similar) and ≥ 0.5 (similar) for ECFP4, and ≥ 0.8 (very similar) and ≥0.6 (similar) for fCSFP4.

An orthogonal method based on pharmacophore similarity, called FTrees, is suitable for scaffold hopping and the retrieval of chemically distant yet related results. This covers the conversion of ring systems such as fusion and decomposition. A distinctive behavior of the algorithm is that it is agnostic to the position of ring decorations (ortho, meta, para position) as well as to the positions of heteroatoms within a ring system (e.g., thiazole and isothiazole are treated as identical), so that several constitutional isomers can all receive a similarity score of 1.0. Overall, common point transformations have only a minor impact on the score, making it more robust than the fingerprint methods. In general, even results with two modifications are still classified as very similar compounds, while compounds with three modifications are still recognized as similar, which is closer to a medicinal chemist’s understanding than the fingerprint scoring behavior. However, while amide modifications are better tolerated than by fingerprint-based methods, in some cases ring bioisosterism can lead to a classification as only similar. This phenomenon is particularly pronounced for aromatic-to-aliphatic changes. Another unique characteristic is that FTrees distinguishes between different halogens as well as oxygen and sulfur heteroatoms. Due to the composition of the score, these values are generally higher than for the other two discussed methods and should not be misinterpreted by the users. This inevitably means that even results with an apparently high score can exhibit large structural deviations from the query. For FTrees our proposed threshold is ≥0.95 for very similar results and ≥0.9 for similar results.

The foundational work on 2D molecular similarity presented here shows that molecular relationships are multifaceted and that the choice of search methods should be aligned with the project’s objectives. Consequently, expectations of what constitutes “similarity” need to be adjusted for each metric and use case, which in turn requires suitable similarity thresholds. To avoid misinterpretation of results, we summarized score ranges for each algorithm (Figure ) that allow method-specific classification of results based on their similarity into very similar, similar, and dissimilar. Although these score ranges can help in filtering down the list of similar compounds, for some queries still a large fraction is within the range of very similar molecules. For those, alternative but commonly used filter criteria should be used, for instance a filter for undesired substructures (e.g., reactive groups), filter for lipophilicity-driving decorations (e.g., polyhalogenation), or clustering and selection of the most similar or smallest representative per cluster.

These insights will hopefully advance the understanding of molecular similarity and the nuances of different scorings, ultimately supporting computational and medicinal chemists to make better-informed decisions in molecule selection and the design of screening campaigns.
