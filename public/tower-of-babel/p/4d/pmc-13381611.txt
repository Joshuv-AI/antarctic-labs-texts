Fosfomycin Trometamol in the Management of Urinary Tract Infection: A Focus on Antimicrobial Susceptibility and Resistance
Authors: Stefania Stefani, Carlo Tascini
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractUrinary tract infections (UTIs) are common bacterial infections in women and vulnerable populations, with increasing incidence and prevalence worldwide. Although originally classified as uncomplicated or complicated, UTIs are now classified as localized (ie, cystitis) or systemic based on clinical signs and symptoms. Oral fosfomycin trometamol, a first-line antibiotic for localized UTIs, is active against several Gram-negative and Gram-positive bacteria, including extended-spectrum β-lactamase (ESBL)-producing Enterobacterales; however, susceptibility testing and clinical breakpoints are recommended only for Escherichia coli. Susceptibility testing is also complicated by breakpoint differences of ≤64 mg/L (CLSI) versus <8 mg/L (EUCAST). This narrative review summarizes recent data on trends in fosfomycin susceptibility and resistance across high- and low-resource settings, supporting its role in treating UTIs and informing antibiotic management strategies. Overall, studies show that urinary E. coli isolates, including ESBL-producing isolates, are highly susceptible to fosfomycin, supporting its use in treating localized UTIs. For UTIs caused by E. coli, an overall 96% susceptibility rate was identified across 13 countries, with no significant differences across high-income and lower-middle-income countries (mean fosfomycin susceptibility [min. max.] 96% [95%, 97%] vs 96.75% [85%, 100%], respectively; p=0.432). Similarly, in a European-wide cross-sectional epidemiological study of E. coli urinary isolates, >95% fosfomycin susceptibility was observed in Belgium, Spain, the United Kingdom, and Russia, versus 91.7% in Italy. Fosfomycin also appears to be an appropriate treatment option for localized UTIs in developing countries, with susceptibility rates of 78% to 100% among E. coli isolates and 85.3% to 100% among ESBL-producing E. coli isolates in India. Intracountry variation in fosfomycin susceptibility rates is observed, highlighting the importance of real-world data on local pathogen susceptibility patterns to optimize antibiotic prescribing and reduce antimicrobial resistance. Fosfomycin also shows promise in treating UTIs in vulnerable patient populations, including pediatric patients, pregnant women, men, and patients with catheter-associated UTIs.

Introduction

Urinary tract infections (UTIs) are commonly encountered in primary health care and frequently occur in women, with around 50%–60% of women experiencing at least one UTI during their lifetime.1,2 Although originally classified as uncomplicated or complicated infections, the European Association of Urology (EAU) now classifies UTIs as localized (ie, cystitis) or systemic UTIs based on the presence of specific clinical signs and symptoms.3,4

The 2021 Global Burden of Disease Study identified a rising burden of UTIs over the last 30 years, with a 66.5% increase in incidence from 2.70 billion cases in 1990 to 4.49 billion cases in 2021, and a 2021 prevalence of 8.56 million UTI cases globally.5 The burden of UTIs was significantly different across age groups, sexes, countries, regions, and socio-demographic index areas, with disease burden particularly pronounced among women and older men, and in low- to middle-socio-demographic index regions. The global incidence and prevalence of UTIs are predicted to continue to trend upwards, with increases of 43.5% and 43.7%, respectively, forecast by 2050.

Most UTIs are caused by members of the Gram-negative Enterobacterales order, including Escherichia coli, Klebsiella pneumoniae, and Proteus mirabilis, with approximately 80% of UTI cases attributed to E. coli. Other causative pathogens include the Gram-positive bacteria Enterococcus faecalis and Staphylococcus saprophyticus.2,6 The updated 2025 EAU Urological Infections Guidelines Panel recommends oral fosfomycin trometamol, pivmecillinam, and nitrofurantoin as first-line treatments for antimicrobial therapy of localized UTIs in women; aminopenicillins and fluoroquinolones are not recommended (Table 1).4 The EAU guidelines also highlighted the role of antimicrobial stewardship to combat the rising threat of bacterial antimicrobial resistance (AMR), providing evidence-based recommendations for antibiotic selection, dosing, and duration. Antimicrobial stewardship aims to optimize clinical outcomes and minimize unintended consequences of antimicrobial use, including toxicity, selection of virulent organisms, and the emergence of AMR, while ensuring therapy is cost-effective without affecting the quality of care.7,8 For example, not repeating urine culture after successful treatment of a UTI is crucial to prevent unnecessary treatment and the development of AMR.9

AMR is common in urinary Enterobacterales isolates. An analysis of antibiotic resistance in urinary cultures from ambulatory patients in the United States identified significant increases between 2011 and 2020 in isolates with an extended-spectrum β-lactamase (ESBL)-producing phenotype and in Enterobacterales isolates not susceptible to nitrofurantoin.10 Globally, the burden of bacterial AMR UTIs increased between 201911 and 2021,5 with approximately 260,000 deaths associated with and 64,890 deaths attributed to bacterial AMR in UTI in 2019, increasing to around 287,200 deaths associated with and 67,467 deaths directly attributable to bacterial AMR in UTI in 2021. E. coli had the highest AMR-related mortality rate, followed by K. pneumoniae; together, they accounted for approximately 58% of all related deaths and 50% of all attributable deaths. Fluoroquinolone-resistant E. coli accounted for the most deaths worldwide from bacterial AMR in UTI in 2019, followed by carbapenems and third-generation cephalosporins, with third-generation cephalosporin-resistant E. coli and fluoroquinolone-resistant E. coli each responsible for more than 6,000 deaths.11

As oral fosfomycin trometamol is one of the recommended first-line antibiotics for the treatment of localized UTIs, and is widely approved and used to treat localized UTIs in women in most countries, the objectives of this narrative review were to summarize the most recent data on the trends of fosfomycin susceptibility and resistance to support its role as an effective antimicrobial agent for UTIs, thereby guiding antibiotic management strategies. A literature search of the PubMed database was performed using the terms “fosfomycin”, “susceptibility”, “resistance”, and “UTI” to identify relevant articles published in English within the last 5 years.

Fosfomycin Trometamol

Fosfomycin trometamol is still an effective treatment for antibiotic-resistant localized UTIs.12 It is delivered as a single oral dose, which promotes excellent adherence, has broad-spectrum bactericidal activity targeting both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria, including ESBL-producing Enterobacterales, and has a low incidence of side effects. Its mechanism of action disrupts an essential step in bacterial cell wall biosynthesis by irreversibly binding to and inactivating UDP-N-acetylglucosamine enolpyruvyl transferase (MurA). MurA catalyzes the formation of UDP-N-acetylglucosamine enolpyruvate from phosphoenolpyruvate and UDP-N-acetylglucosamine in the first committed step in the biosynthesis of peptidoglycan, the main constituent of the bacterial cell wall.13,14

Agar dilution supplemented with glucose-6-phosphate (25 mg/L) is the standard reference method for determining clinical breakpoints for fosfomycin; however, susceptibility testing is complicated by reported differences in minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs). For uncomplicated UTIs caused by E. coli, the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) (M100, 2022) breakpoints for oral fosfomycin are susceptible ≤64 mg/L, intermediate 128 mg/L, and resistant ≥256 mg/L, whereas the European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (EUCAST) breakpoints are susceptible ≤8 mg/L and resistant >8 mg/L.15,16 Although fosfomycin is active against several Gram-negative and Gram-positive bacteria, susceptibility testing and clinical breakpoints are recommended only for E. coli, as there are no official breakpoints for other UTI pathogens.15,16

In the annual CANWARD surveillance study from 2007 to 2020, fosfomycin demonstrated in vitro activity against urinary E. coli isolates, with a fosfomycin MIC90 of 4 mg/L for E. coli and ESBL-producing E. coli isolates reported.17 Conversely, fosfomycin MIC90s ≥64 mg/L were observed for K. pneumoniae, E. faecalis, and P. mirabilis; therefore, extrapolating CLSI and EUCAST breakpoints to non-specified pathogens was not recommended. The different CLSI (≤64 mg/L) and EUCAST (≤8 mg/L) MIC susceptible breakpoints had a limited effect on the number of fosfomycin-susceptible E. coli and ESBL-producing E. coli isolates identified, as less than 5% of isolates tested with fosfomycin MICs >8 mg/L. These results emphasize that a fosfomycin MIC of 4 mg/L is typical for susceptible E. coli strains.12

While fosfomycin is active against many Gram-negative and Gram-positive bacteria, several resistance mechanisms have been identified. Inherent mechanisms that interfere with the binding affinity of fosfomycin to MurA can occur via mutations in the active site of the MurA gene, which prevent fosfomycin binding, and through recycling pathways in peptidoglycan synthesis, which do not involve MurA.14 Acquired resistance may develop via mutations in genes encoding membrane transporters that block fosfomycin cell penetration, the acquisition of plasmid-mediated resistance genes that inactivate fosfomycin, or via amino acid substitutions within the active site of the MurA enzyme. Despite the rapid development of fosfomycin resistance in vitro, fosfomycin resistance develops less readily in vivo.14

The fosA gene encodes a glutathione S-transferase (FosA enzyme), which modifies and inactivates fosfomycin. fosA is commonly found on mobile genetic elements such as plasmids, which facilitate the dissemination of fosfomycin resistance.18 fosA homologs have also been identified in the genomes of Gram-negative bacteria, including Providencia stuartii (with fosA homologs identified in 100% of genomes analyzed), K. pneumoniae (99.7%), Serratia marcescens (99.7%), Pseudomonas aeruginosa (98.8%), Enterobacter aerogenes (98.8%), and Klebsiella oxytoca (96.6%), but are rarely identified in E. coli (4.6%).19

fosA genes are mostly plasmid-borne in E. coli, and most likely acquired from Klebsiella spp.20 Indeed, fosfomycin resistance has been identified in an E. coli isolate containing a fosA gene cluster that originated from Klebsiella variicola, with in vivo transfer postulated to have occurred in the gastrointestinal tract, resulting in an 8-fold increase (from 2 to 1,024 mg/L) in the fosfomycin MIC.21 Notably, the presence of fosA does not necessarily correlate with increased fosfomycin MICs, as fosfomycin MICs ≤32 mg/L have been reported in K. variicola isolates despite the presence of the chromosomal fosA gene.21 Hence, the presence of fosA and its association with fosfomycin resistance may depend on the bacterial species in which the gene is present.20

The presence of transferable plasmid-mediated resistance genes may accelerate the dissemination of fosfomycin resistance and hasten the spread of multidrug-resistant (MDR) strains if they co-occur with other resistance genes.14 Indeed, fosA plasmids that additionally carried ESBL or carbapenemase genes have been identified in E. coli isolates worldwide.18,22 Hence, the judicious use of fosfomycin is recommended to retain its efficacy against uropathogens.

E. coli was found to be highly susceptible to fosfomycin in the “Surveillance of susceptibility and Resistance to Fosfomycin in comparison with other antimicrobial agents study” (SURF).28 This European-wide cross-sectional epidemiological study involved isolating bacteria from urine samples collected from non-hospitalized women with a lower UTI between April and November 2019. E. coli (72.5%) was the most prevalent uropathogen isolated from 2,848 samples, followed by Klebsiella spp. (9.7%), Enterococcus spp. (4.7%), and Proteus spp. (3.6%). Fosfomycin was active against 96.4% of E. coli isolates overall, with activity retained against 92.0% of cefpodoxime-resistant E. coli isolates (ie, inferred to be ESBL-or AmpC-producers). When assessed by country, >95% of E. coli isolates were susceptible to fosfomycin in Belgium, Spain, the United Kingdom, and Russia, while susceptibility in Italy was slightly lower, 91.7%. Fosfomycin also displayed high activity against Gram-negative and Gram-positive UTI pathogens isolated from urine clinical specimens collected at the Walter Reed National Military Medical Center, Bethesda, Maryland, USA, with high rates of susceptibility reported for E. coli isolates, including both ESBL-producing and non-ESBL isolates, as well as for E. faecalis, but not for K. pneumoniae.24

Recently, fosfomycin has emerged as a global empirical choice for treating UTIs caused by E. coli, with an overall susceptibility rate of 96% identified from 37 representative centers across 13 countries in Asia (including the Middle East and Indian Subcontinent), Africa, Europe, and North America.29 Fosfomycin was equally effective across high-income and lower-middle-income countries with no significant differences in susceptibility identified (mean [min., max.] 96% [95%, 97%] vs 96.75% [85%, 100%], respectively; p=0.432). However, intracountry variation was observed within India and Pakistan, highlighting the importance of local epidemiology to optimize antibiotic prescribing and reduce AMR.

Fosfomycin resistance rates in E. coli were also low (0.6%–0.8%) in a nationwide cross-sectional study in Germany among female patients with community-acquired recurrent and non-recurrent UTIs, with rates ranging from 0% in the Northeast to 2.2% in the Southeast for non-recurrent UTIs.31 For community-acquired localized UTIs in other temperate climates, fosfomycin susceptibility rates of 82% and 97% were observed among E. coli isolates from patients in the Ukraine and the United Kingdom, respectively.29 In Canada, 99.2% and 95.8% of all E. coli isolated from urine specimens from patients with localized UTI were susceptible to fosfomycin using the CLSI and EUCAST susceptible breakpoint, respectively; for ESBL-producing E. coli, susceptibility rates were 96.7% and 95.2%, respectively.17 Moreover, favorable fosfomycin susceptibility was recently reported in MDR-resistant E. coli (100%) and K. pneumoniae (92%) isolated from hospital patients in New South Wales, Australia, in 2021.25

In Korea, 96.8% of E. coli urinary isolates (n=63) from adult patients with localized cystitis were susceptible to fosfomycin.52 Similarly, in Korean pediatric patients aged ≤18 years, 97.2% of 108 ESBL-producing E. coli isolates and all 9 ESBL-producing K. pneumoniae isolates were susceptible to fosfomycin with MIC90s of 2 mg/L and 32 mg/L, respectively.53 This study also reported significantly lower fosfomycin susceptibility in isolates from the pediatric intensive care unit compared with the general ward (81.8% vs 99.0%, respectively; p=0.027).

Retrospective studies across the European Union also report high fosfomycin susceptibility rates in urinary E. coli isolates (Table 2). Specifically, ESBL-producing E. coli strains were significantly less susceptible to fosfomycin than non-ESBL-producing strains in all years from 2017 to 2022 in a retrospective descriptive study in Madrid, Spain.32 In Bucharest, Romania, a retrospective cross-sectional study of 524 female patients with localized UTIs, conducted between 1 September 2022 and 28 February 2023, identified E. coli (55.3%) as the most common bacterial isolate, followed by Enterococcus spp. (15.6%).35 Susceptibility to fosfomycin was identified in 96.5% of E. coli isolates and 90.2% of Enterococcus spp. isolates, with susceptibility extrapolated using EUCAST standards. A high degree of susceptibility to fosfomycin was also demonstrated in 99.7% of E. coli isolates and 83.3% of P. mirabilis isolates in children with UTI aged 1 to 18 years in Arad County, Western Romania.36 In contrast, only 45% of K. pneumoniae isolates were susceptible to fosfomycin, suggesting caution when considering this option in pediatric patients.

A retrospective analysis of 134,162 Enterobacterales isolates (72.0% E. coli, 9.7% K. pneumoniae) from UTIs in France identified fosfomycin resistance in 4.4% overall, with resistance lowest in E. coli isolates (1.1%) and highest in K. pneumoniae and the Enterobacter cloacae complex isolates (17.9% and 14.8%, respectively).34

Fosfomycin also shows promise in treating UTIs in other patient populations. For example, oral fosfomycin trometamol was a viable treatment option in 35 difficult-to-treat patients with catheter-associated UTIs.54 In this European multicenter retrospective study, the most common isolates were E. coli (65.7%; n=23), Enterococcus spp. (25.7%; n=9), and Klebsiella spp. (8.6%; n=2), with ESBL strains present in 48.5% of the patients. Importantly, all patients with ESBL strains achieved microbiological eradication, and only four patients failed to respond to fosfomycin despite repeat dosing.

Fosfomycin has also emerged as an empirical choice for treating complicated UTIs caused by E. coli and K. pneumoniae, with susceptibility rates of 100% identified for E. coli (n=330) and 89% identified for K. pneumoniae (n=134).37 In this study, conducted in Oman from September 2022 to August 2023, complicated UTIs were defined as UTIs in pregnant women, men, children, and in patients with an indwelling urinary catheter, renal disease, immunocompromised status, or with an anatomical or functional abnormality of the urinary tract.

Fosfomycin in Developing Countries

Fosfomycin appears to be an appropriate treatment option for UTIs in developing countries, such as India, with prospective and retrospective studies demonstrating its effectiveness against E. coli isolates, including ESBL producers. Notably, the proportion of fosfomycin susceptible urinary isolates varied amongst studies set in India, ranging from 78% to 100% for E. coli isolates, 85.3% to 100% for ESBL-producing E. coli isolates, 80% to 98% for K. pneumoniae isolates, and 67.4% to 90.9% for ESBL-producing K. pneumoniae isolates (Table 2).29,39–50 These results suggest that real-world data on local pathogen susceptibility patterns are crucial for reducing AMR and optimizing antibiotic prescribing.

Concerningly, an increasing incidence of AMR was reported among 250 uropathogens isolated from community-acquired UTIs in a prospective multicentric cross-sectional study covering four geographical regions of India (North, New Delhi; South, Bangalore; East, Bhubaneswar; West, Jodhpur).47 E. coli was the predominant uropathogen (n=170, 68.3%), with ESBL production identified in 52.8% of E. coli isolates, followed by K. pneumoniae (n=44, 17.7%). Across all Gram-negative uropathogens, 51.8% were resistant to ampicillin; however, none were resistant to fosfomycin, supporting fosfomycin as a first-line treatment for community-acquired UTIs in India. Moreover, in cystitis caused by K. pneumoniae, an overall susceptibility to fosfomycin of 89% (range 80% to 98%) was identified from 5 Indian centers, using extrapolated CLSI urine breakpoints.50 Regional variation was identified in the K. pneumoniae susceptibility profile, which was found to be significantly higher in South India than in North India (p<0.05).

The in vitro susceptibility of fosfomycin against ESBL and MDR uropathogenic isolates of E. coli was similarly assessed in a prospective cross-sectional study conducted from April 2022 to December 2022 at the Department of Microbiology, Sarojini Naidu Medical College, Agra, Uttar Pradesh, India.43 Overall, 95.8% of 401 E. coli isolates (48% MDR and 62% ESBL-producing) were sensitive to fosfomycin, with susceptibility observed in 92% of MDR isolates and 96% of ESBL-producing isolates. Fosfomycin was also effective against ESBL-producing Gram-negative UTI-causing bacilli, with susceptibility observed in significantly more E. coli than K. pneumoniae isolates (85.3% vs 67.4%; p=0.008) in a prospective study on urine samples from patients with confirmed UTIs at a tertiary care hospital in India.48

In West Bengal, India, E. coli (60.2%) was identified as the most common pathogen, followed by Klebsiella spp. (26.0%) and Pseudomonas spp. (9.9%), in a hospital-based cross-sectional study, conducted from July 2023 to June 2024, of 181 gram-negative uropathogens isolated from patients with clinical symptoms of UTI.39 In an analysis of the antimicrobial susceptibility pattern, 78.0% of E. coli isolates were sensitive to fosfomycin, as were 80.9% and 83.3% of Klebsiella spp. and Pseudomonas spp. isolates, respectively. Interestingly, resistance to fosfomycin increased in higher age groups, with 40% of patients aged over 60 years resistant, compared with only 7.7% of patients aged 13–20 years, possibly due to the increased frequency of hospitalization and antibiotic exposure in older patients.

Similarly, a retrospective cross-sectional study of 509 uropathogens (46.1% E. coli; 21.1% K. pneumoniae; 7.1% P. aeruginosa) isolated from urine samples collected December 2022 to May 2023 from patients with clinically suspected UTI in Punjab, India, identified ≥93% fosfomycin susceptibility in all Gram-negative and Gram-positive isolates.49 In this study, fosfomycin susceptibility was identified in 95.9% of 172 ESBL producers, 86.3% of 73 metallo-beta-lactamase producers, and 85.9% of 135 MDR uropathogens. Among the ESBL producers, 98.1% of E. coli isolates (106 of 108) and 90.9% of K. pneumoniae isolates (50 of 55) were found to be susceptible to fosfomycin.

Fosfomycin was also active against drug-resistant (MDR, extensive drug-resistant, or pandrug-resistant) Gram-negative bacilli (n=334) isolated from urine samples with clinical suspicion of UTI in Lucknow, India.40 In this cross-sectional observational study, E. coli was the most predominant drug-resistant isolate (37.1%; n=124), followed by K. pneumoniae (34.4%; n=115) and Pseudomonas aeruginosa (21.9%; n=73), with susceptibility to fosfomycin observed in 79.6% isolates overall, and in 88.7% of E. coli, 91.3% of K. pneumoniae, and 58% of P. aeruginosa isolates, interpreted according to CLSI guidelines due to the lack of clear MIC breakpoints for Klebsiella and Pseudomonas isolates. Similarly, in Haryana, India, 97.2% of MDR urinary E. coli isolates (n=71) were susceptible to fosfomycin.44 Across the Indian subcontinent, 85%–100% and 99% of uropathogenic E. coli in Pakistan and the Maldives, respectively, were susceptible to fosfomycin.29

Studies in other developing countries support the use of fosfomycin to treat UTIs. For example, across South America, a cross-sectional study of community-onset UTIs (n=372) in the Bolivian Chaco reported low fosfomycin resistance rates among E. coli isolates, despite MDR identified in 95.3% of the isolates.26 Notably, CTX-M-positive E. coli were significantly more resistant to fosfomycin than CTX-M-negative E. coli, with fosfomycin susceptibility rates of 86.0% vs 97.5% reported, respectively (p=0.0003). In an analysis of 99 ESBL-producing uropathogenic E. coli strains, isolated from patients with UTIs treated in a public hospital in Cusco, Peru, between January and December 2017, resistance to fosfomycin was only 14.1% compared with >80% for ciprofloxacin and trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole.27 Across the Middle East and Africa, 95%–100% of E. coli urinary isolates from patients with localized UTIs in Benin, Oman, and Egypt were susceptible to fosfomycin.29 In Cameroon, all ESBL-producing urinary isolates of E. coli, K. pneumoniae, and E. aerogenes were susceptible to fosfomycin in a cross-sectional, retrospective study of MDR Enterobacterales.38

Fosfomycin is not currently indicated for the treatment of UTIs in Sri Lanka; however, an in vitro cross-sectional study found 100% susceptibility among 101 E. coli urinary isolates recovered from patients with UTI in Sri Lanka, highlighting its potential use in areas with high antibiotic resistance.51

Fosfomycin Resistance Rates Over Time

Several studies report no significant changes in fosfomycin resistance rates over time, although the time periods investigated were relatively short. For example, there were no statistically significant changes in fosfomycin susceptibility of E. coli and K. pneumoniae isolates obtained from the urinary tract samples of community and hospitalized patients between 2016 and 2021 in Granada, Spain.33 In an outpatient setting of a Tertiary Care Hospital in Delhi, India, resistance to fosfomycin decreased in the post-COVID-19 period compared with the pre-COVID-19 period, albeit without significance, with no changes observed in the AMR profile of fosfomycin in Enterobacterales or Gram-positive organisms during the two study periods.55 In a 9-year retrospective analysis of AMR patterns of UTIs associated with Gram-negative bacteria, no significant change in fosfomycin resistance was observed (15.50% in 2014 vs 13.65% in 2022; p=0.577) in an analysis of more than 650,000 urine cultures collected in the Microbiology Department at the University Hospital Southampton, England.56 Conversely, a German retrospective analysis of antimicrobial susceptibility data from 201,152 urine specimens collected between January 2016 and June 2021, reported a significant decrease of 1.2% in fosfomycin resistance rates, from 5.4% to 4.2% (p<0.001).30 Overall, there is a need for additional studies over extended periods to map changes in fosfomycin resistance globally.

Conclusion

Considering the evidence discussed here, single-dose oral fosfomycin trometamol appears to be a viable option for the empirical treatment of community-acquired localized UTIs in women globally. This is in line with the updated EAU guidelines, which recommend oral fosfomycin trometamol as one of the first-line treatments for antimicrobial therapy of localized UTIs in women. The effectiveness of fosfomycin is supported by evidence from both prospective and retrospective studies, with high susceptibility rates of fosfomycin reported against uropathogens, including MDR and ESBL-producing E. coli isolates. However, clinicians should be aware of the discordant CLSI and EUCAST breakpoints (≤64 vs ≤8 mg/L) and the lack of official breakpoints for pathogens other than E. coli, which complicate susceptibility interpretation.

While fosfomycin susceptibility in E. coli exceeds 95% in many high-income countries, rates as low as 78% in parts of India, and 86% in Bolivia and Peru for ESBL-producing E. coli, underscore the need for regional surveillance, particularly in low-resource settings. This is especially important in an era of escalating bacterial AMR, where fosfomycin holds promise as an effective treatment for antibiotic-resistant localized UTIs. It is also imperative to preserve fosfomycin’s efficacy through antimicrobial stewardship, particularly given reports of intracountry variation in susceptibility, highlighting the importance of localized antibiograms in optimizing antibiotic prescribing and reducing AMR. Fosfomycin also shows promise in treating UTIs in vulnerable patient populations, including, but not limited to, patients with catheter-associated UTIs, men, and pregnant women. Moreover, extended longitudinal studies are needed to track fosfomycin resistance trends globally, and establishing official breakpoints for K. pneumoniae and other uropathogens would improve clinical decision-making.
