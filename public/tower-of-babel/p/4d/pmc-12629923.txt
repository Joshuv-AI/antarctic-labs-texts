Scavenging methylglyoxal improves bone quality and defect healing in diabetic mice
Authors: Toshifumi Hikichi, Kumi Kimura, Seiichi Munesue, Yu Oshima, Ai Harashima, Tomo Hamada, Kanu Shimokawa, Yuki Fushitani, Hisanori Goto, Hiroyuki Tsuchiya, Satoru Demura, Hidenori Matsubara, Yasuhiko Yamamoto
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractHyperglycemia in diabetes leads to the formation of methylglyoxal (MG) and accumulation of advanced glycation end-products (AGEs). We previously reported that exogenous MG exposure deteriorated osteoblastic differentiation in vitro and diabetic mice showed delayed bone defect healing along with elevated MG-derived AGE levels. However, whether endogenously formed MG is involved in impaired bone repair and remodeling in diabetes remains unclear. In this study, we investigated the effects of hyperglycemia-induced MG formation on bone quality and defect healing in mice. Using a synthetic MG probe [Ir(ppy)2(DA-phen)], we found that endogenous MG formation deteriorated osteoblastic and osteoclastic differentiation under hyperglycemic conditions in cultured cells. In the bone defect site of streptozotocin (STZ)-induced diabetic mice, along with impaired defect healing, we observed elevated endogenous MG levels and downregulation of alkaline phosphatase (ALP) compared with that in non-diabetic control mice; however, these alterations were alleviated by managing blood glucose levels through insulin supplementation. Furthermore, treatment with pyridoxamine (PM), an MG scavenger, ameliorated these impairments by suppressing MG elevation and upregulating the expression of osteocalcin, osteoprotegerin, and osteoclast-associated receptor genes without improving the diabetic status. These findings suggest that endogenously formed MG is detrimental to hyperglycemia-related delayed bone defect healing in type 1 diabetes mellitus (T1DM). Collectively, this study suggests that MG scavenging by PM and suppression of MG formation by glycemic control are potential therapeutic strategies for T1DM-associated bone disorders.Keywords: T1DM, Methylglyoxal, Pyridoxamine, Impaired defect healing

Lay summary

Diabetes mellitus is a chronic disease, and hyperglycemia is a common feature of uncontrolled diabetes that can eventually lead to vascular complications, such as atherosclerosis, nephropathy, neuropathy, and retinopathy. Other well-known complications of diabetes are wound-healing disorders, including impaired bone defect healing and an increased risk of fractures. However, the pathophysiology and underlying mechanisms of the diabetes-induced deterioration of bone integrity remain largely unknown. In this study, we found that endogenous MG formation was increased in bone cells and defect lesions under diabetic conditions, along with the downregulation of osteoblastic differentiation and osteoclastic activities. Treatment with insulin or the vitamin B6 isomer pyridoxamine ameliorated bone defect impairment in diabetic mice by suppressing MG elevation, with or without lowering blood glucose levels, respectively. In conclusion, MG depletion may be a potential therapeutic strategy for T1DM-associated bone disorders.

Introduction

Patients with diabetes tend to develop osteoporosis characterized by low bone mass, bone fragility, poor bone quality, and an increased fracture risk (Ferrari et al., 2018; de Liefde et al., 2005; Oei et al., 2013; Sellmeyer et al., 2016); moreover, fracture healing is often delayed in these patients. Particularly, the fracture risk is up to four-fold high in patients with hip fractures with type 1 diabetes mellitus (T1DM) and accompanied by a low bone mineral density (BMD) (Vilaca et al., 2020). Impaired bone metabolism in patients with diabetes also increases the risk of pseudarthrosis (Wilson et al., 2024). Hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) levels are a marker of elevated glucose levels in T1DM and correlated with an increased fracture risk, especially at ≥8 % (Hidayat et al., 2021). Fasting glucose and HbA1c levels are significantly higher in patients with T1DM than those in controls, even after insulin treatment (Sewing et al., 2022). Although insulin treatment partially controls hyperglycemia, bone quality and healing remain impaired in T1DM mice (Sewing et al., 2022). Therefore, the hyperglycemic condition is considered a risk factor for bone disorders; however, the molecular mechanism is still unclear.

In our previous study, we demonstrated that exposure to exogenous methylglyoxal (MG), a highly deleterious cell-permeant molecule, deteriorated osteoblastic differentiation in vitro based on alkaline phosphatase (ALP) measurements (Ugaji et al., 2021; Aikawa et al., 2017). MG is the most potent glycation agent associated with the rapid generation of advanced glycation end-products (AGEs) (Thornalley, 2005) and up to 20,000 times more reactive than glucose in the glycation reaction. Endogenous MG is mostly formed via the fragmentation of triose phosphate glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate and dihydroxyacetone phosphate via the glycolytic pathway (Brownlee, 2001). Hyperglycemic conditions in T1DM induce the formation of MG and accumulation of AGEs (Brownlee, 2001). MG can also be produced as a by-product of protein and fatty acid metabolism (Brownlee, 2001; Masania et al., 2016). However, the relationship between osteoblast or osteoclast function and endogenously formed MG remains unexplored (Suh et al., 2018, Suh et al., 2019; Lee et al., 2019).

Osteoclasts are related to bone remodeling and resorption. A previous study showed a higher level of tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase (TRACP-5b), a marker of bone resorption, in patients with early-stage T1DM than that in controls; however, the marker levels are reduced in long-standing patients with T1DM (Sewing et al., 2022). Moreover, osteoclasts are upregulated under hyperglycemic conditions; however, this effect is weakened by the effects of AGEs in vitro and in vivo. Therefore, osteoclast function in T1DM remains controversial and its relationship with endogenously formed MG has not been evaluated (Suh et al., 2018, Suh et al., 2019; Lee et al., 2019). Furthermore, whether external metabolic controls, such as glucose concentration or temperature, alter the formation of endogenous MG remains unknown.

Pyridoxamine (PM), a vitamin B6 isomer, acts as an MG scavenger by forming a two-fold dimer with MG, and reportedly improves renal function and cardiovascular disease in patients with diabetes (Voziyan and Hudson, 2005; Degenhardt et al., 2002a; Lewis et al., 2012; Abouzed et al., 2016). PM prevents MG-induced inhibition of osteoblastic differentiation in vitro and improves defect healing in T1DM mice (Ugaji et al., 2021; Aikawa et al., 2017). Therefore, we hypothesized that suppression of endogenous MG formation would improve impaired bone repair and remodeling in T1DM.

To test this hypothesis, we explored the relationship between endogenous MG formation under hyperglycemic conditions and bone healing. Recently, Zhang et al. developed an MG probe [Ir(ppy)2(DA-phen); PF6] to detect highly reactive MG and demonstrated the detection of exogenous MG in cells and Daphnia magna (Zhang et al., 2017). PF6 is convenient to use because it can be detected in well plates using a fluorescence spectrometer. We chose PF6 because it reacts selectively with MG and enables real-time visualization of its spatial and temporal dynamics in cells and tissues—capabilities that current biochemical assays cannot provide (Zhang et al., 2017). To our knowledge, this is the first application of such an approach in this context, highlighting the potential of a selective fluorescent probe to estimate endogenous MG in situ, despite the semi-quantitative nature of PF6. Biochemical assays such as commercial ELISA kits quantify MG-derived adducts (e.g., MG-H1) rather than free MG. A reaction-based ELISA (ReactELISA) that employs a bifunctional MG-capture probe and a highly specific monoclonal antibody can detect MG in human specimens (Kold-Christensen et al., 2019). Liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry/mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) remains the gold standard for quantifying free MG after derivatization with o-phenylenediamine and the use of 13C3-MG as an internal standard. However, these above biochemical assays are unsuited to dynamic, spatial in vivo monitoring given MG's high reactivity and short half-life since they require derivatization and pre-treatment of samples.

In this study, we established a method to detect and quantify MG in vitro, ex vivo, and in vivo using PF6. In addition, we used PM to study MG production in bone and bone lineage cells under different glucose and temperature conditions using PF6. Finally, we investigated whether MG formation in drill-hole injuries impaired bone repair and explored the effects of continuous and excessive MG formation and PM treatment on osteoporosis in long-standing type 1 diabetic mice.

Materials and methods

Cell culture

Primary osteoblasts were isolated from C57/BL6J neonatal mouse calvariae 3–5 days after birth, as previously described (Ahmad et al., 2018). After isolation, the calvariae were placed in a 1-mL digestion solution containing 0.1 % (w/v) collagenase II (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA, C6885) and dispase II (Fujifilm Wako Pure Chemical Corp., Osaka, Japan, #383-02281) at 37 °C with shaking at 700 rpm. The first supernatant was discarded. Digestion was repeated four more times, and the supernatant was collected in 15-ml falcon tubes containing 500 μL of fetal bovine serum (FBS). Samples were kept on ice throughout the procedure. The collected supernatant was centrifuged at 1,500 rpm for 5 min at room temperature. The cell pellet was resuspended in α-minimum essential medium (α-MEM; Wako Pure Chemical Industries, Osaka, Japan), supplemented with 10 % FBS, 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 μg/mL streptomycin. The cell suspension was plated in 6-well plates (one well per calvaria) and incubated overnight at 37 °C in a 5 % CO₂ incubator. The next day, the cells were washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to remove any non-adherent cells, and the medium was replaced with fresh medium. The cells were allowed to grow for two to three days until they reached approximately 80 % confluence. The cells from each 6-well plate were collected by trypsinization, pooled, and plated in 10 cm dishes. Once the cells reached 80 % confluence, they were used for the experiments. Mouse MC3T3-E1 cells (Riken Cell Bank, Tsukuba, Japan) and the isolated primary osteoblasts were cultured in α-minimum essential medium (α-MEM; Wako Pure Chemical Industries, Osaka, Japan) supplemented with 10 % fetal bovine serum (FBS), 100 U/mL of penicillin, and 100 μg/mL of streptomycin at 37 °C in the presence of 5 % CO2. To induce differentiation, 10 mM β-glycerophosphate, 0.1 μM of dexamethasone, and 50 μM of ascorbic acid were added to the medium.

Primary osteoclast precursors were isolated from the long bones (tibia and femur) of 8–12-week-old C57/BL6J male mice, as previously described (Wittrant et al., 2008). The osteoclast precursors were cultured in 10-cm dishes in α-MEM supplemented with 25 ng/mL macrophage colony-stimulating factor (M-CSF). After 48–72 h, when the attached cells became confluent, the monolayer was washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and the cells were collected in a 15-mL conical tube with the culture medium. The tube was centrifuged at 1,500 rpm for 3 min, the supernatant was discarded, and the pellet was resuspended in 1 mL of culture medium. The numbers of macrophages were estimated using a hemocytometer and seeded in 12-well plates or on a glass-bottom dish at 12 × 103 cells per dish in a 150 μL culture medium supplemented with 25 ng/mL M-CSF. Macrophages were allowed to attach overnight; subsequently, the medium was replaced with culture medium supplemented with M-CSF (25 ng/mL) and receptor activator of nuclear Factor-Kappa B (RANK) ligand (RANKL) (25 ng/mL). The medium was replenished every 48 h. Osteoclasts began to form after 72–96 h. Osteoclasts were defined as TRACP-positive cells containing three or more nuclei.

PF6 probe synthesis and confocal imaging

PF6, a red-emitting iridium (III) complex that serves as a phosphorescent probe for MG, was synthesized according to the developer's protocol (Zhang et al., 2017) and dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide. MC3T3-E1 cells, osteoblasts, and osteoclasts were cultured in glass-bottom dishes to confluence, and then, the medium was replaced with α-MEM containing 5.5 or 25 mM glucose, ±3 mM PM (Sigma–Aldrich, Saint Louis, MO, USA, P9380), 3 μM of PF6. Unless otherwise noted, experiments were performed in medium containing 10 % FBS. The temperature was maintained at 37 °C using a heating pad or at 4 °C using ice. Fluorescence images were acquired at the time points indicated in the figure after PF6 addition, with the exception of the 3 h image, which is unlabeled. Fluorescence imaging was performed on cells cultured in 12-well plates or in glass-bottom dishes using a confocal microscope (Zeiss LSM 980 with Airyscan2; Carl Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany). Nuclei were counterstained with Hoechst® 33342 (Thermo Fisher Diagnostics, Tokyo, Japan) according to the manufacturer's protocol. The settings of the confocal microscope (pinhole, gain, digital offset, and digital gain) were adjusted using cultured cells 3 h after PF6 injection. Imaging data were analyzed using ZEN Blue and ImageJ as previously reported (Schneider et al., 2012).

To analyze the location of PF6 in cellular organelles, MC3T3-E1 cells and osteoclasts were cultured in glass-bottom dishes and transfected with Mito-GFP BacMam 2.0 (C10600, CellLight), ER-GFP BacMam 2.0 (C10590, CellLight), Golgi-GFP BacMam 2.0 (C10592, CellLight) by direct addition to the culture medium for 24 h according to the manufacturer's protocol.

PF6 cell lysis assay

The MC3T3-E1 cells, osteoblasts, and osteoclasts were cultured in 12-well dishes to confluence. For evaluation of exogenous MG, the medium was replaced with α-MEM containing MG (Sigma–Aldrich, M0252) as concentration 200, 400, 600, and 800 μM. For detecting endogenous MG, the medium was replaced with α-MEM containing 5.5 or 25 mM glucose, ±3 mM PM, 3 μM of PF6. Three hours after replacing the medium, the cells were washed twice with PBS and lysed in 1 % NP-40 cell lysis buffer. Absorption (wavelength, 595 nm) was measured using a PerkinElmer Lambda 35 UV–vis spectrometer (PerkinElmer, U.S. LLC, Shelton, CT, USA). Fluorescence intensity was measured in a fluorescence multi-well plate reader (TriStar LB 941, Berthold, Germany) with excitation and emission wavelengths of 405 and 630 nm, respectively. Protein concentrations in the lysates were determined using the BCA assay (Thermo Fisher Diagnostics) according to the manufacturer's protocol and were used for normalization.

Alkaline phosphatase (ALP) and TRACP-5b activity assays

ALP activity in MC3T3-E1 cells and primary osteoblasts was determined 7 days after the induction of osteoblastic differentiation using a TRACP & ALP Assay Kit (TaKaRa Bio, Otsu, Japan) or TRACP & ALP double-stain kit (Fujifilm Wako Pure Chemical Corp., Osaka, Japan), according to the manufacturer's protocol.

TRACP-5b activity in primary osteoclasts was determined 3 days after the induction of osteoclastic differentiation using the TRACP & ALP Assay Kit or TRACP & ALP double-staining kit, according to the manufacturer's protocol. MG and TRAP staining were performed in separate wells.

Calvariae and tibiae culture ex vivo

Calvariae were dissected from C57/BL6J neonatal mice (Japan SLC, Shizuoka, Japan) at 3–5 days after birth. Fluorescence imaging was performed on calvariae in glass-bottomed dishes using a confocal microscope at the time points indicated in the figure after 3 μM PF6 addition. Tibiae were dissected from 12-week-old C57/BL6J mice. The calvariae and tibiae were cultured in α-MEM supplemented with 10 % FBS, 100 U/mL of penicillin, and 100 μg/mL of streptomycin at 37 °C in the presence of 5 % CO2, and 5.5 or 25 mM glucose, ±3 mM PM and 3 μM of PF6 were added. Three hours after the addition of PF6, the calvariae and tibiae were crushed using scissors and subjected to confocal microscopy and absorption measurements.

Diabetic mice with or without treatment of PM or insulin

Seven-week-old male C57BL/6J mice (weight: 21 g) were purchased from Charles River Japan (Yokohama, Japan) and acclimatized for 1 week prior to the start of the experiment. To induce T1DM, mice were intraperitoneally injected with streptozotocin (STZ; Sigma–Aldrich, Saint Louis, MO, USA, S0130, STZ; 100 mg/kg body weight) twice on day 0 and 2 with a modification of a previous study (Nørgaard et al., 2020). Sodium citrate buffer was injected into the control mice (n = 6). The experimental subjects were divided into three or four groups: a control group; a T1DM group (STZ); a T1DM group that received PM treatment (STZ + PM); and a T1DM group that received insulin treatment (STZ + Ins). Non-fasting blood glucose levels in whole blood obtained from the tail vein were measured using a GLUCOCARD G + Meter (Arkray, Kyoto, Japan). Mice with blood glucose levels greater than 500 mg/dL and HbA1c levels greater than 7.0 % were considered diabetic. We determined the HbA1c levels using the Siemens DCA Vantage Analyzer (Siemens Healthcare, Erlangen, Germany).

The STZ-injected mice were categorized by the duration of their T1DM. Those with T1DM for approximately 9 weeks were considered to be in the early stage (Fig. 4A), while those with T1DM for approximately 18 weeks were considered to be in the long-standing stage (Fig. 5A). In the early-stage model, PM administration was initiated at the time of drill-hole injury to evaluate its therapeutic effects on bone healing. In contrast, in the long-standing (18-week) model, PM administration began immediately after hyperglycemia was confirmed, to evaluate its preventive effects on T1DM-induced bone loss.

The mice were maintained under standard cage conditions (24 °C, 12 h light/dark cycle, lights on at 8:45 A.M.) with sawdust bedding and free access to food and water.

Induction of a drill-hole injury in the mouse femur

All experimental mice were anesthetized using isoflurane in the prone position. A lateral skin incision was made, and the muscle was retracted as gently as possible without inducing bleeding. A drill-hole injury was induced by inserting a drill bit with a 0.8-mm diameter fixed to a finger handle in the medial portion of the diaphysis of the femur, 4-mm above the knee joint. The substantial discrepancy was not observed at the level of the drill hole (Supplementary Fig. 1). The hole was drilled through the collateral cortical bone and bone marrow, resulting in a round defect of ~1.0 mm in diameter. The wells were rinsed with PBS to prevent thermal necrosis in the margins. The incised skin was sutured in a sterile manner and anesthesia was discontinued. During surgery, the body temperature was maintained at 37 °C using a heating pad.

3 or 10 days after the drill-hole injury, the mice were anesthetized with isoflurane, and a lateral skin re-incision was made. Excess muscle tissue was removed and the tissue around the drill hole in the right femur was harvested using a 21-G needle. The tissue was then placed in NP-40 lysis buffer. Lysates were utilized for the execution of ALP/TRACP, PF6, and BCA assays.

Computed tomography (CT) scanning

Ten days after drill-hole injury, mice were sacrificed, and the left femurs were scanned using an X-ray CT system (LaTheta LCT-200; Hitachi Aloka Medical, Tokyo, Japan) with a voxel size of 24 μm, tube voltage of 80 kVp, and tube current of 0.5 mA. Three-dimensional and axial images of the tibia at 5 mm distal to the femoral–tibial joint were acquired as DICOM files using Synapse Vincent software (Fujifilm, Tokyo, Japan) and processed using multiplanar reconstruction in oblique coronal planes, maintaining the working axes parallel to the centerline of the bone defect. The region of interest (ROI) was set at the mid-diaphysis of the femur. Cortical bone was segmented from surrounding tissues using the manufacturer's analysis software (LaTheta V3.51), and trabecular bone mineral density (Tb.BMD, mg/cm3), cortical tissue mineral density (Ct.TMD mg/cm3), cortical thickness (Ct.Th, μm) were automatically calculated using the built-in calibration of the LaTheta system. Trabecular bone architecture, including bone volume/total volume (BV/TV, %), trabecular thickness (Tb.Th, mm), trabecular separation (Tb.Sp, mm), and trabecular number (Tb.N, 1/mm), was quantified using the BoneJ plugin for ImageJ (Doube et al., 2010). The CT values at the area of the bone defect were calculated 10 days after the injury.

Histopathological examination

Following X-ray CT imaging, the left femur was extracted and fixed in 4 % paraformaldehyde 10 days after the drill-hole injury. After 12 h of fixation, the specimens were decalcified in 10 % EDTA. Specimens with drill holes were sectioned and embedded in paraffin. Mid-sagittal serial sections (5–7 μm thick) were prepared and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (HE). Histological sections were examined by light microscopy at 200 × magnification.

Total RNA isolation from the tibiae and quantitative reverse transcription PCR (RT-qPCR)

After sacrificing the mice, the right tibiae were collected and frozen in liquid nitrogen. The tissues were ground, crushed in liquid nitrogen, and transferred to a TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific Life Sciences Solutions, Carlsbad, CA, USA). Samples were incubated at 25 °C for 10 min, after which 2-propanol was added. Following centrifugation at 12,000 rpm at 4 °C for 10 min, the supernatant was washed with 70 % ethanol and used for total RNA isolation. The RNA concentration and purity were verified using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The isolated RNA was transcribed into cDNA using PrimeScript™ RT reagent Kit (Perfect Real Time; TaKaRa Bio, Otsu, Japan). Real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) was performed using the Mx3000P Real-time QPCR System (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA) with specific primers (Table 1) and Luna® Universal qPCR Master Mix (NEB, Boston, MA, USA). Amplification data was processed using the 2−ΔΔCt method. Gene expression was normalized to Gapdh.

Statistical analysis

All data are presented as the means ± SEM (standard error of the mean). Data were analyzed using the GraphPad Prism software (version 9.0; GraphPad Prism, San Diego, CA, USA). Conformity to normal distribution was evaluated using the Shapiro–Wilk test. P-values were calculated using a two-tailed Student's t-test for pairwise comparisons or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) or Kruskal-Wails test, followed by post hoc tests. To evaluate differences between all group combinations, Bonferroni's multiple comparison test was employed. Additionally, Dunnett's test was performed to compare each experimental group directly against the control group. Pearson's correlation coefficients were calculated. Effect sizes were calculated using epsilon squared (ε2), eta squared (η2), and partial eta squared (ηp2). Cohen's f values were derived from ε2, η2, and ηp2, and used to perform post hoc power analyses with G*Power (version 3.1, Heinrich Heine University, Düsseldorf, Germany) to estimate the achieved statistical power (1 – β) for significant results. The significance level was set at p < 0.05. Significant differences were detected with a post hoc power of ≥80 %.

Results

Endogenous MG formation in MC3T3-E1 cells and primary osteoblasts in vitro

To evaluate its detectability using PF6, we first measured the fluorescence intensity of PF6 in MC3T3-E1 cells exposed to various concentrations of exogenous MG. Subsequent to a 3-h exposure to PF6, a marked enhancement in the fluorescence intensity of the cells was observed, exhibiting a direct correlation with the concentration of exogenous MG. At concentrations exceeding 400 μM, a plateau phase was attained (Fig. 1A). This outcome substantiated the efficacy of PF6 in the detection of MG. However, this plateau also indicates that PF6 provides only semi-quantitative information at higher MG concentrations, and thus the fluorescence values in this study were interpreted as relative changes rather than absolute concentrations. Subsequently, we proceeded to assess the levels of endogenous MG in MC3T3-E1 cells. The result indicated the efficacy of PF6 in the detection of MG. Next, we assessed endogenous MG levels in MC3T3-E1 cells. Cells were cultured in a medium supplemented with 5.5 mM glucose for a period of 3 h and then exposed to PF6. The fluorescence intensity of PF6, measured at various time points ranging from 0 to 10 min, exhibited a time-dependent increase, suggesting an accumulation of MG within the cytoplasm of MC3T3-E1 cells (Fig. 1B). Furthermore, an evaluation was conducted to ascertain the distribution and localization of PF6 in organelles. This evaluation revealed the presence of signals in the mitochondria (Mit), endoplasmic reticulum (ER), and Golgi apparatus (Golgi) (Fig. 1C).

Given the established role of the glycolytic pathway in the production of endogenous MG (Brownlee, 2001), the present study examined the effects of varying temperatures and glycemic conditions on endogenous MG levels. Furthermore, the levels of MG present in cells cultured with 25 mM glucose were found to be significantly higher in comparison to those cultured with 5.5 mM glucose (Fig. 1D–G). The confocal images and fluorescence intensity values were normalized to protein concentrations in the lysates of MC3T3-E1 cells incubated with PF6. This analysis revealed a significant reduction in the accumulation of MG in cells grown at 4 °C compared with those grown at 37 °C in the 25 mM glucose-treated group (Fig. 1D, E). Furthermore, co-treatment with PM for 3 h reduced endogenous MG levels in the 25 mM glucose-treated group (Fig. 1F, G).

Subsequently, the impact of endogenous MG on osteoblastic function was assessed utilizing primary osteoblasts. The PF6 fluorescence intensity exhibited a marked increase in osteoblasts that were differentiated in the presence of 25 mM glucose for a duration of 7 days, in comparison to those differentiated in the presence of 5.5 mM glucose (Fig. 1H). The application of PM treatment led to a substantial suppression of the fluorescence intensity in the 25 mM glucose group (Fig. 1H). Therefore, we proceeded to investigate osteoblastic differentiation in further detail. As shown in Fig. 1I, there was a marked decrease in ALP activity in the presence of 25 mM glucose compared with 5.5 mM glucose. This decline was restored by PM treatment.

Endogenous MG formation in osteoclasts in vitro

Next, we investigated whether osteoclasts exhibit a similar pattern of MG accumulation under hyperglycemic conditions. To accomplish this, we performed confocal microscopy on multinucleated osteoclasts that were differentiated in vitro for five days and stained with the PF6 probe. The time-dependent signals derived from the probe (from 0 to 10 min) accumulated in the osteoclasts incubated at 37 °C in a medium supplemented with 5.5 mM glucose (Fig. 2A). As demonstrated in Fig. 2B, the localization of PF6 fluorescence was identified in the mitochondria (Mit), the endoplasmic reticulum (ER), and the Golgi apparatus (Golgi). The study demonstrated a significant increase in the fluorescence intensity of osteoclasts incubated with PF6 at 25 mM glucose at 37 °C, in comparison to the levels observed at 5.5 mM glucose at the same temperature (Fig. 2C–F). Incubation at 4 °C led to a significant attenuation of these fluorescence signals (Fig. 2C, D). Furthermore, PM treatment resulted in a significant reduction in PF6 fluorescence signals in osteoclasts exposed to both 25 mM glucose and 5.5 mM glucose (Fig. 2E, F). Furthermore, the degree of osteoclast differentiation level was evaluated based on TRACP-5b activity and the presence of TRACP-positive osteoclasts. The results of Fig. 2G and H indicate a decline in both TRACP-5b activity and the number of TRACP-5b-positive cells in the presence of 25 mM glucose. In addition, PM treatment resulted in a significant restoration of osteoclast differentiation levels, even in the presence of 25 mM glucose (Fig. 2G, H).

To validate MG accumulation in intact bone tissues, we used ex vivo calvariae and tibiae cultures to examine MG levels under different conditions. In this condition, PM treatment significantly inhibited increasing intensity (Fig. 3B, C). The fluorescence intensity of calvariae and tibiae increased significantly under 25 mM of glucose, when compared with 5.5 mM of glucose (Fig. 3D, E). Liquid nitrogen (LIN)-exposed calvariae were utilized as a negative control for complete cessation of cell metabolism (Fig. 3D). Furthermore, the increase was considerably mitigated by PM treatment ex vivo (Fig. 3E).

Effects of PM treatment on bone defect repair in the early-stage of diabetic mice

We examined whether post-injury treatment with PM could reverse delayed bone healing in diabetic mice during the early stages of disease progression. In this 9-week-STZ model, PM was administered from the time of injury; see Fig. 4A for the protocol. A comparison of the body weight of diabetic mice with that of non-diabetic control mice revealed a significant decrease in body weight in the diabetic mice (26.9 ± 0.6 vs. 21.2 ± 0.5 g). Additionally, the levels of non-fasting blood glucose in diabetic mice were significantly higher than those in non-diabetic control mice (150 ± 3 vs. 575 ± 24 mg/dL). Similarly, the HbA1c levels in diabetic mice were higher than those in non-diabetic control mice (4.1 ± 0.0 vs. 10 ± 0.2) at 54 days after STZ injection (Fig. 4B). Following confirmation of diabetic symptoms, STZ-induced diabetic mice were randomly divided into STZ groups with or without PM supplementation (500 mg/L) in drinking water. A drill hole injury measuring 0.8 mm in diameter was introduced into the medial part of the diaphysis of the right femur, located 4-mm above the knee joint, to assess bone injury recovery. The administration of PM treatment was initiated subsequent to the induction of injury. The healing process in the bone defect area was assessed by means of a CT 10 days after the injury. Delayed repair of the damaged site in the diaphysis of the right femur was observed in the diabetic mice (Fig. 4C). Bone union was observed to be delayed in the STZ group, and the bones in the STZ + PM group exhibited increased calcification compared to those in the STZ group (Fig. 4C). The CT value at the drill hole site in the axial view decreased in the STZ group and was restored by PM treatment, indicating the beneficial effects of PM treatment (Fig. 4C, D). A moderate negative correlation was identified between CT values and PF6 fluorescence intensity in the harvested tissue at the site of the drill hole injury (Fig. 4D), suggesting an effect of endogenous MG formation on bone healing (p < 0.05, r = −0.58). An examination of PF6 fluorescence intensity on day 3 after injury revealed that the STZ group exhibited an increase in fluorescence intensity in comparison to the control group. This increase was not counterbalanced by the effect of PM treatment. However, on day 10, the increase in PF6 fluorescence intensity in the STZ group was significantly attenuated by PM treatment (Fig. 4E). Subsequently, we examined the function of osteoblasts and osteoclasts as markers of ALP and TRACP-5b (TRACP) activity, respectively, injured tissues. On day 3, the activity of ALP was minimal in all groups. On day 10, there was a substantial increase in the control group, while it remained low in the STZ group. In contrast, there was a significant increase in the STZ + PM group, which was analogous to the control group (Fig. 4F). TRACP activity levels were found to be considerably elevated in both the STZ and STZ + PM groups in comparison to the non-diabetic control group on day 3. However, a significant decrease in TRACP activity was observed on day 10 following PM treatment (Fig. 4F).

Effects of PM treatment on bone quality and defect repair in long-standing diabetic mice

To assess the long-term impact of hyperglycemia and the potential preventive effects of PM or insulin, we analyzed bone structure and gene expression in diabetic mice 18 weeks after STZ injection. In this model (Fig. 5A), PM or insulin treatment was initiated after confirmation of sustained hyperglycemia. Following confirmation of overt T1DM, STZ-induced diabetic mice were randomly divided into three groups: The STZ group did not receive any treatment [STZ(+) PM(−) Ins(−)].The STZ + PM group was administered water with PM (500 mg/L) supplementation [STZ (+) PM (+) Ins (−)], while the STZ + Ins group received an insulin injection to regulate blood glucose levels [STZ (+) PM (−) Ins (+)]. On day 124 after the initial STZ injection, the mice were sacrificed, and the bone parameters of the femur were assessed by means of CT scanning.

In comparison with non-diabetic control mice [STZ (−) PM (−) Ins (−)], diabetic mice [STZ (+) PM (−) Ins (−)] demonstrated a substantial reduction in body weight (27.4 ± 0.7 vs. 21.5 ± 0.4 g) and a considerable increase in non-fasting blood glucose levels (136 ± 4.8 vs. 576 ± 10.2 mg/dL) 2 weeks after STZ injection (Fig. 5B, C). Following the grouping process, the administration of insulin treatment resulted in a substantial yet incomplete resolution of elevated blood glucose levels and the loss of body mass during the observation period (Fig. 5B, C). Hyperglycemia and body weight changes were not affected by PM treatment compared with those in the STZ group (Fig. 5B, C). CT imaging of the tibiae on day 124 after STZ injection demonstrated the presence of osteoporotic changes in the STZ group, which was reversed by PM and insulin treatments (Fig. 5D). Subsequently, Tb.BMD, Ct.TMD, Ct.Th, and BV/TV of the femur were assessed. A comparative analysis revealed that all indices exhibited a significant decrease in the STZ group compared to the control group. However, these indices demonstrated a significant increase following insulin treatment (Fig. 6A). A significant recovery in Ct.TMD, Ct.Th, BV/TV, Tb.Th, and Tb.Sp were observed in the PM treatment group in comparison to the STZ group. Treatment with insulin exhibited a more pronounced effect in improving osteoporotic changes in comparison to PM treatment (Fig. 6A). The level of PF6 fluorescence intensity in blood was significantly higher in the STZ group than in the control group. This enhancement exhibited a decline in both the STZ + PM group and the STZ + Ins group. Furthermore, a negative correlation was identified between the intensity and both Tb.BMD and Ct.TMD (p < 0.05, r = −0.42, r = −0.37 respectively; Fig. 6B).

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to detect and quantify endogenous MG in real-time and assess its effects on osteoblasts, osteoclasts, and bone parameters in a T1DM context. Endogenous MG is highly reactive; therefore, we opted to use PF6 because it can be easily detected using a fluorescence spectrometer. In previous studies, PF6 has been used to detect MG in cells or Daphnia magna exposed with MG (Zhang et al., 2017). In the present study, we used PF6 to detect endogenous MG at intracellular metabolic concentrations in osteoblasts and osteoclasts in vitro, mouse calvariae and tibiae ex vivo, and defect-site tissues in vivo in mice.

After exposure to MG, the PF6 fluorescence intensity plateaued at concentrations >400 μM, possibly due to cytotoxic action of MG beyond the elimination threshold (Zhang et al., 2017). Consistent with a previous study (Churchill et al., 1944), in the present study, we showed that suppression of the glycolytic pathway by the external control of glycolysis at 4 °C reduced PF6 fluorescence intensity (Fig. 1, Fig. 2). Furthermore, our findings showed that endogenous MG non-specifically accumulated in different organelles in the cytoplasm; however, its accumulation in the nuclei was weak (Fig. 1, Fig. 2).

Incubation under high glucose conditions (25 mM) for 3 h increased endogenous MG production (Fig. 1, Fig. 2, Fig. 3). As glucose concentration alters glucose metabolism (Stewart et al., 2013), this result is reasonable and suggests that endogenous MG levels reflect glucose metabolism. Moreover, high-glucose conditions (Cunha et al., 2014; Liu and Jiang, 2017; Chen et al., 2019) and external MG administration (Ugaji et al., 2021; Aikawa et al., 2017) decrease osteoblast function, mineralization, and proliferation. The present study also showed that high glucose decreased ALP activity, indicating that an increase in endogenous MG levels leads to a decrease in a part of osteoblastic and osteoclastic functions under high-glucose conditions (Fig. 1, Fig. 2). The lack of upregulation of Runx2 and Osterix by RT-qPCR suggests that increased MG levels inhibit the osteoblasts' ability to promote mineralization. Furthermore, we found that high glucose decreased TRACP-5b, a common clinical marker of osteoclast activity, in the presence of 25 μM of RANKL, indicating that excess MG suppressed osteoclast function, at least in vitro (Fig. 2). This finding was supported by a clinical study that reported a decrease in the bone resorption marker, C-terminal cross-linking of collagen in T1DM (Sewing et al., 2022). In contrast, treatment of RAW246.7 cells with a high dose of RANKL (50 μM) upregulates osteoclast activity under hyperglycemic conditions (Wittrant et al., 2008). This difference could be because of the differences in the proliferative cells and the dose of RANKL used in the two studies.

Furthermore, in contrast to our in vitro results, our in vivo results revealed low osteoclast function in the tibiae, but high function at the defect site under hyperglycemic conditions (Fig. 4). Our in vivo results were consistent with those of several previous studies. For instance, Maycas et al. (2017) reported that high glucose levels cause osteoclast precursor recruitment and differentiation via osteocyte-like cells. Dong et al. (2018) reported that high glucose increased osteoclast proliferation at 24 h but decreased osteoclast differentiation and function 7–9 days later. Kawatani et al. reported the inhibition of osteoclastogenesis upon Glo1 knockout, which codes for glyoxalase 1 and reduces MG production (Kawatani et al., 2008). The differences observed in our in vitro and in vivo studies and other studies could be due to the presence of various cell types at the defect sites. Furthermore, high Rankl/Opg expression in the tibiae of diabetic mice could be attributed to differences in osteoclast precursor recruitment and cell interactions (Fig. 7). In vitro, high-glucose conditions suppressed TRACP activity in differentiated osteoclasts, and pyridoxamine (PM) restored this activity (Fig. 2G). By contrast, in the T1DM in vivo setting, bone resorption is intrinsically enhanced owing to insulin deficiency, pro-inflammatory cytokines, and shifts in the RANKL/OPG axis (Hie et al., 2007; Kalaitzoglou et al., 2016; Karalazou et al., 2019). We therefore interpret the in vivo effect of PM as normalization of excessive osteoclastic activity toward physiological levels. Thus, although TRACP readouts differ between the two settings, both sets of data are consistent with PM acting to normalize osteoclast function in a context-dependent manner. These considerations explain the apparent discrepancy between in vitro and in vivo osteoclast TRACP activity. Our data further suggest that endogenous MG suppresses osteoblastic function more than osteoclastic function, and that PM restores osteoblastic function while normalizing osteoclastic activity (Hie et al., 2007; Kalaitzoglou et al., 2016; Karalazou et al., 2019). The concentration of PM used (500 mg/L in drinking water) was lower than in some previous studies, but the actual intake in polyuric T1DM mice (~540 mg/kg/day) was considered sufficient to elicit therapeutic effects (Tanimoto et al., 2007; Degenhardt et al., 2002b).

The ex vivo experiments conducted using calvariae from new pups, which are highly permeable and thin, demonstrated that the real-time MG levels varied according to glucose and temperature conditions, indicating that cell metabolism in the bone affects endogenous MG levels (Fig. 3). Furthermore, PM reduced endogenous MG levels in bone tissues (Fig. 3). Consistent with this finding, previous studies have shown that impaired bone defect healing under glycative stress can be reversed by PM in mice with STZ-induced T1DM (Ugaji et al., 2021; Aikawa et al., 2017). It is also plausible that PM traps reactive carbonyl species other than MG, which may contribute to its beneficial effects on bone-defect healing in diabetes (Voziyan et al., 2002; Chetyrkin et al., 2008; Lee et al., 2018).

We assessed the effect of glycative stress on bone quality in mice with STZ-induced hyperglycemia. We observed reduced bone healing and osteoblast function on day 10 after treatment with STZ, whereas treatment with PM after defect induction prevented the delay in bone healing (Fig. 4). Moreover, the negative correlation between the CT values and PF6 fluorescence intensity showed that MG was directly related to impaired defect healing, which was subsequently reversed by PM (Fig. 4). Osteoclast function was high, with high PF6 values in STZ group at the defect site but not in STZ + PM group, which can be explained by OPG (Fig. 5, Fig. 6, Fig. 7). In addition, we noted that the PM-treated group exhibited a transiently higher FL intensity on day 3 after the drill-hole injury (Fig. 4E). We interpret this as a reflection of accelerated initiation of bone remodeling: normalization of the RANKL/OPG axis may transiently increase metabolic activity and MG formation before the scavenging effects of PM become dominant (Nishida et al., 2021). Although probe-related artifacts or local variability cannot be entirely excluded, this transient surge appears consistent with an earlier onset of remodeling in PM-treated mice. Cawley et al. (2020) reported that local OPG production by osteoblasts suppresses bone resorption; therefore, PM may initially increase osteoblast function and suppress OPG-induced osteoclast function. Preliminary experiments revealed that endogenous MG levels significantly increased immediately and 2 days after defect compared with that at 3 days after defect. To avoid the effect of defect, we evaluated the treatment effects on day 3. Stress effects caused by defect, such as acid accumulation, may also induce MG production. Treatment effects were also evaluated on day 10 when the defect started to heal in control mice.

STZ-treated mice lose pancreatic beta cell insulin secretion and were used as a T1DM mouse model. Bone parameters in STZ-induced diabetic mice at 16 weeks may represent the characteristics of long-standing T1DM, because the mouse lifespan is around 2 years. Tb.BMD, Ct.TMD and cortical thickness were lower in the STZ group than in the other groups at 16 weeks (Fig. 6). Serum MG levels were higher in the STZ group than in the other groups and negatively correlated with Tb.BMD and Ct.TMD, indicating that increased endogenous MG levels in mice weakened bone remodeling and caused osteoporosis (Fig. 6). Insulin suppressed osteoporosis more strongly than PM and suppressed high glucose levels (Fig. 5, Fig. 6). These results are consistent with prior reports that, in well-controlled, long-standing T1DM, lower HbA1c levels are associated with higher BMD (Sewing et al., 2022). In contrast, PM did not affect glucose levels but prevented osteoporosis, especially in the cortical bone (Fig. 5, Fig. 6). Shah et al. reported that young-onset T1DM is associated with cortical bone-size deficits (Shah et al., 2019). Therefore, MG causes osteoporosis, especially in the cortical bone, via osteoblastic dysfunction, and a combination of insulin and PM may synergistically prevent osteoporosis.

As we could not evaluate endogenous MG levels in the bone non-invasively, tissues collected at the drill hole injury site were evaluated using PF6 (Figs. 4E and 6). Pathological and CT images showed that the injury site was filled with trabecular bone material on day 10 (Fig. 5). CT values were more strongly reduced in long-standing diabetic mice than in early-stage diabetic mice, indicating that the longer the disease duration, the lower the bone repair capacity (Fig. 4, Fig. 5). Preventive use of PM or insulin improved delayed bone healing but did not significantly decrease MG levels despite the downward trend (Fig. 6). The results highlight the limitations of using the PF6 assay to assess the chronic effects of T1DM because PF6 reflects cell metabolism. The combined use of insulin and PM may also be beneficial for prevention.

RT-qPCR revealed a decreased expression of Oc, in STZ-treated mice, whereas PM prevented these changes (Fig. 7). This suggests that continued high-glucose conditions lead to MG overproduction and decreased osteoblastic function. Rankl/Opg expression was high in STZ-treated mice, whereas Acp5 and Ctsk expression were not significantly different, likely because high endogenous MG levels downregulate osteoclastogenic factors in the cytoplasm (Fig. 7). These data cause the discrepancy of the osteoclast function between in vitro and in vivo. Especially The results are inconsistent in vivo. It may be explained by cell-cell interaction. One possible explanation is that Rankl/Opg expression ratio was high in STZ, but high level of MG suppressed osteoclast function. In particular, the osteoblastic marker gene, Oc, showed a five-fold lower expression in STZ group than in STZ + PM group (Fig. 7). Our data suggests that endogenous MG suppressed osteoblastic function more than osteoclastic function and PM recovered osteoblastic function. The average blood glucose level of mice is 500 mg/dL in the previous studies (Sewing et al., 2022; Ham et al., 2019); however, in the present study, it was >600 mg/dL, and tibiae were used for RT-qPCR. This may explain the variation in gene expression levels between the present study and previous studies (Sewing et al., 2022; Ham et al., 2019) such as Acp5 and Ctsk expression level. Therefore, our data suggests the potential of the longstanding elevated level of MG suppressed osteoblast function. Future studies should conduct a comprehensive analysis of bone quality, including assessment of the bone volume/tissue volume ratio using micro-CT and bone stiffness. In addition, a method for the real-time detection of MG in bone in vivo should be established.

Long-term PM treatment prevents delays in bone defect healing; however, the effect of increased MG levels is limited. MG is highly reactive; therefore, evaluation of long-term PM use may not be suitable. In addition, MG reflects cell metabolism as demonstrated by the external control of glycolysis. In the present study, we observed that PM, an MG scavenger that forms an MG-lysine dimer (Voziyan and Hudson, 2005), effectively reduced endogenous MG levels for 3 h and restored cell function under high glucose conditions. This indicated that MG did not have negative effects as long as it was removed, highlighting the importance of cell metabolism. Wetzels et al. reported that overexpression of Glo1 or PM treatment did not decrease MG levels in the serum or spinal cord, which could also be attributed to the upregulation of glucose metabolism in mice (Wetzels et al., 2018). Therefore, MG is primarily a target for acute injury therapy than for chronic injury therapy. Our study showed that low temperatures suppressed endogenous MG but suppressed glycemic metabolism. Based on the findings of the present study, cooling the defect site during acute injury, particularly when MG levels are elevated, may promote bone defect healing. MG, a byproduct of glycolysis, should be scavenged, and MG monitoring may guide proper treatment with regard to glycemic control. AGEs accumulate when MG is overproduced and may be a more suitable target for the evaluation of long-term treatment.

In conclusion, the present study revealed that an increase in endogenous MG inhibited the differentiation of osteoblasts and osteoclasts, possibly resulting in the loss of bone quality and delayed bone healing. PM, or more effective MG scavengers, may improve bone quality and repair in patients with T1DM. A major limitation of this study is that it was conducted in mice. Bone metabolism in mice partially differs from that in humans. A minor limitation is that no evaluation of function of bone formation and resorption. While the use of a murine model presents inherent limitations due to interspecies differences in bone metabolism, the findings of this study offer mechanistic insights that may guide future investigations and the development of therapeutic strategies for diabetic bone disorders in humans. Considering the increased risk of postmenopausal osteoporosis in humans, studies using female mice will be required in future work. We acknowledge that future work should aim to correlate PF6-based observations with quantitative biochemical measurements of MG (Rabbani and Thornalley, 2014).
