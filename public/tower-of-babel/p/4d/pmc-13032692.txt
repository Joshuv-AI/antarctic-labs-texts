Activation of aryl hydrocarbon receptor alleviates cholestatic liver injury by inhibiting inflammation
Authors: Qi Han, Likai Wang, Xuzhen Yan, Ning Zhang, Wen Zhang, Hong Li, Wei Chen, Hong You, Aiting Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cholestatic liver injury (CLI) is a severe liver disorder caused by impaired bile flow, for which effective therapeutic options remain limited. Although the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AHR) has been implicated in protection against CLI, the mechanisms underlying its dysregulation and functional role during cholestasis remain incompletely understood. Here, we investigated the role of AHR in cholestatic liver injury and the mechanisms contributing to impaired AHR activation using multi-omics approaches. Using mouse models of CLI induced by a 0.1% 3,5-diethoxycarbonyl-1,4-dihydrocollidine (DDC) diet or bile duct ligation (BDL), we found that tryptophan metabolism and AHR signaling were markedly disrupted during cholestasis. Pharmacological activation of AHR with the tryptophan-derived agonist (1′H-indole-3′-carbonyl)-thiazole-4-carboxylic acid methyl ester (ITE), as well as AAV-mediated AHR overexpression, significantly attenuated liver injury, as evidenced by reduced body weight loss, decreased porphyrin deposition, and diminished infiltration of macrophages and neutrophils. Transcriptomic analysis revealed that ITE treatment suppressed chemokine and inflammatory gene expression. Mechanistically, activated AHR bound to the CCL2 promoter and repressed its transcription, which was associated with reduced recruitment of inflammatory cells. Integrated metabolomic and 16 S rDNA sequencing analyses further demonstrated that cholestasis was associated with altered tryptophan-metabolizing gut microbiota and reduced availability of endogenous AHR ligands, correlating with impaired AHR activation and aggravated liver injury. Collectively, these findings indicate that restoration of AHR activity by tryptophan-derived metabolites or AAV-mediated AHR overexpression alleviates inflammation and ameliorates cholestatic liver injury, highlighting AHR signaling as a potential therapeutic target in cholestasis associated with disordered tryptophan metabolism.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12964-026-02755-w.

Introduction

Cholestatic liver injury (CLI) results from abnormalities in bile formation, secretion, or excretion, leading to liver damage to hepatocytes and cholangiocytes and excessive accumulation of bile acids [1]. This condition is associated with various diseases, including primary biliary cholangitis (PBC), primary sclerosing cholangitis (PSC), and biliary atresia [2–4]. CLI poses a severe risk for postoperative complications, and if untreated, can lead to liver failure and death [5, 6]. Despite its clinical significance, the molecular mechanisms underlying CLI remain incompletely understood, and effective therapeutic options are limited.

Accumulating evidence indicates that inflammation is a central pathological component of cholestatic liver injury. Inflammatory responses triggered by cholestasis play a critical role in the pathological process [7, 8]. Bile stasis–induced hepatocyte injury leads to the release of multiple inflammatory mediators, including tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), interleukin-6 (IL-6), and monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 (MCP-1), also known as chemokine (C-C motif) ligand 2 (CCL2). These mediators promote the recruitment and activation of intrahepatic immune cells, such as Kupffer cells and neutrophils, through autocrine and paracrine mechanisms, thereby amplifying liver inflammation [8]. Consistent with this notion, several pharmacological agents have been shown to mitigate cholestatic liver injury primarily through anti-inflammatory mechanisms, including scoparone, honokiol, melatonin, and tectorigenin [9–13].

The aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AHR) is a ligand-activated transcription factor initially discovered for its role in dioxin toxicity. Recently, AHR has gained attention for its role in immune regulation and anti-inflammatory responses. AHR exerts its anti-inflammatory effects through interactions with various endogenous (such as tryptophan metabolites) and exogenous ligands, regulating multiple signaling pathways. It has also been reported that AHR plays an important role in alleviating liver fibrosis and alcoholic liver injury. Specifically, AHR has been reported to inhibit hepatic stellate cell activation and attenuate liver fibrosis, as well as to improve redox homeostasis in models of alcoholic liver injury [14–18]. Our previous studies demonstrated that AHR overexpression can alleviate cholestatic liver injury by modulating bile acid metabolism [19], However, given the pleiotropic nature of AHR signaling, whether impaired AHR activation contributes to cholestatic liver injury through inflammatory pathways, particularly chemokine-mediated immune cell recruitment, remains unclear.

In this study, we employed dietary 3,5-diethoxycarbonyl-1,4-dihydrocollidine (DDC) feeding and bile duct ligation (BDL) to establish mouse models of cholestatic liver injury. AHR signaling was enhanced either by adeno-associated virus–mediated AHR overexpression or by pharmacological activation using the endogenous AHR agonist 2-(1′H-indole-3′-carbonyl)-thiazole-4-carboxylic acid methyl ester (ITE) [20]. By integrating transcriptomic profiling, serum metabolomics, and 16 S rDNA-based gut microbiota analysis, we investigated how impaired tryptophan metabolism and gut microbiota dysbiosis are associated with reduced AHR activation and exacerbated inflammation during cholestasis. Our findings identify inflammation—particularly chemokine-driven immune cell recruitment-as a key pathological process modulated by AHR activation and provide an integrative framework linking the gut microbiota–tryptophan–AHR axis to cholestatic liver injury.

Materials and methods

Ethics approval

Overall, C57BL/6J male mice (approximately 6–8 weeks old) were used. Experiments with mice were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Usage Committee of the Beijing Friendship Hospital, Capital Medical University (No. 22–2034). All methods were performed following the relevant guidelines.

Cholestatic liver disease model induction and treatment

To develop a cholestatic liver disease model, male C57BL/6J mice (6–8 weeks old, ~ 20 g; Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) were fed either a normal control diet (NCD; standard AIN-76 A rodent diet) or a 0.1% (w/w) DDC diet for 3 weeks. The DDC diet was prepared based on the AIN-76 A formulation with the addition of 0.1% (w/w) 3,5-diethoxycarbonyl-1,4-dihydrocollidine (DDC; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA; #137030), while all other dietary components and their proportions were identical between the two diets. The 3-week DDC feeding period was selected to induce a stable cholestatic liver injury characterized by body weight loss, bile acid accumulation, and inflammatory cell infiltration, while avoiding excessive systemic toxicity, as previously reported [21].

For ITE treatment experiments, mice were randomized into the following groups: NCD plus DMSO, DDC plus DMSO, and DDC plus ITE (MedChemExpress, Shanghai, China; #HY-19317). Group sizes ranged from n = 5–7, with larger sample sizes used for DDC-fed groups to account for increased biological variability associated with cholestatic injury. ITE (5 mg/kg body weight) or vehicle (DMSO, same volume) was administered via intraperitoneal injection three times per week for 3 weeks starting at the initiation of the diet. Mice were anesthetized with sodium pentobarbital at the end of the treatment period, as it provides stable and controllable anesthesia with minimal interference with hepatic inflammatory and metabolic signaling pathways, and serum, liver, and spleen samples were collected and stored for subsequent analyses.

In addition, a bile duct ligation (BDL)-induced cholestatic liver injury model was established. Male C57BL/6J mice (aged 6–8 weeks) were fasted for 8–12 h prior to surgery and anesthetized as described above. Following a midline laparotomy, the common bile duct was carefully isolated, double-ligated with 5 − 0 surgical sutures, and transected between the ligatures. The abdominal cavity was flushed with sterile saline, and the muscle and skin layers were closed with 5 − 0 sutures. Mice were allowed to recover on a warming pad and monitored daily. Two weeks after BDL surgery, mice were euthanized for sample collection.

For systemic AHR overexpression experiments, BDL mice were randomized into two groups (n = 5): BDL plus AAV6-normal control (AAV6-NC) and BDL plus AAV6-Ahr. AAV6 vectors were produced by OBiO Technology (Shanghai, China) and administered via tail vein injection at a dose of 2 × 10¹¹ viral genomes (vg) per mouse prior to surgery.

Hepatocyte-restricted AHR modulation using AAV8 vectors

Hepatocyte-restricted AHR modulation experiments were performed as supplementary studies to further assess the contribution of hepatocyte-intrinsic AHR signaling to cholestatic liver injury. Male C57BL/6J mice (aged 6–8 weeks) were injected via the tail vein with adeno-associated virus serotype 8 (AAV8) vectors carrying either hepatocyte-specific AHR overexpression driven by the thyroxine-binding globulin promoter (AAV8-ovAhr), hepatocyte-targeted AHR knockdown (AAV8-siAhr), or the corresponding control vectors (AAV8-ovNC and AAV8-siNC).

AAV8 vectors were administered at a dose of 2 × 10¹¹ vg per mouse two weeks prior to initiation of the DDC diet to allow for stable hepatocyte-specific transgene expression. Mice were subsequently fed a 0.1% DDC diet for 3 weeks. Body weight was monitored weekly throughout the experimental period. At the end of the study, mice were euthanized under sodium pentobarbital anesthesia, and blood and liver tissues were collected for serum biochemical analysis, histological evaluation, porphyrin deposition assessment, and gene expression analysis.

These hepatocyte-restricted AAV8-based experiments were designed to evaluate the hepatocyte-intrinsic contribution of AHR signaling under cholestatic conditions and were not intended to replace systemic AHR activation or to capture the full spectrum of multicellular regulatory mechanisms involved in cholestatic liver injury.

Randomization and blinding

To minimize potential confounding factors, animals were randomly assigned to experimental groups, and cage positions were regularly rotated throughout the study. Investigators responsible for animal handling were aware of group allocation, whereas sample processing and data analysis were performed in a blinded manner.

Cell culture and transient transfection

The AML12 murine hepatocytes cell line was obtained from Procell (Wuhan, China, # CL-0602) and cultured in an AML12 cell-specific medium (Wuhan, China, # CM-0602) (DMEM/F12 (PM150312)་10% FBS (164210-50)་0.5% ITS-G(100 × )(PB180429)་40 ng/mL Dexamethasone་1% P/S (PB180120)). Cells were grown in 37 °C with 5% CO2 and 95% humidity. After 12 h of cell starvation, LPS (500 nM, MedChemExpress, Shanghai, China, # HY-D1056) was added, along with ITE (10 nM or 100 nM, MedChemExpress Shanghai, China, # HY-19317), or liposome transfection of AHR overexpression plasmid (1ug/mL, Sino Biological, Beijing, China, # MG50786-CM), and the AHR inhibitor CH223191 (CH) (10 µM, MedChemExpress, Shanghai, China, # HY-12684) was added when necessary. After 24 h, cells were collected for related tests.

To transfect AHR plasmid DNA into AML12 cells using LipoMax DNA Transfection Reagent (SUDGEN, Nanjing, China, # 32012), AML12 cells were grown to 60% confluence in 12-wells plates 24 h prior to the transfection, then start by diluting 1 µg of the DNA in 50 µL of Opti-MEM serum-free medium, ensuring it is well mixed. Next, gently mix the LipoMax reagent, take 2 µL, and dilute it in another 50 µL of Opti-MEM, allowing it to sit at room temperature for 5 min. Combine the diluted DNA and LipoMax solutions to a final volume of 100 µL and mix thoroughly. Let the mixture incubate at room temperature for 20 min to form the DNA-LipoMax complex. Add the 100 µL complex to a well of a 12-well plate containing cells, gently rocking the plate to distribute the complex evenly. Finally, incubate the cells at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator for 24 h before proceeding with further experimental steps.

Serum non-targeted metabolomics analysis

Serum samples from a mouse model of cholestatic liver injury were analyzed using a serum untargeted metabolomics approach, with 5 mice per group (disease and healthy controls). 100 µL of serum was mixed with 300 µL of extraction solvent (methanol: acetonitrile = 1:1 (v: v)) containing internal standards, then vortexed and sonicated at 5 °C for 30 min and allowed to stand at -20 °C for 30 min. After centrifugation at 13,000 g for 15 min at 4 °C, the supernatant was dried under nitrogen and reconstituted in 100 µL of reconstitution solution (acetonitrile: water = 1:1 (v: v)) for LC-MS analysis. Chromatographic separation was performed at 40 °C using a UHPLC-Q Exactive HF-X system (Thermo Fisher Scientific) equipped with an ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 column (100 mm × 2.1 mm i.d., 1.8 μm). Mobile phase A was 95% water + 5% acetonitrile (containing 0.1% formic acid), and mobile phase B was 47.5% acetonitrile + 47.5% isopropanol + 5% water (containing 0.1% formic acid). Mass spectrometry analysis was performed in positive and negative ionization modes with a scan range of 70–1050 m/z. Data were processed using Progenesis QI v3.0 software (Waters Corporation, Milford, USA) for peak extraction, alignment, and normalization, and metabolite identification was then performed using the HMDB and KEGG databases. PCA and OPLS-DA were used to determine the degree of dispersion between sample groups. Univariate statistical analysis (t-test) and fold change values ​​(FC ≥ 1.2 or FC ≤ 0.8) were used to screen differential metabolites together. Significantly altered metabolites were further mapped to biochemical pathways using the KEGG database to explore altered metabolic pathways in cholestatic liver injury, with a focus on tryptophan metabolic pathways.

Western blotting

A partial sample of two lobes of the liver from CLI mouse models (n = 4) or the AML12 cells (n = 3) was collected and a western blotting assay was performed according to the previous method [22]. Total cell lysates were prepared from liver tissues or AML12 cells using cell lysis buffer RIPA (Beyotime, Shanghai, China, # P0013B) containing 1% PMSF (Beyotime, Shanghai, China, # ST506) and homogenized using a vortex shaker (Roche, USA). Lysis was performed on ice for 30 min, with vortexing every 10 min. The homogenate was centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 5 min, and the supernatant was collected for analysis. Protein concentration was determined by the BCA method (Beyotime, Shanghai, China, # P0012). 5× SDS loading buffer (Solarbio, Beijing, China, # P1040) was mixed with protein samples and boiled at 100 °C for 10 min. After separation by 10% SDS-PAGE, the samples were transferred to mixed Immobilon® -NC Nitrocellulose Membrane (Millipore, Massachusetts, USA, #HATF00010) due to its suitability for detecting low-to-medium molecular weight proteins with low background, and then blocked with 5% skim milk powder for 2 h at room temperature. They were then incubated with the respective primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. After incubation with the secondary antibody (1: 10000, Zhongshan Golden Bridge Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China, (anti-rabbit, # ZB2301), (anti-mouse, # ZB2305)) for 1 h, chemiluminescence detection was performed using Meilunbio® Fecte Ultrasensitive ECL Luminescent Solution (Meilun Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Dalian, China, #MA0186). The primary antibody against AHR (1:1000, # 4685) was acquired from Cell Signaling Technology (MA, USA), an antibody against C-MYC (1:5000, # AB1007T) was provided by Boao Rui Jing (Beijing) Technology Development Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China) or GAPDH (ab59164,1:10000) was acquired by Abcam (Cambridge MA, USA). After Western blotting, capture an image of the blot. ImageJ was used to measure the band intensities of AHR and GAPDH. The protein level of AHR is calculated by normalizing the intensity of the AHR band to the intensity of the GAPDH, providing a ratio indicative of the level of the AHR protein.

Serum parameters of liver damage

Blood was collected from mice by cardiac puncture, and the collected blood samples were centrifuged at 1000 g at 4 °C for 20 min to obtain serum. The levels of aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), total bilirubin (TBIL) and total bile acid (TBA) were measured using reagents provided by the manufacturer of the Olympus Beckman Coulter AU480 automatic biochemical analysis system (InTec PRODUCTS, Shenzhen, China).

Measurement of mouse hepatic LPS level

Approximately 100 mg of each frozen liver tissue was collected, 1 mL of PBS with 1% PMSF (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China, # ST506) was added, and the homogenate was prepared by a homogenizer, then centrifuged at 5000 g and 4 ℃ for 10 min to obtain the supernatant. The levels of LPS in the supernatant were determined according to the instructions of the Mouse Lipopolysaccharides (LPS) ELISA Kit (Jonln, shanghai, China, # JL20691). Moreover, the concentrations of LPS in the liver were normalized according to protein levels by the BCA method (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China, # P0012).

Transcriptome analysis

Mouse liver tissues were collected and RNA was extracted (n = 4). According to the instructions, the transcriptome analysis was performed by Shanghai Majorbio Biopharm Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). We determined the differentially expressed genes between CON and DDC and between DDC-ITE and DDC based on P-value<0.05 and fold change ≥ 1.5, and performed Venn diagrams on the differentially expressed genes to determine the protective genes or pathogenic genes, and then performed gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto encyclopedia of genes and genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis on these two types of genes, respectively.

RNA extraction, reverse transcription, and quantitative real time-PCR (qRT-PCR)

For mRNA extraction, total RNA from livers and AML12 cells was extracted using the TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, CA, USA, # 15596026) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The reverse transcription was performed with 1 µg of total RNA using PrimeScript™ RT Master Mix (TaKaRa, Shiga Prefecture, Japan, # RRO36A). SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Roche, Basel, Switzerland, # 4710436001) was used for PCR amplification. The PCR reaction was carried out in triplicate. Relative expression of mRNA was calculated using the 2 − ΔΔCT comparative method, with each gene normalized relative to the GAPDH gene for that sample. The primers used in the experiments are listed in Supplementary Table S1.

Histopathological analysis

Liver samples were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, paraffin-embedded, and sectioned into Sect. (4 μm thick) for hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining and Immunohistochemistry on paraffin-embedded (IHC-P) on tissue sections. For HE staining, sections were stained by applying a hematoxylin-eosin staining kit (Solarbio, Beijing, China, # G1120) following the instructions of the manufacturer. For IHC-P staining, the sections were incubated overnight with primary antibodies (CD68 (1:200, MA5-16654), acquired from Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc. (Waltham, Massachusetts, USA); and LY6G (1:100, # 87048), acquired from Cell Signaling Technology (MA, USA)) and then with Alexa Fluor® 488-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:100, Abcam, Cambridge MA, USA, # ab150077) and DAPI (Abcam, Cambridge MA, USA, # ab104139). Images were obtained using an Olympus FluoView 1000 microscope and FV10-ASW 4.2 Viewer software. Quantification of the images was performed from randomly selected sections of at least five fields of each sample using the Image J 1.45 software (NIH, Bethesda, MD).

16S rDNA amplicon sequencing, data processing, and analysis

Fresh samples of mouse cecal contents were taken, frozen in liquid nitrogen, and then subjected to 16S rDNA sequencing analysis at Shanghai Meijie Biopharmaceutical Technology Co., Ltd. according to the instructions (n = 5). After the PE reads obtained by Illumina sequencing were split into samples, the paired-end reads were first quality-controlled and filtered according to the sequencing quality, and then spliced according to the overlapping relationship between the paired-end reads. After quality-controlled splicing, the optimized data was obtained. Then, the optimized data was processed using sequence noise reduction methods to get an amplicon sequence variant (ASV) representing sequence and abundance information. The microbial dysbiosis index (MDI), alpha diversity (Simpson index), and Bray-Curtis-based principal coordinates analysis (PCoA) were performed on the online platform provided by the company. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to determine whether there were differences in the microbial composition between groups unless otherwise stated (P value < 0.05 was considered to be significantly different). In addition, key bacterial groups were identified by the following analyses: (i) LDA effect size analysis was performed to identify the features most likely to explain the differences between the groups, with an LDA score threshold of ≥ 4 being considered a significant contributor to the model; and (ii) Pearson correlation coefficient analysis was performed between the relative abundance of selected bacterial genera and serological indicators (ALT, AST, TBIL, ALP, and TBA), liver LPS, and the content of chemokines and receptors.

Chip assay

AML12 cells were plated in a 10 cm2 culture plate. After incubation with the AHR agonist ITE (100 nM, MedChemExpress, Shanghai, China# HY-19317) for 12 h, the cells were collected, and a ChIP assay was performed using a SimpleChIP Enzymatic Chromatin Immunoprecipitation Kit (Cell Signaling Technology, MA, USA, # 9002) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The primers used for real-time PCR analysis of the DNA fragments of interest among the immunoprecipitated DNA fragments are listed in Supporting Table S1.

Statistical analysis

Data were presented as mean ± SEM and later analyzed via employing GraphPad Prism software 9.00 (La Jolla, CA). Comparisons between two groups were performed using a two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-test, and comparisons of more than two groups were performed using an ANOVA followed by Dunnett’s multiple comparisons test. P-values <0.05 were viewed as statistically significant.

Results

Altered AHR signaling and tryptophan metabolism were found in the 0.1% DDC-diet mouse model

To explore the role of AHR in cholestatic liver injury, we found that a 0.1% DDC diet significantly decreased AHR expression (Fig. 1A-B). Most tryptophan metabolites are ligands of AHR, so we conducted untargeted serum metabolomics analysis on serum samples from 0.1% DDC-fed mice and NCD-fed mice. PCA and OPLS-DA showed clustering within each group and significant separation between the two groups (Fig. 1C-E). The volcano plot results showed that compared with the NCD group, there were 476 up-regulated metabolites and 201 down-regulated metabolites in the serum of the DDC group (Fig. 1F). The heat map results showed significant changes in serum metabolite profiles of the DDC model and NCD mice (Fig. 1G). KEGG enrichment analysis further identified the top ten altered metabolic pathways in the DDC model, among which tryptophan metabolism ranked third (Fig. 1H), highlighting the changes in each metabolite in the tryptophan metabolism pathway (Fig. 1I). These findings suggest that altered tryptophan metabolism may be associated with impaired AHR activation in cholestatic liver injury.

ITE attenuated weight loss and reduced liver injury in DDC-diet mouse model

ITE, an endogenous AHR agonist derived from tryptophan metabolism, is widely used as a non-toxic pharmacological activator of AHR. In this study, ITE was administered via intraperitoneal injection concomitantly with induction of cholestatic liver injury using a 0.1% DDC diet for three weeks (Fig. 2A). The DDC diet caused a marked increase in the relative weights of the spleen and liver, which was not significantly altered by ITE treatment (Fig. S1). However, ITE partially attenuated DDC-induced body weight loss (Fig. 2B). Consistently, ITE treatment markedly alleviated liver injury and the cholestatic phenotype, as evidenced by reduced serum ALT, AST, TBIL, ALP, and TBA levels (Fig. 2C–D). In addition, ITE treatment reduced hepatic porphyrin deposition, as indicated by yellow arrows (Fig. 2E–F). Collectively, these results demonstrate that systemic activation of AHR by ITE ameliorates DDC-induced weight loss and liver injury, consistent with an improvement in cholestatic liver injury. Consistent with these findings, our previous work demonstrated that AHR overexpression partially rescued DDC-induced body weight loss and liver injury [19]. In the present study, we further validated the hepatoprotective role of AHR using an independent bile duct ligation (BDL) model, in which AHR overexpression also alleviated liver injury (Fig. S2).

To further delineate the cellular basis underlying the above observations, we next examined whether hepatocyte-intrinsic AHR signaling alone is sufficient to account for the protective effects observed upon systemic AHR activation. Using AAV8-based hepatocyte-restricted approaches, we selectively modulated AHR expression in hepatocytes in vivo. Hepatocyte-specific AHR overexpression resulted in a modest attenuation of DDC-induced cholestatic liver injury, as reflected by partial improvements in body weight change, serum biochemical parameters, and liver histology (Fig. S3). Notably, this protective effect was clearly less pronounced than that observed with systemic AHR activation by ITE. Conversely, hepatocyte-enriched AHR knockdown using AAV8-siAHR did not exacerbate DDC-induced cholestatic liver injury under the experimental conditions tested (Fig. S4). Together, these data suggest that while hepatocyte-intrinsic AHR signaling contributes to the hepatoprotective effects of AHR activation, it is not sufficient to fully recapitulate the beneficial effects observed with systemic AHR activation in cholestatic liver injury.

Transcriptomic analysis identifies ITE reduces inflammation by regulating chemokine expression

To investigated the mechanism of ITE in alleviating cholestatic liver injury, we first detected the expression of genes related to bile duct proliferation and bile acid metabolism, the results showed that ITE did not affect the expression of these genes (Fig. S5). Thus we performed transcriptomic sequencing analysis on the liver from three groups. Compared to the control group, the DDC group had 4015 upregulated and 2684 downregulated genes, while the DDC-ITE group had 423 upregulated and 498 downregulated genes compared to the DDC group (Fig. 3A-B). The combined analysis identified 350 pathogenic genes upregulated in the DDC group, downregulated in the DDC-ITE group, and 184 protective genes downregulated in the DDC group, upregulated in the DDC-ITE group (Fig. 3C). GO and KEGG enrichment analysis revealed that inflammation-related pathways were significantly enriched in the DDC group (Fig. 3D-E). Specifically, ITE treatment reduced the DDC-induced high expression of chemokine Ccl2/6/24 (Fig. 3F) and chemokine receptor Cxcr2 (Fig. 3G), suggesting attenuation of inflammatory signaling. Consistent with these findings, ITE treatment reduced the high level of LPS in the liver (Fig. 3H) and the proportion of inflammatory cell infiltration was represented by CD68-positive macrophages and LY6G-positive neutrophils caused by DDC diet (Fig. 3I-J). The above transcriptome sequencing analysis and qRT-PCR verification focused on the mechanism of ITE in alleviating cholestatic liver injury on the chemokine pathway affected by ITE. Similarly, we found that similar to the results of ITE treatment, whether it was DDC diet or BDL operation, direct overexpression of AHR could reduce LPS content and inflammatory cell infiltration in the liver (Fig. S6-7).

ITE or overexpression of AHR transcriptionally inhibits the expression of chemokine CCL2

To investigate the impact of ITE and AHR on inflammatory response, we conducted in vitro experiments using the AML12 mouse hepatocyte cell line. LPS stimulation significantly increased CCL2 expression, while the addition of ITE dose-dependently decreased CCL2 expression. Furthermore, we demonstrated that ITE treatment did not affect the proliferation (Ccnd1), pyroptosis (Casp1), apoptosis (Casp3), senescence (P53, P21), cuproptosis (Fdx1), or ferroptosis (Gpx4) of AML12 cells (Fig. 4A, S9A). The AHR inhibitor CH-223,191 significantly inhibited ITE’s effect, indicating that ITE’s anti-inflammatory activity is AHR-dependent. Similarly, cell proliferation and death in samples from each group were unaffected (Fig. 4B, S9B). Additional experiments showed that AHR overexpression attenuated the high expression of CCL2 induced by LPS treatment, while CH-223,191 treatment inhibits AHR function. Similarly, cell proliferation and death in samples from each group were unaffected (Fig. 4C, S9C-D). Using JASPAR software, we predicted that AHR can transcriptionally regulate Ccl2 expression (Fig. 4D), and this was confirmed through ChIP experiments showing that ITE treatment activates AHR, enhances its binding to the Ccl2 promoter region, and is associated with transcriptional repression of CCL2 expression. CYP1A1, AHR’s known downstream target gene, was used as a positive control (Fig. 4E-F). These experiments collectively indicate that activating or overexpressing AHR can inhibit LPS-induced high expression of CCL2.

Intestinal flora disturbance in DDC-diet mice is associated with abnormal tryptophan metabolism and increased LPS

Intestinal microorganisms generate a variety of metabolites by metabolizing tryptophan, and most of these metabolites are natural ligands of AHR. To explore whether tryptophan metabolism disorders in cholestatic liver injury are related to intestinal flora, we analyzed the fresh cecal contents of the DDC group and NCD group by 16s rDNA sequencing. Compared with the NCD group, the intestinal microbial dysbiosis index (MDI) of the DDC group was significantly increased (Fig. 5A). Although there was no significant difference in alpha diversity (Simpson’s index) between the two groups (Fig. 5B), Bray-Curtis-based PCoA showed considerable clustering of the microbiota (Fig. 5C). At the phylum and genus levels, there were significant differences between the DDC and NCD groups (Fig. 5D-E). Linear discriminant analysis (LDA) identified eight core genera with substantial changes (Fig. 5F). Notably, the Lachnospiraceae_NK4A136_group, a genus previously reported to be associated with host metabolic homeostasis, was reduced in DDC-diet mice (Fig. 5G). Some members of the Lachnospiraceae family may participate in tryptophan metabolism and generate tryptophan metabolites. Notably, the dominant bacterium in the DDC group was Escherichia-Shigella (Fig. 5G), which can produce LPS and trigger an inflammatory response [23]. Further Spearman correlation analysis of these key bacterial genera showed that Lachnospiraceae_NK4A136_group was strongly correlated with serum levels of serotonin and 5-hydroxyindoleacetic acid, suggesting a potential association between this genus and host tryptophan metabolism. Moreover, Lachnospiraceae_NK4A136_group was negatively correlated with markers of liver injury and inflammation, whereas Escherichia-Shigella showed positive correlations with these parameters (Fig. 5H).

Discussion

Cholestatic liver injury is characterized by bile acid accumulation in the liver, which subsequently triggers hepatocellular damage, inflammatory responses, and progressive fibrosis. CLI severely impairs patients’ quality of life and can lead to serious complications and increased mortality if left untreated [24–26]. However, its pathogenesis remains incompletely understood and is influenced by multiple factors [8]. In the present study, we provide evidence that insufficient activation of the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AHR) is closely associated with exacerbated inflammatory responses during cholestatic liver injury. This impaired AHR activation may be linked, at least in part, to altered tryptophan metabolism accompanying cholestasis. Using pharmacological activation of AHR with tryptophan-derived metabolites such as ITE, as well as AAV-mediated AHR overexpression, we observed attenuation of liver injury accompanied by reduced inflammation. These effects were consistently supported by reduced serum liver injury markers, decreased porphyrin deposition, and diminished inflammatory cell infiltration observed in both the DDC and BDL models (Fig. 2, Fig. S2, and Fig. S5). These findings suggest that impaired AHR signaling represents an important pathological feature of cholestasis and that restoration of AHR activity can mitigate disease severity, particularly through modulation of inflammatory processes.

Among the inflammatory mediators regulated by AHR activation, CCL2 emerged as a prominent chemokine consistently suppressed in our cholestatic models. CCL2 is well recognized for its role in recruiting monocytes and macrophages and amplifying hepatic inflammation during liver injury [8]. In our study, suppression of CCL2 expression was accompanied by reduced infiltration of macrophages and neutrophils, supporting its functional relevance in the inflammatory response associated with cholestatic liver injury. However, CCL2 likely represents one component of a broader inflammatory network influenced by AHR signaling. Given the complexity of inflammatory regulation during cholestasis, additional cytokines, chemokines, and immune pathways may also contribute to the overall hepatoprotective effects observed following AHR activation. Consistent with this view, previous studies have shown that AHR broadly regulates inflammatory gene networks in hepatic and immune cells [27–29].

Mechanistically, our hepatocyte-based in vitro experiments demonstrate that AHR can transcriptionally repress CCL2 expression under inflammatory stimulation, indicating a cell-intrinsic regulatory capacity of hepatocytes. These findings provide mechanistic insight into how AHR activation can directly modulate chemokine expression at the cellular level. Nevertheless, regulation of inflammation in vivo during cholestatic liver injury is likely more complex and involves coordinated contributions from multiple hepatic cell populations, including immune and stromal cells [30]. Consistent with this view, hepatocyte-restricted modulation of AHR signaling in vivo resulted in only modest or context-dependent protective effects. Specifically, in our newly added experiments, hepatocyte-specific AHR overexpression using an AAV8-based approach led to partial attenuation of DDC-induced cholestatic liver injury, whereas hepatocyte-enriched AHR knockdown did not exacerbate disease severity under the experimental conditions tested (Supplementary Fig. S3-S4). Together, these findings indicate that while hepatocyte-intrinsic AHR signaling contributes to the protective effects of AHR activation, it is not sufficient to account for the full anti-inflammatory and hepatoprotective phenotype observed with systemic AHR activation, highlighting the involvement of additional cell types and intercellular interactions [18].

Ursodeoxycholic acid (UDCA) is currently the primary clinical therapy for cholestatic liver injury and exerts hepatoprotective effects largely through FXR- and Nrf2-related pathways [31–33]. FXR plays a central role in maintaining bile acid homeostasis by regulating bile acid synthesis, transport, and excretion, and also suppresses inflammatory signaling through inhibition of NF-κB pathways [34–39]. In addition, emerging evidence suggests that non-FXR-dependent anti-inflammatory strategies, including herbal extracts and nuclear receptor modulation such as PPARα activation, can alleviate cholestatic liver injury [13, 40–43]. In the present study, we systematically examined the hepatic expression of bile acid–related genes, including key bile acid transporters (Bsep and Mrp2), the bile acid nuclear receptor Fxr, and cholangiocyte markers (Ck7 and Ck19) (Fig. S5). While DDC feeding markedly altered the expression of these genes, pharmacological activation of AHR by ITE did not significantly restore or modify their expression under the experimental conditions examined. In parallel, effective AHR activation was confirmed by robust induction of the canonical AHR target gene Cyp1a1, whereas Fxr expression remained unchanged, as shown in an independent validation experiment (Fig. S8). Collectively, these results indicate that the protective effects of AHR activation observed in this study are unlikely to be mediated through direct regulation of bile acid metabolism or FXR signaling, but are instead primarily associated with modulation of inflammatory responses. Nevertheless, we do not exclude the possibility that AHR may influence bile acid metabolism in other disease contexts or at different stages of cholestatic liver injury.

The relationship between gut microbiota and liver diseases is well established, with the gut-liver axis playing an important role in both disease pathogenesis and therapeutic responses [44, 45]. Gut microbiota influence liver health through metabolite production, modulation of immune responses, and maintenance of intestinal barrier integrity. Notably, intestinal microbes can metabolize tryptophan into a variety of bioactive metabolites that modulate AHR signaling and inflammatory responses in the liver [44].

In this study, cholestatic liver injury was associated with marked alterations in gut microbial composition, including reduced abundance of Lachnospiraceae_NK4A.

136_group and enrichment of Escherichia-Shigella. These changes were accompanied by disrupted tryptophan metabolism, increased LPS levels, and enhanced inflammatory responses [46, 47]. Given the role of tryptophan-derived metabolites as endogenous AHR ligands, these findings support a potential association between gut microbiota dysbiosis, impaired AHR activation, and inflammation during cholestasis. Importantly, these observations are correlative in nature, and further studies are required to define the causal roles of specific microbial taxa. Approaches such as fecal microbiota transplantation or gnotobiotic mouse models will be essential to establish causal relationships. Nonetheless, the convergence of gut microbiota alterations, disrupted tryptophan metabolism, impaired AHR activation, and enhanced inflammatory responses observed across independent experimental layers supports the biological relevance of this regulatory axis in cholestatic liver injury. Importantly, rather than identifying a single entirely novel pathway, our study provides an integrative framework linking gut microbiota dysbiosis, altered tryptophan metabolism, impaired AHR signaling, and inflammatory amplification during cholestasis. This integrative perspective helps to contextualize AHR dysfunction within the broader gut-liver axis and highlights how metabolic and microbial disturbances may collectively contribute to disease progression.

Conclusion

In conclusion, cholestatic liver injury is associated with gut microbiota dysbiosis, altered tryptophan metabolism, and impaired AHR activation, which together correlate with heightened inflammatory responses. Restoration of AHR signaling alleviates liver injury primarily by limiting inflammation, in part through repression of chemokine expression and reduction of inflammatory cell infiltration (Fig. 6). These findings identify AHR as an important immunomodulatory factor in cholestatic liver injury and highlight the therapeutic potential of targeting the tryptophan–AHR axis.
