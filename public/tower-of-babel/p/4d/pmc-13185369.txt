Predictive value of the atherogenic index of plasma for incident hypertension in patients with obstructive sleep apnea
Authors: Shurun Zuo, Yonghong Xu, Lei Ma, Yongliang Du, Haiquan Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundObstructive sleep apnea (OSA) is a major risk factor for hypertension, yet the role of lipid metabolism in this association remains unclear. The atherogenic index of plasma (AIP) reflects lipid dysregulation and atherosclerotic risk, but its value in predicting incident hypertension among OSA patients has not been well established.MethodsThis retrospective study included 175 OSA patients diagnosed by polysomnography between January 2021 and January 2024. Patients were divided into a hypertension group (n = 84) and a non-hypertension group (n = 91) based on the occurrence of hypertension. Clinical, lipid, and sleep parameters were collected. Logistic regression was used to identify independent risk factors, and a nomogram model was constructed. Model performance was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, calibration curves, and decision curve analysis (DCA). Net reclassification improvement (NRI) and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI) were also calculated.ResultsPatients with incident hypertension had higher age, BMI, and AIP levels, and lower minimum oxygen saturation (LsPO₂) (all P < 0.05). Multivariate analysis revealed that age, BMI, and AIP were independent risk factors, whereas LsPO₂ was a protective factor. Each 0.1-unit increase in AIP was associated with an 8% higher risk (OR = 1.08, 95% CI: 1.05–1.12, P < 0.001), and individuals in the highest tertile had a 2.73-fold greater risk. Incorporating AIP into the combined model increased the AUC from 0.729 to 0.810 (P < 0.001), with NRI = 0.416 and IDI = 0.054 (both P < 0.001). The nomogram showed good calibration, and DCA indicated high clinical net benefit across a wide threshold range.ConclusionsAIP is an independent predictor of incident hypertension in OSA patients. Introduction

Obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) is a common sleep disorder characterized by recurrent upper airway collapse and intermittent cessation of ventilation during sleep. Its global prevalence continues to rise, making it a major public health concern [1, 2]. Epidemiological studies have shown that the prevalence of OSA in adults is approximately 4%-5%, and may exceed 30% among individuals with obesity or metabolic syndrome [3]. OSA not only impairs sleep quality and cognitive function but is also closely associated with various cardiovascular and metabolic diseases, particularly hypertension, coronary heart disease, and diabetes mellitus [4]. Recent phenotype-based studies have further emphasized the significant burden of these comorbidities in OSA [5], and highlighted the clinical benefit of utilizing novel body and anthropometric indices in effectively determining cardiovascular disease risk in this population [6]. It has been reported that about 50% of patients with OSA also have hypertension, while approximately 30% of hypertensive patients present with OSA, suggesting a complex bidirectional relationship between the two conditions [7].

In recent years, researchers have attempted to use inflammation- and metabolism-related biomarkers to predict cardiovascular risk in OSA patients [8, 9]. Indicators such as the C-reactive protein-to-albumin ratio (CAR), neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), and red cell distribution width (RDW) have been shown to be associated with hypertension comorbidity in OSA, reflecting systemic inflammation and vascular stress status [10–12]. However, these indicators primarily reflect inflammatory responses and provide limited information regarding metabolic pathways and the overall risk of atherosclerosis. Given that the pathogenesis of OSA-related hypertension involves lipid metabolism disorders and endothelial dysfunction, identifying biomarkers that better represent the “metabolic-vascular coupling” process is of great significance. The atherogenic index of plasma (AIP), calculated as the logarithm of the ratio of triglycerides to high-density lipoprotein cholesterol, comprehensively reflects abnormalities in lipid metabolism and atherosclerotic risk [13]. Elevated AIP levels indicate an increase in small dense low-density lipoprotein particles and are closely associated with insulin resistance, chronic inflammation, and endothelial dysfunction [14]. Previous studies have demonstrated that AIP is strongly correlated with the risk of hypertension, coronary artery disease, heart failure, and metabolic syndrome; however, its predictive value for incident hypertension in OSA populations has not been systematically evaluated [15–17].

Therefore, this single-center retrospective study aimed to investigate the relationship between AIP and the risk of incident hypertension in patients with OSA. Furthermore, we sought to construct and validate a risk prediction model incorporating AIP to facilitate early identification and individualized management of OSA-related hypertension and to provide insights into the interaction between metabolic and hypoxic stress mechanisms.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

This single-center retrospective study included 175 Han Chinese patients who were referred for overnight polysomnography (PSG) due to symptoms suggestive of sleep-disordered breathing (e.g., snoring or excessive daytime sleepiness) and were diagnosed with Obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) at the Department of Respiratory and Sleep Medicine, the Second Affiliated Hospital of Xuzhou Medical University, between January 2021 and January 2024. The study flowchart is illustrated in Fig. 1.

Study flowchart of patient selection and analysis process

Inclusion criteria: (1) Age 18–80 years; (2) Diagnosis of OSA confirmed by PSG (apnea-hypopnea index [AHI] ≥ 5 events/hour, predominantly obstructive events); (3) Complete baseline clinical, laboratory, and PSG data; (4) No history of hypertension or use of antihypertensive medications at baseline. Exclusion criteria: (1) Central or mixed sleep apnea; (2) Obesity hypoventilation syndrome; (3) Previously diagnosed hypertension, current use of antihypertensive medications, or baseline blood pressure meeting the diagnostic criteria for hypertension (systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mmHg or diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mmHg, confirmed by three independent measurements); (4) Severe cardiac conditions (e.g., heart failure NYHA class III-IV, unstable angina, or myocardial infarction within 3 months), hepatic insufficiency (e.g., decompensated cirrhosis or liver failure), severe renal dysfunction (estimated glomerular filtration rate [eGFR] < 30 mL/min/1.73 m²), hematological disorders (e.g., leukemia, lymphoma, or severe anemia), and active malignancies; (5) Autoimmune diseases or recent systemic glucocorticoid use; (6) Pregnancy or lactation; (7) Inability to complete follow-up.

This study adheres to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Xuzhou Medical University (Approval No. 2023120101). Given the retrospective nature of this study, the ethics committee waived the requirement for informed consent.

Grouping and outcome definition

The follow-up period extended from the date of OSA diagnosis until the occurrence of hypertension or the end of the study (January 2024). The median follow-up duration was 9.0 months (interquartile range [IQR]: 6.0–12.0 months). Follow-up data were obtained through reviewing electronic medical records and structured telephone interviews. Participants were divided into a hypertension group and a non-hypertension group according to whether hypertension occurred during hospitalization or follow-up. The diagnosis of hypertension followed the Chinese Guidelines for the Prevention and Treatment of Hypertension (2024 Revision) [18]: in the absence of antihypertensive therapy, hypertension was defined as systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mmHg and/or diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mmHg on three separate office measurements obtained on different days.

Clinical data and laboratory measurements

Data were extracted from the electronic medical record system, including: (1) General information: age, sex, body mass index (BMI), smoking and drinking history, and comorbidities such as diabetes and coronary heart disease (CHD). Diabetes was defined as a fasting blood glucose (FBG) ≥ 7.0 mmol/L, hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) ≥ 6.5%, a previous diagnosis of diabetes, or the current use of antidiabetic medications. (2) Biochemical parameters: after an overnight fast, 5 mL of venous blood was collected the next morning for testing by an automated biochemical analyzer, including total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), FBG, albumin (ALB), and hemoglobin (Hb). (3) Polysomnography parameters: overnight PSG was performed using a Philips Alice 6 LDxS sleep diagnostic system. All PSG parameters were collected at the time of OSA diagnosis, prior to the onset of hypertension, ensuring that the sleep data reflected the baseline physiological status before the outcome event occurred. Main parameters included apnea-hypopnea index (AHI), minimum oxygen saturation (LsPO₂), and oxygen desaturation index (ODI). All sleep monitoring data were manually analyzed by international registered polysomnographic technologists (RPSGT). The scoring criteria strictly followed the American Academy of Sleep Medicine (AASM) Manual for the Scoring of Sleep and Associated Events [19]. (4) Atherogenic index of plasma (AIP): calculated as AIP = lg[TG (mmol/L) / HDL-C (mmol/L)].

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.3.2). Continuous variables with normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (x̄±s) and compared using the independent-samples t-test. Non-normally distributed variables were expressed as median (P25, P75) and compared using the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables were expressed as frequency (%) and compared using the χ² test or Fisher’s exact test. Variables with P ≤ 0.20 in univariate analysis were included in a multivariate logistic regression model, and stepwise selection was used to identify independent predictors. To enhance clinical interpretability and capture non-linear effects, AIP was analyzed both as a continuous variable (per 0.1-unit increase) and as a categorical variable divided into three groups based on risk distribution: Q1 (< 0.15), Q2 (0.15–0.40), and Q3 (≥ 0.40). Trends across groups were tested using the P for trend. Odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (95%CIs) were calculated. Based on the multivariate results, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were plotted, and the area under the curve (AUC) was used to assess predictive performance. A nomogram was developed using the “rms” package in R. Model calibration was evaluated using the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test and 1,000 bootstrap resamples to generate calibration curves. Clinical utility was assessed using decision curve analysis (DCA). Net reclassification improvement (NRI) and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI) were further calculated to quantify the incremental predictive value of the model. To ensure the robustness of the multivariate model, we explicitly assessed the risk of overfitting and multicollinearity. The study included 84 hypertension events and 4 final predictors, resulting in an events-per-variable (EPV) ratio of 21. This exceeds the recommended threshold of 10, suggesting a low risk of overfitting despite the use of stepwise selection. Furthermore, multicollinearity was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF). As shown in Supplementary Fig. S1, no significant collinearity was observed among the predictors (all VIF < 5), confirming the stability of the coefficient estimates. All statistical tests were two-sided, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Regarding sample size sufficiency, a post-hoc power analysis indicated that the study sample of 175 patients provided greater than 90% statistical power to detect the observed Odds Ratio of 2.51 for AIP in the multivariate logistic regression analysis, with a type I error rate (α) of 0.05.

Results

Comparison of general characteristics between OSA patients with and without hypertension

A total of 175 patients with Obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) were included in this study, of whom 84 developed new-onset hypertension and 91 remained normotensive. As shown in Table 1, patients in the hypertension group had significantly higher age (54.45 ± 14.11 years vs. 48.08 ± 12.67 years, P = 0.002) and BMI (30.53 ± 4.97 vs. 28.82 ± 5.64 kg/m², P = 0.035), while the LsPO₂ was markedly lower (65.65 ± 11.18% vs. 73.96 ± 9.06%, P < 0.001). The Atherogenic index of plasma (AIP) level was significantly elevated in the hypertension group (0.43 ± 0.31 vs. 0.21 ± 0.28, P < 0.001), and the prevalence of diabetes was also higher (28.57% vs. 15.38%, P = 0.035). There were no significant differences between the two groups in sex, smoking, drinking, CHD, ODI, AHI or other biochemical parameters (P > 0.05).

Association between AIP and incident hypertension

To further clarify the impact of AIP on hypertension risk, we performed a detailed analysis using different adjustment models (Table 3). When analyzed as a continuous variable, every 0.1-unit increase in AIP was associated with an 8% increase in the risk of incident hypertension in the fully adjusted model (Model 3: OR = 1.08, 95% CI: 1.05–1.12, P < 0.001). Categorical analysis confirmed a significant dose-response relationship. Compared with the low-AIP group (Q1 < 0.15), patients in the moderate (Q2) and high (Q3 ≥ 0.40) AIP groups showed progressively higher risks. Specifically, the high-AIP group had a 2.73-fold increased risk of hypertension (Model 3: OR = 2.73, 95% CI: 1.78–3.68, P = 0.015), with a significant trend across groups (P for trend < 0.001).

Subgroup analysis

To evaluate the robustness of the association between AIP and incident hypertension, we performed subgroup analyses stratified by age, sex, BMI, smoking, drinking, CHD, and diabetes (Table 4). The positive association between AIP and hypertension remained directionally consistent across most subgroups. Crucially, interaction tests showed no significant heterogeneity across any of the prespecified subgroups (all P for interaction > 0.05). For instance, the association did not differ significantly by age (P for interaction = 0.790) or sex (P for interaction = 0.580), although the confidence intervals in the female and CHD subgroups were wide due to limited sample sizes.

Sensitivity analysis

To further verify the stability of AIP as a predictor and rule out the influence of transient hypertension diagnosed during the index hospitalization, we performed a sensitivity analysis by excluding 12 patients who were diagnosed with hypertension within the first month of follow-up. The analysis included the remaining 163 patients (72 in the hypertension group and 91 in the non-hypertension group). As shown in Supplementary Table S1, the baseline characteristics of this sensitivity cohort remained consistent with the primary cohort, with the hypertension group exhibiting significantly higher age, BMI, AIP levels, and prevalence of diabetes, as well as lower LsPO₂ (all P < 0.05). In the multivariate logistic regression analysis (Supplementary Table S2), AIP remained a strong independent risk factor for incident hypertension (OR = 2.51, 95% CI: 1.85–3.16, P < 0.001). Additionally, Age (OR = 1.05, P = 0.005), BMI (OR = 1.08, P = 0.040), and LsPO₂ (OR = 0.93, P < 0.001) maintained their independent predictive value. These results confirm the robustness of the predictive model.

ROC curve and model performance analysis

As shown in Fig. 2A, single-variable ROC analysis revealed that AIP had the highest predictive value for new-onset hypertension in OSA patients (AUC = 0.725), outperforming age (AUC = 0.621), BMI (AUC = 0.627), and LsPO₂ (AUC = 0.687), suggesting that AIP has superior discriminative ability. In the multivariable combined prediction model (Fig. 2B), the baseline model (Model 1: Age + BMI + LsPO₂) achieved an AUC of 0.729 (95%CI: 0.652–0.807). After incorporating AIP (Model 2: Age + BMI + LsPO₂ + AIP), the AUC significantly increased to 0.810 (95%CI: 0.746–0.875), indicating that the inclusion of AIP substantially improved predictive performance.

Further reclassification analysis (Table 5) showed that adding AIP increased the NRI to 0.416 (95%CI: 0.127–0.705, P < 0.001) and the IDI to 0.054 (95%CI: 0.019–0.088, P < 0.001), while the C-statistic increased from 0.729 to 0.810 (P < 0.001). These findings indicate that AIP significantly enhances model discrimination and reclassification, providing added predictive value for assessing new-onset hypertension risk in OSA patients.

Construction and evaluation of the nomogram model

Based on the results of the multivariate logistic regression analysis, four independent variables—age, BMI, LsPO₂, and AIP—were incorporated to construct a nomogram for predicting the risk of new-onset hypertension in OSA patients (Fig. 3). In the nomogram, each variable contributed positively to the predicted risk, with AIP showing the greatest influence, followed by age and BMI, while LsPO₂ acted as a protective factor.

Nomogram for predicting the risk of new-onset hypertension in OSA patients

The calibration curve demonstrated high consistency between predicted and observed probabilities, with the bias-corrected curve nearly overlapping the ideal reference line, indicating excellent calibration of the model (Fig. 4A). DCA (Fig. 4B) showed that the model provided stable and substantial clinical net benefit across a wide range of threshold probabilities, suggesting strong potential for clinical application in risk stratification and individualized decision-making for OSA patients.

Discussion

This single-center retrospective study constructed and validated a nomogram integrating age, Body mass index (BMI), Lowest oxygen saturation (LsPO₂), and the Atherogenic index of plasma (AIP) to predict the risk of incident hypertension in patients with obstructive sleep apnea (OSA). The model demonstrated good discrimination (AUC = 0.810), high calibration consistency, and significant clinical net benefit in DCA. Among these predictors, AIP, as a newly introduced metabolic indicator, was an independent predictor of hypertension development in OSA patients. Crucially, our analysis revealed a significant dose-response relationship, where the risk of hypertension rose progressively with increasing AIP levels. Furthermore, subgroup analyses confirmed that this predictive value remained consistent across diverse clinical settings (including different age groups, sexes, and comorbidity profiles), highlighting the stability of AIP as a biomarker. Its inclusion significantly improved the overall predictive performance of the model, suggesting that metabolic abnormalities are closely associated with the pathogenesis of OSA-related hypertension.

Consistent with previous studies, this study reconfirmed that age and obesity are major risk factors for hypertension in OSA patients. Both factors reflect vascular remodeling and sympathetic overactivation resulting from chronic intermittent hypoxia and metabolic stress [20]. Notably, this study further revealed the potential mechanistic role of AIP in OSA-related hypertension. As the logarithmic ratio of triglycerides to high-density lipoprotein cholesterol, AIP comprehensively reflects lipid metabolism disorders, atherosclerotic risk, and the concentration of small dense low-density lipoprotein (sdLDL) particles. Elevated AIP not only indicates altered lipid composition but is also closely associated with systemic inflammation, oxidative stress, and endothelial dysfunction [21, 22]. In OSA patients, recurrent hypoxia-reoxygenation cycles increase levels of lipid peroxidation products and inflammatory mediators, which promote sdLDL oxidation, monocyte adhesion, and foam cell formation, thereby accelerating atherosclerosis progression [23, 24]. Epidemiological studies have demonstrated that AIP predicts cardiovascular events more sensitively than traditional lipid parameters, highlighting its unique advantage in reflecting early vascular injury [25]. By incorporating AIP into a hypertension risk prediction model for OSA patients, this study found that AIP was independently associated with hypertension, suggesting that lipid-mediated vascular injury may represent a key pathological link between OSA and hypertension and providing new evidence for studying the interaction between metabolic and vascular pathways.

In addition, the study found that LsPO₂ (rather than AHI or ODI) was significantly associated with incident hypertension. Although the AHI is the traditional metric for assessing OSA severity, it primarily reflects the frequency of respiratory events rather than the depth and cumulative burden of hypoxia. This distinction aligns with the ‘hypoxic burden’ hypothesis, suggesting that the physiological stress induced by OSA is better captured by the depth of desaturation than by the frequency of events [26]. Previous studies have similarly indicated that the intensity of hypoxemia triggers more profound sympathetic overactivation and oxidative stress—key drivers of hypertension—compared to the mere number of apnea episodes [27]. Consequently, LsPO₂ appears to be a more sensitive marker for cardiovascular risk stratification in this population. Mechanistically, recurrent oxygen desaturation events can induce oxidative stress, trigger the release of inflammatory cytokines, and activate the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS), thereby increasing vascular stiffness and sustaining hypertension [28]. Furthermore, the simultaneous inclusion of AIP and LsPO₂ in the prediction model suggests a potential synergistic mechanism between lipid metabolism disorder and hypoxic injury. On one hand, dyslipidemia may exacerbate oxidative stress and endothelial inflammation; on the other hand, hypoxia can promote lipid accumulation and metabolic disturbance. These processes may form a vicious cycle, jointly contributing to vascular stiffness and hypertension development [29]. Furthermore, our findings align with recent literature highlighting the usefulness of novel blood biomarkers and lipid-related ratios in OSA screening. For instance, recent evidence has demonstrated the value of combining lipid profiles with other metabolic parameters (such as the uric acid-HDL ratio and albumin indices) as reliable markers for OSAS severity and cardiovascular risk [30].

Moving beyond simple association, this study offers a practical focus on risk prediction. The developed nomogram translates complex multivariate data into a visual and easy-to-use clinical tool. By integrating routine biochemical markers (AIP) with hypoxic metrics (LsPO₂), clinicians can quantitatively predict the individual probability of incident hypertension, facilitating early stratification and personalized intervention strategies (e.g., closer BP monitoring or aggressive lipid management) for high-risk OSA patients. The DCA indicated that the model achieved high clinical net benefit across a wide range of threshold probabilities, suggesting strong applicability for clinical risk stratification, individualized follow-up, and intervention decision-making.

However, several limitations should be noted. First, the single-center retrospective design with a relatively small sample size may introduce selection bias and limit generalizability. Second, the observational nature precludes causal inference; despite excluding baseline hypertension, reverse causality driven by subclinical vascular disease or pre-hypertension cannot be entirely ruled out. Third, residual confounding remains possible as data on specific lipid-lowering medications (e.g., statins), anti-inflammatory drugs (e.g., aspirin), anthropometric indicators of central obesity (e.g., waist circumference), physical activity, socioeconomic status, continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) adherence, and time spent with SpO₂ < 90% (T90) were unavailable. Since lipid-lowering therapies typically reduce AIP levels and are more likely to be used by patients with higher cardiovascular risk, this unmeasured confounding may have attenuated the observed difference between groups, leading to a conservative estimation of the true association. Finally, the study population was predominantly male (~ 75%), potentially restricting the applicability of our findings to female patients. Multi-center prospective studies with comprehensive data collection are needed to validate these results.

Conclusions

In summary, AIP is an important independent predictor of incident hypertension in patients with OSA. Incorporating AIP into a prediction model based on clinical and sleep parameters significantly enhances risk stratification and decision-support capability, providing new insights and evidence for the early identification and individualized management of OSA-related hypertension, as well as a new direction for exploring the interactions among metabolic, hypoxic, and vascular mechanisms.
