Earliest evidence of behavioural handedness in the Ediacaran motile bilaterian Spriggina floundersi
Authors: Scott D. Evans, Jenson Webb, Ian V. Hughes, William Parker, Mary L. Droser
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Fossils of the Ediacara Biota preserve the oldest macroscopic communities that include animals. Classification of many of these taxa has proved contentious. Instead, studies of ecological characters reveal key insights. Here we examine the Ediacaran fossil Spriggina floundersi from the Ediacara Member, Flinders Ranges and surrounding region. Specimens from Nilpena Ediacara National Park (NENP) and the South Australia Museum (SAM) present significant morphological variation. Fossils found in situ on discrete bedding planes at NENP reveal no systematic orientation of features, suggesting variable morphologies formed via biological processes, rather than external forces. Our results support motility in Spriggina, which involved bending about the long axis, propagation of pedal waves, vertical adjustment of the anterior region and horizontal manipulation of repeated body units. A significant number of fossil specimens are bent to the left (right in life). The nature of these bends does not match expectations of anatomical asymmetry and instead constitutes the oldest described evidence of behavioural handedness. Results are consistent with Spriggina as a bilaterally symmetrical, possibly segmented, benthic organism. These characters are unique compared with known Ediacaran ecologies but are common in various extant bilaterian groups, indicating major animal innovations prior to the Phanerozoic.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-53857-x.

Introduction

Active motility is critical for countless extant organisms, particularly animals. Determining whether and how extinct forms were capable of active movement is key in reconstructing ecologies of fossil taxa1,2. Motility is perhaps best evidenced when body fossils can be directly linked to trace fossils3–5. Absent this, modern analogues and functional morphology can be used to infer active movements6,7. Such determinations can be contentious for many fossil taxa, especially in the absence of associated trace fossils and/or when modern affinities are difficult to resolve.

The Ediacara Biota represents the oldest macrofossil evidence for complex animal life8–10. Although key taxa have been linked directly to extant groups11–14, most remain difficult to constrain beyond a metazoan affinity. For such forms, the identification of fundamental characters allows contextualization within the broader emergence and diversification of animals (see refs15,16 and references therein). One such trait, motility, has been proposed for at least ten Ediacara genera, which together represent the earliest metazoans able to move independently12,17–22. This is commonly recognized based on direct associations between body and trace fossils, resulting from active disturbance of the organic substrate readily preserved in the unique taphonomic window that yields this biota21,23,24. The number of known motile taxa represents a small subset of all taxa from the Ediacara Biota (more than 100 and counting). A sessile life habit is convincingly documented for many forms (e.g., rangeomorphs and arboreomorphs anchored to the substrate via the holdfast form genus Aspidella). However, others do not preserve clear evidence for either a sessile or motile life habit and active movement has been convincingly argued absent trace fossil data19,25–27. Thus, for many taxa from this biota, it is unclear whether they were entirely sessile or include motile organisms that did not disturb the mat in a manner that resulted in the common preservation of trace fossils. Even for those taxa where motility is well-established (e.g., Dickinsonia [21]), common trace fossil preservation (with evidence of feeding behaviour but not active body displacement) can leave unresolved questions about the style and rate of motility. Any advancements in unlocking the life mode of enigmatic Ediacaran taxa are key to accurately reconstructing Precambrian communities of early macroscopic life.

Spriggina floundersi Glaessner, 1958 was originally described based on three specimens from the Ediacara Hills, South Australia28. Relatively few studies have discussed the genus in the 65 years since, with most subsequent contributions focused on the validity of interpretations as an arthropod and/or annelid29–36. Several authors have suggested that this organism was motile based on observations of bending and other morphological variants in a handful of isolated specimens, even invoking a pelagic or nektobenthic life habit21,28,31,33,36–38. However, such studies have largely ignored the null hypothesis that variable morphologies observed in these specimens could be the result of external forces, such as current activity, and do not necessitate active motility35,39. Here we present the first detailed specimen-based description of Spriggina since the initial naming of the species, with observations of over one hundred new fossils from South Australia. This includes abundant specimens preserved on the base of individual bedding planes40, allowing the ability to independently test hypothesized motility. Our results are consistent with active body manipulation in Spriggina and allow documentation of significant variation in morphology to parameterize various aspects of motility in the genus, including the discovery of the oldest known evidence of behavioural handedness.

Morphology

The body of Spriggina is an elongate ovoid, with one end of the long axis undivided and wider compared with the opposite extreme end, which tappers to a point (Fig. 1). All conclusive fossil specimens are preserved in negative hyporelief, representing casts of the top of the living animal and commonly exhibiting a well-defined outer margin (Fig. 2). The larger undivided region is typically considered the anterior, apparently based on resemblance to the ‘head’ of many bilaterians and the opposite, tapering end the posterior28,31,36. Absent evidence for the direction of movement, assignment of anterior and posterior is ambiguous, however, we retain these terms given prevalence in existing literature and similarity of morphology to the Anterior–Posterior (A-P) axis in many bilaterians. We refer to the undivided region as the ‘anterior region’. Parallel to the long axis is the median line, commonly preserved in more positive relief (a ridge in the fossil) relative to compared with other parts of the body, beginning at the base of the anterior region and extending to the extreme end of the posterior. In addition to S. floundersi, the species Spriggina ovata29, now Marywadea ovata30, and Spriggina borealis41 have been described within the genus. Weyland and Droser38 introduced the term ‘sprigginamorph’ to unite S. floundersi, M. ovata and mm-scale, unnamed taxa that resemble these forms but have smaller, more densely packed body divisions. This contribution intends to describe S. floundersi, herein referred to by its generic name only. Revised taxonomy is warranted, and future studies may support separation of the specimens reported here into multiple discrete species and/or addition to the known examples of S. floundersi from the Flinders Ranges and surrounding region.

Results

Overall body morphology

Observations of 75 Spriggina from the Ediacara Member are generally consistent with original descriptions of this species28. Variations in overall morphology are numerous (see Sect. 4.2), including the irregularity of the outer margin and expression of the long axis, which ranges from straight to sinuous with multiple bends (Fig. 2; Supplementary Data S1).

Commonly, the median line is preserved in positive relief compared to that of module boundaries and the anterior region (Fig. 2B,E–G,I). Rarely, the relief of all three of these features is consistent along part (anterior portion in Fig. 2A,H) or the entire length of the body (Fig. 2D). Areas of deep negative relief are rarely observed flanking the median line on either side, where modules meet this central axis (Fig. 2C).

Modules emanate from the median line at approximately 90° and commonly bend towards the posterior at some point along their width. The magnitude of this module bend angle varies considerably within and between specimens, from ~ 0° (modules that remain perpendicular to the median line throughout the observed width; Fig. 2I) to over 45° (Fig. 2H). Rarely, adjacent modules exhibit variable bend angles suggestive of independence (Fig. 2C, white triangle). More commonly bend angles show gradational change, if any, between adjacent modules. Module bend angles are generally consistent across the midline.

Module boundaries do not extend across the median line, and this separation prevents precise interpretations of symmetry about the long axis (bilateral vs. offset). The overall appearance is that of a bilaterally symmetrical organism, and in the 32 specimens for which the number of modules on each side of the midline could be accurately estimated, only two appear to have different numbers on either side of the midline. These differ by 2 and 3 modules each (Supplementary Data S1). We attribute this to the difficulty of resolving modules at the posterior based on the grain size of host sediments, rather than original offset symmetry or abnormal growth. In the best-preserved specimens, the anterior module boundary deflects towards the posterior boundary culminating in a point at the outer margin and resulting in a triangular morphology with A-P differentiation.

Morphologic variability

Most specimens exhibit an irregular outer margin that deviates from a smooth, ovate shape (Fig. 2C,E–G; Table 1). Approximately 70% of specimens are bent about the long axis (Fig. 2A–H). Bends are expressed at various locations relative to the anterior and posterior, although many occur near the middle of the long axis (Fig. 3). Bends range in magnitude from 5 to 81o and roughly twice as many specimens are bent to the left (right in life) compared with those that curve to the right. A binomial test indicates significant (p = 0.0017) deviation from a random distribution (an equal number of left and right turns; H0 = 0.5). Approximately one third of specimens are bent in multiple locations (Fig. 2A, with the first bend of greater magnitude near the anterior and a smaller magnitude bend at the posterior end). Modules are not systematically longer or shorter on either side (inside vs. outside) of these bends (Supplementary Data S1). The mean total length of bent specimens is 23 ± 12 mm, larger than the 17 ± 8 mm observed for straight specimens. Rare specimens are incomplete, with portions of their body not preserved (Fig. 2C,E–G, white arrows). These are comparable to ‘lifted’ Dickinsonia previously described from the Ediacara Member42 and Ust-Pinega Formation in the White Sea area43. We interpret them to similarly represent areas of Spriggina not directly in contact with the organic mat at the time of burial, likely due to the presence of sand between the specimen and the organic mat. The outlines of lifts can be smooth, expressed as broadly semicircular missing areas (Fig. 2G) or abrupt, with edges defined by module boundaries (Fig. 2F). We did not observe lifts that extended beyond the midline. Three specimens were also identified that appear to be missing the entire anterior region of their body, which we interpret to similarly result from separation relative to the organic mat. This has been previously reported for a single specimen from NECP37. Irregular margins, bends and lifts are observed in greater relative abundance in the best-preserved specimens (Table 1), consistent with a biotic, as opposed to taphonomic, origin.

In situ communities

Beds 1 T-FA and TC-MM3 are two of the largest excavated surfaces (each ~ 20 m2) with relatively high diversity and evenness compared with similar beds at NENP38,40. Mat textures, ripple sharpness and adherence suggest that 1 T-FA (MMI = 4) represents a slightly more mature surface compared to TC-MM3 (MMI = 3; 44). There is little evidence of current influence on any taxa on 1 T-FA38, and Spriggina body axes, bends and ‘lifts’ exhibit no preferential orientation (Fig. 4A). On TC-MM3, several current indicators – the biological tool mark “mop”, lifted Dickinsonia, and Parvancorina orientations – are consistent with a current orientation of ~ 250o 19,42,45. Quantitative observations of morphological variability

Figure 5 contains the results of morphometric analysis, displayed as plots of PCA scores of Procrustes Landmark Analysis (Fig. 5A) and Elliptical Fourier Analysis (Fig. 5B). In Fig. 5A the axes of the plots are arbitrary cartesian coordinates for the six landmarks, with L1 at the lower right and L6 at the upper left. The associated plots present the average landmark positions as well as the variation in landmarks associated with each principal component (vectors illustrate the direction and extent of 3 standard deviations in landmark location). A major component of the variation in Spriggina shape is described by the relative size of the anterior region (PC1 = 68%, Fig. 5A). A smaller proportion of shape variation likely represents the bending of specimens, with the posterior most landmark deviating laterally from anterior landmarks (PC2 = 13.5%, Fig. 5A). Because this analysis searches for systematic deviation, bends to the left will be offset by bends to the right. This likely contributes to the lower PC score observed despite the commonality of bends. Resolution of systematic bending to the left in the fossil (right in life) despite this offset confirms a directional preference. Investigation of outline variation using Principal Component Analysis of Elliptical Fourier harmonic strengths yields few systematic trends (Fig. 5B). This analysis was repeated with various parameters, always yielding similar results to those presented in Fig. 5B. The lack of clustering in such plots suggests each specimen represents a unique combination of shape variation, with the origin representing relatively straight “unaltered” specimens exhibiting regular outer margins and deviation from this idealized shape extending in all directions in principal component space (Fig. 5B). Individual populations with significant numbers of specimens (1 T-F and TC-MM3) also do not cluster in component space, consistent with a biological rather than environmental control.

Discussion

Body manipulation

Our results provide the first comprehensive, quantitative support for body manipulation by Spriggina. Curvature of the long axis indicates the capacity to bend laterally. Although this could be the result of external forces (i.e. bending under current influence), lack of consistent orientation of such bends between specimens on the same bedding plane and individuals that exhibit multiple bend directions are inconsistent with this hypothesis. Further, directly adjacent specimens (within cm’s) on beds 1 T-F and TC-MM3 exhibit highly divergent anterior orientations and bend directions, best described by the ability of each organism to independently change body shape, attributed to motility21,25. Lack of distinction between the body size of bent and straight specimens suggests bending capabilities were present through all life stages examined.

Recognizing that such bending is related to the active movement of Spriggina, the resultant asymmetry (preponderance of bends to the right in life) could be produced by one of two mechanisms: anatomical or behavioural. The former attributes preferential bend direction to an underlying asymmetry of the body organization. Although difficult to falsify, several observations are inconsistent with this hypothesis. The tight developmental control of left–right asymmetry typically results in very low frequencies of anatomical reversals in various animal groups (0.06% spontaneous left–right reversal in the gut of Drosophila melanogaster46; 1 in 10,000 instances of situs inversus in humans47). We also predict that anatomical differences would be a primary driver of bend direction over secondary factors such current flow or substrate variability, resulting in all or a significant majority (> ~ 90%) of specimens bent in one direction, contra observations here. Additionally, we expect that if these secondary drivers are held constant (as is the case for specimens found on the same bedding plane) all specimens should respond in the same manner based on body construction (i.e., bend in the same direction). The observation that individuals on the same beds (TC-MM3 and 1 T-F) bend to either the right or left and exhibit no preferential orientation between specimens is inconsistent with this hypothesis. Anatomical differences should also produce similar bend angles at similar positions along the main body axis, inconsistent with our results (Fig. 3). In contrast, population level spontaneous motor biases (such as preferred turn directions) in extant animals are expressed in a significant but not overwhelming majority of individuals (see examples and references in48), matching observations of Spriggina.

The dominance of bends to the left in fossils of Spriggina suggests a preference for right turns in life (Fig. 6a) and represents the oldest evidence of behavioural handedness among animals. It is difficult to determine whether this spontaneous motor bias occurred as a response to burial or if bent specimens represent organisms caught-in-the-act of ‘normal’ movement typical in life. However, the presence of both straight and variably bent, non-oriented Spriggina indicates that such behaviour was the result of independent movement by the living organism. Behavioural lateralization is common in extant motile metazoans, including invertebrates48–52, and rare fossil taxa53,54. In living animals, turning biases are widely recognized and attributed to various factors48, including neuronal asymmetry in flying insects50, optimal foraging strategies of ants51 and even unknown causes in giant water bugs52 among others. Given this variability we refrain from assigning this asymmetry to any specific function. Based on the neuronal capabilities of extant taxa in which this type of behaviour is recognized48, we contend that handedness is suggestive of a relatively advanced nervous system in Spriggina. Critically, no such lateralization of activity has been described for other Ediacaran motile taxa, although equivalent data is limited. An examination of trace fossils associated with Dickinsonia (see Fig. 9 of ref21) indicates that, though it was capable of turning both left and right, there are a roughly equal number of turns in either direction observed in all specimens from the Flinders Ranges area, inconsistent with any biases in motor activity in this contemporaneous genus.

Variable orientation of missing portions (interpreted as parts of the body separated from the seafloor along the outer margin) of Spriggina from the same populations suggests that they did not form under current influence, as is the case for Dickinsonia at NENP lifted from the mat covered substrate during depositional events42. The presence of such specimens on surfaces otherwise devoid of evidence for strong current influence (1 T-F38) and mismatch with well-established current indicators on other beds (TC-MM342,45) further corroborates irregular margins as biologically mediated features. These are most parsimoniously interpreted to represent the ability of Spriggina to elevate portions of their lateral margin off the seafloor (Fig. 6b). This is consistent with interpretations of Dickisnonia from the White Sea lifted on opposite sides of the body attributed to factors beyond current influence43, and such features in both genera are likely the combined result of biologic activity and sedimentation. We propose that the size, location and curvature of lifts in Spriggina is consistent with various marine organisms, from flatworms to nudibranchs, that propagate pedal waves along their lateral margins controlled by muscular contraction and relaxation around the central portion of the long axis55–57.

We propose that the inconsistent morphology and size of the anterior region is due to the ability of Spriggina to raise and lower this structure (Fig. 6c). Evidence includes significant variation in landmark analysis of the lateral portions of the anterior region, which could result from variable amounts of sand imparted below this region based on how far above the seafloor it was lifted. Rare specimens with the anterior region entirely absent37 are also consistent with this hypothesis.

Variable bending of modules within and between specimens indicates that these were also manipulated during movement. The common match between adjacent modules suggests connection via tissue in life and coordinated operation, potentially aiding in motility36. We attribute apparently disjointed modules in rare specimens to decay or other postmortem degradation of connective tissue, rather than the presence of independent appendages in Spriggina (cf. ref31).

Ecology

Many sessile, attached benthic organisms can manipulate their bodies (e.g., refs58,59). If Spriggina was attached to the substrate (cf. ref35) it is possible that body manipulation combined with felling prior to or during burial produced the variable morphologies observed without requiring active motility of a free-living organism. The observed random orientation of the long axis on in situ bedding planes is inconsistent with an attached, felled organism60, especially on bed TC-MM3, which contains multiple indicators of current influence42,45. Additionally, the only possible attachment structure in Spriggina is the anterior region35. Known holfasts (Aspidella) are far more common on bedding planes than associated petaloids40,41. Therefore, if the anterior region of Spriggina represents the site of attachment, we would expect this to be more commonly preserved on the burial surface than the rest of the divided body (presumably elevated in the water column in this hypothesis), inconsistent with our observations. Finally, behavioural handedness is a feature of free-living, motile organisms today (see ref48 and references therein), to our knowledge undescribed in sessile, attached forms. Accordingly, the most parsimonious interpretation of the data is that Spriggina was a free living, actively motile organism.

Combined, our observations suggest that Spriggina was able to bend the long axis horizontally, propagate pedal waves along the outer margin, raise the anterior region, and adjust the position of connected modules relative to the A-P axis (Supplementary video S2). This style of motility matches many elongate, vermiform marine taxa today, including both benthic and pelagic forms. The absence of associated trace fossils could support primarily or exclusively pelagic movement. However, the production of Ediacaran trace fossils in shallow marine environments requires disturbance of the relatively cohesive mat, commonly attributed to feeding activity21,23,24. Benthic motile taxa have been proposed without associated trace fossils19,26,27. Simple motion across the mat is also inferred between evidence of feeding activity, for example in Dickinsonia and Kimberlla11,12,21, but does not leave trace fossils in the Ediacara Member (although see ref22 for possible examples from the White Sea region). Thus, the lack of trace fossils associated with Spriggina does not negate movement across the seafloor and a benthic life habit. Preservation on discrete bedding planes is consistent with significant time spent at the sediment–water interface. Lack of distortion that can be attributed to external forces (twisting, folding, tearing etc.) observed in contemporaneous taxa from the Ediacara Member61 suggests that Spriggina did not accumulate on the seafloor following death and significant transport. Further, fully pelagic organisms tend to exhibit clear morphological adaptations to life in open water (e.g., refs62,63), inconsistent with the flattened morphology observed here. No morphological distinction has been recognized between top and bottom in Spriggina, which may suggest that all known specimens are ‘right-side-up’. Pelagic or nektobenthic organisms might be expected to flip over more commonly than strictly benthic taxa (although see64 for a contemporaneous benthic example). Together, this supports a predominantly benthic life habit for Spriggina. Given the style and complexity of movement it is also possible that this organism could periodically lift its entire body off the sediment–water interface, as is common for similar nektobenthic taxa today (flatworms, nudibranchs, polychaetes, etc.).

Lack of evidence for mat disturbance suggests a distinct feeding mode in Spriggina compared with contemporaneous motile taxa. Other Ediacara Biota are interpreted to have fed directly on the mat either via their entire ventral surface (dickinsoniomorphs18,21), by systematically excavated the seafloor (kimberellomorphs11,12), or scavenging organic matter at the sediment water interface and buried shallowly below thin bodies of sand (Ikaria and likely other progenitors of Helminthoidichnites65). Absent any direct evidence it is difficult to confirm how Spriggina may have fed, but it is clear this had minimal observable impacts (if any) on the organic substrate and implies a diversity of feeding habits for motile Ediacaran taxa. One possibility is selective consumption of specific types of organic matter within the substrate, which did not result in recognizable mat disturbance in the cast and mould style preservation of the Ediacara Member. Such precise feeding would have been greatly aided by the presence of a discrete mouth in Spriggina.

Affinities and implications

The cm-scale, symmetry and body patterning of Spriggina is consistent with bilaterians. The style of movement deduced here is perhaps most consistent with that observed in various marine flatworms56. However, the broad motions described are found in a range of marine, free swimming taxa, and are conservatively interpreted to represent shared ecologies of actively motile benthic organisms, rather than direct relatedness.

Repeated units in Spriggina are comparable to segments. Common match between module numbers on the left and right side and careful observation of morphology (Fig. 2) support bilateral, non-offset body symmetry. Although definitions vary, a general rule is that true segments contain morphological distinctions between the anterior and posterior66. Common bending towards the posterior and the triangular morphology of modules at the outer margin allow for such distinctions. Although numerous contemporaneous Ediacara taxa exhibit similar gross body morphologies67, these either lack A-P distinction of repeated units (Dickinsonia, Yorgia, Ikaria), or do not possess defined A-P axes such that apparently consistent morphological features along the long axes can be definitively linked to such polarity (Pteridinium, Inkrylovia). This would represent the oldest evidence of segmentation (~ 559–550 Ma compared with Yilingia < 551 Ma20), potentially supporting an affinity between this Ediacaran form and one of three truly segmented bilaterian groups (annelids, arthropods, and chordates). However, A-P differences within units cannot be resolved in all specimens of Spriggina and it is unclear whether the macroscopic morphological distinction identified here matches the definition of modern groups (commonly recognized on a cellular level for extant segmented bilaterians). Regardless, Spriggina exhibits body patterning consistent with various complex animals known throughout the Phanerozoic.

The interpreted stye of motility in Spriggina is unique to muscular animals today, supporting the presence of body musculature. Coordination of multiple directions of movement rapidly over cm-scales is also consistent with a nervous system. The morphology of this fossil provides an obvious template on which to centralize the arrangement of neurons in a manner comparable to many bilaterians. This organization cannot be tested based on current fossil evidence. There is no evidence that Spriggina responded to external stimuli and fossils do not preserve anatomical features that can be reliably attribute to sensory organs. However, the manner and rate of motility suggest that this organism possessed the ability to adapt to rapidly changing conditions, which likely required sensory capabilities akin to those recognized for various animals from the Ediacaran and throughout the Phanerozoic. Spontaneous motor biases, such as the turning preference observed here, are generally found in taxa with relatively complex nervous systems (see above), also consistent with advanced sensory capabilities in Spriggina.

Many developmental processes likely operating in Spriggina match those previously recognized for Ediacaran organisms16, including regulatory mechanisms for multicellularity, tissue production, musculature, establishment of multiple principle body axes and subsequent patterning found throughout extant animal phyla. The genetic underpinning of behavioural handedness is an area of ongoing research in modern animals, although studies commonly focus on understanding differences at the individual level (i.e., genetic controls on whether an individual is left- or right-handed49,68). The recognition of the importance of the Nodal pathway in left–right asymmetries of both protostomes and deuterostomes69,70 suggests that this and associated regulatory elements may play a role in lateralization of bilaterians 71, including Spriggina. Interestingly, such asymmetry may be initiated by actin filament elongation at the single-cell stage, inherited from actin-driven dynamics of unicellular eukaryotes70,72 and thus available to Ediacaran animals and not necessitating activation of the Nodal pathway. The role of morphological asymmetries in behavioural handedness remains underexplored, and the various pathways to lateralization may suggest an epigenetic control49,71. Whether convergent or conserved, the recognition of handedness in Spriggina suggests that regulatory pathways allowed complex behavioural patterns to emerge in the earliest evolutionary history of bilaterians.

Morphological investigation of Spriggina from South Australia indicates that it was one of the first motile modular organisms. Compared with other Ediacara Biota capable of active movement, it manipulated its body in several distinct, likely coordinated ways. Lack of direct evidence for feeding behaviour suggests a unique method of nutrient capture relative to known motile taxa from this time. Spriggina was potentially segmented with a benthic or nektobenthic life habit. Fossils of this genus are unique in preserving clear evidence of behavioural handedness, the oldest known example in the fossil record. These features are common in Phanerozoic animals, including extant and fossil bilaterians, controlled by various conserved regulatory elements common throughout Metazoa. Thus, although affinities remain difficult to resolve, this iconic taxon represents another example of a ‘Cambrian-type’ organism amongst the enigmatic Ediacara Biota and an important precursor to the diversity of bilaterians that would later dominate Phanerozoic ecosystems.

Materials and methods

Specimens described below are from the South Australia Museum (SAM; designated by five-digit P#s) and Nilpena Ediacara National Park (NENP; designated by bed names with south and east coordinates or “N” numbers for float specimens not associated with a named excavated bedding plane40). Although we examined over 100 total specimens, detailed morphological measurements are available for a subset of these, including 16 from the SAM and 60 from NENP. All were recovered from the Ediacara Member of the Rawnsley Quartzite, exposed throughout the Flinders Ranges and surrounding regions of South Australia73. No age dates have been successfully recovered from the Ediacara Member. Stratigraphic relationships, with fossils recognized between the Wonoka Formation (containing the Shuram Carbon Isotope Excursion) and below the prominent contact with overlying Cambrian strata, suggest an approximate age of 567–538 Ma73,74. Correlative fossils from the White Sea assemblage dated elsewhere support these deposits as between ~ 560 and 550 million years old75,76. Fossils of Spriggina occur on the base of mm- to cm-thick sandstone event beds. Previous research regarding the sedimentological characters of the Ediacara Member constrains fossils of Spriggina to the Oscillation-Ripple, Lingoid Ripple and Flat Laminated, as well as Planar Laminated Sandstone facies representing deposition in an inner ramp setting below fair-weather wave base for the former and below storm-wave base for the later77,78.

Fossils of Spriggina at NENP are relatively rare on systematically excavated bedding planes of the Ediacara Member (< 50 total specimens40). The beds each represent individual storm events that buried Ediacaran communities in situ, with varying, but commonly minimal amounts of taphonomic alteration prior to burial40,45,78. This includes beds with no evidence of current influence or disturbance of the communities37,38 preserving ecological snapshots of the Ediacaran seafloor. Fossil beds contain abundant macrofauna and textured organic surfaces (TOS) evidence of widespread organic mats40,78. Spriggina occurs on 10 of 32 beds at NENP (1 T-A; 1 T-BOF; 1 T-NA; 1 T-F; 1 T-T; LV-FUN; TB-ARB; TB-Sh1; TC-MM2; TC-MM3) with Mat Maturity Index (MMI) values ranging from 2–444.

Specimens of Spriggina were photographed in various lighting to capture morphological detail using a Pentax K-50 DSLR. Latex moulds and 3D scans, collected using the Polyga Compact C506 3D laser scanner, with an accuracy reported to 12 microns, and the FlexScan3D software, were made of key specimens. Scans of figured specimens from NENP are reposited in the public database Morphosource. Morphological observations were primarily based on visual inspection of physical specimens and photographs, with latex moulds and 3D scans used to supplement and confirm characters. All measurements, conducted on digital photographs using the freely available Image J software (https://imagej.nih.gov/ij/), and observations are available as electronic supplementary material (dataset S1).

The number of bends and incomplete outer margins (‘lifts’ cf. ref42) were recorded based on observations of original specimens. Bends were identified visually, based on clear deviation from a straight line superimposed over the specimen. Qualitative observations collected for each specimen include midline relief, nature of the outer margin (regular v. irregular), and whether modules are shorter on the inside or outside of bends. A qualitative taphonomic grade (T1-3) was assigned to each specimen based on the quality of preservation ranging from excellent (T1), with all morphologies clearly preserved, to poor (T3), with minimal details of the original organism replicated. A binomial test for significant deviation (p < 0.05) from a random distribution of bends to the left and right (H0 = 0.5) was conducted using the PAST software package79. The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was used to determine whether the distribution of total length for bent specimens was significantly different (p < 0.05) from the population of straight specimens, also analysed in PAST79.

To investigate the potential influence of external forces on the varied morphologies of Spriggina, we examined the two beds at NENP with the highest number of specimens: 1 T-FA with 20 and TC-MM3 with 8. Mapping of specimens based on south and east coordinates to the nearest cm follows established methods at NENP40. For each Spriggina, we measured the orientation of the anterior, bending of the long axis and lifts. Observations of other taxa, including independent current indicators (e.g., “mop” cf. ref45) were recorded.

Six landmarks (Fig. 1B L1-6) were collected using ImageJ, including: L1) the tip of the anterior most region; L2) the base of the anterior most region; L3) the left edge of the anterior most region; L4) the right edge of the anterior most region; L5) the intersection of the module boundary between the 5th and 6th modules from the anterior along the median line; and, L6) the intersection of the median line with the posterior. The choice of L5 was somewhat arbitrary, but this position was chosen to avoid repetition with points L4 and L6 and based on the presence of at least 8 modules in all specimens examined here with well-defined modules. These six landmark points were imposed on the digital photographs of each specimen, and the resulting images were formatted in Adobe Illustrator for morphometric analysis in R (v4.3.1, R Core Team80). The well-defined outer margin of each specimen was manually traced to create a white polygon. The backgrounds surrounding the traced specimen were filled with a black polygon. The resulting black-and-white flat image produced a distinct outline of the photographed specimens of Spriggina readable within R80. For outline analysis, these polygons were used to generate an additional series of 200 equally spaced X–Y coordinates delimiting the outline of each specimen via an automated edge-following algorithm, starting and ending at landmark L1. Morphometric analysis was carried out in R80 using generalized Procrustes Analysis (procGPA in package shapes, v1.2.781) and Elliptical Fourier Analysis (routines given in ref82). For simplicity in presentation the resulting variations in landmark position (Procrustes) and harmonic strength (Elliptical Fourier) were analysed using Principal Components Analysis.
