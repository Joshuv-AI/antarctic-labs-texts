Acrosomal protein NEP4 is required for sperm retention in, but not entry into, the female seminal receptacle in Drosophila melanogaster
Authors: Takashi Ohsako, Kazuharu Oiwa, Rinkei Nishimura, Kimihide Ibaraki, Rikako Sanuki, Masatoshi Tomaru, Toshiyuki Takano-Shimizu-Kouno, H Jafar-Nejad
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSperm storage in the female reproductive tract is essential for efficient progeny production in animals with internal fertilization. This is particularly so for insects, especially females that have specialized organs for long-term storage. Nep4 mutant males of Drosophila melanogaster produce an average number of morphologically normal sperm, but they cannot fertilize eggs because Nep4 sperm nuclei fail to decondense after entry into the egg. Furthermore, although Nep4 mutant sperm enter the sperm storage organs in a typical manner, they are largely lost from the seminal receptacle within 24 h. It is potentially advantageous for females to discard such defective sperm. However, whether females are actively involved in this rapid sperm loss is not known. First, this study characterized NEP4 expression in wild-type testes. Between the 2 isoforms, the soluble isoform (Nep4-PB) but not the membrane-bound isoform (Nep4-PA) was essential for male fertility. NEP4 protein was localized to the acrosome of mature sperm and remained so in female storage organs. Second, we performed rescue experiments; the rapid sperm-loss phenotype of Nep4 sperm could not be rescued by mating to an eggless female nor by insemination by a wild-type male beforehand. This indicates that Nep4 sperm are likely unable to stay in and autonomously exit the seminal receptacle. This study provides evidence that sperm retention and maintenance in female sperm storage organs involve different mechanisms from those for sperm entry into the storage organs and that the acrosome of Drosophila sperm plays an important role in this process.Keywords: female sperm storage, sperm retention, Nep4, seminal receptacle, Drosophila

Introduction

In animals with internal fertilization, sperm inseminated into a female travel through their reproductive tract and may be stored in their storage organs or reservoirs for subsequent use (Neubaum and Wolfner 1999a). Sperm storage duration varies from hours to many years (Neubaum and Wolfner 1999a; Ingram et al. 2013; Gotoh and Furukawa 2018). Insect females have specialized structures for sperm storage, including spermathecae, in which sperm are maintained in a fertile state for long periods of time, in some cases up to a few decades (Simmons 2001). Female sperm storage can be distinguished from natural sperm longevity in the sense that the female plays a role in promoting sperm survival by providing a suitable environment (Orr and Zuk 2012). Indeed, sperm storage is thought to benefit females as a safeguard against sperm scarcity, by decreasing the costs of repeated mating (Fowler and Partridge 1989) and by increasing their opportunity to obtain the best-quality males available (Birkhead and Møller 1993; Orr and Brennan 2015). In addition, in polyandrous species, sperm storage creates opportunities for sperm competition (Parker 1970) and cryptic female choice (Thornhill 1983; Eberhard 1996), through which males and females strive to maximize their reproductive success. Notably, males (sperm) and females are involved in sperm storage; however, their respective roles and the underlying mechanisms involved are poorly understood.

Drosophila melanogaster females have 2 sperm storage organs, the seminal receptacle and a pair of spermathecae, allowing them to store sperm for 2 wk or more, during which they can produce hundreds of progeny (Kaufman and Demerec 1942). In general, the sperm storage process can be divided into 3 steps: sperm entry into the storage organs or transfer to the storage sites, retention and maintenance of stored sperm, and sperm release from storage (Bloch Qazi et al. 2003). Except for rare cases where sperm are delivered directly in the storage organs (e.g. Thornhill and Alcock 1983), sperm must first find the storage organs or sites. Muscular contractions of the female reproductive tract and sperm motility are involved in the transport of sperm to the storage organs in most cases (Neubaum and Wolfner 1999a; Suarez and Pacey 2006; Tomaru et al. 2018). Sperm motility is regulated by intraflagellar calcium concentrations, and calcium channel polycystic kidney disease 2 (Pkd2) is required for sperm motility change to enter the seminal receptacle in Drosophila (Yang and Lu 2011). Male seminal fluid proteins transferred to the female together with sperm also have a crucial role in sperm transport (Tram and Wolfner 1999). For instance, accessory gland protein 36DE (Acp36DE) in D. melanogaster facilitates the uterine contraction required for sperm transport (Neubaum and Wolfner 1999b; Bloch Qazi and Wolfner 2003), and transglutaminase AGAP009099 mediates mating plug formation to promote sperm storage in Anopheles gambiae (Rogers et al. 2009). Females also produce essential reproductive-tract secretions; Drosophila spermathecal secretory cells and hormone receptor-like in 39 (Hr39) expression are required for recruiting sperm to the spermathecae (Schnakenberg et al. 2011; Sun and Spradling 2013).

Male seminal fluid proteins are also involved in sperm release in Drosophila, including lectin-46Ca (CG1656), lectin-46Cb (CG1652), CG17575, CG9997 (Ravi Ram and Wolfner 2007), and Sex Peptide (Avila et al. 2010). What is unclear is how sperm are retained in the storage organs and how they are efficiently used for fertilization. To explore the mechanisms involved, we identified 2 Drosophila genes required for sperm retention after entry into the storage organs, wasted (wst) and Neprilysin 4 (Nep4). Of note, sperm of wst or Nep4 mutant males enter the sperm storage organs in a typical manner but are largely lost from the seminal receptacle within 24 h (Ohsako and Yamamoto 2011; Ohsako et al. 2021). In addition, they are functionally defective; they cannot fertilize eggs because of sperm chromatin decondensation failure (Ohsako and Yamamoto 2011; Ohsako et al. 2021). It is potentially advantageous for females to discard such defective sperm.

Here, we focused on Nep4 belonging to the neprilysin (M13) family of endopeptidases, which is a male-specific gene whose disruption does not affect viability or female fertility. However, currently it is not known when and where the NEP4 protein is expressed and whether females are actively involved in the rapid sperm loss from the seminal receptacle. In addition, unlike 3 other Neprilysins (Nep1, Nep2, and Nep5) expressed in testis, Nep4 has 2 isoforms, a typical membrane-bound isoform (Nep4-PA) and a potentially secreted, soluble isoform (Nep4-PB, PC; Meyer et al. 2021), but it is not yet clear which isoform is required for male fertility. The aims of this study were 3-fold: first, to determine which isoforms are mainly expressed in testes; second, to characterize the NEP4 protein expression pattern in sperm cells; and third, to investigate the female's role in the rapid sperm loss. For the last aim, we put forward 3 hypotheses for the rapid loss of Nep4 sperm from the seminal receptacle of mated females: (i) the inability of Nep4 sperm to elicit female responses for sperm storage, (ii) the active dumping of sperm by females, and (iii) the inability of Nep4 sperm to remain in the storage organ. We tested these hypotheses by an insemination rescue experiment in which females were doubly mated: first with wild-type males and then later with Nep4 males. If the first hypothesis is correct, Nep4 sperm were expected to be maintained in the storage organ like normal sperm. Under hypothesis 2, Nep4 and normal sperm would be actively removed from the storage organ. Finally, if the third hypothesis is correct, Nep4 sperm would quickly and selectively disappear from the storage organ.

This study reveals that the soluble isoform is the main testis transcript and is required for male fertility. Moreover, the enhanced green fluorescent protein (EGFP)–tagged NEP4 protein was localized in the sperm head, likely in the acrosome, in the testes and seminal vesicles of males and remained so in the female storage organs. The rapid sperm-loss phenotype of Nep4 mutant sperm could not be rescued by mating to an eggless female nor by insemination by a wild-type male beforehand, which suggests the autonomous exit of Nep4 mutant sperm from the female seminal receptacle. This indicates that sperm can change their motility behavior after entry into the seminal receptacle, which is required for retention of sperm in the storage organ.

Materials and methods

Drosophila strains

We obtained y w (DGRC #106349), w; ProtamineB-eGFP/CyO (DGRC #109173), and w; ProtB-DsRed (DGRC #109645) from the KYOTO Drosophila Stock Center (Kyoto, Japan) and y vas-Cas9 (BDSC# 66554) from the Bloomington Drosophila Stock Center (BDSC, Bloomington, IN, USA).

Construction of promoter-specific deletions and C-terminal EGFP tagging of NEP4 protein

To generate isoform-specific deletions, we designed 2 single-guide RNAs (sgRNAs) targeting the proximal promoter region (sgNep4-RA[1] and sgNep4-RA[2]) and 2 sgRNAs targeting the distal promoter region (sgNep4-RC[2] and sgNep4-RC[3]). For the EGFP tagging of NEP4 protein, we designed 2 sgRNAs targeting the stop codon (sgNep4-C[1] and sgNep4-C[2]). All sgRNAs were cloned into the pCFD5 vector (Port and Bullock 2016) singly or in combination.

To implement the Co-CRISPR strategy (Ge et al. 2016; Kane et al. 2017), we injected a pair of plasmids targeting the proximal (pCFD5-sgNep4-RA1 and pCFD5-sgNep4-RA2) or distal promoter (pCFD5-sgNep4-RC2 and pCFD5-sgNep4-RC3) at a concentration of 250 ng/μL each and sgRNA targeting the ebony locus (pCFD3-ebony, 100 ng/μL) into embryos of the y vas-Cas9 strain (BDSC 66554). Similarly, we injected a donor plasmid (pCR-Blunt-Nep4C-EGFP) and a single plasmid containing the 2 sgRNAs (pCFD5-sgNep4-C) into y vas-Cas9 embryos for C-terminal tagging. Injected G0 males were individually mated to w; TM6B, Tb e/e Pr females, and we calculated the frequency of ebony offspring. All F1 males from vials with a frequency of ebony offspring of 20% or more (jackpot) were individually backcrossed to w; TM6B, Tb e/e Pr females.

A few days later, we extracted genomic DNA from these F1 males using the Kaneka Easy DNA Extraction Kit (Kaneka, Japan) and performed PCR screening for deletions or insertions. In this way, we created deletion mutants in the proximal (w; Nep4[A1]/TM6B, Tb[1] e and w; Nep4[A2]/TM6B, Tb[1] e) and distal promoter regions (w; Nep4[B2]/TM6B, Tb[1]) and a transgenic line expressing EGFP-tagged NEP4 protein (w; Nep4[C_EGFP] e/TM6B, Tb e). We also generated w; ProtB-DsRed Nep4[C_EGFP] e/TM6B, Tb e by recombination. The relevant sgRNA target and PCR primer sequences are given in Reagent Table.

Real-time PCR analysis

We extracted total RNA from the testes, reverse transcribed it into cDNA, and quantified Nep4 transcripts by real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) as described in Ohsako et al. (2021) with minor modifications, including use of the NucleoSpin RNA Plus XS Kit (Takara Bio Inc.) and SuperScript IV VILO Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific). qPCR primers (Fig. 1a) were designed to identify Nep4-RA (qNep4-RA: qNep4-RA_F2 and R2), Nep4-RB and -RC (qNep4-RB: qNep4-RB_F2 and R2), and all the transcripts (qNep4_1: qNep4_F1 and R1; qNep4_2: qNep4_F2 and R2). See Reagent Table for sequences. We performed qPCR using PowerUp SYBR Green Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and the StepOne Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems). We normalized expression values to those of RpL32 (qRpL32-F and qRpL32-R).

Western blot experiments

We obtained protein lysates from the testes, ovaries, and heads of Nep4[C_EGFP] and y w flies, of which the latter was used as a negative control. The protein lysates after sonication were subjected to electrophoresis and western blot analysis. We used primary antibodies against green fluorescent protein (GFP; 1:1,000, code: 598, MBL) and tubulin (1:3,000, clone: 6G7-s, DSHB), along with peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies against rabbit IgG (1:10,000, code: 711-035-152, Jackson ImmunoResearch) and mouse IgG (1:10,000, code: 715-035-150, Jackson ImmunoResearch). Proteins were detected using the Immobilon Forte Western HRP substrate (code: WBLUF0100, Millipore), and the signal images were collected by a chemiluminescence imaging system (ImageQuant LAS 500, Cytiva). The signal intensity of the bands was quantified using ImageQuant TL software (version 8.1, Cytiva) and normalized to the corresponding TUB signal.

Concanavalin A histochemistry of testes

Testes were dissected from Nep4[C_EGFP] males in PBS (0.15 M phosphate buffer, pH 7.2, containing 1.1% NaCl, pH 7.2) and fixed in 2% paraformaldehyde for 10 min. Fixed testes were washed several times with PBS, treated with 0.1 M NH4Cl in PBS for 30 min, and rinsed again in PBS. They were incubated for 15 min with 50 μg/mL Concanavalin A (Con A) conjugated to Alexa Fluor 594 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat No. C11253) in PBS supplemented with 1 mM CaCl2 and 1 mM MgCl2 to label the acrosomal region of sperm (Perotti and Pasini 1995). After washing with PBS, the slides were mounted using ProLong Glass Antifade Mountant with NucBlue stain (Life Technologies, Eugene, OR, USA).

Agametic female experiments

Eggless females were generated by crossing UASp-OvoA males with nanos-Gal4-VP16 females (Hayashi et al. 2017; Asaoka et al. 2021; Nishimura et al. 2023). We mated 3-, 4-, or 5-d-old eggless (nanos>OvoA) and normal (nanos>+) virgin females with 3-, 4-, or 5-d-old ProtamineB-eGFP/+; Nep4[c2] or ProtamineB-eGFP/CyO; Nep4[wt] males and collected females that had copulated for 10 min or longer during a 1-h period. Nep4[wt] is a control line in which no CRISPR/Cas9-induced mutation was found. We took images of female reproductive tracts at 1 or 24 h after the end of copulation (AEC) using a Nikon Eclipse NiU upright microscope (Nikon, Japan) equipped with a Nikon DS-Qi2 digital camera (Nikon) and NIS-Elements (BR) imaging software (version 4.6, Nikon). We manually counted sperm in the females’ sperm storage organs as described in Ohsako et al. (2021).

Insemination rescue experiments

To test the 3 hypotheses put forward to explain the rapid loss of Nep4 sperm from the seminal receptacles of mated females, we mated 3-, 4-, or 5-d-old virgin y w females individually with 3-, 4-, or 5-d-old w; ProtB-DsRed (M1) males and collected the females that had copulated for 10 min or longer during a 1-h period. They were aged for 2 d (47 to 49 h) in new vials and then mated individually with 3-, 4-, or 5-d-old y w; ProtamineB-eGFP/+; Nep4[c2] (M2) males. We froze the double-mated females at 1 or 24 h after the end of the second copulation for subsequent sperm counting. We counted ProtB-DsRed (wild type) and ProtamineB-eGFP (Nep4[c2]) sperm in seminal receptacles and paired spermathecae. As a control, we mated y w females once with y w; ProtamineB-eGFP/+; Nep4[c2] males and then froze the females that had copulated for 10 min or longer at 1 or 24 h AEC for subsequent dissection.

Results

The soluble isoform is the major transcript in adult testis

ΔNep4[A1] is a 459-bp deletion in the proximal promoter region; ΔNep4[B2] is a 452-bp deletion in the distal promoter region (Fig. 1b). Neither had an altered Nep4 coding sequence. By contrast, ΔNep4[A2] is a 762-bp deletion that includes a 112-bp coding region common to both isoforms (Fig. 1b) and therefore is expected to be a null mutation.

As expected, Nep4-RB and -RC-specific mRNA expression measured by qPCR with a qNep4-RB_2 primer pair was severely reduced or absent in ΔNep4[B2] and ΔNep4[c2] (Fig. 1f), and the latter was a 1,767-bp deletion spanning exons 2 and 3 (Fig. 1b; Ohsako et al. 2021). By contrast, Nep4-RA-specific mRNA expression did not significantly differ among the 4 alleles studied (Fig. 1e). Because we could not detect Nep4 expression in ΔNep4[B2] and ΔNep4[c2] when using primer pairs designed to amplify the exons common to all 3 isoforms (qNep4_1 and _2; Fig. 1c and d), it is very likely that Nep4-RA was expressed at only low levels in testes. Indeed, the reverse transcription qPCR (RT-qPCR) Ct value for the qNep4-RA_2 pair was significantly larger than the values for the qNep4-RB_2 and qNep4_2 pairs.

The soluble isoform is essential for male fertility

The hatchability of eggs laid by females mated with ΔNep4[B2] homozygous males and ΔNep4[A2] males was zero or almost zero (Fig. 2a). Because the same was shown in ΔNep4[B2]/ΔNep4[c2] heterozygotes and because the hatchability was restored in heterozygotes with wild-type Nep4[wt] (Fig. 2b), the sterility of ΔNep4[B2] males is very likely caused by the Nep4 deletion. By contrast, the hatchability of eggs did not differ between those laid by females mated with ΔNep4[A1] males and those mated with wild-type males (Fig. 2a).

Hatchability of eggs laid by females mated with Nep4 mutant males. (a) Homozygotes and (b) trans-heterozygotes. The bars represent median values. The number of females tested is given in parentheses. The mean number of eggs tested per female was 22.7 ± 0.7, ranging from 5 to 47.

Collectively, these results indicate that the soluble isoform (Nep4-PB) but not the membrane-bound isoform (Nep4-PA) is essential for male fertility.

Persistent expression of NEP4 in the sperm head

Western blotting with an antibody against GFP revealed different expression patterns in the head and testis of Nep4[C_EGFP] males. Consistent with the qPCR results, the major isoform in the testis was smaller than that in the head and likely corresponded to Nep4-PB (Fig. 3). Nep4 mutant sperm are not retained in the seminal receptacle regardless of ovulation

As described in a study by Bloch Qazi and Wolfner (2006), agametic (eggless, nanos>OvoA) females mated with wild-type (Nep4[wt]) males retained more sperm in their seminal receptacles than the control females (nanos>+) 24 h AEC (Fig. 7a, c, and e; median = 385, n = 18 vs median = 147.0, n = 12; Mann–Whitney U test = 0, P < 0.0001). This was also true when Nep4[c2] males were used (Fig. 7b, d, and e; median = 129.0, n = 43 vs median = 23.0, n = 17; Mann–Whitney U test = 38, P < 0.0001). However, even in eggless females, the sperm of Nep4[c2] males were removed from seminal receptacles faster than those of normal males (Fig. 7c, d, and e), which indicated the mechanism involved in the ovulation-independent loss of Nep4 mutant sperm.

Inability of Nep4 mutant sperm to remain in the seminal receptacle cannot be rescued by seminal fluids from normal males

Although the Nep4 male had many more sperm than the first wild-type male 1 h after the end of the second copulation with a Nep4 male, there was no difference between them at 24 h. The reduction during 1 to 24 h was more extensive for Nep4 sperm than normal sperm; indeed, a highly significant interaction factor was found between males and time points (Fig. 8; F = 102.4, d.f. = (1, 66), P < 0.0001). The disproportionate decrease in the number of Nep4 sperm compared with that of normal sperm supports our third hypothesis; namely, Nep4 sperm are unable to remain in the seminal receptacle, and this defect cannot be rescued by seminal fluids from normal males. This further suggests that the function of NEP4 is cell-autonomous. At the same time, the result rejects our first (inability of Nep4 sperm to elicit female responses for sperm storage) and second (active dumping of sperm by females) hypotheses.

Discussion

Here, we showed that NEP4 protein was localized to the acrosome of mature sperm. The sneaky (snky) gene also encodes an acrosomal protein. snky mutant sperm failed to form the male pronucleus like Nep4 mutant sperm, although snky sperm are stored normally in seminal receptacles (Wilson et al. 2006). To the best of our knowledge, this is the first report of an essential role of the Drosophila acrosome in sperm storage.

Although ovulation stimulates and enhances sperm release from seminal receptacles, Nep4 mutant sperm disappeared from the seminal receptacles of eggless females at a higher rate compared with wild-type sperm. This indicates the loss (dumping) of Nep4 sperm independent of muscular contractions of the female reproductive tract associated with ovulation.

Despite the defect in sperm storage, Nep4 mutant sperm still had good sperm competitive ability. Indeed, the fraction of sperm from Nep4 mutant males in seminal receptacles 1 h after the end of a second copulation with Nep4 mutant males ranged from 100% to 61.7% with a median of 92.3% (all storage organs: 95.2% to 45.6%; median, 79.1%). This observation indicates a lack of sperm defects during their entry into seminal receptacles and the very early stage of retention in seminal receptacles.

NEP4 hydrolyzes sarcolamban micropeptides (SCLA and SCLB), which bind and inhibit the activity of a calcium transporter, Sarco/endoplasmic reticulum Ca(2+)-ATPase (SERCA), in muscle tissue. NEP4 prohibits the excessive inhibition of SERCA, leading to the SERCA transport of calcium ions (Ca2+) into the sarcoplasmic reticulum, which, in turn, reduces Ca2+ levels in the cytoplasm ([Ca2+]I, Schiemann et al. 2022). Whether the same mechanism operates in sperm is not known, but Ca2+ plays key roles in sperm capacitation, hyperactivation, and acrosome reaction in mammals (Yanagimachi and Usui 1974; Fraser 1982; Suarez et al. 1993; Breitbart 2002 ; De Blas et al. 2002; Quill et al. 2003; Suarez 2008; Nakidkina and Kuzmina 2022).

A low level of cytosolic Ca2+ in spermatozoa is maintained until capacitation (Singh et al. 1978), which is mainly mediated by so-called Ca2+-ATPases, including SERCA (Nakidkina and Kuzmina 2022). Indeed, a SERCA inhibitor, thapsigargin, induces acrosome reaction via the elevation of [Ca2+]i (Meizel and Turner 1993; Llanos 1998; Herrick et al. 2005). Thus, it is possible that Nep4 disruption maintained or augmented sperm motility because of the inability to maintain a low cytosolic Ca2+ concentration. Because slower sperm are more likely to remain in the seminal receptacle (Lüpold et al. 2012; Manier et al. 2013), high motility may lead to the excessive autonomous exit of sperm from the seminal receptacle. However, this hypothesis contradicts the observation that sperm motility does not significantly change during the few days after mating unless the females are remated to a second male (Manier et al. 2010, 2013). It is possible that untimely or unnecessary hyperactivation occurs in Nep4 mutant sperm, which would reconcile this contradiction. In any case, further investigation is needed to understand the mechanisms by which Nep4 mutant sperm are lost from, and normal sperm are maintained, in female seminal receptacles.

Of note, future studies are required to substantiate our findings. This study offers evidence that sperm retention and maintenance in female sperm storage organs involve different mechanisms from those associated with sperm entry into the storage organs and that the acrosome of Drosophila sperm plays an important role in this process.
