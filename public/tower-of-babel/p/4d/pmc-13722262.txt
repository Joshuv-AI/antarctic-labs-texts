Knowledge, attitude, practice, and perceived barriers of colorectal cancer screening among family physicians in Riyadh second health cluster
Authors: Abdulrahman Abdulaziz Alkthiry, Hassan Saleh Almohammadi, Khalid Saad Alhamlan, Abdulmalik Khaled Albadi, Abdalah Emad Almhmd
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:One of the main causes of cancer-related death globally, colorectal cancer (CRC), is becoming more common in emerging nations. CRC morbidity and death are considerably decreased by effective screening. Family doctors are essential to the implementation of CRC screening.Aim:To assess the knowledge, attitudes, practices (KAP), and perceived barriers related to CRC screening among family physicians working in the Riyadh second health cluster.Methods:In 2025, board-certified family doctors in Saudi Arabia’s Riyadh second health cluster participated in a cross-sectional questionnaire-based survey. A validated self-administered computerized questionnaire covering demographics, KAP, and perceived barriers to CRC screening was used to gather data.Results:A total of 98 physicians participated. Most physicians demonstrated poor knowledge (82.7%) and poor screening practices (82.7%), despite generally positive attitudes toward CRC screening (56.1%). Formal training was significantly associated with better knowledge and attitudes. Patient-related barriers included fear of diagnosis and lack of awareness, while lack of time was the most prominent physician-related barrier. System-related barriers included long waiting times and a lack of patient education tools. Attitude was significantly associated with both knowledge and practice, and a strong positive correlation was observed between attitude and practice scores (r = 0.40, P < 0.001).Conclusion:Despite the fact that family doctors showed favorable sentiments regarding CRC screening, there are still significant knowledge and practice gaps. Implementing CRC screening may be improved by addressing time constraints, improving physician training, and bolstering system-level support.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is one of the leading causes of cancer-related mortality worldwide.[1] CRC is becoming more common in emerging nations.[2]

The glandular, epithelial cells of the large intestine are typically the source of CRC. The cancer develops when specific epithelial cells undergo a number of genetic alterations that dramatically boost their ability to replicate and survive.[34] These hyperproliferative cells result in a benign adenoma, which over time may develop into carcinoma and spread.[56] Screening is essential for early detection and prevention because of the disease’s high incidence and substantial mortality. Because it can identify precancerous polyps, which can be removed before they turn into cancer, it is crucial. Additionally, early-stage colorectal tumors found through screening have a higher chance of being effectively treated, which dramatically lowers death rates.[7]

Regular screening can reduce CRC mortality by about 60%.[8] Screening methods include sigmoidoscopy, colonoscopy, fecal occult blood tests (FOBT), and fecal immunochemical testing (FIT). Colonoscopy is considered the gold standard as it allows detection and removal of polyps in a single procedure.[9] However, screening remains underutilized due to barriers such as cultural stigma, limited awareness, and restricted healthcare access, particularly in underserved populations.[1011] Addressing these barriers is essential to improve screening uptake and reduce disease burden.[1011]

Because they frequently interact with a wide range of patients, primary healthcare providers play a significant role in screening practices. Several guidelines propose that primary healthcare professionals be involved in the implementation of screening.[12] Teaching primary care doctors about screening recommendations, motivating doctors to talk to patients about CRC screening, and enhancing doctors’ ability to monitor and complete CRC screening.[13]

By addressing gaps in primary care CRC screening, this study provides important benefits for both patients and practitioners. Improved physician knowledge and practices can enhance early detection, increase screening uptake, and reduce morbidity and mortality. Better physician communication may also help overcome patient-related barriers such as fear, misconceptions, and lack of awareness, promoting informed decision-making and preventive behaviors.[14] For practitioners, the study identifies key areas for improvement, including targeted training, time management, and system-level support. Addressing these gaps can help healthcare organizations implement effective educational programs, improve workflow efficiency, and strengthen screening practices, ultimately improving preventive care quality and outcomes.[15]

This study aimed to assess the knowledge, attitude, practice (KAP), and perceived barriers of CRC screening among family physicians in Riyadh second health cluster.

Subjects and methods

Study design, location, and time: A cross-sectional questionnaire-based study was done at the Riyadh second health cluster, Riyadh region, Saudi Arabia.

Study participants: The inclusion criteria were board-certified family medicine physicians of both genders working in Riyadh second health cluster, and the exclusion criteria were family medicine residents and those who refused sharing in the study.

The sample size was calculated at a 95% confidence level, assuming an expected response proportion of 50%. An initial sample size was estimated using a 5% margin of error. Given that the total population of family medicine physicians was small (approximately 130 physicians), finite population correction was applied. After adjustment, the minimum required sample size was calculated to be 98 family medicine physicians.

Data collection: A validated self-administered questionnaire was distributed electronically via email. The questionnaire included demographic data, physician awareness regarding CRC screening guidelines, attitudes toward screening, and perceived barriers to implementation. For every correct answer in the knowledge and practice section or a positive attitude, a score of “1” was given, and for every wrong or “I don’t know” answer or negative attitude, a score of “0” was given. The participant was considered as having poor knowledge if less than 50% was correct, fair knowledge if 50–75% was correct, and good knowledge if more than 75% was correct.[1617]

Ethical approval: Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the research ethics committee of King Fahad Medical City Institution Review Board, Riyadh region, Saudi Arabia.

Data analysis: Data were statistically analyzed using the SPSS application version 26. To investigate the association between the variables, the Chi-squared test (χ2) was applied to qualitative data that was expressed as numbers and percentages. Quantitative variables were expressed as mean and standard deviation (mean ± SD) and correlation analysis was performed using Spearman’s test. Statistical significance was defined as a P value of less than 0.05.

Results

A total of 98 physicians participated in the study. The highest proportion of participants was aged 25–35 years, accounting for 66.3%, indicating a predominantly young physician workforce. Females constituted the majority of respondents (54.1%). Regarding professional experience, 50.0% of physicians reported 5–10 years of practice, representing the largest experience category. Most participants were employed in primary healthcare centers (PHCs) (79.6%), while a smaller proportion worked in tertiary hospitals (20.4%). Importantly, the vast majority of physicians (83.7%) reported having received formal training on CRC screening guidelines [Table 1].

Physicians demonstrated a high level of awareness of CRC screening guidelines. The USPSTF guidelines were the most commonly followed, reported by 88.8% of participants. Regarding screening modalities, the FIT was most frequently recommended, selected by 98.0% of physicians. Most correctly identified the recommended starting age for average-risk patients as 45 years (86.7%). Familiarity with FOBT methods was generally high, with 39.8% moderately familiar and 38.8% very familiar. CRC screening discussions were highly prevalent, with 91.8% of physicians discussing screening at every annual visit. The majority (82.7%) recommended or ordered ≤20 FOBT/FIT tests per month, while colonoscopy and sigmoidoscopy were ordered less frequently, with 98.0% reporting ≤20 procedures per month. Regarding referrals, most physicians (89.8%) referred patients requiring colonoscopy to a gastroenterologist, representing the predominant referral pathway [Table 2].

Physicians demonstrated overwhelmingly positive attitudes toward CRC screening. Nearly all participants (98.0%) believed that CRC screening reduces mortality in average-risk patients. Regarding perceived effectiveness, FOBT/FIT was considered effective by 95.9% of physicians, and colonoscopy by 98.0%, indicating strong confidence in these methods. Perceptions of CT colonography were more variable, with 70.4% viewing it as effective, 18.4% uncertain, and 11.2% considering it ineffective. Awareness of stool DNA testing was lower; 45.9% did not know its effectiveness, while 35.7% considered it effective. Most physicians (72.4%) preferred an organized CRC screening program (CRCSP) over opportunistic screening (27.6%). Comfort in discussing CRC screening was high, with 75.5% very comfortable, 15.3% somewhat comfortable, and 9.2% neutral [Table 3].

Physicians reported multiple barriers to effective CRC screening across patient, physician, and system levels. Patient-related barriers included fear of diagnosis (74.5%), lack of awareness (66.3%), fear of the procedure (65.3%), and cultural beliefs (45.9%), while cost was least cited (10.2%). Physician-related barriers were dominated by a lack of time (95.9%). System-related barriers included long waiting times (50.0%), lack of patient education tools (43.9%), and absence of reminder systems (34.7%). Limited access to referral services was reported by 24.5%, and lack of institutional policies or guideline systems was infrequent (2.0%) [Table 4].

Discussion

This study evaluated family doctors’ KAPs, and perceived barriers to CRC screening in the Riyadh second health cluster. Only 17.3% of respondents had either fair understanding or practice, and 56.1% had a favorable attitude, according to the current study. On the other hand, research conducted in the Jazan region in 2025 also revealed a high level of knowledge.[18] Additionally, a study conducted at the National Guard Health Affairs in Riyadh revealed that doctors’ knowledge of CRC screening is adequate.[12]

Additional research has shown that many healthcare professionals must get up to date on CRC screening and the recommendations for commonly used CRC screening methods, such as FOBT, sigmoidoscopy, and colonoscopy.[192021] In a study conducted in Buraydah, Qassim, Saudi Arabia, PHC physicians were found to have a lower level of expertise while having a better attitude toward CRC screening.[22]

Regarding CRC screening procedures, 98.0% of doctors suggested FIT. 86.7% of respondents correctly picked 45 as the beginning age for average-risk screening. This is greater than Demyati (39.2%)[12] and Mosli et al. (32.3%).[23] This percentage is also greater than that found in a study conducted in Buraydah, Qassim, Saudi Arabia, where 75% of PHC doctors would begin screening at age 45.[22] Adults between the ages of 45 and 75 should get screened, according to guidelines made by the US Preventive Task Force and according to the CDC (2020).[24]

Doctors discussed CRC screening at each annual visit in 91.8% of cases. Most recommended or ordered ≤20 sigmoidoscopies or colonoscopies per month (98.0%) and ≤20 FOBT/FIT tests (82.7%), with colonoscopy referrals mainly sent to gastroenterologists (89.8%). Despite 95% believing CRC screening is effective, only 45% of primary healthcare doctors actually perform it.[23] Similarly, Deobald et al. (2013)[25] reported low CRC screening recommendation rates among 773 family physicians.

Nearly all doctors (98.0%) felt that CRC screening lowers mortality, according to their attitudes regarding the procedure. Colonoscopy was deemed successful by 98.0% and FOBT/FIT by 95.9%. 70.4% of doctors thought CT colonography was successful, while 45.9% were unsure about the efficacy of stool DNA testing. The majority favored organized screening programs (72.4%) over opportunistic screening (27.6%). The majority of doctors (75.5%) reported they were quite at ease talking about CRC screening. According to a prior study conducted in Saudi Arabia’s Jazan region, every doctor surveyed had a favorable opinion of CRC screening.[18] This was also seen in a 2014 study by Demyati et al.[12] among family doctors at the National Guard Hospital, where the general attitude toward CRC screening was favorable. According to a study by Hannon et al.[13] on primary care physicians in the United States, 76% of them suggested one or more CRC screening tests in accordance with American Cancer Society recommendations.

The most frequent patient-related obstacles to CRC screening were fear of the procedure (65.3%), lack of awareness (66.3%), and fear of the diagnosis (74.5%). Cost was indicated by 10.2% and cultural beliefs by 45.9%. Lack of time is a physician-related obstacle (95.9%). Long wait times (50.0%), a lack of patient education resources (43.9%), and a lack of reminder systems (34.7%) are system-related obstacles. Lack of institutional policies was 2.0%, and restricted access to referral services was 24.5%.

In this study, male physicians, those with more clinical experience, and doctors working in tertiary hospitals were significantly more likely to demonstrate fair knowledge of CRC screening. Younger physicians with 5 to 10 years of practice and those who had received formal training showed the most positive attitudes toward CRC screening. Regarding practice, physicians and female doctors working in PHCs were more likely to report fair screening practices. Supporting these findings, Demyati (2014) reported that male physicians generally held more favorable attitudes toward CRC screening than females, which may explain why female doctors had comparatively lower favorable attitudes toward CRC screening than toward breast cancer screening. Conversely, male physicians with less formal training were less likely to recommend CRC screening to their patients, highlighting the importance of targeted training programs.[1222262728]

Patient information materials, simpler referral systems, updated training programs, reminder tools, and increased insurance coverage were the tactics suggested by the physicians under study to enhance CRC screening. These tactics of patient-directed internet outreach and provider-focused education were also described in earlier research as useful, multifaceted, and inexpensive.[2930]

In this study, males, more experienced physicians, and those working in tertiary hospitals were more likely to have fair knowledge of CRC screening. Younger doctors with 5–10 years of experience and formal training exhibited the most positive attitudes. Regarding practice, physicians and women in PHCs were more likely to report fair practices. Similarly, Demyati (2014)[12] found that male doctors generally held more favorable attitudes toward CRC screening than females, which may explain why female doctors had lower favorable attitudes toward CRC screening compared to breast cancer screening. Conversely, less-trained male doctors were less likely to recommend screening.[22]

The results of this study showed a strong positive correlation between knowledge and attitude scores as well as between attitude and practice scores. Previous investigations found the same substantial relationships between KAP components and CRC screening.[3132] According to earlier research, a lack of awareness regarding CRC screening may have contributed to the low participation rate for CRCSPs. Additionally, participants who were comparatively ignorant of CRC screening had negative views toward it since their lack of understanding about it made them reluctant to take part in the screening.[263334]

Primary healthcare physicians (PHPs) in PHCs are primarily responsible for the early diagnosis of cancer and chronic disorders. Therefore, starting screening initiatives at the primary healthcare level is the best option.[1920] Prior research included teaching primary care doctors about screening recommendations, encouraging doctors to talk to their patients about CRC screening, and enhancing doctors’ ability to monitor and complete CRC screening.[1920212223]

Limitations

A limitation of the present study was the use of a self-reported questionnaire that could have a recall bias. Another limitation was the cross-sectional study design that could reveal the associations between variables but not the causal relationships.

Conclusion

This study reveals a significant gap between family physicians’ positive attitudes and their actual knowledge and practices regarding CRC screening in the Riyadh second health cluster. Although physicians recognized the importance and effectiveness of CRC screening, most demonstrated inadequate knowledge and suboptimal screening practices. Attitude emerged as a key factor influencing practice, often outweighing knowledge alone. Major barriers included patient-related fears, low awareness, physician time constraints, and system-level inefficiencies. These findings emphasize the need for structured educational programs, streamlined referral systems, reminder tools, and accessible patient education materials. Future research should evaluate the impact of targeted educational and system-level interventions, while longitudinal and multicenter studies are recommended to assess causality and improve the generalizability of findings across diverse healthcare settings.
