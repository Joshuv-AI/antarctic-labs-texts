Epidemiological characteristics of pulmonary thromboembolism in children: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Jianing Liu, Xiaofeng Ni, Linan Zeng, Xia Song, Sha Diao, Lina Chen, Hanmin Liu, Lingli Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesPulmonary thromboembolism (PTE) is rare but potentially life-threatening in children and differs substantially from adult PTE in epidemiology and risk factors. We conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis to summarise the epidemiology, clinical characteristics and risk factors of paediatric PTE.DesignA systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines.Data sourcesWe searched eight databases (PubMed, Embase (Ovid), MEDLINE (Ovid), Cochrane Library and four Chinese databases) on 2 March 2023, with an updated search on 2 July 2024.Eligibility criteriaWe included original studies reporting epidemiology (incidence/prevalence, major clinical outcomes and long-term sequelae), clinical characteristics, or risk factors of PTE in patients younger than 18 years. Reviews, case reports/series without extractable epidemiological data and studies not focused on paediatric PTE were excluded.Data extraction and synthesisTwo reviewers independently screened studies, extracted data and assessed risk of bias using appropriate tools for observational studies. A random-effects model was applied to pool prevalence and incidence estimates, along with related outcomes, and their 95% CIs, regardless of the degree of heterogeneity.ResultsFrom 14 438 records, 26 studies were included: 16 cross-sectional, 9 cohort and 1 case–control study. 23 studies were marked as high quality and three as moderate quality. The pooled incidence of paediatric PTE was 0.016% (95% CI 0.0011% to 0.0298%). Autopsy-confirmed prevalence was 5.29% (95% CI 1.28% to 9.30%). Case fatality was 4.04% (95% CI 0.49% to 7.58%), and recurrence was 26.31% (95% CI 19.62% to 33.01%). Available evidence suggested an increasing trend in PTE occurrence over recent years. Female sex and oral contraceptive use were frequently reported clinical characteristics in adolescents. Reported risk factors clustered into genetic predisposition, underlying diseases, immobilisation or surgery and central venous catheter-related thrombosis.ConclusionsPaediatric PTE remains uncommon but appears to be increasing, with notable recurrence and non-negligible fatality. Given heterogeneous study settings and definitions, further high-quality, population-based studies are needed to refine epidemiological estimates and clarify age-specific risk profiles, thereby informing prevention strategies and clinical management.Trial registration numberPREPARE-2023CN922.

Introduction

Pulmonary embolism (PE) refers to a clinical syndrome resulting from obstruction of pulmonary arteries or their branches by various emboli.1 Pulmonary thromboembolism (PTE) is the most common type of acute PE, accounting for more than 90% of PE2 and is caused by thrombi originating from the venous system, right heart or in situ pulmonary arterial thrombosis that obstruct the pulmonary artery or its branches. The main pathophysiological features and clinical characteristics are pulmonary circulatory and respiratory dysfunction.3

PTE, though rare in children, poses a life-threatening risk, often leading to severe complications and increasing mortality.4 In recent years, with the advancement of imaging technology, the diagnosis rate and incidence of PTE have gradually increased,46 attracting widespread attention in the medical community. Importantly, paediatric PTE is frequently underdiagnosed in clinical practice because its presenting symptoms and signs are often non-specific (eg, dyspnoea, chest pain, tachycardia) and commonly attributed to more prevalent paediatric respiratory or cardiac conditions. The diagnostic uncertainty, together with the low prior probability of the disease, increases the risk of missed or delayed diagnosis, which may in turn worsen clinical outcomes. Moreover, paediatric PTE differs from adult PTE in clinical contexts, with risk clustering around childhood-specific comorbidities and healthcare exposures, and dedicated paediatric evidence and guidance remain limited. For these reasons, a comprehensive synthesis of paediatric-specific epidemiology, clinical characteristics and risk factors is needed to support clinician vigilance and timely evaluation when PTE is a realistic differential diagnosis. Therefore, we conducted this systematic review and meta-analysis to describe the epidemiology (incidence/prevalence, the frequency of major outcomes and long-term sequelae), clinical characteristics and risk factors of PTE in children, aiming to enhance clinicians’ awareness and focus.

Methods

This study was undertaken to inform the clinical guideline ‘Management of pulmonary thromboembolism in children: an evidence-based expert consensus’, registered in the Practice guideline REgistration for transPAREncy (PREPARE; PREPARE-2023CN922).7

Search strategy

We conducted a comprehensive search across PubMed, Embase (Ovid), Cochrane Library, CBM, CNKI, Wanfang and VIP Database to identify the primary studies focusing on the epidemiological characteristics of PTE in children. The initial search was conducted on 2 March 2023, covering all records from database inception to that date, and an updated search was conducted on 2 July 2024. In addition, we searched grey literature, including conference abstracts, theses/dissertations and other non-peer-reviewed reports where relevant to find more potential studies. The search strategy used both subject headings and free-text terms. A full list of search terms and complete search strings for each database is provided in online supplemental appendix 1. Age filters or search formulas were applied to restrict participants to <18 years.

Eligibility criteria

Inclusion criteria: (1) Participants were children aged <18 years with PTE. (2) Original studies that reported at least one of the following for paediatric PTE: epidemiology (incidence/prevalence, the frequency of major outcomes and long-term sequelae, and for the long-term sequelae with chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension, chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension (CTEPH) selected as the representative via expert consultation), clinical characteristics or risk factors. (3) As many paediatric studies did not separate PTE from PE, we retained PE-labelled studies unless they clearly described non-thrombotic PE.

Exclusion criteria: (1) Studies explicitly reporting non-thrombotic PE (eg, fat, air, amniotic fluid, tumour, septic or foreign-material embolism) were excluded. (2) We excluded studies clearly describing isolated primary in situ pulmonary arterial thrombosis without an embolic source; studies labelled PE/PTE without aetiological specification were included. (3) Studies in which paediatric data could not be extracted separately, or in which relevant epidemiological, clinical-characteristic or risk-factor data for children were not extractable. (4) Duplicate publications, abstracts without full text, case reports/series without extractable epidemiological data or studies lacking sufficient methodological detail.

Study selection and data extraction

After removing duplicates, two researchers (XN and JL) independently reviewed the titles and abstracts based on the inclusion criteria. Then, the same authors independently reviewed the full text of potentially relevant articles for inclusion and extracted data. Any disagreements were resolved by discussion or consulting a third reviewer (LZ). When necessary, we contacted the original authors via email to clarify crucial but uncertain information. The extracted data included: (1) the basic information of included studies, including title, first author, country, year and study type; (2) the basic information of participants, including sample size, gender and age; (3) outcome, including incidence rate, case-fatality rate, recurrence rate, long-term sequelae rate (CTEPH), risk factors and clinical characteristics. In this review, clinical characteristics refer to descriptive, non-comparative data reported for children with confirmed PTE (eg, demographic distribution and presenting context), whereas risk factors refer to variables that the original studies explicitly examined in association with PTE occurrence (eg, derived from comparative or multivariable analyses).

Quality assessment

Two researchers (XN and JL) independently reviewed the risk of bias of included studies, and any disagreements were resolved by discussion or consulting a third reviewer (LZ). The Cochrane Risk of Bias8 was used to evaluate the randomised controlled trials, the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale9 was used for cohort and case–control studies, and an 11-item checklist, which was recommended by the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality, was used for cross-sectional studies.10 For cohort studies and case–control studies, a total score of 1–3 indicates low quality, 4–6 indicates moderate quality and 7–9 indicates high quality. For cross-sectional studies, a total score of 0–3 indicates low quality, 4–7 indicates moderate quality and 8–11 indicates high quality.

Data analysis

The pooled prevalence rate and 95% CI were calculated using Stata V.17.0 (Stata Statistical Software: College Station, Texas: StataCorp). The analysis assessed heterogeneity using the I² statistic and applied the random-effects model regardless of heterogeneity.11 For cases with clear clinical heterogeneity, we prespecified subgroup analyses (by study setting, region and age group), and for outcomes reported by more than 10 studies, we conducted meta-regression to identify the sources of heterogeneity. Given high residual heterogeneity and small numbers per stratum, subgroup results were summarised descriptively rather than pooled meta-analytically.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and/or the public were not involved in the design, or conduct, or reporting or dissemination plans of this research.

Results

Literature research and study characteristics

A total of 14 438 studies were identified through the search. After screening, 26 studies were finally included, of which 16 (61.54%) were cross-sectional studies, 9 (34.62%) were cohort studies and 1 (3.84%) was a case–control study. The literature screening process and results are shown in online supplemental appendix 2. The basic characteristics of the included studies are shown in table 1. The two reviewers demonstrated substantial agreement in quality assessments (κ=0.623).12 Among the 26 included studies, 23 were high-quality (88.46%), 3 were moderate-quality (11.54%) and no studies were deemed low quality (online supplemental appendix 3).

The epidemiology of PTE in children

A pooled result of meta-analysis showed that the incidence rate of PTE in children was 0.016% (95% CI 0.0011% to 0.0298%, I2=92.90%) across 11 studies (total denominator n=3 531 047)61322 (online supplemental figure 1). Incidence estimates were primarily derived from hospital-based or discharge-database cohorts and should therefore be interpreted as incidence among children with healthcare contact rather than population-wide community incidence. The incidence of PTE in hospitalised children in high-income countries such as Canada, the USA, Finland and South Korea was 0.00024% (numerators and denominators were not reported),23 0.003% (numerators and denominators were not reported),24 0.0063% (202/3 225 439)6 and 0.0085% (6/70 462).19 In contrast, studies from tertiary medical centres in low- and middle-income countries such as Pakistan and Brazil have shown even higher incidence rates, with rates of 0.013% (3/22 320),15 0.015% (20/134 617)16 and 0.033% (23/70 305),25 respectively.

Several factors can increase the incidence rate of PTE in children. For instance, the incidence rate is 0.0078% (12/153 220) in children after surgery,26 0.012% (7/58 716) in children acutely hospitalised due to trauma,20 0.034% (4/11 775) in adolescents with idiopathic scoliosis undergoing spinal fusion surgery27 and 1.63% (8/492) in children with sickle cell disease.13 Additionally, some studies have shown a bimodal distribution of age at onset of paediatric PTE, with peaks in children under 1 year and during adolescence,28 29 while another study reported that the incidence of paediatric PTE increases with age, with adolescents (aged 13–18 years) being the most likely group to experience PTE, accounting for 55% of cases (p<0.001).4

Many studies showed an annual increase in the incidence of PTE in children. A study including 48 tertiary children’s hospitals in the USA revealed that the PTE incidence rose from 0.02% to 0.06% between 2001 and 2014, representing a 200% increase in incidence (p<0.001).4 Similarly, data from the Korean Health Insurance Review and Assessment Service databases between 2009 and 2013 also demonstrated a steady annual increase in PTE incidence across all age groups.30 Hospital episode statistics from the UK showed a significant relative increase of 331.3% in PTE incidence among children and adolescents, although the absolute numbers remained low, rising from 0.2/100 000 in 1998 to 0.8/100 000 in 2014.5 However, a study in Finland found that from 2011 to 2017, the incidence of PTE remained stable across all age groups, while the PTE incidence in the young (0–24 years) group decreased from 0.007% to 0.0041%.6

A pooled meta-analysis showed that the autopsy-confirmed prevalence of PTE in children was 5.29% (95% CI 1.28% to 9.30%, I²=96.75%) for four studies (n=18 943)14 17 21 22 (online supplemental figure 2). Specifically, a consecutive autopsy study of fresh stillbirths and neonatal deaths found a PTE prevalence of 13.94% (46/330),14 while a retrospective analysis of autopsy found PTE prevalences of 0.046% (8/17 500)17 and 3.46% (38/1098),21 respectively.

A pooled meta-analysis showed that the case-fatality rate of PTE in children was 4.04% (95% CI 0.49% to 7.58%, I2=66.52%) for four studies (n=3537)16 19 24 31 (online supplemental figure 3). And pooled meta-analysis also showed that the recurrence rate of PTE in children was 26.31% (95% CI 19.62% to 33.01%, I2=0.00%) for three studies (n=161)18 26 32 (online supplemental figure 4).

Only one small sample study reported the incidence of CTEPH, with the incidence of 4.00% (1/25).33

A cohort study of 3440 hospitalised children with PTE identified several medical complications associated with in-hospital mortality. These included coagulation disorders (OR=6.54, 95% CI 4.28 to 9.99), congestive heart failure (OR=6.08, 95% CI 3.74 to 9.90), limb fractures (OR=6.08, 95% CI 2.90 to 12.79), neurocranial surgery (OR=5.48, 95% CI 1.94 to 15.50), cardiothoracic surgery (OR=4.70, 95% CI 2.64 to 8.33), neurological disorders (OR=4.43, 95% CI 2.57 to 7.63) and central venous catheter (CVC) (OR=3.16, 95% CI 2.04 to 4.91).24

The risk factors of PTE in children

The risk factors for children with PTE encompass both inherited and acquired risks, which are closely associated with the patient’s genetics, disease and treatment modalities. Several epidemiological studies have indicated a correlation between ethnicity and PTE occurrence. The diagnosis rate of PTE among black children is 1.6 per 100 000 per year, 2.38 times (95% CI 2.29 to 2.47)29 higher than that of white children, which is 0.7 per 100 000 per year. Additionally, African American and Hispanic paediatric patients are more likely to experience recurrent PTE compared with white patients (12%, 10.7%, 8%; p<0.001).4 Gender also plays a role in PTE occurrence. A study in the USA revealed that the overall prevalence of PTE among hospitalised children is higher in males than in females (54% vs 46%, p=0.0016).4 Similarly, a German study showed a sharp increase in female admissions due to PE starting at ages 12–13, with the gender disparity ratio (female patients with PTE/male patients with PTE) peaking at ages 16–17.34

A small-sample study in Turkey identified common inherited risk factors for PTE, including the factor V Leiden mutation (16.6%), anticardiolipin antibody (11%), hyperhomocysteinaemia (11%). Common acquired risk factors included previous deep vein thrombosis (DVT) (37.4%), central venous line (25%), immobility (25%), major surgery (20.8%), trauma (20.8%) and so on.35

A nationwide cohort study in the USA indicated that risk factors for PTE in children include age >12 years, history of DVT, CVC, a hospitalisation history within 1 year, surgery and so on.24 Additionally, a national surveillance study in Canada found that exogenous hormone therapy or pregnancy (35.5%), obesity (29%), congenital thrombophilia and active or recent COVID-19 infection are also closely associated with the occurrence of PTE.23 A study on thrombotic disorders in children, including PTE in children, identified the following risk factors for thrombosis: CVC placement (26%), malignancy (14%), infection (14%), antiphospholipid antibody syndrome (9%), inherited thrombophilia (9%), congenital heart disease (6%).15 Risk factors may vary among children with different comorbidities. For instance, a cohort study showed that risk factors for PTE in children with sickle cell disease include older age, concurrent central nervous system vasculopathy, treatment with erythrocyte apheresis and a previous diagnosis of DVT, etc.13

A cohort study of stillbirths and neonatal deaths found that fetal maternal erythroblastosis and intravenous therapy may be associated with the occurrence of PTE.14 Another study reviewing autopsy reports of children with PTE also identified shunted hydrocephalus (15.2%), accidental trauma (8.4%), heart disease (4.5%), infection (4.4%), neoplasia (4.0%) and general medical illness (1.8%) as important risk factors for PTE.21

A small sample size study in the USA found differences in risk factors between children with massive pulmonary embolism (MPE) and submassive pulmonary embolism (SMPE). Compared with children with SMPE, those with MPE were more likely to have been hospitalised before PTE (89% vs 13%, p<0.001), have major comorbidities (89% vs 25%, p=0.002), have CVCs (67% vs 17%, p=0.001), be critically ill (56% vs 8%, p=0.009), be immobilised (67% vs 13%, p=0.005) and have undergone surgery (44% vs 4%, p=0.01).33 A study including children returning to the emergency department due to chest pain or respiratory symptoms found that risk factors for recurrent PTE included CVC, May-Thurner syndrome, critical illness, obesity, systemic lupus erythematosus, antiphospholipid syndrome (APS), inherited thrombophilia and immobilisation due to surgery.32

Discussion

Incidence and temporal trends

In this study, we found that the pooled incidence rate of PTE in children is 0.016%. In reviews including all age groups, the incidence of PTE is 39~115/100 000,36 which is higher than that in children. Several factors may account for the lower incidence of paediatric PTE compared with adults. First, compared with adults, the incidence of venous thromboembolism in children is significantly lower, strongly suggesting some effective protective mechanisms in children.37 As children age, their coagulation system gradually matures, with a significant correlation between the concentration of most coagulation factors and age, and this physiological phenomenon is known as ‘developmental hemostasis’.37 Second, compared with adult patients with PTE, paediatric patients with PTE often exhibit more concealed symptoms, which increases the risk of misdiagnosis.38 Consistent with this, our results indicate that autopsy series report a higher prevalence of paediatric PTE than clinically detected cohorts, suggesting under-recognition in routine practice. After the onset of symptoms, paediatric patients with PTE typically wait for an average of 7 more days before being diagnosed.39 Finally, it is noteworthy that widely used adult PTE screening tools, such as the Wells criteria,40 Geneva score,41 Pulmonary Embolism Rule-Out Criteria42 and D-dimer level testing, have not been sufficiently validated for use in paediatric patients.43 This poses significant challenges for clinicians in the screening and diagnosis of paediatric PTE.

Most of the included studies indicate that the incidence of PTE in children is showing an upward trend, which also exists in adult patients.5 44 The rise may be attributed to various factors, including the increase in obesity rates, the growing use of oral contraceptives among adolescents and the application of advanced diagnostic technologies, among others.4

At the same time, advances in medical care have improved survival rates for children with debilitating conditions or life-threatening illnesses. However, these intensive treatments can increase the risk of thrombotic events. For example, CVCs are widely used in the management of critically ill children but significantly increase the incidence of thrombotic events.15 32 Furthermore, studies have shown that the upward trend in PTE incidence is directly proportional to the increased awareness and active intervention of paediatricians toward PTE.16 However, recent research indicates a potential overdiagnosis of PTE,45 with no significant association observed between the incidence of PTE and the use of pulmonary angiography,6 resulting in unnecessary radiation exposure for paediatric patients and increased healthcare costs.45

Clinical characteristics and risk factors

Due to the non-specificity of clinical symptoms of PTE in children, identifying PTE can be challenging. In this study, we conducted a meta-analysis of the clinical characteristics of children with PTE based on published studies, aiming to provide a reference for early clinical identification of PTE in children. There are significant differences in the risk factors of PTE between children and adults. For instance, in older patient populations, risk factors include chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, malignancy, DVT and stroke.46 In contrast to adults, the comorbidity context of PTE in children is often dominated by paediatric-specific conditions and care processes. Catheter-associated thrombosis, congenital heart disease, severe infection/inflammation and chronic paediatric disorders (eg, nephrotic syndrome, sickle cell disease, systemic lupus erythematosus/APS) recur across paediatric cohorts and may contribute a larger share of events than in adult populations, where age-related cardiopulmonary disease and metabolic comorbidity are more prominent drivers.32 This difference suggests that paediatric PE/PTE prevention and surveillance should remain anchored to these child-specific comorbidity patterns rather than directly extrapolating adult risk frameworks. This study summarised the current research findings on risk factors for PTE in children, with notable discrepancies among the results. Currently, clinical prediction models used to identify children at risk of PE have limitations such as small sample sizes and inadequate consideration of various factors.47 Future research should incorporate patient, process and system-level factors into the PTE risk prediction model.

Implications for clinical practice and public health

Given the rarity of paediatric PTE, broad population screening is unlikely to be cost-effective; however, contemporary paediatric venous thromboembolism/PE guidelines recommend targeted vigilance and prophylaxis in clearly defined high-risk contexts (eg, perioperative care, prolonged immobility, critical illness, malignancy or long-term central venous access), where preventable risk factors often cluster.48 49 In adolescents, especially girls, oestrogen-containing oral contraceptives are a well-established risk factor for venous thromboembolism, and thrombotic risk assessment should therefore be integrated into routine contraceptive prescribing, with consideration of progestin-only or non-hormonal options in those with additional thrombotic risks.50 51 At a public-health level, concise educational initiatives for paediatricians, adolescent-medicine providers, patients and families may improve awareness of modifiable risk factors and support earlier medical attention when relevant symptoms occur.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, paediatric PTE is a rare condition; consequently, the number of eligible epidemiological studies—particularly those reporting mortality, recurrence and long-term sequelae—was limited, and many estimates were derived from specific clinical settings (eg, hospitalised or autopsy cohorts), which may reduce precision and generalisability. Second, although this review focused on PTE, primary paediatric studies often used PE and PTE terminology interchangeably and did not consistently specify embolic versus in situ aetiology; while we excluded studies clearly describing non-thrombotic PE and identifiable isolated in situ thrombosis, a small degree of diagnostic overlap cannot be fully excluded and may have contributed to heterogeneity in pooled risk profiles. Third, our study identified high heterogeneity (I2) across most outcomes, primarily related to clinical and methodological variability, which includes differences in diagnostic ascertainment (imaging practice, screening intensity, autopsy vs clinical cohorts), study setting, populations (population-based vs hospital-based series) and age or comorbidity profiles. To account for this, we conducted a meta-analysis by random-effects models and supplemented it with a narrative synthesis. The results of meta-regression showed that diseases and year of publication were the major sources of heterogeneity in incidence estimates (R2=99.04%, I2 from 99.35% to 65.80%). Subgroup analysis based on patient disease type revealed that the incidence was indeed higher among children examined by autopsy, and the heterogeneity associated with publication year was consistent with the increasing trend in the incidence of PTE in our narrative synthesis. Despite the use of random-effects models, residual heterogeneity reduces the precision of pooled estimates, which should therefore be interpreted with caution. Finally, we restricted inclusion to English and Chinese publications, which may have introduced language bias. However, publication bias could not be assessed, as most outcomes were based on fewer than 10 studies and involved very few events, which limits the interpretability of publication bias tests such as funnel plots and Egger’s test. Thus, we conducted a comprehensive multi-database search and tried to find more grey studies to minimise the bias. And the restriction of language may lead to omission of relevant data from other regions, as a result, pooled estimates may not fully generalise to settings with different healthcare systems, diagnostic intensity or population risk profiles.

Conclusion

According to existing epidemiological studies, the incidence of PTE in children is relatively low but shows an increasing trend, indicating that PTE in children is becoming an increasingly significant public health issue. Future research should focus more on identifying the epidemiological characteristics of PTE in children, including disease burden, risk factors and prognosis. This would provide a scientific foundation for clinicians to develop preventive strategies, optimise treatment plans and design long-term management measures.
