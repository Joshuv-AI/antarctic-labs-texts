Mechanobiology of intervertebral disc degeneration: From pathological mechanisms to therapeutic approaches☆
Authors: Han Qiao, Kai Zhang, Jie Zhao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntervertebral disc degeneration (IVDD) is a complex mechanobiological process driven by abnormal mechanical loading, resulting in structural failure, inflammatory activation, and cellular dysfunction. Understanding these mechanobiological interactions offers novel therapeutic targets to interrupt the degenerative cascade and improve clinical outcomes in IVDD treatment. This review summarizes the fundamental characteristics of the mechanobiological microenvironment in IVDD, detailing mechanosensitive spinal components and providing evidence for mechanobiology-based therapies. By enhancing insight into disc degeneration from a mechanobiological perspective, researchers can develop innovative therapeutic strategies to restore a healthy disc microenvironment and spinal stability clinically, offering a promising outlook for patients with IVDD. However, current studies primarily focus on in vitro models and in vivo animal studies, limiting clinical translation. Therefore, effectively translating these research findings into practical clinical treatments remains a notable future challenge.Keywords: Intervertebral disc degeneration disease, Mechanobiology, Biochemical mechanics, Disc microenvironment, Intervertebral disc space height

Introduction

The intervertebral disc (IVD) is the fibrocartilaginous tissue located between two bony vertebrae that provides a cushioning effect for spinal load,1,2 which support the cornerstone of spinal physiology.3 It is comprised of three distinct components: the interior nucleus pulposus (NP), the exterior annulus fibrosus (AF), and the superior/inferior cartilaginous endplates (CEPs) (Fig. 1). Essentially, the collagen type II and proteoglycans in the NP provide the IVD with water absorptivity and load dispersibility for the spinal skeleton.4 The NP is tightly surrounded by the exterior AF, which is a multilayer concentric structure of collagen fibers that restrains possible herniation of the NP.5 Conversely, the CEPs are positioned both above and beneath the NP/AF within the intervertebral space (IVS) and consist of 600 ​μm-thick hyaline cartilage structures.6 However, these structures change with load bearing and aging.7 When the NP breaches the CEP longitudinally into the vertebral body, it typically signifies the formation of Schmorl's nodes on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), indicating increased inflammation, vascularization, and neurogenesis through the CEP. This process is often associated with chronic low back pain (LBP) in patients with lumbar IVD degeneration (IVDD).8

An important issue to identify is the mechanobiological and biomechanical phenomena occurring during IVDD.9,10 As the aged IVD progresses, daily activities, such as standing, sitting, and lifting apply repetitive forces to the spine, which can induce AF tears or NP disruption.11 Numerous cellular events—including cell apoptosis, senescence, and the transition of notochordal NP cells into chondrocytic cells—are subsequently initiated. These changes lead to injured discs with a cascade of effects involving imbalanced homeostasis and biomechanical degeneration.12,13 The relationship between disc mechanical microenvironment and cellular pathophysiological changes—referred to as mechanobiology—is well recognized as a critical driver promoting IVDD.14,15 As a result, a pro-inflammatory IVD microenvironment is significantly induced after mechanical stimuli, characterized by elevated expression of IL-1β, TNF-α, IL-6, and metalloproteinases (MMPs).16,17 On one hand, this inflammatory microenvironment is closely linked to the generation of discogenic back pain18 in patients with LBP; on the other hand, it contributes to accelerated degradation of the extracellular matrix (ECM), proteoglycans, and collagen. This degradation impairs the load-sharing ability of disc, accelerating degenerative progression and creating a vicious cycle of IVDD.3

Therefore, the mechanobiological features occurring during IVDD must be distinguished. By illustrating the mechanobiological environment in this review, we aim to summarize targeted mechanobiological therapies, thus providing a solid foundation for future translation into clinical practice based on current mechanobiological findings.

Mechanobiology: integrating mechanics into biology

Understanding mechanobiology in the IVD is key to comprehend the transition from IVD homeostasis to degeneration. Previous studies have investigated significant structural failures during IVDD, demonstrating that AF disruption, disc herniation, and CEP destruction are the primary hallmarks of biomechanical disequilibrium following various loading patterns.11 The remodeling processes of IVD cells are regulated by mechanical loads such as compression and tension.19 Unlike dynamic stimuli, static force was found to impede the transport of nutrients and oxygen into IVD cells, which varies depending on CEP permeability.20 Conversely, dynamic compression facilitates mechanical, chemical, and electrical communications within IVD cells.21 Mechanical stimuli alter gene and protein expression profiles, including fluctuating expression of glycosaminoglycan (GAG), MMP-1, and aggrecanase, which induce NP degradation.22 Continuous compressive load has been shown to increase ADAMTS-4 expression while decreasing TIMP-3 in IVD cells, signaling the onset of disc degeneration after extended dynamic loading compared to limited stimulation.23

Hence, different types of compressive forces lead to specific expression patterns of key IVD-related genes. Dynamic stress is more associated with anabolic processes, whereas static force tends to trigger a catabolic cascade.19 This distinction provides a theoretical basis for developing prevention and treatment strategies for LBP caused by consistent postures in clinical settings. In conclusion, the term “mechanobiology” can be understood as the integration of mechanics and biology, indicating that mechanical forces are important regulators of IVD biological events, which reciprocally shape the microenvironment in IVDD.

Mechanobiological microenvironment in IVDD

Mechanotransductive events

Hypoxia-related events

The conversion of mechanical signals into biological cascades is referred to as mechanotransduction pathways.24 A series of cellular activities are considered consequences of mechanotransduction within IVD. The avascular nature of the IVD contributes to unique hypoxia-related cellular activities within IVD cells. It has been shown that stress-mediated apoptosis can be inhibited by the hypoxic state in the IVD microenvironment via hypoxia-inducible factor (HIF)-1α/YAP signaling.25 The notable roles of the Piezo family in mechanotransduction pathways are key to understanding the conversion from mechanical stress to biological events, especially in the musculoskeletal system.26,27 Aberrant compressive loading— for example, cyclic compression at 1.0 ​Hz with 15 ​% compression intensity — has been shown to upregulate Piezo1, which contributes to apoptosis and senescence of NP cells.28 Studies on an unstable spinal skeleton in rats showed that cyclic mechanical stretch at 20 ​% strain, 0.5 ​Hz for 36 ​h significantly promotes AF cell apoptosis, accompanied by increased Piezo1 expression and inhibition of Calpain2/BAX/Caspase3 signaling.29 More importantly, knockdown of Piezo1 by shRNA demonstrated therapeutic effects for NP cells through downregulation of intracellular calcium levels after abnormal mechanical stretch stress,30 indicating a potential treatment target for future biomechanical therapy of IVDD in clinical settings.

Furthermore, following degeneration, the shift from hypoxia to excessive oxidative stress deeply influences the biological events in IVD cells. It has been shown that the pathological redox state of degenerative IVD leads to upregulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) while failing to eradicate ROS overload. This contributes to further degradation of NP matrix and cell death.31 Mechanical force-induced oxidative stimuli provoke an overreaction of endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress, impairing normal mitochondrial function and promoting cellular apoptosis in IVD cells32 (Fig. 2). Conversely, downregulation of oxidative overload in IVD plays a major role in attenuating disc damage via macrophage polarization-mediated protection.33

One potential contributor to these changes is redox imbalance. Various environmental risk factors disrupt redox homeostasis in IVD cells, leading to excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, which induces endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress and mitochondrial dysfunction, ultimately altering cellular activity. Additionally, autophagy responds to hypoxia in NP-derived stem cells under mechanical stress via HIF-associated apoptotic resistance.34 Instead of degenerative exosomes, CEP cells secrete normal exosomes that exert protective effects against IVDD through the PI3K/AKT/autophagy.35 Oxidative responses are negatively modulated by TP53-induced glycolysis and apoptosis regulator, which impedes load-related apoptosis and autophagy in IVD cells.36 Altogether, these findings provide evidence that constant mechanical stress promotes IVDD via programmed cell death.37 Therefore, regulating reactive oxygen levels, including modulating the hypoxic microenvironment, could significantly impact pathological IVDD and offer effective treatment strategies for spinal surgeons in clinical practice.

Neuroinflammation

The distinct neuroinflammatory microenvironment in degenerative IVD is an emerging area that explores the mechanobiological crosstalk between mechanical stress and cellular behavior. Despite ongoing debate about the role of immune cells in the degenerative atmosphere of IVDD, the presence of macrophages is consistently demonstrated to be important.38 This role is supported by evidence that enhanced interactions between IVD cells and macrophages reduce the paw withdrawal threshold in IVDD models, suggesting a potential therapeutic target for disrupting the immunoinflammatory cascade in IVDD.39 Substrate stiffness modulates biochemical events in immune-related macrophage activation, implicating ECM rigidity as a regulator of the immune system.40

Similarly, variations in loading frequency (0.32–0.5 ​MPa at 10 ​Hz over 3 days) and nutrient diffusion rates produce increases in interleukins (ILs), nerve growth factor (NGF), and interferon γ in degenerative IVD cells, which stimulate survival and function of microglia in the spinal cord.41 Innate immune responses are activated after hyperphysiologic strains, exemplified by upregulation of Toll-like receptors, NGF, and tumor necrosis factor α (TNF-α), highlighting possible therapeutic targets linking mechanical strain, inflammatory factors, and neurogenesis in vivo.42 It is believed that varying proportions of NP subpopulations contribute to significant neurite outgrowth from dorsal root ganglia,43 potentially explaining severe discogenic pain in certain patients with IVDD.

Ferroptosis

Ferroptosis signaling has emerged as a novel milestone in unraveling the extent of mechanotransduction during IVDD.44 Dynamic free iron release into the cytoplasm is positively regulated by the interaction between the C-terminus of nuclear receptor coactivator 4 (NCOA4) and ferritin heavy chain 1 within phagolysosomes, which subsequently activates autophagosomes and autolysosomes.45,46 Piezo1 has been reported to regulate stress-induced iron influx in NP cells, identifying a connection between mechanical stimuli and ferroptosis.47 Studies showed that inactivation of Piezo1 mitigates mitochondrial oxidative stress and intracellular iron concentration, attenuating the progression of IVDD in vivo.47 Current research also demonstrated the crucial role of selenium in NP cells, which can retard ferroptosis advancement and reduce ER stress through the Piezo1 channel in mechanically loaded IVDD.48 Additionally, several ferroptosis-related genes in NP tissues were identified through bioinformatic analyses.49 Results showed upregulation of NCOA4 and PCBP1 and the downregulation of GPX4 under IL-1β stimulation, correlating further with the expression of T cells, natural killer cells, and macrophages.

ECM synthesis and degradation

Homeostasis between ECM synthesis and degradation is a vital marker of the IVD microenvironment.50 Biochemical analyses revealed that TGF-β-induced activation of Smad3, RhoA/ROCK1, and MAPK signaling pathways contributes to the formation of sulfated GAG (sGAG) in IVD tissue.51 Furthermore, TGF-β improves the balance of CCN2/3 in the disc, a process further enhanced by hypoxia to maintain normal IVD synthetic function.52 TGF-β and IL-1Ra have also been shown to regulate levels of collagen I/II, aggrecan, and fibronectin, likely stimulated by M1/M2 macrophage polarization.53,54 ECM remodeling is also dysregulated by various miRNA, as exemplified by the significant alteration of miR-93 in NP cells with the elevated MMP-3 to target collagen and aggrecan.55

During degenerative phases, IVD cells release proinflammatory factors, such as TNF-α, IL-1α/β, IL-6, and IL-17, leading to increased expression of catabolic molecules, including matrix MMPs, which exacerbate ECM degradation.56 Additionally, downregulation of ZIP4 attenuates ECM degradation, modulated by the HDAC/NFκB/Sirtuin/FoxO signaling axis.57 Oxidative load levels have also been linked to activation of FOXO and MMPs.58 Other studies demonstrated that, in addition to the relationship between high glucose and ROS, oxidative stimuli appear to impair ECM functionality via the MAPK pathway.59 These findings provide mechanistic insights that offer significant opportunities for developing translational therapies targeting ECM degradation in IVDD.

Senescence

Aging of the spinal IVD is driven by the progression of cellular senescence within spinal tissues. Accumulation of DNA damage in NP cells is a major biomarker of cellular senescence. DNA damage is partly attributed to activation of the p53/p21/Rb axis in NP cells subjected to cyclic tensile strain at 20 ​% elongation, 1 ​Hz for 2 day, regardless of ROS presence.60 Following oxidative stimulation, cellular senescence is largely irreversible, with p53 activation initiating G1/G2 cell cycle arrest, impairing cell proliferation and enhancing senescence in the disc.61

Similar to humans, IVD cells inevitably lose their ability to activate and mature in response to developmental signals during senescence.62 Previous histological studies identified cellular senescence following various compressive stresses on IVD cells.63 This was further clarified by findings that mechanical pressure induces activation of the Piezo/NFκB/periostin pathway, promoting NP cell senescence, accompanied by increased expression of classic senescence markers, such as p16INK4a and p53.64 Notably, the CEP undergoes a similar process, indicated by elevated proinflammatory cytokines (e.g., TNF-α) that increase its mechanical sensitivity to stress.65 This leads to microfractures in CEP structures, accelerating uneven load distribution deterioration during IVDD.

Notably, all the mechanobiological events are not independent. For example, Piezo expression responds to changes in substrate stiffness, which further enhances cellular apoptosis and senescence under oxidative stress.66 The ECM actively participates both as a cause and consequence in these processes, as evidenced by changes in the tensile properties of the IVD in vivo (Fig. 3).

Mechanosensitive spinal components

NP

The mechanosensitive capability of the IVD depends on its anatomical structure. Among its components, the NP is most important, as it largely determines whether a herniated disc triggers radiating lower limb symptoms.67 With aging progression, the chondrocyte-like NP cells become loose and less elastic, impairing their ability to retain water, which leads to notable degenerative collapse of the IVS.68 The transition from notochordal to chondrocytic NP cells during maturation is critical, marking the acquisition by NP cells of a spherical, small, and dense phenotype.69,70 This feature enables NP cells to better withstand spinal load while secreting various ECM-related molecules.71 For example, different matrix stiffness levels regulate the expression of Kidins220/AMPK signaling pathways that target glycolysis in degenerative NP cells.72 Mechanical overloading causes autophagy dysfunction through altered levels of CHMP4B and TFEB in NP cells.73 Additionally, the proportional relationship between collagen and proteoglycan shifts, while aggrecan interacts more actively with hyaluronan in NP,67,74 collectively enabling NP cells to adapt to inevitable senescence and degeneration.

Importantly, as previously mentioned, NP cells reside centrally within the IVD, surrounded peripherally by the AF. This environment is characterized by hypoxia and nutrient scarcity, conditions critical for NP cell survival and proliferation.75 Proteoglycan production in NP is modulated by oxygen levels; low oxygen promotes increased aggrecan and hyaluronic acid production in IVD tissue, indicating optimal conditions for maintaining NP mechanical integrity.76 Studies revealed that HIF-promoted NP stem cells exhibit increased resistance to apoptosis under mechanical overload.34 The absence of HIF in animal models compromises the ability of IVD to resist spinal compression, supported by the aggravation of fibrocartilage development and disruption of mechanical homeostasis.77 Accordingly, aggrecan modulus and hydraulic permeability levels are regulated by hypoxic conditions, exemplified by increased production of substrate-linked proteins, such as cytokeratin, collagen I, and collagen II, which regulate disc degeneration.78

AF

Serving as the outer protective layer surrounding the NP, AF cells are crucial targets in mitigating IVD herniation, particularly in minimally invasive surgeries, such as percutaneous endoscopic lumbar discectomy in clinical practice. The bioactivity of the AF is largely determined by its biostructure.79 Biomechanically, the AF structure is distinct, as demonstrated by the detailed distribution of procollagen type IIA isoform in the superficial layers of the inner AF.80 This triple helix structure of procollagen provides the AF with architectural integrity within the collagen transition region of the IVD. Scanning electron microscopy of rat AF revealed distinctive collagen fibers approximately 1 ​μm in diameter forming microtubules parallel to the collagenous fibers, which confer efficient anti-elastic modulus and water absorbance.81 An integrated finite element model validated a 40 ​% decline in shear resistance caused by load pressure, suggesting that structural deterioration of interlamellar interactions likely initiates IVDD clinically.82

Furthermore, the elastic failure rate of tensile fibers within the interlamellar matrix of the AF has been investigated, showing these fibers possess strong anti-strain capabilities against slow-motion stimuli. Therefore, elastic failure in the disc often indicates tensile effects rather than shear effects.83 Ultrastructural examination of the AF network further revealed unique matrix fiber characteristics, including diverse profiles, magnitudes, and angles, transitioning from tangent-wise to perimeter-wise orientation.84 Beyond micromorphological studies, quantitative analyses have also indicated specific mechanical patterns associated with fiber distribution in the AF, which significantly influence disc degenerative behaviors.85

CEP

Within IVD tissue, the CEP has been identified as the most vulnerable structure, susceptible to accumulated microfractures under continuous mechanical spinal compression.86 Avascularity is a significant characteristic of IVD tissue.87 Therefore, the transport of oxygen and nutrients—essential for NP and AF cell survival—across the CEP barrier is believed to determine the degenerative rate of the IVD.88 Studies examining porcine CEP tissues showed that various compressive loads had negligible effects on nutrient transport through the CEP, whereas significant changes were observed in both the NP and AF.89 With increased nutrient transport through the CEP, the NP within the IVD tends to migrate upward or downward with aging, potentially breaching the CEP and invading vertebrae, indicating the presence of Schmorl nodes in patients with IVDD.90 This process is critical because microfractures in the CEP typically lead to decreased intradiscal pressure, transferring spinal load onto the peripheral AF tissue and thereby increasing the risk of disc herniation.91 Thus, the integrity of the CEP biostructure is essential for maintaining normal IVD configuration and function. Supporting this, ossification of the CEP has been shown to impede nutrient transfer through its porous channels, thereby facilitating IVDD.92

Collectively, the IVD microenvironment exemplifies biological mechano-adaptation to spinal stress, where NP, AF, and CEP function as structurally distinct yet functionally integrated components of a mechanotransduction system. This vicious cycle, initiated by abnormal spinal load accumulation with aging, leads to CEP alteration, AF injury, and NP herniation, shaping IVD cellular fate and homeostasis and accelerating degenerative forces that propel IVDD progression. These insights suggest potential therapeutic targets for clinical treatment based on mechanobiological evidence from both in vitro and in vivo studies.

Mechanobiological therapies for IVDD

Biomaterial approaches

It is widely accepted that a profound understanding of mechanobiology in IVDD should ultimately translate into the development of clinical therapeutic strategies. Currently, biomaterial and tissue engineering approaches that mimic the native mechanobiological environment of the IVD offer a promising toolkit for IVDD therapy. Various bioengineering materials have been used to protect spinal stability by promoting osteogenesis, including DNA plasmids, siRNA, miRNA, and exosomes.93,94 Organs-on-chip (Ooc) model use microfluidic approach to reestablish IVD mechanobiologic atmosphere for novel drug development.95 Previous studies demonstrated that constructing a spatially mimetic hydrogel scaffold with quercetin enabled elimination of senescent NP cells under pathological stimulation.96 The mechanical properties of the scaffold were further validated in rat models for resistance to abnormal shear forces. Additionally, a nanoCRISPR-modified scaffold was designed to reconstruct normal AF architecture, providing a biocompatible bone microenvironment that facilitates AF cell activation, migration, and ECM remodeling.97 Another study used mesenchymal stem cell (MSC) membrane-coated keratin nanoparticles to target NP cells, attenuating cellular senescence and promoting ECM remodeling.98 Given that hypoxia is important for MSC differentiation into NP phenotype, hypoxic scaffolds constructed in vitro showed increased expression of IVD substrate-related proteins, implying the potential advantages of biomaterial approaches for IVD regeneration.99 Hydrogel scaffolds transplantable into discs have been solidified to inhibit disc extrusion.100 Huang et al. reported revascularization of IVD allografts in goats after surgery.101 Although this differs from normal IVD, it provides useful insights for managing nutrient-deficient disc conditions.

Beyond tissue engineering, reconstructing the mechanobiologic properties of the IVD ideally involves restoring a native-like healthy disc to treat degeneration. Previously, artificial spheres were used to restore pre-collapsed IVS and dissipate spinal stress.102 However, this sometimes caused significant subsidence of interbody cages into spinal CEP during long-term follow-up.103 Consequently, refining material design to simulate normal NP function remains a primary goal for spinal scientists. The clinical application of advanced devices, such as Nubac, Newcleus, and PDN prostheses has shown promising outcomes.104, 105, 106, 107 Total disc replacement (TDR) is another viable alternative for spinal disc restoration. The conventional ProDisc-L employs specialized polyethylene and alloy materials to provide segmental stabilization with preserved mobility.108 The novel ProDisc-II has also demonstrated improved long-term postoperative clinical satisfaction.109,110

Surgical approaches

The deepening understanding of mechanobiology in IVDD has informed the development of surgical strategies. Aligned with advances in spinal mechanobiology, these insights have enhanced the efficacy of spinal surgery and guided the future evolution of surgical practices. Repair of the AF is crucial for spinal decompression without vertebral implants. As previously mentioned, the normal biofunction of the IVD is closely linked to the integrity of the CEPs.111 CEP structure plays a notable role in resisting spinal mechanical loads, while irregular CEP morphology strongly correlates with more severe disc degeneration, particularly in the lower lumbar segments.111,112 Other studies have shown that vertebroplasty surgery can reduce CEP pressure.113 Therefore, careful consideration of CEP structure is essential to prevent recurrence following minimally invasive endoscopic surgery.

To date, with increasing emphasis on spinal stability, lumbar interbody fusion (LIF) surgery has gained widespread attention for treating IVDD. Unlike TDR, LIF with an interbody cage facilitates bone fusion between operative vertebrae, restabilizing the degenerated spine with pedicle screw support. Both the mechanobiology and biomechanics of the spine must be completely considered when performing LIF. Researchers have previously used autologous cortical bone to achieve interbody fusion.114 However, continuous loading often caused subsidence of the interbody bone, necessitating further stabilization treatments. Consequently, revolutionary cage materials have been developed, including the widely used polyetheretherketone (PEEK) cage.115 PEEK cages provide adequate mechanical support to resist daily spinal wear and erosion and exhibit promising osteogenic properties for interbody fusion. Furthermore, our group synthesized MSC-enhanced interbody cages to achieve higher spinal fusion rates and desirable IVS maintenance.116 Nonetheless, LIF surgery still risks damage to the back muscles and the posterior ligament complex, which can lead to severe complications, such as flat-back syndrome and adjacent segment disease (ASD) (Fig. 4). These complications likely stem from mismatches between bone implants and spinal biomechanics.

Another important factor in IVDD mechanobiology is IVS height (IVSH). IVDD progression causes IVSH collapse, decreasing range of motion (ROM) at degenerated spinal levels in multiple directions while significantly increasing ROM at adjacent levels.117 We previously defined IVSH ≥14 ​mm as high IVSH (hIVSH),118 which occurs in approximately 15.4 ​% of healthy adult volunteers. Thus, full restoration of IVSH contributes to recovery of lumbar lordosis and overall sagittal spinal balance, including in patients with hIVSH.118, 119, 120 Studies have shown that during LIF, the height of the interbody cage should match the natural IVSH to avoid overstretching.121 More importantly, restoring precollapsed IVSH to its original height helps recover posterior tensile band tension, slowing ASD progression.120 Therefore, spinal surgeons are advised to restore collapsed IVSH to original height to improve long-term prognosis.

Bioactives

The roles of bioactive growth factors during IVDD have long been recognized. A number of growth factors (IL-1β, TNF-α) are found to correlate with the mechanical related ECM degradation while activating pain-sensing pathways and cellular senesecence.56 It was showed that dynamic compression of 7 ​% strain with TNF-α stimulation on CEP could induce the catabolic construction of IVD.122 More importantly, the integrin and nonintegrin receptors on cells could respond to varying ECM mechanical properties,123 which could transform extracellular mechanical stimuli into sensible cellular signaling like FAK cascade.124 The substrate mechanical stiffness tends to promote NP cell-cell crosstalk via bioactive ligands on NP cells, showing that the soft ECM (<720Pa) is able to regulate NP cell morphology and cellular behaviors.125 Herein, the integrin ligands interact with both collagen and fibronectin of ECM within AF and NP section, which could exert significant effects on intervertebral mechanics.126 Cyclic stretch induces the escalated expressions of IL-8 and miRR-155-5p cytokines in AF cells, which contribute to the activation of both brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) and mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) signaling to promote IVDD progression.127

Cell therapies

As “mechanobiology” encompasses both mechanics and biology, its ultimate manifestations lie in cellular biological behaviors. Targeting specific cell types or events is therefore expected to yield effective therapeutic outcomes against IVDD.128 Various cell types have shown responsiveness to limited mechanical forces, suggesting feasibility for developing individualized cell therapy strategies.129

Selecting an appropriate cell source is critical for successful cell therapy. Decades ago, chondrocytes were first used in cell-based treatments for IVDD,130 demonstrating regenerative effects by supporting NP cells in producing hyaline-like cartilage. Comparative studies of different chondrocyte types revealed that under 5 ​% oxygen atmosphere, nasal chondrocytes produced higher levels of sGAGs and lower collagen levels without TGF-β stimulation.131 Additionally, varying ratios of NP cells and MSCs showed that MSC administration in vivo significantly upregulated ECM production, highlighting MSCs as promising delivery vehicles for targeted IVDD therapy.132 A phase I clinical trial injecting autologous MSCs combined with hyaluronic acid into degenerated IVDs showed promising results, including reduced LBP and increased water content on MRI.133

Furthermore, fibroblasts, emerging as critical participants in musculoskeletal disease activation, are attracting increasing attention.134 Fibroblast-related cytokines have been shown to modulate during disc degeneration progression.135 In IVDD animal models, osteophyte formation was significantly reduced after allogeneic fibroblast administration, indicating the translational potential of fibroblasts in cell therapy.136 This effect may be due to NP cell fibrosis regulated by TGF-β release in vivo following fibroblast treatment, which preserves disc mechanical properties, such as IVSH and spinal stability.137

Collectively, cell therapies for IVDD are rapidly advancing. However, challenges remain, including ethical considerations, cellular characteristics, transplantation conditions, and long-term safety, necessitating further in-depth translational research.

Conclusions and challenges

Understanding the crucial role of mechanobiology in IVDD pathogenesis is significant for developing potential mechanobiology-based therapeutic approaches against IVDD in clinical practice. Herein, we reviewed that IVD tissue undergoes progressive degradation following abnormal load stress stimulation, which further disrupts ECM homeostasis via upregulation of catabolic enzymes and proinflammatory cytokines. Mechanotransduction pathways—including hypoxia, neuroinflammation, ferroptosis, ECM remodeling, and senescence-related events—were summarized, highlighting key cellular activities during mechanical stress-induced NP apoptosis, AF rupture, and CEP microfractures that exacerbate disc degeneration.

With increasingly deepened insight into degenerative disc mechanobiology, various innovative therapeutic strategies involving biomaterials and surgical approaches have been proposed. These aim to restore the mechanobiological microenvironment of the disc by simulating a vivo-like healthy IVD or by restabilizing spinal kinematics. However, despite significant advancements in mechanobiological understanding of cellular and molecular interactions during IVDD, most mechanistic studies remain focused on in vitro models and animal experiments, limiting translation into clinical practice. Despite the promising preclinical outcomes from various biomaterial-based therapeutic approaches, a critical challenge is the definition of “successful outcome” in early phase clinical studies. It is undoubtedly acknowledged that there is an obvious difference between the success in biological results and the success in clinical results. The former lies in the radiologic or serum results, which do not translate into patient-centered clinical efficacy. Therefore, an integrated evaluation that comprise radiologic, biochemical, mechanobiologic and clinical outcome assessment is of importance in providing solid evidence for IVDD study.

However, it is a well-acknowledged concept for spinal surgeons that the radiologic severity of disc herniation does not always reflect the degree of clinical symptoms. While the radicular leg pain is correlated with the nerve root compression from degenerative disc, the cellular mechanobiologic events occurred in IVD tissue is far more complex and multifactorial. It is showed that the pathologic changes including disc herniation, AF tear, CEP microfracture which derived from mechanobiologic effects are necessary but not sufficient for pain induction clinically. Instead, it could be attributed to a cascade of inflammatory and immune responses,138,139 which is not easily observable in regular clinical radiology. Therefore, for future spinal mechanobiology study, one of the very important topics is the study on the connection between structural degeneration with neurobiology, particularly in radiologic aspect. The novel biomarkers and imaging approaches that can uncover the “mechanobiologic pain” is crucial to bridge the gap between biomechanical structure and patient symptoms. More importantly, successful therapeutic strategies should not only include the restoration of mechanical function of IVD, but the remodeling of the neuro-inflammatory microenvironment in IVD as well. Furtherly, greater research priority should be given to mechanobiological and biomechanical changes during the pre-degenerative stage of IVDD to enable effective implementation of the clinical policy of “early detection, early diagnosis, and early treatment.” As mechanobiological knowledge of IVDD evolves, we anticipate it will bring new breakthroughs for IVDD treatment in the future.
