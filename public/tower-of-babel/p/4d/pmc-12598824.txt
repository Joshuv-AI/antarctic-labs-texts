TFNAs induce autophagy via activation of the nonclassical cGAS–STING signalling pathway to attenuate acute lung injury in mice
Authors: Chenru Wei, Yingying Liu, Bohan Pan, Jin Zhang, Jiqiu Chen, Ping Ling, Yang Xiang, Shihui Zhu, Yu Sun
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAcute lung injury is a common and fatal inflammatory condition in critically ill patients. Tetrahedral framework nucleic acids (TFNAs) have good potential for treating inflammatory diseases. The aim of this study was to use TFNAs in the treatment of acute lung injury (ALI) in mice to investigate the effect and possible mechanism.MethodThe characteristics of the TFNAs, including particle size and cellular uptake, were detected. TFNAs were subsequently used to treat an ALI mouse lung epithelial cells (MLE12) model with or without an autophagy inhibitor. Flow cytometry and Western blotting (WB) were performed to detect apoptosis and autophagy. The oxidative stress level was assessed by measuring the malondialdehyde (MDA) content, superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity and reactive oxygen species (ROS) content. A survival curve of the ALI model mice treated with TFNAs was constructed, and the lung injury score was assessed through pathological staining. The lung wet/dry weight ratio and inflammatory cytokine content in bronchoalveolar lavage fluid were measured and recorded. Transcription sequencing was performed to elucidate the biological processes associated with TFNA treatment. Finally, the regulatory effect of the cGAS–STING signalling pathway on TFNA-induced autophagy was explored.ResultThe synthesized TFNAs are typical nanomaterials. TFNAs significantly reduced the apoptosis rate according to flow cytometry and decreased the BAX/BCL2 ratio in MLE12 cells. Meanwhile, the autophagy level increased, as indicated by the increased expression of the ATG5, ATG7 and LC3II proteins when the cells were incubated with TFNAs. TFNAs could also inhibit the accumulation of ROS, increasing SOD activity and reducing the MDA content. Autophagy inhibitors can significantly inhibit the autophagy and antiapoptotic effects of TFNAs. In the ALI mouse model, TFNAs effectively reduced mortality, BALF inflammatory factor levels, pulmonary oedema, lung injury scores and neutrophil infiltration. The protective effect was significantly reduced with the use of autophagy inhibitors. In addition to autophagy, antigen processing and presentation, antiviral biological processes, and cytoplasmic membrane signal receptor complex functions were significantly upregulated, indicating that TFNAs might activate the cGAS–STING signalling pathway. Inhibition of the cGAS–STING signalling pathway effectively suppressed TFNA-induced autophagy.ConclusionThis study is the first to demonstrate that TFNAs protect MLE-12 cells against LPS-induced oxidative stress injury via autophagy activated by the nonclassical cGAS–STING signalling pathway. Therefore, TFNAs can attenuate ALI and improve patient prognosis in mice. These findings indicate that the cGAS–STING signalling pathway may be a basic mechanism contributing to various therapeutic immunologic effects. This study demonstrated the value of TFNAs in the treatment of ALI, with potential clinical translational value.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12890-025-03970-w.Keywords: TFNAs, Oxidative stress, Apoptosis, Autophagy, CGAS–STING

Introduction

Acute lung injury (ALI) and acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) are common complications in critically ill patients, with aetiologies including aspiration, pneumonia, sepsis, and trauma [1–3]. Despite significant advancements in pharmacological interventions and ventilator management, mortality remains high, with approximately 40% of patients succumbing to these conditions [4, 5]. A key pathophysiological feature of ALI/ARDS is damage to alveolar epithelial cells (AECs). Studies have shown that during the course of ALI, AEC apoptosis and disruption of the alveolar epithelial barrier occur [6, 7], leading to the accumulation of alveolar fluid and proinflammatory cytokines/chemokines, a reduction in surfactant production, and impaired pulmonary gas exchange occur [8, 9]. Therefore, inhibiting inflammatory damage to AECs and maintaining their normal function are crucial for preserving the integrity of the alveolar barrier and significantly improving the prognosis of acute lung injury.

Autophagy is a highly conserved metabolic process in eukaryotic cells that utilizes lysosomes to degrade self-proteins and damaged organelles. In an experimental model of ALI induced by lipopolysaccharide (LPS), increased expression of LC3II was observed in AECs, whereas mice with a knockout of the Atg7 gene exhibited increased mortality after ALI [10]. ALI mice treated with the autophagy inhibitor 3-methyladenine (3-MA) presented significantly elevated serum levels of IL-1β and TNF-α [11]. Appropriately promoting autophagy can alleviate inflammatory damage, promote the survival of AECs, and maintain the function of the alveolar epithelial barrier. Surprisingly, the inhibition of autophagy attenuates oxidative stress injury [12]. The reason for this difference may lie in the different pathological stages and degrees of autophagy.

Tetrahedral framework nucleic acids (TFNAs), as a nanomaterial, consist of four single-stranded DNA chains that complement each other to form a stable three-dimensional pyramid structure. Previous studies have indicated that TFNAs exhibit protective effects against ROS damage in inflammatory and degenerative diseases such as type 2 diabetes, acute kidney injury, and retinal and cerebral ischaemia‒reperfusion injury, among others [13–17]. TFNAs have excellent cell membrane affinity [18, 19]. After their cellular uptake, TFNAs undergo trafficking through the microtubule system and ultimately enter lysosomes, where they intersect with the process of autophagy.

Therefore, we hypothesized and demonstrated through in vitro and in vivo experiments that TFNAs can induce autophagy, alleviate oxidative stress and apoptosis in mouse pulmonary epithelial cells (MLE-12) via the cGAS–STING signalling pathway, and alleviate ALI damage in mice. Owing to their outstanding cellular protective effects against oxidative stress damage, TFNAs hold tremendous clinical potential and value in the treatment of ALI.

Materials and methods

Synthesis and characterization of TFNAs

Four special ssDNAs were designed (SANGON, China) (denoted S1 to S4, Table 1). For the immunofluorescence assay to evaluate cellular uptake and autophagy activation, the S1 chain was modified with Cy3. A mixture containing TM buffer and four ssDNAs at a concentration of 1.0 μM each was preheated to 95 °C for 10 min and then quickly cooled to 4 °C for 20 min, as reported previously [20]. Agarose gel electrophoresis (AGE) was used to determine the molecular weight distribution of the ssDNA or TFNAs to clarify the molecular weight. The particle size and zeta potential were verified via a dynamic light scattering analyser (Malvern Panalytical, UK). The morphology of the TFNA was identified via transmission electron microscopy and scanning electron microscopy. The ssDNA sequences are shown in Table 1.

MLE-12 culture

Dulbecco's modified Eagle’s medium/nutrient mixture F-12 (DMEM/F12; Gibco, USA) was used to culture MLE-12 cells at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 humidified incubator (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The medium was replenished every 2–3 days.

ALI cell model

MLE-12 cells were seeded into 96/6-well plates and incubated under standard conditions for 12 h. After incubation with 2% foetal bovine serum (FBS) DMEM/F12 for 1 h, PBS buffer (control group, NC; LPS group, L), 250 nM TFNAs (LPS + TFNAs group, LT), 250 nM TFNAs with 5 mM 3-MA (LPS + TFNAs + 3-MA group, LT3), 500 nM rapamycin (LPS + rapamycin group, LR), and 5 mM 3-MA (LPS + 3-MA group, L3) were added to different wells and incubated for 6 h. H-151 (1 μM) was added to the LTH group, which was subsequently incubated for 3 h. These groups were subsequently incubated with 2.0 μg/mL lipopolysaccharide (L4391, Sigma, USA, LPS) for 24 h, except for the NC group. 3-MA (HY-19312) and H-151 (HY-112693) were purchased from MCE (MedChemExpress, USA).

Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR)

Total RNA extraction was performed via a Fast-Pure Cell/Tissue Total RNA Isolation Kit (RC112-01, Vazyme, China). For cDNA synthesis, RNA and PrimeScript™ RT Master Mix (Perfect Real Time) (RR036A, Takara, Japan) were used. Quantitative PCR (qPCR) was carried out using cDNA and TB Green® Premix Ex Taq™ II (Tli RNaseH Plus) (RR820A, Takara, Japan), with the primers listed in Table 1. Data quantification was performed via the 2−ΔΔCT method. The forward and reverse primers are detailed in Table 1.

Western blotting

After washing with PBS three times, RIPA buffer (WB3100, New Cell & Molecular Biotech, China) was used to lyse the cells to harvest protein samples. After quantification, SDS‒PAGE was employed to separate the proteins. The proteins were transferred onto PVDF membranes (Millipore, MA, USA). Five percent BSA was applied to block the membranes, which were further treated with the corresponding primary antibodies (anti-GAPDH ab8245, anti-BAX ab32503, anti-Bcl2 ab182858, anti-caspase-3 ab184787, anti-cleaved caspase-3 ab214430, anti-Atg5 ab108327, anti-Atg7 ab133528, anti-beclin-1 ab207012, Abcam, UK; anti-LC3I/II 12741S, CST, US; anti-cGAS 26416–1-AP, anti-STING 19851–1-AP, ProteinTech, China; anti-IRF3 T55779 abmart; anti-p-IRF3 TA2436 abmart). An enhanced chemiluminescence reagent was used to visualize the immunoreactivity after incubation with the appropriate secondary antibodies. The antibodies and reagents were applied according to the manufacturer’s protocols.

Immunofluorescence staining

The ALI cell model was also incubated with 50 μM chloroquine. After washing with PBS, the autophagosomes were labelled with an Autophagy Assay Kit (ab139484; Abcam, UK) to determine the level of autophagy via the fluorescence density. Intracellular ROS were detected with DCFH-DA (D6470, Solarbio, China). Then, the cells were fixed with 4% (w/v) paraformaldehyde. The cells were permeabilized and blocked with Triton X-100 (0.1% v/v) and 1% BSA. After washing with PBS, the cell slides were incubated with primary antibody for 1 h at room temperature (anti-LC3I/II 12741S, CST, US; anti-cGAS 26416–1-AP, anti-STING 19851–1-AP, ProteinTech, China; antibody/1% BSA = 1:200). Anti-LC3I/II was used for colocalization immunofluorescence staining. After being washed with PBS, the slides were incubated with secondary fluorescence antibody for 1 h at room temperature (A-11008, A-11012; Thermo Fisher, US; antibody/1% BSA = 1:200). After washing with PBS, the nuclei were labelled with 4,6-diamino-2-phenyl-indole (DAPI; 4083, CST, US; DAPI/PBS = 1:500). Images were acquired via a fluorescence microscope (Leica, German).

Flow cytometry

For flow cytometry, an Annexin V-APC/PI Apoptosis Kit (AT101, Liankebio, China) and an Autophagy Assay Kit (ab139484, Abcam, UK) were used to test the levels of apoptosis and autophagy in the different groups. The antibodies and reagents were applied according to the manufacturer’s protocols.

In vitro antioxidant capacity assay

The cellular antioxidant capacity was assessed by measuring the malondialdehyde (MDA) content and superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity. The cells were seeded in 6-well plates at the appropriate density and cultured for 24 h to reach a cell density of 70%. The original medium was removed, and the cells were washed with PBS. DMEM/F12 supplemented with 2% FBS was added to the control group, while DMEM/F12 supplemented 2% FBS and different drugs was added to the experimental groups. After 6 h of incubation, hydrogen peroxide was added to each group at a final concentration of 500 μmol/L. After 24 h of incubation, the cell suspensions were collected for subsequent assays. The SOD activity and MDA content were measured via a SOD Activity Assay Kit (BC5165, Solarbio, China) and an MDA Content Assay Kit (BC0025, Solarbio, China), respectively. The methods used were based on those described by Yuan M [21] and Jun L [22].

ALI mouse models and 72-h survival curves

All C57BL/6 mice (male, 6 weeks) were purchased from the Gempharmatech Company. All animal experiments were conducted in strict accordance with the guidelines for experimental animals and were approved by the ethics committee. Three percent isoflurane was used for the induction of anaesthesia in the mice, and after the loss of the righting reflex, 1.5% isoflurane was used for maintenance of anaesthesia. The mice were treated with 50 μL of PBS (control group, sham) or a mixture containing 40 mg/kg LPS and the drug via a nasal drip (LPS group, L; LPS + 250 nM TFNAs group, LT; LPS + 250 nM TFNAs + 15 mg/mL 3-MA group, LT3). All the groups contained 20 mice each, except for the control group, which included 5 mice. The condition of the mice was observed and recorded every 6 h, and a 72-h survival curve was constructed.

Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA)

BALF (bronchoalveolar lavage fluid) was extracted from the lungs of the mice at 72 h. The lungs were lavaged 3 times with 500 μL of PBS via the trachea to obtain 1.5 mL of BALF. Then, the BALF was centrifuged at 3000 × g and 4 °C to isolate the supernatant for ELISA. The levels of IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α were measured via ELISA kits (EK0394, EK0411, EK0527; Boster Bio, US). The procedures were performed in accordance with the previous method [23].

Lung wet/dry weight ratio

The mouse lungs were weighed to determine the wet weight at the time of death. The lungs were subsequently dried in an oven at 65 °C for 72 h until the dry weight was reached. The lung wet/dry weight ratio was calculated to assess lung oedema.

Lung histology

Lung samples were sectioned into 4-mm slices and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h. Following dehydration, the samples were embedded in paraffin. Subsequently, 40-μm sections were obtained via an SM2000R microtome (Leica, Germany). The sections were deparaffinized in xylene, rehydrated through a graded alcohol series, and subjected to H&E staining. Haematoxylin was used to stain the nuclei, resulting in a blue or purple hue, whereas eosin was applied to stain the cytoplasm and other tissue structures, producing a pink colour. Myeloperoxidase (MPO; anti-MPO ab208670, Abcam, USA) staining was performed to identify neutrophils. After being rinsed in water, the sections were dehydrated with alcohol, cleared with xylene, and sealed for microscopic examination. Lung injury was assessed on the basis of H&E images, and neutrophil infiltration was quantified by the ratio of MPO-positive nuclei.

Transcriptome sequencing analyses

Transcriptome sequencing was conducted on MLE-12 cells from the L, LT, LT3, and LR groups. RNA extraction, purification, reverse transcription, library preparation, and sequencing were carried out by Hangzhou Cosos Wisdom Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Hangzhou, China) following the manufacturer’s protocols (Illumina, San Diego, CA). The RNA-seq transcriptome library was constructed via the Illumina® Stranded mRNA Prep Ligation Kit (San Diego, CA) with 1 μg of total RNA. Messenger RNA was initially isolated via poly(A) selection using oligo(dT) beads and subsequently fragmented. Double-stranded cDNA was synthesized via a SuperScript double-stranded cDNA synthesis kit (Invitrogen, CA) with random hexamer primers (Illumina). The cDNA underwent end repair, phosphorylation, and ‘A’ base addition as per Illumina’s library construction guidelines. Libraries were size-selected for 300 bp cDNA fragments via 2% low-range Ultra agarose and amplified via PCR with Phusion DNA polymerase (NEB) for 15 cycles. Quantification was performed via a Qubit 4.0, and the paired-end RNA-seq library was sequenced on a NovaSeq 6000 sequencer (2 × 150 bp read length). Following quality control and read mapping, differential expression and functional enrichment analyses were conducted.

Statistical analysis

All tests were performed at least three times independently. Protein immunoblot images were measured in greyscale via the Analyze-Gels plug-in in ImageJ software. The fluorescence intensity and average optical density (AOD) were also measured via ImageJ software. The data are presented as the means ± SEMs. All the data were statistically analysed via GraphPad Prism version 9. Differences between groups were calculated via one-way analysis of variance, and 0.05 was regarded as statistically significant. We used Dunnett's multiple comparisons test and Tukey’s HSD multiple comparisons test as post hoc tests. In all figures, * represents comparisons with the control group, * represents p < 0.05, ** represents p < 0.01, and *** represents p < 0.001.

Results

Characterization of TFNAs

The TFNAs were synthesized from four ssDNAs (S1, S2, S3 and S4) via specific base pairing (Fig. 1A). The DLS peaks indicated that the diameter of the TFNAs was in the range of 14–24 nm (Fig. 1B). Zeta potential assays revealed that the nanoparticles were negatively charged and that the charge of the TFNAs was −30.9 mV (Fig. 1C). TEM was used to evaluate the geometrical structure of the TFNAs. Triangular structures approximately 17 nm in size were detected in the analyses (Fig. 1D). The AGE results revealed that one TFNA molecule was composed of four ssDNA molecules (Fig. 1E). The flow cytometry results shown in Fig. 1F–G revealed a significant increase in fluorescence intensity in MLE-12 cells incubated with TFNAs-Cy3. Immunofluorescence images (Fig. 1H) revealed that TFNAs-Cy3, but not ssDNA-Cy3, could be efficiently taken up by MLE-12 cells. Concurrently, intratracheal administration of TFNAs-Cy5.5 revealed pulmonary retention persisting at least 48 h (Sup Fig. 1A).

TFNAs stimulate the autophagy of MLE-12 cells

The immunofluorescence results shown in Fig. 2A indicated that TFNAs-Cy3 colocalized with LC3I/II, suggesting that TFNAs may be associated with autophagy. The flow cytometry results (Fig. 2B) revealed that the relative autophagy levels in the L group (1.02 ± 0.02, p = 0.6822) were not significantly different from those in the NC group. After intervention with TFNAs or rapamycin, relative autophagy levels were significantly higher in the LT (1.68 ± 0.01, p < 0.0001, p < 0.0001) and LR (1.47 ± 0.02, p < 0.0001, p < 0.0001) groups than those in the NC and L groups. In contrast, relative autophagy levels were significantly lower in the LT3 (0.91 ± 0.01, p = 0.0004, p < 0.0001) and L3 (0.70 ± 0.02, p < 0.0001, p < 0.0001) groups. Moreover, the autophagy level of the LT group (p < 0.0001) was significantly greater than that of the LR group. However, the autophagy level was significantly lower in the L3 group (p < 0.0001) than in the LT3 group after intervention with 3-MA. Immunofluorescence images (Fig. 2C) revealed significantly more autophagosomes in the LT and LR groups than in the other groups. The WB results (Fig. 2E–F) revealed that the relative expression of the autophagy-related genes Atg5, Atg7, Beclof1 and LC3II was significantly greater in the LT and LR groups than in the L group, whereas the relative expression of genes (except for Atg7) was significantly lower in the LT3 and L3 groups than in the L group.

TFNAs inhibit the apoptosis of MLE-12 cells stimulated with LPS via autophagy

The flow cytometry results (Fig. 2D) revealed that the apoptosis rates of the LT3 (34.52 ± 0.88%, p = 0.0032) and L3 (44.22 ± 0.93%, p < 0.0001) groups were significantly greater than that of the L group (28.82 ± 2.13%). After intervention with TFNAs or rapamycin, the apoptosis rates were significantly lower in the LT (13.27 ± 0.13%, p < 0.0001) and LR (17.77 ± 1.25%, p < 0.0001) groups than in the L group. Additionally, the apoptosis rate of the LT group (p = 0.0184) was significantly lower than that of the LR group. In contrast, the percentage of apoptotic cells in the L3 group (p < 0.0001) was significantly greater than that in the LT3 group.

The WB results (Fig. 2E–F) revealed that the relative expression of the apoptosis-related genes BAX/BCL-2 and cleaved caspase-3 was significantly greater in the L3 group than that in the L and LT3 groups, whereas no significant difference in expression was observed between the L and LT3 groups. However, the relative expression of BAX/BCL-2 in the LT and LR groups was significantly lower than that in the L group. The relative expression of BAX/BCL-2 in the LT group was significantly lower than that in the LR group.

TFNAs suppress oxidative stress

The expression levels of both IL-6 (p = 0.0049) and TNF (p = 0.0362) in the LT group were significantly lower than those in the L group (Fig. 3A). The expression level of only TNF (p = 0.0362) in the LT group was significantly lower than that in the L group. The expression levels of IL-6 and TNF in both the LT3 (p < 0.0001, p < 0.0001) and L3 (p < 0.0001, p < 0.0001) groups were significantly greater than those in the LT group. The expression levels of HO-1 and Nrf-2 in both the LT (p < 0.0001, p < 0.0001) and LR (p < 0.0001, p < 0.0001) groups were significantly greater than those in the L group (Fig. 3A). As shown in Fig. 3B, the MDA content (pM/10^4 cells) in the L (1.24 ± 0.09, p < 0.0001, p < 0.0001), LT3 (1.18 ± 0.06, p < 0.0001, p < 0.0001) and L3 (1.25 ± 0.09, p < 0.0001, p < 0.0001) groups was significantly greater than those in the LT (0.56 ± 0.06) and LR (0.79 ± 0.05) groups. Those of the LT group (p = 0.0004) were significantly lower than those of the LR group. However, no significant difference in the expression levels was observed among the L, LT3 and L3 groups. SOD viability (in U/10^4 cells) was significantly greater in the LT (5.45 ± 0.44 × 10–3, p < 0.0001, p < 0.0001, p < 0.0001) and LR (4.48 ± 0.16 × 10–3, p = 0.0222, p < 0.0001, p < 0.0001) groups than in the L (2.85 ± 0.31 × 10–3), LT3 (3.38 ± 0.29 × 10–3) and L3 (3.09 ± 0.16 × 10–3) groups. The SOD activities of the groups in descending order were LT, LR, L, LT3 and L3, with statistically significant differences between each of the two groups. The fluorescence intensity of DCF/DAPI can be used to semiquantitatively assess the level of cellular oxidative stress. As shown in Fig. 3C–D, the ROS levels of the LT (p = 0.0115) and LR (p = 0.0384) groups were significantly lower than those of the L group, whereas the opposite was true for the L3 group (p = 0.0135). Furthermore, the ROS level of the LT group (p = 0.0093) was lower than that of the LR group.

TFNAs alleviate LPS-induced inflammation to improve the prognosis of ALI in mice

The 72-h survival curves (Fig. 4A) indicated that the survival rate of the LT group was significantly greater than those of the L (p = 0.0168) and LT3 (p < 0.0001) groups. Moreover, the survival rate of the LT3 group (p = 0.0006) was significantly lower than that of the L group. As shown in Fig. 4B, the concentrations of IL-1b, IL-6 and TNF-α in the BALF of the LT group were significantly lower than those in the BALF of the L and LT3 groups. Furthermore, that of the LT3 group was significantly lower than that of the L group. The results of the dry/wet ratio (Fig. 4C) indicated that the inhibition of autophagy with 3-MA exacerbated LPS-induced pulmonary oedema, in contrast to TFNAs. The lung inflammation score of the LT group was significantly lower than those of the L (p = 0.0424) and LT3 (p = 0.0156) groups, whereas no significant difference was detected between the L and LT3 groups (p = 0.09876) (Fig. 5A–B). The average optical density (AOD) of the MPO in the LT group was also significantly lower than those in the L (p = 0.0009) and LT3 (p < 0.0001) groups (Fig. 5C–D). That of the LT3 group was significantly greater than that of the L group (p < 0.0001).

Special anti-inflammation effect of TFNAs

After data cleaning, we first performed dimensionality reduction on each group of data and presented the differences in principal components via principal component analysis (PCA) plots. The PCA plot (Fig. 6A) revealed that the principal components, such as the gene expression profiles, of each group had distinct characteristics. We subsequently selected the top 500 genes with the highest LogFC values from each group to construct a heatmap. The heatmap (Fig. 6B) displayed unique expression patterns in each group. Using the L group as the control, we identified differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in the LT, LT3, and LR groups. Among the DEGs (LT vs. L), 1122 genes were upregulated, and 642 genes were downregulated (Fig. 6C). Among the DEGs (LT3 vs. L), 1052 genes were upregulated, and 262 genes were downregulated. Among the DEGs (LR vs. L), 262 genes were upregulated, and 147 genes were downregulated. To explore the potential regulatory effects of TFNAs beyond autophagy, we took the intersection of DEGs (LT vs. L) and DEGs (LT3 vs. L) and then excluded the genes that overlapped with DEGs (LR vs. L) (Fig. 6D). Consequently, we identified 729 genes unrelated to autophagy activation and performed GO functional enrichment. Figure 6E shows significant upregulation of genes related to antigen processing and presentation, antiviral biological processes, and cytoplasmic membrane signal receptor complex functions.

TFNAs induce autophagy through activation of the cGAS–STING signalling pathway

The immunofluorescence results shown in Fig. 7A indicated that TFNAs-Cy3 colocalized with cGAS, suggesting that TFNAs were recognized as exogenous nucleic acids by cGAS, leading to the activation of the cGAS–STING signalling pathway after being taken up by cells. WB results (Fig. 7B–C) confirmed the increase in cGAS and STING expression levels (LT vs. L, cGAS p < 0.0001; STING p = 0.0002). Concurrently, a significant elevation in phosphorylated IRF3 (p-IRF3) levels was observed (Sup Fig. 1B). Additionally, immunofluorescence images and semiquantitative analysis (Fig. 7D–E) indicated that after the application of TFNAs, the levels of the cGAS and STING proteins within the cells significantly increased (LT vs. L, p < 0.0001; p = 0.0002). These findings suggested that the cGAS–STING signalling pathway was activated by TFNAs. However, after H-151 was used to inhibit STING function, the protein expression of LC3II protein in the LTH group was significantly lower than that in the LT group (p < 0.0001). As shown in Fig. 7E–F, the level of autophagy in the LTH group was significantly lower than that in the LT group (p < 0.0001). These findings indicated that inhibition of the cGAS–STING signalling pathway effectively suppressed TFNA-induced autophagy development. These results suggest that TFNAs induce autophagy through activation of the cGAS–STING signalling pathway. Correspondingly, IHC analysis of cGAS and STING, combined with IF assessment of IRF3 in murine lung tissues, demonstrated marked upregulation in both LT- and LPS-treated groups. Strikingly, co-administration of the autophagy inhibitor 3-methyladenine (3MA) substantially attenuated all three signaling components (Sup Fig. 1C-E).

Discussion

As nanomaterials, TFNAs are easily able to penetrate tissues and be taken up by cells [18]. After being taken up by cells, TFNAs ultimately enter lysosomes via the microtubule system [18, 24]. Intersections with organelle structures exist between the autophagy pathway and the TFNA metabolic pathway.

In this study, TFNAs significantly increased the expression of autophagy-related proteins, and many autophagosomes appeared in the immunofluorescence images. Moreover, after the use of autophagy inhibitors, the antioxidative stress and antiapoptotic effects of TFNAs were significantly reduced, indicating that the promotion of autophagosome formation and maturation is a key process in the inhibition of oxidative stress damage by TFNAs. This result is consistent with previous studies in which TFNAs demonstrated autophagy-regulating effects in other cell types, which contributed to alleviating oxidative stress and reducing apoptosis [25, 26]. These findings not only reveal the potential value of TFNAs as a therapeutic strategy but also broaden our understanding of the role of autophagy in lung injury.

Compared with rapamycin, which increases autophagy only, TFNAs have the capacity to suppress inflammatory cytokine expression and activate the Nrf2/HO-1 signalling pathway. This phenomenon suggested that TFNAs possessed unique antioxidant advantages beyond their role in inducing autophagy.

Furthermore, we explored and elucidated for the first time the potential mechanisms by which TFNAs induce autophagy. After excluding autophagy interference, GO enrichment analysis revealed that the main changes induced by TFNAs in cells were related to the immune response, antigen presentation, and the antiviral response. Considering that TFNAs are exogenous DNA drugs that can be actively transported into cells, the cGAS–STING signalling pathway might be key in sensing TFNAs and mediating downstream effects. The cGAS–STING signalling pathway is the primary effector for cells to sense and respond to the abnormal presence of double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) in the cytoplasm, which establishes an effective innate immune response of the host defence to tissue damage and pathogen invasion [27, 28]. The recognition of dsDNA by cGAS depends on the length of the DNA. Experiments have shown that dsDNA fragments longer than 45–60 base pairs more readily induce cGAS activation [29, 30]. TFNAs, with 252 base pairs, can induce an immune response through the classical cGAS–STING signalling pathway.

This may explain why TFNAs have various therapeutic effects on many cell lines. Moreover, TFNAs can activate autophagy via a nonclassical pathway. When activated STING proteins are translocated to the ER‒Golgi intermediate compartment (ERGIC), STING, as a potential autophagy receptor, uses ERGIC as the primary membrane donor, promotes LC3 lipidation, and induces autophagy, thus eliminating invading pathogens and cytoplasmic DNA.

Immunofluorescence images demonstrated the colocalization of cGAS and TFNAs. Following TFNA incubation, the protein expression levels of cGAS and STING were significantly increased. Additionally, the use of a STING inhibitor resulted in a marked reduction in the number of autophagosomes. This seems to explain the mechanism by which TFNAs induce autophagy. This study is the first to indicate that TFNAs induce autophagy through activation of the cGAS–STING signalling pathway.

The findings of this study offer a new perspective and mechanism for the use of TFNAs in the treatment of ALI (Fig. 8A). As novel nucleic acid structural materials, TFNAs not only possess good biocompatibility but also have the potential to reduce oxidative stress damage by regulating autophagy pathways. Notably, the application of TFNAs is not limited to ALI. In other diseases involving oxidative stress and dysregulated autophagy, such as neurodegenerative diseases, TFNAs may also exert similar protective effects [31].

Overview content schematic. Schematic diagram showing the biological process by which TFNAs induce autophagy through the activation of the cGAS–STING signalling pathway. First, TFNAs enter the cytoplasm and are bound by cGAS, catalysing the formation of cGAMP from ATP and GTP. cGAMP binds and activates the STING dimer protein anchored on the endoplasmic reticulum. As the STING protein is transported along the direction of the endoplasmic reticulum–ER–Golgi intermediate compartment (ERGIC)–Golgi body, an endosome forms and encloses exogenous DNA (TFNAs) to participate in the autophagic process induced by the nonclassical cGAS–STING pathway. Some TFNAs are degraded after enzymatic digestion. The undegraded part is expelled from the cell to continue participating in the cycle. The classical cGAS‒STING pathway is simultaneously activated. Activated STING recruits the key kinase TBK1, the transcription factor IRF3, and numerous regulatory proteins, forming a functional signalling complex and leading to the phosphorylation of IRF3. Activated IRF3 dimerizes and enters the nucleus to mediate the immune response

Although this study demonstrated that the cGAS–STING signalling pathway contributes to the autophagy induced by TFNAs, gene knockout or overexpression experiments could be performed to further clarify the mechanism involved. LPS not only triggers oxidative stress but also activates multiple inflammatory pathways, such as the NF-κB and NLRP3 inflammasome pathways. Whether TFNAs indirectly influence autophagy and oxidative stress by modulating these inflammatory pathways is also an important area for future research.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study demonstrated that TFNAs can increase autophagy in MLE-12 cells to reduce inflammatory damage, apoptosis and immune infiltration, thereby improving the survival rate of mice with ALI. This study is the first to prove that TFNAs induce autophagy through the activation of the cGAS–STING signalling pathway.
