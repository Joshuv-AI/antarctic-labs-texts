Estimation of the muscle force by perineural intramuscular electrical stimulation in healthy volunteers
Authors: Jae Min Kim, Tae-Woo Kim, Hye Jung Park, Se Won Lee, Yeun Jie Yoo, Mi-Jeong Yoon, So-youn Chang, Sun Jae Won
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The present study aimed to evaluate the elbow flexor force induced by perineural intramuscular stimulation compared with surface electrical stimulation (ES) and maximal voluntary contraction. Thirty nondominant arms of healthy volunteers were evaluated. Isometric elbow flexion force was evaluated using a surface electrode stimulation at the biceps brachii muscle, a perineural intramuscular stimulation around the musculocutaneous nerve, and maximum voluntary contraction. The elbow flexion force was measured at the wrist volar area in a 90° elbow flexion posture, fixed with a rigid elbow orthosis. Pain and discomfort associated with ES were evaluated using a numeric rating scale. The mean maximum elbow flexion force was 16.6 ± 4.1 kgf via voluntary contraction. The mean elbow flexion force by ES was 2.9 ± 2.0 kgf, stimulation intensity was 24.8 ± 5.5 mA, and the numeric rating scale was 5.0 ± 2.5 via surface electrode stimulation and 3.1 ± 2.0 kgf, 5.0 mA, and 3.8 ± 1.9 via perineural stimulation, respectively. ES provides 16% to 18% of the maximal voluntary contraction force in elbow flexion, which corresponds to a fair grade of muscle force. Perineural intramuscular stimulation can generate an equivocal contraction force with less discomfort in elbow flexion than surface electrode stimulation.

1. Introduction

The generation of optimal force from muscle contraction is one of the proficiencies of the human neuromuscular system. Various elements, such as muscle fiber type, metabolic condition, neural control system, and anatomic structures, are involved in generating muscle force. Voluntary muscle contraction is triggered by action potentials through the corticospinal tract, peripheral motor nerves, and neuromuscular junctions.[1] Alternatively, electrical stimulation (ES) can artificially induce muscle contraction and has been used for rehabilitation in various paralytic diseases, such as stroke, spinal cord injury, and peripheral neuropathy.[2]

ES training has been shown to recover muscle size and function,[3,4] improve bone mineral density surrounding tendon and joint structures,[5–8] and improve patient performance.[8,9] Increased blood flow, metabolic capacity, and fatigue resistance have also been reported.[4,10,11] However, the power output evoked by an ES is either weak or moderate.[5,12,13] Quadriceps contraction via ES can rotate a cycling pedal but is not strong enough for the patient to stand up without arm support. The reported torque generated in the quadriceps muscle by ES was 13 to 135 Nm in patients with complete spinal cord injury[5] and reached no more than 160 Nm even after a year of ES training.[5] These studies were performed in patients with paralytic diseases, such as spinal cord injury, estimating the force by ES of the paralytic muscles; therefore, it was impossible to measure the maximal voluntary contraction force.

The force generated by muscle contraction depends on numerous factors, such as muscle fiber length, fiber type, architecture, location of the end plate zone, and attachment point to the tendon, which differs among individual muscle fibers.[14,15] To our knowledge, no studies have investigated data regarding muscle force estimation using ES in healthy volunteers, particularly in the upper extremity muscles. In addition, no studies have evaluated the muscle force using intramuscular ES in healthy volunteers. With the advancements in robotic technology, various types of exoskeletal robots have been introduced and used for rehabilitation. The exoskeletal system can support the paretic limbs of patients with the optimal force for performing tasks. The combination of functional ES and exoskeletal support would be ideal for the rehabilitation of patients with disabilities. From this perspective, estimating the force that can be generated by ES will be meaningful as reference data for developing various types of rehabilitation systems. In addition, intramuscular ES can deliver the stimulation more accurately to the target muscle or nerve. In contrast, the surface ES has the potential to reduce the force that moves joints by stimulating unintended muscles or nerves. We hypothesized that the intramuscular ES may be able to generate greater force than the surface ES. The present study aimed to estimate the elbow flexion force evoked by ES by intramuscular perineural stimulation compared with surface ES and maximal voluntary contraction.

2. Methods

The study protocol is approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Catholic University of Korea, College of Medicine, Yeouido St. Mary’s Hospital (SC16ONSI0103) and was conducted in compliance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and its contemporary amendments and Good Clinical Practice. All the participants provided written informed consent.

Thirty healthy male volunteers aged 20 to 50 years were recruited. Exclusion criteria included any history of arm paralysis, shoulder fracture, dislocation, surgery, or neuropathy in the extremities. The circumference of the arm was measured at the midpoint of the biceps muscle belly at full extension of the elbow joint. The elbow flexion force was measured as the peak force on the wrist volar area in a supine position with 30° shoulder abduction and 90° elbow flexion secured by the metal orthosis, which prevents shoulder and elbow movement during ES. The forearm was fully supinated during the tests. Before the ES, the maximal voluntary elbow flexion force was measured as the peak force on the wrist volar area in the nondominant arm of all volunteers. The ES and the acquisition of the compounded motor unit action potential were performed by the Medelec Synergy electromyography machine. First, for the surface electrode stimulation, the stimulation point and intensity for surface ES were identified around the axillary area, which showed the largest compound muscle action potential recorded in the muscle belly of the biceps brachii muscle using 0.5-ms pulse-duration single stimuli. The recording electrode was placed at the midpoint of the biceps muscle belly, and the reference electrode was placed on the biceps brachii tendon in the antecubital fossa. Subsequently, 15 Hz of monophasic ES was applied for 5 s, and the process was repeated 3× at intervals of more than 30 s. Among these 3 stimulations, the median maximum force at the palmar region of the wrist was recorded. Second, a perineural stimulation was performed. Using ultrasonography with a 5- to 12-MHz linear array transducer, the triangular appearance of the musculocutaneous nerve was identified immediately after penetrating the coracobrachialis.[16] A perineural intramuscular electrical stimulus was then applied (Fig. 1). The monopolar needle electrode (28G × 37 mm) was inserted with ultrasonography guidance. The stimulation started with a 2-mA stimulus intensity and was gradually increased until the largest motor unit action potential was evoked. The largest motor unit action potential was defined when the motor unit action potential no longer increased as the stimulus was gradually increased by 0.5 mA. The largest motor unit action potential was obtained at a stimulus intensity of ≈3 to 4 mA for all the participants. To provide supramaximal stimulation and reduce the bias that may be caused by insufficient stimulation intensity, the peak force was obtained at a stimulation intensity of 5 mA. Other parameters such as frequency (15 Hz, pulse duration of 0.5 ms, and pulse interval of 66 ms), stimulation duration (5 s), stimulation interval (>30 s), and volunteer position were kept consistent in the same way as the surface electrode stimulation. Sequential stimulation was performed 3×, and the median value of the force was obtained. Subjective discomfort due to ES was evaluated using a numerical rating scale ranging from 1 (no discomfort) to 10 (maximal discomfort).

Perineural stimulation. Using ultrasound guidance, the tip of the needle electrode was placed within 2 mm of the musculocutaneous nerve just after penetrating the coracobrachialis where it has a triangular appearance. LH = long head, N = nerve, SH = short head.

2.1. Statistical analysis

The elbow flexion force and motor unit action potential amplitude during stimulation and stimulation intensity are presented as the mean ± standard deviation. The Shapiro-Wilk test was performed to check the normal distribution. A paired t test was performed to compare the elbow flexion force between surface electrode stimulation and perineural stimulation. The Pearson correlation test was performed to identify the relationship between the parameters of contraction forces. All analyses were performed using SPSS, version 12.0 (P < .05).

3. Results

The average age of the participants was 30.2 ± 9.4 years, height was 174.1 ± 4.9 cm, and body mass was 72.2 ± 8.7 kg. The average mid-humerus circumference was 30.4 ± 3.0 cm, all participants were right-handed, and the study was conducted using their nondominant arm. The mean voluntary contraction force is 16.6 ± 4.1 kgf. 4. Discussion

The ES provided 16% to 18% of the maximal voluntary contraction force in elbow flexion. This finding is similar to that reported in patients with cervical cord injuries.[17] Considering that the mass of the forearm and hand is ≈2 kg,[18] this strength corresponds to a fair grade (Medical Research Council scale of 3) in muscle strength. However, the perineural intramuscular stimulation showed no significant difference in the evoked contraction force compared with the surface electrode stimulation. Owing to the significantly lower stimulation intensity, perineural intramuscular stimulation produced less discomfort than surface electrode stimulation despite the pain related to needle penetration of the skin. In addition, the maximal voluntary force showed a significant correlation with the force of perineural intramuscular stimulation but not with the force induced by surface electrode stimulation. This result suggests that perineural intramuscular stimulation may be a reliable method for the ES to evoke contraction force in the elbow flexor muscle.

ES-induced motor unit recruitment is nonselective, and electrically stimulated contractions cause increased metabolic costs and rapid muscle fatigue.[19] A low-pulse frequency (16 Hz) was shown to reduce rapid muscle fatigue compared with a high pulse frequency (30 Hz) at the expense of force production.[20] With the advancements in robotic technology, patients can be supported by external assistance. In such a situation, reducing muscle fatigue would become more important to enhance the patient’s performance and facilitate the use of the ES system. Thus, in the present study, low-pulse frequency stimulation (15 Hz) was used, as it is considered more practical for patients requiring prolonged stimulation.

However, the appropriate frequency of ES in rehabilitation remains controversial. In general, 20- to 50-Hz patterns are used.[21,22] Fuglevand and Keen[23] reported that a 30-Hz frequency preserved a force better than 15 Hz during a sustained maximum voluntary contraction. Thus, further studies comparing various frequencies such as 15 Hz, 30 Hz, or higher versus maximal voluntary contractions in control subjects would be necessary to identify more optimal and balanced stimulation methods for maximal muscle power generation and decreased fatigue.

Ideally, ES is delivered at the motor point, which is usually located around the muscle belly. Because muscle contraction causes the active muscle to glide under the skin with surface electrode stimulation, the ES point and intensity recordings are prone to variability. In addition, in the preliminary trial of this study in which needle ES was performed at the biceps muscle belly, the pain and discomfort around the biceps area persisted for over 2 days. This occurred because the stimulation needle was fixed at the entry point by the skin and other connective tissues, but the muscle was contracted by the ES, which caused muscle damage at the needle tip. To minimize this variability and risk in the present study, ES was performed in a fixed position using a metal orthosis with 90° elbow flexion to minimize the change in muscle length during ES. In addition, perineural ES to the musculocutaneous nerve was performed at the proximal area just distal to the coracobrachialis muscle. As a result, none of the participants reported pain or discomfort at the needle stimulation site 15 minutes after the test in the present study. Ideally, intramuscular ES at the motor point around the muscle belly would be an acceptable option for functional ES if the stimulation electrode is implantable and could move with muscle contraction.

Surface electrode stimulation or perineural stimulation of peripheral nerves, such as the musculocutaneous nerve in this study, has technical limitations for complex motions such as normal fine hand motion. With the advancement of technology, implantable electrodes have been used in various medical fields.[24,25] However, several technological obstacles remain to its use for musculoskeletal paralysis. In the upper extremities, the precise control of muscle contraction is important. To precisely control muscle contraction by ES, it is necessary for the implantable intramuscular electrode to be small enough to be located on the motor point so that it can stimulate an individual motor point without affecting other muscles.

The present study had several limitations. First, stimulation was performed in healthy volunteers only on the nondominant arm. In addition, it has been studied only in men. In patients with chronic diseases, myopathy, or neuromuscular disorders, the muscle may not produce the same strength as in healthy volunteers. Therefore, further studies are necessary in both men and women with various paralytic diseases. Second, the brachioradialis and pronator teres can act as weak elbow flexors. In maximal voluntary contraction, the muscle contraction forces can be summed up to the estimated force. The muscle force that can be induced by ES may be slightly stronger than 16% to 18% of the maximal voluntary contraction force found in the present study considering the effect of the brachioradialis and pronator teres muscles. Third, stimulation for a longer time than 5 s (which was studied in the present study) may evoke stronger power. However, if stimulation longer than 5 s is necessary to evoke meaningful strength in the ES system, it will mean that these ES systems may be difficult to use in daily life. For example, if we imagine a patient with arm paralysis wearing the ES aid system on the patient’s paretic arm to help take a spoon of water, 5 s would be too long for most people. For a practical ES system, it is necessary to develop a more rapid responsive system that can evoke meaningful strength. Finally, the needle insertion is more invasive than the attaching surface electrode. The needle insertion can make volunteers nervous, which can affect the results.

In conclusion, ES could evoke at least 16% to 18% of the maximal voluntary contraction force, which may be considered a fair-grade muscle force in elbow flexion. Perineural intramuscular nerve stimulation evoked an equivalent contraction force and did not cause more discomfort than surface electrode stimulation did. The results of the present study may be helpful for the design of activities using functional ES during rehabilitation treatment and ultimately in the development of new medical devices.
