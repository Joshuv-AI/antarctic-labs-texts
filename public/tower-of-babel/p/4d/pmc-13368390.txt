Connecting the dots between Mycoplasma pneumoniae encephalitis (MPE) and encephalitis lethargica: a case report and presentation of a diagnostic tool for MPE
Authors: Matthias Endmann, Khaled Elsayed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSince its initial discovery in the 1940s, Mycoplasma pneumoniae has emerged as a commonly diagnosed bacterium, gaining recognition as a global cause of primary atypical pneumonia. M. pneumoniae is a significant cause of upper and lower respiratory tract infections worldwide, predominantly among children. Encephalitis is one of its most severe extrapulmonary manifestations and may result in significant neurological sequelae. We present a case of M. pneumoniae encephalitis and introduce a diagnostic tool to support its diagnosis.Keywords: Mycoplasma pneumoniae, Encephalitis, Encephalitis lethargica

Introduction

Since its initial discovery in the 1940s, Mycoplasma pneumoniae has emerged as a commonly diagnosed bacterium, gaining recognition as a global cause of primary atypical pneumonia. M. pneumoniae is a significant cause of upper and lower respiratory tract infections worldwide, predominantly among children. In periods with high prevalence, up to 40% of community-acquired pneumonia cases in hospitalized children are attributed to this pathogen. M. pneumoniae can also cause extrapulmonary manifestations in the skin, hematologic, cardiovascular, musculoskeletal, or nervous system in up to 25% of cases [1]. Among the severe complications, encephalitis is one of the most common [2], with 5%–10% of pediatric encephalitis cases being associated with M. pneumoniae infection, and it results in persistent neurological sequelae in up to 60% of affected individuals [3].

Case Report

A previously healthy 10-year-old boy fell unexpectedly and unobserved onto his left side. The fall onto the parquet floor initially resulted in an impact to the left shoulder and then to the parietal region of the head. Following the fall, he exhibited a tonic-clonic seizure lasting 1–2 minutes. Subsequently, he became briefly rigid. His mother witnessed this episode and called the ambulance. Upon arrival, another seizure occurred. The child was given 7.5 mg of buccal midazolam by the emergency physician. He was in a stable condition, conscious with normal vital signs, euglycemia, and normal blood pressure values. Subsequently, he was transported to our clinic. His parents reported that the child had been suffering from a prolonged respiratory tract infection with cough for the preceding 2 weeks. Two days prior to the incident, his parents described a change in behavior characterized by a lack of motivation, loss of appetite, and blank staring.

Because it was not clear whether the seizures or the fall came first, we performed cranial computed tomography, which did not show any abnormalities. The blood gas analysis, blood glucose level, C-reactive protein, white blood cell count, red blood cell count, platelets, lactate dehydrogenase, alanine aminotransferase, aspartate aminotransferase, gamma-glutamyl transferase, creatine kinase, lipase, immunoglobulin G (IgG), IgM, IgA, and D-dimers were all normal. Only the erythrocyte sedimentation rate was high (45 mm/hr). An electroencephalogram (EEG) on the second day showed generalized rhythmic delta activity, indicating encephalitis or encephalopathy (Figure 1). An EEG taken on the fifth day showed significantly faster activity (Figure 2). Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the brain on the second day showed hyperintensity in the basal ganglia, suggesting encephalitis (Figures 3, 4). A follow-up MRI on the fifth day showed light regression of lateral ventricular edema (Figure 4). A lumbar puncture on the second day in the hospital showed mild pleocytosis (23/µL), normal cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) glucose and normal CSF protein. Multiple CSF and serum analyses at different times showed no evidence of antibodies that might indicate autoimmune encephalitis (Table 1). Multiplex polymerase chain reaction (PCR; BioFire Meningitis/Encephalitis Panel; bioMérieux), and single M. pneumonia PCR analyses of the CSF were negative (Table 2). Due to pleocytosis exceeding the normal range we initiated empiric intravenous antibacterial therapy (ceftriaxone) and antiviral therapy (acyclovir).

We discontinued both of those treatments on the following day after receiving the negative PCR results for the CSF that excluded herpes simplex virus and bacterial infection (Table 2). After 24 hours, a greater than fourfold rise in serum M. pneumoniae-specific IgM antibodies was detected. Additionally, at that point, M. pneumoniae could be detected from a throat swab by single PCR, and no other viral or bacterial pathogens were detected by multiplex PCR (BioFire Respiratory 2.1 Panel; bioMérieux). Therefore, we started treating the child with oral doxycycline and intravenous methylprednisolone. The diagnosis of M. pneumoniae encephalitis (MPE) was confirmed by detecting a specific intrathecal antibody synthesis (IgM) against M. pneumoniae (antibody specific index [AI], 3.4; normal range, <1.5).

The patient showed a slight clinical recovery after receiving corticosteroids. However, beginning on the fifth day, he developed new symptoms: dysarthria, muscle stiffness, dysphagia, drooling of saliva, and aphasia. The patient was referred to an intensive care unit (ICU) at a tertiary center, where he received intravenous immunoglobulin (2 g/kg: 400 mg/day) for 5 days, in addition to continued corticosteroids. However, the symptoms continued to worsen, including tonic dystonia, hyperreflexia, and intermittent positivity for the Babinski reflex. At the beginning of his illness, nonverbal communication by means of eyelid closure or handshake was possible. Later, even that became impossible. Although an MRI on the 11th day showed regression of the signs of inflammation, his clinical situation was so unstable that plasmapheresis was conducted. Seven cycles were performed over 10 days, and after that he showed improvement of his neurological symptoms, but he still had swallowing difficulties and mild ataxia. As a treatment for dystonia and Parkinsonian symptoms, he received a combination of ʟ-dopa, benserazide, and trihexyphenidyl. During his 4-week stay in the ICU, he was able to interact only with eye movement and handshaking. By discharge, he was able to walk and talk again and started neurorehabilitation. After that rehabilitation, the reproduction of motor movement was successful; however, persistent impairment and learning difficulties led to his reclassification into a different category at school.

Discussion

This report presents a case of MPE diagnosed with clinical signs of encephalitis lethargica. Our patient fulfilled the clinical diagnostic criteria (Figure 5) [4].

Diagnostic criteria for encephalitis lethargica [4]

The diagnosis of encephalitis lethargica is established when at least three of seven major criteria are fulfilled. In the present case, criteria 1, 2, 5, and 7 were fulfilled.

Encephalitis lethargica is an atypical form of encephalitis. Also known as “sleeping sickness,” it was extensively described by the neurologist von Economo in 1917. It is a serious sequela of brain inflammation that can lead to lethargy, sleep attacks, and Parkinson-like symptoms. According to the literature, the association between a loss of consciousness and flu-like symptoms is common. Both the causative agent and neuropathological pattern remain unknown. The link between the loss of consciousness and flu-like symptoms was the profound somnolence reported in encephalitis lethargica and influenza [5].

During the so-called Spanish Flu in 1918, millions of cases of encephalitis lethargica worldwide were described, suggesting an association with influenza infection. In recent times, scientists have only sporadically dedicated themselves to this forgotten and misunderstood clinical picture. The most accepted explanatory model for the origin of encephalitis lethargica is an immunologically mediated post-infectious process. The neuropsychiatric symptoms develop after inflammation of deep gray matter, particularly the basal ganglia, which is involved in the control of mood, emotion, behavior, and volition [6]. M. pneumoniae infection as a potential cause has previously been discussed in the literature. Von Economo himself had doubts about the influenza theory [7], and later histological findings showed no evidence of influenza DNA [4]. Recent papers emphasize that, contrary to what the name suggests, the Parkinsonian syndrome, not sleep, is the main issue [8].

M. pneumoniae infections can occur at any age, but lower respiratory tract disease is primarily observed in school-aged children and young adults [9,10]. Many M. pneumoniae infections are subclinical. However, a dramatic course leading to a fatal outcome has been reported [11]. M. pneumoniae transmission occurs through the respiratory route, and infection typically persists for several weeks. Encephalitis is the most severe extrapulmonary manifestation associated with M. pneumoniae infection. In prospective long-term studies, the percentage of acute encephalitis cases attributed to M. pneumoniae ranged from 5% to 10% [12].

The pathogenesis of encephalitis associated with M. pneumoniae remains unclear. It could be a result of direct central nervous system (CNS) infection, the production of neurotoxins by the bacterium, or immune-mediated damage. An immune-mediated injury could result from cross-reacting antibodies targeting antigen(s) shared by Mycoplasma and the brain, pathogen-induced immunosuppression, immune complex vasculopathy, or a neurological reaction involving vascular microthrombi [3]. In cases of acute encephalitis, direct infection of the brain is uncommon but has been observed in both adults and children [11]. Thus, M. pneumoniae in the CSF through culture or PCR serves as strong evidence for its causality. However, the PCR detection rate of M. pneumoniae in the CSF of patients with encephalitis caused by this pathogen is low, ranging from 0% to 14% [13]. Nonetheless, the absence of the organism from the CSF does not definitively rule out M. pneumoniae as a potential cause because CNS disease can have an indirect immunological basis [14].

In the literature, an MPE diagnosis relies on detecting positive Ig titers in blood or positive M. pneumoniae PCR results from nasopharyngeal swabs. An enzyme immunoassay test can distinguish between IgG and IgM, which can help to differentiate current infections from past ones because reinfection is not always associated with the formation of new IgM [15]. But neither positive nasopharyngeal swab PCR nor increased levels of immunoglobulins against M. pneumoniae is proof that the pathogen has caused a particular case of encephalitis.

Instead, the identification of intrathecal-synthesized antibodies against M. pneumoniae is required. It is important to distinguish between intrathecally produced antibodies and those that have diffused from the blood to the CSF when diagnosing an inflammatory disease of the CNS [16].

In our experience, assessment of the AI in CSF and serum is not well established or widely recognized for diagnosing MPE, even though it was recommended long ago [17]. However, without that analysis, the diagnosis of MPE remains uncertain. In contrast, the use of the AI is well established for diagnosing neuroborreliosis and neurosyphilis.

M. pneumoniae lacks a cell wall, and beta-lactam antibiotics are ineffective. Alternatively, tetracyclines, quinolones, and macrolides can be used in the treatment of infections. Due to side effects in children, macrolides are the first-choice antibiotics, but except for azithromycin, they do not penetrate into the CSF [18]. However, understanding the pathogenesis of the disease plays a role in treatment. The importance of antibiotics in treating MPE is still not proven. In some clinical trials and reports, children recovered without antibiotics. Intravenous immunoglobulin, steroids, and plasmapheresis (as a last resort) have been proposed as therapeutic options for M. pneumoniae–associated CNS disease. In our case, immune therapy with methylprednisolone and immunoglobulin failed, but plasmapheresis was successful.

To our knowledge, this case is the first in Germany in which MPE was diagnosed and confirmed with evidence of specific intrathecal antibody (IgM) synthesis against M. pneumoniae, positive serology, and detection of the pathogen in the throat. Therefore, we strongly recommend testing for intrathecal antibodies against M. pneumoniae to secure an MPE diagnosis. Moreover, the connection between MPE and encephalitis lethargica is not unlikely, as reported before [7].
