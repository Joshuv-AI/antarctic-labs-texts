Factors associated with non-participation of preterm infants at two and five years in a large population-based French healthcare network
Authors: Lucas Anzelin, Matthieu Hanf, Laurence Desplanques, Michèle Granier, Laurence Leloup, Alexandre Lapillonne, Véronique Pierrat, Sylvie Escolano, Pascale Tubert-Bitter, Anne C.M. Thiébaut
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAlthough recommended for the early identification and intervention for developmental impairments, monitoring of preterm infants over several years is hampered by loss of follow-up that inevitably occurs. This study aimed to assess the non-participation of preterm infants in their two- and five-year visits as well as associated factors.MethodsInfants born before 33 weeks of gestational age, who were enrolled between 2016 and 2022 in a prospective, population-based cohort in Paris area, France, and were eligible for their two- and five-year visits were selected. Among factors reflecting parental socio-economic characteristics, medical characteristics of the mother and her pregnancy, perinatal characteristics of the child, and contextual variables, those associated with the likelihood of non-participation in the two- or five-year visit were investigated using univariate and multivariable logistic regression models and multiple imputations.ResultsOf the 12,685 and 6,661 preterm infants followed-up at two- and five-years, respectively, 4,871 (38.4%) and 4,069 (61.1%) did not participate in the two- and five-year visits. Factors associated with non-participation in the two-year visit were more recent year of birth, younger maternal age, several children at home, lower educational level, mother unemployed or on parental leave, father with an intermediate occupation, tobacco during pregnancy, appropriate or large birth weight for gestational age, and birth in maternity unit other than inclusion. Conversely, factors associated with participation in the two-year visit were a pregnancy achieved by assisted reproductive technology, lower gestational age, postnatal corticosteroid prescription, administration of monoclonal antibodies against respiratory syncytial virus, a bronchopulmonary dysplasia, breastfeeding at discharge, a private or nonhospital referring physician, and living more than 10 km from the referring physician. Factors associated with non-participation in the five-year visit were almost identical.ConclusionFactors associated with non-participation of preterm infants within a healthcare network were found to be multidimensional. These findings are critical for the development of more intensive and individualized follow-up strategies for preterm infants.Trial registrationThe SEV-IDF program is registered with the French data protection authority for clinical research (CNIL, No.1003270/1802983/1802601/1800966/1800657).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12887-026-06862-y.

Background

Preterm infants are at particularly high risk for increased morbidity and mortality, as well as long-term motor, cognitive, and sensory impairments [1–7]. Therefore, they should be monitored from birth to detect any potential adverse outcomes and minimize future sequelae through appropriate care and interventions [8]. The period of the first years of life is critical to diagnose disabilities or sensory deficits [9]. Current recommendations emphasize the need to monitor preterm infants over a long period of time to identify abnormalities that may develop later in life [8]. As non-adherence is inevitable in long-term medical follow-up, a large number of preterm infants are usually lost to follow-up [10, 11, 21]. From a medical perspective, these infants represent a missed opportunity for early detection and treatment of sequelae caused by preterm birth. Identification of factors associated with non-participation is therefore critical for designing effective patient-retention interventions in this population.

To understand the reasons for high non-participation rates in preterm birth cohorts, several studies have attempted to analyse factor associated with loss to follow-up [13–20]. Most of these studies focused on short-term outcomes, usually around two years of age, and were limited to a single dimension of explanatory variables, either socio-economic and demographic, [11, 13–15, 17–20] clinical, [11, 14–18, 20] geographic, [11, 17, 20] or specific cohort or program factors [14, 16, 20]. This methodological heterogeneity of published studies leads to scattered results [10, 21].

The purpose of this study was (i) to assess the non-participation rates of preterm infants born before 33 weeks of Gestational Age (GA), at their two- and five-year visits, and (ii) to identify perinatal, medical, and socio-economic factors associated with non-participation in a large healthcare population-based network in the Paris metropolitan area in France. Our interest was to evaluate whether similar factors were associated with short (two-year visit) and longer term (five-year visit) follow-up when, respectively, motor and cognitive development are assessed.

Methods

Population and study design

This population-based study included preterm infants born before 33 weeks of GA in the Ile-de-France (IDF) region (i.e., Paris metropolitan area), alive at hospital discharge, and prospectively enrolled in the follow-up program for vulnerable children (SEV, Suivi des enfants vulnérables) [22]. The SEV-IDF program was launched in 2016 with the following dual objectives: to detect early clinical anomalies in all preterm infants born in the region and to provide data for epidemiological studies. The program includes all 59 maternities with a neonatal unit located in the IDF region, 15 of which have neonatal intensive care units. These 59 maternities are organized into six perinatal networks based primarily on their geographic location (Figure S1). After parental consent is obtained, new-borns are included in the follow-up program before hospital discharge and monitored until the age of seven. According to the health regional agency reports, the SEV-IDF program inclusion rate relative to the population has remained stable over the years and is estimated at 79.4% in 2023 [23, 24].

Pregnancy, delivery and infant discharge characteristics are recorded prospectively by the paediatricians in charge in the maternity. The subsequent monitoring of the children enrolled in the program is conducted in a standardized manner at four months, one year, and two years of corrected age, and then annually until seven years of age through a network of approximately 500 trained physicians. These referring physicians are affiliated to private or public hospitals, maternal and child health clinics, or are private practitioners. Each visit until the child reaches the age of seven is pre-scheduled on specific dates based on the child’s date of birth. To confirm appointments, parents must contact the medical office a few months before the scheduled date to set a final date and time. These visits are free of charge for the parents. At the hospital discharge and during each visit, clinical data are entered by, respectively, the hospital neonatal service and the referring physician, into a centralized database that allows the organization of follow-up. The collected data can then be anonymized and extracted for statistical analysis to enable epidemiological studies.

The children considered for the present study were those included between January 1, 2016 and December 31, 2024, and aged at least 2.5 years or 5.5 depending on the analysis.

Explanatory variables

We simultaneously evaluated several dimensions as factors potentially associated with non-participation in visits: parental socio-economic characteristics, medical characteristics of the mother and her pregnancy, perinatal characteristics of the child, as well as contextual variables evaluated at enrolment in the SEV-IDF program.

Parental socio-economic characteristics were: place of residence, mother’s age at birth, mother’s partnership status, healthcare coverage, housing conditions defined as an individual housing or other (Near relative / Home placement / Emergency establishment), number of children at home, household economic resources, mother’s educational level and each parent’s working situation and occupation. The socio-professional categories were based on the French National Institute for Statistics and Economic Studies (INSEE, Institut National de la Statistique et des Etudes Economiques) classification [25], grouped as follows: (1) high-grade professionals (e.g., executives, professionals); (2) intermediate occupations (e.g., technicians, mid-level supervisors); (3) employees (e.g., administrative or service personnel) or farmers; (4) working parent with unreported occupation; (5) parent on parental leave; (6) unemployed parent.

Among medical characteristics of the mother and her pregnancy, we considered pregnancy achieved by assisted reproductive technology, tobacco and alcohol consumption during pregnancy (at least one consumption), antenatal prescription of corticosteroids, chorioamnionitis (with clinical and histological diagnosis), induced preterm birth and mode of delivery.

Perinatal characteristics included: gestational age derived from the expected date of delivery estimated from the mother’s date of last menstrual period, child’s sex, birth weight defined as Small for gestational age (SGA, < 10th percentile for Fenton z-score) [26], Appropriate (AGA, 10th-90th percentile for Fenton z-score) or Large (LGA, > 90th percentile for Fenton z-score), multiple birth, length of initial hospitalization, hearing examination (performed using otoacoustic emissions or automated auditory brainstem response testing), retinopathy of prematurity (stage II or higher), post-natal corticosteroids prescription, administration of monoclonal antibodies against Respiratory Syncytial Virus (RSV), surgery during neonatal hospitalization, bronchopulmonary dysplasia defined as the requirement for supplemental oxygen or need for ventilator support at 36 weeks of gestational age), neurological evaluation (a clinical evaluation, except for infants born before 28 weeks of GA who underwent brain imaging at 40 weeks of GA) to detect abnormalities on ultrasound/magnetic resonance imaging (no risk, low, moderate or high risk, as detailed in Supplementary Table 1), and breastfeeding at discharge.

In addition to individual variables collected by paediatricians, we considered the following contextual variables describing geographical and medical context at inclusion: the urban level of the place of residence as defined by INSEE [27], the distance from home to the referring physician, the maternity risk level at birth (level 1 corresponding to a maternity without neonatal unit, level 2 corresponding to a maternity with neonatal unit with the personnel and equipment to provide care to infants born after 33 weeks of GA and level 3 corresponding to a maternity with a neonatal intensive care unit with the personnel and equipment to provide care to infants born before 33 weeks of GA), the type of referring physician (hospital-based or private) and the perinatal network of inclusion,

Outcome

The outcome of interest for the present study was non-participation at the two- and five-year visits in the SEV-IDF program. To calculate non-participation rate at the two-year visit, we selected children older than 2.5 years of corrected age at the end date, i.e., those whose expected date of birth was before July 1st, 2022. Similarly, children eligible at the five-year visit were those older than 5.5 years, i.e., those born before July 1st, 2019. Children who died or left the IDF region before two or five years of age were excluded from the corresponding analyses (Fig. 1). A child was classified as a non-participant at the visit if he/she had not completed the corresponding visit within 6 months of the scheduled date, regardless of any previous or subsequent visit.

In a sensitivity analysis, we also considered a more stringent definition where only children who did not participate in the two-year visit and were not seen again by the five-year visit were classified as lost to follow-up at the two-year visit

Statistical analysis

The distributions of variables were described using numbers and proportions before multiple imputations (MI) of missing data. All explanatory variables were considered for MI using a bootstrap and expectation-maximization algorithm as implemented in R MICE package. Fifty independent imputed data sets were generated. We used univariate logistic regression models to assess crude associations of studied characteristics with not participating in either the two-year or the five-year visit before and after MI. Associations were expressed as odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (95%CI). To select variables in our final multivariable model, only factors with a p-value < 0.2 in univariate models were kept, except for sex, gestational age, network of inclusion and year of birth, which were forced in all models. Variables with a variance inflation factor greater than 5 were considered collinear and excluded [28]. Length of hospitalization was also excluded because it was strongly correlated with GA (Spearman correlation coefficient = 0.73). All analyses were performed using the statistical software R 4.1.1. All statistical tests were two-sided. P-values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Ethics approval

This observational study was performed in accordance with the French regulations. The cohort of infants enrolled in the SEV-IDF program is registered with the French data protection authority in clinical research (Commission Nationale de l’Informatique et des Libertés, CNIL, No. 1003270/1802983/1802601/1800966/1800657)

Results

According to the Regional Health Agency, 25,246 children were eligible for the SEV-IDF program between January 1, 2016, and December 31, 2024. Among these, 17,691 preterm infants with a GA of less than 33 weeks were included in the SEV-IDF program (baseline population). Of these, 12,685 were eligible for analysis at the two-year visit and 6,661 were eligible infants at the five-year visit (Fig. 1). Of the eligible infants, 4,871 (38.4%) missed the two-year visit, 2,215 (33.3%) were considered lost to follow-up at the two-year visit under the more stringent definition, and 4,069 (61.1%) missed the five-year visit. Descriptive statistics of all children included and those eligible at the two- and five-year visits are shown in (Table 1) Characteristic distributions were similar across the three populations. Missing rates varied among variables, and were mostly below 25%.

Factors associated with non-participation in the two-year visit

In univariate analyses comparing characteristics of children who did and did not participate in the two-year visit (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Table 2), we found that children whose mothers are younger, single mother, have lower educational level, an employee occupation, or are on parental leave or unemployed, with healthcare coverage for low income, housing conditions other than individual housing, more children at home, household economic resource other than parents’ work, or whose fathers have any occupation other than high-grade were more likely to be non-participants.

Univariate and multivariable associations with two-year non-participation after multiple imputations, SEV-IDF program, 2016–2024 (N = 12,685)

Among clinical variables, pregnancy achieved by assisted reproductive technology, ante- and post-natal prescription of corticosteroids, caesarean delivery, lower GA, multiple birth, longer length of hospitalization, retinopathy of prematurity, prescription of anti-RSV monoclonal antibodies, surgery during neonatal hospitalization, bronchopulmonary dysplasia, moderate risk at the neurological evaluation and breastfeeding at discharge were associated with greater participation. Conversely, non-participating children were more likely to have been exposed to tobacco use during pregnancy and to have been born AGA or LGA.

Among contextual variables, children born in level 1 or 2 maternity hospitals (compared to those born in higher-risk level 3 hospitals) and those included in networks RSPP, NEF, or RPVO (compared to RPSOF) were more likely to be non-participants, while those followed by a private or non-hospital referring physician and those living more than 10 km from their referring physician were less likely to be non-participants.

Similar univariate associations were found in analyses before MI of missing data (Supplementary Table 3).

In the final multivariable model, the following factors were statistically significantly associated with greater odds of not participating in the two-year visit: more recent year of birth, younger maternal age, several children at home, lower educational level, mother unemployed or on parental leave, father with intermediate or unknown occupation, tobacco during pregnancy, AGA or LGA, birth outside the maternity unit of inclusion and network of inclusion RSPP, NEF and RPVO (compared to network RPSOF). Conversely, factors associated with participation in the two-year visit were a pregnancy achieved by assisted reproductive technology, smaller GA, postnatal corticosteroid prescription, administration of monoclonal antibodies against RSV, bronchopulmonary dysplasia, breastfeeding at discharge, a private or non-hospital referring physician and living more than 10 km from the referring physician. No variable other than length of initial hospitalization was excluded a posteriori for collinearity.

In the sensitivity analysis using a more stringent definition of loss to follow-up at the two-year visit (Supplementary Table 4), more recent year of birth, mother’s age between 18 and 25 years, mother with lower education level (secondary compared to higher education), an intermediate occupation, unemployed or on parental leave, tobacco during pregnancy, AGA or LGA, children born in a maternity with risk level 1 or 2, birth outside the maternity unit of inclusion and network of inclusion RSPP, NEF, RPVO and MYPA (compared to network RPSOF) were associated with increased odds of non-participation while lower gestational age, bronchopulmonary dysplasia, breastfeeding at discharge, a non-hospital referring physician and living more than 10 km from the referring physician for factors associated with decreased odds.

Factors associated with non-participation in the five-year visit

At the five-year visit, we observed univariate associations similar to those found in the analyses at the two-year visit, after (Fig. 3 and Supplementary Table 5) as well as before MI (Supplementary Table 6). In the final multivariable model, some factors changed compared to the two-year model. Factors associated with increased odds of not attending the five-year visit were: younger maternal age (18–24 years old compared to 25–34), two or more children at home, mother unemployed or on parental leave, AGA or LGA and network of inclusion RSPP and RPVO (compared to network RPSOF). Conversely, factors associated with participation in the five-year visit were more recent year of birth (2018–2019 compared to 2016–2017) a lower gestational age (≤28 weeks and 29–30 weeks compared to 31–32 weeks), postnatal corticosteroid prescription, breastfeeding at discharge, private referring physician, MYPA network and living more than 10 km from the referring physician. No variable other than length of initial hospitalization was excluded a posteriori for collinearity.

Discussion

Principal findings

In a large cohort of preterm infants, we observed non-participation rates of 38.4% and 61.1% at the two- and five-year visits, respectively. Non-participation was associated with a wide range of individual socio-economic, clinical, and contextual factors. It was generally higher among children from a lower socio-economic environment and among those in better health at birth.

Strengths of the study

One major strength of our study is its large sample size. At the time of the analyses, nearly 20,000 children were enrolled in the SEV-IDF program, resulting in cohorts of preterm children after two and five years of follow-up among the largest to date. The long-term follow-up of the children is another strength of the study and will allow us to study relevant outcomes, such as cognitive and language development, schooling or psycho-emotional difficulties. Another and final strength of this study lies in the number and diversity of the available variables, although additional data on the mother’s health, her geographical origin and any medical conditions that arose during pregnancy might have been valuable. The lack of specific groups of data or dimensions is often cited as one of the limitations of other studies assessing factors associated with optimal follow-up [11, 13, 29].

Limitations of the data

The main limitations of the study come from the dual nature of the program and the networks’ objectives, with (i) an assistance value for children and families on the one hand, and (ii) a tool for assessing care and needs for families. In real life, this raises the question of why parents engage in follow-up. The operational nature of the SEV-IDF program could result in lower quality of the data collected, especially for healthy children. Lower data quality is notably reflected by the frequency of missing data. By using an appropriate methodology that assumes that data were missing at random, we were able to limit the loss of observations and the selection bias that might result [3, 6, 30].

The underreporting of specific reasons for exiting the network, such as moving outside the IDF region, could lead to slightly overestimated non-participation rates. During an email reactivation campaign, only 19 families (< 0.1%) replied that they had moved out of the IDF region, even though they had not declared their exiting the network. Children with severe disabilities, such as cerebral palsy, severe cognitive impairment, or severe behavioural problems, are monitored in more appropriate programs than the SEV-IDF program. This could also explain the dropout rate although to a limited extent, as only 79 out of 17,691 children were identified to have left the program for this reason. In the absence of information about their network dropout, other children are considered lost to follow-up even though they are being cared for in another specific care structure. This under-reporting inevitably leads to a bias because these children are usually more clinically fragile [31]. It is not possible to estimate the exact number of affected children at this time, but efforts are underway to establish a system for referencing these children within the SEV-IDF program. However, we believe their number is small enough not to bias the results. In a similar cohort, fewer than 5% of preterm children developed cerebral palsy [3]. Consistently, we found that the healthier children, not those with severe conditions, were more likely to miss their two- or five-year visit.

Moreover, data entry practices may vary across follow-up networks. Depending on network-specific human resources, policies and the practices of referring physicians within each network, some may be more rigorous in recording visit details than others, particularly for healthy children. This may partially explain the observed lower participation in specific networks or for hospital-based physicians. This may strengthen the association between better child health and lower participation.

Although the results at the five-year visit were generally comparable to those at the two-year visit, the lack of statistical power due to the smaller sample size may have prevented some factors from being statistically significant at the later visit. Another interpretation could be that, in the context of long-term follow-up, socioeconomic and clinical characteristics are less associated with participation while the organization has a greater influence.

Finally, caution is required not to over-interpret the results given the multiplicity of tests, which may have increased the likelihood of statistical significance by chance. Nevertheless, the variables we found to be statistically significantly associated with loss to follow-up were consistent with those reported previously.

Interpretation

Overall, the non-participation rates observed in the SEV-IDF program were higher than those reported in other studies, although the populations varied across studies with respect to the degree of preterm birth (extremely, very, or moderate-to-late). Thus, a systematic review of 20 studies from nine countries found that non-participation rates at 18 to 24 months ranged between 0% and 28.4% in extremely preterm children (born before 28 weeks of gestational age) [10]. A study investigating factors of non-participation in preterm children (born before 33 weeks of GA) reported a non-participation rate at two years of 26.8% [14], while the rate of loss to follow-up at five years was 19% in the French EPIPAGE-2 cohort also focusing on preterm children [4]. Finally, a non-participation rate of 10.5% at two years and 21.9% at five years was reported in the French LIFT cohort of infants born before 37 weeks of gestational age [32]. The higher rates observed in our study may result from the operational nature of the SEV-IDF program, yielding a real-life cohort rather than a traditional research cohort with a particular focus on participant follow-up such as EPIPAGE cohorts [4]. Another possible explanation is that the IDF is a region with a large number of migratory movements, both to or from other regions, and within the IDF region itself [33]. These frequent moves pose specific challenges in terms of follow-up management, requiring effective coordination between the various perinatal health professionals, unlike in other French regions such as the LIFT cohort [32].

Most factors we found to be associated with non-participation were consistent with those reported in the literature. In a recent systematic review of 46 preterm child cohorts [21], the characteristics most described (in three cohorts or more) were younger, foreign-born (variable not available in this study), or multiparous mother, lower socio-economic status, lower maternal education, being a single parent, higher gestational age, better lung function, poorer cognitive performance, and severe disabilities. Similarly, in our study, a lower socio-economic environment was reflected in mothers having lower levels of education or not working. We also found that younger (compared to age over 25 years) maternal age was associated with a higher risk of non-participation. Higher non-participation in households with more children likely reflects greater household workloads and thus difficulties in regularly attending the SEV-IDF program. These results on socio-economic factors have also been observed in similar studies [4, 14, 17].

Better participation among children born more prematurely and among children with a post-natal prescription of corticosteroid therapy suggests that children with more favourable clinical birth characteristics are more likely to miss their two-year visit. These clinical factors associated with lower non-participation have been identified in the past [4, 11, 13, 17, 18, 20] Birth outside the inclusion maternity hospital — corresponding to a neonatal transfer from the birth facility to a unit better suited for specialized care and follow-up — may represent an additional burden for parents. This situation might lead them to perceive inclusion in the SEV-IDF program less positively. This perception could be reinforced by poor communication or a lack of clear information regarding the reasons for the transfer and the objectives of the SEV-IDF program, and may consequently result in reduced engagement with follow-up procedures. In the same line, several studies of high-risk infants have shown that follow-up could be influenced by the place of birth and the level of care of the maternity hospital [11, 14, 17].

Geographically, children who lived more than 10 km from their follow-up site had better participation than children who lived less distant. Despite multivariable adjustment, we cannot rule out residual confounding by socioeconomical or child’s health conditions. Children living far from follow-up sites are also those with other individual or structural characteristics associated with better follow-up (e.g., network RPSOF or MYPA in our data). Thus, more vulnerable children require care at large hospitals, which are generally farther away than referring physicians. Consequently, these greater distances may also reflect poorer child health. Differences in non-participation rates across inclusion networks, types of referring physician, and levels of care of the maternity hospital may reflect territorial socioeconomic disparities [23, 24] The geographical accessibility of local medical resources for families and the saturation of healthcare facilities vary considerably throughout the IDF region. Moreover, the resources deployed by each network to ensure continuity of care also vary. Even if these effects are less pronounced at the five-year visit than at the two-year one, and even less in the sensitivity analysis with a stricter definition, this demonstrates the importance of adjusting explanatory models for these factors, which may play a crucial role in follow-up studies.

Conclusion

This study highlights possible reasons for non-participation in cohorts of preterm children. Understanding the underlying mechanisms that lead to non-participation will help perinatal networks and referring physicians make targeted efforts to keep children in follow-up. In practice, efforts should be made to improve follow-up for the high-risk groups identified in this study. Of the two groups at risk of non-participation that we have identified, those in good health do not really represent a missed opportunity. On the other hand, there is a missed opportunity for children living in more precarious conditions. One course of action could be to strengthen support for networks in the most disadvantaged areas. Importantly, our findings suggest that these mechanisms are multidimensional and need to be considered simultaneously.
