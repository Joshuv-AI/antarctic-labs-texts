Impact of Age and Sex on Anatomical Variations of the Infra-acetabular Screw Corridor: An Imaging Study
Authors: Lei Wang, Yu Zhang, Guoyong Yin, Chun Lu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study investigates how age and sex influence the morphology of the infra-acetabular screw (IAS) corridor by examining its anatomical features and relationships with adjacent critical structures across diverse age and sex cohorts. Additionally, surgical simulations were performed to identify reliable anatomical landmarks and refine intraoperative fluoroscopic images.MethodsA retrospective analysis (2016–2024) was performed on 178 unilateral acetabular fracture cases (105 males, 73 females; mean age, 55.74 ± 15.0 years). Participants were stratified into 4 groups based on sex and age (threshold: 60 years): young males (n = 57), elderly males (n = 48), young females (n = 36), and elderly females (n = 37). Pelvic models were reconstructed using Mimics 21.0 software to measure infra-acetabular corridor diameter (IACD) and obturator groove depth (OGD). IAS placement was simulated to determine the entry point, screw dimensions, and minimum distances to adjacent structures. Simulated fluoroscopic images assessed the sensitivity and specificity for cortical breach detection at incremental screw diameters.ResultsYoung males demonstrated marginally greater distances to the eminentia iliopectinea compared to elderly males (13.04 ± 1.86 vs. 12.32 ± 1.20 mm, p < 0.05). Elderly females had a significantly larger IACD than young females (4.98 ± 0.95 vs. 4.51 ± 0.58 mm, p < 0.05), and female OGD exceeded that of males (11.16 ± 2.10 vs. 9.66 ± 1.98 mm, p < 0.05). A strong negative correlation was identified between IACD and OGD (r = 0.842): IACD = 8.730–0.358 OGD. The modified iliac-outlet view reduced false positives from 76.4% to 44.38% and improved sensitivity.ConclusionsThe IAS entry point should be more medial in young males than in elderly males. Elderly females exhibited a larger IACD. The negative correlation between IACD and OGD results in a narrower IACD in females. The modified iliac-outlet view improved sensitivity and reduced false positives for cortical breach detection.Keywords: Acetabular fracture, Infra-acetabular corridor diameter, Obturator groove depth, Fluoroscopy

Robust fixation is essential for maintaining reduction in both-column acetabular fractures. However, achieving absolute stability remains challenging in cases of severe fracture comminution or osteoporosis, as conventional plate-screw constructs often fail due to insufficient fixation strength.1,2) Lv et al.3) identified inadequate screw fixation in the pelvic brim plate when securing comminuted quadrilateral plates, leading to redisplacement of the quadrilateral fragment and subsequent central hip dislocation—a key contributor to postoperative reduction loss. In geriatric populations, osteoporosis further compromises screw fixation, increasing the risk of fracture displacement even when the quadrilateral fragment is relatively intact.4) With the aging population, the incidence of acetabular fractures in the elderly is steadily rising, and both-column fractures account for a significant proportion (19.3%).5,6) Therefore, there is an urgent need to enhance the fixation strength of both-column fractures to address these challenges.

In 2011, Culemann et al.7) first proposed the concept of inserting a screw below the acetabulum to address the separation of the anterior and posterior columns, coining the term “infra-acetabular screw” (IAS). The IAS synergizes with ilioinguinal plating and column screws to form a biomechanically reinforced framework, increasing plate fixation strength by up to 50%. Biomechanical studies by Gras et al.8) and Marintschev et al.9) further validated the IAS’s role in enhancing stability, offering a promising solution for managing osteoporotic both-column fractures in elderly patients with osteoporosis.

The osseous corridor of the IAS is adjacent to the acetabular fossa and in close proximity to critical neurovascular structures. Although previous studies have analyzed the influence of local anatomical features on the IAS corridor,10,11,12) several issues remain unresolved: (1) existing research often lacks sufficient sample sizes and neglects age-related anatomical changes. Although Gras et al.13) and Zhao et al.12) have identified sex-based differences in IAS corridor dimensions (diameter, length, entry point), the impact of aging on these parameters remains unexplored despite evidence of pelvic morphological shifts with age.14,15) (2) Currently standard fluoroscopy is recommended— including the inlet view (c-arm tilted 30° cranially), obturator-outlet view (c-arm rotated to the injured side and tilted 30° caudally), and iliac-outlet view (c-arm rotated 15° to the healthy side and tilted 30° caudally). These methods are suggested to require high sensitivity and specificity,16) but their clinical efficacy has not been rigorously validated, nor has the potential for more effective imaging techniques been explored.

To address these limitations, this study utilized Mimics software to reconstruct 3-dimensional (3D) models from computed tomography (CT) data of unilateral acetabular fractures. Anatomical measurements related to the IAS fixation corridor were conducted on the unaffected acetabulum, and patients were stratified by age and sex for comparative analysis. Additionally, virtual surgery was simulated to determine the optimal IAS placement based on anatomical findings. Subsequently, simulated fluoroscopic images were generated to evaluate the false positive and false negative rates for detecting cortical breach, with the aim of identifying the most effective intraoperative imaging method.

METHODS

The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the First Affiliated Hospital with Nanjing Medical University (IRB No. 2025-SR-524), and written consent was obtained for anonymized data usage.

We conducted a retrospective analysis of pelvic CT scans from 178 Chinese patients diagnosed with unilateral acetabular fractures between January 2016 and June 2024. Only contralateral (uninjured) pelvic CT data were evaluated. Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) unilateral acetabular fracture with an intact contralateral pelvis, (2) complete high-resolution pelvic CT datasets (slice thickness ≤ 2 mm), and (3) age > 18 years. Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) preexisting hip deformity or developmental dysplasia, (2) prior acetabular trauma, and (3) incomplete imaging data. Participants were stratified into 4 groups by sex and age (cutoff, 60 years): young males (n = 57), elderly males (n = 48), young females (n = 36), and elderly females (n = 37).

Pelvic CT data were imported into Mimics 21.0 software (Materialise) in digital imaging and communications in medicine (DICOM) format for 3D model reconstruction. The pelvic model’s transparency was reduced, and the axial view was adjusted parallel to the posterior obturator foramen with a 30° cranial tilt. The pelvis was finely adjusted to maximize the translucent area, identifying the “teardrop region” (Fig. 1). The high-CT-value edges surrounding this region delineated the boundaries of the IAS osseous corridor. A virtual cylindrical rod (representing the IAS) was inserted perpendicular to the teardrop’s cross-sectional plane. The maximum screw diameter was defined as the largest dimension without cortical breach. Screw length was measured from the entry point to the distal corridor boundary. Key anatomical distances were quantified: (1) entry point-to-iliopectineal eminence center and (2) entry point-to-arcuate line (Fig. 2).

The pelvic model was rotated 90° contralaterally to expose the obturator groove (Fig. 3). Reference points were defined as follows: point C, the intersection of the obturator notch and obturator groove; point D, the medial bony prominence of the quadrilateral surface at the obturator notch. A line connecting points C and D was established, and the deepest groove point (E) was identified. Obturator groove depth (OGD) was measured as the perpendicular distance from point E to line CD.

Intraoperative fluoroscopy was simulated using the “fluoroscopy” radiation module. CT validation of the virtual screw position (maximum safe diameter) within the osseous corridor served as the gold standard for screw safety.17) The pelvis was rotated to simulate standard intraoperative fluoroscopic views: (1) inlet view (30° cranial tilt); (2) obturator-outlet view (30° lateral rotation toward the injured side and a 30° caudal tilt); and (3) iliac-outlet view (15° lateral rotation toward the healthy side and a 30° caudal tilt). Perforation of the cortical bone in any fluoroscopic view can be considered a false positive. Through adjustment of pelvic rotational parameters, the modified protocol was tested, specifically adjusting the iliac-outlet view to 5° lateral rotation toward the healthy side and a 30° caudal tilt. Therefore, the modified intraoperative fluoroscopic views are as follows: (1) inlet view (30° cranial tilt); (2) obturator-outlet view (30° lateral rotation toward the injured side and a 30° caudal tilt); and (3) Iliac-outlet view (5° lateral rotation toward the healthy side and a 30° caudal tilt). After increasing the virtual screw diameter in CT to confirm cortical penetration, we observed which of the 2 fluoroscopic protocols could detect the screw breach (Fig. 4).

All data were analyzed via IBM SPSS version 23.0 statistical software (IBM Corp.). The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to assess the normal distribution of the data. One-way analysis of variance was utilized for normally distributed data, whereas non-parametric tests were applied for non-normally distributed data. Independent samples t-tests were conducted to compare OGD between males and females. Additionally, simple linear regression analysis was performed to evaluate the relationship between the OGD and the infra-acetabular corridor diameter (IACD). A statistical significance threshold was established at p < 0.05.

RESULTS

Anatomic parameters across age and sex groups and key pelvic measurements for the 4 cohorts—young males (n = 57), elderly males (n = 48), young females (n = 36), and elderly females (n = 37)—are summarized in Tables 1 and 2. Young males exhibited an IACD of 5.38 ± 0.88 mm and length of 101.12 ± 4.44 mm, showing no significant difference from elderly males (5.14 ± 0.95 mm and 101.02 ± 3.83 mm, respectively) (p > 0.05). However, the entry-to-eminentia iliopectinea distance was notably greater in young males (13.04 ± 1.86 vs. 12.32 ± 1.20 mm, p < 0.05). Elderly females demonstrated a wider IACD than young females (4.98 ± 0.95 mm vs. 4.51 ± 0.58 mm, p = 0.022), with comparable entry point parameters (p > 0.05). Between sexes, elderly males and females showed equivalent corridor diameters (5.14 ± 0.95 vs. 4.98 ± 0.95 mm, p > 0.05), but differed significantly in corridor length (101.02 ± 3.83 mm vs. 90.95 ± 2.76 mm, p < 0.05), entry-to-arcuate line distance (7.98 ± 2.97 mm vs. 5.06 ± 1.86 mm, p < 0.05), and eminentia iliopectinea proximity (12.32 ± 1.20 mm vs. 13.29 ± 1.17 mm, p < 0.05). Males exhibited shallower OGD than females (9.66 ± 1.98 mm vs. 11.16 ± 2.10 mm, p < 0.001). A robust negative correlation linked IACD to OGD: r = 0.842 (p < 0.05), modeled as IACD = 8.730–0.358 OGD (Fig. 5).

DISCUSSION

This study reveals that postmenopausal osteoporosis in females significantly impacts the IACD, with bone loss leading to a larger IACD in elderly females compared to younger females. In males, age influences the optimal screw entry point, necessitating minor adjustments relative to the center of the eminentia iliopectinea. Additionally, a notable negative correlation exists between the IACD and OGD, suggesting that surgeons can estimate the screw diameter by observing the OGD on the contralateral side. Furthermore, this study highlights that the standard fluoroscopic method has a high false positive rate for detecting cortical breaches, while a modified iliac-outlet view (5° lateral rotation towards the healthy side and 30° caudal tilt) demonstrates superior clinical efficacy, providing valuable guidance for the precise placement and verification of IAS in acetabular fractures.

Gras et al.,13) based on measurements from 523 normal pelvic CT scans, reported average IACD of 7.7 mm and 6.9 mm, and lengths of 106.4 mm and 96.2 mm for males and females, respectively. In contrast, Zhao et al.12) found that the average IACD in the Chinese population was significantly narrower, measuring 5.54 mm and 4.49 mm, with lengths of 100.23 mm and 87.74 mm for males and females, respectively. These discrepancies may stem from racial variations, as the Chinese population exhibits shorter and narrower IACDs. Our study found that in young and elderly males, the average IACDs were 5.38 mm and 5.14 mm, with lengths of 101.12 mm and 101.02 mm, respectively. For young and elderly females, the average lengths were 90.67 mm and 90.95 mm, showing no statistically significant differences (p > 0.05). However, the average IACD in elderly females (4.98 mm) was significantly larger than in young females (4.51 mm). While previous studies have demonstrated a significant correlation between pelvic anatomical parameters and age, they have neglected to consider sex as a critical confounding factor. Our findings reveal that the IACD in elderly females is significantly larger compared to young females, a phenomenon not observed between elderly and young males. This suggests that post-menopausal osteoporosis in females further reduces bone mass around the corridor, leading to a compensatory increase in its IACD. Clinically, this suggests that larger IAS diameters may be necessary in elderly females to enhance screw fixation.

During IAS placement via the medial window of the ilioinguinal approach, the eminentia iliopectinea serves as a critical anatomical landmark for determining the entry point. Baumann et al.18) suggested an entry point located 10.2 mm anterior and 10.4 mm medial to the iliopectineal eminence, offering high stability. Zhao et al.12) reported average distances from the entry point to the arcuate line and iliopectineal eminence as 7.16 mm and 13.9 mm in males and 4.82 mm and 15 mm in females, respectively. Our measurements align closely with findings of Zhao et al.12) However, we observed that the optimal entry point in elderly males is slightly more lateral than in young males, reflecting age-related anatomical changes. This phenomenon was not observed in females, likely due to the less pronounced eminentia iliopectinea in females, complicating precise measurements.

In identifying the IACD, we found that the diameter of the teardrop region is most influenced by the OGD19) (Fig. 3), as the medial edge of the corridor is adjacent to the depth of the obturator groove, representing the narrowest part of the screw pathway. Adjusting the entry angle can easily breach the OGD, with limited tolerance, risking injury to the obturator neurovascular structures. Therefore, we measured and compared OGD across groups, using the palpable bony prominence on the medial edge of the quadrilateral surface as a reference. Our findings confirmed that male OGD is significantly smaller than female OGD, and a negative correlation exists between IACD and OGD. This validates our hypothesis that OGD is a critical factor influencing IAS diameter. Preoperative assessment of OGD can thus provide a personalized estimate of the ideal screw diameter, aiding in screw placement.

No prior studies have explored the optimal fluoroscopic method for intraoperative verification of IAS placement. While some authors have proposed standard fluoroscopic views, their sensitivity and specificity remain unvalidated. Lim et al.20) in a follow-up of 39 patients with IAS, found that the outlet view (perpendicular to the IAS) effectively avoided joint penetration. Ma et al.,11) using Mimics to simulate fluoroscopy by adjusting pelvic transparency and rotation, suggested that the pelvic inlet view (15° and 25°) could visualize the maximum IAS diameter. However, these studies focused on a single fluoroscopic view and lacked comprehensive exploration of sensitivity and specificity. Intraoperative verification of cortical breaches should involve multiple fluoroscopic images, with any breach in 1 view indicating suboptimal screw placement. To address this, we employed Mimics to simulate fluoroscopy and validate the efficacy of standard views. Our results revealed a false positive rate of 76.4% in standard fluoroscopy, identifying 136 cases of presumed cortical breaches. To mitigate this issue, we modified the iliac-outlet view by rotating 5° laterally towards the healthy side and 30° caudal tilt, reducing the false positive rate to 44.38% with only 79 cases of presumed breaches. This modified approach significantly improved specificity. When the screw diameter was increased by 0.5 mm, only 21 cases (11.8%) showed cortical breaches in axial CT views, with false positive rates of 64.33% and 40.76% for standard and modified fluoroscopy, respectively, and sensitivities of 33.33% and 47.62%. When the screw diameter was increased by 1 mm, the breach rate rose to 91.01%, with false positive rates of 68.75% and 50% for standard and modified fluoroscopy, respectively, and sensitivities of 77.16% and 88.27%. These findings indicate that the modified iliac-outlet view offers higher sensitivity and specificity, outperforming the standard view in verifying screw placement.

This study is subject to several principal limitations. First, anatomic parameters and screw trajectories were derived solely from CT-based 3D reconstructions, omitting intraoperative variables (soft tissue impedance, exposure limits, or fracture displacement). Clinical validation is required to confirm these findings. Second, the study did not assess the impact of osteoporosis-related indicators on pelvic anatomical morphology, nor did it consider the direct relationship between bone loss and pelvic anatomical characteristics. Third, as a single-center retrospective study, the results may not be generalizable to the broader population, and discrepancies may exist across different regions and ethnic groups. To address these limitations, we plan to conduct multicenter collaborative studies with expanded sample sizes in future research.

In summary, elderly females exhibit larger IACD than young females, and the optimal entry point in elderly males is slightly more lateral than in young males, highlighting the influence of age on IACD parameters. Additionally, females have deeper OGD than males, and the negative correlation between OGD and IACD underscores sex-specific differences in IACD dimensions. Preoperative personalized measurement of IACD parameters using digital software can aid in determining ideal screw size and placement. Furthermore, the modified iliac-outlet view (5° lateral rotation towards the healthy side and 30° caudal tilt) demonstrates superior sensitivity and specificity, enabling more precise and efficient screw placement, reducing intraoperative fluoroscopy time and confirming the safety and reliability of IAS insertion.
