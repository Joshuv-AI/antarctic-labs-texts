Bridging perspectives: experiences and future directions for patient involvement in clinical registries – a survey-based study
Authors: Lars van de Sanden, Jacqueline ter Stege, Jitske Tiemensma, Dominique Sprengers, Liesbeth M Timmermans, Misha D P Luyer, Mira S Staphorst
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn the Netherlands, patient participation is mandatory in clinical registries. However, the practical implementation of this requirement remains unclear, and collaboration between patient representatives, chairs of Clinical Audit Boards (CABs) and registry coordinators has not been examined from a multistakeholder perspective.ObjectiveTo explore experiences, perceptions and needs regarding collaboration between patient representatives, CAB chairs and registry coordinators and to identify strategies to strengthen patient participation in clinical registries.MethodsAn online survey across 26 clinical registries was distributed in 2025 to three stakeholder groups: patient representatives, chairs of CABs and registry coordinators from the Dutch Institute for Clinical Auditing. The questionnaire assessed frequency and quality of collaboration, perceived value of patient input, challenges and improvement needs. Responses were analysed using descriptive statistics and thematic analysis.Results48 respondents participated. Overall, attitudes towards patient participation were positive. CAB chairs expressed the most favourable views on collaboration, whereas registry coordinators reported more mixed experiences, reflecting their intermediary role between governance and daily operational practice. Key challenges included limited structural clarity and role definition, methodological complexity of indicator development and data interpretation, questions of representativeness and differences between patient-identified priorities and registry requirements. Across all groups, there was a strong call for improved onboarding, training and more structured and consistent engagement.ConclusionsAlthough patient participation in clinical registries is formally established, experiences with its implementation vary widely across stakeholder groups. Strengthening structural support, mutual understanding and feedback mechanisms is essential to move from symbolic to substantive participation. Developing and evaluating a structured framework for patient engagement in clinical registries may enhance the contribution of these registries to learning, quality improvement and shared decision-making in clinical practice.

Introduction

Patient-centred care has become a guiding principle in modern health systems, emphasising that healthcare should align with patients’ values, preferences and experiences. Within this paradigm, shared decision-making (SDM) integrates patient values into individual clinical decisions,1 while patient and public involvement (PPI) extends patient participation to the organisational and system level, including healthcare policy, research and quality improvement. Meaningful PPI can enhance the relevance of quality indicators and ensure that evaluations reflect what matters most to patients.24

Reflecting this broader shift toward patient-centred health systems, many countries have begun embedding patient participation within governance structures, including clinical guideline bodies and quality measurement programmes.5

In the Netherlands, patient participation in national clinical registries has been required by Dutch legislation since 2025.6 Specifically, Dutch quality governance regulations mandate that patient perspectives are structurally embedded in the governance of a registry, positioning patient participation not merely as a normative ideal but as a statutory requirement.6 The Dutch Institute for Clinical Auditing (DICA) currently manages 26 clinical registries and is the largest holder of clinical quality registries in the Netherlands, as well as one of the largest worldwide. DICA operationalises the mandate by integrating patient representatives into registry governance structures, typically in the Clinical Audit Boards (CABs): disease-specific multidisciplinary governance committees composed of healthcare professionals that oversee the quality of patient care within a specific clinical registry, for instance through indicator development, data interpretation, and quality improvement initiatives. These registries increasingly support SDM by providing clinicians and patients with accessible, patient-relevant outcome information, such as patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs)79, as well as public reports and ‘patients-like-me’ dashboards presenting personalised outcome information. As these registries mature and expand their influence within clinical practice and organisational performance, understanding how patient perspectives can be meaningfully integrated into these processes becomes increasingly important. However, the existence of a formal mandate does not guarantee meaningful patient participation in practice. A recent systematic review of collective patient participation identified persistent barriers and facilitators across more than two decades of research, concluding that limited progress has been made in structurally embedding meaningful patient participation at the organisational and policy level.10 Other research shows that participation often risks becoming tokenistic when roles are poorly defined, preparation is insufficient or communication lacks reciprocity.1113 The complex, data-driven nature of clinical registries may exacerbate these challenges, particularly when methodological expertise is required to contribute substantively to discussions about indicator development, validity or data interpretation. Systematic insight into how participation is currently experienced across stakeholder of registries and enabling conditions remains limited.

This study therefore aims to explore current patterns of patient involvement, the experiences, perspectives and needs of patient representatives, chairs of CABs and registry coordinators involved in Dutch clinical registries. By examining similarities and differences across these stakeholder groups, we seek to identify practical lessons for strengthening collaboration and to contribute to the development of more robust templates for meaningful patient participation within clinical registries.

Methods

The Consensus-Based Checklist for Reporting of Survey Studies for this study is provided in online supplemental table 1.14

Study design

We conducted a cross-sectional survey study with a quantitative design, including open-ended questions to provide additional context. The study took place in clinical registries coordinated by DICA (online supplemental table 2). Each registry is governed by a CAB, comprising healthcare professionals, a registry coordinator and in most registries, one or two patient representatives. A scientific committee further evaluates scientific feasibility of research proposals and requests for access to registry data.

Patient involvement

Patient representatives were involved in this study through their structural roles within the governance of Dutch clinical registries. Their experiences and priorities informed the focus on meaningful patient participation. A patient representative reviewed the questionnaire for relevance and clarity, supported recruitment by introducing the survey during a patient representative meeting, and contributed to the analysis and interpretation of the findings. Two patient representatives also coauthored the manuscript. After analysis, results were discussed with the broader group of patient representatives, who reflected on the findings and identified priorities for future research and dissemination.

Participants

Eligible participants were: (1) patient representatives formally appointed to registry governance structures, contributing patient perspectives to registry governance, (2) chairs of CABs, responsible for leading the clinical audit board and the governance of the registry and (3) registry coordinators, who manage the daily operation of the registry and support collaboration between stakeholders. A convenience sampling strategy was used. All 88 individuals active in these roles in mid-2025 were invited to participate.

Questionnaire development

The online questionnaire was developed iteratively, informed by previous research on PPI and reviewed for clarity, feasibility and relevance by three stakeholder representatives (a patient representative, a chair of a CAB and a registry coordinator).

The questionnaire consisted of 15 items, including closed-ended questions (5-point Likert scale) on frequency of involvement, satisfaction with collaboration and perceived impact, as well as open-ended questions on experiences, challenges and suggestions for improvement.

Given the exploratory nature of this study and its focus on capturing perspectives across roles and registries rather than within-group comparisons, and in order to minimise identifiability in a relatively small and specialised study population, no demographic characteristics were collected. The full questionnaire in Dutch and English is provided in online supplemental tables 3 and 4.

Data collection

The online questionnaire was distributed between June and July 2025 using a secure web-based survey platform. Participation was voluntary and anonymous. Respondents could skip questions and withdraw before submission. Participants received an email invitation and up to two reminders. To enhance response rates, we actively promoted the survey during a meeting with all patient representatives, a meeting with the CAB chairs and a registry coordinator meeting. To minimise the risk of multiple participation, respondents were asked to indicate the clinical registry they were affiliated with; responses were stored without personal identifiers on a secure server with restricted access, ensuring confidentiality and protection against unauthorised access.

Analysis

Quantitative data were analysed descriptively. Missing responses were not imputed and percentages were calculated based on available data. Qualitative data were analysed thematically following Braun and Clarke’s six-phase approach.15 Two researchers (LvdS, MSS) independently coded all responses, reconciled discrepancies and iteratively refined a shared coding framework. Themes were developed inductively and compared across stakeholder groups. To enhance rigour, emerging findings were discussed with an internal advisory group consisting of a patient representative, a registry coordinator and CAB.

Ethics

According to Dutch regulations, survey-based research that does not involve patient data, clinical interventions or identifiable information does not fall under the Medical Research Involving Human Subjects Act (WMO).16 Ethical review was therefore not required. All participants received written information about the purpose of the study, data handling procedures and confidentiality. Survey submission implied informed consent.

Results

Participants

A total of 48 respondents completed the survey. This corresponds to response rates of 35.7% for patient representatives (n=10 out of 28 representatives, representing 11 different registries), 53.3% for chairs of CABs (n=16 out of 30, representing 16 different registries) and 73.3% for registry coordinators (n=22 out of 30, representing 22 different registries). Participants represented all 26 registries included in the study. It should be noted that some registries had multiple CAB chairs, registry coordinators, or patient representatives invited to participate, which explains why the number of invited individuals exceeds the number of registries represented. Also, one of the participating patient representatives is involved in two registries.

Most respondents reported that patients participated in registry meetings at least three times per year, while contact outside registry meetings varied considerably between groups. Reported experience of patient representatives in clinical registries ranged from <1 year (30%), 1–2 years (50%) to >2 years (20%).

Involvement of patient representatives in registry topics

As shown in table 2, patient representatives are most frequently involved in developing quality indicators and discussing registry outcomes, with 60–75% of chairs of CABs and registry coordinators reporting such involvement. These were also the areas in which patient representatives most wished to maintain or expand their role. Involvement of patient representatives in research-related activities, such as reviewing research proposals, was limited across all groups (typically 10–20%), although patient representatives expressed substantially greater interest in contributing to these tasks, indicating a clear gap between current and preferred participation. PROM development and the development of SDM tools were areas of strong alignment between stakeholder groups. For these topics, all stakeholder groups reported a higher preferred involvement of patient representatives (up to 100%) than current involvement (up to 50%). Views diverged on expanding the involvement of patient representatives in developing registry content: registry coordinators would like to see less involvement of patient representatives while the percentages of patient representatives and chairs of CABs for current and preferred involvement showed no difference.

Perceived value of collaboration between stakeholder groups

Collaboration was generally rated positively across groups, though to varying degrees (table 3). Chairs of CABs expressed the highest satisfaction with collaboration, patient representatives were moderately satisfied and registry coordinators reported mixed levels of satisfaction (86%, 60% and 56% reported being satisfied or very satisfied, respectively). A majority of respondents (70–80%) agreed that patient involvement adds value, but registry coordinators were less consistent with the contribution of patient representatives to registry processes (35% were satisfied or very satisfied). Approximately 60% of chairs of CABs and 50% registry coordinators felt patient representatives were sufficiently knowledgeable about the technical and methodological aspects of clinical registries.

Complexity, knowledge and learning needs

All three groups identified the technical and methodological complexity of clinical registries as a major barrier to collaboration with patient representatives. Registry coordinators particularly emphasised the specialised knowledge required for indicator development and data interpretation, noting that this makes (equal) participation challenging. Chairs of CABs and patient representatives also recognised the difficulty of translating registry outputs into patient-relevant information. Patient representatives described a steep learning curve, and reported gaining confidence over time. Across groups, there was a call for accessible explanatory materials, introductory training and visual outlines of registry processes.

Structural and organisational conditions

Structural and organisational conditions, particularly unclear role definitions, inconsistent meeting structures and limited availability of clinicians and patient representatives, strongly shaped the quality of collaboration. Registry coordinators most frequently noted unclear expectations regarding the scope of patient involvement and the responsibilities of patient representatives, and expressing a need for explicit guidance and consistent frameworks as the focus of registries increasingly shifts toward patient relevance. Chairs of CABs highlighted challenges in recruiting and retaining representatives, particularly when no patient organisation exists, as well as issues with availability and attendance. Patient representatives similarly noted organisational inconsistency, such as the absence of meeting agendas or unclear invitations, and emphasised the need for regular check-ins to support effective participation. Overall, clear structure and organisation were seen as essential prerequisites for effective participation.

Relational dynamics and equality

Relationships between stakeholders generally appeared constructive, with patient representatives describing open dialogue and supportive interactions. Chairs of CABs valued the diversity of perspectives brought into discussions. However, registry coordinators reported difficulties achieving equal dialogue, citing instances where technical discussions excluded patient representatives or where contributions were perceived as less nuanced. Several respondents noted that meaningful participation did not necessarily require patient representatives to engage at the same technical level, but rather to have sufficient basic understanding to contribute their experiential perspective. Patient representatives described mostly positive interactions, emphasising open communication, relationship-building and feeling increasingly valued over time. These dynamics indicate that, despite positive intentions, relational equality is not yet fully realised.

Representativeness and legitimacy

Stakeholders differed in how they viewed the legitimacy of patient input. Registry coordinators most often questioned whether patient representatives’ contributions reflected broader patient populations or were based primarily on personal experience. Chairs of CABs raised similar concerns, especially in registries without patient organisations. In contrast, patient representatives emphasised strong ties to their communities through patient organisations, surveys and patient days. These contrasting views highlight differing expectations of what constitutes adequate patient representation.

Perceived value, impact and differences in perspectives

Perceptions on the value of patient participation varied between stakeholder groups. Chairs of CABs consistently viewed patient perspectives as essential to governance and decision-making. Registry coordinators expressed the widest range of views, ranging from clear added value in areas such as developing and implementing PROMs and patient factsheets to concerns that participation sometimes functioned as a procedural requirement with limited impact. Patient representatives generally felt increasingly valued while also highlighting differences in perspective when patient priorities did not fully align with the methodological and technical priorities of the clinical registries. Several respondents noted that these differences were not limited to the level of influence or decision-making authority, but also concerned the substantive focus of registries. Wheras patient representatives emphasised lived experience and immediate relevance, registry coordinators prioritised methodological rigour, feasibility and long-term system improvement.

Overall, stakeholders recognised the importance of patient participation but faced persistent challenges related to complexity, structural clarity, relational equality and differing views on representativeness and value. Meaningful participation appeared most successful when roles and processes were clearly defined, expectations were aligned and relational dynamics were actively supported.

Discussion

Overall, our findings indicate that collaboration between patient representatives and other key stakeholders in clinical registries is perceived positively, although perceptions varied across groups. Chairs of CABs were most positive about the collaboration, emphasising that patient perspectives add meaningful value to registry governance. Registry coordinators expressed more mixed views. Positioned between chairs of CABs and patient representatives, they observe both the strengths and day-to-day operational challenges of integrating patient perspectives into complex, data-driven registry processes. This intermediary position may explain their more mixed perceptions, but also places them in a unique role to bridge perspectives and facilitate more effective collaboration. Patient representatives were generally positive about collaboration, emphasising open communication and feeling increasingly valued as they grew into the role despite the complexity of registry work. At the same time, they highlight organisational inconsistencies, such as unclear meeting agendas or expectations, and noted occasional differences in perspectives when the chairs of CABs and registry coordinators have other priorities than the issues they wished to raise. Across groups, respondents highlighted persistent structural and relational barriers that complicate meaningful participation, underscoring the gap between the formal mandate for participation and its practical enactment.

An important finding of this study is that the implementation of patient participation differed considerably across registries and stakeholder groups. Stakeholders not only vary in how patient representatives are involved, but also in what they expect patient participation to achieve, ranging from improving methodological relevance and governance legitimacy to enhancing the usefulness of registry outputs for patients and SDM in clinical practice. These differing expectations help to explain the observed variation in experiences and underscore the need for clearer, shared frameworks.

Five different themes emerged from the open-ended questions on the survey. Patient representatives tended to emphasise relevance and lived experience, while registry coordinators prioritised methodological rigour, feasibility and long-term comparability of data. When these perspectives are not explicitly aligned, participation risks being perceived as symbolic rather than substantive.

Importantly, our findings suggest that relational equality should not be interpreted as requiring patient representatives to attain the same level of technical or methodological expertise as clinicians or registry coordinators. Rather, equality appears to hinge on mutual recognition of different forms of expertise. While a basic understanding of registry structures and indicators is necessary for meaningful participation, the distinctive value of patient representatives lies in their experiential knowledge of living with disease and undergoing treatment. Clarifying this distinction may help align differing expectations and support more effective collaboration.

A related issue is whether patient representatives reflect the broader patient population. While some stakeholders question this, full representativeness may neither be feasible nor necessary for meaningful participation. Individual patient perspectives can provide valuable insights that would otherwise be absent. At the same time, meaningful representation may be strengthened when patient representatives are connected to patient organisations, enabling them to bring in perspectives from a broader constituency alongside their own lived experience. This requires not only having personal experience, but also the ability to reflect on and articulate collective patient perspectives, for example, through active and structured consultation with the patient community (e.g. through surveys or organised feedback mechanisms). In addition, involving more than one patient representative per registry may further enhance diversity of input and support continuity. Rather than aiming for ‘perfect’ representativeness, it may be more important to acknowledge and align differing expectations about what constitutes legitimate patient input. Together, these results illustrate a system in transition: patient participation is valued and increasingly expected, yet the practical structures, tools and shared understandings that support meaningful collaboration are still developing. When such conditions are present, patient involvement is highly valuable and contributes to more responsive, patient-relevant clinical registries.

Comparison with existing literature

Our findings align with international evidence showing that patient involvement can drift toward tokenism if roles, responsibilities and influence are not clearly articulated.11 17 The knowledge asymmetry between professionals and patient representatives and relational inequality, also observed in the current study, is widely documented in the PPI literature.4 Structured onboarding, continuous learning opportunities and shared interpretation of data can help mitigate these challenges and strengthen confidence and legitimacy. The cultural and methodological differences identified in this study parallel observations from evaluations of PPI within research and quality improvement. Patient priorities and data-driven priorities for clinical registries are rooted in different epistemologies, and bridging these is necessary to prevent marginalisation of patient perspectives.4 13 Our findings are also consistent with research showing that patients involved in clinical registries demonstrate a high level of commitment and willingness to contribute over time.18

Implications for practice

The findings of this study point to several important implications for practice. First, our findings highlight the need for a framework that defines minimum standards for patient participation within clinical registries. Such a framework should be developed collaboratively by key stakeholders (ie, patient representatives, registry coordinators, chairs of CABs and registry organisations) commissioned by and aligned with the (Dutch) quality governance regulations that mandate patient participation in clinical registries. The Dutch Patient Federation has previously developed a white paper on patient participation in quality registries,19 which provides valuable guidance and principles for involvement. Building on this foundation, a renewed framework could further operationalise patient participation within the specific context of clinical registries by explicitly incorporating perspectives from registry governance and clinical audit boards. This framework should clarify procedural expectations, define the required competencies of patient representatives to support meaningful participation and specify their roles and added value within registry governance. It should also delineate when and how patient representatives are involved in registry processes and establish clear expectations for how their input is considered and communicated. Explicitly aligning such a framework with the regulatory mandate would help clarify what ‘meaningful participation’ should entail in practice, thereby reducing ambiguity among all stakeholder groups. Establishing shared standards would help bring consistency across registries and reduce reliance on individual initiative.

Second, the results underscore the importance of capacity-building for all stakeholders. Training modules for patient representatives, chairs of CABs and registry coordinators, covering topics such as indicator development, registry governance, PROMs and effective collaboration, but also group dynamics, facilitation of inclusive dialogue and the substantive value of patient-identified priorities, could foster mutual understanding, reduce knowledge asymmetries and enhance equality. Providing accessible onboarding tools, particularly visual and narrative explanations of registry workflows, may enhance the ability of patient representatives to contribute meaningfully.

Third, registries would benefit from more transparent feedback mechanisms that demonstrate how patient input influences indicator development, reporting decisions or quality improvement activities. Such mechanisms not only increase accountability but also strengthen the perceived value of participation among both patient representatives, CABs and registry coordinators.

Finally, this study highlights the potential of cross-registry learning structures, such as annual meetings or communities of practice. These platforms could support sharing of successful strategies, allow peer learning among patient representatives and encourage innovation in participatory governance. Over time, such initiatives may contribute to a stronger culture of co-production within the registry landscape.

Strengths and limitations

A key strength of this study is that it provides one of the first comparative examinations of patient participation across multiple clinical registries, bringing together the perspectives of patient representatives, chairs of CABs and registry coordinators. By including stakeholders from a wide range of clinical registries and combining quantitative and qualitative data, the study offers both breadth and depth in understanding current collaborative practices. The study design enabled us to capture not only how frequently and in which contexts patient representatives are involved, but also the underlying motivations, challenges and relational dynamics that shape collaboration in registry governance. This approach yields a nuanced and practice-relevant picture of patient participation within complex, data-driven infrastructures.

Several limitations should also be considered when interpreting the findings. The response rate was modest, particularly among patient representatives, which may limit the representativeness of our findings. As participation was voluntary, this may have introduced self-selection bias. Respondents who chose to participate may have been more engaged with, or more critical of, patient participation than non-respondents. However, we expect the impact of this bias to be limited, as responses were obtained from all stakeholder groups across all 26 registries and reflected a broad range of views, including both positive and critical perspectives. Moreover, similar themes emerged consistently across groups, suggesting that the findings capture shared experiences rather than the views of a single subgroup.

This study was conducted within the organisational and regulatory context of DICA and the Dutch healthcare system, where patient participation in clinical registries is legally mandated. However, DICA represents the largest and most diverse clinical registry organisation in the Netherlands and one of the largest internationally, which supports the relevance of our findings for other large-scale registry systems.

Also, the questionnaire-based design captures reported experiences and perceptions but does not fully elucidate the underlying values, motivations and reasoning that shape views on patient participation. Future research could address this limitation by applying qualitative designs, such as in-depth interviews or focus groups, to explore these underlying perspectives in greater depth.

While this context may limit direct transferability to settings with different governance arrangements, many of the themes identified closely align with international literature on patient and public involvement. These convergences suggest that the findings may be relevant to other registry-based learning systems seeking to move patient participation from formal requirement toward meaningful partnership.

Future research

Future research should build on these findings by adopting longitudinal or comparative study designs. Such approaches would allow examination of how patient participation develops over time as representatives gain experience and as registries further professionalise their participatory structures. In addition, dedicated research is needed to develop and refine structured frameworks for patient participation, for example, through systematic reviews of existing participatory models and qualitative studies such as focus groups with patients and registry stakeholders. Such a framework could subsequently be evaluated longitudinally to assess their influence on collaboration, perceived impact and substantive outcomes.

Conclusion

Meaningful patient participation in clinical registries requires more than fulfilling a mandate: in the Netherlands, this mandate is rooted in legislation,6 but fulfilling it alone is insufficient; meaningful participation also requires structure, shared understanding, support and reciprocal feedback. Our findings illustrate that the legal and organisational expectation for patient participation does not automatically translate into meaningful involvement in practice. Strengthening patient participation directly supports the statutory functions of clinical registries. More structured involvement can help ensure that indicators and ‘patients-like-me’ dashboards better reflect what is clinically meaningful and what is relevant for SDM in clinical practice. Likewise, well-supported patient participation can enhance learning and improvement cycles by introducing patient-derived priorities earlier in indicator development and quality improvement discussions.

This study provides actionable insights to guide the next phase of patient involvement within clinical registries. By embedding collaborative processes and support structures, registry organisations can advance from obligation to partnership, ensuring that quality measurement better reflects what matters to patients.
