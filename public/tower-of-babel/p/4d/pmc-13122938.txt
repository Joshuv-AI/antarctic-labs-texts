Comprehensive analysis of RNA sequencing reveals DNA damage response and immune-associated alternative splicing and RBP regulators contributing to preeclampsia
Authors: YanHua Wang, WenXia Li, ZhiHui Li, KePing Qiang, JiangYong Shen, LiJuan Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPreeclampsia (PE), a life-threatening hypertensive disorder of pregnancy, remains poorly characterized at the post-transcriptional regulation level. While alternative splicing (AS) perturbations drive pathological processes in numerous diseases, their systematic investigation in PE pathogenesis is lacking.ResultsThrough integrative analysis of placental RNA-seq data (n = 18; 9 early-onset severe PE vs. 9 controls) using SUVA-based splicing quantification and RBP interactome mapping, we identified 276 conserved regulated splicing events (PE-RAS) with dominant isoform usage (pSAR ≥ 50%, pvalue ≤ 0.05). These events disproportionately affected DNA damage response (DDR) pathways, particularly in DYRK2 (Δsplicing ratio = 0.33, p = 2.8e-3) and FZR1 (Δsplicing ratio = 0.23, p = 2.2e-4), whose aberrant splicing correlated with DNA repair function. Co-expression network analysis revealed 11 upregulated RNA-binding proteins (RBPs) (e.g., DUSP1, FLNB; FC > 2, FDR < 0.05) orchestrating DDR-associated splicing through sequence-specific interactions (|r| >0.6, p < 0.01). Strikingly, these RBP-AS axes coincided with immune microenvironment remodeling, manifesting as resting NK cells, resting memory CD4 + T-cell and Neutrophils cells depletion, potentially linking splicing dysregulation to maternal-fetal interface inflammation. Background

Preeclampsia (PE), a multisystemic disorder affecting about 10% of pregnancies globally [1], manifests as new-onset hypertension and proteinuria after 20 weeks of gestation [2]. This condition contributes significantly to maternal mortality and fetal complications including intrauterine growth restriction [3, 4]. Pathophysiologically, early-onset PE is triggered by poor placentation in early pregnancy, with the core mechanism being inadequate remodeling of the uteroplacental spiral arteries, leading to impaired placental perfusion and hypoxia. Late-onset PE occurs in the late stages of pregnancy, and its mechanism is related to the mechanical limitations of placental growth reaching its size limit, resulting in impaired perfusion within the placenta (interstitial space of the villi) without significant abnormalities in spiral artery remodeling. Regardless of the initial triggers and differences in the stages of disease onset, both types ultimately lead to the core pathological change of syncytiotrophoblast stress in the placenta. When the syncytiotrophoblast is in a state of chronic stress, it further induces cellular dysfunction, promoting the release of antiangiogenic factors (such as soluble fms-like tyrosine kinase 1, sFlt-1) and proinflammatory cytokines. Once these factors enter the maternal circulation, they establish systemic endothelial dysfunction. This progression transforms the disease from a “localized placental abnormality” to “maternal systemic symptoms” [5]. While oxidative stress, immune maladaptation, and angiogenic imbalance are recognized contributors, emerging evidence implicates transcriptomic dysregulation as a critical yet underexplored pathogenic layer [6–9].

Alternative splicing (AS), the process generating proteomic diversity through differential exon inclusion, regulates > 90% of human multi-exon genes [10]. Beyond expanding transcriptome complexity, AS dynamically integrates cellular stress signals by modulating mRNA stability, translation efficiency, and subcellular localization [11]. Perturbations in splicing fidelity have been mechanistically linked to a variety of diseases [12], yet their role in pregnancy-related syndromes remains nascent. Recent studies suggest RBFOX2 regulates trophoblast differentiation through alternative splicing, influencing cell fusion and invasion, with implications for placental development and PE [13]. However, genome-wide characterization of PE-associated AS events and their regulatory networks remains lacking.

RNA-binding proteins (RBPs), comprising > 2,000 human factors [14, 15], orchestrate AS decisions through sequence-specific interactions with pre-mRNA cis-elements. These molecular conductors integrate signaling pathways with spliceosome activity via post-translational modifications [15–17]. In placental development, RBPs like IGF2BP2 stabilize pro-angiogenic transcripts [18], while SORBS2 directs trophoblast motility through cytoskeletal mRNA regulation [19]. Despite these clues, systematic analyses of RBP-AS interplay in PE—particularly within DDR and immune pathways—are absent.

Employing SUVA [20]—a novel splicing quantification tool based on splice site usage variation and enables precise detection of complex splicing architectures through splice junction decomposition—we conducted the first multi-dimensional dissection of placental alternative splicing landscapes in early-onset severe PE (EOSPE). Through systematic interrogation of 18 matched RNA-seq profiles (GSE148241), we identify differentially spliced genes and potential RBP regulators in placental tissues. In doing so, we pinpoint key AS changes linked to both DDR and immune dysfunction, offering novel insights into how splicing dysregulation may intersect with the established pathophysiological processes of PE. Our findings underscore AS as an integral layer in PE (especially EOSPE) pathogenesis and open new avenues for splicing-focused interventions in this life-threatening pregnancy complication.

Methods

Data acquisition and preprocessing

Publicly available RNA sequencing dataset (GSE148241) comprising 9 EOSPE and 9 normal control placental samples were retrieved from the NCBI Sequence Read Archive (SRA; https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/sra). SRA-formatted files were converted to FASTQ format using the SRA Toolkit (v2.11.0) with default parameters. Raw sequencing reads underwent quality control processing including adapter trimming and low-quality base removal (Phred score < 20) via FASTX-Toolkit (v0.0.13). Processed reads were evaluated using FastQC (v0.11.9) to verify base quality distributions and sequence duplication levels.

After reviewing the relevant original literature and supporting documentation of GSE148241, we found that this dataset only provides overall statistical data on clinical characteristics and does not contain detailed clinical information for individual samples, such as each patient’s age, body mass index (BMI), and gestational age at delivery [21]. Nevertheless, the GSE148241 dataset has been widely used and published in numerous studies related to preeclampsia [21–23]. It features clear sample sources, standardized collection procedures, and strictly controlled sample quality; its clinical diagnostic criteria comply with authoritative guidelines in this field, and its relevance to preeclampsia has been fully verified in previous studies, thus gaining high recognition in the academic community. Therefore, in this study, 9 EOSPE samples and 9 control samples were carefully selected from the GSE148241 dataset for subsequent analysis.

Transcriptome alignment and quantification

High-quality reads were mapped to the human reference genome (GRCh38.p13) using HISAT2 (v2.2.1) [24] with default parameters. Uniquely mapped reads were retained for downstream analysis. Gene-level expression quantification was performed using HTseq-based house-script with fragments per kilobase million (FPKM) normalization.

Differential expression profiling

Raw count matrices were analyzed using DESeq2 (version 1.42.0) [25] under negative binomial distribution assumptions. Data normalization incorporated size factors to adjust for library composition bias. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were defined by absolute log2 fold change ≥ 1 and FDR < 0.05. RBPs were extracted from DEGs using a curated compendium of 2,141 experimentally validated RBPs [26–29].

Splice variant detection

AS landscapes were characterized using SUVA (v2.0.0) [20]. To minimize false positives, we made minor adjustments to the default settings. Specifically, a p-value threshold of 0.05 was employed to identify significantly different regulated splicing (RAS) events. Splicing events were classified into five canonical types (alt3p, alt5p, contain, olp, ir) by SUVA. For each AS event identified by SUVA, two primary splicing junctions are typically involved, except for ir events, which involve one junction and two boundary regions. The splicing ratio was calculated by dividing the read count of the “short” splicing junction by the total reads spanning both junctions. In ir events, the “short” junction corresponds to the intron-retaining boundaries, while the other junction represents exon-exon splicing.

SUVA computes two key metrics for each AS event: the difference in splicing ratios between conditions and the proportion of splice site usage (pSAR). The pSAR is derived by dividing the reads supporting the specific alternative splicing event by the total reads spanning the splice site, which includes all splicing junctions and boundary reads. This metric quantifies the dominance of the AS event at a given splice site. To ensure robust read support and focus on biologically significant events, only RAS events with pSAR ≥ 50% were retained for further analysis. Each SUVA-identified event can be mapped to one or more classical splicing types, including mutually exclusive 3’ UTRs (3pMXE), mutually exclusive 5’ UTRs (5pMXE), cassette exon (cassetteExon), exon skipping (ES), alternative 5’ splice site (A5SS), alternative 3’ splice site (A3SS), mutually exclusive exon (MXE), A5SS&ES, A3SS&ES, and intron retention (IR).

Immune deconvolution

CIBERSORT [30] (v1.03) was applied to FPKM-normalized data with LM22 signature matrix to estimate abundances of 22 leukocyte subtypes. Cell-type proportions between groups were compared using Student’s t-test.

Co-regulatory network construction

Three-dimensional associations between differentially expressed RBPs (DERBPs), AS events (pSAR ≥ 50%), and immune cell fractions were modeled through pairwise pearson correlation analysis (|r| ≥0.6, pvalue ≤ 0.01).

Functional annotation

Gene Ontology (GO) and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses were conducted through KOBAS (v3.0) with hypergeometric testing. Significance thresholds were set as FDR < 0.05 after Benjamini-Hochberg correction. Network visualizations were generated using Cytoscape (v3.9.1).

Placental tissue collection

The International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics (FIGO) defined early-onset preeclampsia (EOSPE) in its 2019 guidelines as “pregnancy termination before 34 weeks of gestation” [31]. In accordance with this guideline, we collected a total of 12 placentas—6 from EOSPE patients (PE) and 6 from control individuals (PC)—for the validation of gene expression and splicing events. All patients have signed the informed consent. All placental samples were collected from the General Hospital of Ningxia Medical University between 2022 and 2024. The specific clinical sample information is shown in Supplementary Table 1. The clinical characteristics of normal pregnant women and those with PE are shown in Table 1. Among the 12 samples, there were no statistically significant differences in maternal age or BMI between the two groups (P > 0.05). However, compared with the PC group, the PE group showed significant differences in gestational age, neonatal weight, systolic blood pressure, diastolic blood pressure, and urine protein qualitative results (P < 0.05). The placenta tissue samples were all obtained from placentas collected via cesarean section, with the sample collection process not involving the maternal labor contractions phase.

Quantitative real‑time polymerase chain reaction (qRT–PCR)

Total RNA was extracted using a Total RNA Extraction Kit (TaKaRa, Japan). The cDNA was reverse transcribed from total RNA by a PrimeScript™ RT reagent Kit (TaKaRa, Japan), and the reverse transcription reactions were performed on a reverse transcription PCR amplifier (EasyCycler, Germany). Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was conducted using a fluorescent quantitative PCR kit (TaKaRa, Japan) on a fluorescent quantitative PCR instrument (BIO-RAD, USA). The qPCR reactions were run under the following conditions: 30 s at 95 °C for 1 cycle, 5 s at 95 °C and 30 s at 60 °C for 40 cycles, finally, 15 s at 95 °C, 1 min at 60 °C and 15 s at 95 °C for 1 cycle. All groups underwent three technical replicates. The relative expression levels of target genes were obtained by the 2^(-ΔΔCt) method, with glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) as an internal reference. Statistical workflow

Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed on variance-stabilized counts using factoextra (v1.0.7). All analyses were implemented in R (v4.3.2) with custom Python (v3.9) scripts for pipeline integration.

Results

Genome-wide profiling reveals conserved splicing dysregulation in EOSPE

To systematically characterize AS landscapes in PE pathogenesis, we analyzed placental transcriptomes from 18 matched samples (9 EOSPE vs. 9 controls; GSE148241) using SUVA. Global splicing analysis identified 517 differentially regulated AS events (PE-RAS; p < 0.05) across five canonical types, with alternative 3’ splice sites (alt3p, 29.2%) and alternative 5’ splice sites (alt5p, 38.7%) predominating (Fig. 1A-B). Strikingly, 58.8% of PE-associated splicing events exhibited complex architectures involving the pairing of one or more splice sites inside a predefined splicing model with those outside the model (Fig. 1C), suggesting coordinated spliceosome rewiring in disease progression.

To concentrate on RAS events that primarily impact host genes, we employed pSAR (see Methods) as a screening parameter, excluding any events with a pSAR below 50%. In total, 276 events with a pSAR of 50% or greater were selected for downstream analysis (Fig. 1D). Principal component analysis (PCA) of these PE-RAS events demonstrated clear stratification of PE and control groups along the first principal component (32.2% variance, Fig. 1E), with increased dispersion among PE samples, indicative of heightened splicing heterogeneity. Unsupervised clustering of PE-RAS splicing ratios further confirmed disease-specific isoform switching patterns (Fig. 1F).

GO enrichment analysis was performed on the genes harboring RAS events with a pSAR of 50% or higher (Fig. 1G). The results show significant enrichment for core cellular processes, including cell cycle progression, actin filament organization, cell division, and DNA repair. Other notable pathways involve cytoskeleton organization, transcriptional regulation (particularly transcription by RNA polymerase II), and protein polyubiquitination. These findings suggest that PE-RAS events are predominantly linked to fundamental biological functions, potentially influencing both the structural organization of the cell and the regulation of key genomic processes.

Highly conserved regulatory alternative splicing events in DNA damage pathways of EOSPE

We next focused on the subset of RAS events enriched in DNA repair and cellular responses to DNA damage (Fig. 2A; pathways highlighted in Fig. 1G). From these, we identified three genes—DYRK2, FZR1, and HAUS7—showing significant AS alterations in PE. Specifically, read-mapping patterns for DYRK2 revealed decreased usage of its proximal 3′ splice site in PE relative to normal controls, corresponding to a significantly lower splicing ratio (Fig. 2B). Similarly, in FZR1, intron retention (IR) was reduced in PE, leading to a decreased overall splicing ratio (Fig. 2C). Meanwhile, HAUS7 demonstrated diminished selection of its proximal 3′ splice site in PE (Fig. 2D). Validated by qRT–PCR, the splicing ratios of these three RAS events in the PE group were all reduced, which is consistent with the analysis results of the RNA-seq dataset GSE148241.

Previous studies indicate that DYRK2 preserves genomic integrity in the face of DNA damage by regulating Cullin neddylation [32] and facilitating repair responses [33, 34]. Likewise, FZR1 and HAUS7 are reportedly essential for DNA damage sensing and repair in various disease contexts [35, 36]. Thus, these splicing alterations in DYRK2, FZR1, and HAUS7 could play critical roles in driving the PE phenotype, underscoring the importance of AS dysregulation in the pathogenesis of PE.

Construction of a co-regulatory network of RBPs and RAS in EOSPE

To further elucidate the regulatory functions of RBPs during the transition from normal pregnancy to PE, we first identified DEGs in the disease-development process. Among the 846 DEGs, we intersected them with 2,483 known RBP genes and identified 18 RBP genes associated with PE (Fig. 3A). After applying an expression threshold (mean expression > 1 across all samples), 12 of these DERBPs remained. A heatmap of these 12 DERBPs showed consistent upregulation in PE samples compared to controls, suggesting that RBPs are broadly activated in PE (Fig. 3B).

Next, we performed Pearson correlation analysis between the expression levels of DERBPs and the splicing ratios of PE-related alternative splicing events (PE-RAS) identified in Fig. 1. We retained only correlations with coefficients ≥ 0.6 and p ≤ 0.01, then conducted GO pathway analyses on the associated splicing events. The results revealed strong enrichment in DNA damage response pathways (Fig. 3C).

Finally, by focusing on RBPs linked to DNA damage repair, we built an RBP-RAS interaction network (Fig. 3D). Each node in the network was supported by binding evidence from the RNAinter database. Taken together, these findings suggest that the upregulation of these RBP genes in PE may influence disease progression by modulating splicing events critical for DNA damage repair.

Dynamic changes in RAS associated with immune microenvironment regulation in PE vs. normal samples

Accumulating evidence suggests that alterations in the immune microenvironment play a pivotal role in the pathogenesis of PE [37]. To investigate this further and assess how AS may influence immune cell dynamics, we performed a series of analyses comparing normal and PE samples. First, we observed significant shifts in immune cell composition between the two groups (Fig. 4A). PCA of immune cell populations showed clear segregation of PE samples from normal controls along the first principal component, although some intra-group variability remained (Fig. 4B).

In the PE group, M1 macrophages, resting mast cells, and resting dendritic cells significantly increased, whereas resting CD4 + memory T cells, resting NK cells, Neutrophils, and CD8 + T-cell populations declined (Fig. 4C). Boxplots detailing the proportions of these immune subtypes further underscored the differences between PE and normal samples (Fig. 4D). To explore potential regulatory links, we constructed networks integrating DERBPs, RAS, RAS genes (RASGs), immune cells, and GO terms (Fig. 4E). Among the genes highlighted, FZR1 emerged as a key splicing target (Fig. 2C). Previous studies have shown that loss of FZR1 increases DNA damage susceptibility and exacerbates disease progression [35], hinting at a functional role for AS in modulating the PE immune milieu. Taken together, these results underscore how specific immune cell populations and AS alterations contribute to the evolving immune microenvironment in PE. Moreover, they point to AS dysregulation as a critical component of PE pathogenesis and a potential target for future therapeutic strategies.

Validation of PE-RBPs in normal and PE samples.

To verify the involvement of RBPs in PE, we experimentally validated key RBPs identified in our analyses. In line with the bioinformatics predictions, FLNB, LIMCH1, PRDX6, NDRG1, and DUSP1 were markedly upregulated in PE samples using qRT–PCR (Fig. 5A). The study also indicated that DUSP1 can regulate the innate immune response and is associated with DNA damage [38]. LIMCH1 is involved in the regulation of cell migration and adhesion [39]. FLNB and NDRG1 are related to cell proliferation and movement [40, 41]. PRDX6 is associated with cellular oxidative stress [42]. Among them, FLNB, NDRG1, and PRDX6 have been have been proven to be related to the pathophysiology of PE. Together with the previous analysis, these results collectively suggest that AS is indeed involved in the regulatory mechanisms of PE pathogenesis.

Discussion

In this study, we conducted the first global analysis of AS events and associated genes related to PE using SUVA, and analyzed and identified the potential regulatory network of RBPs and RASGs related to PE. Our analysis indicates that the RNA-binding proteins DUSP1, FLNB, LIMCH1, NDRG1, and PRDX6 are correlated with the aberrant AS of genes such as DYRK2 and FZR1. We speculate that the aforementioned RBPs may influence DNA damage and immune-related responses in PE by modulating the AS of these genes. Definitive conclusions await further experimental validation. However, these findings can provide preliminary theoretical basis for in-depth analysis of the molecular regulatory mechanisms underlying the pathogenesis of PE and the screening of potential molecular markers for early diagnosis of PE.

Our research revealed that AS events identified during the development of PE were significantly enriched in pathways related to DNA damage and cell cycle. Preeclamptic placental trophoblast cells are known to have abnormal cell cycle mechanisms and increased apoptosis [43, 44]. There is a certain correlation between DNA damage and PE. Previous studies have indicate that increased oxidative stress and disruption of the compensatory response to placental oxidative DNA damage may affect the transcriptional regulation of placental genes involved in implantation and the determination of placental and/or cellular fate (apoptosis), thereby contributing to the pathogenesis of PE [45, 46]. Our findings extend the field by demonstrating that specific splicing alterations in key regulatory genes can directly affect these critical pathways, leading to the development of PE.

In our investigation of AS in PE, we specifically focused on AS events related to DDR and DNA repair. We observed significant changes in the AS of genes DYRK2, FZR1, and HAUS7 in PE, suggesting that alterations in their splicing may play a key role in the pathogenesis of PE. Regarding DYRK2, in addition to its involvement in the regulation of cell cycle and survival in cancer cells [47], it also participates in a variety of cellular stress responses and the maintenance of cellular homeostasis, including responding to DNA damage, regulating innate immune responses, and hypoxia signaling pathways [48, 49]. FZR1 is not only involved in the regulation of the cell cycle but is also associated with DDR [35, 50]. In mammals, the absence of FZR1 affects placental development [50]. Although HAUS7 is not a conventional DNA repair enzyme gene, it serves as a key subunit of the Augmin complex and ensures chromosome stability by regulating mitotic spindle assembly, thereby reducing DNA damage [36]. There is no direct evidence linking DYRK2, FZR1, and HAUS7 to PE, but based on our findings, the mechanism may be related to their inherent responses to cellular stress.

By intersecting the differentially expressed genes in PE samples with the reported RBP genes, we identified RBP genes associated with PE. Among them, DUSP1 was initially characterized as a gene induced by growth factors, involved in the G0/G1 phase transition and apoptosis [51]. DUSP1 is also a central regulator of innate immunity, capable of affecting inflammatory responses [52]. Additionally, it has been reported that DUSP1 may play a role in the process of DNA damage and repair by modulating the activity of JNK and BAX, safeguarding the stability of mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA), and inhibiting the activation of the cGAS-STING signaling pathway [38]. FLNB is a cytoskeletal protein that directs various cellular functions, including membrane stability, ion channel transport, adhesion, proliferation, extension, and motility [53], and studies have shown that FLNB plays a key role in endothelial cell migration and adult endothelial cell angiogenesis [40]—processes closely linked to placental vascular remodeling in pregnancy. Notably, while it has been previously reported that FLNB is downregulated at both the mRNA and protein levels in the placentas of PE [54], our analysis of EOSPE placental samples reveals significant upregulation of FLNB, which contradicts this prior finding. This discrepancy may stem from differences in study design. Our research focuses specifically on EOSPE (a subtype with severe placental ischemia-hypoxia, characterized by markedly elevated blood pressure, proteinuria, and reduced gestational age), where FLNB upregulation might represent a compensatory response to improve trophoblast invasion or endothelial repair; in contrast, the prior study did not specify PE subtype. FLNB is implicated in the pathological process of PE, where it is associated with angiogenesis/antiangiogenesis proteins, defects in insulin signaling, and immune dysregulation [55]. This also provides theoretical support for the potential functional significance of the abnormal FLNB expression observed in EOSPE in this study. LIMCH1 is characterized as an actin stress fiber-associated protein, involved in focal adhesion formation and cell migration [39]. NDRG1 is involved in various cellular processes, including differentiation, proliferation, and invasion. Studies have shown that NDRG1 is upregulated in the placentas of pregnancies complicated by both early-onset and late-onset PE, which is associated with impaired trophoblast invasion in PE [41]. The increased expression of NDRG1 inhibits angiogenesis in PE through the PI3K/AKT pathway [56]. PRDX6 is a pleiotropic oxidative stress response protein that can protect cells from reactive oxygen species (ROS)-induced damage [42]. Overproduction of PRDX6 is associated with increased antioxidant status, inhibition of apoptosis, and oncogenic effects [57]. The role of PRDX6 in antioxidant stress may be related to the pathophysiological processes of PE. According to our findings, we preliminarily speculate that these RBPs may play a role in the cell cycle and DNA damage repair in PE by regulating the AS of key genes such as DYRK2 and FZR1. Based on the results of the qRT–PCR, the expression levels of these RBP genes were significantly higher in the NC group than in the PE group. This hypothesis requires further confirmation through functional experiments. Emphasizing the interrelationship between AS and PE can provide novel insights for disease therapy.

It is known that the maternal immune system is indirectly involved in the pathophysiological changes of PE, which involves abnormal activation of innate immune cells and T cell imbalance that disrupts normal immune regulation and interacts with inflammatory responses [58–60]. Innate immune cells such as NK cells, macrophages, dendritic cells, and mast cells participate in trophoblast invasion and spiral artery remodeling [61–63]. Neutrophils are associated with inflammatory responses, angiogenesis, endothelial dysfunction, and mitochondrial dysfunction in PE [37, 64, 65]. Studies have shown that CD8 + T cells play a role in PE through the control of cytokine production and cytotoxic capabilities, which are crucial for trophoblast invasion [66]. CD4 + T cells play a central role in mediating hypertension in response to placental ischemia [67]. Increasing evidence suggests that AS plays a significant role in the formation of the immune microenvironment. In our findings, it was also observed that specific RBPs may affect the infiltration of immune cells in PE by regulating the AS of key genes. These findings provide a new perspective for further research into the immunological mechanisms of PE and the development of new biomarkers or therapeutic targets.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study highlights the key role of AS in mediating the pathogenesis of PE (especially EOSPE). The study reveals that the expression of RBPs regulating DNA damage-related AS events is significantly upregulated in EOSPE, and both these AS events and RBP genes are associated with immune cells. This study reveals the regulatory role of AS in DNA damage and the immune microenvironment, which has potential significance for the pathogenesis of PE and lays the foundation for future research aimed at developing new therapeutic strategies. Despite the insights provided, our study has several limitations. First, the number of available samples was relatively small, which may limit the generalizability of our findings. Second, although we identified multiple AS events and potential RBPs implicated in PE, their precise functions and regulatory mechanisms remain unclear. In particular, it is necessary to verify the roles of these AS events and RBPs through in vitro or in vivo functional experiments. For instance, utilizing trophoblast cells with knockdown and overexpression of candidate RBPs can be used to analyze their regulatory effects on the AS of specific genes, as well as their impacts on cellular functions and immune regulation. Mouse models with placenta-specific knockdown/overexpression of candidate RBPs could be constructed to detect PE phenotypes and changes in target AS events in pregnant mice. Additionally, the complexity of immune cell interactions and communication in PE warrants further investigation. Finally, although controls in cohort 2 were sampled at later gestations than the preeclampsia placentas, the key transcriptomic signals were reproduced in a gestational-age-matched subset of our cohort (Supplementary Figure S1-2 and Supplementary Table S2), suggesting they are unlikely to be driven solely by developmental timing. However, we cannot fully exclude an effect of gestational age; accordingly, further validation and functional experiments are needed. Addressing these limitations will help to refine our current understanding and broaden the theoretical and clinical implications of our findings, ultimately advancing research into PE and other pregnancy-related complications.
