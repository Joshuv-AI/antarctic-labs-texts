Systems mapping of bidirectional endosomal transport through the crowded cell
Authors: Marlieke L.M. Jongsma, Nina Bakker, Lenard M. Voortman, Roman I. Koning, Erik Bos, Jimmy J.L.L. Akkermans, Lennert Janssen, Jacques Neefjes
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SummaryKinesin and dynein-dynactin motors move endosomes and other vesicles bidirectionally along microtubules, a process mainly studied under in vitro conditions. Here, we provide a physiological bidirectional transport model following color-coded, endogenously tagged transport-related proteins as they move through a crowded cellular environment. Late endosomes (LEs) surf bidirectionally on Protrudin-enriched endoplasmic reticulum (ER) membrane contact sites, while hopping and gliding along microtubules and bypassing cellular obstacles, such as mitochondria. During bidirectional transport, late endosomes do not switch between opposing Rab7 GTPase effectors, RILP and FYCO1, or their associated dynein and KIF5B motor proteins, respectively. In the endogenous setting, far fewer motors associate with endosomal membranes relative to effectors, implying coordination of transport with other aspects of endosome physiology through GTPase-regulated mechanisms. We find that directionality of transport is provided in part by various microtubule-associated proteins (MAPs), including MID1, EB1, and CEP169, which recruit Lis1-activated dynein motors to microtubule plus ends for transport of early and late endosomal populations. At these microtubule plus ends, activated dynein motors encounter the dynactin subunit p150glued and become competent for endosomal capture and minus-end movement in collaboration with membrane-associated Rab7-RILP. We show that endosomes surf over the ER through the crowded cell and move bidirectionally under the control of MAPs for motor activation and through motor replacement and capture by endosomal anchors.

Introduction

Cells are packed with organelles that collectively contribute to a complex but highly organized intracellular architecture. Organelle density is particularly high near the microtubule organizing center (MTOC), localized close to the nucleus, and is surrounded by the Golgi apparatus, parts of the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) network, some mitochondria, and most vesicles of the endolysosomal system.1,2,3,4 Microtubules projecting from the MTOC provide tracks for motor protein-assisted transport of all organelles between the perinuclear (PN) region and cell periphery. While mitochondria move slowly along microtubules and endosomes relatively fast, both move in a bidirectional and stop-and-go manner.5 Bidirectional movement is powered by two motor protein types, dynein and kinesin. Dynein in complex with its co-factor dynactin and activating adaptors, including BICD- and Hook-family proteins, moves endosomes to the microtubule minus end, toward the MTOC,6,7,8,9 while kinesin motors (KIF family) transport cargo in the opposite direction.10,11,12

To facilitate transport, motor proteins and their cargo need to interact. This is controlled by Rab- and Arf-family GTPases and their effector proteins or by membrane phosphoinositides.13,14,15 Rab7-marked late endosomes (LEs) bind the effector proteins RILP or FYCO1, recruiting dynein-dynactin or kinesin-1 (KIF5B), respectively,16,17,18 while Arl8-marked LEs recruit dynein and kinesin motors through its effectors RUFY or SKIP.19,20,21 This process involves ER:LE membrane contact sites (MCSs). Here, the ER-resident protein Protrudin transfers kinesin-1 onto FYCO1,18 while the Rab7-interacting cholesterol sensor ORP1L interacts with the ER-resident protein VAPA at low cholesterol levels, dissociating dynein-dynactin motors from Rab7-RILP.22 Motor proteins also need activation. At microtubule plus ends, Lis1 activates dynein and likely dissociates following functional dynein-dynactin complex assembly.9,23,24,25,26,27,28 Activated dynein and kinesin motors move over microtubules decorated by microtubule-associated proteins (MAPs) and various posttranslational modifications (PTMs) affecting motor binding and motility.13,29 For example, MAP2 competes with dynein and kinesin for tubulin binding, thereby obstructing motor-driven transport.30,31,32 MAP9 prevents dynein-mediated transport by blocking the loading of dynein-dynactin subunit p150glued onto microtubules while enhancing kinesin-3 motility.31 Kinesin-1 activation and microtubule binding involves the MAP7 family (MAP7 and MAP7D1–3).33,34,35,36,37

Three models have been proposed explaining bidirectional transport. The recruitment model stipulates that one motor type binds the cargo membrane at any moment in time to determine movement direction, altered by motor replacement. However, many cargoes simultaneously bind kinesin and dynein-dynactin motors, resulting in the tug-of-war model.38,39,40,41 Here, both motor types associate to cargo, and the motor type providing the strongest force determines the net movement direction.6,42 Yet single-motor-type inhibition showed motor co-dependence, unlike the prediction by the tug-of-war model.43 A third model, the coordination model, also insinuates simultaneous cargo membrane binding of kinesin and dynein-dynactin motors. Transport direction is determined by respective motor proteins’ activation and inactivation,44,45,46 depending on various factors including microtubule tethering.47 The different models are mostly deduced from elegant biophysical experiments in isolated systems. While these studies provided information on physical motor properties such as the stalling force, velocity, and dissociation rates, Rab effector protein contribution and other cellular components were usually ignored.

We studied endosomal transport characteristics in intact cells, by following endogenous transport-related proteins fluorescently tagged using knockin technologies. While distinct vesicle subtypes show differences in speed and movement processivity, they all exhibit bidirectional stop-and-go movement. We focused on LE transport-related factors including organellar “roadblocks,” motor attachment to LE membrane anchors, and MAPs. LEs easily bypass mitochondrial roadblocks by hopping to and gliding along microtubules. Meanwhile, LE movement is associated to the ER allowing constant motor loading and detachment by MCS-resident proteins Protrudin and ORP1L. Although the Rab7-effector-motor axis is conditional for LE movement, motors are poorly associated to Rab7 effectors RILP and FYCO1. Rather, bidirectional movement is controlled in part by various MAPs. DyneinHC requires its activator Lis1,48 as well as the MAPs EB1, CEP169, and MID1, for recruitment to the microtubule plus end. Here, dyneinHC assembles with its co-factor dynactin, and the motor complex then moves toward the MTOC, catching endosomal membrane anchors like Rab7-RILP. We propose a modified coordination model that incorporates microtubule plus-end proteins and ER:LE MCS in controlling endosomal bidirectional movement, visualized in the crowded yet highly dynamic cell.

Results

Bidirectional stop-and-go movement is a general endosomal and vesicular transport feature

Live spinning disk microscopy (in MelJuSo cells) reveals bidirectional movement in a stop-and-go manner of LysoTracker+ LEs/Lys (Figure 1A; Video S1A). To explore endosomal movement in an objective and quantitative manner, we developed a tool, AnalyzeTrackMate, for annotation of moving and non-moving (stop-movement threshold = 0.25 μm/s) parts of TrackMate (Fiji)-generated tracks to calculate various features (Figure 1A). These include stop percentage of total track, stop duration, movement duration between stops, distance traveled between stops, and movement speed, as determined for LysoTracker-marked LEs (Figures 1A and S1A). To illustrate LE directional changes, we used AnalyzeTrackMate to calculate the angle between moving track fragments before and after a stop and displayed these angles as a rose plot, with 0° unaltered and 180° movement in the opposite direction (Figure S1B). Cellular and microtubule orientations were not integrated, which exclude anterograde and retrograde transport direction dissection. LysoTracker+ compartments largely overlap with Rab7+ LEs (Figures S1C and S1D). To determine whether LE transport characteristics differ from those of other vesicles, we compared endogenously tagged EGFP-eRab7a+ endosomes (LEs) with EGFP-eRab1a (Golgi-to-ER vesicles), mScarlet-eRab5a (early endosomes [EEs]), EGFP-eRab6a (ER-to-Golgi vesicles), EGFP-eRab11a (recycling endosomes [REs]), and EGFP-eRab14a (Golgi-to-endosome vesicles) (Figures 1B and S1E; Video S1B). All vesicle types showed bidirectional stop-and-go movements (Figures 1B–1D; Video S1B) with some differences in transport behavior (Figures 1E–1I and S1F). Rab7a+ LEs showed significantly higher stop percentage per track, as illustrated by a longer average stop duration (Figures 1F and S1F). Rab11a+ REs were relatively stationary, with few moving REs that showed comparable stop duration and stop percentage to Rab7a+ endosomes (Figures 1E, 1F, and S1F). Rab6a+ vesicles moved on long tracks with fewer and shorter stop moments, longer distances traveled, and longer movement durations between stops (Figures 1E, 1G, 1H, and S1F). Rab1a+, Rab7a+, Rab11a+, and Rab14a+ vesicle velocities were comparable (∼0.43 μm/s), with Rab6a+ vesicles moving slightly faster (∼0.48 μm/s) and Rab5 EEs mildly slower (∼0.38 μm/s) (Figures 1I and S1F). Mapping the crowded cellular environment

Vesicular structures move bidirectionally through the cytoplasm densely packed with protein complexes, filaments, and organelles. To portray this crowded environment, we reconstituted the full cytoplasmic volume of an epon-embedded MelJuSo cell through serial 100-nm-thin sections and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) image acquisition50 (Figures 1J and S1G). Per section, 10 × 10 images were recorded and stitched into a single image (Figure 1J). Next, 26 serial section images were aligned into a volume of about 2.5 × 25 × 25 μm. Cellular structures were then manually annotated and assigned. Further, the ER network was identified based on associated ribosomes, mitochondria by their cristae, Golgi by the PN pan-shape structures, and microtubules by tubular 25-nm-in-diameter structures. Large vesicular structures were assigned as endosomes (mixture of different endosomal subtypes and lysosomes), which may also include peroxisomes (Figures 1J–1L; Video S2A). While endosomes predominantly cluster in a PN endosomal cloud of around 10.3 × 16.5 μm in MelJuSo1 (Figure S1H), the ER and mitochondria are present across the cytoplasm (Figure 1J; Video S2A). When endosomes are released from the PN cloud, they are subject to long-range microtubule-based transport into the periphery where they encounter organelles and other cellular components in all dimensions (Figures 1K and 1L; Videos S2B and S2C). These potential cellular roadblocks have their own dynamics undetectable by EM.

Endosomal transport is supported by a highly organized cellular architecture

To test under dynamic conditions whether endosomal bidirectionality is a consequence of obstructions by large organelles such as mitochondria, we depleted the peripheral cytosol from mitochondria by SEPT9-MitotagRFP expression.51 This SEPT9 chimera is targeted to mitochondria recruiting dynein-dynactin motors that induce minus-end transport and mitochondrial clustering close to the MTOC, clearing the peripheral cytoplasm (Figure 2A). Using LysoTracker, we imaged LE movement in either SEPT9-MitotagRFP transfected or untransfected cells and quantified their transport by Trackmate/AnalyzeTrackMate (Figures 2A–2C; Video S3A). LE bidirectionality still occurred in the absence of peripheral mitochondria, as shown in kymographs (Figure 2B), with no difference in movement duration, movement distance, speed, stop duration, and stop occurrence (Figure 2C). Next, we visualized LE movement relative to peripheral mitochondria to assess how LEs pass these obstacles. Like LEs, mitochondria bind microtubules by motor proteins.52,53,54 Co-staining of mitochondria and microtubules in fixed cells showed consistent mitochondrial alignment along microtubules surrounded by peripheral LEs (Figure 2D). Time-lapse EGFP-eRab7a LEs imaging showed that 50% of the endosomes move in close proximity to peripheral mitochondria (Figure 2E). Various LE routes to bypass mitochondria were observed, defined as gliding, hopping, and in rare cases, pausing/obstruction (Figures 2F and 2G; Video S3B). While most LEs glide along mitochondria, this movement was sometimes combined with hopping between microtubules (Figures 2F and 2G; Video S3B). Gliding and hopping behavior was uniform for all moving endosomes, both in proximity to and at a distance from mitochondria (Figure 2F). Sporadically, an endosome showed a long pause in movement (>15 s) nearby a mitochondrion (Figure 2F), yet this was insignificant to the overall LE transport behavior (Figures 2C and 2E; Video S3B). These pauses may allow contact formation between the LEs and the mitochondria for inter-organelle communication.55,56 Contacts between the LEs and the mitochondria have been shown to occur for a few seconds with even kiss-and-run contacts (>0.5 s), in line with our observations.57,58 Endosomal movement may also be obstructed by “crossing” microtubules.45 We treated cells with nocodazole to destroy the microtubule network. Immediately after nocodazole wash-out, we imaged LE transport behavior on the newly formed sparse microtubules. The largest cellular organelle is the ER, which communicates with other cellular organelles through MCSs.59,60,61 Like mitochondria, the ER aligns to the microtubule network, shaping the ER network either directly or indirectly through interactions with moving endosomes62,63,64 (Figure 3A; Video S5). The ER regulates endosomal transport through ER:endosome MCSs. Proteins like ORP1L and the ER-resident protein Protrudin regulate LE loading with, respectively, dynein-dynactin or KIF5B motor proteins.18,22 To determine the architectural ER:endosome MCS characteristics at high resolution, we used supervised machine learning to reconstruct 3D MCSs between ER and LEs (Figures 1J and 3B). All annotated endosomes, those in the PN cloud and periphery, formed MCSs with ER membranes (Figure 3B). Zoom-in 3D reconstructions show irregularly shaped MCSs, often covering multiple endosomal surface areas (Figures 3C and S2A; Videos S6A and S6B), as also observed for ER:mitochondria MCSs (Figures S2B and S2C; Video S6C). To visualize MCSs in a dynamic manner, we endogenously tagged Protrudin with GFP (EGFP-eProtrudin) (Figures 3D and S2D). Time-lapse imaging showed dynamic ER areas enriched for EGFP-eProtrudin that were continuously moving in both directions, while remaining juxtaposed to LysoTracker+ LEs (Figures 3D and 3E; Video S7A). EGFP-eProtrudin enrichments were not due to ER deformations, since cells expressing a GFP-tagged signal sequence (ssER-GFP) to mark the ER did not show these enrichments (Figures 3D and 3E; Video S7B). Imaging of cells expressing both EGFP-eProtrudin and endogenously mScarlet-tagged Rab7a (mScarlet-eRab7) or Rab5a (mScarlet-eRab5) revealed specificity of Protrudin+ MCSs for Rab7+ LEs (Figures 3D, 3E, and S2E; Videos S7C and S7D). Contacts between the ER and maturing endosomes have also been observed in COS-7 and U2OS cells with overexpressed Rab5 and Rab7.65 Since the ER is aligned parallel to microtubules (Figure 3A), we tested whether their absence following nocodazole treatment affects ER:LE Protrudin+ MCS formation. After nocodazole treatment, the tubular peripheral ER structure was lost, and LE movement was inhibited (Figure S2F; Video S8A),66,67,68 while EGFP-eProtrudin remained concentrated juxtaposed to LysoTracker+ LEs (Figures 3F and 3G; Video S8B). Bidirectionally moving LEs feature stably bound but poorly motor-loaded Rab7 effectors

LE transport is coordinated by the small GTPase Rab7, its effector proteins RILP or FYCO1, and associated motor proteins dynein-dynactin or KIF5B.16,17,18 Motor interactions are controlled at MCSs, including Protrudin for KIF5B loading on Rab7-FYCO1 and the dynein-dynactin regulator ORP1L associated to Rab7 and the ER protein VAPA. To determine the dynamics of endogenous Rab7-effector-motor transport complexes, we created cell lines harboring EGFP/mScarlet-tagged combinations of the motor proteins KIF5B and dyneinHC (DYNC1H1) or the effector proteins RILP and FYCO1 (Figures S3A–S3C). Endogenously EGFP-tagged KIF5B (eKIF5B-EGFP) and mScarlet-tagged DYNC1H1 (mScarlet-eDyneinHC) expression did not alter LE movement (Figure S3D; Video S9A). In addition, ectopic HA-RILP or HA-FYCO1 expression in these cells showed motor recruitment to clustered LEs at the microtubule minus or plus end, confirming proper motor function (Figures S3E and S3F).16,17 Most endogenously tagged KIF5B and DYNC1H1 motors were present in the cytosol, with some detectable DYNC1H1 (but not KIF5B) at the microtubule plus ends (Video S9B), as previously observed with overexpressed dynein.9,69 Generally, both motor proteins were undetectable on endosomes above the cytosolic background, implying low numbers of endosome-associated motors. When motors were detected on vesicles, KIF5B and DYNC1H1 did not switch while moving bidirectionally, as observed for kinesin motors70 (Figure 4A; Video S9C). The recruitment model proposes mutual-motor-type binding and release, which do not fit these observations. To improve endogenous motor detection at LE membranes, we overexpressed a single (2xHA)FYCO1-T2A-(2xHA)RILP construct for equal overexpression of both Rab7 effectors (Figures S3G and S3H). This resulted in increased KIF5B and DYNC1H1 recruitment to LEs without affecting LE transport features (Figures S3I–S3K; Video S10A). LEs were slightly larger, which may explain the somewhat slower transport rate. Again, LEs did not switch motor proteins while changing direction (Figure 4B; Video S10B). KIF5B and DYNC1H1 levels per peripheral endosome varied. Most, but not all, endosomes had higher mScarlet-DyneinHC fluorescence, compared with eKIF5B-EGFP (Figure S4A), which was unrelated to movement direction (Video S10B). Unlike endogenous motor proteins, endogenously mScarlet-tagged FYCO1 (mScarlet-eFYCO1) and GFP-tagged RILP (EGFP-eRILP) effector proteins were easily detected, with both effectors bound simultaneously to the endosomal membrane during bidirectional movement (Figure 4C; Video S11). Most, but not all, endosomes contained higher EGFP-eRILP fluorescence when compared with mScarlet-eFYCO1, which was unrelated to transport direction (Figure S4B; Video S11). Apparently, bidirectional LE movement cannot be explained by Rab7 effector or motor switching. To compare the relative motor protein amounts versus effector proteins, we mixed the endogenously tagged motor and effector cell lines and determined overlap of each endogenously tagged protein with CD63-stained endosomes. Notably, mScarlet-eFYCO1 and EGFP-eRILP overlap with CD63, eKIF5B-EGFP, and mScarlet-DyneinHC was undetectable (Figures S4C and S4D). Quantifying motor protein numbers per endosome is often based on in vitro data, due to the challenges associated with quantifying these levels in intact cells. When the photon number provided by EGFP or mScarlet is known and detected by the microscope camera, combined with endogenous tagging of the relevant proteins with these two fluorescent proteins, the endogenous expression number can be calculated. We used our endogenously tagged cell lines as well as EGFP-eRab7a cells (Figures S1E and S4E) to determine Rab7a and effector and motor protein numbers present on a single LE. We first calibrated the absolute fluorescence intensity count (arbitrary units) for EGFP and mScarlet by quantifying the photocount while bleaching EGFP and mScarlet tetramers (see STAR Methods for details) (Figure S4F). Cells harboring endogenously tagged motors and effectors were imaged, and protein quantity was estimated according to the corresponding EGFP and mScarlet signal intensities (Figures 4D and S4F). Endogenous tagging using CRISPR-Cas9 can yield a heterozygous or homozygous knockin. We checked for untagged protein presence by western blot and corrected endosomal proteins’ quantification accordingly. Untagged protein was detected for Rab7a, KIF5B, and RILP, while FYCO1 and DYNC1H1 showed complete tagging (exclusively upward-shifted protein) (Figures S3B, S3C, and S4E). We estimated 863 ± 453 Rab7a proteins, 138 ± 106 FYCO1 proteins, 528 ± 340 RILP proteins, 61 ± 29 KIF5B motors, and 36 ± 18 dynein motors per endosome (Figure 4D). Of note, DYNC1H1 and KIF5B numbers could only be calculated in rare cases when visible on endosomes, suggesting that the estimated numbers represent maximal numbers, with most endosomes associated to considerably fewer motor proteins. Collectively, Rab7 effector proteins FYCO1 and RILP are simultaneously bound to LEs while moving bidirectionally. They occupy most Rab7 proteins, yet only few effectors are occupied by motor proteins.

Dynamic anchorage of motor proteins to endosomal membranes

Motor and effector protein swapping does not occur during bidirectional transport, yet motor and effector proteins bind endosomes dynamically. If so, effectors and motors must continuously be replaced or activated. We used fluorescence recovery after photobleaching (FRAP) to measure mScarlet-eRab7a, mScarlet-eFYCO1, and EGFP-eRILP endosomal membrane dynamics (Figures 4E, 4F, and S5A–S5C). While mScarlet-eRab7a and EGFP-eRILP were dynamic with, respectively, 83.4% and 62.8% mobile fractions (MFs), mScarlet-eFYCO1 had an MF of just 14.2%. The small mobile mScarlet-eFYCO1 fraction had a recovery half-time (RHT) of 140 s. Mobile mScarlet-eRab7a and EGFP-eRILP had an RHT of 200 s (Rab7a) and 20 s (RILP) (Figures 4E, 4F, and S5A). To determine endosomal motor protein dynamics, we visualized endogenous mScarlet-eDyneinHC and eKIF5B-EGFP by overexpressing their respective effector proteins HA-RILP or HA-FYCO1 (Figures S3E, S3F, and S5B). FRAP on PN clustered mScarlet-eDyneinHC and peripheral clustered eKIF5B-EGFP resulted in MFs of 47.8% and 78.8%, respectively, with corresponding RHT of around 20 s for both (Figures 4G and S5A). Of note, overexpressed GFP-RILP and GFP-FYCO1 show different dynamics, compared with their endogenous counterparts, which may influence the observed motor dynamics (Figures S5B and S5D). The FRAP data indicate dynamic Rab7-effector-motor complexes at LE membranes, with continuous replacement of RILP, FYCO1, and their associated motor proteins. Rab7 effectors RILP and FYCO1 bind with different kinetics that could in time alter the ratio of the two effectors on LEs.

Optogenetic kinesin motor accumulation at endosomal membranes induces plus-end stop-and-go transport

The tug-of-war model is based on an altered force of balance between kinesin and dynein motors, creating intermediate speeds since one motor force opposes the others.71 To test whether motor equilibrium altering affects LE velocity and loss of stop moments, we applied optogenetics, inducing light-controlled kinesin recruitment to Rab7+ LEs, boosting plus-end movement. Since KIF5B requires an additional light chain, we used kinesin-3 (KIF1A) instead for a two-factor optogenetic system. mCherry-Rab7a linked to iLID (iLID-mCherry-Rab7a) was co-expressed with KIF1A-Venus and an SSPB unit (KIF1A-Venus-SSPB) in cells. These two constructs dimerize after activation by blue light, collecting KIF1A on LEs.72 Swift KIF1A accumulation to LEs followed immediately after light activation, leading to massive anterograde movement (Figure 4H; Video S12). LE track analysis revealed almost universal plus-end movement (see rose plot), but still in a stop-and-go manner (Figure 4I; Video S12). Unlike cells expressing solely iLID-mCherry-Rab7a or KIF1A-Venus-SSPB/mCherry-Rab7a (without iLID), KIF1A recruitment to iLID-mCherry Rab7 yielded shorter stop duration and longer movements between stops without affecting LE speed (Figure 4J). Of note, results obtained from KIF1A may not mimic KIF5B function. Motor protein numbers linked to LEs determine the net movement direction but not stop-and-go nature or LE speed, as would have been predicted in the tug-of-war model. What then triggers stops and reversed movement of LEs?

LE movement is controlled by MAPs

While Rab proteins and their effectors anchor motor proteins at defined organelles, they do not determine LE stop-and-go behavior, leaving MAPs as an alternative. To identify MAPs involved in endosomal transport, we performed a Gene Ontology (GO) term analysis selecting for the term “microtubule,” using MelJuSo RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) data.73 We identified 21 expressed candidate MAPs that were each small interfering RNA (siRNA), silenced, and followed by time-lapse LE imaging using LysoTracker and microtubule detection by SPYtubulin (Figure 5A). The microtubule surface index was determined for each depletion to assess MAP silencing effects. No clear microtubule network effects (compared with siC) were detected for any MAP silencing, with the exception of MAP4, yet this does not exclude more subtle effects by other MAPs (Figures S6A and S6B). Endosomal movement was evaluated using TrackMate/AnalyzeTrackMate (Figures 5B, 5C, and S6C). Of the 21 tested MAPs, 18 affected endosomal transport, illustrating the importance of MAPs in motor protein function and subsequent LE movement (Figures 5B, 5C, and S6C). siRNA targeting ELM2, ELM4, MAP1B, MAP2, MAPRE2, MAPRE3, MID1, MID1IP1, NICKAP5L, and PAFAH1B1 yielded more peripheral endosomes often at the cost of the endosomal PN cloud (Figures 5B, 5D, and 5E). MAP6D1, MAP7D3, MAP1LC3B, and MAPRE1 silencing enlarged endosomes possibly by affecting fission and fusion events (Figures 5B and 5E). Last, silencing MAP1S and MAP4 decreased peripheral LE numbers with only few peripheral tracks available for analyses (Figures 5B and 5D). Of note, MAP4 depletion altered the microtubule network with microtubules accumulating in the PN region possibly yielding less peripheral endosomes (Figure S6A). AnalyzeTrackMate analyses revealed that silencing MAP1B, MAP1LC3, MAP1S, MAP9, MAPRE1, MAPRE2, NCKAP5L, and PAFAH1B1 altered endosomal transport dynamics, decreasing LE movement distance and movement duration while stop duration and stop occurrence increased (Figures 5C, 5E, and S6C). Some depletions, such as CAMSAP1, ELM2, and MID1IP1, only increased stop duration while ELM4 depletion only affected LE movement behavior (Figures 5C, 5E, and S6C). MAPRE3 depletion showed the opposite effect as both stop duration and stop occurrence were decreased (Figures 5C, 5E, and S6C). MAPRE3 encodes for the microtubule plus-end protein EB3, an EB1 (MAPRE1) homolog, yet it locates more proximally at the microtubule plus end.74,75 Why EB3 affects endosomal transport differently from EB1 is unclear. Dynein activator Lis1 affects dynein- and kinesin-mediated transport of select endosomes

The strongest effect on LE transport followed Lis1 depletion (encoded by the PAFAH1B1 gene), a microtubule plus-end protein and dynein-motor activator required for efficient cargo transport (Figures 5C and S6D).9,76,77,78,79,80,81,82 After Lis1 silencing, LEs arrested in both directions (Figure 5B), suggesting that Lis1 affects both dynein- and kinesin-mediated transport, which was confirmed in HeLa cells (Figures S6E and S6F; Video S13A). To test whether Lis1 showed vesicle-type specificity, we silenced Lis1 in endogenously EGFP-tagged Rab1a, Rab5a, Rab6a, Rab7a, Rab11a, and Rab14a cells (Figures 6A–6F; Video S13B). Silencing Lis1 dispersed Rab1a+, Rab5a+, and Rab7a+ endosomes, suggesting again plus-end and minus-end transport inhibition (Figure 6A). In addition, Rab1a+, Rab5a+, and Rab7a+ endosome movement dynamics were limited after Lis1 depletion with increased stop features and decreased movement features (Figures 6B–6F). Rab6a+, Rab11a+, and Rab14a+ vesicle dynamics were unaffected by Lis1 silencing (Figures 6B–6F), yet alterations in vesicle distribution were observed for Rab6+ and Rab14+ vesicles (Figure 6A). Most Rab6a+ vesicles moved to the cell tips, suggesting kinesin activity at the cost of dynein-motor activity following Lis1 silencing. Rab14a+ vesicles lost their PN cloud localization, a general dynein inhibition effect, yet kinesin does not fully take over since tip localization was absent. Of note, changes in movement dynamics may not be reliably measured for Rab14+ vesicles since they move short distances under unperturbed conditions. In control cells, Rab11a+ endosomes already localized in the periphery, suggesting that for Rab11+ endosomes kinesin, and not dynein, is the main motor. Lis1 silencing did not affect this. While Lis1 binds and activates dynein motors used by all vesicles for minus-end transport, the effect on vesicle transport differs for different vesicle subtypes and sometimes in both directions. To examine anterograde transport, we explored Lis1 depletion effects on the kinesin effector protein FYCO1 (mScarlet-eFYCO1) and the kinesin loading protein Protrudin (EGFP-eProtrudin). Lis1 depletion resulted in dispersed endosomes, without any FYCO1 alteration on LysoTracker-stained LEs (Figure 6G) or on Protrudin+ MCSs juxtaposed to LEs (Figure 6H; Video S13C). This suggests that Lis1 does not affect LE KIF5B loading apparatus. EB1, MID1, and CEP169 are essential for dynein and Lis1 recruitment to microtubule plus ends

Lis1 promotes DYNC1H1 release from its inhibited Phi-conformation into an open, active conformation.27,83,84 At the microtubule plus end, Lis1-activated DYNC1H1 assembles with the dynactin subunit p150glued, priming the complex for cargo transport toward the MTOC.9,23,24,25,27,28,85,86 Endogenously EGFP-tagged Lis1 (EGFP-eLis1) locates Lis1 at the growing microtubule plus ends and the MTOC (Figures 7A, S7A, and S7B; Video S14A). Endogenous Lis1 tagging is critical as GFP-Lis1 overexpression localized cytosolic, overshadowing endogenous plus-end Lis1 localization (Figure S7C). Lis1 does not have a microtubule binding site and thus requires other MAPs for microtubule binding. To determine these, we selected MAPs with similar effects as Lis1 on endosomal transport and/or endosomal localization (Figure 5E). These MAPs were silenced in EGFP-eLis1 cells, and Lis1 location at the microtubule plus ends was quantified using the TrackMate spot recognition tool. Depletion of three MAPs resulted in loss or limited Lis1 recruitment to microtubule plus ends (Figures 7B, S7D, and S7E). These were EB1 (plus-end protein encoded by MAPRE1 gene) targeting dynein and dynactin to the microtubule plus end,28,87 CEP169 (MTOC and plus-end protein encoded by NCKAP5L gene), and MID1 (an E3 ligase with unknown localization). We endogenously mScarlet-tagged these MAPs in EGFP-eLis1 cells. EGFP-eLis1 co-localized with eEB1-mScarlet and mScarlet-eCEP169 at the plus end and MTOC, while mScarlet-eMID1 localized cytosolically (Figures 7C, S7F, and S7G; Videos S14B–S14D). Whereas eEB1-mScarlet and mScarlet-eCEP169 localized to the growing microtubule tips across the cell (Figure 7C), EGFP-eLis1 accumulated at peripheral microtubule plus ends (Figure 7D). Since Lis1 binds DYNC1H1, we tested whether these MAPs affected DYNC1H1 recruitment. Indeed, silencing EB1, CEP169, or MID1 prevented DYNC1H1 accumulation at plus ends, suggesting that DYNC1H1 and Lis1 form a complex in the cytosol before plus-end recruitment (Figures 7E and S7H). The dynactin subunit p150glued is recruited to the plus end independently of Lis1, CEP169, and MID1 (Figures 7F, S7I, and S7J), in agreement with earlier reports.9 p150glued recruitment required EB1 protein since EB1 depletion yielded p150glued deposition across the entire microtubule (Figures 7F and S7J). After active dynein-dynactin complex formation, Lis1 should dissociate.23,25,76 To visualize this, we overexpressed HA-RILP inducing CD63-stained LEs’ MTOC clustering (Figures 7G and 7H). While mScarlet-DyneinHC and EGFP-ep150glued were concentrated at the clustered CD63-marked LEs, EGFP-eLis1 was undetectable (Figures 7G and 7H). This suggests that most Lis1, unlike dynactin, is released before Rab7-RILP-mediated LE transport occurs, in agreement with observations that Lis1 dissociation initiates cargo transport.76,88,89 However, endosomes are often not located at microtubule plus ends for acquiring the dynein-dynactin motor. To visualize this, we created cell lines endogenously expressing EGFP-eLis1 and either mScarlet-eRab5a, -eRab6a, or -eRab7a (Figures S7B and S7K). EEs and LEs were mostly distant from the Lis1-containing microtubule plus ends, with Rab5+ EEs moving toward the minus end while passing the plus ends in the opposite direction and Rab7+ LEs moving mostly in areas lacking Lis1 (Figure 7I; Video S15). Surprisingly, anterograde moving Rab6+ vesicles often overlapped with Lis1-labeled plus ends, suggesting that anterograde transport is restricted by microtubule length (Figure 7I; Video S15). When Rab6+ vesicles reach the microtubule plus end, movement is limited, and they sometimes move retrograde with a shrinking microtubule. This suggests microtubule plus-end control of both retrograde transport by dynein-motor assembly and anterograde transport by microtubule length limitations. Discussion

Bidirectional endosomal transport has been intensely studied under various in vitro and in vivo conditions, leading to various models. Here, we exploited modern genome editing technologies to study endogenous endosomal transport inside living cells in real time. Using a systems approach, we placed endosomal movement in the context of greater intracellular architecture and interrogated the contributions of the ER, endosomal anchors, and microtubule-associated factors in controlling efficient endosomal transport by dynein and kinesin motors. Rab7 effectors and motor protein evaluation revealed that in an endogenous setting, far fewer motors associate with endosomal membranes relative to effectors. This implies coordination of transport with other endosome physiological aspects through GTPase-regulated mechanisms, such as RILP and/or FYCO1 functions in V-ATPase activity regulation and homotypic fusion with lysosomes in the context of endocytosis and autophagy.17,90,91,92,93

Because GTPase effector protein recruitment alone could not account for endosomal movement directionality, we explored effects of microtubule-associated elements. Endosomal transport was limited after Lis1 depletion, a dynein-motor binding protein,48 whose mutations are associated with the brain malformation lissencephaly.94 While some studies suggested that Lis1 inhibits dynein-mediated transport,95,96 we and others identify Lis1 as a positive cargo transport regulator.76,77,78 Lis1 depletion slightly decreased the cargo speed, in line with in vitro findings that Lis1 increases dynein-dynactin complex velocity.28,97,98 Other groups reported that Lis1 initiates dynein-mediated transport without affecting dynein-dynactin motor speed.25,76 These differences could result from variations in context (in vitro versus in vivo, adaptor proteins presence) and cell type.

We show that the MAPs EB1, CEP169, and MID1 control DYNC1H1 and Lis1 deposition at microtubule plus ends. Yet in our hands, a described MAP involved in Lis1 binding to dynein, MAP1B,99 did not alter Lis1 recruitment to the microtubule plus end. This suggests MAP redundancy, cell-type specificity, or cytosolic dynein activation by Lis1, which may be sufficient. CLIP170, another Lis1 interactor and microtubule plus-end binder, was not tested in our MAP screen because of poor expression levels in MelJuSo cells. CLIP-170 can interact with both Lis1 and p150glued in a competitive manner,100,101 yet our experiments show that Lis1 silencing failed to prevent p150glued recruitment to the plus ends. p150glued recruitment requires EB1 only, since p150glued binds microtubules through the EB1 CAP-Gly domain.102,103,104,105

Our data show that Lis1 depletion affects both retrograde and anterograde endosomal transport. DYNC1H1 and Lis1 are detected at the microtubule plus end, unlike KIF5B, indicating that KIF5B location and activation is fundamentally different. KIF5B loading on LEs may occur at ER:LE MCSs where Protrudin resides18 and/or at the KIF5B-rich MTOC (where also DYNC1H1 and Lis1 locate). KIF5B activation may involve another MAP, possibly MAP7-family proteins.35 This insinuates that Protrudin in ER:LE MCS may perform motor loading, while motor activation occurs when the Rab7-FYCO1-KIF5B complex contacts microtubules for activation by MAP7.

We propose that under endogenous conditions, dynein-motor formation and activation for endosomal transport occur at the growing microtubule plus end where MID1, EB1, and CEP169 recruit Lis1-activated DYNC1H1. EB1 also recruits p150glued to assemble the dynein-dynactin complex for movement toward the MTOC. On the way, they may be captured by Rab7-RILP anchors at the LE membrane. LEs surf over the ER and in the resulting MCS, and Protrudin can load KIF5B on Rab7-FYCO1. Motors and Rab7 effectors bind only temporarily and are then replenished with help from the ER and specialized MAPs. Motor replacement and activation thus determine the net LE movement direction. Considering vesicle movement in the crowded cell context and the involvement of proteins fluorescently tagged at endogenous expression levels provides a more comprehensive mechanistic understanding of complex molecular processes, unmatched by studies in isolation, revealing cell biology nuances undetectable under overexpression conditions.

Experimental model and study participant details

Cell culture

MelJuSo (human melanoma, female) cells were kindly provided by Prof. G. Riethmuller (LMU, Munich) and authenticated by PCR-single-locus-technology (Eurofins genomics, sample code 19 ZE 000486) as MEL-JUSO. MelJuSo were cultured at 37 degrees/5% CO2 in IMDM (Gibco) supplemented with 7.5 % fetal calf serum (Greiner). HeLa (human cervical carcinoma, female) cells purchased from ATCC and authenticated by PCR-single-locus-technology (Eurofins genomics, sample code 19 ZE 000491) as HeLa 229. HeLa were cultured at 37 degrees/5% CO2 in DMEM (Gibco) supplemented with 7.5% fetal calf serum (Greiner).

Method details

Endogenously tagged cell line generation

MelJuSo cells harboring endogenously tagged proteins (EGFP or mScarlet) were created using CRISPR/Cas9. gRNAs (see key resources table) were cloned into px330 vectors and co-transfected with cEndo-GFP/mScarlet-PURO, nEndo-PURO-GFP/mScarlet, cEndo-GFP/mScarlet-BLAST, nEndo-BLAST-GFP/mScarlet containing HR sequences (see key resources table) into MelJuSo cells using X-tremeGENE HP (Roche). Six days post-transfection, EGFP- and mScarlet-positive cells were selected using Puromycin (1ug/ml, Gibco) or Blasticidin (10ug/ml, Gibco), and colonies derived from single cells were expanded and validated. All cell lines were cultured at 37 degrees, 5% CO2 and routinely (negatively) tested for mycoplasma.

Sanger sequencing

Genomic DNA isolates from cell lines using ISOLATE II Genomic DNA kit (BIO-52066, Bioline) were amplified using primers mentioned in the key resources table and sequenced using a BigDye Terminator Kit (Applied Biosystems). Snapgene (GSL Biotech) was used for sequence analysis.

Track analysis

Vesicle tracking was performed using Fiji software TrackMate for Fiji (Vesicle diameter = 0.6-1μm, depending on vesicle-type size; thresholds and other parameters were chosen as appropriate based on control samples; the same parameters were used across all samples and all independent experiments). To analyze these tracks, we developed a python based script (AnalyzeTrackMate) to analyze TrackMate.xml files. The additional functionality that this provides is twofold: tracks can be smoothed over time and tracks are split into different fragments based on the (smoothed) velocity. The smoothing improves the interpretation of the tracks while still maintaining sufficient detail to discern start/stop events. This is however inherently linked to the frame-rate at which images are acquired and needs to be evaluated carefully. Splitting of tracks into fragments is based on two criteria: a speed threshold and an direction change threshold. Both criteria are based on the smoothed tracks. The speed threshold is filtered with an additional local median filter (size 3) to remove spurious single-frame stops or moves. After splitting a track into fragments, the following metrics are calculated for each fragment: distance (total length of fragment), distance_end_to_end (euclidian distance between start and endpoint of fragment), theta (average direction of fragment), dir_change (change in direction with respect to previous moving fragment), duration (total time of fragment). When multiple subsequent fragments are moving (but split because of a direction change), also the total distance and duration between stops is calculated. For each track the following aggregated metrics are calculated: number of fragments, number of edges, number of stops, total stop duration, number of moving fragments, total moving duration, total track duration and distance. After parsing the TrackMate.xml file and analyzing the different fragments, the script exports all results to an.xlsx file and also exports vector illustrations in.svg format. The program and source code are available upon request to the authors.

Fractional distances

To calculate fractional distances, we normalized the fluorescent intensities along multiple line ROIs (ROI manager ImageJ/Fiji) to median. To exclude non-vesicular (background pixels), we determined the signal threshold. Pixels below this threshold were excluded from the analysis. Plotted are the distances corresponding to the remaining vesicular pixels as fraction of the distance from the nucleus to the plasma membrane.

Quantification of protein numbers using intensity calibration

First, the concentration of EGFP and mScarlet tetramers was adjusted such that distinct single molecules were clearly visible at 100x magnification. Imaging was performed on a Dragonfly spinning disk microscope (Andor/Oxford instruments) in TIRF mode, equipped with a Zyla 4.2+ camera (Andor/Oxford instruments) and a climate chamber. To determine the number of photons per fluorophore, we acquired 1000 frames at maximum laser power and 100ms (EGFP) or 30ms (mScarlet) exposure time and spots were localized using Thunderstorm in ImageJ and boxed out in 7x7 pixels; only the non-touching boxes were kept. Intensity traces were saved in.csv format. Traces were analyzed in Matlab 2021b (the Mathworks). An average background intensity trace was subtracted from all individual traces and each trace was analyzed using k-means clustering to determine intensity levels. These intensity levels were sorted, and the differences between the levels were collected. After analyzing all intensity traces, the median of the differences between the k-means-clustered intensity levels was takes as the intensity per fluorescent molecule in TIRF mode. To convert from TIRF mode to confocal intensity, we acquired images of fluorescent cells at multiple locations using both modalities and measured the ratio in intensities of the entire fields of view. To determine the number of fluorescent proteins present on a vesicle, cells were imaged in confocal mode after which the total intensity (integrated density) in an encircled region fully enclosing a vesicle was measured. Next, the background intensity was subtracted by measuring an encircled area of identical size with no discernable vesicles present. Protein numbers were calculated using the calibration measurements from the tetramer intensities. Acquisition parameters were kept identical when operating in a specific modality.
