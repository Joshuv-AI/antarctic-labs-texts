Circular RNA circSLC39A10 promotes prostate cancer progression by activating Wnt signaling via the miR-936/PROX1/β-catenin axis
Authors: Kailai Chen, Xiang Pan, Shiheng Zhang, Meiqi Xu, Xi Chen, Feng Pei, Mengyuan Wu, Fanlong Meng, Bin Sun, Manjie Zhang, Yakun Luo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Circular RNAs (circRNAs) play a crucial role in the initiation and development of cancers. Understanding circRNAs’ functions and molecular mechanisms in tumor development is expected to reveal new diagnostic indicators and therapeutic targets of prostate cancer (PCa). In our study, we identified a new circRNA hsa-circ-0057553 (circSLC39A10) in PCa from a bioinformatic microarray analysis. The levels of circSLC39A10 were observed to be markedly elevated in both prostate cancer cells and tissues. This increased expression was associated with multiple clinicopathological features, suggesting its potential as a new diagnostic indicator for PCa. CircSLC39A10 exhibited oncogenic effects on the proliferation, migration, invasion, and metastasis of prostate cancer cells both in vivo and in vitro. CircSLC39A10 was identified as a factor that promoted the malignant progression of PCa cells through the miR-936/PROX1/β-catenin pathway, ultimately leading to the activation of Wnt signaling. Overall, circSLC39A10 is an oncogenic circRNA with potential as a biomarker for PCa. The identified circSLC39A10/miR-936/PROX1/β-catenin axis shows promise as an innovative therapeutic target for PCa.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s11658-025-00814-7.

Introduction

Prostate cancer (PCa) stands as a formidable adversary in the landscape of male malignancies, with a global incidence that underscores the urgency of effective therapeutic strategies [1]. The complexity of PCa is further amplified by its clinical heterogeneity, ranging from indolent to aggressive forms, which can evolve into castration-resistant prostate cancer (CRPC) [2] and neuroendocrine prostate cancer (NEPC) [3], both of which pose significant challenges regarding treatment.

Circular RNAs have recently gained recognition as significant regulators of cancer biology, offering a new paradigm in our understanding of post-transcriptional gene regulation [4]. CircRNAs are synthesized through backsplicing, which joins the 5′ and 3′ ends of exons, creating a covalently closed loop structure. This unique configuration endows circRNAs with greater stability compared with linear RNAs, making them resistant to exonuclease degradation [5]. The expression patterns of circRNAs are often tissue-specific and developmentally regulated, which contributes to their potential as biomarkers [6].

The interplay between circRNAs and cancers is multifaceted, with evidence suggesting their involvement in regulating key cellular mechanisms of example cell migration, proliferation, apoptosis, and invasion [7]. For example, circMMP2(6,7) forms a complex with β-catenin and the arginine methyltransferase PRMT5, driving transcriptional activation through histone H3R2me1/H3R2me2s modifications, which upregulates the level of genes that support bone metastasis in breast cancer [8]. Meanwhile, studies have shown that circASH2 interacts with Y-box binding protein 1 (YBX1) and tropomyosin 4 (TPM4), leading to remodeling of cytoskeletons. This process is crucial for the suppression of liver cancer cell migration and invasion, which are essential steps in the metastatic progression of cancers [9]. Recently, dysregulation of a subset of circRNAs has been identified in prostate cancer (PCa) tissues [10]. Ultimately, these circRNAs regulate PCa progression by affecting cell growth, programmed cell deaths, epithelial–mesenchymal transition, and sensitivity to chemoradiotherapy [11]. Nevertheless, the molecular mechanisms and biological functions of circRNAs associated with PCa still require comprehensive exploration.

The Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway plays a key function in regulating multiple cellular processes and is often disrupted in cancers [12]. In PCa, dysregulated activation of the Wnt/bata-catenin pathway correlates with both onset and advancement in tumors, as well as the development of castration resistance [13]. Especially noteworthy are the interactions between circRNAs and the Wnt/β-catenin signaling axis, which could offer novel perspectives into the mechanisms underlying PCa and pinpoint potential targets for interventions [14].

In this study, we recognize a new circRNA, namely circSLC39A10 (or called hsa_circ_0057553), utilizing the microarray data of circRNA from previous studies on human PCa tissues [15]. Subsequently, our findings revealed that circSLC39A10 expression notably increased in high-grade PCa tissues and androgen-independent cells. More importantly, circSLC39A10 modulates PROX1 expression by interacting with miR-936, which promotes the proliferation and metastasis of PCa cells both in vivo and in vitro, ultimately activating the Wnt/β-catenin pathway, opening novel avenues for regulating PCa development and metastasis.

Materials and methods

Bioinformatics analysis

The possible interactions between miRNAs and circRNAs were predicted using online tools such as circBase, TargetScan, and miRDB. miRNA target gene identification was performed through the mirDIP, TargetScan, miRPathDB, and miRWalk platforms. Transcriptomic datasets including TCGA-PRAD (https://www.cancer.gov/ccg), GEPIA (http://gepia.cancer-pku.cn), and GEO (GSE140927, containing four tumor and four adjacent samples; https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo) were interrogated for tumor–normal tissue differential expression and coexpression patterns. Enrichment analysis was conducted via the Bioconductor’s cluster profile package (version 4.14.4) using R (version 4.3.3) to explore biological functions in circSLC39A10-knockdown (sh-circSLC39A10) and control (sh-Ctrl) PCa cells. The heatmap of the gene was drawn by using the R (version 4.3.3) software with the “heatmap” package. The heatmap demonstrated 21 significant differential expression genes between PCa tissues and adjacent nonneoplastic tissues. Differential expressed genes were identified using the DESeq2 package with P value adjusted to lower than 0.05 and log2 fold change greater than 1.

Cell culture

We purchased normal prostate epithelial cells RWPE-1 (cat. no CRL-3607) and human PCa cell lines (LNCaP, cat. no. CRL-1740; 22Rv1, cat. no. CRL-2505); VCaP, cat. no. CRL-2876; C4-2, cat. no. CRL-3314; DU145, cat. no. HTB-81; PC-3, cat. no. CRL-3471) from the American Type Culture Collection. We verified these cells through short tandem repeat (STR) profiling. Culture media specifications were standardized as follows: RWPE-1 in Keratinocyte-SFM (Invitrogen); VCaP/C4-2 lineages in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium; LNCaP/22RV1 populations in RPMI-1640 base medium; PC3/DU145 models respectively maintained in Ham's F-12 K and minimum essential medium (all media reagents sourced from Invitrogen, USA). Cellular propagation was executed under controlled atmospheric conditions (37 °C, 5% CO2, humidity saturation). All cell passages did not exceed ten generations. Cell cultures were routinely screened for Mycoplasma contamination every 14 days via PCR assays.

Oligonucleotide synthesis and transfection

We synthesized the miR-936 mimic and inhibitor oligonucleotides at RiboBio (Guangzhou, China). The detailed sequences for knockdown are listed in Supplementary Table 1. The cells were then transfected with these oligonucleotides. The transfection was carried out with RiboFECT™ CP Reagent (RiboBio, China) following the guidelines provided by the manufacturer. In brief, 1 × 105 cells were seeded per well in 24-well plates containing 1 mL of antibiotic-free standard growth medium and cultured until reaching 40–50% confluency. SiRNA, plasmids, microRNA inhibitor, or microRNA mimic was prepared at working concentrations riboFECT™ CP Buffer as indicated. Following a 15-min exposure to riboFECT™ CP Transfection Reagent, nucleic acid complexes were supplemented to the culture medium for sustained incubation over a 72-h period under standard growth conditions.

Lentivirus infection

We purchased lentivirus systems from Fenghbio (Hunan, China) for overexpression of circSLC39A10 (OE-circSLC39A10: pLV-CMV-hsa_circSLC39A10-EF1-ZsGreen1-T2A-Puro) and negative control (OE-Ctrl: pLV-CMV-EF1-ZsGreen1-T2A-Puro) or knockdown of circSLC39A10 (sh-circSLC39A10: pLV-CMV-sh-circSLC39A10-EF1-ZsGreen1-T2A-Puro) and negative control (sh-Ctrl: pLV-CMV-EF1-ZsGreen1-T2A-Puro). The detailed sequences for knockdown of circSLC39A10 are listed in Supplementary Table 2. Lentiviruses were employed to transduce prostate cancer (PCa) cells with polybrene added (8 µg/mL; Sigma-Aldrich). Following infection, cells were selected with puromycin (2 µg/mL; Merck) for a period of 2 weeks. The efficiency of infection was then assessed through western blot and RT-qPCR analysis.

Transwell assay

Matrigel-coated 24-well chambers were filled with medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), while cells resuspended in a serum-free medium were added to the upper chamber. The cells migrated through the filter attracted by the lower chamber medium. After 24 h, nonmigratory cells were eliminated, and the migrating cells were fixed with methanol and stained with Crystal Violet before being imaged under an inverted fluorescence microscope (Olympus IX73).

Cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8)

A total of 2 × 103 cells were cultured in 96-well plates at a density of 2 × 103 cells per well. Following experimental interventions, cellular responses were evaluated at specified intervals (0 h, 24 h, 48 h, 72 h, and 96 h post-treatment), Cell Counting Kit-8 solution (Abcam Biotech Co., Ltd.) was introduced into the wells. Absorbance values at a wavelength of 450 nm were subsequently measured using a microplate reader to assess cell viability.

Colony-formation assay

Post-treatment cellular clonogenicity was evaluated by seeding 1 × 103 cells/well in six-well culture plates under standard incubation conditions (37 °C, 5% CO2). Following 14 days of continuous culture, colonies were methanol-fixed and stained with 0.1% Crystal Violet for macroscopic visualization. After air-drying, colony enumeration was performed, with triplicate independent experiments conducted for statistical validation.

Wound healing assay

Cells were seeded at a density of 5 × 105 cells/well in six-well culture plates. Upon reaching approximately 70% confluence, experiments were subjected to the designated treatments. A uniform scratch was made using the tip of a 10-μL sterile pipette. The scratch width at the same position was photographed at 0 h and 24 h via an optical microscope. ImageJ software was utilized to calculate the wound area and assess the cell migration rate.

Western blot assay

Western blotting assay was conducted following established protocols from a previous study [16]. Protein lysates were extracted, separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS)-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE), transferred onto nitrocellulose (NC) membranes, and probed using standard procedures with antibodies against proteins including anti-β-catenin (ab32572, Abcam), anti-Wnt3a (ab219412, Abcam), anti-Wnt5a (ab235966, Abcam), anti-ZEB1 (ab203829, Abcam), anti-c-Myc (ab32072, Abcam), anti-CyclinD1 (ab134175, Abcam), anti-PROX1 (ab199359, Abcam), and anti-β-actin (ab8226, Abcam) with anti-GAPDH (ab8245, Abcam) serving as loading control. The dilution rate of primary antibodies was 1:1000, and the dilution rates of secondary antibodies were 1:10,000. We used 20 μg of total protein for western blotting analysis as indicated.

Reverse-transcription quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR)

Cell and tissue RNA isolation was achieved using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA) in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol. Subsequently, cDNA was generated from total RNA by using the RevertAidTM First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Toyobo, Japan) under the following conditions: 25 °C for 5 min, 42 °C for 1 h, and 70 °C for 5 min. RT-qPCR was performed on a LightCycler 480 (Roche) with SsoAdvanced Universal SYBR Green Supermix (Bio-Rad, USA). The amplification regimen started with 95 °C denaturation (5 min), followed by 30 repetitions of three-stage cycling: denaturation (94 °C, 45 s), annealing (60 °C, 30 s), and elongation (72 °C, 5 min). Primers for circRNA, miRNA, and mRNA, synthesized by Shanghai Sangon Co., Ltd., were utilized in the assays. Relative expression levels were determined using the 2−ΔΔCt method. Our experiments were conducted in triplicate. The primer sequences are detailed in Supplementary Table 3.

RNA sequencing

RNA integrity was verified using spectrophotometry (NanoDrop 2000; Thermo Fisher) prior to polyA-selected mRNA enrichment. Sequencing library construction utilized the Hieff NGS™ MaxUp Dual-mode mRNA Library Prep Kit (YEASEN, 12301ES96) following manufacturer specifications, including unique barcode incorporation for sample multiplexing. Size selection (150–200 bp inserts) was achieved through magnetic bead-based purification (YEASEN, 12601ES56). Library amplification employed 2× Super CanaceTM High-Fidelity Mix with adapter-specific primers, followed by quantification on a Qubit® 2.0 fluorometer. Pooled libraries underwent paired-end sequencing on Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platforms. The RNA-seq datasets generated in this study are publicly accessible through the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) BioProject database under accession no. PRJNA1217928.

RNA fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH)

Cy3-conjugated circSLC39A10 and FAM-tagged miR-936 probes (RiboBio, China) were hybridized to PC3/DU145 cellular specimens using the FISH kit (C10910, RiboBio). Nuclei were counterstained using 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI), followed by signal acquisition via laser scanning confocal microscopy (Zeiss LSM 900).

RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP) assay

The RIP assay was executed via the Magna RIP Kit (Sigma-Aldrich) per manufacturer's guidelines. DU145 and PC3 prostate cancer cell lysates were prepared with RIP-specific buffer containing protease and RNase inhibitors (1:100 dilution) and then combined with anti-AGO2 (ab186733, Abcam) or anti-IgG (ab172730, Abcam) antibody-conjugated beads (Millipore) overnight at 4 ℃. On the subsequent day, treatment was carried out using proteinase K (HY-108717, MCE) buffer. RNA was extracted from the immunoprecipitated samples using the RNeasy MinElute Cleanup Kit (QIAGEN, Germany). RNA obtained was subsequently converted into cDNA through reverse transcription, which was subsequently analyzed by RT-qPCR assay.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP)

To promote cross-linking between proteins and DNA, 5 × 105 cells were exposed to a final concentration of 1% formaldehyde, and the treatment was halted by adding glycine to terminate the reaction. Next, the cell lysates were sonicated to fragment the DNA into pieces ranging from 500 to 1000 bp. Equal volumes of the chromatin supernatants were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with 1 μg of either anti-PROX1 or anti-IgG antibodies. Afterward, reverse cross-linking of the protein–DNA complexes was performed to release the free DNA. The released DNA was subsequently analyzed using RT-qPCR. The primer sequences utilized in this study are listed in Supplementary Table 4.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) prostate adenocarcinoma specimens (n = 78) with matched adjacent tissues were procured from Harbin Medical University Fourth Affiliated Hospital’s Urology Department (2021.1–2023.1). Ethical compliance was secured via institutional review board approval (no. 2021-WZYSLLSC-29). For IHC experiments, histologic sections from tissues embedded in paraffin wax underwent antigen retrieval in citrate buffer for 15 min. Subsequently, the sections were treated with normal goat serum for 30 min to prevent nonspecific binding. Ten tissue sections were treated with primary antibody Ki67 (ab15580, Abcam) listed below and incubated overnight at 4 °C. Avidin–biotin peroxidase detection systems with 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB) substrate were utilized to label the locations of antigens, followed by counterstaining with hematoxylin (ab220365, Abcam). Immunohistochemical signal intensity and positively stained areas of tissue sections were independently evaluated and scored by observers.

Mouse xenografts and treatments

BALB/c nude males (6 weeks old, Vital River) were housed in specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions (25 ± 1 ℃, 40–60% humidity, 12-h photocycle) approved by Harbin Medical University’s Animal Ethics Committee (2024-DWSYLLC2-02). For subcutaneous xenografts, DU145 cells (4 × 10⁶/mouse) suspended in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) were implanted subcutaneously following trypsinization. Tumor dimensions were serially measured until terminal resection at 4 weeks for histopathological evaluation. Metastatic modeling involved tail vein injection of luciferase-tagged DU145 cells (1 × 10⁶/mouse), with bioluminescent monitoring (IVIS Lumina II, PerkinElmer) at 40 days post-inoculation. Tissue processing included hematoxylin–eosin (HE) staining and IHC analysis following standard protocols. Luciferin substrate (MCE) was administered per manufacturer guidelines prior to in vivo imaging.

Statistical analysis

Triplicate biological replicates were systematically executed for all experimental protocols. Quantitative results, expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), with comparative analyses performed through Student's t-test or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) contingent upon data distribution patterns. Statistical analysis was conducted using GraphPad Prism 9.0 software, and -value below 0.05 was regarded as statistically significant.

CircSLC39A10 derives from the solute carrier family 39 member 10 (SLC39A10) gene. SLC39A10 functions as a zinc transporter [17] and has emerged as a significant player in the progression of various cancers, often being overexpressed in gastric adenocarcinomas and being found to be related to adverse patient outcomes [18]. Furthermore, high expression of SLC39A10 correlates with tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes and immune checkpoints such as CTLA4, TIM3, and TGFB1 in hepatocellular carcinoma [19]. In this work, we aimed to dissect the function of circSLC39A10 in PCa cells.

We initially examined the circSLC39A10 expression in 78 paired PCa tissues and adjacent normal tissues via RT-qPCR assay. Initial findings revealed a significant upregulation of circSLC39A10 in tumor tissues compared with normal tissues (Fig. 1B). Subsequently, to evaluate the clinical relevance of circSLC39A10, samples were stratified into groups based on expression levels. Statistical analysis demonstrated that circSLC39A10 overexpression strongly correlated with advanced tumor stage (P = 0.0013) and higher Gleason scores (P = 0.0006), whereas no associations were observed with age or prostate-specific antigen (PSA) levels (P > 0.05) (Table 1). Comparative analysis across PCa cell lines (LNCaP, C4-2, VCaP, 22RV1, PC3, and DU145) and the nonmalignant prostate epithelial cell RWPE-1 confirmed elevated circSLC39A10 expression in all cancer cells (Fig. 1C). Specifically, circSLC39A10 expression was higher in androgen-receptor-independent PCa cells (PC3 and DU145) than in other androgen-receptor-dependent PCa cells (LNCaP, VCaP, C4-2, and 22RV1). Thus, PC3 and DU145 cells were selected for further investigation in this study (Fig. 1C). According to the annotation information in the circBase database, circSLC39A10 is formed by exons 2 (1019 nucleotides), 3 (208 nucleotides), 4 (170 nucleotides), 5 (189 nucleotides), 6 (121 nucleotides), 7 (369 nucleotides), 8 (81 nucleotides), and 9 (191 nucleotides) of the SLC39A10 gene. The back-splicing event of circSLC39A10 cDNA was confirmed through Sanger sequencing (Fig. 1D). To validate the structural uniqueness of circSLC39A10, divergent and convergent primer sets were designed for differential amplification of its circular isoform versus linear SLC39A10 transcripts. Agarose gel electrophoresis results indicated that circSLC39A10 could merely be enhanced from cDNA, thereby excluding the potential for genomic rearrangements or trans-splicing events in PC3 cells (Fig. 1E). RNase R resistance assays (Fig. 1F) and actinomycin D treatment experiments (Fig. 1G) demonstrated enhanced stability of circSLC39A10 compared with its linear transcript. Subcellular fractionation and FISH analyses further confirmed predominant cytoplasmic localization of circSLC39A10 in DU145 and PC3 cells (Fig. 1H, I), supporting its potential function as a cytoplasmic sponge for proteins or miRNAs. Overall, these findings suggested that circSLC39A10 was a steady circRNA situated in the cytoplasm, being a potential biomarker candidate for PCa. circSLC39A10 promotes the proliferation, migration, and invasion of PCa cells in vitro

To investigate the involvement of circSLC39A10 in the tumorigenesis of PCa, we designed a lentiviral system to knock down circSLC39A10 in PC3 and DU145 cells. The lentiviral vector system (sh-circSLC39A10) effectively downregulated the expression of circSLC39A10, with no impact on the expression of the SLC39A10 gene (Fig. 2A). Subsequent experiments utilizing the results of CCK-8 and colony formation assays demonstrated a decrease in the rate of cell growth sh-circSLC39A10-treated PC3 and DU145 cells, indicating an impact on cellular growth (Fig. 2B, C). Furthermore, Transwell and wound healing assay indicated that sh-circSLC39A10 treatment significantly suppressed invasive and migrational capacity in DU145 and PC3 cells, suggesting a potential function of circSLC39A10 in regulating cell invasion and migration (Fig. 2D, E). Additionally, increased apoptosis was observed in DU145 and PC3 cells following sh-circSLC39A10 treatment, implying its involvement in regulating apoptosis (Fig. 2F; Supplementary Fig. S1). The oncogenic effect of circSLC39A10 in subcutaneous and metastatic tumor models

To evaluate the tumorigenic role of circSLC39A10 in vivo, subcutaneous DU145 cell xenograft models were established in BALB/c nude mice to assess the tumor-promoting effectiveness of circSLC39A10 in vivo. In summary, mice with DU145 tumors were randomly assigned to two groups: one group received a peritumoral injection of sh-Ctrl, while the other received sh-circSLC39A10. We analyzed the circSLC39A10 expression in sh-circSLC39A10 DU145 cells using RT-qPCR (Fig. 3A) and found that sh-circSLC39A10 significantly reduced the expression of circSLC39A10. Following an intervention, we noted that the sh-circSLC39A10 group exhibited a more pronounced reduction in tumor weight and size compared with sh-Ctrl group (Fig. 3B, C). The inhibitory efficacy, calculated on the basis of tumor weight, reached approximately 60% (Fig. 3D). Furthermore, immunohistochemical (IHC) analysis demonstrated that sh-circSLC39A10 treatment markedly reduced Ki67 expression levels in xenograft tumors (Fig. 3E, F). These findings indicated that circSLC39A10 stimulated the growth of prostate cancer cells in vivo. Furthermore, we generated lung metastasis models following a previous study [20] to examine whether circSLC39A10 regulates tumor metastasis in vivo. A fortnight following the intravenous delivery of sh-circSLC39A10-DU145-luc cells through caudal venous administration, the mice were then subject to bioluminescence imaging to determine tumor development in vivo. As shown in Fig. 3G, the mice were imaged by an IVIS system to visualize lung metastases. Additionally, it was observed that the fluorescence intensity was reduced in the sh-circSLC39A10 group. Correspondingly, HE staining demonstrated significant suppression of pulmonary metastatic nodule formation in sh-circSLC39A10 group compared with control cohort (Fig. 3H, I). circSLC39A10 promotes the malignant behavior of PCa cells via modulating Wnt signaling

Although circSLC39A10 exhibits oncogenic properties in PCa cell lines and xenografts, the downstream signaling mechanisms of PCa cells remain elusive. To further elucidate the circSLC39A10-mediated regulatory network of PCa cells, we employed RNA-seq to analyze transcriptome changes in DU145 cells treated with sh-circSLC39A10. By comparing the total mRNAs from sh-circSLC39A10-treated DU145 cells with those from sh-Ctrl cells, we identified 669 upregulated differential expressed genes (DEGs) and 1449 downregulated DEGs (Fig. 4A, B). The heatmap generated from the results revealed two hierarchical clusters based on similar gene expression profiles from three replicates under each condition (Fig. 4B). In KEGG enrichment analysis (Fig. 4C), the DEGs were predominantly enriched in pathways such as “focal adhesion,” “cell adhesion molecules,” “adhesion junction,” and “PCa,” underscoring the crucial roles of circSLC39A10 in DU145 cells. Notably, “Wnt signaling” emerged as a significantly affected pathway (Fig. 4C; Supplementary Fig. S2A). Within the Wnt signaling pathway, irregular modulation of the transcriptional regulator β-catenin (gene name CTNNB1), a key element of the Wnt signaling pathway, triggers initial stages of carcinogenesis [21]. Furthermore, several previous studies have demonstrated that various circRNAs promote tumorigenesis via modulating Wnt signaling in cancers [22], including PCa [23]. Moreover, as shown in the heatmap (Fig. 4D), among the top ten regulated genes related to Wnt signaling, we identified that CTNNB1 was the most downregulated after silencing circSLC39A10 in DU145 cells, and that the downstream targets of CTNNB1, such as MYC [24], CCND1 [25] or ZEB1 [26], were also decreased upon sh-circSLC39A10 treatment. Importantly, we also found that the expression of circSLC39A10 was positively related to CTNNB1 expression in the 78 PCa tissues (Supplementary Fig. S2B). Therefore, we next sought to study whether circSLC39A10 triggers Wnt signaling by activating β-catenin in PCa cells. As shown in Fig. 4E–G, circSLC39A10 depletion significantly reduced the expression of Wnt signaling components (CTNNB1, WNT3A, WNT5A, CCND1, MYC, and ZEB1) at both the transcriptional and protein level in DU145 and PC3 cells. Consistently, RT-qPCR and western blotting analyses revealed upregulation of these Wnt-related genes in overexpressed-circSLC39A10 (OE-circSLC39A10) DU145 and PC3 cells (Supplementary Fig. S2C, D). Notably, we also found that OE-circSLC39A10 resulted in accumulation of β-catenin in the nucleus of DU145 and PC3 cells, with its cytoplasmic expression being markedly reduced (Fig. 4H, I). Overall, the data highlight the role of circSLC39A10 in promoting the malignant behavior of PCa cells through activation of the Wnt/β-catenin axis. circSLC39A10 acts as a sponge for miR-936 in PCa cells

Given that circSLC39A10 promotes the functionality of Wnt signaling via β-catenin, we then investigated the molecular process underlying circSLC39A10 triggering the Wnt/β-catenin pathway in PCa cells.

CircRNAs have a recognized function as molecular sponges for microRNAs, controlling expression of particular target genes. We cross-analyzed the circBase, miRanda, and CircInteractiome databases to predict potential microRNAs that could interact with circSLC39A10. Multiple candidate microRNAs were found to have target sites for circSLC39A10 (Fig. 5A). Further, we aimed to identify microRNA targets of circSLC39A10 and analyzed the effects on these microRNAs in sh-circSLC39A10 DU145 or sh-circSLC39A10 PC3 cells (Fig. 5B). We subsequently observed that miR-936 was the only one that was significantly upregulated in the sh-circSLC39A10-treated PCa cells (Fig. 5B). As shown in Fig. 5C, we found that OE-circSLC39A10 notably inhibited miR-936 levels in DU145 or PC3 cells. More intriguingly, Edachery et al. demonstrated that miR-936 exhibited tumor-suppressive characteristics by modulating ANXA2 overexpression in metastatic PCa that is independent of hormones [27]. Therefore, we selected miR-936 as a priority for further investigation. In addition, our analysis revealed an inverse correlation between circSLC39A10 and miR-936 levels in 78 patients with PCa (Fig. 5D). For further confirmation, interactions between circSLC39A10 and miR-936 were validated through a dual-luciferase reporter assay. Plasmids harboring the wild-type (WT-circSLC39A10) and mutated (MUT-circSLC39A10) forms of circSLC39A10 were constructed on the basis of the predicted binding sequences between circSLC39A10 and miR-936 identified through the CircBank database (Fig. 5E). The paired plasmid vectors were introduced into DU145 and PC3 cellular systems comprising negative control (NC mimic) and miR-936-overexpressing experimental cohorts through transfection. Quantitative luciferase profiling (Fig. 5E) revealed that WT-circSLC39A10 exerted significant transcriptional repression in miR-936-abundant environments compared with NC mimic, whereas MUT-circSLC39A10 displayed no discernible regulatory impact on reporter signals across comparative conditions. To elucidate the interaction between miR-936 and circSLC39A10 in gene regulation mechanisms, we performed RNA-binding protein immunoprecipitation (RIP) experiments in prostate cancer cell models PC3 and DU145. These investigations employed Argonaute 2 (AGO2)-specific immunoprecipitation, given its established function as an essential component of RNA-induced silencing complexes (RISC) that mediate miRNA activity through RISC-mediated regulatory pathways [28]. Both circSLC39A10 and miR-936 exhibited significant coprecipitation with AGO2 proteins, indicating that both of them were present in RISCs (Fig. 5F, G). These results indicate that circSLC39A10 acted as a molecular sponge by directly binding miR-936. Moreover, we also found that miR-936 inhibitor significantly induced CTNNB1 expression in DU145 and PC3 cell lines (Fig. 5H; Supplementary Fig. S3). Our results implied that miR-936 serves as a negative regulator of the Wnt/β-catenin axis for PCa cells. To further explore this, we examined whether miR-936 could counteract the stimulatory effects of circSLC39A10 on CTNNB1 and its downstream targets. As shown in Fig. 5I, J, knocking down miR-936 (miR-936 inhibitor) prominently reversed the downregulation of CTNNB1, WNT3A, MYC, and ZEB1 caused by sh-circSLC39A10 at the transcription and protein level for DU145 and PC3 cells, respectively. MicroRNAs, acting as post-transcriptional regulators, exert their regulatory effects by inducing mRNA degradation or translational repression of target transcripts [29]. Therefore, we investigated how miR-936 affected its downstream factors. A total of 145 potential downstream candidates of miR-936 were identified after cross-analyzing predictions from the miRDB, TargetScan, miRPathDB, and miRWalk databases (Fig. 6A). We then further analyzed the overlap of these 145 candidate genes linked to Wnt/β-catenin signaling obtained from the GeneCards website (11,041 genes). As shown in Fig. 6B, 91 genes were both predictive of downstream targets of miR-936 and linked to the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway. Among these, PROX1 is particularly noteworthy. circSLC39A10 activates Wnt signaling through PROX1-mediated activation of CTNNB1 transcription in PCa cells

Since the data above confirmed that PROX1 is an important effector in the regulation of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway through the circSLC39A10/miR-936 axis, we next sought to explore the molecular mechanism through which PROX1 regulates the Wnt/β-catenin pathway. Earlier research demonstrated that PROX1 played a key role as an activator of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway, thereby playing a role in promoting cancers [32, 34, 35]. Specifically, in hepatocellular carcinoma cells, PROX1 facilitates β-catenin nuclear translocation, thereby activating the Wnt/β-catenin pathway to promote tumorigenesis [34]. However, the molecular mechanism through which PROX1 regulates β-catenin in PCa cells remains unclear. We then found that knockdown of PROX1 (sh-PROX1) reduced levels of β-catenin in DU145 and PC3 cells at transcription and protein levels (Fig. 8A, B). Furthermore, we also demonstrated that overexpression of PROX1(OE-PROX1) promotes β-catenin nuclear translocation in DU145 or PC3 cells (Supplementary Fig. S5), which indicates that PROX1 is a potential promoter of Wnt/β-catenin signaling in PCa cells. Next, to explore the mechanism through which PROX1 regulated CTNNB1 transcription, we identified four potential binding motifs of PROX1 on the CTNNB1 promoter from the Jaspar database (Fig. 8C). We further demonstrated that PROX1 bound to these four motifs of CTNNB1 promoter to enhance CTNNB1 transcription in DU145 and PC3 cells (Fig. 8D). More intriguingly, we also found that sh-circSLC39A10 reduced the binding density of PROX1 on the CTNNB1 promoter (Fig. 8E). Furthermore, in sh-circSLC39A10-treated DU145 and PC3 cells, β-catenin also decreased after binding to the promoters of its downstream genes, including ZEB1, MYC, or CCND1 (Fig. 8F–H). Discussion

Various diseases, particularly cancers, have been found to involve the differential expression of different circRNAs. Owing to their stability, high abundance, conservation, and specific spatiotemporal distribution, these molecules have emerged as a focus of biomedical research over the past few years [36]. In this study, our results reveal a novel function of the circular RNA circSLC39A10 (circBase ID: hsa_0057553) in the advancement of PCa. We identified that circSLC39A10 serves as an important modulator of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway, which has been widely investigated for its involvement in cancer initiation and progression. Specifically, we demonstrated that circSLC39A10 facilitated PCa cell proliferation, migration, and invasion by acting as a sponge for miR-936 and thereby releasing the transcription factor PROX1 owing to its inhibitory effect. More intriguingly, Ding et al. also reported a circular RNA (has_circ_0057558) that also originates from the SLC39A10 gene [37], containing six exons (exon 5 and exon 10) and with sequence length of 1121 bp. We now report circular RNA circSLC39A10 (hsa_circ_0057553), which contains eight exons with sequence length of 2348 bp. The back-splicing sites of circSLC39A10 (hsa_circ_0057553) are composed of exon 2 and exon 9. Although these two circRNAs merely originate from the same host gene SLC39A10, the structure is different. Moreover, to the best of our knowledge, there are no reports investigating the role of hsa_circ_0057553 in cancer, to date. Our results highlight the complexity of the regulatory mechanisms involving noncoding RNAs in cancer biology. Circular RNAs, a group of noncoding RNAs, have drawn increasing interest in recent years and garnered considerable attention owing to their potential involvement in a range of diseases, including cancers [4]. In contrast to linear RNAs, circRNAs are distinguished by their unique covalently linked loop structures, providing them with remarkable stability and improved resistance to degradation. This characteristic makes them promising candidates as biomarkers and therapeutic targets [38].

A key finding of our work is the discovery of miR-936 as a target for circSLC39A10. MicroRNAs (miRNAs), a group of small noncoding RNAs, play an essential role in the regulation of gene expression and multiple cellular processes [39]. miR-936, in particular, has been highlighted in the downregulation in various cancers, such as gastric cancer, non-small cell lung, and PCa [40–42]. Research has shown that miR-936 regulates chemoresistance in NSCLC by targeting GPR78 and the activating Galphaq Rho GTPase pathway. This finding implied that miR-936 enhances the chemosensitivity of NSCLC cell lines to chemotherapy, providing a novel therapeutic strategy [43]. Furthermore, the research of Liu et al. revealed that miR-936 expression is significantly reduced in both clinical gastric adenocarcinoma specimens and gastric cancer cell lines. The overexpression of miR-936 was found to suppress cell proliferation, migration, and invasion, enhancing cell apoptosis in gastric cancer cell lines. ERBB4 was validated as a direct target of miR-936, and the miR-936/ERBB4 axis was shown to regulate Akt-related pathways, controlling gastric cancer progression [44]. For PCa, the study demonstrated that miR-936 loss promoted the emergence of an androgen-independent metastatic phenotype of PCa [27]. ANXA2, a calcium-dependent protein binding to phospholipids, was identified as a target of miR-936, with its overexpression associated with advanced stages of PCa. The study suggested that the development of miR-936 as a targeted therapeutic molecule might offer a means of controlling metastatic PCa and improving the outcomes of patients with PCa [27]. Thus, miR-936 shows a promising association with malignant progression of cancers. The current results thus reveal the critical impact of interactions between circSLC39A10 and miR-936 for PCa tumorigenesis.

Numerous studies have shown that circRNAs function as sponges for miRNAs to influence miRNA-regulated gene expression in cancers [45]. In this study, we demonstrate that circSLC39A10 directly interacts with miR-936, leading to derepression of PROX1, a transcription factor associated with cell survival and proliferation. Specially, PROX1 has been found to be overexpressed in PCa and contributes to neuroendocrine plasticity as well as liver metastases [33]. In our study, we proposed a new model in which circSLC39A10 served as a molecular sponge for miR-936, thus reducing the suppressive effect on PROX1. Meanwhile, our findings suggested that PROX1 was identified as a genuine target for the circSLC39A10/miR-936 axis in PCa cells.

The Wnt/β-catenin axis serves a critical role in cellular functions, including cell growth, differentiation, and migration. β-Catenin is an essential element of the varied Wnt signaling pathway, significantly contributing to intracellular communication that is vital for embryonic growth, sustaining balance within the body, and cancer progression [46]. Dysregulated activation of β-catenin has been associated with progression in numerous cancers, including PCa [47]. We found that circSLC39A10 contributed to triggering of β-catenin mediated through PROX1 in PCa cell lines. More intriguingly, we further showed that PROX1 regulated transcription of CTNNB1 through associations with CTNNB1 promoter in PCa cells. Taken together, our results prove that circSLC39A10 could enhance β-catenin expression mediated by PROX1, further promoting both the stimulation of Wnt signaling pathway and the oncogenic behavior in prostate cancer cell lines.

Conclusions

This study is the first to highlight and confirm that circSLC39A10 exerts its oncogenic role in PCa by acting as a sponge for miR-936 to stimulate the PROX1/β-catenin signaling axis to facilitate the invasion, metastasis, and proliferation of PCa (Fig. 9).
