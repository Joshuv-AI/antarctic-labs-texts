Salivary responses to an experimental activation of the HPA axis in pigs: Effects of ACTH on analytes related to stress, immune system, inflammation, redox status and metabolism
Authors: María Botía, José Joaquín Cerón, Silvia Martínez-Subiela, Alba Ortín-Bustillo, Alberto Muñoz-Prieto, Damián Escribano
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Salivary biomarkers were evaluated in pigs following experimental ACTH administration.•Cortisol, cortisone and total esterase activity showed significant post-ACTH responses.•This study describes for the first time the dynamics of salivary cortisone in pigs.•Saliva proved to be a suitable non-invasive matrix for assessing acute stress responses.Keywords: ACTH, Biomarkers, Pig, Saliva, Stress

Introduction

Assessing stress and welfare in pigs requires reliable biomarkers that can be measured using non‑invasive methods. Saliva is particularly suitable for this purpose because its collection avoids handling stress and reflects multiple physiological systems, including endocrine, inflammatory, immune, redox and metabolic pathways (Tvarijonaviciute et al., 2020). The interest in salivary biomarkers has grown alongside societal and scientific concerns regarding animal welfare, especially in intensive production settings where animals are frequently exposed to social, environmental, and management‑related stressors (Porcher, 2022).

Acute stress activates the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis, leading to the release of corticotropin‑releasing hormone (CRH), adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH), and glucocorticoids (GCs) (Palme, 2019). GCs, and cortisol in particular, are widely recognised as indicators of stress and have been the subject of extensive research in saliva (Hellhammer et al., 2009). Cortisone, the inactive metabolite of cortisol, is also of interest because its conversion in saliva has been demonstrated to enhance the detection of HPA activation (Cornes et al., 2015). These hormones are a core component of the endocrine stress response, and their concentrations in saliva provide an accurate reflection of systemic changes.

In addition to glucocorticoids, several salivary analytes have been proposed as biomarkers of stress or welfare. Some, such as chromogranin A (CgA) and alpha‑amylase (sAA), reflect sympathetic‑adrenal‑medullary (SAM) activation (Toda et al., 2008). These analytes respond to adrenergic stimulation and are typically associated with psychosocial stressors (Tammayan et al., 2021). Their presence allows the differentiation between HPA responses and sympathetic activation, which do not necessarily occur in parallel. Other analytes, like total esterase activity (TEA) or butyrylcholinesterase (BChE), have been associated with acute stress responses in pigs, although their mechanistic link to the HPA axis remains less defined (Tecles et al., 2016, 2017). Similarly, oxytocin has been explored as a biomarker of positive welfare, reflecting affiliative behaviours, social bonding, and sensory stimuli rather than HPA axis activation (Lürzel et al., 2020; López-Arjona et al., 2020c).

Saliva also contains biomarkers of inflammation and immune function, including the acute phase proteins haptoglobin (Hp) and C-reactive protein (CRP) and also calprotectin (S100A8/A9), adenosine deaminase (ADA), and total proteins (TP). It also has markers of redox changes, such as cupric reducing antioxidant capacity (CUPRAC), and metabolic status, such as urea and creatinine (Contreras-Aguilar et al., 2021; Rubio et al., 2019). Although these analytes are not classical indicators of HPA activation, GCs can modulate inflammatory and immune pathways (Cain & Cidlowski, 2017; Peinado-Acevedo et al., 2024). Therefore, evaluating their response to ACTH provides insight into secondary physiological consequences of HPA stimulation.

Despite the extensive catalogue of saliva biomarkers available for pigs, the use of ACTH challenges has been limited to the evaluation of cortisol in saliva (Wolf et al., 2020) and CRP in serum (Burger et al., 1998). To the authors’ knowledge, no studies have yet examined the response of a broader biochemical panel in saliva to HPA activation.

Therefore, the objective of this study was to determine how activation of the HPA axis through ACTH administration affects a comprehensive panel of salivary analytes. For this purpose, a profile including biomarkers of stress such as cortisol, cortisone, CgA, sAA, TEA, BChE, oxytocin; immunity and inflammation such as Hp, CRP, calprotectin, ADA and TP; redox status such as CUPRAC; and metabolism such as urea and creatinine were analysed in saliva of pigs after an ACTH injection.

Materials and methods

Study animals

A total of 22 Landrace x Large White growing male pigs in the mid-fattening period were included in the study. At the time of sampling, pigs were 14 weeks old, weighing approximately 50 kg. Animals were housed at the ‘Granja Docente Universitaria’ of the University of Murcia located in Guadalupe (Murcia, Spain), with ad libitum access to a nutritionally balanced commercial diet and water and a minimum space of 0.65 m2 per animal (Council Directive 2001/88/CE of 23 October 2001 amending Directive 91/630/CEE concerning minimum standards for the protection of pigs).

The pigs were divided into two groups in the same pen. The first group (n = 11) was injected with 10 µg/kg ACTH analogue (Cosachten Depot 0.25 mg/ml, Dechra, United Kingdom) intramuscularly in the neck. In the second group (n = 11), the animals were treated with physiological saline (0.9% sodium chloride) through the same route. The volume of saline administered was selected to match the volume range used for ACTH injections, thereby minimising potential effects related to injection volume. This protocol of ACTH injection has been adapted from a previous study by (Wolf et al., 2020).

On the day of ACTH or saline injection, saliva samples were collected approximately one hour before administration (B) at 9.00 h and then at 20, 40, 60, 90, and 120 min post-injection (T20m, T40m, T60m, T90m and T120m, respectively) and again 24 h later (T24h).

Saliva was collected using Salivette tubes (Sarstedt, Aktiengesellschaft and Company, d-51,588 Nümbrecht, Germany) containing a sponge (Esponja Marina, La Griega E. Koronis, Madrid, Spain) instead of a cotton swab as previously reported (López-Martínez et al., 2022). During the sampling period, animals were temporarily prevented from eating and drinking to avoid contamination of the saliva samples. Pigs were allowed to chew the sponge, which was clipped to a flexible thin metal rod, for approximately one minute (or longer if required) until thoroughly moistened. The sponge was then placed into the Salivette tube. Tubes were kept refrigerated until arrival at the laboratory (< 2 h), where they were immediately centrifuged (3000 × g, 10 min, 4 °C), and the supernatant was collected. This collection method yielded an average volume of 0.5 mL of saliva per animal. All samples were stored at −80 °C until the end of the experimental sampling period and subsequently analysed.

Biochemical analysis of saliva

Biomarkers of stress

Cortisol was measured by an automated chemiluminescence immunoassay (Immulite 2000, Siemens Medical Solutions Diagnostics) previously validated for its use in pigs (Escribano et al., 2012), with results expressed in µg/dL.

Cortisone and oxytocin were measured by two amplified luminescent proximity homogeneous (AlphaLisa) assays previously validated in pig saliva (Botía et al., 2024; López-Arjona et al., 2020b), with results expressed in ng/mL and pg/mL, respectively. CgA was measured by an AlphaLisa assay with coefficients of variation intra and inter-assay below 15%, and a limit of detection of 3 ng/mL.

The sAA was measured by a commercial assay (a-amylase, OSR6182, Beckman Coulter) previously validated in porcine saliva in an Olympus AU400 autoanalyzer (Beckman Olympus AU400 Chemistry Analyzer, Beckman Coulter) (Fuentes et al., 2011), and its activity was expressed as IU/L.

BChE was analysed by a spectrophotometric method as previously described, using butyrylthiocholine iodide (BTCI, Sigma-Aldrich Co., St. Louis, MO, USA) as substrate (Tecles et al., 2016) and its activity was expressed as nmol of substrate hydrolysed/mL sample/min.

TEA was measured by a method based on the hydrolysis of the substrate 4-NA (Sigma-Aldrich Co, St Louis, Mo, USA) by salivary esterases previously reported in pigs (Tecles et al., 2017), with results expressed in IU/L.

Biomarkers of inflammation

Hp concentration was measured by an assay based on AlphaLisa technology previously used in pig saliva (Ortín-Bustillo et al., 2024) with results expressed in mg/L.

Calprotectin was analysed with a commercially available immunoturbidimetric assay (BÜHLMANN fCal Turbo assay, Laboratories AG) previously validated using an Olympus AU400 autoanalyzer (López-Martínez et al., 2023) with results expressed in mg/L.

CRP concentration was measured by an assay based on AlphaLisa technology previously used in pig saliva, with results expressed in µg/L (Ortín-Bustillo et al., 2024).

Biomarkers of the immune system

ADA was analysed with a commercially available spectrophotometric automated assay (Adenosine Deaminase assay kit, Diazyme Laboratories), previously validated for porcine saliva (Tecles et al., 2018) with results expressed in IU/L.

TP was analysed using a commercial colorimetric kit designed to measure urinary and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) proteins (Protein in Urine and CSF, Spinreact) (Ortín-Bustillo et al., 2022).

Biomarkers of redox status

The CUPRAC was assayed by a previously validated method for porcine saliva (Rubio et al., 2019) and its activity was expressed as mmol/L.

Other analytes

Urea and creatinine were measured using commercial kits from Beckman (Beckman Coulter Inc., Fullerton, CA, USA). These analyses were carried out using an Olympus AU600 autoanalyzer as described previously (Contreras-Aguilar et al., 2021), and its results were expressed as mg/dL.

The specific volumes of saliva utilized for each assay were: 10 µL for cortisol, 10 µL for cortisone, 10 µL for oxytocin, 10 µL for CgA, 20 µL for sAA, 10 µL for BChE, 50 µL for TEA, 5 µL for Hp, 10 µL for calprotectin, 5 µL for CRP, 10 µL for ADA, 2 µL for TP, 5 µL for CUPRAC, 7 µL for urea and 20 µL for creatinine.

The Ethical Committee on Animal Experimentation (CEEA) of the University of Murcia approved the research protocol for this study with approval number CEEA-235/2016, according to the European Council Directives regarding the protection of animals used for experimental purposes. Also, all methods were carried out in accordance with relevant and ARRIVE guidelines for the care and use of animals.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics for each analyte were obtained by routine methods and are expressed as median and interquartile range. Data normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. When the normality assumption was not met, data were transformed using the natural logarithm prior to analysis. Changes in each analyte over time were assessed using a General Linear Model for repeated measures (GLM-RM), with time as the within-subject factor and group (ACTH vs. Control) as the between-subject factor. Sphericity was evaluated using Mauchly’s test; when violated (p < 0.05), Greenhouse–Geisser corrections were applied to adjust the degrees of freedom. When significant main effects or interactions were found, pairwise comparisons were performed using the Bonferroni post hoc test. All analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 28.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), and statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

When evaluating the overall differences between treatments, the GLM analysis revealed a significant effect of group (Table 2). Specifically, pigs administered ACTH exhibited significantly higher overall concentrations of endocrine stress markers, including cortisol (p = 0.004), and cortisone (p < 0.0001), as well as TEA (p = 0.009), compared to the saline control group. Similarly, regarding inflammatory responses, the ACTH group showed significantly higher levels of Hp (p = 0.009) and calprotectin (p = 0.002) overall. In contrast, analytes predominantly associated with the adrenergic system (CgA, sAA), positive welfare (oxytocin), immune system (ADA, TP), and redox status (CUPRAC) showed no significant differences between the ACTH and control groups.

Discussion

The present study evaluated the salivary biochemical response of pigs to an experimentally induced activation of the HPA axis by ACTH administration. The dosage of 10 µg/kg employed in this study, in accordance with previous experimental models in pigs (Larzul et al., 2015; Wolf et al., 2020), was specifically chosen to induce a robust stimulation of the adrenal cortex in an experimental setting and may resemble an acute stress response. Furthermore, the inclusion of a broad panel of salivary biomarkers provides a wide interpretation of which salivary components can be sensitive to an acute endocrine stress.

The activation of the HPA axis after the ACTH administration was evidenced by the marked increases in salivary cortisol and cortisone. Cortisol concentrations increased markedly between 40 and 90 min after stimulation and returned to baseline values within 24 h, confirming a transient endocrine response. This increase in salivary cortisol is consistent with a previous report made by Wolf et al. (2020) in terms of timing (90 min after injection), although the magnitude of increase was lower in our report (3.22-fold versus 11-fold). This difference in the magnitude of change between the two studies should be explored in more detail. However, it may be explained in part by methodological factors, such as assay cross-reactivity to the different corticoids, rather than by biological differences. For instance, a 6% cross-reactivity with corticosterone was recorded with the assay used by Wolf et al. (2020), in contrast to the 0.9% observed with corticosterone in the Immulite assay that was used in our report (U.S. Food & Drug Administration, 2020).

Cortisone demonstrated a temporally distinct response in comparison with cortisol, exhibiting significantly higher salivary concentrations at 20 and 120 min following injection. Although cortisol and cortisone reflect HPA axis activation, their temporal dissociation is consistent with the complex regulatory dynamics of the axis, which involves pulsatile secretion and multiple feedback mechanisms capable of generating non-linear glucocorticoid responses following acute stimulation (Lim & Khoo, 2025). At the peripheral level, salivary cortisone concentrations are strongly influenced by local conversion of cortisol via 11β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase type II in the salivary glands (Debono et al., 2016; Perogamvros et al., 2010). In this context, the second increase in cortisone observed at 120 min, reaching 0.88 ng/mL, can be interpreted as a delayed metabolic consequence of the preceding cortisol peak, which occurred at approximately 90 min. By contrast, the early increase in cortisone detected at 20 min may reflect rapid initial activation of the 11β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase type II within the salivary glands, and its precise physiological significance remains to be fully elucidated. Overall, these findings support the view that salivary cortisone provides complementary information to cortisol in the assessment of acute stress responses.

Within the group of stress-related biomarkers, in addition to the increases in cortisol and cortisone, a significant increase at T120m in TEA was observed. This is consistent with a previous report in which pigs were exposed to pre-slaughter stress (transport and lairage) (López-Arjona et al., 2020a). TEA represents the combined activity of several salivary enzymes, primarily carbonic anhydrase VI (CA-VI), lipase, and cholinesterase. Each of these enzymes has been shown to respond to stress. Specifically, studies in pigs have demonstrated that lipase and cholinesterase are the major contributors to TEA elevation under acute stress events such as nasal restraint, while CA-VI becomes more relevant in prolonged or pathological conditions such as lameness (Tecles et al., 2017). In our study, however, BChE did not show significant changes after ACTH administration. This suggests that the observed increase in TEA could be driven primarily by components other than cholinesterase, such as salivary lipase activity. Moreover, the significant differences between groups reinforce the interpretation that TEA reflects downstream metabolic or enzymatic effects associated with glucocorticoid action rather than nonspecific variability.

In contrast to cortisol, cortisone, and TEA, other markers that are associated with the adrenergic response to stress, such as CgA and sAA, did not show significant alterations following administration of ACTH. This phenomenon may be attributable to the distinct mechanism of activation exhibited by these biomarkers. Specifically, their release is triggered by SAM axis rather than HPA axis activation (Taupenot et al., 2003; Toda et al., 2008). Similarly, oxytocin showed no significant changes, which aligns with recent evidence suggesting that salivary oxytocin does not reliably respond to the activation of the HPA axis. Instead, its release appears to be more closely associated with social or sensory stimuli of low intensity, such as maternal interactions or affiliative contact, rather than acute endocrine challenges like ACTH administration (Uvnäs-Moberg et al., 2024).

With regard to inflammatory markers, Hp demonstrated a marked increase at 40, 60, 90, and 120 min following ACTH administration. This finding is consistent with a previous report, which established that activation of the HPA axis by stress stimuli can induce the synthesis of proteins such as Hp, even in the absence of infection or tissue damage (Black, 2002). This response, although commonly associated with infectious or traumatic processes, can be initiated solely by stress. Therefore, the increase in Hp concentration in saliva observed in this study could be a direct consequence of ACTH-induced neuroendocrine activation. Furthermore, CRP values demonstrated no significant differences after ACTH injection, remaining within the values reported for animals without inflammation (<10 µg/L) (Cerón et al., 2022). This finding leads further support to the hypothesis that the glucocorticoid effect, and not inflammation, is responsible for the observed increase in Hp. This would be consistent with the documented impact of corticosteroids on acute-phase proteins in other animal species. Support for this assertion can be found in the literature, for example, in the study by Caldin et al. (2009) on dogs with hyperadrenocorticism, which reported increases in Hp with CRP within the reference range.

Calprotectin concentrations showed a significant increase at 120 min following ACTH administration, consistent with prior research associating this biomarker with stress-induced inflammation (López-Martínez et al., 2023). Although ACTH is not a direct inducer of calprotectin, it stimulates cortisol release, which can indirectly influence gut inflammation. As described by Oligschlaeger et al. (2019), stress-induced cortisol may impair intestinal barrier function, alter microbiota composition, and contribute to inflammatory processes. While such effects are often associated with prolonged exposure, our findings suggest that, under certain conditions, acute and short-term ACTH stimulation can elicit measurable increases in calprotectin levels within two hours of administration.

Regarding redox status biomarkers, no significant changes were observed in CUPRAC levels following ACTH administration, suggesting that this biomarker may not respond to short-term endocrine stimulation. This finding is consistent with the observations reported by Rubio et al. (2021), who documented that CUPRAC levels did not change in the first hours after calves were subjected to management-related stressors such as weaning and regrouping, but peaked several days later. This could indicate that acute activation of the HPA axis does not necessarily translate into immediate oxidative or antioxidant changes. These observations suggest a temporal disconnect between stress hormone dynamics and redox system responses, at least at an acute stage.

No significant changes were observed in biomarkers related to immune function (ADA and TP), or general metabolism (urea and creatinine). The stability of these analytes indicates that short-term activation of the HPA axis does not necessarily translate into immediate alterations in these physiological systems. This finding is relevant for biomarker selection, as it highlights that not all salivary analytes commonly associated with immunity or metabolism are suitable indicators of acute endocrine activation.

The absence of significant changes in the saline-treated control group indicated that repeated saliva sampling did not induce measurable stress responses, reinforcing the suitability of saliva as a non-invasive matrix for longitudinal assessment. On the other hand, the ACTH group showed significantly higher concentrations of endocrine stress markers (such as cortisol, cortisone, and TEA) and acute-phase proteins (such as Hp and calprotectin) after the injection than the control group. However, no differences in markers of sympathetic activation, immunity or general metabolism were observed between the groups. This could indicate that ACTH induces a highly targeted and selective biochemical response in saliva.

The main study limitation is a relatively modest sample size (n = 11 per group). Although this number is higher than that employed in other research utilising the ACTH administration model in pigs (with four injected animals and two controls) (Wolf et al., 2020), further research with a larger cohort would allow for the validation of these findings and the exploration of potential individual differences in response to ACTH. Another possible limitation is the duration of follow-up, which in our case was mainly focused on evaluating the short-term effect.

Overall, it can be concluded that in our experimental conditions, the administration of ACTH in pigs produces significant increases in cortisol and cortisone, indicating an activation of the HPA axis. However, it does not produce significant changes in analytes related to the adrenergic system, such as CgA and sAA, or oxytocin, a biomarker associated with positive welfare. In addition, it produces an increase in Hp and calprotectin but not changes in other markers of inflammation and the immune system, such as CRP or ADA. These data contribute to a better understanding of the physiological response of saliva components to ACTH, which is one of the major mechanisms involved in acute stress.
