Indicator-based assessment of social sustainability in urban water management across contrasting governance contexts
Authors: Raid Alrowais, Rukhshanda Rehman, Muhammad Tariq Bashir, Bandar A. Alwushayh, Nadir Mutaza, Md. Munir Hayet Khan, Wassef Ounaies
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Social sustainability is often insufficiently addressed in urban water management, particularly regarding equity, inclusion, and governance processes, which constrain progress toward resilient and socially just urban water systems. This study introduces and applies an indicator-based, framework-driven analytical approach to operationalize social sustainability in urban water management. Four principal dimensions—awareness, water-use practices, equity, and inclusion are assessed using a structured semi-quantitative scoring system, complemented by qualitative interpretation. The framework is implemented in two contrasting urban contexts, Peshawar (Pakistan) and Al-Jouf (Saudi Arabia), to investigate how varying socio-economic, climatic, and governance conditions influence social sustainability outcomes within a unified analytical structure. Descriptive statistics, radar-based visualization, sustainability gap assessment, and scenario-based sensitivity analysis are employed to evaluate indicator performance and relative influence. The aggregated findings indicate that the composite social sustainability index achieves approximately 38% of its normalized theoretical maximum, reflecting moderate to low social sustainability within the assessed framework. Awareness and water-use practices demonstrate moderate indicator performance, whereas equity and inclusion consistently exhibit weak outcomes across both contexts. Sensitivity analysis reveals that hypothetical enhancements in equity and inclusion produce significantly greater improvements in overall index performance (approximately 18–22%) compared to similar changes in awareness or behavioral indicators, underscoring the predominant role of governance-related dimensions in the model. Rather than highlighting numerical disparities between cities, the comparative analysis uncovers structurally similar constraints on social sustainability across cities with divergent development and institutional settings. The results underscore that while improvements in awareness and individual behavior are necessary, they are insufficient to achieve socially sustainable urban water management without equity-focused governance reforms and inclusive institutional mechanisms. By rendering social sustainability dimensions analytically explicit and comparable, the framework facilitates more informed governance reflection and establishes a basis for future empirical integration and policy evaluation in urban water systems.

Introduction

Social sustainability has emerged as a critical concern in urban water management (UWM), reflecting a growing emphasis on developing sustainable water systems (SWS) that integrate social participation, equity, and community engagement alongside technical frameworks. Previous research has primarily addressed the challenges faced by urban water systems that disproportionately affect low-income populations, underscoring the necessity of community involvement as a fundamental component of SWS1,2. Numerous studies indicate that community participation enhances legitimacy, trust, and adaptive capacity in UWM, particularly when communities are engaged from the outset of planning and decision-making processes3–5. Participatory tools such as citizen science, co-production models, and community-driven monitoring initiatives are increasingly recognized for their potential to empower local stakeholders and advance governance objectives6–8. However, operationalizing community engagement remains challenging. The literature documents that participation is often restricted to consultative roles, failing to address underlying power asymmetries and community heterogeneity9–11. Marginalized populations frequently have limited influence over decisions directly affecting water access and service quality12,13. These findings emphasize that, while community engagement is widely endorsed, its effectiveness is shaped by governance structures, institutional commitment, and recognition of social diversity. Therefore, social sustainability in urban water management requires mechanisms that ensure engagement translates into equitable outcomes.

Equitable access to water resources is widely recognized as a foundational element of social sustainability, yet recent studies highlight persistent disparities linked to socio-economic status, gender, and spatial marginalization14–16. Research focusing on informal settlements and low-income urban areas reveals that inadequate infrastructure, informal water markets, and socio-political exclusion continue to exacerbate inequities in water service distribution17–19. In response, scholars increasingly advocate for justice-based frameworks that incorporate distributive, procedural, and recognition justice into urban water governance analyses20,21. These approaches emphasize fair resource allocation, inclusive decision-making, and acknowledgment of historically marginalized groups. Governance structures play a pivotal role in shaping equity outcomes. Evidence suggests that multilevel governance and collaborative models enhance resilience and inclusivity when institutional and community coordination is effectively managed22,23. Community-based governance and co-management arrangements have demonstrated positive impacts on access and social empowerment, particularly when supported by enabling policy frameworks24,25. Nevertheless, several studies caution that these models are susceptible to elite capture, weak accountability, and insufficient institutional capacity, which may perpetuate rather than reduce existing inequalities10. These findings underscore the need for governance institutions that explicitly address power dynamics and institutional fragmentation.

Policy frameworks are increasingly perceived as decisive tools for promoting social sustainability in UWM. The empirical data support that adaptive, inclusive, and coherent policies can increase equity in access, more intensive community involvement, and performance over time of the system14,22,26. In contrast, research also shows that a lack of proper application of policies frequently leads to an inability to meet local demands, causing dissatisfaction and discrimination, as well as a violation of rights12. These results indicate that policy effectiveness is based not only on technical design but is also likely to be influenced by social responsiveness and institutional coordination. The use of technological innovations (innovative water systems, digital twins, and participatory data platforms) in facilitate socially sustainable water governance is also discussed in recent literature27,28. The technologies can also be used to improve transparency, enable stakeholder cooperation, and allow data-driven decision-making in alignment with the needs of the communities29. Nevertheless, some studies warn that solutions based on technology might become the support of inequalities as the problems of digital access, technical capacity, and accountability of the governing structure are not solved30. Regardless of the increased interest in these methods, the literature tends to consider policy, technology, and social equity as distinct fields instead of developing such integrated strategies as socially sustainable UWM.

Evidence from diverse urban settings demonstrates that inclusive and participatory governance arrangements can substantially improve social outcomes in water management when effectively institutionalized. In Medellín, Colombia, integrated governance platforms linking municipal utilities, local government agencies, and community organizations have enabled more equitable service expansion and tariff structuring in informal and marginalized neighborhoods31. This example illustrates how coordination across institutional levels can address social exclusion in service provision. Similarly, Singapore’s Public Utilities Board has implemented long-term public engagement and communication strategies that combine education, behavioral incentives, and stakeholder consultation32. These initiatives reinforce water conservation practices and sustain high levels of institutional trust and accountability. Despite differences in political context, institutional capacity, and socio-economic conditions, these cases collectively show that governance models prioritizing inclusion, participation, and accountability can significantly influence the social dimensions of urban water systems. Furthermore, these examples suggest that improvements in awareness and individual behavior are most effective when supported by governance structures that facilitate sustained participation and equitable decision-making, rather than relying solely on voluntary or informational measures.

A comprehensive review of the literature on equity, governance, and community engagement in UWM reveals that while the individual impacts of these components are well documented, a significant gap remains regarding their interconnections, as illustrated in Fig. 1. The studies referenced in Fig. 1 primarily examine these factors independently, offering limited analysis of the relationships among policy effectiveness, community engagement, and equity in social sustainability, as noted by Koebele et al.22 and Azizi et al.15. This fragmented approach constrains holistic evaluation and limits the ability to assess trade-offs between efficiency and equity. Additionally, social sustainability outcomes often lack standardized and functional indicators, resulting in reliance on qualitative or context-specific case studies with limited comparability33,34. Furthermore, while technological and governance innovations are increasingly promoted, their alignment with social justice principles and institutional realities, particularly in developing urban contexts, remains insufficiently explored.

This study responds to these gaps by developing and applying an indicator-based analytical framework that operationalizes social sustainability in urban water management through four interrelated dimensions: awareness, water-use practices, equity, and inclusion. Rather than advancing a new theory or producing confirmatory empirical measurements, the contribution of this work lies in the structured translation of widely discussed social sustainability concepts into a transparent, semi-quantitative assessment architecture. The framework integrates standardized scoring, aggregation logic, visual diagnostics, and exploratory sensitivity analysis to support systematic examination of how different social dimensions contribute to overall sustainability performance. The framework is applied comparatively to two contrasting urban contexts, Peshawar (Pakistan) and Al-Jouf (Saudi Arabia), to demonstrate its analytical applicability across differing governance, socio-economic, and climatic settings under a consistent evaluative structure. This comparative application is intended to illustrate common structural patterns and diagnostic insights rather than to establish statistically differentiated outcomes between cities. By emphasizing operational clarity, internal consistency, and interpretive transparency, the study provides a transferable tool for diagnosing social sustainability gaps and exploring governance-related sensitivities in urban water systems. The framework is designed as a foundation for further empirical refinement, expanded data collection, and future confirmatory analysis, rather than as a definitive measurement of social sustainability outcomes.

Methodology

Study area and comparative context

This study examines two contrasting urban settings, Peshawar (Pakistan) and Al-Jouf (Saudi Arabia), to enable a comparative analysis of urban social sustainability in water management across diverse socio-economic, climatic, and governance contexts (Fig. 2). Figure 2 was produced using QGIS version 3.34 (https://qgis.org) and publicly available open-source geographic data. These cities were deliberately selected to facilitate evaluation of social responses within urban water systems under different development trajectories and institutional arrangements, using a consistent analytical framework. Peshawar is among the fastest-growing metropolitan areas in northwestern Pakistan, characterized by high population density, rapid urbanization, and significant demands on water infrastructure. Urbanization, aging distribution networks, and limited institutional capacity have resulted in intermittent water supply, spatial disparities in service provision, and increased dependence on groundwater resources. Key social challenges in Peshawar’s water management include unequal access to clean drinking water, affordability concerns for low-income populations, and insufficient community participation in water-related decision-making. In recent years, public awareness of water scarcity and conservation has increased, largely due to persistent service unreliability and climate-induced stress. However, governance remains highly centralized, restricting inclusive and participatory management strategies. Consequently, Peshawar exemplifies the challenges faced by rapidly developing cities in the Global South, which are susceptible to a range of social, infrastructural, and governance issues in urban water management.

Al-Jouf is located in an arid region of northern Saudi Arabia, where water scarcity is a defining environmental constraint. The city’s water supply relies primarily on non-conventional resources, centralized planning, and regulated distribution systems, reflecting a policy-driven approach to water security. While Al-Jouf experiences fewer service disruptions compared to many urban areas in developing economies, social sustainability challenges persist, including limited stakeholder participation, centralized decision-making, and inequitable access for certain population groups. Water scarcity is widely acknowledged, shaped by the arid climate and a longstanding emphasis on conservation, with water consumption patterns influenced by regulatory measures and pricing mechanisms rather than supply instability. The inclusion of both Peshawar and Al-Jouf in this study broadens the analytical scope, capturing the dynamics of social sustainability across varying governance capacities, climatic conditions, and socio-economic contexts. Peshawar represents a rapidly growing, infrastructure-constrained urban system, whereas Al-Jouf exemplifies a resource-limited but institutionally robust environment. Together, these cases provide a robust basis for identifying context-specific challenges and extracting transferable insights for urban water management strategies globally.

The selection of Peshawar and Al-Jouf is intended not to produce statistically contrasting results, but to assess the analytical sensitivity of the proposed social sustainability framework across diverse governance and urban water management contexts. These cities differ systematically in contextual dimensions relevant to the selected indicators, such as governance structures, infrastructure reliability, drivers of water scarcity, and institutional approaches to public engagement. These factors are expected to shape social sustainability pathways in distinct ways within the framework. For instance, infrastructure stress and intermittent service in Peshawar are likely to influence awareness and water-use practices through direct experiences of scarcity. Conversely, centralized governance and regulatory control in Al-Jouf are anticipated to affect inclusion and participatory opportunities, even with relatively stable service provision. Applying a consistent indicator structure and aggregation method to both cities enables the framework to determine whether contextual differences result in distinct social sustainability profiles or if common structural constraints prevail. Thus, the comparative analysis serves a diagnostic function, evaluating how awareness, behavior, equity, and inclusion respond to contrasting conditions within a unified assessment framework. The primary analytical aim is to examine the performance of contextually distinct urban systems within a consistent evaluative structure, rather than to maximize inter-city differentiation.

Methodological framework and research design

This research analyzes social sustainability in urban water management systems using a structured, framework-based design. The adopted framework defines social sustainability as a multidimensional construct shaped by equity, community engagement, policy effectiveness, and governance practices. Rather than relying solely on infrastructure-oriented or technical evaluations, this approach emphasizes the establishment of institutional and social processes to address water management challenges in urban areas. Multiple analytical stages are implemented within a systematic and transparent workflow, facilitating the assessment of social sustainability indicators as illustrated in Figs. 3 and 4a,b. Figure 3 presents the complete research design and evaluation process, while Fig. 4a details the evaluation of social sustainability, including factors, sub-factors, and the weighting of each contributing element.

Figure 4b illustrates the hierarchy of the social criterion, including relevant factors and sub-factors. A predefined criterion and scoring logic are synthesized and interpreted using an analytical, rather than experimental, framework. The framework highlights the significance of these factors in social sustainability, which is shaped by complex, interlinked, and context-dependent human and institutional dynamics. While the adopted framework aligns with previous studies35, existing literature typically evaluates the contribution of individual factors to city development, rather than their combined impact on urban water management. The methodology integrates both numerical and qualitative evaluations to ensure that phenomena related to social sustainability, such as participation, equity, and inclusion, are assessed in a comparable and structured manner.

The research design follows sequential steps, including framework development, indicator selection, data analysis, and synthesis of results (Table 1). Each stage is logically connected to ensure methodological coherence and reproducibility. Visual workflow representations clarify the analysis process and enhance comprehension. By systematically linking social indicators to broader governance and policy regimes, the design enables analysis that moves beyond descriptive assessment. The framework-based methodology ensures consistent evaluation of social sustainability while remaining adaptable to context-specific variations. This balance is crucial in urban water management research due to significant variability in social conditions across regions. The methodological framework thus provides a robust foundation for rigorous, transparent, and consistent exploration of social sustainability, aligning with current sustainability assessment practices.

The proposed framework distinguishes between its conceptual structure and empirical implementation. Conceptually, social sustainability is structured as a hierarchical construct with four dimensions: awareness, water-use practices, equity, and inclusion. These dimensions are selected for their relevance to governance and participation outcomes in urban water management. The conceptual architecture defines the assessment scope and the interrelationships among social dimensions but does not generate results. Empirically, the framework is implemented through a standardized, stepwise procedure. Each sub-factor is scored on a fixed 1–10 scale, based on structured secondary data and expert-informed stakeholder assessments, using predefined interpretative criteria (see Sect. 2.3). These criteria translate qualitative descriptions of awareness, behavior, access, and participation into numeric scores, ensuring consistency across cases. Indicator scores are normalized into sustainability status values using a predefined classification scheme (see Sect. 2.5), enabling comparability across dimensions. Dimension-level scores are aggregated using equal-weight arithmetic means to generate a composite social sustainability index. This aggregation method is intentionally simple and transparent to support reproducibility and avoid implicit weighting biases. Analytical synthesis includes descriptive statistics, radar-based visualization, and sustainability gap analysis to identify strengths and weaknesses across indicators. Sensitivity analysis is conducted as a scenario-based exercise, systematically varying indicator status values within predefined bounds to assess their influence on the composite index. These scenarios are designed to explore the internal responsiveness of the framework to changes in governance and behavior dimensions, rather than to serve as empirical predictions. Collectively, these steps operationalize the conceptual framework into a reproducible analytical workflow applicable across diverse urban contexts.

Data source and structure

This analysis utilizes structured and integrated secondary data designed to assess social sustainability characteristics within urban water management systems. The dataset comprises systematic data related to social awareness, stakeholder engagement, equity, inclusion, governance practices, and institutional performance (Table 2). Primary empirical data collection methods, such as household surveys, interviews, or administrative microdata, are not employed. Instead, a structured analytical scoring dataset has been developed to operationalize and demonstrate the proposed social sustainability framework. This dataset serves as an interpretive assessment matrix rather than an empirical observational dataset. Indicator scores are determined through expert-informed analytical judgment, drawing on documented characteristics of urban water systems in Peshawar and Al-Jouf, including published reports, policy documents, prior empirical studies, and established knowledge of governance and service provision. These sources guide the qualitative positioning of each indicator within predefined scoring ranges; however, no new primary measurements or respondent-level observations are generated. Each indicator receives a value on a fixed 1–10 scale according to standardized interpretative criteria (Table 2), which define how levels of awareness, water-use behavior, equity of access, and stakeholder inclusion are translated into numerical scores. The resulting dataset is a constructed analytical representation intended to support internal consistency, comparability across indicators, and reproducibility of the assessment procedure, rather than to estimate population-level parameters or empirical prevalence. Accordingly, the dataset should be regarded as an integral component of the methodological framework. Its primary function is to facilitate structured aggregation, visualization, and sensitivity analysis of social sustainability dimensions, rather than to provide empirical evidence of measured social outcomes. While the framework is intended for application to empirical datasets in future research, the current use serves as a methodological demonstration and diagnostic exercise. The dataset is organized hierarchically to reflect the conceptual framework of social sustainability criteria, factors, and sub-factors. Each component is defined with a clear operational description to ensure consistency in analysis. Aggregation at various levels is possible, enabling both intensive assessment and a comprehensive evaluation of social sustainability performance. This hierarchical structure enhances the ability to trace the connection between initial data inputs and final assessment outcomes. The dataset is informed by stakeholder expertise and situational knowledge of urban water systems. Given the subjective nature of social sustainability, the secondary data structure allows for controlled interpretation and reduces variability associated with ad hoc data collection. The data may be reused for further analysis, provided that the evaluation system and scoring methodology are clearly defined and consistently applied. To maintain analytical integrity, the dataset is considered part of the methodological framework rather than an independent empirical contribution. The focus remains on data interpretation, organization, and synthesis within the methodology. This approach positions the research contribution as an analytical integration of social sustainability dimensions, in contrast to the primary data generation typical of framework-based sustainability assessments.

Although expert-informed scoring improves structured comparability, it also introduces normative interpretation within predefined thresholds. To reduce bias, identical scoring criteria were applied consistently across all cases, and no context-specific adjustments were incorporated into the aggregation logic. This approach maintains the internal coherence of the framework, while recognizing that empirical validation will require integration of primary data in future applications.

Social sustainability dimensions and indicator selection

The selection of social sustainability dimensions and indicators is informed by an extensive review of recent literature on urban water governance and sustainability assessment. Social sustainability is structured through a framework encompassing key societal concerns, including social awareness, community involvement, equitable access, inclusion of marginalized groups, and institutional responsiveness (Table 3). These dimensions are designed to address both outcome- and process-oriented aspects of social sustainability. Indicators serve to operationalize abstract social concepts into measurable variables. Definitions and justifications are provided alongside each indicator to ensure consistent interpretation. Indicator selection is based on relevance to urban water contexts, sensitivity to governance and policy variables, and capacity to distinguish variations in social performance. Emphasis is placed on indicators that reflect stakeholder involvement, equity in service provision, and inclusiveness in decision-making. The framework is organized to comprehensively represent social sustainability themes without redundancy. Higher-level dimensions group indicators to facilitate analysis and aggregation. This structure enables both disaggregated examination of specific social issues and integrated evaluation of overall social sustainability performance. The research concept and indicator selection logic are grounded in established literature, ensuring conceptual robustness and methodological clarity. The indicators provide a systematic approach for linking social theory to applied evaluation, thereby enabling meaningful assessment of social sustainability in urban water management systems.

To operationalize abstract social sustainability concepts, each dimension was defined using a rule-based scoring rubric to minimize subjectivity and ensure consistency across cases. This process aligned qualitative characteristics of urban water governance with predefined numerical score ranges, rather than relying on direct empirical measurement. Each indicator was evaluated on a fixed 1–10 scale, anchored to descriptive performance thresholds: low scores (1–3) indicate minimal presence or effectiveness (such as limited awareness, highly unequal access, or absence of participatory mechanisms), mid-range scores (4–7) reflect partial or uneven implementation, and high scores (8–10) denote widespread presence, institutionalization, or effective functioning of the respective social dimension. These anchors were established in advance and applied uniformly across both cities. Indicator scores were determined through structured interpretation of documented urban water conditions, governance arrangements, and policy practices reported in the literature and official sources. For instance, awareness scores are based on the prevalence of public communication on water scarcity, evidence of conservation-oriented behavior, and recognition of water-related risks. Equity scores reflect disparities in service reliability, affordability, and spatial coverage, while inclusion scores are determined by the presence, formality, and influence of stakeholder participation mechanisms in water-related decision-making, rather than their mere existence. To maintain internal consistency, all indicators were assessed using the same interpretative criteria and scoring thresholds across cases, regardless of local context. While contextual differences inform the qualitative justification for a score, they do not alter the scoring logic. This approach ensures that score differences reflect actual differences in indicator performance, not variations in interpretation or evaluative standards. The resulting indicator set provides a transparent and comparable analytical representation of social sustainability dimensions, suitable for aggregation, visualization, and sensitivity analysis within the framework.

In short, the composite index construction is deterministic and rule-based. As the framework does not rely on sampled observations or stochastic variation, inferential statistical measures such as confidence intervals, hypothesis testing, or significance levels are not applicable. Consequently, the analytical outputs represent structured comparative positions within the constructed assessment space rather than probabilistic estimates of empirical variability.

Analytical procedure and synthesis workflow

The analysis process is carried out according to a stepwise synthesis workflow, which is aimed at guaranteeing transparency, consistency, and reproducibility. First, the evaluation processes of social sustainability indicators apply in practice that allow converting the dataset inputs into indicator-based values of performance (Table 4). These values are standardized positions of social sustainability in various aspects of the framework. Structure-synthesis rules are then used to aggregate results at the indicator level within their respective dimensions. The process enables strengths and weaknesses to be identified in the components of social sustainability and traceability to be maintained to the underlying indicators. The aggregation logic is specified directly to eliminate the subjectivity of the bias and provide the reliability of the analysis across analytical levels. The synthesis process ends by summarizing the results at the dimension level into a comprehensive picture of social sustainability. Such a combined assessment gives the possibility to obtain a holistic interpretation without excluding the possibility of studying individual dimensions. The use of visual representation to aid comprehensibility and increase the ease of result interpretation is supported by the use of workflow diagrams and hierarchical models. Internal consistency and logical coherence are considered throughout the analytical process. Uncertainty in social assessments is also taken into account by the consideration of sensitivity. The workflow is made to be flexible, which can be replicated or extended to new research on social sustainability in urban water systems. Such a systematic synthesis technique guarantees that the methodological contribution of the study has analytical rigor and an integrative point of view.

Score ranges and corresponding status categories used in this framework are intended as heuristic interpretive guides rather than empirically calibrated representations of population coverage or service provision levels. While numerical ranges are employed to facilitate aggregation, visualization, and comparative interpretation, they are not validated against administrative records, utility statistics, or household-level service coverage data. As such, no direct correspondence is assumed between indicator scores and the proportion of the population experiencing a given level of service, inclusion, or awareness. Status categories are therefore used to express relative performance positions within the constructed assessment space, allowing indicators and dimensions to be interpreted consistently across cases without implying quantitative population-level measurement. This approach supports analytical clarity and internal consistency while avoiding unsubstantiated claims of empirical calibration.

The sustainability status categories presented in Table 4 serve as heuristic interpretive bands that facilitate aggregation and visualization. These categories are not empirically calibrated against service coverage statistics or population-level survey data. Consequently, the classification system is designed to enable consistent comparative interpretation rather than to indicate measured proportions of affected populations.

Result and discussion

Assessment of social sustainability performance in urban water systems

The results of the social sustainability performance indicate that urban water systems have moderate performance in general, and the aggregated social sustainability score is 7.71, which equals around 38% of the theoretical achievable social sustainability level (Table 5). This value represents a relative position within the constructed index space and should not be interpreted as an empirical estimate of population-level social sustainability. Because the index is based on an analytical scoring construct rather than empirical measurement, percentage values are interpreted as relative index positions rather than observed social conditions. This finding shows that the social aspect is significantly underdeveloped in comparison to its potential level to contribute to the sustainable management of urban water. This analysis indicates that there is an evident imbalance in the extent of alterations between social sub-factors, with the inadequate progress of awareness and behavioral practices not being able to compensate for the minor areas of its failures in equity and inclusion. Disparities in performance (especially) have been central to the recent urban water literature, which cites social sustainability as the most challenging dimension to operationalise because it relies not only on technical solutions but also on the quality of governance and the capacity of an institution17,22. Such uneven distribution of social benefits plays an essential role in the decline in the social sustainability index, indicating that aggregate performance is susceptible to the lack of equity-based indicators. Similar results are detailed in the recent case-based and comparative studies, where the lack of equity in access and coverage of the marginalized groups is present, even despite the increase in public awareness of the subject and the shift to conservation behavior2,36.

According to the responses of stakeholders, cities experience a process whereby urban dwellers are becoming more aware of water scarcity and practice an orientation towards conservation, even though such efforts are more responsive than being integrated into the inclusive governance framework. This finding correlates with the literature that highlights that behavioral change generated by awareness can frequently occur due to scarcity, unreliability of services, or economic constraints, and not as an active sustainability planning37,38. Consequently, there are weak and lopsided socially sustainable outcomes. The sustainability gap analysis also contributes to the understanding of a significant difference between the present and desirable state of social sustainability, especially in the field of equity and inclusion. The barriers of governance that arise in ascertaining limitations to the entire performance include restrictions in the form of inadequate stakeholder participation systems, poor institutional coordination, and ineffective policy implementation. Other recent articles also claim that the weaknesses of policy delivery and social awareness conversion are restricted by the fragmentation of governance structures and top-down policymaking22,26. The study’s findings affirm that the interplay between behavioral variables and structural governing conditions determines the social sustainability of urban water systems. Although moderate awareness and water-use behavior are a good starting point, social sustainability can be achieved more effectively through targeted policy changes, inclusive decision-making models, and equity-centered governance reforms. The results support the recent literature demands of interdisciplinary social-institutional strategies that go beyond individual behavior change to systemic change in urban water management39,40.

The results highlight a clear relationship between awareness, individual water-use behavior, and broader social sustainability outcomes. While awareness and behavioral indicators perform moderately within the framework, their contribution to overall social sustainability is constrained when considered in isolation. This pattern suggests that awareness and behavior function primarily as enabling conditions rather than as direct drivers of structural change in urban water systems.

Behavioral change can contribute to social sustainability when it is institutionally recognized, aggregated, and translated into formal decision-making processes. For example, sustained conservation behavior or collective demand for improved services may create social pressure that legitimizes governance reform, supports policy adoption, or strengthens accountability mechanisms. However, in the absence of inclusive institutions, participatory channels, or equitable access frameworks, individual behavior changes tend to remain fragmented and have limited capacity to influence system-level outcomes.

The sensitivity analysis reinforces this interpretation by showing that hypothetical improvements in equity and inclusion produce substantially greater gains in composite social sustainability than comparable improvements in awareness or behavior. This does not imply that awareness and behavior are unimportant, but rather that their effectiveness is mediated by governance structures that determine whose behaviors are recognized, whose voices are heard, and how social demand is incorporated into policy and service provision. In contexts characterized by centralized decision-making or uneven service access, behavior change may reduce short-term stress on water systems without addressing underlying social sustainability deficits. Taken together, these findings suggest a feedback relationship in which awareness and behavior can support structural reform only when embedded within inclusive governance arrangements. Equity and inclusion act as transmission mechanisms that convert individual and collective behaviors into durable institutional change. Without such mechanisms, behavior-oriented interventions risk reinforcing existing inequalities or producing symbolic rather than transformative outcomes. This interaction underscores the importance of aligning behavior-based strategies with governance reforms to achieve socially sustainable urban water management.

Sub-factor analysis of awareness and water usage practices

The sub-factor analysis indicates that the levels of awareness of water-related problems and of water-use practices are comparatively stronger elements of social sustainability in the evaluated urban water systems, but their performance is not the best; it is still moderate (Fig. 5). These findings indicate that a significant proportion of urban dwellers act responsibly regarding water use, suggesting that some awareness programs and behavior change initiatives partially succeed.

Based on qualitative input of the responses by the stakeholders, people are generally more aware of water stress, intermittency, and other publicized messages of water scarcity, not because of formal educational or participatory initiatives, but because of lived experience. This fact is consistent with current literature that points out that awareness within the context of urban water is frequently realised in a reactive manner, where it is not the conditions of crisis or disruption of services that trigger it, but institutional involvement that is long-term41,42. Consequently, the level of awareness by socio-economic categories is not evenly spread. It does not always lead to the cohesion of the collective action or the commitment in behavioral terms. The fact that the water usage practices have improved comparatively means that people are ready to change their consumption patterns either because there is scarcity or cost pressure. Similar literature alerts in varied urban settings also record that households implement water-efficient strategies, including decreased use, reuse, and priority in those they view as essential to react to perceived danger or uncertainty in supply1. In the analysis, however, it seems that such practices are usually limited to structural failures, such as the untrustworthy infrastructures, the absence of access to water-efficient technologies, and no incentives for ongoing conservation.

Although there was moderate performance on both sub-factors, the outcomes show that there is an urgent severity of misconnection between awareness, behavior, and the overall results of social sustainability. Lack of inclusive governance systems denies the informed and conservation-oriented citizens a chance to participate in the decision-making. The finding backs up the discussions in the literature that awareness and individual behavior change, although needed, are not adequate, without participatory platforms that can enable communities to influence water management policies and service provision43–45. Sub-factor analysis suggests that the awareness and use of water practices offer a legally imperfect contribution to social sustainability. Their results support current studies that advocate integrated interventions involving institutional processes that entrench awareness campaigns, increase access to water-saving technology, and connect the change of individual behavior with participatory integrated governance. In the absence of such integration, the benefits of increasing awareness and water-use practices may not lead to sustainable and socially equal results in cities with water systems25.

Equity and inclusion gaps in urban drinking water systems

The equity and inclusion analysis has shown the most significant areas of weakness in the socio-sustainability of urban drinking water systems (Fig. 6). Such findings underline that a small percentage of the population is found to exhibit fair access to water services or significant participation in water-related decision-making. The significantly lower scores in equity and inclusion in comparison with others, in turn, in contrast to awareness and the consumption of water, as they were found to perform moderately, reveal clearly that the benefits are not evenly distributed among the stakeholders, and that the implications of addressing the needs of a tiny group of participants are limited. Additional qualitative data on stakeholder contributions show existing inequality in access to safe, reliable, and affordable drinking water, especially among people of color and the deprived groups. People often stated that access to water, quality of services, and their affordability in different neighborhoods are pretty different, which supports the existence of social and spatial disparities. These observations can be compared to the recent literature, which records inequitable service delivery and exclusionary forms of governance as among the prevalent problems in city water systems, particularly in fast-growing cities46. These kinds of inequities destroy confidence in institutions and create weaknesses in the social acceptability of water management exercises.

Inclusion-related failures are especially salient, as they are manifested in the low involvement of stakeholders in the planning, monitoring, and decision-making. The findings indicate that the water governance system has not yet transitioned to decentralization, and the community has few avenues to engage in the governance except through information distribution. Similar tendencies are mentioned in recent studies, claiming that formal participation activities tend to fail to empower communities and introduce local knowledge to the decisions47–49. They lack inclusive platforms that can help the marginalized groups bring up issues, hence entrenching inequitable consequences. Comprehensively, the equity and inclusion inequities are significant limitations of the social sustainability of urban drinking water systems. The results are that the increase in awareness and individual behavior cannot compensate for the structural inequalities established in governance and service delivery systems. In line with recent academic work, the findings highlight that the development of socially sustainable urban water will take the intentional equilibrium-based policies, prioritized investments in underserved regions, and institutional reforms with the focus on inclusive participation and procedural fairness10.

Sustainability gap analysis and implications for policy and governance

The sustainability gap analysis is an in-depth analysis of the comparison between the present situation in terms of social sustainability and the desired benchmark conditions, which is found to have a massive amount of deficit in more than one aspect. On a quantitative basis, the general level of social sustainability of 38% shows that there is a big gap between the present state and the desired level of social sustainability, and that the most significant gaps are characterized by equilibrium and inclusion (Fig. 6). As awareness and water-use habits are rated at moderate levels, low-status attributes related to equity and inclusion disproportionately undermine the overall results. This imbalance has shown that average performance does not define social sustainability; rather, it is the weaker parts of the system. The gap analysis shows that systemic, minuscule changes in individual behavior or awareness will not help close gaps. Qualitative data indicate that institutional barriers, especially disjointed institutional responsibilities, poor coordination of the agencies, and feeble implementation of equity-focused policies, are the leading factors in perpetuating these gaps. The current literature also focuses on the fact that social goals cannot be operationalized successfully when it comes to urban water management due to the lack of institutional coherence and policy fragmentation50–52. This usually leads to the failure of equitable and inclusive results due to well-intentioned policies.

In terms of policy, the findings highlight the necessity to shed the technocratic solutions and shift to governance solutions that formally focus on social sustainability. According to the literature reviewed, cities that have systems of integrated governance and participatory decision-making have a more substantial likelihood of minimizing the existence of sustainability gaps and positively impacting the social outcomes2. The findings of this paper support these insights, implying that the effectiveness of a policy should not be attributed to design only, but also the capacity to implement and the involvement of stakeholders. There are also significant implications of the sustainability gap analysis for long-term urban resiliency. Increased vulnerability to shocks, i.e., water shortage and climate variability, is caused by persistent inequities and exclusion. In line with the latest studies, the results indicate that the engagement of the policy solution, the mechanisms of collective governance, and regular monitoring of the social outcomes are necessary to mitigate the social sustainability gaps53,54. It is critical to address these gaps, not only to achieve social justice but also to have a comprehensive sustainability and resilience of urban water systems.

Comparative analysis of social sustainability in Peshawar and Al-Jouf

The comparative study of Peshawar and Al-Jouf offers a good understanding of the way social sustainability in urban water management is exhibited in two different socio-economic, climatic, and governance scenarios, as shown in Fig. 7. Using the social sustainability evaluation framework to analyze both cities makes it possible to compare the performance of these two cities in a systematic way and the distinctive drivers and constraints that are specific to the context. Quantitatively, the overall social sustainability of both cities is moderate, with its primary limitation being the inadequacies in equity and inclusion as opposed to awareness and water usage patterns. Although the overall level of performance is not dramatically different in the two urban settings, the factors that lead to these differences are radically different, as revealed by the aggregate scores. Rapid urbanization, population pressure, and infrastructure stress are strong influences on social sustainability performance in Peshawar. Average awareness scores and the necessities to consume water imply that the number of residents is becoming more aware of the water shortage and tends to make consumption-conservation-driven decisions, which are primarily due to unpredictable supply and service availability.

Nevertheless, scores on equity and inclusion are low, which means that access to reliable drinking water is not uniform across the neighborhoods, and marginalized and low-income populations are severely impacted. The qualitative information indicates a reduction of community involvement in water management, with decision-making being centralized and responsive. The traits are typical of those found in developing urban centers that are rapidly growing and where the institutional capacity is unable to cope with the growth rate.

On the contrary, Al-Jouf has to work in an environment with limited resources but organization, in which water management depends on the arid climatic conditions and centralized planning models. The moderate scores of awareness and water usage practices in Al-Jouf indicate the overall awareness of the water shortage and meeting the norms of water consumption regulation. In contrast to Peshawar, service unreliability in Al-Jouf determines the behavioral compliance less and policy compliance and cultural adjustment to scarcity more. However, the status of equity and inclusivity is fragile, because there are still inequalities in access to specific population groups, and the possibility of the population to engage in the governance of water resources is still low. There is qualitative evidence indicating that, though service provision is more predictable, the processes involved in governance are mostly top-down and limit the involvement of stakeholders.

The comparative analysis reveals that different quantitative results may be generated from different structural conditions. In Peshawar, the issues of social sustainability hand in hand can be linked to the infrastructural shortage and unequal distribution of socio-economic aspects. In contrast, in Al-Jouf, they can be connected to the centralization of governance and the lack of participatory mechanisms. It is the significance of analysis beyond performance scores and into the contextual processes through which social sustainability results are produced. Notably, the fact that both cities have been involved makes the study a substantial contribution to the research in relation to urban water management. Peshawar is a developing, high-growth urban environment, and Al-Jouf is a resource-constrained yet policy-based environment. Taken together, they demonstrate that social sustainability is not dictated by water availability or economic capacity only, but the ability of the governing systems to transform awareness and behavior into accessible and equitable results. The results of the comparison underscore both the generalizable lessons (i.e., the necessity of inclusive governance and policy whose implementation is based on the equity criteria) and the fact that the interventions must be context-specific. The comparison, therefore, will be a strong source of evidence that one may employ to realize the socially sustainable city water management strategy in different environments worldwide.

Although identical numerical scores are observed across several sub-factors in Peshawar and Al-Jouf, these values should not be interpreted as evidence of equivalent urban water conditions. Identical indicator scores observed across cities result from the standardized scoring logic embedded in the analytical framework, rather than from any presumption of contextual equivalence. These scores denote relative performance categories based on predefined interpretative thresholds. Consequently, similar values indicate analogous structural positioning within the assessment model, even if the underlying causal factors differ significantly between urban contexts. Subsequently, the framework evaluates relative performance positions within a standardized analytical structure rather than measuring context-specific causal processes. Different contextual drivers may therefore converge toward similar indicator outcomes when they produce comparable structural constraints within the assessed dimensions. In Peshawar, lower equity and inclusion scores primarily emerge from infrastructure stress, rapid urban expansion, and uneven service distribution, which limit reliable access and participatory engagement. In contrast, in Al-Jouf, similar scores arise from centralized governance arrangements and limited participatory mechanisms despite comparatively stable service provision. While the causal pathways differ, both contexts occupy similar positions within the framework because equity and inclusion remain institutionally constrained. The comparative exercise, therefore, highlights functional similarity in social sustainability outcomes rather than contextual equivalence, reinforcing the diagnostic purpose of the framework: to identify structurally shared governance challenges across diverse urban environments.

Integrated statistical analysis of social sustainability indicators

The combined analysis presented in Table 5 synthesizes findings from descriptive statistics, radar chart analysis, and sensitivity analysis to provide a comprehensive assessment of the social sustainability performance of urban water systems in Peshawar and Al-Jouf. Descriptive data reveal similar patterns in both cities, with awareness of water-related issues and water usage behavior emerging as the strongest social indicators (Fig. 8). These results indicate a moderate to high degree of behavioral change, reflecting increased public awareness of water scarcity and a corresponding shift toward conservation-oriented practices. However, persistent deficiencies in equity and inclusion highlight ongoing structural inefficiencies that limit equitable access to water services and hinder effective stakeholder participation (Table 6). The overall social sustainability score, approximately 38% of the maximum possible, suggests that behavioral changes alone are insufficient to achieve socially sustainable urban water systems. Radar chart analysis further corroborates these findings, illustrating imbalances across social sustainability dimensions. The radar profiles for both cities are nearly identical, showing moderate extensions in awareness and water usage axes and pronounced contractions in equity and inclusion axes. The consistency in social sustainability patterns across both contexts suggests that these challenges are not unique to individual cities, despite differences in national environments, organizational structures, and climates. The radar analysis thus confirms that deficiencies in equity and inclusion are widespread obstacles in urban water management, aligning with literature indicating that distributive and procedural justice remain underdeveloped in most urban water systems.

The sensitivity analysis further quantifies the contributions of individual social indicators to overall sustainability outcomes, as detailed in Table 6. The observed imbalance indicates that the evaluation framework is more responsive to governance-related indicators than to those reflecting individual behavior. These results suggest that reforms targeting policy and institutional frameworks to promote equitable access and inclusion offer the most effective opportunities for improving social sustainability. While awareness and responsible water use provide a necessary foundation, long-term social sustainability in urban water systems primarily depends on addressing structural inequalities and governance constraints. The convergence of statistical, visual, and sensitivity-based evidence strengthens the validity of these conclusions and informs the prioritization of policy interventions and institutional reforms in both cities.

The sensitivity analysis was designed to assess the internal responsiveness of the composite social sustainability index to variations in individual dimensions, rather than to address empirical uncertainty. A deterministic, scenario-based approach was applied uniformly to both case studies, utilizing normalized status values instead of raw indicator scores. In each scenario, all indicators within a single dimension (awareness, water-use practices, equity, or inclusion) were simultaneously adjusted, while indicators in other dimensions remained at baseline values. Aggregation rules, weighting structures, and normalization procedures were held constant across scenarios. Dimension-level adjustments involved a fixed proportional change of ± 20% to normalized status values, constrained between 0 and 1. This adjustment range was chosen to represent a moderate yet plausible shift in dimension performance within the assessment framework. Following adjustment, dimension scores and the composite index were recalculated using the arithmetic aggregation method described in Sect. 2.6. Each scenario thus isolates the effect of a single dimension on the composite index, enabling comparative evaluation of the relative influence of each dimension on overall social sustainability. The sensitivity analysis serves as an exploratory diagnostic tool to assess structural dominance within the framework and does not constitute empirical improvement scenarios, forecasts, or policy outcomes.

The sensitivity analysis demonstrates that modifications to equity-related indicators result in the largest proportional changes in the composite social sustainability index, with simulated increases of approximately 18–22% within the analytical model. These results should not be interpreted as predictive evidence that equity reforms will produce equivalent outcomes in practice. Instead, they highlight the internal responsiveness of the assessment framework to governance-related dimensions, indicating that equity is a structurally significant variable within the model. Accordingly, the policy implications of this study are diagnostic, identifying areas where institutional interventions may yield comparatively greater improvements in index performance, while acknowledging that empirical validation would require context-specific implementation studies. The sensitivity scenarios serve as structural diagnostics of the framework, rather than as predictive policy simulations. Adjustments to equity and inclusion dimensions demonstrate internal leverage within the index architecture and do not represent empirical forecasts of governance reform outcomes. Consequently, the results reflect model responsiveness rather than the magnitude of real-world impacts.

Comparison to prior studies

Comparison with previous literature highlights the unique contribution and novelty of this research in advancing knowledge of social sustainability in urban water management, as summarized in Table 7. Existing studies have primarily examined social aspects such as equity, community involvement, or governance in isolation, often relying on qualitative evaluation, theoretical models, or analyses of individual cities. While these works provide valuable insights into social issues and policy shortcomings, they rarely integrate quantitative approaches that enable systematic comparison across diverse urban contexts. In contrast, the present study develops and applies a comprehensive, indicator-based model that incorporates awareness, water-use behavior, equity, and inclusion within a composite social sustainability index. Furthermore, unlike most prior research limited to a single national or institutional context, this study conducts a comparative analysis of two cities from different countries, thereby enhancing the generalizability of its findings. The inclusion of radar analysis, sustainability gap assessment, and sensitivity testing further distinguishes this work by explicitly quantifying the relative influence of governance-related indicators. Consequently, the research provides robust, policy-relevant evidence that equity and inclusion are critical determinants of social sustainability, addressing a significant gap in the current literature on urban water management.

Although previous studies have advanced empirical measurement, participatory assessment, and context-specific evaluation of social sustainability in urban water management, this study does not aim to supersede or empirically outperform these approaches. Its primary contribution is the structured operationalization and transparent aggregation of social sustainability dimensions that are often discussed but inconsistently assessed in the literature. The framework does not assert superior empirical generalizability or quantitative precision compared to existing studies; instead, it offers an analytically coherent structure that facilitates systematic comparison, visualization, and exploration of diagnostic sensitivity under clearly defined assumptions. Thus, this study serves to complement, rather than replace, prior empirical and context-specific research.

This study faces several significant limitations that affect the empirical interpretability of its findings. First, the assessment is based on a constructed, expert-informed analytical scoring dataset rather than on primary empirical observations or validated administrative data. Consequently, indicator scores and composite index values reflect relative analytical positions within the framework rather than actual measured social sustainability conditions. This restricts the ability to draw empirical inferences, make population-level conclusions, or conduct statistically generalizable comparisons between cities. Second, the analytical workflow is deterministic and does not incorporate stochastic variation or sampled data. As a result, inferential statistics and conventional measures of uncertainty are not applicable, and previous reporting inconsistencies highlight the need to align analytical outputs explicitly with their methodological foundations. Although these issues have been addressed through clarification and revision, they underscore the sensitivity of composite index construction to scoring assumptions and aggregation rules. Third, the lack of empirical calibration linking indicator scores to service coverage, participation rates, or governance outcomes limits the interpretability of numerical values and percentage expressions. These values should be regarded as heuristic indicators of relative performance rather than validated quantitative measurements. Collectively, these limitations reinforce the framework’s function as a diagnostic and exploratory tool rather than a replacement for empirically grounded social sustainability assessment.

The comparative application of the framework to Peshawar and Al-Jouf is designed to demonstrate analytical transferability and diagnostic sensitivity, rather than to produce statistically generalizable findings. Selecting two contrasting urban contexts enables the framework to be tested under different governance structures, service provision conditions, and socio-economic settings, while preserving analytical clarity and transparency. The comparison aims to illustrate how diverse contexts are interpreted within a unified social sustainability assessment structure, rather than to infer population-level differences or statistically test inter-city variation. Since indicator scores and composite indices are deterministically derived from a rule-based analytical framework, conventional statistical analyses are not applicable. The assessment does not utilize sampled observations or stochastic variation, and quantitative outputs represent relative positions within a constructed index space rather than empirical distributions. Therefore, the credibility of the analysis depends on transparency, internal consistency, and reproducibility of the scoring and aggregation logic, rather than on inferential statistics.

Research limitation and future research direction

The analysis is based on a constructed, expert-informed scoring dataset developed to operationalize the proposed framework rather than on primary empirical observations. Consequently, the resulting index values and percentage expressions represent analytical positions within the framework and should not be interpreted as empirical measurements of population coverage or social outcomes. While this limits empirical inference, it enables transparency, reproducibility, and diagnostic exploration of how different social sustainability dimensions influence composite performance. Future research could expand the application of the framework to a larger set of cities and integrate empirical datasets, such as household surveys, administrative service coverage statistics, or participatory scoring exercises. Such extensions would enable statistical analysis, cross-city comparison at scale, and empirical validation of indicator–outcome relationships. The present study provides a methodological foundation for these future efforts by establishing a transparent and transferable analytical structure for assessing social sustainability in urban water management. Despite such contributions, this research has certain limitations that must be considered and responded to in future studies. To begin with, the evaluation is based on a numerically scoring model in the form of a framework, based on structured secondary data and explanations by the stakeholders, as opposed to primary empirical surveys. This method increases the comparability and analytical consistency, but it might not adequately represent localized perception, understanding of informal practice, and fast-changing socio-political processes that contribute towards how urban water is governed.

Second, the chosen indicators, such as the awareness, water-use practices, equity, and inclusion, give a narrow but comprehensive depiction of social sustainability. There were also other pertinent dimensions, such as gender-exclusive effects, cultural norms, power distance, and informal water markets that were not explicitly modeled and could be incorporated to further streamline the evaluation. Third, the comparison was performed on two cities, which, despite their deliberate contrast in the context of the socio-economic and government business, limit the extrapolability of the results into different urban typologies. Moreover, the framework assumes the stability of weights of the factors across contexts, but the relative importance of social indicators can change in relation to the level of institutional development, political systems, and stressors of climatic activity. Future studies, in turn, must target an increase in the implementation of the framework in a larger number of cities in various parts of the world and development paths to increase the external validity. Studies with the time-series data would make possible the assessment of the dynamics of the social sustainability as a reaction to policy reforms, climate variability, and investments in infrastructure construction. The combination of primary data collection, including household surveys, participatory workshops, and interviews with marginalized populations, would further strengthen empirical resourcing and inclusiveness. In addition, this research may be extended in various ways in future research through a more detailed class of weighting schemes and techniques known as advanced decision-support tools, including multi-criteria decision analysis or system dynamics modeling, to offer more realistic depictions of complex social-institutional interactions. Lastly, associating social sustainability indicators with environmental and economic performance metrics would help present a more holistic approach to urban water management and offer more serious advice to policymakers who aim to achieve efficiency, resilience, and social justice.

Conclusions

The present research is a systematic assessment of social sustainability in urban water management applied to the combination of awareness, water-use behavior, equity, and inclusion in a cohesive analytical structure. By conducting a comparative evaluation of Peshawar and Al-Jouf, the study proved that the concept of social sustainability is not developed despite average degrees of civil enlightenment and the conscientious use of water. The quantitative findings indicated that the lack of equity and stakeholder inclusion has a profound impact on overall sustainability performance, whereas the qualitative findings identified governance fragmentation and centralized decision-making as the key limitations. The results substantiate the claim that achieving social sustainability is possible not only through behavioral change but also through structural reforms, inclusive governance systems, and policy implementation grounded in equity demands. The framework that has been studied enhances existing knowledge because it provides an indicator-based method that can be transferred and utilized to promote evidence-based decision-making in a variety of urban water management settings.

Reorganizing urban water governance with an emphasis on equity and inclusion is essential for achieving sustainable outcomes. The findings may assist policymakers in shifting away from technocratic and supply-driven governance models toward approaches that prioritize procedural fairness and stakeholder engagement. The framework is particularly valuable for rapidly urbanizing cities with limited institutional capacity, as it enables authorities to identify gaps in social sustainability and target governance reforms that address marginalized groups and inequalities in service delivery.

The indicator-based evaluation prepared within this study presents a worthy instrument of gauging the social performance of water policies beyond the technical performance estimates. With the help of this framework, the policymakers can observe the conversion of the policy tools to equitable access and participatory decisions. Its numerical character makes it possible to compare it across cities and time, supporting the adaptive policy design and enabling accountability through the facilitation of policy goals and their connections to quantifiable social sustainability outcomes.

By applying the same framework to two distinct socio-economic and governance contexts, this study demonstrates the universality of social sustainability evaluation techniques. The method can be extended to other cities globally to facilitate comparative analysis, identify systemic barriers, and generalize key lessons. This approach bridges the gap between localized case studies and the broader global discourse on sustainability in urban water management research.

This study underscores the necessity of integrating social dimensions into broader sustainability and resilience planning models. Urban planners and water utilities can utilize the proposed methodology to align infrastructural development with social justice considerations. By directly linking awareness, behavior, governance, and equity, the framework supports long-term planning strategies that foster system resilience, sustainability, and societal acceptance.

Future urban water management should be grounded in equity-based governance reforms, infrastructure improvements, and demand-management measures. Policymakers are encouraged to institutionalize inclusive participation processes, strengthen inter-agency coordination, and systematically monitor social sustainability using standardized reference frameworks. Targeted investments in underserved areas and transparent policymaking are necessary to address persistent sustainability gaps. Expanding the application of this framework to additional cities and incorporating longitudinal data will enable evaluation of changes over time. Implementing social sustainability principles within national water policy can significantly enhance urban resilience and support progress toward sustainable development goals.

The findings of this study have broader implications for urban water management beyond the specific cases analyzed. In various urban contexts, initiatives to enhance social sustainability frequently emphasize technical efficiency or awareness-based interventions, while fundamental structural issues related to equity and inclusion are often overlooked. The analytical results from this framework-based assessment indicate that governance dimensions, particularly equitable access and meaningful stakeholder inclusion, play a critical role in determining overall social sustainability performance. This suggests that global urban water strategies focused solely on behavioral change, without concurrent institutional reform, are unlikely to achieve lasting social sustainability outcomes.

For policymakers and water authorities, these findings highlight the necessity of explicitly integrating equity and inclusion into urban water governance frameworks. Policy measures should strengthen participatory decision-making mechanisms, enhance transparency and accountability in service provision, and prioritize underserved or marginalized populations in infrastructure planning and regulatory oversight. Rather than treating social considerations as secondary to technical planning, the results emphasize the need for governance structures that actively translate social priorities into operational and policy decisions.

The diagnostic insights provided by the framework suggest several policy-relevant directions. First, urban water governance reforms should advance from symbolic participation to institutionalized inclusion, ensuring that stakeholder engagement substantively influences planning, budgeting, and service delivery. Second, equity considerations—including affordability, spatial coverage, and service reliability—should be incorporated into performance monitoring systems alongside technical indicators. Third, awareness and behavior-based programs should be coordinated with governance mechanisms that enable collective behavioral change to inform regulatory adjustments and infrastructure investments. While these recommendations are illustrative rather than prescriptive, they demonstrate how the framework can support reflective and context-sensitive policy development.

This study identifies several directions for future research. First, applying the framework to a broader and more diverse set of cities would facilitate examination of how social sustainability dimensions differ across political, economic, and cultural contexts. Second, integrating primary empirical data—such as household surveys, interviews, or participatory workshops—would ground indicator scoring in localized perspectives and enable statistical analysis of social sustainability outcomes. Third, longitudinal applications of the framework could assess how changes in governance arrangements or policy interventions affect social sustainability trajectories over time. Finally, further research could refine indicator weighting schemes or investigate alternative aggregation methods to test the robustness of composite social sustainability assessments. Collectively, these avenues underscore the framework’s potential as a foundation for more empirically grounded, policy-relevant, and comparative research on social sustainability in urban water management.

In summary, this study demonstrates the analytical feasibility of operationalizing social sustainability in urban water management using a structured and transparent framework that integrates awareness, behavior, equity, and inclusion. Although the comparative application reveals structurally similar constraints in two contrasting urban contexts, these findings should be viewed as illustrative rather than empirically definitive. The study does not establish causal relationships or generalizable performance rankings; instead, it provides a diagnostic perspective for examining interactions among social dimensions within a unified assessment structure. By clarifying assumptions, aggregation logic, and interpretive boundaries, the framework serves as a foundation for future empirical refinement rather than as a final evaluative tool. Its main contribution is to facilitate structured reflection on governance-related social sustainability gaps and to enable more consistent comparison across studies when combined with robust empirical data. Thus, the paper advances methodological clarity and transparency while recognizing the need for further empirical validation.
