Association between platelet-to-hemoglobin ratio and contrast-induced acute kidney injury after percutaneous coronary intervention
Authors: Qiqiang Li, Leigang Tian, Lijian Li, Yingming Shi, Yujing Li, Linxiao Deng, Yong Liu, Kedong Han
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIn patients suffering from coronary artery disease, the measured platelet–hemoglobin ratio (PHR) is a key biomarker of prognosis. However, the association between PHR and contrast-induced acute kidney injury (CI-AKI) outcomes after percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) are unclear.MethodsIn a retrospective study, 26,479 patients (63.3 ± 11.5 years of age, 22.1% female) who underwent PCI were enrolled. CI-AKI was defined as serum creatinine levels above 0.3 mg/dL, exceeding the baseline by more than 50%, in the first 48 h following contrast exposure. The relationship between PHR and CI-AKI was clarified through univariate and multivariable logistic regression analyses.ResultsIn summary, CI-AKI occurred in 1,707 patients (6.4%) following PCI. The association of an increased PHR with elevated CI-AKI incidence was determined via multivariate logistic regression analysis (odds ratio for Q4 vs. Q1, 1.42; 95% confidence interval [95% CI], 1.20–1.68; P < 0.001). Restricted cubic spline analysis indicated a linear PHR–CI-AKI relationship (non-linearity P = 0.060).ConclusionA high PHR was strongly correlated with elevated CI-AKI incidence, indicating PHR as a factor independently associated with CI-AKI.

Introduction

As a common issue encountered during percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI), contrast-induced acute kidney injury (CI-AKI) is characterized by a marked reduction in kidney function triggered by iodinated contrast agents [1–4]. CI-AKI is the third highest cause of AKI acquired in a hospital setting, following reduced perfusion of the kidneys alongside drug-related injury to kidney function [5]. This condition is characterized by extended hospital stays, higher treatment expenses, and worsened mortality outcomes [4, 6], placing a heavy economic, physical, and psychological burden on patients, especially those undergoing PCI. Therefore, it is necessary to recognize at-risk CI-AKI patients for the optimization of prevention strategies and clinical management. Difficulties exist in the assessment of risk factors and identification of at-risk patients of developing CI-AKI [1, 3, 7]. Advanced age, diabetes mellitus (DM), heart failure, hypertension, and pre-existing renal dysfunction are reported CI-AKI risk factors; other factors that additionally contribute to the onset of this condition remain mostly unknown [1, 2, 7].

An increasing amount of research has demonstrated the prognostic capability of complete blood counts for the prediction of negative cardiovascular disease (CVD) outcomes [8, 9]. Related studies have associated a high circulating platelet count with poor prognosis in CVD [10–12], the related inflammatory response mechanism [13, 14], and platelet activation [15]. Similarly, low hemoglobin levels contribute to a poor CVD prognostic factor [16, 17] through worsening myocardial ischemia [18], elevated cardiac output, neurohormonal activation [19, 20], and adverse remodeling of the left ventricular [20, 21].

The platelet–hemoglobin ratio (PHR) is an easily obtainable prognostic marker in CVD. Some research reports have indicated the PHR is independently associated with adverse outcomes for patients with coronary artery disease (CAD), showing potentially stronger association than hemoglobin levels or platelet counts [22–24]. However, the relationship between PHR and CI-AKI among CAD patients who have undergone PCI remains unclear and has thus become our current research focus.

Materials and methods

Patient selection

A cohort study with a multicenter, retrospective, and observational design was performed. The data were obtained for the period of January 2007 to December 2020 from the Cardiorenal Improvement II (NCT05050877) registry for five tertiary teaching hospitals in southern China that serve as regional central centers [25], enrolling 72,693 patients with coronary artery disease who underwent PCI according to contemporary clinical guidelines. PCI was indicated based on the treating clinicians’ integrated assessment of symptoms, high-risk coronary anatomy, documented ischemia and other clinical factors. The inclusion criteria encompassed (i) patients above 18 years of age who had undergone treatment for PCI and (ii) those with baseline serum creatinine (Scr) levels recorded and repeated within 48 h after contrast delivery. Patients with the following comorbid conditions were excluded: (i) estimated rate of glomerular filtration (eGFR) below 15 mL/min/1.73 m; (ii) severe liver or kidney dysfunction, infectious diseases, or malignant tumors; (iii) lacking PHR data; and (iv) missing CI-AKI information. The study finally included a total of 26,479 patients (Fig. 1). This study was performed according to the Helsinki Declaration, with approval from the Ethics Committee of Guangdong Provincial People’s Hospital.

Data collection and grouping

Patient demographics and clinical data, e.g., age, gender, hematological parameters, medications, previous medical history, PCI-related data, and previous complications, were obtained from the hospital electronic health records system. Using a standard automatic biochemical analyzer, clinical laboratory technicians measured the hematological parameters and recorded the results. The PHR was then calculated as follows: PHR = platelets ÷ hemoglobin (109/g).

Each participant was assigned to one of four quartiles according to the PHR: Q1 (n = 6,625, PHR ≤ 1.36), Q2 (n = 6,616, 1.36 < PHR ≤ 1.66), Q3 (n = 6,619, 1.67 < PHR ≤ 2.03), and Q4 (n = 6,619, PHR ≥ 2.03).

Medical definition and patient follow-up

The measurements for each patient were first taken at admission (baseline) and an additional time after PCI (within 48 h). If two or more postoperative Scr measurements were collected within 48 h post-PCI for a patient, the calculation was performed using the highest Scr value. The primary outcome of this study was CI-AKI, which was defined using preoperative and postoperative Scr levels according to the KDIGO criteria. Specifically, within 48 h following CM exposure, elevated Scr levels exceeding 26.5 µmol/L (0.3 mg/dL) were observed relative to the baseline or a 1.5-fold increase compared to the baseline within the past 7 days [26].

Utilizing standard technology, experienced interventional cardiologists performed PCI treatment, following standard clinical practice. Patients subjected to elective surgery were hydrated using normal saline (0.9% NaCl) at 1 mL per kg per h before surgery and 6 to 12 h after, thereby halving the frequency of severe cardiac insufficiency (defined as left ventricular ejection fraction below 40% or pulmonary edema). Clinical pharmacotherapy was provided according to the condition of each patient.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were defined as the median with an interquartile range or mean ± SD, while those for categorical variables were shown as frequency (%). Categorical and continuous variables were respectively compared using the Pearson chi-squared test and t-test. To assess the relationship between the CI-AKI odds ratio (OR) and the PHR, univariate and multivariable logistic regression analyses were conducted to estimate odds ratios (ORs) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs), with PHR analyzed as a continuous variable (per 1-unit increase), and by quartiles. Restricted cubic spline (RCS) models were employed to assess the dose-response relationship between continuous PHR and CI-AKI. The following models were developed: (i) an unadjusted crude; (ii) adjusted for age and gender; (iii) fully accounting for multiple confounders, e.g., age, congestive heart failure (CHF), DM, eGFR, hypertension, gender, renin-angiotensin system inhibitors, and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C). To identify the optimal PHR cutoff value, the study employed receiver operator characteristic (ROC) analysis using the “ROCit” R package, and the predictive ability of PHR for CI-AKI was assessed. The prespecified subgroups underwent exploratory analysis with R software (version 4.3.2) from the Vienna-based R Foundation for Statistical Computing. As a key criterion for statistical significance, a two-sided P-value below 0.05 was employed for all analyses.

Results

PHR quartiles

This study comprised 26,479 patients who had undergone PCI. For the study group, the average age was 63.3 ± 11.5 years, with 5,854 (22.1%) being women. Compared to those in the lowest PHR quartile (Q1), patients in the highest quartile (Q4) were slightly older and less likely to be male. Smoking prevalence decreased across increasing PHR quartiles. Furthermore, Q4 patients had increased cardiovascular risk factors and comorbidities, including previous acute myocardial infarction, DM, CHF, chronic kidney disease, stroke, hyperlipidemia, and hypertension (P < 0.001), compared to Q1 patients. With respect to laboratory parameters, baseline eGFR was higher in Q1 patients than in Q4 patients. No significant differences were indicated across quartiles regarding the use of guideline-directed medications (Table 1).

Correlation between the PHR and CI-AKI

A total of 1,707 (6.4%) patients developed CI-AKI. To relate the PHR and CI-AKI risk, the study conducted univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses (Table 2). Model 1 revealed that, compared with Q1 patients, Q4 patients exhibited a higher risk of CI-AKI (OR 1.43; 95% confidence interval (95% CI), 1.25–1.63; P < 0.001). The association in Q4 remained significant after adjusting for age and gender (Model 2; OR 1.45; 95% CI, 1.27–1.67; P < 0.001). For Model 3, i.e., the fully adjusted multivariable model, the highest PHR quartile remained independently associated with elevated CI-AKI risk (adjusted OR, 1.42; 95% CI, 1.20–1.68; P < 0.001). In fully adjusted logistic models, each 1-unit higher PHR was associated with 15% higher CI-AKI risk (OR 1.15, 95% CI 1.05–1.26; P = 0.003). To reveal the correlation between the PHR and CI-AKI incidence rate, an RCS model with multiple adjustments was employed (Fig. 2). The risk of CI-AKI showed a gradual increase with increasing PHR value, with no statistically significant evidence of non-linearity (non-linearity P = 0.060).

ROC analyses of the PHR for CI-AKI

The clinical diagnostic performance of the PHR for CI-AKI was assessed using ROC curves (Fig. 3). The optimal cutoff value was 2.05, with an area under the ROC curve of 0.55 (95% CI, 0.53–0.57). At this cutoff, the sensitivity was 32.6%, the specificity was 75.7%, the positive likelihood ratio (+ LR) was 1.34, and the negative likelihood ratio (-LR) was 0.89. The fully adjusted multivariable Model 3 achieved an AUC of 0.70 (95% CI, 0.68–0.71), with sensitivity 61.6%, specificity 68.9%, +LR = 1.98, and − LR = 0.56.

Discussion

The association between the laboratory parameter PHR and CI-AKI incidence after PCI was investigated. A total of 1,707 CI-AKI cases occurred in 26,479 patients (6.4% incidence rate). Higher baseline PHR values in patients were correlated with a higher likelihood of CI-AKI onset. The PHR value was determined as an independent CI-AKI risk factor following PCI through multivariate logistic regression.

CI-AKI features a complex pathophysiological mechanism that has not been clarified to date. However, several factors likely have a role in its development: inflammation, intrarenal vasoconstriction, oxidative stress, reactive oxygen species formation, reduced renal blood flow, decreased NO production, renal ischemia, tubular epithelial injury, and vascular endothelial injury [26, 27]. Several potential risk factors for CI-AKI are currently known, including advanced age, DM, hyperuricemia, receipt of multiple iodinated CM doses within a 24-h timeframe, the volume and type of CM, and the type of CM intervention [1, 2, 7, 28]. The rate of CI-AKI incidence is markedly low, affecting fewer than 1% of patients lacking associated risk factors [29]. This rate is higher among high-risk patients, reaching nearly 15% [30]. As evidence indicates that CI-AKI can be prevented [28, 31], it is essential to distinguish potential at-risk patients and apply preventative measures to reduce CI-AKI incidence.

Regarding CI-AKI for patients suffering from DM or pre-existing kidney dysfunction, an increased platelet count is an independent risk factor [32]. However, for those undergoing coronary angiography, anemia is also associated with increased CI-AKI risk [33]. A high circulating platelet count [10–12] has been shown to lead to poor outcomes in CVD, involving potential mechanisms also found in CI-AKI, such as inflammatory response and platelet activation [15]. Additionally, low hemoglobin levels [16, 17] are a independent risk factor of a poor prognosis in CVD. Low hemoglobin is caused by a decrease in oxygen-carrying capacity, which is also a factor in CI-AKI. Moreover, a mechanistic correlation exists between reduced hemoglobin levels and increased platelet count. Platelet aggregation may increase the possibility of renal ischemia, while anemia can increase the aggregation of whole blood cells. Low hemoglobin levels are often associated with inflammation. These biomarkers of inflammation, which include fibrinogen, inflammatory cytokines, and the von Willebrand factor, may directly increase platelet reactivity. According to anemia status, the bone marrow may become overly active, followed by the release of more platelets [34].

The PHR is a unique and accessible prognostic metric in CVD. Multiple studies have found that the PHR was independently associated with adverse outcomes in CAD patients, with a stronger association than hemoglobin levels or platelet counts [22–24]. Zheng et al. [22] retrospectively observed 6,046 patients with CAD after PCI and identified PHR ≥ 1.92 mg/L as a key factor independently associated with long-term survival outcomes. Işık et al. [23] retrospectively investigated 884 patients with STEMI undergoing PCI and indicated PHR as an independent determinant of in-hospital mortality among STEMI patients. Bao et al. [24] obtained data from 2,599 hospitalized individuals who had undergone coronary angiography and were found to have CAD with CHF complications. Those authors associated elevated PHR levels with a higher long-term risk of mortality (all-cause) in their patient cohort.

Despite the above evidence, no studies have focused on the PHR–CI-AKI relationship. The PHR was independently association with CI-AKI in post-PCI patients. As verified by multiple logistic regression analyses, CI-AKI risk in high-PHR patients was 1.39-fold greater than that in low-PHR patients. This comprehensive indicator may potentially be applicable in the identification of at-risk patients to facilitate preventive measures to lower CI-AKI incidence via precise patient treatment.

Given that increased PHR was associated with higher risk of CI-AKI onset, all patients receiving PCI should routinely undergo complete blood count testing upon admission as well as frequent repeat testing during hospitalization. The PHR is a readily available and reproducible biomarker that is easily calculated, with no additional cost to the patient or health care system. CI-AKI is linked to considerable morbidity and a poor prognosis. Measures for improving patient outcomes are needed, including antioxidant therapy (N-acetylcysteine/ascorbic acid), pre-PCI intravenous dilation (isotonic saline/NaHCO3), and low-osmolality or isotonic CM. Strong evidence has suggested that statins can lower CI-AKI risk. For patients with a high PHR, administering antiplatelet or anticoagulant drugs, controlling progression of the inflammatory response, and supplementing with erythropoietin analogs or iron to improve anemia can lower the frequency of CI-AKI and improve patient prognosis.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, as a retrospective observational study, residual confounding cannot be excluded despite multivariable adjustment for established risk factors. Second, urine output data, such as urinary albumin or urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio, were unavailable in the registry due to high proportion of missing data. Third, CI-AKI diagnosis relied solely on serum creatinine criteria may lead to underdiagnosis of CI-AKI. Finally, our cohort primarily consisted of Chinese (Asian) participants undergoing PCI in southern China, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other ethnic groups. Future validation in more diverse populations should be cautioned.

Conclusion

In this large multicenter retrospective cohort of 26,479 patients with CAD undergoing PCI, we demonstrated that an elevated platelet–hemoglobin ratio (PHR) was independently associated with CI-AKI. Our findings suggest that PHR may serve as a practical tool for early risk stratification in PCI patients, enabling more targeted preventive strategies such as intensified hydration, minimization of contrast volume, and optimization of anemia or anti-inflammatory management in high-PHR individuals.
