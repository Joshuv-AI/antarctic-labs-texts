Cerebellar neural populations orchestrate dopamine reward signaling with single-trial precision
Authors: Liang-Yin Lu, Peng Chen, Ting-Yu Liang, Liang-Ying Chen, Wen-Chuan Liu, Wei-Xiang Chen, Jye-Chang Lee, Wen-Sung Lai, Ming-Kai Pan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Reward-based learning requires neural circuits to rapidly register outcomes and adapt behavior, yet how subcortical circuits contribute to temporally precise reward signaling remains elusive. Here, we identify a cerebellar mechanism encoding rewards with single-trial precision. Utilizing cleared-tissue tractography, electrophysiology, and two-photon imaging in a mouse foraging task, we describe a brief population silencing in the deep cerebellar nucleus (DCN), termed instantaneous population silencing (IPS), triggered by synchronized activity of Purkinje cells (PCs). This transient silencing emerges 100 ms after reward, scales with reward prediction errors, and induces rebound phasic firing of ventral tegmental area (VTA) dopamine neurons. Such single-trial temporal accuracy enabled precise reward control via PC-to-DCN or DCN-to-VTA projections. A single 5-ms optogenetic pulse suffices to elicit one IPS and corresponding phasic VTA response, shifting behavior with single-trial precision. Our findings underscore the cerebellum’s role in real-time reward coding, illustrating how population dynamics achieve temporal and single-event precision.

Introduction

Reward-based shaping of behavior is a fundamental aspect of learning and decision-making1–11, with reward detection as its cornerstone. Dopaminergic neurons in the ventral tegmental area (VTA) have been identified as the primary reward center, reporting rewards and reward-prediction errors (RPEs) at 120–150 ms after reward delivery1,12–14. These neurons modulate the nucleus accumbens (NAc) and the prefrontal cortex, thereby influencing learning and decision-making12,15,16. To support the central role of VTA-based reward processing, the brain must extract each reward event from the immense array of sensory inputs in a timely and unambiguous manner, discriminating between rewarding and non-rewarding events, achieving single-event resolution and transmitting the reward signals to the VTA with minimal delay. Previous studies have provided valuable evidence on the cortical or subcortical regulation of VTA activity17–20. Yet such upstream signals typically unfold over hundreds of milliseconds to seconds21, raising the question of how they can account for the rapid, phasic dopamine responses observed in the VTA. One possibility is that a key upstream structure is specialized for the rapid computation of high-dimensional information and transmits temporally precise signals to the VTA. Moreover, because phasic VTA activity encodes RPE magnitude, such a structure must also rapidly compare internal expectations with actual reward outcomes within this narrow temporal window.

Recent evidence suggests that the cerebellum plays an essential role in reward processing22,23. Cerebellar granule cells3, climbing fiber inputs to cerebellar Purkinje cells (PCs)24–26, and deep cerebellar nucleus (DCN) neurons27 are involved in reward encoding. The cerebellum could regulate VTA dopaminergic activity via the DCN-to-VTA pathway4, providing key anatomical and functional connections for cerebellar reward coding. Moreover, the cerebellar granule cells support high-dimensional representation of sensory data and internal models28,29. The cerebellum has multiple parallel processing microcircuits30, a feature that may allow for complex parallel computations within 150 ms—the reward-encoding timeframe of VTA neurons—by transforming sensory inputs and internal predictions into reward codes. This leads to the critical questions: what are the precise neuronal codes transmitted from the cerebellum to the VTA, and how do these cerebellar-to-VTA codes achieve single-trial precision for reward processing?

To address this, we combined multi-modal in vivo techniques—including tract tracing, single-unit extracellular recordings, two-photon calcium imaging, and closed-loop optogenetics in mice performing a two-choice foraging task. We analyzed single-neuron firing patterns and population codes for reward detection, with a focus on single-trial precision and temporal accuracy across every mouse. Finally, we provided precise optogenetic stimulation to manipulate reward-based choices on a single-trial basis, enabling behavioral engineering of the mouse’s mental processing.

Results

A two-choice foraging task reveals trial-by-trial dynamics of reward

To investigate how neuronal activity encodes reward information within a dynamically changing process, we trained mice to perform a two-choice foraging task, a standard reward-based decision-making task31,32 (Fig. 1a). Each trial began with a nose-poke into the center initiation port. Mice then chose to nose-poke into either the left or right reward port to receive condensed milk. To introduce greater dynamism in reward detection across trials, we provided an 80% reward probability at one port, then switched ports every 22–28 trials without prior cues (Fig. 1b). Compared to the pre-training condition, well-trained mice exhibited higher choice accuracy and completed the task more rapidly (Fig. 1c–f). To quantify latent neurocognitive processes underlying dynamic reinforcement learning, we also applied a reward-punishment Q-learning model33 to evaluate trial-by-trial expected reward values (Q values, the expected value of each choice, see Eq. 1 in “Methods” and Supplementary Fig. 1a–f) and RPEs, defined as the difference between actual and expected reward outcomes. These model-derived variables captured the expected single-trial dynamics and evolved systematically across trials (Supplementary Fig. 1g–j). Specifically, Q-values for the rewarded choices increased progressively, while RPEs showed large negative deflections at block transitions (rewarded side transition) and gradually decayed to near zero as the animal adapted to the dynamic reinforcement (Supplementary Fig. 1g–j).

Next, we performed single-unit recordings in the VTA (Supplementary Fig. 2a) during the behavioral task (Video S1). Consistent with previous reports12, approximately 16% of VTA neurons exhibited phasic firing-rate changes in response to rewarded events (Supplementary Fig. 2b–g), with magnitudes that varied according to history-dependent RPEs (Supplementary Fig. 2h, i)14. Specifically, repeated successful reward acquisition gradually reduced positive RPEs, accompanied by a corresponding decrease in VTA phasic firing rates. Similarly, repeated failure to obtain rewards reduced the magnitude of negative RPEs, along with a corresponding attenuation of VTA phasic firing. These findings provide a behavioral and electrophysiological framework for investigating circuit-level reward coding.

Loss of DCN-to-VTA inputs abolishes reward-driven choice behavior

The DCN-to-VTA projection is associated with reward-related modulation in social behavior4. To investigate the precise neuronal codes for rewards via the DCN-to-VTA pathway, we first verified the anatomical connections between the DCN and VTA using retrograde Fluoro-Gold labeling (Supplementary Fig. 3a–f) and cerebellar tissue clearing with fiber track labeling (Supplementary Fig. 3g–j). VTA-projecting neurons were distributed across multiple deep cerebellar nuclei (Supplementary Fig. 3), with a predominant contribution from the dentate nucleus—the lateral nucleus of the DCN (Supplementary Fig. 4). Consistent with this anatomical organization and previous studies4,34–38, optogenetic manipulation of dentate projections can modulate VTA neuronal firing (Supplementary Fig. 5).

Next, we tested the necessity of DCN-to-VTA inputs for reward-driven choices. We virally expressed SYP-miniSOG, an optogenetic tool to inhibit synaptic vesicle release39,40, in the bilateral dentate nucleus and implanted optical fibers in the VTA bilaterally (Fig. 1i). When exposed to blue light, miniSOG generates free radicals and disturbs the synaptophysin function for synaptic vesicle docking39. Optogenetic inhibition of DCN-to-VTA axonal terminals was applied in the middle and final blocks of the experiment during reward delivery (Fig. 1j), thus inhibiting the synaptic transmission from DCN axonal terminals. This targeted suppression impaired choices toward the rewarded side (Fig. 1k) and reduced overall accuracy (Fig. 1l). Photoinhibition did not alter reaction time (Fig. 1m, n) or motor coordination (Supplementary Fig. 6), suggesting that the behavioral impairment was not attributable to deficits in motivation or motor function. Further analysis of choice strategies revealed a reduced win-stay proportion without affecting loss-switch behavior (Fig. 1o, p), indicating impaired reward detection.

Consistent with the behavioral effects, inhibition of the DCN-to-VTA pathway disrupted the formation of positive expected Q-values estimated by the reinforcement learning model (Supplementary Fig. 7a–c). Across animals, photoinhibition reduced Q-values and increased RPE magnitude (Supplementary Fig. 7d, e), further supporting impaired reward detection.

In brief, the optogenetic manipulations confirmed that the DCN-to-VTA projection is required for normal reward detection and reward-guided choice behavior.

Individual neurons do not encode reward with single-trial precision

If the DCN-to-VTA pathway is necessary to encode reward information, the activity of DCN neurons should change at reward time. To explore the exact reward codes in the DCN, we recorded single-unit activities with multi-tetrodes in the dentate nucleus (Fig. 2a). First, we identified neurons with firing-rate differences between reward versus non-reward trials (Fig. 2b–d). Approximately 1.8 and 4.1% of DCN neurons showed decreased (negative-tuning) and increased (positive-tuning) firing rates during rewarded trials (Fig. 2e). Next, we assessed whether these expert neurons could encode reward events with single-trial accuracy. Next, we evaluated whether individual DCN neurons encode reward through burst-firing patterns. We did not observe significant burst modulations of individual neurons between reward and non-reward trials (Supplementary Fig. 8a–c). We further evaluated potential individual neuronal codes through other temporal coding mechanisms, including phase tuning to local field potentials (Supplementary Fig. 8d–g) and frequency-vector tuning (Supplementary Fig. 8h–k). Again, none of the tested temporal coding mechanisms of individual neurons could explain reward events with single-trial precision. True positive rates were uniformly low and below 0.5 (average at ~0.23), while false positive rates were consistently high and above 0.5 (average at ~0.8) (Fig. 2j).

To unbiasedly identify other hidden profiles of DCN neuronal activities for rewards, we trained a supervised support vector machine (SVM)41,42 on all recorded DCN datasets (Supplementary Fig. 9). Despite leveraging machine learning, we did not identify individual DCN neurons capable of signaling rewards with single-trial precision.

Together, reward detection cannot be explained by changes in firing rates, firing patterns, firing probabilities, and other hidden factors at the level of individual DCN neurons.

DCN population silencing encodes reward with single-trial precision

If single neurons do not carry reward signals with single-trial precision, how exactly does the cerebellum determine whether there is a reward and when is the reward timing? The population activity in a substantial number of DCN neurons is pivotal in sensory coding and motor frequency computation43–47. Therefore, we hypothesized that reward coding depends on coordinated activity of a large population of neurons across a spatial region (Fig. 3a), an emergent property that does not exist at the single-neuron level. First, we examined the activity of the DCN neuronal population with a temporal resolution of 10 ms. At a single-trial resolution, we organized the spike raster from population DCN neurons and discovered an instantaneous population silencing (IPS) happening around 100 ms following reward delivery (Fig. 3b, c, left column). Conversely, this instantaneous drop of population firing was absent in non-reward trials (Fig. 3b, c, right column). Further analysis of firing-rate changes confirmed an IPS (Fig. 3d) after each reward delivery, which emerged consistently in every single reward trial but was absent in non-rewarded trials or at any behavioral timing other than rewards (Fig. 3e, f). To quantify the IPS accuracy of reward detection at the single-trial level, we identified the negative peaks of IPSs across various thresholds and evaluated the reward-detecting performance using a receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (Fig. 3g). We found a high accuracy of single-trial reward detection of IPS (Fig. 3h). Moreover, the trial-by-trial jitters of IPS time were around 10 ms (Fig. 3i), presenting the high temporal precision of IPSs. Group analyses across different mice confirmed the robustness of IPSs in single-trial accuracy for reward detection (Fig. 3j–l). Notably, while mean IPS timings varied across different mice, IPS jitters remained equal or smaller than 12 ms (Fig. 3m, n), supporting the temporal precision of IPS codes within each mouse. The accuracy of single-trial reward detection was superior to the individual rate-tuning neurons (Fig. 3o–q). To further explore the duration of IPS events, we also computed the IPS codes with higher temporal resolutions of 5 and 2 ms (Supplementary Fig. 10). We divided RPE values into five levels spanning negative to positive prediction errors (Fig. 4b). Population firing-rate changes across these levels revealed a systematic modulation of IPS amplitude (Fig. 4c): trials with larger positive RPEs exhibited stronger silencing, whereas trials with negative RPEs showed minimal or no IPS responses (Fig. 4c, d). Within individual mice, IPS magnitude was strongly correlated with RPE size, with a mean correlation coefficient of −0.75 (Fig. 4e–g). These results indicate that larger and positive RPEs were associated with deeper population silencing. Importantly, IPS magnitude did not correlate with the expected value of the subsequent trial (Supplementary Fig. 11a–g), in contrast to its strong correlation to RPEs (Supplementary Fig. 11h). We also performed a model-free analysis to examine the relationship between IPS magnitude and the number of trials following reward-port reversal (Supplementary Fig. 12). Consistently, IPS magnitude increased across subsequent trials, reflecting a transition from negative RPEs (small IPS magnitude) to positive RPEs (big IPS magnitude).

Synchronization of Crus I Purkinje dendritic activity reports reward

We next investigated the generator of DCN IPS at the circuit level. The synchronous silencing of population DCN neurons could be attributed to synchronous GABAergic inputs from PCs. To test this hypothesis, we used two-photon calcium imaging to directly monitor population dendritic activity of PCs at Crus I, a cerebellar cortical region that provides direct input to the dentate nucleus27,34,48–53. Pcp2-GCaMP6f mice underwent craniotomy and were trained to perform a head-fixed version of our two-choice foraging task (Fig. 5a–d). Upon reward delivery, PCs exhibited synchronous increments in dendritic calcium activity (Fig. 5e, f, and Video S2). The synchronous activation of PCs was observed only after reward events, but not at any time between or during non-reward events (Fig. 5g–i). Quantification revealed significantly higher recruitment of PC dendrites during rewarded compared to non-rewarded trials (Fig. 5j, k). Consistently, this reward-evoked PC activation was also observed across most of the recorded Crus I region (Fig. 5l, m,Supplementary Figs. 13 and 14). Optogenetic-induced IPS modulates reward-driven choice behavior

The temporal precision of IPS timing in the DCN suggests an instantaneous neuronal event with a short duration at the millisecond level. If the IPS mechanism is precise and robust, we should be able to generate rewarding behavioral changes by introducing one IPS per trial at the expected reward time (Fig. 6a). To test this hypothesis, we virally expressed ChR2 in PCs—GABAergic neurons upstream of the DCN—of cerebellar Crus I lobule and implanted fiber-optic cannulae into bilateral DCN (Fig. 6b, c). We illuminated the PC axonal terminals with a single 5-ms pulse per non-rewarded trial, creating one cycle of axonal depolarization that allowed only one synchronized action potential across axons and thus one IPS in the DCN (Fig. 6d, e). We also evaluated the internal value updating using a Q-learning model, which reports the estimated reward (Q) values and RPEs with single-trial resolution. In trial-by-trial behavioral dynamics, each optogenetic IPS created a positive shift of Q value per non-rewarded choice and systematically altered the choice strategies (Fig. 6k and Supplementary Fig. 15).

To directly test the functional role of IPS in the DCN-to-VTA projection, we applied a pathway-specific optogenetic silencing strategy by virally expressing HcKCR1, a light-gated potassium channel, into the DCN (Supplementary Fig. 16a). A 5-ms light pulse was sufficient to transiently silence DCN neurons (Supplementary Fig. 16b–e) and induced a sharp IPS-like firing rate drop within the 0–5 ms window (Supplementary Fig. 16f–h; 5-ms temporal bin). We then applied the same 5-ms light pulse to silence populational DCN-to-VTA axons at their terminals (Fig. 6m–o). This brief axonal inhibition elicited clear rebound phasic responses in VTA neurons (Fig. 6p–s), indicating that IPS-like activity in the DCN-to-VTA projection is capable of generating downstream phasic dopaminergic signals associated with reward processing.

In brief, optogenetically evoked IPS, either via PC-to-DCN or DCN-to-VTA axons, sufficiently elicits phasic VTA responses for rewards on a single-trial basis. One optogenetic IPS per choice sufficiently rendered a non-rewarded event rewarding and changed mouse behaviors.

Resolving conflicting evidence in DCN-to-VTA pathway

Our findings support that IPS, causing transient population silencing of DCN-to-VTA axonal outputs, can encode rewards with single-trial precision. Notably, social reward has been associated with the excitatory DCN-to-VTA outputs4, supported by positive fiber photometry activity for rewards and increased social activity by excitatory optogenetic stimulation. While two-choice rewards and social rewards can be regulated by different mechanisms, an alternative explanation is that the details of reward codes can only be revealed by approaches with single-trial precision and millisecond-ranged temporal resolution. We first examined the fiber photometry paradox, which suggests that DCN-to-VTA axonal excitation encodes rewards. By virally expressing dentate neurons with jGCaMP8f, a calcium indicator, we utilized VTA-implanted fiber photometry to monitor DCN-to-VTA axonal dynamics during the two-choice foraging behavior (Fig. 7a–d). Similar to those observed in social rewards, we observed a robust increase in activity at the axonal terminals at 0 to 1 s only after reward delivery, but not during other periods or non-rewarded trials (Fig. 7e–g). The magnitudes of the fiber photometry signals correlated with history-dependent RPEs (Fig. 7h, i). These data seemed to support the axonal excitation for reward encoding. However, when aligning DCN IPSs, VTA phasic responses, and DCN-to-VTA fiber photometry altogether, the temporal inconsistency appears (Fig. 7j). The fiber photometry signal peaked >400 ms post-reward, well after the VTA phasic responses at 160 ms (Fig. 7i, gray arrows and 7j), and is unlikely to be the direct cause of VTA reward codes. In contrast, a transient decrease of photometric signals at ~100 ms was temporally aligned with the IPS (Fig. 7i, black arrows). While the increased fiber photometric signals could be an important indicator of RPEs and easier to identify, their time courses do not support the underlying neuronal activity causing the phasic VTA responses for rewards. Approaches with millisecond resolution are required to identify the precise coding for reward detection and resolve the temporal incompatibility. We also reexamined the VTA dataset obtained during the behavioral task (Supplementary Fig. 2) and found that VTA phasic firing was consistently preceded by a brief reduction in firing rate at a millisecond timescale (Supplementary Fig. 17). We next examined the second paradox: optogenetic excitation of the DCN-to-VTA pathway can produce reward-like effects. We designed an experimental setup that allowed bidirectional modulation of DCN activity while simultaneously recording VTA dopaminergic neurons (Fig. 7k, upper panel). A red-shifted opsin, ChrimsonR, was virally expressed in DCN neurons of DAT-GCaMP6f mice, enabling optogenetic manipulation of the DCN and fiber photometry recording of VTA simultaneously (Fig. 7k). We found that optogenetic activation of the DCN (firing acceleration) led to a transient increase of activity in dopaminergic neurons, whereas the termination of stimulation (mimicking a sudden drop of population firing rates in IPS) also triggered a sharp and transient increase in dopaminergic neuronal activity (Fig. 7l–o). Both stimulation onset and offset caused classical phasic reward responses in VTA. Although an increased population DCN firing rate was not observed in our recordings, both optogenetic activation and silencing of DCN-to-VTA axons can trigger VTA responses.

In summary, increased fiber-photometry signals and reward-causing optogenetic stimulation do not necessarily indicate that the excitatory DCN-to-VTA pathway is the physiological reward code. However, it requires electrophysiological approaches with millisecond precision to reveal the IPS mechanism and identify the temporal paradox. These results also highlight the importance of designing optogenetic manipulations based on physiologically observed neuronal dynamics.

Discussion

In this study, we identified a cerebellar reward-coding mechanism with temporal precision and single-trial accuracy. In the PC-DCN-VTA pathway, each reward event triggers an episode of population PC synchrony, which generates instantaneous silencing of population DCN neurons, or IPS, and subsequently causes rebound phasic firings of VTA neurons. The IPS reliably appears at and only at 100 ms after reward delivery, with the temporal jitters around 8–12 ms. The single-trial precision of this IPS mechanism allows us to render a non-reward trial rewarding with precise optogenetic manipulation. One 5-ms pulse of light per choice, causing one depolarizing cycle of population PC-to-DCN axons, was sufficient to generate one IPS and thus one phasic VTA event for reward coding. IPS-mimicking silencing of DCN-to-VTA axons, one 5-ms light pulse per choice, showed the same effect of phasic VTA responses for rewards. The above reward coding mechanism is summarized in Supplementary Fig. 18.

This study has several limitations. First, the population synchrony of PCs was assessed using calcium imaging, which has limited temporal resolution. Due to the inherently slow dynamics of calcium signals, this approach cannot capture synchrony at millisecond precision. Soma-targeted voltage imaging may offer sufficient spatial and temporal resolution to address this issue. However, confirming synchrony mechanisms at single-trial precision would require a voltage-sensitive construct with a high signal-to-noise ratio to avoid the need for trial averaging. Second, while the IPS magnitudes were correlated with the sizes of history-based RPEs, we cannot assess their correlation with single-trial precision. Our behavioral paradigm involved self-initiated choices with a fixed reward size, which allowed us to study trial-by-trial reward detection, but limited RPE quantification to reward history and model-based estimations. Future studies using Pavlovian conditioning paradigms with variable reward sizes may allow more precise quantification of RPEs based on reward sizes. Notably, IPS is a rapid event (2–10 ms), whereas differentiation of reward-size-dependent RPEs likely requires reward consumption, which unfolds over longer timescales. Consistent with this, reward consumption has been associated with later and more sustained dopamine activity in the VTA54–58. Technically, the number of recorded neurons used for IPS computation directly affects the resolution of IPS magnitude. The number of simultaneously recorded single units in our study may not have provided sufficient resolution to represent RPE as a continuous variable. Third, we only focused on the dentate nucleus of DCN, which is supported by current evidence of cerebellar cognitive processing and reward-based learning4,34–38. While VTA-projecting DCN neurons were predominantly expressed in the dentate nuclei, some neurons were located in the interposed and fastigial nuclei. The reward-based contribution of these extra-dentate neurons remained to be studied. Finally, we could not precisely estimate the population size of IPS-generating neurons. The machine-learning evidence supports the need for population coding and the universally poor reward detection of individual DCN neurons, whether they project to VTA or not. However, we did not separate population activity based on axonal projections. The population size of IPS may potentially be smaller when considering VTA-projecting neurons exclusively.

Our data support that synchronous PC outputs could drive IPS codes in DCN. Notably, the dendritic calcium signals of PCs are driven by climbing fiber (CF) inputs, which originate from inferior olivary (IO) neurons dynamically coupled through gap junctions59,60. The role of the IO and CFs in reward or RPE generation and transmission remains an area of active investigation. Climbing fiber signals have traditionally been associated with motor learning, error correction61, and motor control46. However, recent evidence suggests their broader involvement in cognitive and reinforcement learning processes24,25,62–64. The temporal dynamics of CF activity align with key components of RPE computation, such as detecting unexpected rewards and driving behavioral adjustments22,65. Notably, granule cells, the source of parallel fibers, also have a known contribution to reward processing3. Specifically, granule cells form a temporal basis of ramping activity throughout a learning process, and continuously update predictions of CF-related reward signaling66. This process could potentially facilitate the timing determination and amplitude computation of IPS. Additionally, DCN outputs send collaterals to the parvocellular red nucleus (RNp), which, in turn, provides feedback to the IO67,68. These feedback innervations have been linked to motor learning in primates69.

How might IPS drive VTA phasic firing? Current evidence suggests at least two potential mechanisms. First, VTA neurons express T-type calcium channels and are capable of generating rebound burst firing70,71. Although the precise cellular mechanism remains unsolved, IPS may transiently hyperpolarize these neurons, a known mechanism that relieves T-type channel inactivation and promotes subsequent burst generation72–75. Second, IPS may act through local VTA interneurons76–80. DCN inputs could influence dopamine neurons indirectly via interneuron circuits, such that IPS induces transient silencing of inhibitory interneurons, resulting in phasic disinhibition of VTA dopamine neurons. Although both rebound bursting and interneuron-mediated disinhibition have been described in the VTA70,71,76–82, their specific roles in mediating DCN-to-VTA signaling remain to be established.

Notably, excitatory inputs to VTA dopamine neurons can also induce burst firing83,84. In line with this, both our results (Fig. 7k–m) and prior work4 demonstrate that optogenetic excitation of DCN-to-VTA axons can evoke phasic VTA activity for rewards. However, while the DCN-to-VTA pathway can use this excitatory mechanism for generating VTA phasic firing, our data do not support the existence of such excitation as an endogenous mechanism in this pathway, at least not in mice. These findings underscore the importance of interpreting causal manipulations in the context of native neuronal dynamics. In contrast, the delayed (~500 ms) increase in DCN-to-VTA axonal activity observed with fiber photometry (Fig. 7e–j) may contribute to other aspects of reward processing. Reward consumption has been associated with later and more sustained dopamine activity in the VTA54–58,85–87, with temporal profiles consistent with the observed elevation in DCN-to-VTA photometric signals.

Methods

Animals

All experimental procedures were conducted following the guidelines and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of National Taiwan University (Protocol numbers: B201900034, B202000003, and B202100150). Mice were housed in the central and satellite facilities of National Taiwan University under a reversed 12-h dark-light cycle with ad libitum access to food and water. The animal room was maintained at 22 ± 2 °C and 50 ± 10% relative humidity. All experimental mice were 2–6 months of age, and both sexes were used. Wild-type C57BL/6J and Vglut2-ires-cre (Jax #016963) mice in both sexes were used for DCN and VTA experiments, including electrophysiological recordings, fiber photometry axonal recording, transgenic and viral-based optogenetic manipulation. Pcp2-cre mice (Jax #004146) were used for PC-to-DCN optogenetic activation experiments, and crossed with tTA2-GCaMP6f mice (Ai148 (TIT2L-GC6f-ICL-tTA2), Jax #030328) were used for two-photon calcium imaging.

Viral vectors and tracer

Two-choice foraging task

General task structure

To investigate how the cerebellum encodes reward-related information, we utilized a standard two-choice foraging task31,32. In this task, mice learned to initiate the process by inserting their noses into a central “initiation port.” Subsequently, two sets of light-emitting diode (LED) modules located above the choice ports were illuminated as the “Go” light cue. The two choice ports were located adjacent to the “initiation port.” At this point, the mice had to select either the left or right choice port and waited for another 0.8 s to receive a reward (approximately 10 µl of condensed milk, Nestlé S.A., with a concentration of 10 g/100 ml dissolved in water). In the task, the two choice ports were presented with different reward probabilities: one port had an 80% chance of being followed by a reward, while the other was a no-reward choice. The rewarded choice port was switched every 22–28 trials (25 trials on average) across blocks without prior alert. Well-trained mice could go through eight blocks with a total of 200 trials within 45 min while maintaining a 70% accuracy rate. Only the well-trained expert mice were recruited for this study.

Apparatus and equipment

The custom-built behavioral chamber was composed of a four-sided acrylic enclosure measuring 180 mm in width and 250 mm in height. It featured a grid floor made up with acrylic, two nose-poke reward ports measuring 4.5 cm × 8.4 cm, positioned on the left and right sides of the wall, located 1.6 cm above the grid floor. Above these nose-poke ports were a set of LED light cues. In the center, between the two nose-poke ports, was the “initiation port” (3 cm × 3 cm). Detection of mouse nose-pokes and entries into the reward port was accomplished using infrared emitters and IR detectors from LITEON Electronic, Inc., placed on the inner side of the ports. Condensed milk rewards were administered through an open-source syringe pump88, and delivered through a 16-gauge metal tube located within the reward port. Each instance of licking contact with the tube was detected and recorded using infrared emitter/sensor pairs adjacent to the tube. All components were controlled through two Arduino MEGA2560 boards through custom-written scripts on MATLAB. To synchronize mice behavioral events and neuronal recording data, the digital and analog signals from each port were transmitted to a DAQ (data acquisition) device (Cerebus, BlackRock microsystem, or OpenEphys system) through a solid-state relay (SSR) switching device to timestamp nose-pokes, licking events.

Shaping procedure

We designed a training procedure for mice to acquire the behavioral task. Two weeks post-surgery, the food-restricted mice were acclimated to the behavioral chamber for a period of 20 min, during which they were equipped with optical fibers and recording wires for habituation. Over the next few days, mice participated in a choice-reward associative training session. Their objective was to earn 10, 25, and then 50 rewards (positive reinforcement, PR) by nose-poking into the initiation port and choice ports (left or right) within a 45-min window. Mice advanced to the next reward goal once they met the daily criteria. Following stable associative behavior, they moved on to three consecutive forced switching sessions, each consisting of 25 left-rewarded and 25 right-rewarded trials, all with a 100% reward rate. The forced switching sessions were designed to train mice to shift between two choices and ensure that the mice didn’t develop a preference for one side. After completing these sessions, the mice progressed to the formal task, which was identical to the previous one except for the reduced reward rate of 80% at the reward port. The side of the reward port was switched every 22–28 trials (averaging 25 trials) across eight blocks without prior notification. Electrophysiological or fiber photometry recordings commenced once the learning criteria were met. These criteria included achieving a correct rate of at least 70% for the first two blocks and maintaining a total accuracy not falling below 70%. Mice were also required to complete 200 trials within 45 min. In experiments involving optogenetic interventions, light stimulation was paired with the reward delivery time on the third, fourth, seventh, and eighth blocks. To prevent the mice from developing a side preference, the initial reward port assignment (left or right) was pseudorandom. After each daily session, food was provided in the mice’s home cage, adhering to a restricted diet of 2.0–3.0 grams of food per day. Mice that didn’t meet the training criteria within one month were excluded from the experiments.

Rotarod analysis

Mice were placed on an accelerating rotarod from 4 to 40 rpm over 5 min. During the training phase (days 1–3), each mouse performed two consecutive trials per day. On day 4, baseline performance was recorded without optogenetic manipulation. On day 5, optogenetic inhibition during the task was performed using a 473 nm laser with 1-s pulses delivered at 0.1 Hz. Time (Latency to fall) and speed were recorded when one of the following criteria was met: the mouse fell off the rod, the mouse made one passive rotation while hanging without walking, or the mouse remained on the rod for the full duration of the trial (5 min).

Fiber photometry recording

Equipment and system setup

AAV9-Syn-jGCaMP8f-WPRE was injected into the DCN (AP: −6.24 mm, ML: +2.3 mm, DV: −2.3 from bregma). Optical fiber was implanted above the dorsal VTA (AP: −3.3 mm, ML: −0.5 mm, DV: −4.0 mm from bregma). To detect the GCaMP indicators, we employed a system that involved two light-emitting diodes (LEDs), one emitting light at 465 nm and the other at 405 nm, and these LED lights were modulated at frequencies of 211 and 531 Hz, respectively. These modulated light beams were passed through an integrated fluorescence mini-cube (ilFMC4, Doric Lenses). The mini-cube transmitted a light beam through a low-autofluorescence fiber cable (400 μm, 0.48 NA, Doric) to the optical fiber inserted into the mouse brain. The LED power at the tip of the fiber cable was maintained at approximately 70 µW. The fluorescence signals from GCaMP and the isosbestic background signals were conveyed back through the same fiber cable, returned to the mini-cube, and subsequently passed through a 525 nm filter to the fluorescence photoreceiver module (Model 2151, Newport). The photoreceiver output was then connected to an analog input port of the photometry console using a BNC cable, which allowed the demodulation of calcium-dependent signals (excited at 465 nm) and calcium-independent signals (excited at 405 nm) based on preset carrier frequencies. The photometry console was under the control of the Doric Neuroscience Studio Software and saved the acquired data files.

Fluorescence trace extraction

To analyze GCaMP fluorescence signals, we first removed the light bleaching effect to both channels by fitting exponential decay and subtracted them from signals. Then the corrected 465 nm signals were subtracted by 405 nm signals. The sampling rate was 30.01 Hz, resulting in a temporal resolution of 33 ms. The retrieved data directly received Z-score normalization in the next step without smoothing. We calculated the z-scored dF/F for GCaMP as (F − F0)/F1, where F0 was calculated as the global mean of the fluorescence signals, and F1 was calculated as the standard deviation of these signals. Behavioral events were timestamped into the photometry console through digital/analog BNC inputs. These timestamps were utilized for subsequent event-related analyses. All the procedures described were carried out using the fiber photometry analysis toolbox (https://github.com/leomol/FPA) custom-written MATLAB scripts.

In vivo extracellular recording

Optetrode equipment and implantation

Our custom-made multi-channel microdrive optetrode was constructed following previously established procedures90. The optetrode was assembled with a custom-made electrode interface board (EIB), three-to-seven tetrodes (0.0005” high-purity (99.95%) tungsten wire from the California Fine Wire Company), one thicker tungsten wire as local referencing channel, one 3D-printed housing, one movable screw, and an optical fiber. The optetrode equipment was surgically implanted into the target brain regions, such as the DCN or VTA, and secured in place with dental cement (Super Bond, Sun Medical Co. Ltd). The cerebellar electrodes, grounding, and referencing electrode implantation followed the previously established procedures40,46.

Data recording and spike sorting

We collected the continuous data with the CerebusTM Multi-Channel Neural Signal Processor (Blackrock microsystems) or the Open Ephys system. Behavioral events were recorded through analog input channels, and the electrical signals from neurons underwent amplification and band-pass filtering. For local field potentials (LFPs), a low-pass filter at 250 Hz was applied, while for spike data, a high-pass filter at 250 Hz was used. All signals were then sampled at a rate of 30 kHz. Raw continuous data were sorted with Offline SorterTM (Plexon, Inc., Dallas, Texas, USA) or with Kilosort3 software (https://github.com/MouseLand/Kilosort). Offline Sorter extracted spikes by thresholding, and spike features were then extracted based on principal component analysis (PCA). Units were classified using k-means and further verified based on parameters including inter-spike intervals (ISIs), shapes of the waveform, and sorting quality across time. Kilosort3 extracted spikes using template matching across all recorded channels. Post-hoc correction with Phy GUI (https://github.com/cortex-lab/phy) involved manual steps, including investigating cross-correlograms and examining spike waveforms across all channels.

Firing rate normalization and clustering

The firing rate of each unit was calculated using a 10-ms bin window from spike rasters obtained from behavioral trials. To assess changes in firing rates related to behavior compared to a baseline, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were adopted by comparing the probability distribution of firing rates during 100 ms time windows with the baseline period (defined as the 500–400 ms before initiation nose poke). For reward-tuned neurons, defined by firing-rate changes in response to rewards, we included approximately 5% of neurons under a 95% confidence interval (1.96 standard deviations) that exhibited either a decrease (negative-tuning neurons) or an increase (positive-tuning neurons) in firing rate. Each included neuron underwent thorough evaluation for single-trial reward detection accuracy on an individual basis, assessing true positive and false positive prediction rates, along with an unbiased ROC analysis. The area under the ROC curves (auROC) was calculated using the trapezoidal integration method. To explore behavior-related responses at the population level of VTA units, we conducted principal component analysis (PCA) using the singular value decomposition algorithm. This allowed us to reduce the data’s dimensions while capturing most of the variance in the auROC. Subsequently, we applied k-means clustering to the first three principal components of the auROC, employing correlation distance metrics. The quality of the clustering results was further assessed using silhouette values to ensure high internal consistency within each cluster.

Single-unit selection for population analysis

Based on the microdrive design, the single units were retrieved from different depths (50 μm forward per session) for each behavioral recording. The units from different sessions should fulfill all 3 criteria to be pooled together for population analysis: (a) they were recorded from the same mouse; (b) they were recorded at different depths;and (c) for each selected reward or non-reward trial used to construct the population, the choice and choice outcome history were matched across three consecutive trials. To ensure each reward event is unique on a single-trial basis, each single trial of each session is unique and can only be used once.

IPS analysis

Recorded single-unit activity was linearly summed, and the instantaneous firing rate (FR) was calculated by dividing the total firing rates of all neurons by the number of neurons, with the temporal bin sizes of 10, 5, or 2 ms. We then computed the frame-by-frame differences in firing rates (ΔFR) and applied a shifting window of 5 frames (current and ±2 frames). The values of ΔFR, defined by frame (n + 1) minus frame(n) in a 10-ms bin (Fig. 3), were then used for the main IPS analysis. IPS based on 2-ms and 5-ms bins were also calculated and presented in the Supplementary Figs. The ΔFRs were then used for the IPS detection by the MATLAB function “findpeaks.”

Burst detection

Single-unit bursts were detected with the same criteria as our previous studies46. A burst was defined as an event containing at least four spikes, with inter-spike intervals (ISIs) of 15 ms or less between consecutive spikes.

Spike-phase analysis during the rewarding period

To investigate the phase-locking of single unit spikes in the DCN, we first extracted the DCN LFPs and band-passed the signals from 4 to 16 Hz as the rewarding oscillation of interest. We then calculated their instantaneous phase by applying the Hilbert transform and corrected by π/2. Single unit spike times were extracted through the methods described above, and down-sampled to 5000 Hz to match the corresponding sampling rate of LFPs. To extract the spike-phase coupling during the reward, we set the 0–500 ms after the reward delivery as a time window. The coupled phases were revealed and can be plotted on a polar histogram. To quantify the strength of the phase bias, we introduced a polarity index. This index was defined as the sum of each phase as a unit vector, divided by the number of vectors, and then normalized to a value ranging between 0 and 1. A polarity index of 0 indicates that spike times are randomly distributed throughout the data, while a value of 1 signifies that every spike occurs precisely at the same phase of the data. All the steps were implemented by the custom-written MATLAB script described in our previous works40,46,91.

Machine learning-based search for temporal features of reward codes

For machine-learning-based classification of neuronal temporal features, we employed a support vector machine (SVM) classifier trained on trial-wise spike train data from DCN and VTA single-unit activity during reward processing. Single-unit spike trains were first converted into estimated firing rates using Gaussian kernel smoothing, which preserved key temporal features while transforming discrete binary spike train data into continuous numerical representations. In a supervised learning framework, trial-by-trial unit firing rates served as the input feature matrix X (n × t), while corresponding trial outcomes (reward presence or absence) were used as labels Y (0 or 1, of dimension n), where n represents the number of input trials and t the number of time frames recorded per trial. To ensure generalizability, trials were split into training and testing datasets, and the SVM model was trained to classify reward outcomes based on neuronal activity. We evaluated the model’s predictive performance using three key metrics: (1) accuracy, the proportion of correctly classified trials, calculated as (true positives (TP) + true negatives (TN))/total trials, where TP refers to correctly identified reward trials, and TN refers to correctly identified non-reward trials; (2) sensitivity, the model’s ability to correctly detect reward trials, calculated as TP/(TP + false negatives (FN)), where FN represents missed reward trials that were incorrectly classified as non-reward; and (3) F1-score, the harmonic mean of precision and sensitivity, balancing false positives and false negatives. Related source code is available on GitHub: https://github.com/cnchi/HappyML.

Optogenetics

We surgically implanted optical fibers (FT200UMT, Thorlabs) equipped with a 1.25 mm LC/PC ceramic ferrule (CFLC230-10, Thorlabs) into the VTA (AP: −3.3 mm, ML: ±0.5 mm, DV: −4.1 mm from bregma, 7° angle) or the DCN (AP: −6.24 mm, ML: ±2.3 mm, DV −2.1 mm from bregma). Two-photon calcium imaging

Brain craniotomy

The surgical procedure was mostly similar to the one described earlier, except for creating a cranial window. We began by removing the connective tissue and muscle covering the cerebellum and subsequently drilled a cranial window with a diameter of 3 mm in the skull. This window was positioned over the cerebellum covering the Crus I lobule. A 3 mm glass cover slip was then inserted into the skull opening and sealed with dental cement. A custom-made titanium plate was mounted on the skull as a head-fixation apparatus under the two-photon microscope. After completing the surgical procedures, all mice were individually housed and allowed a 2-week recovery period before participating in the subsequent behavioral experiments.

Equipment and system

We performed two-photon calcium imaging utilizing a custom-built two-photon microscope equipped with a Ti:sapphire laser (MaiTai, Spectra Physics). A 940 nm laser was employed for sample excitation at an approximate power level of 200 mW. Scanning was controlled by our custom-made software (JadeBio System 4.0, Southport) utilizing galvanometer scanning mirrors. Image acquisition utilized a 16× 0.8 NA objective (CFI LWD Plan Fluorite Objective, 3.0 mm WD, Nikon) with an acquisition rate of 23.3 frames per second, covering a field of view at a resolution of 512 × 512 pixels. To capture regions containing Purkinje cell dendrites, we focused on brain tissue approximately 100 µm beneath the glass surface.

Behavioral task

We adapted our two-choice foraging task into a head-fixed version, which was controlled by an Arduino MEGA2560 board using custom-written scripts. The mice were head-fixed and restrained inside a custom-made plastic tube. At the beginning of each trial, an 800 ms 2 kHz tone was emitted through a speaker to indicate the start of the trial. The mice were trained to choose and lick one of the two metal spouts positioned in front of their mouth. Licking actions were detected using a capacitive sensor connected to the metal spouts. A decision was defined as the mice licking the same spout twice in a row. Subsequently, a reward, consisting of approximately 6 µl of 10% condensed milk, was dispensed on the side chosen by the mice through the metal spouts. During the training session on day one, mice could receive a reward from either of the two metal spouts. Once the mice met the criteria (50 trials within 45 min), they were further trained for 5–7 days to learn to switch their choice between the left and right spouts. During the imaging sessions, the mice had to select which side they expected would yield a higher reward without receiving any explicit notification. Their licking behavior and other actions were recorded with a high-speed camera at 120 frames per second.

Data preprocessing and imaging analysis

The preprocessing of the data began with our custom MATLAB scripts, which included trial-by-trial frame alignment, correction for dropped frames, and extraction of behavioral events. Next, we employed Suite2p (https://github.com/MouseLand/suite2p) to perform frame registration and detect regions of interest (ROIs) corresponding to Purkinje dendrites using a semi-automated approach. The parameters used for ROI detection are as follows: threshold_scaling: 1.0, max_overlap: 1.0, diameter: 18, 20, flow_threshold: 0, and other parameters were set to their default values for dendrite/axons detection. ROI-based calcium traces were extracted from the registered imaging data and used for subsequent event-aligned analyses.

Histology and brain clearing

Histological validation was performed with the following procedures. After the completion of behavioral tasks, mice were perfused with PBS, and their brains were post-fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA, Bionovas Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Toronto, Ontario, Canada) for 24 h at 4 °C. For brain tissue clearing, we sliced the brain with 500 µm thickness and cleared the tissues with RapiClear (SunJin Lab) for 1 week. We used a fluorescent confocal microscope (SP8, Leica) to validate the histology of cleared tissues. For frozen sections, brains were immersed in a 30% (w/v) sucrose solution for 24 h and then sectioned at a thickness of 80 µm using a cryostat. The brain slices were initially rinsed with 1× PBS three times (3 × 5 min) and subsequently incubated in a blocking buffer consisting of 1% BSA diluted in 1× TBS for 2 h at room temperature (RT). To label the VTA dopaminergic neurons, the slices were stained overnight with an anti-tyrosine hydroxylase Alexa 555-conjugated antibody (1:200, Merck Millipore, Burlington, Massachusetts, USA) or a rabbit anti-tyrosine hydroxylase antibody (1:1000, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA). To label the cerebellar Purkinje cells, the slices were immersed with a rabbit anti-calbindin antibody (1:200, Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, Massachusetts, USA) diluted in the blocking buffer at 4 °C. After the staining with 1° antibody, the slices were washed three times with 1× PBS (5 min, 5 min, and 10 min) and stained with 2° antibody (Alexa 594 donkey anti-rabbit antibody) for 2 h at room temperature. Nuclei were labeled with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA). The brain sections were then rinsed in 1× PBS three times (3 × 10 min) before being mounted onto slides. Images of the brain sections were captured using a Nikon Eclipse 80i microscope equipped with 4× and 10× objectives or a fluorescent confocal microscope (SP8, Leica) and were subsequently analyzed using ImageJ (https://imagej.net/).

Quantification and statistical analysis

Sample sizes were pre-determined by power analysis and adjusted based on the data point distribution. Data analysis was unblinded but objective, based on pre-determined computation programs. Randomization was performed for the two-choice foraging task. The first reward-getting side was randomly assigned to either right or left in each session. In the process of unit functional clustering, we used silhouette coefficients to determine the optimal number of clusters, evaluating how well each unit fit within its cluster and how distinct each cluster was from others. To compare unit firing rates across different behavioral conditions, we employed a two-sample Kolmogorov–Smirnov test to investigate whether the observed data came from the same distribution. For non-normally distributed paired behavioral data, we applied a repeated-measures non-parametric test known as the Wilcoxon matched-pairs signed rank test to compare two groups. In cases of normally distributed data, we used two-tailed paired or unpaired Student’s t tests, or in some cases, one-way ANOVA to assess differences between groups. All statistical analyses were carried out using Prism (GraphPad Software, Inc., San Diego, California, USA), standard MATLAB functions, or custom-written scripts. We reported group data characteristics using mean and standard error of the mean (SEM) to describe the central tendencies and sampling deviations. Significance levels were set as two-tailed, with *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, and ****p < 0.0001 indicating different levels of statistical significance.
