The effect of psyllium consumption on anthropometric indices: a systematic review and dose-response meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials
Authors: Zeinab Gholami, Zamzam Paknahad
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe available evidence suggests that psyllium may have a beneficial effect on anthropometric indices. Therefore, this study aims to investigate the impact of psyllium consumption on various anthropometric indices, including Waist-to-Hip Ratio, BMI, Waist Circumference, and body weight.MethodsWe conducted a systematic review and dose-response meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials, searching databases such as Scopus, the Information Sciences Institute Web of Science, and PubMed for relevant articles published between March 15, 2022, and July 22, 2025.ResultsThe review includes 27 randomized controlled trials that evaluate the effects of psyllium husk on the anthropometric parameters mentioned above in adults. The findings indicate that psyllium significantly increased body weight, with a WMD of 3.57 (95% confidence intervals: 1.43, 5.72; p-value < 0.05). However, there was a non-significant decrease in BMI, with a WMD of −0.06 (95% confidence intervals: -0.68, 0.55; p-value > 0.05), and a non-significant decrease in WHR, with a WMD of -0.10 (95% confidence intervals: -0.34, 0.12; p-value > 0.05). Additionally, there was a non-significant increase in WC, with a WMD of 0.72 (95% confidence intervals: −0.93, 2.37; p-value > 0.05).ConclusionIn summary, this study found that psyllium significantly increased body weight.RegistrationThe study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Isfahan University of Medical Sciences (IR.MUI.RESEARCH.REC.1402.090, Grant number: 140247). Introduction

The accumulation of excess fat in individuals with obesity has a complex origin, but it is generally accepted that this results from an imbalance between energy intake and expenditure [1]. The prevalence of obesity has reached a level that surpasses undernutrition and infectious diseases as the leading cause of poor health. Obesity is associated with various health conditions, including sleep-breathing disorders, certain types of coronary heart disease, cancer, and diabetes mellitus. The global rise in obesity can be attributed to a combination of genetic predisposition, increased access to high-calorie foods, and a reduced need for physical activity in modern society [2]. Anthropometry encompasses fundamental components such as head circumference, weight, height, waist circumference, and BMI, which assess body fat distribution and skinfold thickness [3]. Accurate and repeated anthropometric measurements can serve as valuable tools for detecting potential medical, nutritional, or social issues in children, as well as underlying medical concerns like eating disorders. Additionally, these measurements can assist in evaluating body composition in athletes, the nutritional status of pregnant women, and assessing individuals who are obese [3].

Dietary fiber includes two main types: lignin and indigestible carbohydrates (known as insoluble fiber), and isolated indigestible carbohydrates (known as functional fiber) [4]. Psyllium is a readily available source of dietary fiber [5] and is native to Middle Eastern countries, Iran, and India [6, 7]. One meta-analysis indicated that the psyllium seed coat consists of hexose sugars, uronic acid, and pentose. This combination works as a mixture of unfermented fibers that forms a viscous, soluble gel [8].

One study found that consuming 10.5 g of soluble fiber daily for 8 weeks led to reductions in body weight, BMI, waist circumference, and hip circumference in type 2 diabetes patients [9]. One study found that adding 10 g of psyllium pre-mixed in cookies twice per day for 12 weeks was effective in alleviating symptoms of constipation, lowering cholesterol and blood glucose levels, reducing body weight, and increasing HDL cholesterol levels in individuals with type 2 diabetes and chronic constipation [10]. However, Akbarian et al. found that a 12-week administration of 10 g/d Plantago psyllium had no effect on various anthropometric measures in patients with NAFLD [11]. One meta-analysis suggested that psyllium can be incorporated into the diet and alongside drug therapies to help reduce LDL cholesterol and blood sugar levels, although it may not affect BMI or weight [12]. One meta-analysis indicated that Increased fiber consumption is potentially linked to reductions in waist circumference, BMI, and overall body weight [8]. Consumption of combination of produced herbal candy (Portulaca oleracea, Plantago psyllium, and peanut oil) for 8 weeks are effective in reducing weight and appetite in obese and overweight people [13]. One Meta-analysis shows that psyllium administered shortly before meals (10.8 g/day for 4.8 months) is effective in reducing body weight, BMI, and WC in obese and overweight participants [14]. Another meta-analysis revealed that increased psyllium consumption caused a weight loss of 3.70 kg in adults [15]. One study show that psyllium can suppress appetite and energy intake, and reduce body weight [16].

Research question: In a systematic review and meta-analysis study, does psyllium consumption affect average changes in weight, BMI, WC, and WHR compared to control group?

Testable hypothesis: In a systematic review and meta-analysis, psyllium consumption compared to control group have an effect on average changes in weight, BMI, WC, and WHR.

Although the overall effect of psyllium on outcomes is well established, dose-dependent differences, the dose-response relationship, population differences, variations in outcomes, inconsistent dosages, duration of interventions, and the methods used to analyze these effects have not been clearly reviewed in previous studies, and the linear models applied may have potential limitations There is a lack of up-to-date systematic reviews and meta-analyses on this research, which may have different efficacy and side effect profiles compared to earlier generations. Given the conflicting findings and limited research in this area, the aim of the present study was to determine the effect of psyllium consumption on weight, BMI, WC and WHR in adults. We decided to conduct a systematic review and meta-analysis to further explore the effects of psyllium husk consumption on anthropometric indices in the adult population.

Method

Search strategy and study selection

The investigation was conducted in accordance with the principles outlined in the PRISMA guidelines. We performed a systematic review to evaluate the impact of psyllium supplementation on anthropometric indices. Our search encompassed all relevant studies available in four English-language databases (Scopus, PubMed, and WOS) that published between March 15, 2022, and July 22, 2025. For data extraction, we employed the following keywords: (“lunelax” OR “mucilage polysaccharides” OR “mucilage” OR “Plantago ovata” OR “plant mucilage” OR “psyllium” OR “ispaghul” OR “Metamucil” OR “isogel” OR “plantago” OR “psyllium husk” OR “ispaghula” OR “Plantago psyllium” OR “psyllium fiber”) AND (“pilot study” OR “trial” OR “controlled clinical trials” OR “randomly” OR “double-blind randomized controlled trial” OR “cluster randomized controlled trials” OR “clinical trial” OR “controlled trial*” OR “randomized controlled trial” OR “RCTs” OR “controlled clinical trial” OR “RCT” OR “cRCTs” OR “clinical trials” OR “clinical trial*” OR “randomized” OR “intervention*” OR “single-blind” OR “double-blind” OR “placebo” OR “cross-over studies” OR “single-blind randomized controlled trial” OR “follow-up studies” OR “random allocation” OR “single-blind method” OR “double-blind method” OR “assignment” OR “parallel” OR “cross-over” OR “comparative study”), used alone or in conjunction with “AND” and/or “OR.” We also screened the reference lists of relevant articles for additional studies. To ensure accuracy, our search was restricted to studies involving only human participants. To prevent potential duplication of efforts, two independent researchers (Z.P. and Z.Gh.) evaluated the main titles and abstracts. Furthermore, we manually searched for additional gray literature (e.g., inclusion of theses, trial registries, or conference abstracts) and activated alert systems for the PubMed and Scopus databases. In cases where we were unable to access certain articles, we reached out to the corresponding authors via email.

Eligibility criteria

This meta-analysis was conducted in accordance with the PICOS standards (Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcomes, and Study design). Accordingly, Eligible studies were RCTs involving adult participants aged 18 years and older. The intervention of interest was psyllium supplementation in any form, with control group as the comparator. The outcome was defined as changes in anthropometric indices. All adult populations were included irrespective of baseline health status.

The criteria for inclusion and exclusion were as follows:

Inclusion Criteria: (a) Randomized controlled trials (either crossover or parallel design); (b) Participants aged over 18 years; (c) Studies examining the impact of psyllium on anthropometric indices compared to a control group.

Exclusion Criteria: (a) Individuals under the age of 18; (b) Research limited to conferences, seminars, letters, or reviews; (c) In vitro studies or studies involving animal cell cultures; (d) Non-RCT studies; (e) Studies with incomplete and inaccurate data; (f) Research periods shorter than 14 days; (g) Articles lacking a control group; (h) Articles without baseline means and SD; (i) Non-English articles; and (j) Articles without accompanying SD data.

We excluded articles that did not report mean and SD, such as those that only reported numbers without any measures of mean and SD. However, some articles reported the mean ± SE. Since we aimed to standardize the measures of dispersion, we converted all SE to SD.

Data extraction

The data extraction form was completed by qualified researchers (Z.P. and Z.G.) using spreadsheets and word processing software. Both researchers carefully reviewed all selected articles. data extraction wasn’t be blinded. We reached out via email to obtain full-text articles that were previously unavailable. After thoroughly analyzing the full texts, we collected relevant data, including the authors’ names, average age of participants, publication year, study location, duration of intervention, participant population, health status of participants, sample size, study design, gender distribution, psyllium dosage, and pre- and post-intervention means ± SD of anthropometric indices. If any mean ± SD values were missing from published figures, we utilized graph digitizing software for extraction. Any additional study components were separately reported and recorded.

Quality assessment

To assess the risk of bias, two researchers (Z.P. and Z.Gh.) used the Cochrane Collaboration’s risk of bias assessment tool (Cochrane RoB 2.0 tool). Seven criteria were evaluated for each study: (a) Randomization process, (b) Deviations from intended intervention, (c) Measurement of the outcome, (d) Missing outcome data, (e) Selection of the reported result, (f) Overall risk of bias. The overall risk of bias judgment in ROB 2 is determined as low risk if all domains are low risk, some concerns if at least one domain has some concerns but none are high risk, and high risk if any domain is rated high risk [17, 18]. Data synthesis and statistical analysis

Our primary objective was to evaluate changes in anthropometric indices using a random-effects model to calculate the mean changes and SD [19]. We utilized CMA software alongside the random-effects model to assess the heterogeneity of the studies, which enabled us to obtain accurate pooled prevalence estimates with 95% confidence intervals. In academic research, an I² value greater than 50% is interpreted as an indicator of significant heterogeneity, suggesting that a random-effects model should be applied to account for variability in the data. To address heterogeneity, we employed both meta-regression and subgroup analyses in our evaluation. Specifically, meta-regression was used to explore the relationship between the duration of the studies and the dosage of psyllium. In all statistical analyses, a significance level of P < 0.05 was deemed appropriate. The analysis was conducted using version 3 of CMA [20]. If the SD of the mean difference was not reported in the articles, we calculated the SD using the following formula: SD change = √(([SD baseline]² + [SD final]² – [2R × SD baseline × SD final])) [21]. We also used the formula SD = SE to derive SD from SE. In cases where data were presented solely in graphical form, without corresponding measures of central tendency or variability, we utilized the Get Data Graph Digitizer software to extract the necessary quantitative information. We used WMD in data synthesis and statistical analysis because WMD In statistics, it refers to an average of differences in which each difference is assigned a weight. The WMD is appropriate when all included studies measure the same outcome using the same unit or scale (for example, weight in kilograms). It represents the average difference between intervention and control groups across studies, weighted by the precision of each study. WMD provides results in clinical units. In summary, WMD is preferable where the outcome metric is consistent across studies, ensuring effect sizes express actual differences in clinical units, facilitating clearer interpretation and application of findings.

Studies that are suspected to contribute to heterogeneity (e.g., studies with low quality, studies with unusual doses, or studies with specific population characteristics) are excluded. Then, the meta-analysis was reperformed, and the new results were compared with the initial results. If the exclusion of one or more studies led to a significant change in the effect size or a reduction in heterogeneity, that study (or studies) is considered a potential source of heterogeneity.

To assess the robustness of the results and identify potential sources of heterogeneity, a sensitivity analysis was conducted using the leave-one-out method. In this approach, one study was removed at a time from the meta-analysis, and the impact on the overall effect size and heterogeneity was evaluated. Overall, the sensitivity analysis indicated that the results were partially dependent on specific studies, and their removal could influence both the overall effect estimate and the degree of heterogeneity. This highlights the importance of considering the methodological quality of included studies and conducting additional analyses to assess the robustness of the findings. In this meta-analysis, a very high level of heterogeneity was observed (I² >90%), indicating substantial variation across the included studies. Several factors may have contributed to this heterogeneity. First, differences in psyllium dosage across studies (ranging from low doses to more than 10 g/day) could have significantly influenced the outcomes, as the metabolic effects of psyllium are likely dose-dependent. Second, the duration of intervention varied widely among studies, with some lasting only a few weeks while others extended for several months. Short-term interventions may not have been sufficient to produce meaningful changes in anthropometric indices. Third, differences in participant characteristics—such as age, gender, health status, and baseline BMI—could have affected individual responses to the intervention, thereby increasing variability. Additionally, variation in study quality, including aspects like randomization methods, blinding, and control for confounders, may have also played a role. This high degree of heterogeneity limits the reliability and generalizability of the pooled estimates and highlights the importance of sensitivity and subgroup analyses to identify potential sources of variability. Therefore, findings should be interpreted with caution, and future research should prioritize rigorous study design and standardized. The studies were organized based on baseline anthropometric measurements (mean ± SD), study duration (in days), participants’ BMI, and the dosage of psyllium (gr/day). 10 g, 50 days, and 50 years old represent the median or a value close to the median, making it a suitable cutoff to categorize studies into “low” and “high” for dose groups, duration groups, and age, respectively. The population was divided into two subgroups (healthy and unhealthy). Study design and Quality subgroups analyses were conducted. Meta-regression analyses were conducted to explore potential sources of heterogeneity and to examine dose-response relationships. Subgroup analyses helped identify sources of heterogeneity publication bias was assessed using funnel plots or statistical tests (e.g., Egger’s or Begg’s test).

Results

Search results

Figure 1 presents a flowchart illustrating the study screening and selection process. We activated alert notifications in PubMed and Scopus to aid in identifying relevant papers. After removing 2,070 duplicate entries, we identified 2,334 articles. We then reviewed the abstracts and titles of articles and subsequently screened 187 full-text studies. We excluded 160 articles for various reasons: 101 were unrelated, 5 had no control group, 2 were animal studies, 5 focused on children, 6 were non-English articles, 2 were not RCTs, 19 duration less down 2 weeks, 4 did not report SD, and 2 lacked baseline mean or SD data. Ultimately, 27 RCTs were included in the final meta-analysis (see Fig. 1).

Study characteristics

This scientific review identified 27 studies published between 1988 and 2022 that met the inclusion criteria. The durations of these studies varied from 14 to 180 days and involved a total of 1,352 participants, consisting of 669 controls and 683 cases. The studies were conducted in various countries, including the Gaza Strip, Malaysia, USA, Iran, Italy, USA, USA, Canada, Poland, Germany, Canada, USA, Brazil, Pakistan, the USA, and Spain. The average age of participants ranged from 24 to 61 years, and most studies included both male and female subjects. In these studies, psyllium husk was administered in daily doses ranging from 0.002 to 25 g (see Table 3). To ensure comprehensiveness and avoid excluding potentially influential studies, all articles containing relevant data were included in the analysis regardless of the initial dose. Although very low doses may have less physiological significance, this approach prevents the omission of studies that might demonstrate effects even at low doses and thus enhances the overall validity of the analysis. Additionally, these low-dose studies were carefully examined in the quantitative and sensitivity analyses and their findings were taken into account in the interpretation of the results.

Sensitivity analysis

The I² statistic was used to assess heterogeneity, categorized as follows: (I² < 25%) low heterogeneity, (I² = 25–50%) moderate heterogeneity, (I² = 50–75%) severe heterogeneity, and (I² >75%) high heterogeneity [44]. We observed significant heterogeneity for WC (I² = 89.24%, p < 0.001), BMI (I² = 83.58%, p < 0.001), WHR (I² = 98.00%, p < 0.001), and weight (I² = 93.72%, p < 0.001). Sensitivity analysis indicated that the removal of individual studies had a measurable impact on WHR (WMD changed from − 0.16 to 0.003) and BMI (WMD changed from − 0.22 to 0.12). Additionally, changes were noted for one study regarding weight (WMD changed from 0.18 to 5.35) and WC (WMD changed from − 0.38 to 1.98) (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Subgroup analysis

Subgroup analyses showed variations in the effect of psyllium on BMI, with significant differences identified between those consuming more than 10 g psyllium per day and those consuming less than 10 g psyllium per day. Regarding body weight, while differences were observed, they were not statistically significant for dosages above or below 10 g per day. Notably, significant results emerged for interventions lasting 50 days or longer. Ultimately, our findings indicated that increasing duration of consumption of psyllium could significantly increase body weight; however, there were no significant differences in BMI, WC, or WHR (refer to Supplementary Figs. 2 and 3). Subgroup analyses showed that BMI and WC significantly increased in the unhealthy group, but decreased insignificantly in the healthy group. Weight significantly decreased in the healthy group and increased significantly in the unhealthy group. Subgroup analyses based on age showed that BMI, weight, and WC significantly increased in participants younger than 50 years, while in participants older than 50 years, BMI and weight significantly decreased. Baseline subgroup analyses indicated that participants weighing less than 80 kg showed a significant decrease in weight, whereas those weighing more than 80 kg showed a significant increase. In the Study design and Quality subgroups analyses, only a few cases showed differences compared to the others which made these differences insufficient for extensive evaluation. Dose-response analysis

Meta-regression indicated linear trends between intervention characteristics and outcomes. A steeper slope in the regression line indicates a stronger association. Psyllium dosage had a significant negative effect on weight (slop: −0.28; p value < 0.05), BMI (slop: −0.07; p value < 0.05), and WC (slop: −0.53; p value < 0.05) (Fig. 6), suggesting that higher doses of psyllium were associated with greater reductions in these anthropometric indices. However, the duration did not significantly affect any of these variables (p value > 0.05) (Supplementary Fig. 9). Baseline of outcome had a significant effect on weight (slop: 0.05; p value < 0.05). However, this variable was not significantly related to changes in BMI (slop: −0.002; p value > 0.05) and WC (slop: 0.11; p value > 0.05) (Supplementary Fig. 10). Participant’s age also showed no significant associations with weight (slop: −0.04; p value > 0.05), BMI (slop: 0.004; p value > 0.05), and WC (slop: −0.04; p value > 0.05) (Supplementary Fig. 11). Publication bias

The results of Egger’s test indicated that there was no evidence of publication bias in studies examining the impact of psyllium on BMI, weight, and WHR. Specifically, the p-values were as follows: BMI at p = 0.29, weight at p = 0.21, and WHR at p = 0.65. the Egger’s test found no proof of publication bias on them; but there were in WC (p = 0.03). Begg’s test disclosed no evidence of publication bias in studies examining the effect of psyllium consumption on BMI (p = 0.62), weight (p = 1.00), WC (p = 1.00), and WHR (p = 1.00). Discussion

This study found that psyllium significantly increased body weight but had a non-significant reduction in BMI and WHR, along with a non-significant increase in WC. We observed significant heterogeneity for WC, BMI, WHR, and weight. Sensitivity analysis indicated that the removal of individual studies had a measurable impact on WHR and BMI. Additionally, changes were noted for one study regarding weight and WC. Subgroup analyses showed variations in the effect of psyllium on BMI, with significant differences identified between those consuming more than 10 g psyllium per day and those consuming less than 10 g psyllium per day. Regarding body weight, while differences were observed, they were not statistically significant for dosages above or below 10 g per day. Notably, significant results emerged for interventions lasting 50 days or longer. Ultimately, our findings indicated that increasing duration of consumption of psyllium could significantly increase body weight; however, there were no significant differences in BMI, WC, or WHR. Subgroup analyses showed that BMI and WC significantly increased in the unhealthy group, but decreased insignificantly in the healthy group. Weight significantly decreased in the healthy group and increased significantly in the unhealthy group. Subgroup analyses based on age showed that BMI, weight, and WC significantly increased in participants younger than 50 years, while in participants older than 50 years, BMI and weight significantly decreased. Baseline subgroup analyses indicated that participants weighing less than 80 kg showed a significant decrease in weight, whereas those weighing more than 80 kg showed a significant increase. In the Study design and Quality subgroups analyses, only a few cases showed differences compared to the others which made these differences insufficient for extensive evaluation. Meta-regression indicated linear trends between intervention characteristics and outcomes. A steeper slope in the regression line indicates a stronger association. Psyllium dosage had a significant negative effect on weight, BMI, and WC, suggesting that higher doses of psyllium were associated with greater reductions in these anthropometric indices. However, the duration did not significantly affect any of these variables. Baseline of outcome had a significant effect on weight. However, this variable was not significantly related to changes in BMI and WC. Participant’s age also showed no significant associations with weight, BMI, and WC. The results of Egger’s test indicated that there was no evidence of publication bias in studies examining the impact of psyllium on BMI, weight, and WHR. the Egger’s test found no proof of publication bias on them; but there were in WC. Begg’s test disclosed no evidence of publication bias in studies examining the effect of psyllium consumption on BMI, weight, WC, and WHR. Therefore, trim and fill analysis were performed, However, after trim and fill analysis, the BMI, weight, WC, and WHR (no imputed study) were decreased.

Several studies have demonstrated the beneficial effects of psyllium supplementation on anthropometric and metabolic outcomes. For example, adding psyllium husk to the diet has been reported to reduce body weight, BMI, WC, and hip circumference [9]. In individuals with type 2 diabetes and constipation, psyllium supplementation has also been shown to alleviate constipation symptoms, lower cholesterol and blood glucose levels, and increase HDL-C [10]. Conversely, Akbarian et al. found that a 12-week administration of Plantago psyllium did not significantly affect anthropometric measures in patients with NAFLD [11]. Overall, psyllium may be incorporated into dietary and pharmacological interventions to reduce LDL-C and blood glucose, although its effect on BMI and overall weight appears to be limited [12]. Additionally, consumption of an herbal candy containing Portulaca oleracea, Plantago psyllium, and peanut oil was effective in reducing weight and appetite in overweight and obese individuals [13]. Evidence from meta-analyses further supports these findings, indicating that psyllium supplementation, particularly when administered shortly before meals, can reduce body weight, BMI, and WC in overweight and obese adults [14, 15]. One study show that psyllium can suppress appetite and energy intake, and reduce body weight [16]. Increased dietary fiber intake may contribute to reductions in WC, BMI, and body weight [8].

Fiber-rich diet has been shown to reduce hunger, decrease energy intake, increase satiety, support weight loss, and prolong chewing time. Consumption of soluble fiber improves postprandial satiety by increasing gastric viscosity, which delays gastric emptying and reduces subsequent hunger. Key mechanisms involve the enhanced action of peptide YY, GLP-1, cholecystokinin, and gastric distension, as well as the slower absorption of macronutrients, collectively contributing to improved satiety and reduced postprandial blood glucose levels [9]. Enhanced satiety associated with slower glucose absorption can reduce food intake, thereby contributing to weight loss. Furthermore, psyllium husk consumption has been linked to the production of short-chain fatty acids by gut microbiota [45, 46]. Psyllium, a viscous soluble fiber with low fermentability, minimizes gastrointestinal discomfort while exerting multiple effects on body composition. These include modulation of gastric function, increased satiety, regulation of intestinal hormone secretion, and improved postprandial blood glucose responses [47]. Psyllium is rich in soluble fiber, which can reduce carbohydrate absorption as well as postprandial glucose and insulin responses. This effect may help prevent postprandial hypoglycemia, enhance satiety, and increase energy expenditure. Psyllium has also been shown to elevate levels of cholecystokinin, which activates satiety centers and suppresses food intake, and to stimulate GLP-1 secretion, thereby slowing gastric emptying and reducing appetite [13]. By improving glycemic control and increasing satiety, psyllium consumption may reduce caloric intake and insulin resistance, ultimately contributing to weight loss [14]. An unexpected finding of this meta-analysis was a significant increase in body weight following psyllium supplementation, Most prior research suggests that psyllium promotes weight loss or weight maintenance through enhanced satiety, improved gastrointestinal function, and reduced fat absorption. Several factors may explain this discrepancy. First, psyllium’s high water-binding capacity could lead to temporary weight gain due to gastrointestinal water retention, particularly in short-term studies. Second, differences in baseline BMI may influence individual responses, with participants of higher BMI potentially experiencing different weight changes compared to those with normal BMI. Third, methodological variations, such as inconsistent weight measurement techniques, inadequate control of dietary intake or physical activity, and small sample sizes, may have contributed to these unexpected results. Therefore, further long-term studies with rigorous control of confounding variables and detailed mechanistic investigations are warranted to clarify the physiological basis of this paradoxical weight increase following psyllium intake [8]. considered. Although the 3.57-kg weight gain observed in the psyllium group was statistically significant, its clinical implications remain uncertain. In adults, such an increase may be substantial, particularly if it reflects fat mass gain. However, in the absence of body composition data, it is unclear whether this change is attributable to fat, lean mass, or fluid retention. Given psyllium’s high water-binding capacity, transient weight gain due to gastrointestinal fluid retention is plausible, especially in short-term studies. If the increase is due to lean mass or fluid, it may be clinically benign or even beneficial, whereas an increase in fat mass could have adverse metabolic consequences. Future studies should include detailed body composition assessments to clarify the nature and significance of this weight change [8, 48].

Conclusion

The aim of this study was to investigate the effects of psyllium on WHR, WC, BMI, and weight, in light of the unclear results found in the existing literature. Our findings indicate that psyllium can significantly increase weight while leading to a non-significant decrease in BMI and WHR, alongside an increase in WC. Subgroup analyses indicated that consumption of more than 10 g psyllium can effect on BMI. For body weight, non-significant differences were evident for dosage. Psyllium interventions lasting 50 days or more can significantly increase body weight, but there is no significant difference in BMI, WC, or WHR. psyllium’s impact on anthropometric indices remains inconclusive and warrants further research. We emphasize the need for future trials with standardized dosages, longer durations, and control of confounding variables such as diet and physical activity.
