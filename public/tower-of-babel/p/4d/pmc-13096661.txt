ULTRA-MoCap: A Multimodal IMU and sEMG Dataset for Upper Body Joint Kinematics Analysis
Authors: Oliver Fritsche, Steven Camacho, Md Sanzid Bin Hossain, Tyler Halfpenny, Carlos Arciniegas, Joseph Dranetz, Dexter Hadley, Zhishan Guo, Hwan Choi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHuman upper limb movement prediction from multi-modal wearable sensors has applications in rehabilitation, sports analysis, assistive device control, and human-computer interaction, offering less intrusive alternatives to camera-based motion capture (MoCap) systems. Yet, current datasets often lack comprehensive multi-modal data integrating inertial measurement unit (IMU) and surface electromyography (sEMG) sensors and have limited coverage of upper limb kinematics with insufficient fidelity to capture isolated single-joint elbow motions and coordinated multi-joint shoulder movements. To address these gaps, we introduce the Upper Limb Tracking with Multi-modal Capture (ULTRA-MoCap) dataset, which integrates multi-modal sensing with IMU, sEMG, and high-fidelity upper limb kinematics modeling from a multi-degree-of-freedom musculoskeletal model. Data from IMUs on the hand, wrist, and forearm, along with combined sEMG/IMU sensors on the biceps brachii, triceps brachii, and deltoid, were collected from thirteen subjects performing five upper limb movements at different speeds. This paper describes the data collection, sensor configuration, and processing pipeline and highlights ULTRA-MoCap’s potential to support a wide range of research across biomechanics, wearable sensing, and motion analysis.Subject terms: Biomedical engineering, Mechanical engineering

Background and Summary

The study of human motion encompasses both full-body datasets and those focused on specific segments, such as upper or lower limbs. Upper limb motion, in particular, is important for performing daily tasks and is critical in fields like robotics1–3, human-action recognition4,5, fitness training6,7, and rehabilitation8,9. Despite their value, many current datasets have limitations regarding sensor integration, tracked joints, number of subjects, and breadth of actions10–16. The dataset presented in this paper aims to add to the existing corpus of motion data with multiple modalities and high-fidelity joint kinematics.

Many existing datasets have limited modality and a small number of wearable sensors, especially when targeting upper limb biomechanics. Table 1 summarizes publicly available upper-limb datasets involving healthy, unimpaired individuals and focuses on datasets incorporating wearable sensors (IMU and/or sEMG) and/or upper-limb kinematics. We selected these datasets because they are closest in scope to ULTRA-MoCap and serve as common benchmarks for upper-limb motion analysis. Clinical or pathology-specific datasets were excluded because ULTRA-MoCap focuses exclusively on healthy, unimpaired adults. Many of the datasets do not provide any surface electromyography (sEMG) data, which is critical for understanding muscle activation during upper limb movement. Datasets such as VIDIMU10, TotalCapture11, and Arm-CODA15 provide inertial measurement unit (IMU) data but lack sEMG, restricting their utility in neuromuscular modeling. Conversely, datasets like KIN-MUS UJIs12, Ninapro DB914, and HD-FW KIN16 offer sEMG and kinematic information but do not include any IMUs, limiting their relevance for wearable sensor-based motion analysis. On the other hand, datasets that provide IMU data are often sparse or focused on a narrow anatomical region. For example, Ninapro DB513 provides only one accelerometer in a Myo armband, while TotalCapture11 allocates only two IMUs to the upper limbs, decreasing the fidelity of upper-extremity tracking. In addition to modality gaps, many datasets have a specific anatomical focus, often targeting only the hand or forearm joints, not including key joints like the shoulder and elbow. For instance, KIN-MUS UJIs12, Ninapro DB513, and DB914 mainly focus on hand and wrist movements. As a result, a dataset that provides both IMUs and EMGs with detailed full upper limb (i.e., wrist, elbow, and shoulder) tracking, can provide more insights into how multi-modal sensors relate with those upper limb kinematics during different tasks. Our ULTRA-MoCap dataset addresses these requirements by offering synchronized sEMG, IMU, and MoCap-based upper limb joint kinematics for the wrist, elbow, and shoulder.

Recent years have seen rapid advances in the prediction and estimation of joint kinematics using wearable sensors attached to various upper body segments, including the hand, upper arm, and lower arm. For example, Lin et al.17 employed a BERT-based method to continuously estimate hand movement from sEMG signals, while Liu et al.18 introduced NeuroPose, a 3D hand pose tracking system using sEMG wearables. Du et al.19 proposed a TCN-LSTM hybrid model for continuous wrist joint angle estimation. Additional methods have explored deep learning architectures for wrist kinematics prediction using sEMG, such as CNN-based regression20, CNN-LSTM hybrids21, and domain adaptation strategies for inter-subject generalization20. Sommer et al.22 estimated elbow joint angles from biceps brachii, triceps brachii, and brachioradialis sEMG using autoregressive models, and Wang et al.23 used a transformer-based approach for elbow angle prediction. Little et al.24 evaluated machine learning techniques for elbow kinematics using multimodal wearables. Ma et al.25 also introduced an SCA-LSTM model for estimating upper-limb joint angles from sEMG. While many of these studies focus on isolated joints like the wrist or elbow, our dataset targets coordinated upper limb motion—including shoulder, elbow, and wrist—using synchronized IMU, sEMG, and MoCap-based upper limb joint kinematics, enabling broader application in biomechanics and intelligent assistive systems.

ULTRA-MoCap fills a critical gap in upper limb motion research by offering a synchronized, multi-modal dataset that combines MoCap-based upper limb joint kinematics, IMUs, and sEMG data. With its focus on coordinated shoulder, elbow, and wrist motion, the dataset provides full anatomical coverage and offers broader joint and modality coverage than most existing upper-limb datasets by capturing these signals within a single synchronized framework. This multi-modal synchronous design enables better insights into joint kinematics, supporting valuable insights for applications such as rehabilitation, human-computer interaction, and intelligent assistive technologies.

Methods

The study (STUDY00006743) was approved by the institutional review board (IRB) at the University of Central Florida. All participants provided their informed consent in writing prior to participating in the study. The consent form also specified that participants’ de-identified data would be retained and could be shared externally without requiring additional informed consent.

Participants

The study involved 13 healthy adult participants (10 males, 3 females), age 24.4 ± 4.4 years, height 167.9 ± 7.4 cm, and weight 64.7 ± 12.6 kg. The selection criteria ensured that all participants had no known musculoskeletal injuries to avoid impacting the range of motion (ROM) or sensor data collection. Performed Tasks

All the participants performed a total of 16 trials consisting of 5 distinct upper-limb exercises: arm swing, crossbody reach, elbow flexion, shoulder rotation, and overhead reach, for different conditions. Movement speeds were intentionally varied based on the characteristics and physical demands of each task. For arm-swing trials, a fourth “very fast” speed was included because the motion is cyclic and low-load, enabling participants to perform even high-speed repetitions for the full 30-second duration without strain. In contrast, movements involving isolated elbow or wrist rotations were limited to three speeds as adding a higher speed increased the risk of discomfort or early fatigue during a 30-second trial. For the overhead-reach task, participants performed all three variations at a single self-selected comfortable speed to ensure that elevation angle (90°, 180°, and maximal ROM) was the only deliberate source of variation across trials. High-elevation reaching is more physically demanding than the other tasks, and maintaining multiple speed conditions during 30-second trials would have increased fatigue and reduced consistency of form. Using one speed therefore ensured safe, repeatable performance while still capturing the elevation-related differences of interest. A sample overview of each exercise is provided in Fig. 1. For each trial, subjects were instructed to perform the target movements at a constant speed continuously for 30 seconds. To reduce the risk of muscle fatigue, which could influence the quality of sEMG signal, one-minute rest period was provided between each trial. Arm Swing

Subjects performed bilateral arm swings in the sagittal plane with palms facing down. The movement started with the hands in front, swinging above the head, and then back as far as the subject felt comfortable. Four self-selected speed conditions were tested: slow, normal, fast, and very fast. The trials were ordered by progressively increasing speed. The objective was to capture the full ROM for the shoulder joint.

Crossbody Reach

Each subject performed crossbody reach at three different speeds: slow, normal, and fast. For this task, subjects began with their hands above their heads and reached across their body to touch the opposite side of their thigh. As with the prior trials, speed was subject-determined. This movement involved a combination of shoulder flexion and adduction, focusing on ROM in both sagittal and frontal planes, respectively.

Elbow Flexion

The task of elbow flexion was recorded for each subject at three speeds: slow, normal, and fast. Subjects kept their upper arms stationary at their sides, palms facing up, and performed repeated bilateral elbow flexion and extension. Subjects were asked to keep their bodies still and move only their forearms—from the elbow to the hand—without using or moving their upper arms. The task was designed to capture the full ROM of the elbow joint.

Shoulder Rotation

Subjects began with their shoulders abducted to 90°, with elbows flexed at 90°. They then rotated their shoulders to turn their palms downward and back, staying within a comfortable range of motion. This movement is designed to capture the shoulder’s internal and external rotation. This task was performed at three self-selected speeds: slow, normal, and fast.

Overhead Reach

The overhead reach task involved shoulder abduction with three distinct levels of reach: maximal overhead reach, shoulder level (180° ROM), and parallel to the ground (90° ROM). These movements were performed in the frontal plane with both arms placed at either side open with palms open. For each particular level, subjects raised their arms laterally to the designated level and returned to the resting position.

MoCap

A twelve-infrared-camera-based motion capture system (Vero, Vicon, U.K.) was used to track marker trajectories at a sampling frequency of 100 Hz. A custom full-body marker set with sixty markers (thirty-seven used for upper body tracking and the remainder used for lower, based on a modified Helen-Hayes marker set26) was used for capturing the motion data. A denser upper-body marker configuration was chosen to improve tracking robustness in the shoulder and elbow regions, which are highly sensitive to soft-tissue motion and occlusion. The setup and marker placement are provided in Fig. 2. Vicon Nexus software was used for marker data processing and gap filling to ensure that missing or unlabeled marker trajectories were handled appropriately. Short gaps were filled automatically using built-in algorithms, while longer gaps were corrected manually using cyclic, pattern, rigid body, or spline interpolation techniques to ensure data continuity and integrity. The data were meticulously reviewed frame by frame to ensure no swapping occurred between markers.

Wearable Sensors

Wearable sensors with integrated sEMG and IMU capabilities were used as part of the multi-modal data collection setup. The placement strategy for these sensors was designed to balance data richness with signal reliability, participant comfort, and natural movement. Because wearable units require stable skin contact and introduce some physical bulk, placing too many on a single limb can increase the likelihood of sensor movement or subtly influence movement execution during repeated trials. To avoid these issues while still capturing informative upper-limb signals, sensors were positioned over major superficial muscles—including the biceps brachii, triceps brachii, and deltoid—and along key limb segments on the hand and forearm. This configuration reflects the anatomical locations shown in Fig. 2(b), where the three sEMG/IMU units (S4-S6) are placed on the upper arm over muscles, and the remaining IMUs (S1-S3) are placed from the hand through the mid-forearm to the proximal forearm to track distal segment dynamics. sEMG

sEMG data were collected using an Avanti Trigno Wireless EMG/IMU system (Delsys, Boston, MA) at a sampling frequency of 2000 Hz. sEMG electrodes were placed on the right biceps brachii, triceps brachii, and deltoid muscles. To ensure standardized and reproducible placement, electrodes were positioned following SENIAM-recommended locations27,28. For the biceps brachii, the electrode was placed along the line from the acromion to the cubital fossa, approximately one-third of the distance from the cubital fossa, where the long and short heads converge—providing a representative composite signal. The triceps electrode was placed over the long head, midway between the posterior acromion and the olecranon. The deltoid electrode was positioned over the middle deltoid belly, aligned with the direction of the muscle fibers, reflecting its primary role in shoulder abduction. These muscles were chosen because they are superficial, produce reliable EMG signals, and play a major role in driving upper-limb motion29, enabling the sensors to capture informative muscle activation patterns throughout the various movements. sEMG placements can be seen in Fig. 2.

IMU

In addition to sEMG, acceleration and angular velocity data were captured from six Delsys Trigno Avanti IMU sensors placed on the right arm (Fig. 2) at a sampling rate of 2000 Hz. Three of these six IMUs (S4, S5, and S6) were placed to collect sEMG and IMU data from the right biceps brachii, triceps brachii, and deltoid muscles. The other three IMUs were positioned on the center of the hand, at two evenly spaced points along the forearm, and on the upper arm and back to capture comprehensive motion data for the upper limb.

Musculoskeletal Modeling and Joint Kinematics

We utilize the scaling and inverse kinematics (IK) tools of the open-source musculoskeletal modeling software OpenSim30 to process the marker trajectories acquired from the motion capture system. Specifically, we use the scaling tool of OpenSim30 to create a custom musculoskeletal model to match the subject’s anthropometric measures by aligning markers placed on anatomical landmarks (model markers) with the corresponding experimental marker data from the motion capture. The bilateral upper extremity and trunk (BUET) model developed by Chen et al.31 was used as the generic model prior to subject-specific scaling. This model is particularly well-suited for analyzing complex upper limb movements, as it incorporates detailed anatomical representations of the shoulder girdle, scapula, and clavicle, along with the rotator cuff and elbow-spanning muscles. It was originally developed for studying cross-country sit-skiing but is adaptable to various upper-body motion analyses. During the scaling process, we ensured that the root-mean-square error (RMSE) between model markers and experimental markers was less than 3 cm for all subjects. After scaling, we used the IK tool to compute joint angles by minimizing the difference between the trajectories of the model markers and experimental markers. For the IK process, we ensured that the RMSE remained below 4 cm during each frame to maintain tracking accuracy. Although data from 60 markers were collected, only 37 markers on the upper extremities and trunk were used for the scaling and IK processes. This selective inclusion excluded lower extremity markers to maintain compatibility with the generic model and improve the accuracy of upper limb kinematic analysis.

Technical Validation

Experimental Procedures

For the experimental procedures, validation of proper setup and trial completion was carried out through a series of systematic steps, including (1) confirming marker and sensor placement, (2) verifying marker visibility in Vicon Nexus, (3) checking sensor signal continuity after each trial, and (4) reviewing all recorded trials for marker stability. All the participants wore sixty MoCap markers and six IMU/EMG sensors, followed by a visual inspection and verification of correct placement by cross-checking with our custom marker set and SENIAM guidelines to ensure proper placement of all markers and sensors. Marker placement was performed by trained experimenters following a standardized protocol, with all sessions overseen by experienced team members to ensure consistency across subjects. To further verify the correct placement of markers and sensors, the Vicon Nexus visual interface was used to confirm marker visibility through the camera system, ensuring reliable motion capture and accurate sensor data collection. As for the procedures involving the subjects performing each distinct exercise, with every trial, coordinators ensured that subjects performed the motion for at least 30 seconds. After each trial, EMG/IMU sensors were checked to ensure continuous data collection and proper contact between the sensors and the subject. Once all trials were completed, the coordinator reviewed each recording in Vicon Nexus to assess marker visibility and stability throughout the duration of the trial. If any errors were identified during these checks, the corresponding trial was repeated. These validation procedures ensured the reliability of the collected data for further processing.

Data Processing

Several validation processes were implemented to ensure the integrity and reliability of the collected data. These included MoCap camera calibration, visual verification of MoCap data, scaling and IK processing in OpenSim, and final inspection of the generated joint kinematics.

Motion and Sensor Collection

Before initiating data collection, the Vicon MoCap cameras were calibrated using the Vicon Nexus system. If a marker detached during a trial, the recording was restarted to ensure complete and accurate data capture. MoCap data were visually inspected frame by frame to ensure no switching between the marker labels and gaps in the marker trajectories. This verification process was repeated multiple times per trial as necessary to maintain data quality. Marker trajectories were considered valid only if they followed anatomically correct paths throughout the trial. For static trials, extensive revision was generally unnecessary. However, each static trial was reviewed to confirm the presence of all 60 markers. Once verified, the MoCap data were prepared for processing in OpenSim30.

Scaling and Inverse Kinematics

Static trials from each subject were processed in OpenSim, with marker weights adjusted, to achieve an RMSE of 3 cm or less between the experimental and OpenSim model virtual markers33. This level of accuracy ensured that the subject-specific model was appropriately scaled and suitable for running the IK tool for dynamic trials. The motion trials were processed via the IK tool using the scaled subject model to achieve an RMSE below 4 cm. Once the motion files were generated with this level of accuracy, they were visually inspected to confirm anatomical correctness and temporal consistency. After successful visual verification, the motion data were considered ready for further analysis.

Validation with Classification

To validate the quality of the sensor data in our collected dataset, we trained a hybrid Conv1D-BiGRU classifier as a representative deep learning architecture to classify five exercise types, including overhead reach, elbow flexion, arm swing, crossbody reach, and shoulder rotation. By leveraging data from both IMU (accelerometer and gyroscope) and sEMG sensors, the model was able to learn distinct signal patterns associated with each movement type. This classification task provides an effective way to assess the accuracy and reliability of the sensor data in our dataset.

Model Architecture

We implemented a hybrid Conv1D-BiGRU architecture that begins with an input convolutional front-end, which extracts morphological features from the raw sensor streams. As we included sEMG data, extracting morphological feature help to distinguish different shapes of IMU and sEMG. Then, we added the bidirectional GRU layer, which helps to capture temporal dependencies across the time-series sequence. The output from the final recurrent layer was aggregated over time using mean pooling and passed through a fully connected layer to produce the exercise type. We trained three variants of this model: an IMU-only model, an sEMG-only model, and an early-fusion IMU+sEMG model that concatenates both modalities along the channel dimension.

Evaluation Protocols

We employed three data-splitting techniques to validate the dataset. First, we use the leave-one-subject-out cross-validation (LOSO-CV) approach34,35 to evaluate the model’s ability to generalize to unseen subjects, ensuring proper validation of the deep learning framework. Next, we introduce two additional evaluation settings to examine whether sEMG signals are highly personalized in a way that may degrade performance when testing on unseen individuals. Case 1 (Within-Subject Personalized Training) evaluates each subject independently by splitting that subject’s data into an 80% training/validation pool and a 20% held-out test set. This setting captures the upper-bound performance when a model is trained and tested solely on the same individual, highlighting the degree to which sEMG patterns are person-specific. Case 2 (Cross-Subject + Personalized Training) uses the same 20% personalized test set as Case 1, but expands the training data by combining all data from the other 12 subjects with the personalized 80% training set. This setting measures how cross-subject data affect performance when personal data from the target subject are included alongside external subjects, allowing us to observe whether introducing population-level variability increases uncertainty and degrades sEMG performance. Together, these evaluation modes quantify the strength of inter-subject variability in sEMG signals and clarify how much personalization may be needed for robust EMG-based classification.

Implementation Details

Data windows of 200 timesteps (2 seconds) were used during training and testing without any overlapping. The model was trained to minimize cross-entropy loss between predicted and true labels. Training was performed for 100 epochs using the AdamW optimizer (learning rate = 0.001, weight decay = 0.01) with a batch size of 128. A ReduceLROnPlateau scheduler and an early-stopping criterion with a patience of 10 epochs were applied to ensure stable optimization and prevent overfitting. Gradient clipping with a max norm of 1 was applied to ensure stable optimization. All data inputs were standardized using z-score normalization before model training (Fig. 5).

Performance Evaluation

Performance was evaluated using accuracy, precision, recall, and confusion matrix analysis36. Across the LOSO-CV, IMU-based classification achieved a mean accuracy of 94.63%, and fusing the IMU with sEMG improved the mean accuracy to 95.67%. On the other hand, the sEMG-only model performed poorly with 53.22% accuracy, which could have been due to strong inter-subject variability in the sEMG signals. The IMU and sEMG+IMU conditions exhibited highly separable class distributions, confirming the consistency and reliability of the kinematic data, whereas sEMG alone showed substantial overlap between movement types. Per-class precision and recall were fairly consistent within each modality (Table 5), while OR performed the poorest overall. As we combined three types of movement in the OR class (max ROM, 180° ROM, and 90° ROM), which differ more than the speed changes contained within the classes, the models had more difficulty classifying the OR condition. This pattern is most pronounced in the sEMG-only condition, where variability in muscle activation and the presence of three OR variants increased model confusion. The IMU and fused models showed clean OR boundaries, highlighting the stabilizing role of kinematic information. Conversely, the EF condition appeared to be the easiest for the models to classify. This was likely due to the simplicity of the movement executed and the isolated use of the biceps brachii to perform that motion.

To further assess modality generalization, we evaluated two additional settings: (Case 1) within-subject personalized training, where sEMG accuracy rose to 87.52%, IMU achieved 99.84%, and sEMG+IMU reached 99.68%, demonstrating that sEMG-only models benefit substantially from personalization; and (Case 2) mixed-subject training, where sEMG improved moderately to 68.76%, yet IMU-only and fused models remained near-perfect at 99.53%, confirming that IMU data generalizes strongly across individuals. These results suggest that inter-subject variance in sEMG signals makes cross-subject classification difficult, likely due to differences in individual control strategies (e.g., co-contractions or reliance on different muscle groups) used to perform the same movement. Detailed per-class precision, recall, F1-scores, and confusion matrices for Case 1 and Case 2 are included in the supplementary materials (Tables S1-S2, Figures S1-S2).

Overall, these results demonstrate that the dataset provides high-quality multi-modal sensor signals suitable for reliable modeling, while also revealing meaningful modality-dependent behavior that validates the rigor of our evaluation framework. Through multiple validation approaches—including careful review of data collection protocols, verification of processing pipelines, visualization of kinematic trajectories, and machine learning-based classification analyses—we further confirm the reliability and technical quality of the ULTRA-MoCap.

Limitations and Future Work

We used a compact configuration of sEMG and IMU sensors to reduce bulk and improve user comfort, future studies can expand the dataset using thin, low-profile sEMG37 and IMU wearables to broaden muscle coverage without restricting natural movement. The movement set focused on controlled shoulder and elbow motions to provide clean, repeatable data for joint-level benchmarking, while future work may explore more complex ADL-based and scapula-intensive movements.

Potential Research Applications

This dataset serves as a versatile benchmark for multi-modal sensor fusion, enabling tasks such as kinematics estimation, motion prediction, and exercise classification. These applications offer valuable insights into how integrating diverse sensing modalities can advance rehabilitation, assistive device control, and human movement analysis. Building on these capabilities, ULTRA-MoCap’s synchronized MoCap kinematics, sEMG, and IMU signals support a wider set of upper-limb research directions than existing public datasets. Beyond sensor-to-kinematics modeling, the dataset enables sparse-sensor kinematic estimation, IMU-sEMG fusion, and data-driven prediction of upper-limb joint trajectories across different speeds. It is also suitable for developing movement-classification models, evaluating sensor-placement strategies, and assessing robustness under reduced or missing sensing—critical considerations for practical wearable systems. With full marker trajectories, subject-specific musculoskeletal models, and multi-DOF joint angles, ULTRA-MoCap further supports biomechanics investigations such as neuromuscular-kinematic coupling, joint coordination, inter-subject variability analysis, and validation of inverse-kinematics or musculoskeletal-modeling workflows. Because only the right arm is instrumented with sensors and exercises were performed symmetrically on both arms, the dataset additionally enables testing whether sensor placement affects kinematics relative to the non-instrumented limb. Overall, these capabilities make ULTRA-MoCap a useful dataset for both machine-learning and biomechanics research.
