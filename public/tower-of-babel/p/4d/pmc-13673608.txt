Medical students as assistants to physicians and hospital volunteers in Poland: a nationwide mapping study of emerging clinical roles
Authors: Maciej Biskupski, Marzena Zygo, Marek Majewski, Kamila Ćwik, Anna Torres, Kamil Torres
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHealth workforce shortages across Europe are limiting care capacity and these shortages are expected to worsen. In Poland, hospitals have increasingly engaged medical students in paid assistant-to-a-physician roles (PA) and structured volunteer positions (VOL), which may support service delivery while also offering students clinical experience. However, the scale, governance, supervision, eligibility criteria, working-time rules, and scopes of practice for PA/VOL engagement have not yet been mapped nationally. This study aimed to characterize the extent, distribution, and organisational features of PA/VOL engagement across Polish public hospitals.MethodsA nationwide mapping study of all public hospitals in Poland was conducted. They were sent a structured 10-item request and asked them to report on their current PA/VOL engagement, the units involved, the eligibility requirements, the supervision arrangements, the working-time limits, the internal regulatory acts and the template duty scopes.ResultsOf the 549 hospitals contacted, 394 responded, yielding a response rate of 71.8%. Among responding hospitals, 99 (25.1%) reported employing PAs and/or engaging VOLs, comprising 562 PAs and 491 VOLs. There was pronounced regional variation: in some regions hospitals engaged hundreds of medical students, whereas in others these roles were not reported at all. Emergency departments were the most frequently reported settings. Ward/unit heads were most commonly indicated as supervisors, and hospitals reported various monthly hour caps. Only 36 of the 99 hospitals engaging students in PA and/or VOL roles reported having internal regulations governing these roles. Eligibility criteria for PAs most often required completion of Year 3 or 4, while 22 hospitals reported no requirements beyond student status. Duty profiles differed: VOL tasks more often reflected bedside support, whereas PA tasks were predominantly linked to documentation and care coordination.ConclusionsMedical student clinical engagement in Poland has expanded rapidly yet it remains fragmented and unevenly governed. There is marked variation in eligibility criteria, supervision descriptions, working-time protections, and internal regulations. Implementing a minimum national framework that defines the scope of roles by training stage, requires mandatory onboarding training, establishes clear lines of supervision, and ensures working-time safeguards could reduce unwarranted variation while improving patient safety, educational value, and utility of workforce.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12913-026-15190-x.

Introduction

Health workforce shortages remain a major constraint on timely access to care across Europe and are expected to worsen as demand rises and the workforce ages [1–4]. In Poland, capacity constraints are particularly evident in the comparatively low number of key clinical staff: in 2023, the country reported 3.9 practising physicians per 1,000 population (below the EU average of 4.3) and 5.9 nurses per 1,000 (below the EU average of 8.5) [1]. Stakeholder analyses in Poland further describe these shortages as system-level risks, affecting service availability and continuity, and emphasizing the need for multi-pronged responses beyond single-policy levers [2].

One prominent policy response has been to increase the supply of future physicians by expanding undergraduate medical education [5]. Legislative reforms in Poland have broadened the range of higher education institutions eligible to deliver medical degree programmes, accompanied by a substantial rise in admission quotas — reported to have increased by 92.3% between 2013 and 2023, reaching 10,289 available first-year places in 2023 [5]. While this expansion may help to address long-term workforce shortages, it does not directly resolve the short-term staffing pressures experienced by hospitals and clinical units [1, 2, 5].

Concurrently, medical students have repeatedly been mobilized to support health services during periods of acute strain, most visibly during the COVID-19 pandemic [6, 7]. Polish studies document large-scale student volunteering in various hospital and system-support tasks, highlighting both service contributions and learning-related benefits reported by students [6, 7]. These experiences have intensified interest in more structured forms of student engagement in clinical environments that can support service delivery while safeguarding educational priorities and patient safety [6–8].

In Poland, this interest has developed along two parallel institutional routes. The first is a paid assistant-to-a-physician role, commonly termed Asystent Lekarza (PA), in which medical students are employed or engaged under civil-law arrangements to support defined hospital tasks. The second is structured hospital volunteering (VOL), in which students provide unpaid support under volunteering agreements. For clarity, the PA role described in this study refers specifically to a hospital-based role performed by medical students in Poland and is distinct from the established non-physician physician assistant / physician associate profession known from the United States and other countries.

The coexistence of paid and unpaid pathways likely reflects different starting points for student engagement. Paid PA roles may emerge where hospitals identify a need for a more regular and formalised form of student support within clinical services. In contrast, VOL roles may more often reflect students’ own motivation to enter the clinical environment, gain exposure to patient care, develop practical skills, and contribute to hospital activity. Thus, PA roles can be viewed as more hospital-driven and service-oriented, whereas VOL roles may be more student-driven and experience-oriented.

However, the scale of these arrangements, their geographic distribution, governance and supervision models, eligibility criteria, internal policies, time-limit rules, and reported scopes of practice have not been comprehensively mapped at the national level. Therefore, the aim of this study was to map the landscape of medical student employment as PAs and engagement as VOLs across public hospitals in Poland, and to characterize (1) the extent and regional distribution of engagement, (2) the clinical settings in which students are deployed, (3) the supervision arrangements and working-time limits, (4) the eligibility requirements and the presence of internal regulatory acts, and (5) the reported scopes of duties for both roles.

Methods

This nationwide, cross-sectional study mapped the use of medical students in two hospital-based roles in Poland: an assistant-to-a-physician role and hospital volunteering. The sampling frame comprised all public hospitals in Poland identified via the National Health Fund (NFZ) hospital listings [9]. A structured information request, developed specifically for this national mapping study, was sent to each eligible hospital. It was not intended to measure latent attitudes, perceptions, or behavioural constructs, and therefore was not designed as a psychometric survey instrument. Instead, it was constructed to capture factual institutional arrangements across predefined domains derived from the study objective: the extent and regional distribution of student engagement, the clinical settings involved, eligibility requirements, internal regulation, supervision arrangements, working-time safeguards, and reported scope of duties. These domains were treated as the conceptual framework for describing how medical student engagement was organised at hospital level.

The item set was developed and reviewed by a multidisciplinary author team with expertise in medical education and accreditation (K.T.), legal and regulatory aspects of hospital practice (M.Z.), and healthcare management and quality (K.Ć.). This process was used to support face and content relevance of the items for the Polish hospital context.

A structured set of 10 questions was used to capture following information: (1) whether the hospital had active employment/civil-law agreements with medical students to perform PA duties and, if so, the number of currently active agreements, the number involving students from universities affiliated with the Conference of Rectors of Academic Medical Universities (KRAUM), and the year in which such agreements began; (2) whether the hospital had active volunteering agreements with medical students and, if so, the number of active agreements and the number involving KRAUM-affiliated students; (3) the hospital units/organisational cells in which PAs and VOLs performed duties; (4) eligibility requirements/required qualifications for both roles; (5) the existence of an internal legal act regulating recruitment/engagement procedures (with a request to provide the document if available); (6) the presence of any formalised career or progression pathway; (7) whether work performed during concurrent academic teaching/placements could be credited toward curricular requirements when aligned with learning outcomes; (8) supervision arrangements; and (9–10) template scopes of duties and the maximum permitted working time for volunteers and for PAs.

Hospital responses (including any attached internal documents) were reviewed and extracted into a structured dataset. Quantitative items (e.g. the number of agreements, years of initiation, limits on working time) were recorded as reported. Free-text descriptions of hospital units, supervision arrangements, eligibility criteria, and scopes of duties were coded into harmonised categories to enable frequency summaries. Where a response contained multiple elements, all applicable categories were retained. Accordingly, percentages for hospital-unit categories, supervision arrangements, and duty categories were calculated using the relevant hospital denominator and may sum to more than 100% when categories were not mutually exclusive. Items not reported or insufficiently specified were coded as “not specified”.

Analyses were descriptive. The prevalence of PA/VOL engagement among responding hospitals, total reported numbers of engaged students, and the distribution of engagement across voivodeships was summarized. We also tabulated the frequency of reported hospital units, supervision models, eligibility requirements, internal regulatory acts, working-time limits, and duty categories. Figure and tables were prepared to visualise regional distribution and the most commonly reported organisational and role-related characteristics. The base map template used to create Fig. 1 was obtained from Free Vector Maps (freevectormaps.com; Map of Poland with Provinces - Single Color) [10].

For additional contextual analysis, hospitals reporting PA and/or VOL engagement were classified by locality size: rural location, town below 50,000 inhabitants, town of 50,000–100,000 inhabitants, city of 100,000–500,000 inhabitants, and city above 500,000 inhabitants. To contextualise regional variation, Statistics Poland data on the number of medical students in each voivodeship as of 31 December 2025 were used to provide voivodeship-level background in Supplementary Table 1.

This study did not involve human participants, human material, or identifiable human data. The dataset consisted exclusively of aggregated institutional information and non-sensitive internal documents provided by public hospitals in response to a written information request. No patient-level data were collected. Therefore, ethics committee approval and informed consent were not required for this study.

To contextualise the findings in the discussion, we conducted a targeted review of relevant peer-reviewed literature using PubMed, supplemented by grey literature, focusing on clinical employment and assistantship-type roles for medical students in other countries.

Results

Of the 549 hospitals identified, 394 (71.8%) provided a response. Based on the data received, 562 medical students were employed in hospitals in PA roles, and 491 medical students were engaged as hospital VOLs. Overall, 99 hospitals in Poland were identified as employing medical students in PA and/or VOL roles (25.1% of responding hospitals). The first hospital reported implementing PA employment in 2021, which is the earliest reported start of PA employment.

Of the students whose medical schools were reported, at least 86% of PAs and 58% of VOLs were affiliated with universities belonging to the Conference of Rectors of Academic Medical Universities (KRAUM). However, multiple hospitals noted that they do not systematically record students’ university affiliation, suggesting that these proportions may be underestimated.

Regional variation in student engagement was substantial (Fig. 1). The highest number of PAs was reported in Pomeranian voivodeship (n = 210), while the highest number of VOLs was reported in Greater Poland voivodeship (n = 102). Several voivodeships reported very low or zero counts for one of the roles, including no PAs in Opole and Holy Cross voivodeships and no VOLs in Warmian–Masurian voivodeship. Notably, the University Clinical Centre in Gdańsk reported the largest PA workforce nationwide, employing 202 PAs across 24 hospital departments/units.

To describe the characteristics of hospitals reporting PA and/or VOL engagement, we further analysed their regional distribution and locality size. When classified by locality size, 51 of the 99 hospitals were located in cities with at least 100,000 inhabitants, including 15 in cities above 500,000 inhabitants and 36 in cities of 100,000–500,000 inhabitants. A further 13 hospitals were located in towns of 50,000–100,000 inhabitants, 33 in towns below 50,000 inhabitants, and 2 in rural locations. This indicates that reported PA/VOL engagement was concentrated mainly in urban clinical settings, particularly in cities with at least 100,000 inhabitants. Detailed contextual information on locality size and voivodeship-level medical student population is provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Across hospital units, the Emergency Department was the most frequently reported setting in which students were engaged (Table 1). In terms of governance, the ward/unit head was most commonly indicated as the direct supervisor for PAs/VOLs (Table 2). National summaries of eligibility criteria and working-time limits, alongside the regional distribution of hospitals engaging medical students in PA and/or VOL roles, are presented in Table 3. Eligibility criteria for employment in the PA role most commonly included completion of the 4th (n = 43) or the 3rd year (n = 18) year of medical school. Other frequently listed requirements included university approval, up-to-date occupational health clearance, a criminal record check, and liability/accident insurance (OC/NNW). Notably, 22 hospitals reported no additional requirements beyond being a medical student. Only a minority of hospitals (n = 36) reported having internal regulations governing the engagement of PAs and/or VOLs.

Reported scopes of practice differed between volunteers and PAs (Table 4). For VOLs, the most frequently cited duties included assisting medical staff in patient care (n = 44), assisting during physical examination and/or medical history taking (n = 36), and administrative/documentation support (n = 25), followed by nursing/caregiving assistance (n = 22) and assisting with procedures/interventions (n = 12). Less frequently reported were participation in ward rounds (n = 8), emotional support/patient communication (n = 7), and participation in diagnostics/imaging (n = 6); scope of duties was not specified in 10 instances (n/a). For PAs, the most frequently reported tasks were documentation/data entry/registration (n = 49), monitoring test results and notifying the physician (n = 32), administrative/office duties (n = 29), and assisting with admissions and discharges (n = 27), alongside patient logistics/transport coordination (n = 26). Clinical-facing activities were also commonly reported, including assisting with examinations and procedures (n = 21) and patient communication (n = 22), as well as technical assistance during procedures (n = 14) and participation in ward rounds (n = 12). Scope of duties was not specified in 10 instances (n/a).

Across all responses, no hospital described a formalised “career” or progression pathway for students in either the PA or VOL role. In addition, when asked whether students could obtain academic credit (e.g., course, placement, or internship credit) for work activities aligned with learning outcomes, hospitals consistently responded negatively, explaining that such recognition is governed by university regulations rather than by hospital policy.

Discussion

This national mapping study shows that, while Polish public hospitals have begun to engage medical students as PAs and VOLs, the model is still fragmented and unevenly distributed. Engagement was concentrated in certain regions and institutions, with some hospitals reporting large PA workforces while others reported none. In Poland, PA roles were predominantly oriented toward administrative and care-coordination activities (e.g. documentation, data entry, admission and discharge processes, patient logistics, and test-result monitoring and reporting), whereas VOL roles more commonly involved general supportive activities closer to bedside care. These patterns suggest that hospitals may be using students as a service buffer during staffing strain, but without consistent national safeguards. International experience provides several useful comparators for interpreting the Polish findings, particularly in relation to role design, supervision, and integration with clinical teams [11].

In New South Wales, Australia, the Assistant in Medicine programme was introduced during the COVID-19 medical workforce surge as a supervised, paid role for final-year medical students. The programme was developed through collaboration between the Ministry of Health, medical schools, health districts, and the Health Education and Training Institute, and included a defined position description, identification of required skills and capabilities, paid employment conditions and a part-time structure [12, 13]. Evaluation findings suggested that the role was well received and useful during the pandemic response, and that it may have supported transition to internship; however, the same evaluation also identified implementation challenges, including variable access to electronic medical records, uncertainty about scope, and the need for clearer communication and standardisation [13]. A pilot study in an emergency department setting further suggested potential benefits for workflow and efficiency, although such findings should be interpreted as early and context-specific rather than definitive evidence of system-wide effectiveness [14].

The Israeli medical student resident assistant model provides another relevant comparison. It represents a longer-standing, paid hospital-based role in which medical students in their clinical years assist physicians during evening shifts under supervision [15]. The model illustrates how student employment can become embedded in hospital workforce practice over time, but it also highlights the need for more systematic evaluation: the available evidence is largely descriptive and does not establish causal effects on patient outcomes, service quality, or educational benefit [15]. Finally, qualitative evidence from transitional student-to-doctor roles shows that students’ learning and preparedness may be enhanced when they are genuinely integrated into clinical teams, given meaningful but bounded responsibilities, and supported by appropriate organisational conditions, including defined duties and adequate access to clinical systems [16]. Taken together, these examples do not prove that student assistantship models are universally effective or safe. Rather, they show that when such roles are introduced, their value and safety depend heavily on explicit role definition, supervision, onboarding, communication, and alignment between hospitals and medical schools.

A key finding of this study is the wide variability in locally defined requirements (including instances where “being a medical student” was sufficient). In contrast, several jurisdictions implement senior-student clinical employment through formal role descriptions, defined eligibility thresholds, and specified supervision responsibilities. For example, Denmark has a recognised pathway in which senior medical students (> 8th semester) can apply for paid, time-limited subordinate medical posts (locum doctor employments; lægevikar), with typical tasks including ward rounds and preparation of admission records—illustrating a step-up in responsibility that is explicitly linked to training stage and embedded within the workforce structure [17]. While the Danish model is not identical to “assistant to a physician”, it demonstrates that structured progression and role clarity for senior students are achievable at national scale. Similarly, the Assistant in Medicine programme provided a concrete example of role definition: the programme specified eligibility, required completion of mandatory training and orientation, and included explicit working-time protections—a cap of 32 h per week and a prohibition on night shifts [13, 18]. Collectively, these components align with the domains in which our Polish data showed marked variability, particularly eligibility thresholds, onboarding/preparation requirements, and working-time protections, alongside differences in how supervision was described across hospitals. Evidence from the UK’s “Medical Student Technician” (MST) initiatives developed during the pandemic similarly highlights the use of job descriptions and training frameworks: MST posts were designed around tasks for which students were deemed competent through a trust training programme, with duties undertaken under appropriate supervision [19, 20]. Taken together, international comparators reinforce the idea that student employment in clinical environments is most defensible when training, competence assurance, and supervision arrangements are explicit, rather than left to informal local interpretation.

These international comparisons should be interpreted primarily as examples of more structured governance, rather than as definitive evidence of effectiveness or safety. The available evidence remains limited and heterogeneous. However, consistent emphasis from regulators and education bodies on clear role boundaries, supervision, and training provides a strong rationale for minimum standards. In Poland, such a framework could also enable future evaluation of effectiveness, educational value, and safety, which is currently difficult in a fragmented and locally variable system.

Only a minority of hospitals in our study reported having internal policies that regulate the acceptance of PAs/VOLs. This suggests that student engagement often proceeds without codified internal governance at a national level. This is important because some of the most frequently reported PA tasks (e.g. documentation and monitoring/reporting of test results) can affect clinical workflow and decision-making. International regulators and education bodies consistently emphasise that when students undertake clinical tasks, systems should ensure clear role boundaries, appropriate supervision, and training to support safe participation [21, 22]. Without such mechanisms in place, two adjacent hospitals may interpret PA very differently, creating inconsistent expectations for staff and students, as well as uneven safeguards for patients.

Another related concern is working time protection. Our data show that hospitals reported varying caps on student hours; however, the absence of a national limit risks situations where paid work competes with curricular requirements. The NSW AiM model explicitly framed the role as part-time and prohibited night shifts, positioning employment as supplementary rather than substitutive and reducing the likelihood that students’ primary role as learners was displaced by service needs [13, 18]. A similar top-down Polish standard (e.g., a national monthly cap and/or prohibition of night duty for student roles) would reduce variability and protect educational progression.

A further implication of the current model is the weak formal integration between hospital-based work and undergraduate curricula. Notably, when asked whether students could obtain academic credit (e.g., course, placement, or internship credit) for work activities aligned with learning outcomes, hospitals consistently responded in the negative, explaining that such recognition is governed by university regulations rather than hospital policy or decision-making. However, many of the tasks reported in our dataset clearly involve acquisition and repetition of competencies that overlap with core clinical learning outcomes. From a system perspective, the absence of a transparent pathway for recognising these competencies risks undermining the educational “value return” of student engagement and may inadvertently frame PA/VOL work as service contribution without formal curricular benefit. Closer coordination between hospitals and universities could enable competence-based recognition under defined standards, while preserving patient safety safeguards and protecting students’ primary role as learners.

Our results suggest that PAs frequently perform administrative and logistical duties. While such work may be valuable to hospitals, a purely administrative PA model risks losing the “win–win” logic that underpins student assistantships internationally: service contribution coupled with structured learning, integration into teams, and development of readiness for practice. Research on student assistantships (as supervised participation in junior-doctor-like duties) highlights perceived benefits for preparedness and transition, but also underscores the importance of shared expectations about what constitutes meaningful assistantship activity [23–25]. For Poland, this suggests that future national guidance should encourage hospitals to design PA roles that include educationally meaningful, supervised clinical participation within clear boundaries—rather than restricting PAs to clerical substitution. In practice, this could be operationalised through a nationally defined scope-of-practice framework linked to year of study (what tasks are permitted and under what supervision), complemented by mandatory onboarding and competence sign-off for specific tasks.

Where university affiliation was reported, the majority of PAs and a substantial proportion of VOLs were affiliated with KRAUM universities. This simultaneously implies that a non-trivial fraction may come from outside the traditional academic medical university group. In Poland’s rapidly expanding medical education environment, public reporting has highlighted controversies around programmes operating without clear accreditation outcomes or with accreditation concerns [26]. Importantly, the Polish Accreditation Committee (PKA) maintains an official database indicating that if a programme does not appear in the database, it has not received accreditation [27]. This raises a legitimate policy question: should hospitals apply additional verification and governance safeguards when employing students from programmes without established accreditation status, particularly in patient-facing contexts? The intent is not to stigmatise individual students, but to protect patient trust and ensure that clinical employment aligns with transparent quality assurance.

Future directions

At present, the status quo in Poland can be characterised as a decentralised, hospital-led model based largely on local discretion. Individual hospitals decide whether and how to engage medical students as PAs and/or VOLs, and local practice varies in relation to role descriptions, eligibility criteria, onboarding, supervision arrangements, working-time limits, internal regulations, academic recognition, and basic record-keeping. This approach allows local flexibility, but it also makes role boundaries, safeguards, and evaluation inconsistent across hospitals.

Against this, our findings support the development of a minimum national framework for medical student participation in volunteering and other hospital-based activities at a health system level in Poland. Ultimately, the policy should aim to be a win–win model that is: supportive of service delivery while demonstrably contributing to students’ clinical development and readiness for supervised practice.

Limitations

This study relied on hospitals’ self-reported administrative responses, as well as any attachments they provided, rather than direct observation of practice. Although the study covered the full national sampling frame of public hospitals and achieved a response rate of 71.8%, non-response may still have introduced bias. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted as a broad national overview of responding public hospitals rather than as a complete census of all hospital practice in Poland. Role descriptions and duties were coded from free-text responses and may therefore fail to capture nuances adequately.

Conclusions

This nationwide mapping of Polish public hospitals revealed that a quarter of responding institutions engaged medical students as PAs and/or VOLs. This yielded a substantial national footprint (562 PAs and 491 VOLs) but with pronounced regional and institutional concentration. PA duties were most commonly oriented toward documentation, coordination, and workflow support activities, whereas VOL duties more often reflected broader supportive care tasks. Both roles were frequently deployed in emergency settings. Notably, implementation was characterised by significant heterogeneity in eligibility criteria, working time limits, and the specificity with which supervision was described. Only a minority of hospitals reported internal regulations governing the engagement of PAs/VOLs.

Hospitals did not report formalised progression pathways for either role in their responses and consistently indicated that academic credit for work aligned with learning outcomes is governed by university regulations rather than hospital policy. Where university affiliation was reported, the majority of engaged students were linked to KRAUM-affiliated universities, highlighting the importance of transparent credential verification and governance as medical education expands to include a wider range of providers.

Overall, these findings suggest that student engagement in Polish hospitals has emerged rapidly since 2021 and is currently shaped largely by local solutions rather than a coherent national framework. Developing minimum national standards covering role definition and scope, mandatory onboarding/training, explicit supervision and accountability, working time protections, and basic monitoring requirements could reduce unwarranted variation and strengthen patient safety, educational value, and workforce utility.
