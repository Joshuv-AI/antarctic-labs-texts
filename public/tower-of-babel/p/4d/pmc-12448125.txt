A unified account of current-future control and affordance-based control for running to catch fly balls
Authors: Dees B. W. Postma, Frank T. J. M. Zaal
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Current-future control and affordance-based control offer two distinct approaches to understanding the visual guidance of action. Current-future control strategies, such as the optical acceleration cancellation strategy, are essentially error-nulling strategies. That is, the visual guidance of action is predicated on nulling the error around the critical value of some optical invariant. Whether error nulling is (still) possible, given the action capabilities of the agent, is not specified in current-future control strategies. There is no specification of affordances. Affordance-based control resolves this issue by incorporating action capabilities into the control of action. Although promising, affordance-based control strategies have been criticized for their lack of specific predictions for the control of movement. Affordance-based control strategies are under-constrained in the sense that there is no specific control law that guides action within the space of possibilities. In this contribution, we resolve this issue by showing that current-future control and affordance-based control need not be fundamentally different and that their guiding principles can in fact be reconciled. We propose a new control strategy within the fly-ball paradigm and show its effectiveness through simulations. We show that our model makes clear predictions about the control of interceptive behavior while also informing the fielder about the catchability of fly balls.

Introduction

Many activities in everyday life and sports are visually guided. Current-future control and affordance-based control offer two distinct approaches to understanding the visual guidance of such actions. Yet, neither of these two approaches can fully account for all aspects of behavior. Current-future control strategies have been criticized for their inability to account for the influence of affordances on the control of behavior (Fajen, 2005a; Fajen, 2005b; Fajen, 2005c; Fajen, 2007). Affordance-based control strategies, on the other hand, have been criticized for lacking specific control laws that guide behavior within the boundaries of action (Postma, 2019). In the affordance-based control framework, control of action is described in terms of possibility rather than in terms of actuality (Harrison, Turvey, & Frank, 2016).

In this paper, we consider the fly-ball paradigm to show that current-future control and affordance-based control can be unified in a way that preserves the favorable qualities of both approaches. First, we introduce the fly-ball paradigm, discuss current-future control strategies for catching fly balls, and illustrate the problem of action boundaries. Second, we introduce a new model that unifies current-future control and affordance-based control for the fly-ball paradigm. Third, we show the effectiveness of this model through simulations. We show that our model guides the simulated fielder to the right place in the right time to make a catch (control aspect) and informs the simulated fielder about the affordance of catchability (affordance aspect). Fourth, we show that our model can be specified in optical terms. Finally, we discuss the broader implications of our work and show that the unification of current-future control and affordance-based control is not unique to the fly-ball paradigm but can be generalized to other paradigms, as well, such as the braking paradigm (Lee, 1976; see also Bardy & Warren, 1997; Yilmaz & Warren, 1995). We argue that current-future control models are incomplete without affordances and that affordance-based control models are incomplete without a guiding control law.

The fly-ball paradigm

The fly-ball paradigm describes the scenario of a baseball outfielder attempting to catch a ball hit deep into the outfield (Figure 1). Catching a fly ball can be challenging; outfielders may need to cover a considerable distance in a short time span to make a catch (Adair, 2002). In this endeavor, precision is key, as outfielders need to make sure not to overshoot or undershoot their target.

Chapman (1968) was among the first to consider the physics of catching a fly ball. Chapman showed that the rate of change of the tangent of the elevation angle of the ball, d(tanα)/dt, is constant for outfielders who are running at the constant velocity that would get them to the right place in the right time to make the catch.1 Figure 1B illustrates how tanα increases at a constant rate when the fielder is standing at the future interception location with the ball. Consequently, the Chapman strategy to intercepting fly balls is about controlling running velocity such that d(tanα)/dt is constant. Whenever d(tanα)/dt is decreasing, the outfielder is running such that the ball will ultimately land in front of the fielder; whenever d(tanα)/dt is increasing, the ball will fly overhead. Based on (the sign of) d(tanα)/dt, outfielders can adjust their running velocity such that the ball will land neither in front nor behind them.

When outfielders track the ball with their gaze, d(tanα)/dt might be directly picked up from the gaze angle of the ball relative to the horizontal. A more general solution, also useful when gaze is not directed at the ball, can be found in the optical acceleration cancellation (OAC) strategy (Michaels & Oudejans, 1992). The OAC strategy is analogous to the Chapman strategy but captures its rationale in terms of the optical position of the ball, which is the projection of the ball on a planar image plane (see Figure 1C). When d(tanα)/dt is constant, the rate of change of the optical position of the ball is constant, as well. Thus, keeping constant the optical velocity of the ball will lead a fielder to the interception location with the ball in time. As keeping optical velocity constant is equivalent to nulling optical acceleration, this strategy has become known as the optical acceleration cancellation strategy. Because the OAC strategy provides a more general solution to the case of running to catch fly balls, the OAC strategy is used here to refer to the control principles originally put forward by Chapman.2

The OAC strategy has proven quite successful in characterizing the locomotor behavior of outfielders running to make a catch in baseball (Dienes & McLeod, 1993; Fink, Foo, & Warren, 2009; McLeod & Dienes, 1996; Michaels & Oudejans, 1992). Studies have shown that outfielders’ running paths align with the running paths that would be predicted by the OAC strategy. Simulation studies have further shown that the OAC strategy is robust against neuronal delay and non-parabolic ball trajectories resulting from air drag (Kistemaker, Faber & Beek, 2009; Tresilian, 1995). Moreover, it has been shown that locomotor patterns are not merely coincidental to the use of optical acceleration but are in fact causally related to the use of optical acceleration (Fink et al., 2009). In an experiment by Fink and colleagues (2009), participants were tasked to catch fly balls in virtual reality. By applying mid-flight perturbations to the (virtual) ball trajectories, they examined how participants responded to sudden changes in the vertical motion of the ball. It was found that participants adjusted their locomotor velocity in a manner that was consistent with the use of the OAC strategy. Further evidence in favor of the OAC strategy stems from studies scrutinizing the gaze behavior of outfielders running to make a catch. Outfielders are shown to maintain continuous visual contact with the ball while running to make an attempted catch (e.g., Oudejans, Michaels, Bakker, & Davids, 1999), even for fly balls that they perceive to be uncatchable (Postma, den Otter, & Zaal, 2014).

The problem of action boundaries

Although there is clear scientific evidence in support of the OAC strategy, the OAC strategy cannot account for the full range of behaviors that is associated with the act of catching a fly ball. This is because neither the affordance of catchability nor the influence that it exerts on behavior is accounted for by the OAC strategy. The OAC strategy only specifies whether an outfielder's current running velocity is sufficient to get to the right place in the right time. If it is not, the strategy does not specify whether it is possible for the outfielder to catch the ball, given their maximum running speed. This is a crucial issue. Outfielders might abandon pursuit if a fly ball is no longer perceived to be catchable. Indeed, fielders might not even initiate pursuit if they feel a fly ball is uncatchable. These and related phenomena cannot be accounted for by the OAC strategy and are exemplary for all current-future control strategies. This issue is known as the “problem of action boundaries” (Fajen, 2007).

To address the problem of action boundaries, (Fajen 2005a; Fajen 2005b; Fajen 2005c; Fajen 2007) developed the concept of affordance-based control for the case of braking a car to a safe stop. The prominent current-future control strategy in the braking paradigm is the τ˙ strategy. Lee (1976) proposed that drivers might visually regulate the rate of deceleration to brake their car to a safe stop by attending to an optical variable called τ˙, which is the first derivative of the relative rate of optical expansion of an object. Much similar to the OAC strategy, the τ˙ strategy holds that motorists should control the rate of deceleration by keeping τ˙ at the critical value of −0.5. When τ˙ is greater than −0.5, the current rate of deceleration is greater than necessary, causing the vehicle to stop short of the target. Conversely, when τ˙ is smaller than −0.5, the current rate of deceleration is insufficient, causing the vehicle to crash into the target. As such, braking behavior can be controlled by striving to keep τ˙ at or close to −0.5.

Similar to the OAC strategy, action boundaries play no part in the τ˙ strategy, thus rendering it impossible to perceive whether it is (still) possible to brake the car to a safe stop, given its maximum deceleration. Fajen proposed that, rather than using τ˙, motorists rely on the ratio of ideal deceleration (dideal) over maximal deceleration (dmax) to control their braking behavior. Ideal deceleration is the rate of deceleration that is required to bring the car to a safe stop without having to make any further braking adjustments, and maximal deceleration is the maximum rate of deceleration that can be exerted. When the ratio of dideal over dmax is smaller than 1, safe braking is afforded; when the ratio of dideal over dmax is greater than 1, safe braking is no longer afforded. From this affordance-based control model, the driver strives to keep the ratio of dideal over dmax well below 1.

Fajen showed that the ratio of dideal over dmax is optically available (Fajen, 2005a) and that motorists take their action boundaries into account in braking a car to a safe stop (Fajen, 2005a; Fajen, 2005c). A prime example of the latter finding stems from an experiment in which participants were required to perform a virtual braking task (Fajen, 2005c). Participants viewed a simulated scene in which they approached a row of stop signs along a linear path. Using a hand-operated brake, the rate of deceleration could be controlled. Participants were encouraged to brake as they would do in everyday life. The maximal rate of deceleration (i.e., the strength of the brake) was manipulated as a between-subjects factor. When considering the value of ideal deceleration at braking onset, significant differences were found between groups: Participants that were equipped with a weaker brake started braking at lower values for ideal deceleration than participants equipped with a stronger brake. When, however, ideal deceleration at braking onset was expressed in intrinsic units, the significant differences between groups were no longer present: All participants started braking when the ideal rate of deceleration was approximately 50% of the maximal rate of deceleration. This finding supports the notion that motorists are sensitive to their action boundaries and that they take their action boundaries into account in visually guided braking.

The influence of action boundaries not only is tangible at braking onset but can also be observed when motorists are nearing their action boundary (i.e., when they approach their maximum rate of deceleration). In a similar virtual braking task, Fajen (2005a) showed that participants are more likely to increase deceleration when the ideal rate of deceleration is close to the maximum rate of deceleration than when the ideal rate of deceleration is low. This finding is particularly interesting because, in analyzing the data, Fajen only analyzed those instances for which the current rate of deceleration was already sufficient to come to a safe stop (i.e., τ˙ = −0.5). This shows that motorists did not have to increase braking pressure to come to a safe stop, but did so anyway in order to keep ideal deceleration well below their maximal rate of deceleration. This finding further highlights the influence that action boundaries exert on the control of behavior.

The problem of control laws

Affordance-based control, however, is not perfect either. Affordance-based control has been criticized for its inability to make specific predictions about the control of visually guided action (Harrison et al., 2016; Postma, 2019). In the case of braking, any and all braking adjustments that would keep the ratio of dideal over dmax below 1 are permissible. Consequently, there is an infinite set of deceleration paths that would bring the car to a safe stop. From a control perspective, this is problematic. Research shows that there is lawfulness to the control of braking (Lee, 1976). Under conditions where ideal deceleration is well within the action boundaries of the motorist, deceleration paths align with the use of τ˙ (Lee, 1976; Yilmaz & Warren, 1995). Behavior is not randomly controlled within the limits of action but can be characterized by the kind of lawfulness described by current-future control models. This holds true for braking, as well as for other visually guided actions such as steering (Fajen, 2001; Kim & Turvey, 1999; Wann & Land, 2000), intercepting moving targets (Fajen & Warren, 2003; Fajen & Warren, 2004; Fajen & Warren, 2007), and catching fly balls (Chapman, 1968; McBeath et al., 1995; McLeod et al., 2006; Michaels & Oudejans, 1992).

Currently, affordance-based control models offer no explanation for the consistent behavioral patterns that are observed in the control of visually guided action, and current-future control models offer no explanation for the influence that action boundaries exert on the control of behavior. A unification of control paradigms is required to better understand the visually guided control of action. In the next section, we illustrate for the fly-ball paradigm how the OAC strategy can serve as the basis for an affordance-based control strategy that is able to account both for the control of action, as well as for the influence of action boundaries.

Affordance-based prospective control in catching

In this section, we propose an affordance-based prospective control model for catching fly balls. This model unifies current-future control and affordance-based control and combines the favorable qualities of both. Our model takes the general form of (1)aidealamaxwhere aideal denotes the ideal acceleration that would lead an outfielder to the right place in the right time to make a catch and |amax| denotes the maximum acceleration that an outfielder is able to bring forth. The ratio of aideal over |amax| can be positive or negative and is dependent on the fielder's direction of travel relative to the trajectory of the ball. In a coordinate system where a ball is shot in the positive direction of travel, the ratio of aideal over |amax| would be positive for fly balls landing behind the fielder's initial position and negative for fly balls landing in front. Thus, the sign of Equation 1 informs the fielder about the required direction of travel. Catching is afforded when the ratio of aideal over |amax| is between −1 and 1 (i.e., -1<aideal|amax|<1). Conversely, catching is not afforded when the ratio is outside these bounds (i.e., aideal|amax|<-1 or aideal|amax|>1).

A special quality of Equation 1 is that it also contains a control law that guides the continuous visual guidance of locomotor behavior in fly-ball catching. That is, locomotor behavior can be controlled by nulling the ratio of aideal over |amax|. When the ratio of aideal over |amax| equals zero, ideal acceleration equals zero as well, meaning that the fielder is running at the constant velocity that will get them to the right place in the right time to make a catch. This control strategy results in locomotor patterns that align with the OAC strategy. Accordingly, our model captures the control principles put forth in current-future control models while also accounting for the influence that action boundaries exert on the control of behavior. Over the next couple of sections, we explain how we modeled aideal over |amax|. Later, we present the results of a simulation study where we show the effectiveness of our model.

This is at odds with research that shows that fielders have a well-attuned sense of which fly balls are catchable and which are not (Fajen, Diaz, & Cramer, 2011; Postma, Lemmink, & Zaal, 2018), and that movement initiation is not always needed to perceive catchability (Postma, Smith, Pepping, van Andel, & Zaal, 2017; Postma et al., 2018).

Maximal acceleration (amax)

In the previous section, we showed how ideal (constant) acceleration is derived from the OAC strategy. In this section, we discuss the action boundary of running to catch fly balls, which is given in the form of maximal acceleration. In recent work, we investigated the behaviorally relevant agent–environment factors that determine the catchability of a fly ball (Postma et al., 2018). We showed that neither maximal running velocity nor maximal running acceleration in isolation could be uniquely related to the affordance of catchability (see also Oudejans, Michaels, Bakker, & Dolné, 1996). The findings suggested that, instead, the action boundary for catching fly balls is determined by a dynamic, nonlinear combination of maximal running velocity and maximal running acceleration. In a follow-up study, we investigated this hypothesis (Postma, Wieling, Lemmink, & Zaal, 2022). We had participants perform maximal-effort sprints in the forward running direction and in the backward running direction. We recorded their location over time and modeled their acceleration as a function of their running velocity. The ratio of ideal acceleration over maximum acceleration

With ideal constant acceleration derived from the OAC strategy (Equation 7) and maximal acceleration identified from earlier work (Equation 8), it is now possible to formalize an affordance-based prospective control model for running to catch fly balls: (9)XY˙2-XYY¨-X˙Y˙Y2Y2+XY˙-X˙YX2Y˙2-2YY¨2XY2aX˙c+cn-2X˙c-X˙cpeak2Equation 9 is simply the expanded form of Equation 1: aideal|amax|. Just as in the braking paradigm, this formulation holds that a fly ball is catchable when the ratio of aideal over |amax| is between −1 and 1 and that a fly ball is not catchable when the ratio of aideal over |amax| is outside this range. Nulling the ratio of aideal over |amax| will guide the outfielder to the right place in the right time to make a catch. So far, this shows that current-future control and affordance-based control are not mutually exclusive and can indeed be unified into one coherent account of catching fly balls.

Equation 9 aligns with the optical acceleration cancellation strategy

Outfielders that guide their locomotor behavior according to the principles of the OAC strategy (i.e., nulling optical acceleration) will arrive at the right place in the right time to make a catch. Several scientific studies have shown that outfielders’ locomotor patterns are aligned with the use of optical acceleration (Dienes & McLeod, 1993; Fink et al., 2009; Kistemaker, Faber & Beek, 2009; McLeod & Dienes, 1996; Michaels & Oudejans, 1992). Equation 9 perfectly aligns with these and other observations that support the use of optical acceleration in fly-ball catching. Figure 6 shows optical acceleration (Figure 6B) and the required constant acceleration (Figure 6C) for a simulated fielder running to null the ratio of aideal over |amax|. It can be seen that optical acceleration equals zero when required constant acceleration equals zero. Consequently, the locomotor patterns of an outfielder running to null optical acceleration will coincide with the locomotor patterns of an outfielder running to null the ratio of aideal over |amax|.

Equation 9 informs about the affordance of catchability

The ratio of ratio of aideal over |amax|, as specified by Equation 9, informs directly about the catchability of fly balls. Whenever the ratio of aideal over |amax| is between −1 and 1, the fly ball is still catchable. Whenever the ratio of aideal over |amax| is greater than 1 or smaller than −1, the fly ball is no longer catchable. Figure 7A shows a simulated outfielder presented with a range of uncatchable fly balls. The simulated catcher is accelerating maximally to intercept the projected fly balls. Figure 7B shows the ratio of aideal over |amax|; it can be seen that the ratio of aideal over |amax| surpasses the critical value of −1 and 1 for all ball trajectories. Figure 7B further shows that there is a temporal structuring to the ratio of aideal over |amax| reaching its critical threshold value: Fly balls for which greater distances have to be covered to make a successful catch cross the critical threshold sooner than fly balls for which this distance is smaller. In the present example, the fly balls that land closest to the fielder are perceived uncatchable after around 1.6 seconds, whereas fly balls that land far away (e.g., 2 meters and 60 meters) can already be perceived as uncatchable after 1 second. In extreme cases, when a projected fly ball will land very far away from the initial position of the fielder, the fielder might observe the fly ball to be uncatchable right away. See, for example, the fly ball that flies overhead and lands at the 85-meter mark. The required constant acceleration that would be required to intercept the ball is greater than the maximum acceleration that the fielder can produce right at the start of the attempted catch. As such, one would expect the fielder to perceive the fly ball to be uncatchable right after the ball has been hit (or likely after some few hundred milliseconds when also taking response time into account).

With the OAC strategy, the rate of change in optical acceleration is greater for uncatchable fly balls that are projected behind the initial position of the fielder than for fly balls that are projected in front of the initial position of the fielder. This asymmetry in the OAC strategy is independent of the fact that the action boundary for running backward is likely smaller than the action boundary for running forward. In Figure 7, the action boundary for running forward and running backward were assumed equal. When assuming dissimilar action boundaries for forward running and backward running, an asymmetry in the temporal structure for catchability would also be apparent in our model. However, for our model this asymmetry would be directly related to the action boundaries of the fielder, whereas in the OAC strategy there is an inherent asymmetry in how optical acceleration evolves over time even before accounting for differences in action boundaries.

Equation 9 allows for flexible navigation of the affordance space

For fly balls that are well within reach of the outfielder, there are many different kinematic trajectories that will get the outfielder to the right place in the right time to make the catch. Outfielders that prioritize acceleration right at the start of an attempted catch will quickly reach the constant running velocity that will get them to the right place in the right time to make a catch. Outfielders that prioritize running paths that minimize jerk might never reach a constant running velocity but still make a successful catch (e.g., dark blue lines in Figure 9). Discussion

In the present contribution, we offer an account on how current-future control and affordance-based control can be unified in the context of the fly-ball paradigm. We have presented the results of a simulation study and show our model to have four favorable characteristics. First, our model guides the simulated outfielder to the right place at the right time to make a catch. Second, our model aligns with (empirical work on) the OAC strategy. Third, our model is informative of the affordance of catchability. Fourth, our model allows outfielders to flexibly navigate the affordance space for catching. In the remainder of this section, we reflect on the implications of the current findings and point to future work for further advancements.

Translating affordance-based prospective control to other tasks

In the present contribution, we developed the concept of affordance-based prospective control by considering the fly-ball paradigm. Yet, its underlying principles are not unique or incidental to the fly-ball paradigm. The same approach might be taken to develop similar such models for other visually guided tasks, too. Using the braking paradigm as an example, we outline the steps that have to be taken to turn a current-future control model into an affordance-based prospective control model. For the sake of brevity, we only discuss the steps in conceptual terms here. In Appendix 2, we present the associated mathematical operations.

Affordance-based prospective control models take the general form of Xideal/Xmax, where Xideal represents the kinematic property that the agent needs to control to successfully carry out the task at hand, and Xmax represents the associated action boundary (expressed in the same units as Xideal). Xideal can be identified by considering the extant control law that has been formulated for the task.

In the context of the braking paradigm, the τ˙ strategy has been put forward as an effective way to brake a car to a safe stop (Lee, 1976; Yilmaz & Warren, 1995). The τ˙ strategy holds that motorists should brake to keep τ˙ constant at −0.5. The first step in transforming the τ˙ strategy into a affordance-based prospective control model is to find the kinematic property that should be nulled. In the case of braking that would likely be jerk (i.e., the rate of change of deceleration). The jerk term is not present in the equation for τ˙ but can be obtained by taking its derivative, τ¨. When a motorist is braking at the ideal rate to come to a safe stop, τ˙=-0.5 and τ¨=0. From this set of equations, ideal jerk (Jideal) can be calculated—that is, the rate of change of deceleration that is required to control τ˙ around its margin value of −0.5. The optical specification of Jideal can be given using the same optical variables that are also employed for the optical specification of the τ˙ strategy.

The second step to turn the τ˙ strategy into an affordance-based prospective control model is to find the action boundary that matches Jideal. To establish the action boundary that limits the action possibilities of the agent an empirical study is required that invites the agent to perform the intended behavior at or near their action boundary. In the case of braking, the action boundary is determined by the characteristics of the brakes. Stronger brakes produce a greater rate of change of deceleration than weaker brakes. Still, the action boundary for braking is not static. The maximal rate of deceleration that can be produced is dependent on the momentary kinematic properties of the car. That is, deceleration profiles at high velocities look different from deceleration profiles at low velocities. Modeling action boundaries as a function of the relevant properties of the agent-environment system allows for realistic models on action and will yield maximal jerk (Jmax).

The third and final step is to match Jideal and Jmax such that the dimensionless ratio of the two fulfils the following two conditions: (1) nulling the ratio of Jideal over Jmax produces the same braking behavior as the τ˙ strategy; and (2) the ratio of Jideal over Jmax should accurately reflect the affordance for safe stopping.

Here, we have illustrated in broad strokes how the methodology that we outlined in this paper can be applied to the braking paradigm (see Appendix 2 for more detail). In future work, we set out to further extend the reach of our model by also investigating the continuous visual guidance in other tasks, such as interception in a two-dimensional horizontal plane.

Optical specification of affordance-based prospective control

We have shown how the ratio of aideal over |amax| can be optically specified. Yet, the (visual) perceptual system might not be (sufficiently) sensitive to the optical variables that we used to optically specify X, X˙, Y, and Y˙. This might, for example, be the case for higher order optical variables such as the rate of change in the optical angle of the radius of the ball (θ˙) or the rate of change of the elevation angle of the ball with the horizontal (α˙) (Babler & Dannemiller, 1993; Michaels & Oudejans, 1992; Saxberg, 1987; Todd, 1981; Tresilian, 1995). Furthermore, the trigonometric approach to optical specification that we presented is contingent on the fielder knowing (and accurately perceiving) the radius of the ball, which can indeed appear quite small when the ball is far away. Whether the optical variables involved for specifying Equation 9 fall within the visually perceptible range is an empirical question. Whereas some optical variables (e.g., α˙) may be perceptible from the outset of an attempted catch, other optical variables may only reach perceptual threshold levels after some time (e.g., θ˙). Psychometric studies in ecologically valid environments (cf. Todd, 1981) should shed light on the sensitivity of the (visual) perceptual system to pick up on the optical variables proposed here. Virtual reality setups that allow for unconstrained running patterns may be employed to flexibly manipulate the optical information that is available to the fielder, such as by manipulating the (optical) size of the ball.

Further, it should be noted that the route to optical specification presented above is one of many (see also Appendix 1). Indeed, it might be the case that the visual perceptual system makes use of different (combinations of) optical variables to detect the ratio of aideal over |amax| than the ones we presented earlier. Indeed, even extraretinal information may play a role in perceiving the catchability of fly balls (Bongers & Michaels, 2008). Again, virtual reality may offer an approach to investigating which optical variables are involved in perceiving the required constant acceleration to make a catch in baseball. By systematically manipulating the optical variables that are made available to a fielder in an attempted catch, more can be learned about the optical variables that are involved in catching fly balls and in perceiving the affordance of catchability (Fink et al., 2009).

Another point regarding the optical specification of our model is that it currently ignores air resistance. Leaving out air resistance allowed us to set the horizontal acceleration of the ball to equal zero and the vertical acceleration of the ball to equal the gravitational constant. In reality, these assumptions do not hold. Fly balls travel less far and reach lesser heights under real-world conditions than under idealized parabolic conditions. Additionally, ball spin exerts a pronounced effect on the trajectory of the ball. Although these and other factors relating to air resistance can be accounted for in the model, it would further up the order of optical variables required to capture the spatial (kinematic) relations. However, the perceptual system might not require all this extra information to adequately guide behavior. In their simulation study, Kistemaker and colleagues (2009) showed that the OAC strategy is “robust against the presence of air friction” (p. 246). Because of the prospective nature of control, fielders are enabled to continuously adapt their locomotor behavior to the (idealized) value for optical acceleration. The same holds true for our model.

Required instant acceleration versus required constant acceleration

Rozendaal and van Soest (2003) showed that the required (instant) acceleration that is necessary to cancel out optical acceleration could be derived from the OAC strategy. Earlier, we argued that required instant acceleration is likely not the correct candidate to guide locomotor behavior in fly-ball catching. First is because the required instant acceleration is nearly infinite at the start of an attempted catch, rendering all fly balls to be perceived uncatchable by an outfielder at the onset of an attempted catch. Second, Rozendaal and van Soest assumed that the fielder would be able to produce the required acceleration instantaneously, which is not possible given that outfielders are limited in the magnitude of jerk that they can bring forth. Because of this, we propose that locomotor behavior in fly-ball catching is instead guided by required constant acceleration (Equation 7). Contrary to its instantaneous counterpart, required constant acceleration is small at the start of an attempted catch.

Although required instant acceleration is an unlikely candidate for guiding locomotor behavior in the context of affordance-based control, it does have an interesting characteristic that renders it very suitable for guiding locomotor behavior if the fly ball is (clearly) catchable: Required instant acceleration informs the outfielder of both the required direction of adjustment as well as of the required magnitude of adjustment. In other words, required instant acceleration tells the outfielder not only to speed up or to slow down but also by how much. Required constant acceleration (Equation 7), on the other hand, is indicative of the required magnitude of adjustment but does not provide exact information on what acceleration is exactly needed to cancel out optical acceleration. In that sense, required instant acceleration appears to offer outfielders more fine-grained information for control. Conceived as such, required constant acceleration provides more information about the required magnitude of adjustment than optical acceleration but less information than exact OAC as formalized by Rozendaal and van Soest (2003).

Navigating the affordance space

One of the key characteristics of affordance-based control is that it allows for a flexible navigation of the space of behavioral possibilities. In the context of braking, this means that drivers can adopt a wide range of deceleration trajectories to come to a safe stop. Some drivers might adopt more conservative braking styles, whereas others might adopt more aggressive braking styles (Harrison et al., 2016). In other words, there is a range of behavioral trajectories that all lead to safe stopping. The same holds true for running to catch a fly ball. Some fielders might accelerate such that they will intercept the ball with a low terminal velocity, and other fielders might accelerate such that they will intercept the ball with a high terminal velocity. The decision to choose one acceleration path over another is likely interdependent on the next action that the fielder intends to undertake (e.g., passing or tagging). In modeling catching behavior, we assumed that fielders would accelerate maximally to null the ratio of dideal|dmax| as quickly as possible. Similarly, for the OAC strategy, it is typically assumed that fielders accelerate maximally to null optical acceleration as quickly as possible. Thus, a question that remains largely unaddressed, by both current-future control models and affordance-based (prospective) control models, is why would agents prefer one movement solution over another? With the model presented in this paper, the analysis of behavioral trajectories can now explicitly be made with reference to the action capabilities of the agent—an important characteristic in the control of action (Fajen, 2007).

Conclusions

In the current contribution, we have introduced the concept of affordance-based prospective control, specifically for the fly-ball paradigm. We have shown that current-future control and affordance-based control are not mutually exclusive but that both approaches can be unified, encompassing the concept of affordances while retaining control laws to guide behavior. Through simulations, we showed that our model successfully guides simulated outfielders to the future interception location with the ball (if catchable) and that the affordance of catchability can be directly perceived. Furthermore, we have shown that simulated locomotor patterns are consistent with the optical acceleration cancellation strategy. Finally, we have shown that affordance-based prospective control is not unique to the fly-ball paradigm but can be extended to cover other paradigms of motor control, as well, such as the braking paradigm. With the present contribution, we argue that current-future control models are not complete without affordances and that affordance-based control models are not complete without a control law.
