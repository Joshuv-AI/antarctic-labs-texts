Perspectives of heads of nursing studies in Croatia about students’ motivation to enroll in the master of nursing program despite uncertainty about future salary and status: a qualitative study
Authors: Andrea Vuksa, Marta Čivljak, Ivica Matić, Vesna Mijoč, Marin Čargo, Snježana Čukljek, Kata Ivanišević, Mario Marendić, Mihajlo Lojpur, Zrinka Puharić, Aleksandar Racz, Nataša Skitarelić, Marija Ljubičić, Sanja Zoranić, Marijana Neuberg, Karlo Ložnjak, Dijana Majstorović, Sandra Bošković, Silvije Šegulja, Aleksandra Stevanović, Mara Županić, Štefanija Ozimec Vulinec, Biserka Sedić, Vesna Turuk, Milan Milošević, Štefica Mikšić, Livia Puljak
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn Croatia, the great majority of Master of Nursing (MN) graduates do not receive a salary increase or improved working position in public healthcare institutions upon graduation. Thus, it is unclear what are students’ motives for enrolling in MN studies and how institutions address that uncertainty.AimTo explore the perspectives of heads of MN studies in Croatia about students’ motives to enroll in the MN program despite high uncertainty about future salary and status.MethodsA qualitative study based on semi-structured interviews was conducted among heads of all MN studies in Croatia.ResultsWe included 18/21 heads of MN studies in Croatia. Participants indicated that the main students’ motives for enrollment in MN studies were personal and career development and optimism regarding recognition in the future. They reported that MN students were often drawn to particular programs, the university’s location, academic standards, and tuition fees. Their perception was that MN graduates acquire specific knowledge and various competencies that should open up new possibilities for them in their future careers. Participants acknowledged current difficulties for MN graduates in the labor market, and the discrepancy between the competencies that master’s studies provide and the needs of the workplace. They pointed out that establishing specialized MN programs could be a potential solution for faster acknowledgment of nurses’ competencies and much-desired validation of their MN degrees.ConclusionAccording to the heads of MN studies in Croatia, students were optimistic when enrolling in MN studies, hoping that eventually their degree would be recognized in the public healthcare system and that MN studies were a worthwhile investment in their future. Solutions were proposed to ensure that MN degrees will be recognized in the public healthcare system in the future.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-025-07892-3.Keywords: Nursing education; Students, nursing, graduate; Motivation; Qualitative research; Phenomenology

Introduction

As the healthcare system continuously evolves, nurses must acquire advanced knowledge and skills through Master of Nursing (MN) studies [1]. Motivating nurses to continue their education is essential, given the benefits it provides to patients [2], organizations, and professionals [3]. Also, a higher level of education improves the quality of treatment and health outcomes, such as reducing healthcare costs [3] and lowering the mortality rate [4].

Understanding the motives for nurses’ enrollment in MN programs is crucial for developing successful educational strategies to encourage nurses’ development. Several motivations influence nurses to pursue MN studies; two major motives are professional and personal [5]. The desire for professional and personal improvement and the professional socialization process are also recognized in the study of nurses participating in an MN program [6]. Motivating factors for continuing professional development include the desire to grow and develop new roles, improve job opportunities, achieve professional goals, and being interested in the impact of nursing studies on patient care [7].

Correlating with previous studies, Pelletier et al. reported that the most important motivation for pursuing MN was a desire for satisfaction on both personal and career levels, improved professional status, and better employment chances [8]. Also, nurses considered the MN degree to be an opportunity to raise their qualifications and improve their position in current employment [9]. Those results were supported by Murphy et al., who reviewed the literature on continuing education among nurses in Ireland, the United Kingdom, the United States, and Australia and found that nurses’ main reasons for pursuing professional development involve improving possibilities for promotion [10].

Unlike nurses in many other countries [10], in Croatia, most MN graduates are not eligible for a salary increase or improved working status in healthcare institutions upon graduation. Most nurses in Croatia work in the public sector, where salaries are defined as the product of the salary calculation base and the salary coefficient, increased for a fixed percentage for each year of pensionable service. Very few nurses in the public sector in Croatia are currently eligible for the salary coefficient of a nurse with an MN degree. Such positions are defined as nurses working as department heads, at emergency hospital admission units, units for controlling intrahospital infections, improving healthcare quality, conducting specialized diagnostic-therapeutic procedures, as well as operating room team leaders, palliative care coordinators, etc [11].

Based on the data published by the Croatian Institute for Public Health, in 2024, there were 34,968 nurses employed in all healthcare institutions in Croatia, both public and private. Among them, 828 (2%) were employed at positions with an MN salary coefficient [12]. According to the data provided by the Croatian Nursing Council on May 18, 2025, there were 3,774 nurses in Croatia with completed MN studies in nursing (personal communication). Thus, 22% of nurses with MN education in Croatia are employed in positions of MN nurse.

Similarly, Malaysian nurses also encounter difficulties in terms of recognition of their degree status [9]. Even if nurses in Malaysia had degrees, the government had officially declared that it would not affect their pay awards or promotions. It is worth emphasizing that the nurses’ desire for personal and professional development overwhelmed other potential motivators, such as the lack of compensation for their upgraded status in the workplace [9].

Given the ongoing mismatch between the number of nurses completing MN programs and those actually employed in roles that recognize this advanced qualification, it is crucial to understand what drives students to pursue MN studies despite persistent uncertainty regarding career advancement, salary, and recognition. To date, nurses’ motivations for enrolling in MN studies in Croatia from the perspectives of MN programs heads have not been explored.

The study aimed to explore the heads of nursing studies’ perspectives in Croatia about students’ motivation to enroll in the Master of Nursing program despite uncertainty about future salary and status.

Methods

Study design

This was qualitative research based on semi-structured interviews. Interviews were conducted in October and November 2023, one-on-one with the researcher, without the presence of other people, through a video link, using Zoom platform, Google Meet, or Teams proposed by the participants.

Methodological orientation and theory

This study is grounded in an interpretivist paradigm, drawing on a phenomenological epistemology. Interpretivism posits that reality is socially constructed and can be best understood through the subjective experiences and interpretations of individuals. Given the study aim, to explore how heads of MN programs in Croatia perceive students’ motivations, we adopted a perspective that prioritizes individual meaning-making in context.

Accordingly, we used interpretative phenomenological analysis (IPA) [13] as the analytic approach. IPA is particularly suitable for examining how participants make sense of complex, often ambiguous phenomena, and aligns well with the goal of understanding the perceived motivations behind enrolling in MN programs despite systemic uncertainties. This methodological choice reflects our focus on both the content of participants’ experiences and the interpretative work of the researchers in analyzing these accounts.

Ethics

The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Catholic University of Croatia (decision dated September 29, 2023; Classification number: 641-03/23 − 03/054; Registration number: 498-15-06-23-088). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants through e-mail. The participants did not receive any financial compensation as part of the research. All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations.

The research team and reflexivity

The research team was composed of professionals from the fields of nursing, medicine, higher education, and research methodology, all thoroughly described in Supplementary file 1.

Participant selection

This study included heads and deputy heads of all currently operating MN studies in Croatia. The term “heads” refers to all individuals who manage MN study programs in Croatia. “Heads” included deans, deputy deans, department or division heads, and other titles assigned by the institution to individuals whose primary responsibility is the leadership of the MN programs. Higher education institutions with the MN programs were identified using the MOZVAG information system, supporting quality assurance procedures implemented by the Agency for Science and Higher Education (ASHE) in Croatia. We excluded rectors of integrated universities and heads of institutions that coordinate study programs other than nursing if these institutions have other individuals whose primary task is the management of the MN programs. The anticipated sample size was in line with the guidelines in the literature on qualitative research methods [14].

Based on the information obtained from MOZVAG in August 2023, ten eligible institutions were operating MN study programs in Croatia (Supplementary file 1).

We specifically included heads of nursing studies as participants because, in their leadership roles, they have a comprehensive overview of student motivations, challenges faced by graduates, and institutional barriers or facilitators related to MN program enrollment and outcomes. Their perspectives bridge the gap between individual student experience and systemic educational and workforce policies, making their insights especially relevant for understanding broader trends in nursing education and professional development in Croatia.

In line with reflexive thematic analysis methodology [15], and given the inclusion of the entire eligible population of MN program heads across Croatia, we focused on achieving analytic sufficiency rather than rigid data saturation. Given the small and well-defined population, restricting the sample could have excluded some subgroups and geographic regions, potentially biasing the findings. By including all participants, we ensured comprehensive representation across institutions and all regions of the country.

Approaching the participants

The interviewer did not personally know individuals who took part in the study. Participants were invited to participate via publicly available e-mail contacts. Text of the invitation and a detailed information sheet about the study are presented in Supplementary file 2.

Data collection

Semi-structured interviews were used to collect data using an interview guide. The interview guide (Supplementary file 3) was created based on discussions among co-authors and reviewing similar studies [5, 7, 9, 10]. The draft interview guide underwent multiple author’s iterations until it was finalized.

We performed two pilot interviews with two deputies before completing interviews with our participants. Based on the feedback from pilot interviews, we excluded an example from one question. Since there were no revisions in other parts of the interview guide, we included pilot interviews in the main study sample.

Each interview was audio-recorded and transcribed to Microsoft Word. All conversation records were stored on a secure server. The duration of conversations was on average between 30 and 40 min. The interviews were not repeated. Author AV made all transcripts of the interviews, and another author (KL) checked all transcripts. All audio files were verbatim transcribed, including all spoken words, sound effects, silences, and expressions.

Data analysis

We implemented the systematic text condensation four-stage process [16], which incorporates: (1) overall impression - subject recognition; (2) identification and categorization of relevant elements - from topics to codes; (3) condensing - from code to meaning; and (4) synthesizing - from condensing to description and concepts. To get a general idea, the researchers first read the interviews. As a result, early themes were identified. Following a discussion with the co-authors, the units and codes were selected and decided upon after re-reading the interviews. The text was coded by one researcher (AV), and the coding was checked by another researcher (IM). There were no disagreements between the two authors. To represent the participants’ original ideas, entire sentences from the interview transcripts were used as quotes. When quoting participants, we used alphanumerical codes P1 (Participant 1) to P18 (Participant 18).

The complete text of the results and their interpretation were sent to the participants for verification and comment. New comments from the participants were incorporated into the interpretation.

Methodological rigor

To enhance the rigor of this qualitative study, we followed principles of trustworthiness outlined by Lincoln and Guba, including credibility, dependability, confirmability, and transferability [17]. Credibility was supported by conducting pilot interviews to refine the interview guide and by including verbatim quotations in the results [18]. Furthermore, credibility was strengthened through investigator triangulation, where transcripts and coding were independently reviewed by two authors, with no discrepancies identified. Member checking was conducted by returning the full interpretations to participants for feedback, and all received comments were incorporated. Dependability was ensured through independent verification of all transcripts by a second researcher (IM), and by maintaining an audit trail during analysis [19]. Confirmability was addressed through reflexive journaling and team discussions to bracket potential biases. Transferability was supported by providing rich descriptions of the study context, participants, and sampling method, which enabled readers to assess the applicability of the findings to other settings. These procedures are consistent with best practices in qualitative research methodology [17, 18].

Reflexivity

Reflexivity was promoted through discussions with the co-authors about emerging codes and themes, minimizing researcher bias. Inclusion of various participant quotations in the findings was used to support confirmability. Nonetheless, during analysis, no new themes emerged toward the end, indicating analytic sufficiency in line with the principles of reflexive thematic analysis [15].

Reporting

The study was reported in line with the guidelines for qualitative research (Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research, COREQ) [20].

Results

Study participants

Out of 21 heads and deputy heads, one could not participate due to academic obligations and two did not respond to invitation. Finally, 18 individuals from all MN institutions in Croatia participated in the study. Most of the participants were women (n = 12/18) with a nursing academic background (n = 12/18). Participants’ age ranged from 38 to 65 years. The remaining participants were physicians and one psychologist (n = 6/18). Their experience in leadership of the MN programs with a mean SD of 5.1 ± 4.1 years.

Presentation of results was structured according to major themes derived through interpretative analysis. Within each theme, we include subthemes where applicable, and use verbatim excerpts from participant interviews to illustrate and contextualize the reported experiences and viewpoints.

Major theme 1: motives for enrolling in MN studies despite uncertainties

The main themes identified regarding the motives of students for enrolling in MN studies were personal development, career advancement, and optimism (Fig. 1).

Themes and subthemes of motives for enrolling in Master of Nursing studies despite uncertainties

Subthemes related to personal development as theme 1 were expanding the social network, gaining more knowledge, being professionally recognized, competition, and trends among nurses.

Expanding the social network

Participants indicated that the social dimension served as an important motivational factor, reflecting the value students place on building relationships that may enhance both their personal growth and professional opportunities.

“It’s interesting how some of them think: “It broadens my horizons, expands my acquaintances, I can meet new people, connect with other colleagues.” (P9)

Gaining more knowledge

According to the participants, the desire for personal growth, increased knowledge, and greater confidence in professional practice played a central role in students’ decisions to pursue MN studies, often outweighing purely financial considerations.

“Some of the students, who were by chance, and fortunately for us, more of them, said that they were enrolling in graduate studies to upgrade their knowledge to better profile themselves in their work.” (P10)

“And they basically want to improve themselves and their knowledge, to be more confident in their work, and this does not mean that they are primarily going in the financial direction.” (P11)

Professional recognition

The anticipation of greater professional recognition and the ability to provide higher quality education to clients were perceived as strong incentives. Aspirations for improved status and acknowledgment as experts within the nursing field complement other personal and career-related motives for advancing education.

“It seems to me that there is a greater intrinsic motivation there, that they will better educate their clients, that they will be recognized as better experts.” (P6)

Competition

Students’ wish to keep pace with their peers or maintain equal standing within their professional environment, as per participants.

“Students often say: “A colleague in my department has a university degree, so I want one too.” (P4)

Trends

The reference to studying being “fashionable” indicates that external trends and the desire to belong or not be left behind may have played a significant role in motivating enrollment, beyond individual aspirations or career planning.

“Students tell me: “Everyone is enrolling, so I am also enrolling; it is fashionable to study, so I am also studying; everyone is studying something, so I am also studying.” (P1)

Optimism

Participants noted a sense of optimism and a forward-looking attitude among students, which emerged as theme 2. Despite current uncertainties about formal recognition or reward for the MN qualification, participants observed that students continue to enroll, driven by the hope that their investment in education will ultimately be acknowledged, reflecting long-term benefits.

“I believe that one of the most important factors is that they hope that the degree will be recognized after all.” (P6)

“I think, above all, hope for a better future. Hope that one day it will come true, that the salary coefficient will increase and that the salary will be higher. That is, I think, the first factor.” (P14)

Subthemes that pointed to theme 3 as career advancement were easier employment, more opportunities, getting MN credentials, getting a job that requires a degree, being able to work on the same level as physicians, improved conditions, better salary, fear of losing the job and moving abroad (Supplementary file 4, Table 1).

Easier employment, more opportunities

Statement points to a pragmatic motivation for entering the MN program, such as improving employability and increasing job mobility. The anticipation of broader opportunities and a smoother path to new or better positions reflects a recognition of the MN degree as a strategic advantage in a competitive labor market.

“Perhaps the expectation that it will be easier for them to find another job, to achieve that goal when they finish their studies.” (P2)

Improved conditions, better salary

The MN qualification was perceived as a necessary credential for progression into leadership roles, suggesting that for many students, the pursuit of advanced education is closely tied to aspirations for higher status and remuneration within healthcare institutions.

“The possibility of advancement at the workplace, that is, applying for a better workplace or a higher position at the workplace, e.g., head nurse, head nurse of a ward, clinic, institute…” (P14)

Participants also indicated that, when enrolling in MN studies, students were not informed regarding future career uncertainties:

“Colleagues went with one, according to me, the wrong assumption that it would be automatically recognized, just like a bachelor’s degree. I get the feeling that at first, they were quite uninformed about the fact that it would not be recognized automatically. “(P8)

Despite uncertainties about formal recognition and career prospects, students enrolled in MN programs primarily driven by intrinsic motivations such as personal and professional growth, including gaining knowledge and confidence. Social influences like trends and competition also contribute, while practical goals like improved employment opportunities and career advancement are important factors. Persistent optimism about future salary increases and professional recognition further motivates their decision, although some students were not fully aware of current limitations regarding credential acknowledgment.

Major theme 2: benefits and opportunities provided by pursuing MN studies

While nurses may not see an immediate salary increase or status after graduation, from the perspective of the majority of participants, nursing students can gain valuable new competencies and opportunities through MN studies (Fig. 2, Supplementary file 4, Table 2).

Benefits and opportunities provided by pursuing a Master of Nursing studies

“Well, actually, I think it offers them a lot of opportunities. Although not everyone gets a higher salary after a master’s, they gain competencies that are important to their personal development, perhaps as professionals in the nursing profession.” (P7).

Development of career opportunities

Participants noted that obtaining an MN degree opens diverse career paths beyond clinical practice, including roles in research, education, and the pharmaceutical industry. Participants emphasized that MN graduates could have new opportunities such as teaching, mentoring, and working in related healthcare sectors, which broaden their professional scope.

“They can somehow be more interested, maybe actually motivated for science, for research.” (P7)

“New opportunities offered to them after completing their master’s degree include working in high schools, working as mentors at the workplaces where they currently work, which they are very interested in.” (P9)

“The advantage is also the possibility of working in other areas of medicine, as a travelling salesman, that is, as a person who works in the pharmaceutical industry, promotion and sale of modern dressings, aids for people with disabilities.” (P9)

Furthermore, participants noted that although students acquire knowledge about scientific research, the knowledge alone may not be enough.

“You have a broader level of knowledge from all that, but the research foundation that you should have for a higher instance, that is, for the highest instance of education, I definitely do not think you have such a strong foundation, right?” (P12)

The majority of participants (n = 15/18) suggested that differences in MN programs strongly impact the level of interest among potential students. They indicated that students may be attracted to specific programs, the proximity/convenient location of the university, the level of academic rigor, and the cost of tuition (Supplementary file 4, Table 3).

It is important to note that the participants have pointed out a potential discrepancy between the competencies that MN studies provide and the needs of the workplace (Supplementary file 4, Table 3).

According to the majority of participants (n = 15/18), the introduction of a universal MN degree, equivalent to all institutions in Croatia, would not be beneficial in the recognition of coefficients. Some of them emphasize that upon graduating, nurses have the same MN credentials.

“I think that these differences between the studies have no influence on that, because, in fact, upon completion of our university graduate studies, everyone becomes a Master of Nursing.” (P2)

“I don’t think they have any influence on the mere recognition of the salary coefficient, perhaps on the interpretation of the title. Namely, they are all Master of Nursing.” (P9)

Common career aspirations of nurses when considering enrolling in an MN program, from participants’ perspective, were mostly less demanding workplace, interest in scientific research, being able to participate in education, continuing education, desire for leadership positions, moving abroad, or just wanting to keep up with the competition (Supplementary file 4, Table 4).

Research work

For some students, a key motivator was the desire to engage with scientific inquiry and contribute to nursing knowledge.

“I know for a fact that there are people who are interested in the research part and then enroll in that study.” (P1)

Easier workplace

Some students viewed completing an MN degree as a strategic way to attain positions with more favorable working conditions, such as escaping shift work or securing roles with greater autonomy. The aspiration to become a head nurse or similar position is perceived not only as a step up in status, but also as an opportunity for more regular work hours and a more manageable workload.

“It is possible that they perceive graduate study as one of the ways to free themselves from shift work.” (P3)

“Someone who came with the motive I want to be the head nurse or I want… I have to say that there have been such cases… they say - when I finish more school, I will work less.” (P15)

Participating in education

Participants perceive that graduates are inclined to continue their involvement in academia or training by working directly with students. This reflects a commitment to fostering the next generation of nurses and suggests that contributing to education is an important and valued career pathway following MN studies.

“All of them would actually go further into education, all of them would work with students.” (P17)

Management

Some MN students are motivated by ambitions to assume leadership and managerial roles within clinical and hospital settings. Participants perceived a readiness among certain students to take on responsibilities such as organizing and managing teams or entire departments. Advancing into leadership positions serves a significant career aspiration, reflecting the importance of developing managerial competencies through MN studies.

“Some of them, I think, are ready and willing to take on some leadership positions” (P5)

“Maybe, as well, ambitions to organize, manage the work at their clinic, at, I say, their hospital.” (P15)

Moving abroad

The perception of participants was that acquiring an MN degree equips students with skills and competencies that are recognized and valued internationally. The motivation to pursue MN studies was partly driven by the desire to enhance employability and professional opportunities beyond the Croatian healthcare system. This highlights the role of global labor market dynamics in shaping students’ educational choices and career aspirations.

“Lately, it seems to me that this is the acquisition of skills and competencies that will be well valued abroad.” (P14)

Participants emphasized that an MN degree broadens career opportunities beyond clinical practice, including roles in research, education, mentoring, and the pharmaceutical sector, thereby expanding graduates’ professional scope. However, concerns remained about variability between programs and potential mismatches between acquired competencies and workplace needs, highlighting the ongoing challenge of aligning education with professional recognition and career advancement.

Major theme 3: motives for enrollment in postgraduate doctoral studies

Participants indicated that very few MN students expressed a desire to continue their education to postgraduate doctoral studies.

“I think that maybe around 5% of people are interested, ask, up to a maximum of 10%.” (P1)

“Very little. So far, I haven’t met such students.” (P2)

“Minimal interest, per enrolled generation, 3–5 students express the desire for further advancement in the scientific sense.” (P14)

Participants reported that those students are often inspired by intrinsic motives for pursuing postgraduate doctoral studies, such as personal satisfaction, overall progress, continuing their education, being a part of the faculty, or enhancing their professional image (Supplementary file 4, Table 5).

Those students who do demonstrate interest in pursuing postgraduate doctoral studies appear to be primarily motivated by intrinsic factors, including personal fulfillment, academic progression, continued learning, affiliation with academic institutions, and enhancement of their professional profile. However, financial constraints were cited as a significant barrier limiting engagement in doctoral education, potentially restricting access despite expressed interest.

“Well, not much of them express such desire. Since they are educating themselves, that means they alone have to raise funding for MN studies, and later for doctoral studies.” (P11)

Major theme 4: motivating nurses who May hesitate to enroll in master of nursing programs

Given that nurses do not receive a higher coefficient and salary increase after earning their MN degree, heads and institutions use various general and specific incentives to motivate students, including promoting their specific programs, research work, giving examples of former students, or even just via casual conversation or classroom lectures. However, others suggest that they already have a sufficient number of enrolled students and do not require any additional motivation (Supplementary file 4, Table 6).

Specific incentives

There was active roles that heads of nursing studies used in encouraging and motivating students to pursue academic and professional advancement beyond the MN degree. These efforts reflected the leaders’ commitment to fostering a culture of lifelong learning and professional growth, which serves as an important incentive for students to engage deeply with their studies and envision broader career possibilities.

“For example, when I have students with Master’s and bachelor’s theses, I always motivate them, “let’s go further, let’s publish it somewhere”. I try to involve them in research.” (P3)

“We try very hard, during our lectures, we bring to students the best examples, alumni, who also used to work at bachelor’s positions and after completing MN studies were not initially recognized for their professional qualifications… However, they eventually became directors of hospitals, directors of health centers, and they got their satisfaction.” (P9)

“I motivate them by always telling them that knowledge is something that no one can take from you.” (P10)

“These are mostly conversations, either at lectures or individual consultations.” (P14)

Such targeted and informal incentives reflect a leadership commitment to cultivating lifelong learning and professional development, thereby helping hesitant nurses envision broader career possibilities and engage more deeply with their studies.

Major theme 5: collaboration between MN studies in Croatia and the job market and healthcare system.

Almost all participants (n = 16/18) agreed that enrollment quotas in MN studies may not align with the requirements of job markets. The majority (n = 12/18) pointed out that currently there is likely an excess of individuals with MN degrees in Croatia given the current restricted conditions in the job market. However, several participants (n = 4/18) highlighted that even though the number of individuals with MN degrees may presently seem excessive, considering the present labor market conditions, it was too low to move the nursing profession forward.

“But I think that in the end, there are slightly more of them compared to what is needed on the labor market.” (P2)

“Considering the labor market and what we would have to do… I think there are even too many of them at the moment” (P17)

“I think that as far as the labor market is concerned, yes, but as far as the advancement of the nursing profession is concerned, I think that it is good for everyone to complete MN studies.” (P7)

Participants indicated that the curricula and programs offered at the MN studies in Croatia were developed in collaboration with the healthcare system primarily through personal occasional interest checks and informal market research. Participants reported that the institutions collaborated with various associations and sought advice from other academic institutions when designing the curricula for MN studies (Supplementary file 4, Table 7).

Participants emphasized the significance of participants collaborating on an individual level with healthcare associations and local communities to generate opportunities for nurses with MN degrees who are not automatically eligible for a salary increase.

“I personally participate in the National Committee for Nursing within the Ministry of Health, where, therefore, we are fighting in this sense. I participate in the work of the Council, in the commissions of the Croatian Council of Nurses, where they are also persistently working on recognition of MN degrees.” (P1)

“Me specifically, I can only highlight and emphasize and promote, motivate, and thus give my personal contribution through these associations.” (P3)

“We are very actively involved in that. I mean, from state institutions to our chamber, to the local community.” (P8)

Participants indicated that they advocate and provide support for nurses who have completed an MN program, mostly on a personal level, and are expecting the anticipated advancements in salary or status. Some participants opined that this topic might be outside the scope of their authority (Fig. 3, Supplementary file 4, Table 8).

Participants’ support for nurses who completed Master of Nursing studies

Most participants agreed that the number of nurses enrolled in MN programs currently exceeds the demands of the Croatian job market, reflecting a mismatch between enrollment quotas and employment opportunities. However, a minority highlighted that despite this apparent surplus, the current numbers remained insufficient to drive meaningful advancement in the nursing profession. Collaboration between MN programs and the healthcare system was primarily informal and, based on periodic consultations, with curricular development relying on input from healthcare associations, academic peers, and community stakeholders. Participants highlighted their active personal involvement in advocacy through professional bodies and local institutions to promote recognition of MN qualifications and support nurses awaiting corresponding salary or status advancements. Nonetheless, many perceive those institutional limitations restrict their authority to influence systemic changes in professional recognition and remuneration.

Major theme 6: participants’ recommendations for future

According to half of the participants, in the future, the establishment of specialized MN programs would be helpful, as well as the importance of estimating the number of MN graduates required by the job market, and a better definition of workplaces.

“Perhaps the opening of these specialist studies would be our future, as a kind of strategy.” (P2)

“Perhaps we need to better articulate which jobs require a master’s degree in nursing. It is necessary to make a careful analysis of the current situation and needs, and on the basis of it, revise/supplement the competencies of the Master of Nursing.” (P4).

The majority drew attention to the long-term uncertainty of MN nurses’ professional future in Croatia.

“Because looking in general for the whole of nursing, if the only message is to finish the studies without acknowledging it, I don’t know how good and sustainable it is and how motivating it can be in the long run.” (P13)

Participants emphasized the need for developing specialized MN programs tailored to evolving professional roles and job market demands. They highlighted the importance of accurately assessing the required number of MN graduates and clearly defining relevant workplaces to better align education with job market needs. Additionally, concerns were raised about the ongoing uncertainty surrounding professional recognition and career sustainability for MN graduates in Croatia, underscoring the necessity for strategic planning to enhance motivation and long-term prospects within the nursing profession.

Discussion

Heads of MN programs in Croatia indicated that students enroll in MN studies in Croatia due to intrinsic and extrinsic motives, as well as optimism. Participants acknowledged current difficulties for MN graduates in the labor market, in terms of finding employment with remuneration that would be commensurate to the level of education. However, participants also highlighted the need for MN education as a prerequisite for the advancement of the nursing profession.

Through MN studies, nurses can acquire specific knowledge and various competencies that should open up new possibilities for them in their future careers. Intrinsic motivations include a desire for personal improvement and the enhancement of knowledge and abilities [21]. Other studies similarly noted that master’s nurses have an intense intrinsic motivation for personal improvement and development [22, 23]. In support of these findings, Nalle et al. reported that 83% of nurses believed that continuing their education at a higher level had resulted in a major benefit in terms of increased knowledge and skills [24].

Extrinsic factors such as job security, financial gain, and career advancement were reported as influential in decisions to enroll in MN programs [7, 21]. Despite the absence of universal immediate advantages in terms of salaries and job conditions upon graduation, heads of MN programs considered that part of nurses are driven by external motivational factors while enrolling in MN studies. From their perspective, the desire to pursue an MN degree could also be motivated by the possible chances it could provide in the future, such as increased career opportunities and achieving a professional reputation. Earlier studies also recognized these numerous career benefits such as higher income, better working conditions, and increased chances of promotion as key reasons for pursuing higher nursing education [10, 25, 26].

The perception of lacking recognition of degree and lack of rewards discouraged nurses from enrolling in master’s programs in South African universities, based on a qualitative study, where part of nurses indicated that continuing education seemed like a waste of time [27]. A lack of adequate recognition of postgraduate master’s qualification by authorities and a salary that is not competitive were two obstacles that discouraged African nurses from pursuing an MN degree. As a result, academic institutions constantly raised concerns about the low enrollment of nurses in master’s studies [27].

Our findings imply that Croatian nurses have a strong sense of optimism when enrolling in MN studies, hoping that the MN degree would benefit them in the long run. This is backed up by statements made by heads of MN, who asserted that their enrollment is consistently high. Moreover, they personally engage in conversations with bachelor’s students to encourage them to pursue a master’s degree.

Heads of MN programs see MN programs as a great foundation for not only delivering clinical care, but also for engaging in research, leadership, and giving special emphasis on nursing education. These findings are significant in light of the recent challenges faced in 2022, such as the growing number of nursing faculty retirements and resignations and an absence of qualified faculty to educate future nurses [28, 29]. At North Carolina College, there is a requirement for a student-to-faculty ratio of 10 students to 1 faculty member. Nursing faculty often face challenges in managing this ratio, leading to reduced educator input and limited learning outcomes for students. The requirements of nursing education could benefit from a collective world effort [28].

Some participants pointed out a discrepancy between the qualifications and the requirements of workplaces. This result was in line with that of Salifu et al. which indicated that the nursing education curriculum insufficiently prepared nursing students to confront the practical challenges of the clinical environment [30]. Norwegian nurses with MN degrees acknowledged their managers and colleagues were even unaware of the full extent of their qualifications and potential contributions to the workplace. Some individuals viewed MN nurses as a threat because those with MN degrees were occasionally more qualified than other department staff [31]. Inadequate curriculum implementation and poor integration of nursing theory in practice were recognized worldwide [32].

However, heads emphasized informal collaboration with healthcare institutions, and associations creating curriculums. In addition, they put forward recommendations regarding the definition of workplaces and the establishment of specialized MN studies. An experienced clinical nursing specialist can greatly enhance healthcare organizations by improving clinical outcomes [33]. Studies published by Conidine in 2001 and 2005 suggested that specific education of nurses in a specialist area may enhance clinical decision-making processes and that this may lead to improved patient care in the emergency department settings [34, 35]. Thus, specialized MN studies in Croatia could be beneficial for both patients and nurses; they could help with motivating future students and might help bridge the gap between competencies gained at MN studies and employers’ requirements.

The importance of partnership between educational institutions and employers to discuss academic programs that might potentially influence nursing care was also recognized by Mbombi et al. [27]. It is essential to clearly define and recognize the skills that specialist nurses need [36]. Although nursing in Croatia is recognized as an independent profession with work autonomy, the competencies acquired from MN studies do not currently permit the administration of medications without a physician’s approval, leaving nurses supervised. Enhancing competence in specialized MN studies could lead to further strength of their autonomy.

A systematic review published in 2012, about the effect of postgraduate nursing education at the master’s level on patient care revealed five themes, including increased confidence and self-esteem, enhanced communication, personal and professional growth, knowledge and application of theory to practice, and analytical thinking and decision making [37]. Those recurrent themes indicate that there are apparent positive gains for nurses who undertake MN studies, related to professional and personal growth which can lead to an increased ability to positively influence patient care, leading to improved care delivery or improved patient outcomes [37]. Ge et al. published a systematic review in 2015, which showed that palliative care, continuity of care, mental health, transition care, post-transplant care and central venous catheter care were improved when patient care was delivered by nurses with MN degrees [38]. Those studies indicate that higher education of nurses is beneficial for the health system. This can be seen also in the responses of participants in this study. However, they recognized that for the current labor market in Croatia, with few available positions for nurses with MN degrees, enrolment rates into MN programs may be high. However, considering the needs of the healthcare system, more nurses with MN degrees would be beneficial.

Some nurses could enroll in MN studies as a prerequisite for continuing education towards postgraduate doctoral studies. However, our participants perceived that there is a limited interest in doctoral studies among MN students, with motivations primarily based on personal interest. Kinsella et al. earlier observed that the majority of participants considered intrinsic motives more essential than extrinsic factors when selecting postgraduate nursing schools [21].

The findings of this study offer several clear implications for heads of MN programs. By understanding the diverse motivations that drive students, particularly their focus on career advancement in uncertain contexts, program leaders can tailor recruitment strategies and provide more targeted academic and psychosocial support. Awareness of students’ concerns about job security and uncertain professional recognition enables heads of nursing to advocate for clearer career pathways at institutional and policy levels. Additionally, open communication with students about their aspirations and expectations can foster a supportive educational environment, enhance student satisfaction, and contribute to higher rates of program completion and retention. Ultimately, these actions can help strengthen the reputation of MN programs and build a robust, future-ready nursing workforce.

To our best knowledge, this is the first study that explored the perceptions of the heads of MN studies in Croatia regarding possible motives of students for enrolling in MN studies, considering the paucity of positions for nurses with MN degrees in the labor market. The strength of our study lies in the fact that we included the majority of heads and deputies of MN programs (18/21; 86%), representing all MN programs in Croatia.

Building on the present findings, future research should directly examine the motivations and experiences of students themselves to provide a more comprehensive understanding of factors influencing enrollment in MN programs. Future studies could also explore in depth whether there are indeed discrepancies between the MN curricula and requirements of workplaces, and potentially contribute to revising the MN programs. Additionally, quantitative studies are needed to assess correlations between motivational factors and subsequent career outcomes, such as employment rates and job satisfaction. Further exploration of institutional and systemic factors that shape employment prospects for MN graduates, such as workplace policies, recognition mechanisms, and regional differences, would also be valuable for informing educational and workforce policies.

Limitations

This study had several limitations. First, the sample was limited to the heads of MN programs in Croatia; therefore, the findings may not be generalizable to other countries or institutional contexts. Second, as the data were gathered solely from the perspectives of program heads, there may be social desirability bias, and the views expressed may not fully capture the diversity of student motivations or experiences. Third, data was based on self-reports obtained through interviews, which may be influenced by participants’ subjective interpretations or recall. Finally, while we aimed for comprehensive coverage by including every eligible institution, perspectives of students themselves and other stakeholders, such as employers or policymakers, were not included.

Conclusion

Perspectives of heads of MN studies in Croatia revealed a complex of intrinsic and extrinsic motivations, alongside optimism about long-term career benefits, influencing the choice of Croatian nurses to pursue a master’s degree, despite challenges in the employment sector.

The perceived discrepancy between the qualifications obtained through MN programs and the actual needs of workplaces needs collaborative efforts between universities and healthcare providers. That can enhance the credibility and value of MN degrees in Croatia. As a potential solution, heads of MN studies recommended the development of specialized MN programs. By following these suggestions, there is potential for faster acknowledgment of nurses’ competencies and much-desired validation of their Master of Nursing degrees.
