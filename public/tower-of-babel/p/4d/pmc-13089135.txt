Comment on “Clinical Outcomes of Flexor Tenolysis Following Zone 2–3 Flexor Tendon Repair: A Retrospective Review”
Authors: Anuradha Mokkapati, Rhushvi Thakkar, Anjna Rani
Published: 2026

To the Editor:

We read with great interest the study by Shigley et al1 evaluating outcomes of flexor tenolysis following zone 2–3 flexor tendon repair. Defining realistic expectations after this technically demanding secondary procedure is clinically important, particularly in the context of persistent stiffness despite structured rehabilitation.

Although total active motion improved from 146.8° to 183.5° and proximal interphalangeal joint motion increased significantly, the functional interpretation of these gains deserves closer consideration. Postoperative total active motion remained below the arc typically associated with near-normal digital kinematics, and only half of digits achieved excellent or good Modified Strickland grades. Angular improvements, although statistically significant, do not necessarily equate to restoration of coordinated grip, fine motor control, or work capacity.2 Incorporating validated functional metrics such as grip strength recovery, dexterity testing, or return-to-activity timelines would better contextualize the clinical impact of these motion gains.

Outcome variability across subgroups also raises mechanistic questions. Dual tendon repairs, revision tenolysis, and concomitant digital nerve injuries introduce distinct biomechanical and neurophysiologic constraints that influence tendon excursion and motor relearning.3 Given the modest sample size within several subgroups, observed differences may reflect complex interactions among repair bulk, pulley mechanics, proprioceptive feedback, and scar remodeling rather than isolated procedural effects. Multivariable modeling integrating these interacting variables could clarify which factors independently predict durable motion recovery.

The notable frequency of reoperation further suggests that peritendinous fibrosis may recur in susceptible patients. Surgical adhesion release reinitiates local inflammatory cascades, and without optimized load progression during rehabilitation, renewed fibroproliferation may compromise early gains.4 Prospective studies standardizing postoperative motion protocols and quantifying therapy adherence could identify modifiable determinants of sustained improvement.

Taken together, the findings support flexor tenolysis as a motion-enhancing intervention, yet emphasize that statistical improvement should be interpreted alongside biomechanical efficiency and long-term functional restoration.
