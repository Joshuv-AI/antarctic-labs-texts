Demographics and psychosocial determinants of quit intention among oral smokeless tobacco users: an analytical cross-sectional study in Saudi Arabia
Authors: Mohammed Jafer, Ibtisam Moafa
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTobacco use remains a significant global public health challenge, with a noticeable increase in the consumption of oral smokeless tobacco (OST) in response to stricter smoking regulations. Despite recognizing the health risks associated with OST, there is limited research on the factors influencing individuals’ intention to quit, particularly in Saudi Arabia. The study’s aimed to investigate OST behavior and the demographics and psychosocial determinants of the intention to quit among OST users.MethodsA cross-sectional analytical study was conducted, utilizing a convenient sample of current OST users. A structured questionnaire was developed informed by the World Health Organization’s Global Adult Tobacco Survey, literature review, and experts’ consultation. The questionnaire evaluated demographics, tobacco behaviors, perceived control beliefs, subjective norms, and intention to quit. Data were collected through an online platform and subsequently analyzed using SPSS (version 29). Descriptive and inferential statistics were done including multinomial logistic regression to identify which factors significantly predicted the intentions to quit.ResultsA total of 341 OST users participated (mean age: 39.1 years; 58.4% male; 61.5% married). While 46% expressed a positive intention to quit, demographic factors alone were not significant predictors. Key predictors included OST use for over 15 years, past quit attempts, dentist advice, subjective norms, and perceived control. Gender moderated these effects: females receiving quit advice were 4.48 times more likely to intend to quit, while males showed increased quit intention with stronger subjective norms (AOR = 0.547).ConclusionsThe findings of this study revealed key determinants influencing OST users’ intention to quit: the longer duration of OST use, prior attempts, dentist quit advice, perceived control beliefs, and subjective norms. Future research is needed to explore gender differences concerning the effectiveness of dentist’s advice and subjective norms.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24386-5.

Background

Tobacco use is a leading cause of preventable illness and death globally, responsible for around 8 million mortality annually [1]. In Saudi Arabia, it accounted for approximately 14,238 mortality (9.1% of all deaths) in 2021 [2]. As of 2022, 16.5% of adults aged 15 and older were current smokers, with 26.3% of men and 1.9% of women smoking [3]. Among youth aged 13–15, 9.4% reported using tobacco products [4]. The economic cost of smoking, including healthcare and productivity losses, exceeded 38.8 billion Saudi Riyals [5]. Tobacco smoke contains harmful substances that affect both smokers and non-smokers through secondhand exposure [6]. In recent years, there has been a shift in the use of e-cigarettes and oral smokeless tobacco (OST) [2, 3]. OST is a type of tobacco that is not smoked but snuffed, sniffed, or chewed, typically made of tobacco leaves, pepper, lime, coloring and flavoring agents [4]. The use of OST has been linked to serious health consequences, including anemia, cardiovascular diseases, preterm delivery, pancreatic and oral cancer [5–9]. It was estimated that OST usage was responsible for more than 90,000 deaths worldwide in 2019 [10]. Furthermore, the initiation of OST use during adolescence was found to be a predictive factor for the future adoption of smoking tobacco in late adolescence [11]. National Saudi surveys in 2019 indicated that about 2% of adults report using OST products, but this figure rises among specific groups [12]. For instance, the 2022 Global Youth Tobacco Survey revealed that 3.6% of Saudi adolescents aged 13 to 15 use culturally significant products like Shammah and Dokha, particularly in the southern and western regions [13]. Shammah consists of tobacco powder, black pepper, lime, and flavorings, used by placing it in the mouth and spitting out the residue [9]. While comprehensive data on the economic impact of OST use in Saudi Arabia is limited, the health risks—including oral cancer, periodontal disease, and cardiovascular issues—are well-documented [9].

The rise of OST use can be attributed to various factors, including the comprehensive focus on smoking tobacco policies (e.g., taxation) and the promotion/portrayal of OST as a potentially less harmful alternative due to the absence of smoke (inhalation) [14, 15]. Unlike smoking tobacco, which is prevalent among males all around the world, OST appears to be a common substance among females [16–20]. The popularity of OST among female populations was evident in Europe, North America, South Asia, Sudan, Yemen and Saudi Arabia [20–27]. Numerous studies have identified several factors influencing the use of OST across individual, social and environmental levels. These factors included awareness of OST health effects, attitudes toward its use, past quit attempts, concurrent use of OST with other substances such as smoking or Ghat (Catha edulis), family/peer influence, social/cultural acceptance and easy accessibility [24–26]. Challenges such as withdrawal symptoms, lack of support, and lack of how to quit, are frequently cited in the literature as factors hindering successful quitting [26–30]. Conversely, factors that facilitate quitting tobacco include changing lifestyle, changing friends, receiving quit advice from health professionals, using nicotine replacement therapy, and finding distractions through perceived natural alternatives such as chewing gum or cloves or drinking coffee [3, 19, 23]. Established theoretical frameworks, including Social Cognitive Theory (SCT), the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB), and the Transtheoretical Model (TTM), provided valuable insights into the determinants of health behavior change [24, 25]. One key factor is perceived behavioral control (i.e., self-efficacy), which refers to an individual’s confidence in his ability to quit OST [10, 26–30]. This concept was emphasized in both SCT and TPB [31, 32]. Another influential factor highlighted by the TPB is the subjective norm, which refers to an individual’s perception of social expectations regarding the adoption of a particular behavior [31]. Additionally, the TTM highlights the stages of behavior change, indicating that past behavior (e.g., quit attempts) plays a significant role in the process of quitting [33].

Despite widespread acknowledgments of the health risks associated with smoking, comparable efforts to address OST were relatively limited, particularly in the Arabian context [34–38]. Significant research gaps remain regarding the psychosocial factors that influence OST cessation. Therefore, the objective of this study was to investigate the potential factors that influence the intention to quit OST among users in Saudi Arabia. This study examined how various factors, such as demographics, tobacco characteristics, and psychosocial factors, contributed to users’ intentions to quit OST. The research question was: What demographic and psychosocial factors best predict the intention to quit OST among users in Saudi Arabia? The significance of this study lies in its contribution to a deeper understanding of the factors that influence the intention of OST users to quit in Saudi Arabia. Expanding on existing literature, this study can guide targeted cessation interventions by addressing the unique needs of OST users in the country and other countries with similar use patterns.

Primary hypothesis

Concurrent use of OST with other substances (tobacco products or Ghat), past quit attempts, perceived control over quitting, and subjective norms influence the intention to quit OST.

Methods

Study design and sample

An analytical cross-sectional study was conducted among OST users in Saudi Arabia between March 5 and August 5 2024 using an online Google Form survey. in Arabic, as the participants are native Arabic speakers. Participation was voluntary and anonymous; no identifying information was collected. The study utilized a convenience sampling technique. The study adhered to the STROBE checklist guidelines.

Questionnaire

The self-reported questionnaire was developed based on a comprehensive literature review, relevant studies’ previous findings, an adaptation of the Global Adult Tobacco Survey (GATS) questionnaire and consultations with experts in the field [26, 39, 40]. The English-translated version of the Arabic questionnaire utilized in the study is provided in supplementary file 1. The average time required for participants to complete the questionnaire ranged from 4 to 5.50 min.

Direct measures

Demographic characteristics

The initial section of the questionnaire gathered information regarding the participants’ demographic characteristics, including gender, marital status, employment status and education attainment.

Tobacco use behaviours

The subsequent section focused on the onset and frequency of OST usage, as well as the concurrent use of Ghat and other tobacco products with OST (e.g., cigarettes, e-cigarettes, Hookah).

Subjective norms (SNs)

Subjective norms were measured using a five-point scale. One item was asked: “ Most of the people who are important to me accept that I use OST”. Response alternatives ranged from “Strongly Agree” to “Strongly Disagree.”

Perceived behavioral control (PBC)

PBC was measured using a five-point scale. One item was asked: “How confident are you in your ability to quit OST within the next six months?”. Response alternatives ranged from “unconfident at all” to “highly confident.

Intention

The final question assessed the outcome measure, which is the participants’ intention to quit using OST in the next six months, by one item: “Do you intend to quit OST within the next six months?; response alternatives, no, not sure or yes.

Belief-based measures

All items used as belief-based measures (normative and control beliefs) were extended from a recent study of interviews among 31 OST users [26], and were measured using five-point scales. An index was calculated for each construct by adding the belief composites for each variable, respectively.

Normative Beliefs

Seven normative beliefs composites were created by multiplying injunctive normative belief strengths (expectations by others) or descriptive normative beliefs (actions of others) by the motivation to comply (motivation to comply with others’ opinions) or identification with the referent (motivation to comply with others’ opinions and actions). An example of an injunctive normative belief strength measure is: “My family/parents …. my use of OST”; response alternatives range from strongly disapprove to strongly approve. An example of a measure of motivation to comply is: “For me, having my family/parent approval is….”; response alternatives range from not important at all to very important. An example of a descriptive normative belief strength measure is: “Having friends/colleagues use OST during breaks or social gathering is….”; response alternative ranged from extremely unlikely to highly likely. An example of a measure of identification with the referent is: “For me, using what my friends/colleagues use or drink during break or social gathering is……”; response alternative ranged from not important at all to very important.

Control Beliefs

Sixteen control beliefs were identified by multiplying control belief strength (factors that facilitate or hinder cessation of OST) by the power of control (perceived power on these factors). An example of a measure of control belief strength is: “The accessibility to OST is.…….”; response alternatives ranged from very difficult to very easy. An example of a measure of the power of control factor: “Easy accessibility to OST makes my likelihood of quitting OST is ….”; response alternatives ranged from extremely unlikely to high likely.

Other predictors

Other factors assessed in the model included OST users’ recall of receiving verbal advice from their dentists to quit using OST.

Validity and reliability

The questionnaire underwent a comprehensive validation process. Face validity was established through feedback from seven OST users representing diverse backgrounds. Minor revisions were made to improve clarity and readability. Content validity was assessed by a panel of five experts in dental public health, psychology, and tobacco counseling. The experts evaluated item relevance, scaling, and cultural appropriateness, leading to the removal of culturally sensitive items such as alcohol use, which was deemed irrelevant in the Saudi context due to legal and cultural norms.

Construct validity was evaluated using Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA), which confirmed the theoretical structure with factor loadings exceeding 0.60 across all items. Internal consistency was measured using Cronbach’s alpha, with values ranging from 0.872 to 0.918, indicating very good reliability. Test-retest reliability was also conducted with a subsample of 50 participants over a two-week interval, yielding Intraclass Correlation Coefficients (ICC) between 0.78 and 0.86, demonstrating good stability.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics including frequency and percentages were utilized to describe the variables related to sample demographics (e.g., gender), characteristics of OST use (e.g., onset of use) and perceived control beliefs about the likelihood of quitting OST. Chi-square test as well as Correlation analysis was conducted using Kendall’s tau (τ), which is arithmetically bound between − 1 to + 1, following these specifications: strong positive agreement: tau > 0.45, moderate positive agreement: tau > 0.27, and weak positive agreement: tau is > 0.09. Multinomial logistic regression (MNLR) was conducted to test predictive relationships between participants’ gender, initiation of OST use, perceived control beliefs, subjective norms, past quitting attempts and dentist quit advice. Moreover, the model explored possible interaction terms. The model regressed OST users’ intention to quit on their gender, onset of OST use, control beliefs, subjective norms, past quitting attempts and dentist advice about quitting OST. The MNLR was comprised of two binary logit models where: (a) OST users indicating they are uncertain about quitting and (b) OST users indicating positive quit intention, were compared against a (c) baseline/reference category included OST users indicating negative quit intention. The model’s overall fit was evaluated using the likelihood ratio chi-square test, Pearson and Deviance chi-square tests, and Nagelkerk and Cox and Snell’s pseudo-R2.

Results

Demographic and tobacco use characteristics

A total of 341 OST users participated in the study, with a mean age of 39.11 years (median age 40), ranging from 16 to 80 years. More than half of the participants were male, married, and literate, with varying educational backgrounds—some had university or higher education, while only were non-literate. Nearly half of the participants reported having family and friends who use OST or use OST in combination with Ghat. A significant portion of the participants had a long-standing history of OST use, which commenced at a young age and continued up to the time of data collection. In general, male OST users were younger, more educated, more likely to be married, have full-time jobs and frequently use Ghat with OST than females. Associations between intention to quit OST and study variables

Subjective norms (SNs)

Correlation analysis indicated a relatively weak but statistically significant positive correlation between the ranks of quit intention and two aspects of descriptive norms: the extent to which participants believe it is important to use what friends or colleagues use during breaks and gatherings (𝜏_b = 0.174, p < 0.001) and the extent to which participants believe it is important to align with family members’ choices during meetings (𝜏_b = 0.170, p < 0.001). A negative and relatively weak statistically significant correlation was observed between the social norm (the belief that using OST is normal in Saudi Arabia) and quit intention (𝜏_b = − 0.130, p = 0.007). This may suggest that participants who view OST as less normal are more likely to express a positive intention to quit.

Perceived behavioral control (PBC)

Among the 341 participants, 33.2% (n = 113) reported high perceived behavioral control (PBC) over quitting OST. Of these, more than half (54.9%, n = 62) also expressed a positive intention to quit. In contrast, those with low PBC (46.1%, n = 157) were much less likely to intend to quit, with only 31.9% (n = 50) indicating a positive intention.

Participants identified several internal control factors they believed could support cessation. The most frequently mentioned facilitators were: having a quit plan, changing friend’s circle, and possessing determination and true intention to quit. Conversely, common internal barriers included: having withdrawal symptoms, life stress, and lack of social/psychological support.

In terms of external control beliefs, participants commonly cited tobacco cessation centers and use of natural alternatives like gum or cloves as helpful. However, easy accessibility to OST and the influence of family or friends who use tobacco were widely viewed as barriers. Gender differences were also noted. Female participants were more likely to perceive the use of natural alternatives and knowledge of how to quit as helpful facilitators. These perceptions were further supported in the correlation analysis (Table 4). There was a moderate, statistically significant positive correlation between perceived facilitators and participants’ PBC.

While most items showed moderate associations, beliefs such as “changing friend’s circle” and “determinant and true intention” were positively correlated but with weaker correlations. On the other hand, negative control beliefs—such as life stress and lack of social support—were inversely correlated with PBC, ranging from weak to strong correlations (Table 4). Finally, participants with higher PBC scores were significantly more likely to intend to quit, as indicated by a moderate positive correlation between PBC and quit intention (𝜏_b = 0.294, p < 0.001).

Multinomial logistic regression model

The final model showed a statistically significant decrease in the unexplained variance from the baseline (null) model to the final model (Table 5).

Predictor variables associated with the outcome

The omnibus test (Likelihood ratio χ²) of the effect of each of the predictors on the model was assessed to determine which predictor contributed significantly to the overall model fit. After adjustment, the likelihood ratio tests were statistically significant for the following predictors: initiation of OST use, past quitting attempts, dentist quit advice, PBC and SNs. In addition, the model was statistically significant for the interaction terms gender*dentist advice and gender*SNs. Table 4 demonstrates the regression coefficients, adjusted odds ratios, and percentage change in odds per unit increment in each predictor for the logit functions simultaneously estimated with the multinomial logit model. For the first binary logit function model (Uncertain versus Negative quit intention), initiation of OST use more than 15 years ago, past quitting attempts, dentist quit advice, and the PBC were statistically significant. For the second logit function (Positive quit intention versus Negative quit intention), initiation of OST > 15 years, past quitting attempts, dentist’s advice, SNs, PBC and the interaction terms gender*dentist advice, and gender*SNs were found to be statistically significant.

Interaction term

The positive slope for the interaction term gender*dentist advice illustrated that the participants’ recall of receiving the dentist’s quit advice interacted with their gender to predict positive intention to quit OST. As gender changes from male (coded 1) to female (coded 2) in combination with a change from not receiving quit advice (coded 1) to receiving quit advice (coded 2), the change in the odds of expressing positive quit intention compared to negative one was 4.48. In contrast, the negative slope for the interaction term gender*SNs showed that as SNs increase, the probability of male participants expressing positive quit intention is higher than that of their female counterparts.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this study is the first to investigate OST demographics and psychosocial factors that influence quit intention among users in Saudi Arabia. Nearly half of the participants had positive intentions to quit OST. Key predictors of positive quit intention included longer duration of OST use, previous quit attempts, dentist’s quit advice, PBC, and SNs. In contrast, demographics and tobacco characteristics (other than long duration of use) did not significantly influence users’ intention to quit OST.

A vast majority of research has shown that prolonged tobacco use negatively influences users’ intention to quit [41]. However, our study presents a different perspective, revealing that a longer duration of OST use is associated with a higher likelihood of expressing a positive intention to quit. One explanation for this finding is that a possible heightened sense of vulnerability to tobacco-related illness may have developed after experiencing health issues related to tobacco use over time [42]. This increased vulnerability might have influenced their beliefs, leading to a positive intention to quit [42]. Another explanation, based on self-affirmation theory, suggests that individuals may be motivated to maintain a positive self-image in response to health-related feedback [43]. Long-term OST users may engage in self-affirmation, focusing on positive aspects of their identity, such as their roles as a loving father/mother and the desire to set a positive model for their children [43–46]. However, this interpretation remains tentative and requires further empirical investigation.

A history of past quit attempts was found to be a strong predictor of quit intention, with those who tried to quit previously being more likely to quit again. This finding is in line with a recent network analysis study, which found that the number of prior quit attempts had the strongest positive association with current motivation to quit [47]. Moreover, it is in line with a previous systematic review, which considered past quit attempts a high predictor of quit attempts [47, 48].

Another important finding of this study is that the dentist’s advice plays a significant role in encouraging female users of OST to consider quitting. This observation is consistent with previous research showing that healthcare professionals, particularly dentists, play a crucial role in motivating tobacco cessation [49]. A systematic review and meta-analysis highlighted dental clinics as a promising setting for tobacco cessation interventions, as dentists often meet with patients more frequently than physicians [50]. A possible explanation for the benefits females gain from dentist’s advice on quitting tobacco is that, in general, studies have shown that females are more likely to comply with cessation advice from credible healthcare providers such as dentists [51]. In the current study sample, female participants were more likely than their male counterparts to report receiving advice from dentists, which could further clarify the observed gender difference. Additional research is needed to explore gender differences concerning the effectiveness of dentist’s advice.

Another key finding from the present study is that PBC when evaluated through perceived belief-based (indirect) measures, is a better predictor of positive intention to quit than direct measures. This aligns with past research that found belief-based measures such as perceived barriers and facilitators capture a broader and more specific range of factors that shape behavioral intentions than general direct measures [31, 52]. For instance, research on smoking cessation found that indirect measures of PBC exploring the subtle aspects of control beliefs highly represent intentions to quit [53]. Therefore, future research should focus on belief-based measures for better insights into factors affecting OST cessation intentions.

Finally, subjective norms were found to significantly influence the intention to quit OST use among male users, as supported by both direct and indirect measures. This observation can be attributed to several contextual factors. Prior research indicated that social influences, including peer influence and societal expectations, disproportionately affect males compared to females within specific cultural frameworks [31, 54]. In Saudi Arabia, traditional gender roles often position males in more prominent social and public environments, which may lead to increased normative influences concerning substance use, including OST [55]. Conversely, female users were less affected by subjective norms, likely due to cultural restrictions that discourage public tobacco use and generally smaller social networks, which limit discussions about cessation [56]. Additionally, the prevalence of tobacco use tends to be significantly higher among males than females across various societies, including those in the Arabian Gulf region [56]. Consequently, males may exhibit greater sensitivity to the subjective norms surrounding cessation, facing amplified pressure to quit due to expectations from peers and familial networks.

Strength and limitations

This study presents one of the first national investigations into the use of oral smokeless tobacco (OST) and quit intentions in Saudi Arabia. It provides insights relevant to public health planning and Vision 2030, based on established behavioral theories, including the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB), Social Cognitive Theory (SCT), and the Transtheoretical Model (TTM). Strengths of this study include the use of validated theoretical frameworks, culturally adapted survey instruments, and robust statistical techniques. However, several limitations should be considered. The cross-sectional nature of the self-reported data restricts causal interpretations and may introduce biases related to recall or social desirability. Additionally, the gender imbalance and the use of convenience sampling through online platforms may limit the generalizability of the findings and the ability to calculate the response rate. To address these concerns, participant anonymity was ensured, and the survey instrument was piloted and refined to ensure its accuracy and reliability. For future research, it is recommended to adopt longitudinal designs to assess behavior changes over time, incorporate objective measures such as biomarkers, employ stratified or probability-based sampling methods, and combine online and offline approaches could enhance representativeness and external validity.

Conclusions

The study identified key predictors influencing users’ intentions to quit OST in Saudi Arabia. These determinants included the duration of OST use, past quit attempts, dentist advice, control beliefs, and subjective norms. It emphasized dentists’ important role in supporting cessation efforts and the understanding individuals’ control beliefs in predicting their intention to quit. In contrast, demographic and tobacco characteristics did not significantly affect users’ intention to quit. These insights can guide effective, culturally tailored public health strategies to reduce tobacco use and improve overall health in alignment with the Saudi 2030 vision.
