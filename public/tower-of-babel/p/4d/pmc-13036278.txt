Economic Burden of Developmental Disorder Treatment in Korea and Various Overseas Policies: A Study on the Reinforcement for National Assistance
Authors: Hayeon Kim, Dong-Gyun Han, Hee-Sun Kim, Seongwoo Seo, Yoonjae Lee, Haemi Choi, Jeongeun Lee, Jae-Gu Kang, Tae-Hyeong Kim, Eunseol Ko, Min-Hyeon Park
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDevelopmental disorders are characterized by delays in motor skills, language, cognition, emotional regulation, and social abilities. The most common conditions within this category include intellectual disabilities and autism spectrum disorder, which not only impact individuals and families but also have broader effects on the socioeconomic system. Despite the necessity of early detection and intensive treatment for developmental disorders, limited coverage by the national health insurance system in South Korea imposes a significant financial burden on families.MethodsIn this study, a structured survey was conducted on 105 caregivers of infants and children younger than six with developmental disorders, 52 medical doctors (child and adolescent psychiatrists, pediatricians, and rehabilitation physicians), and 51 treatment specialists to assess the economic circumstances related to developmental disorder treatment.ResultsAmong 105 children (68.6% male), most initiated treatment between ages 2 and 4, with speech therapy (88.4%) and sensory therapy (64.0%) being the most utilized services. Although Applied Behavior Analysis had the highest session intensity and cost, its voucher utilization was remarkably low (2.4%), contributing to a significant perceived financial burden, with 72.4% of caregivers reporting high burden (mean score 3.99/5). Consequently, specialists reported a discrepancy between recommended and accessible treatments due to costs, rating the reasonableness of current voucher fees at only 1.98 out of 5.ConclusionGiven the substantial financial burden, the introduction of new policies and revisions to existing policies are urgently needed to support the treatment costs for individuals with developmental disorders.Keywords: Developmental Disorder, Treatment, Insurance, Autism, Financial Burden, Korea

INTRODUCTION

Developmental disorders are a group of conditions characterized by impairments in physical, cognitive, language, or behavioral development, typically manifesting in early childhood and persisting across the lifespan. Among these, intellectual disabilities and autism spectrum disorder (ASD) are serious conditions that can significantly impact one’s entire life. The Ministry of Health and Welfare of South Korea classifies intellectual disabilities and ASD as severe disorders within developmental disorders. It provides additional benefits to individuals affected by these conditions. Developmental disorders are known to impose a significant burden not only on individuals with developmental disorders and their families but also on the socioeconomic system.

Globally, the prevalence of ASD has been increasing, accompanied by a steep rise in the associated economic burden. As of 2015, the prevalence of ASD in South Korea was reported to be 11 per 100,000 individuals, reflecting an approximately twofold increase from 2008 (5 per 100,000). The annual economic burden of ASD in 2015 was estimated at 12.3 billion KRW (which corresponds to approximately 8,536,000 USD based on the exchange rate as of February 2025), representing a nearly fourfold increase from 3.4 billion KRW in 2008. According to previous studies, given the number of ASD patients in South Korea (7,728 individuals), the estimated economic burden per patient is approximately 15 million KRW.

The additional costs associated with intellectual disabilities and ASD have been found to be approximately 287,000 KRW and 608,400 KRW per month, respectively. These monetary amounts represent 174% and 369% higher costs than the average additional monthly cost of 165,100 KRW for all disabilities. Notably, more than half of the additional costs associated with developmental disorders are associated with childcare and education; these costs are significantly greater than those associated with other disabilities.1 These enormous treatment costs are due to speech therapy, cognitive therapy, psychotherapy, Applied Behavior Analysis (ABA), etc., which are recognized to provide moderate to high effectiveness for intellectual disability and ASD children.2,3,4

Second, in South Korea, there is still no comprehensive system for tracking developmental disorders or for establishing guidelines for their assessment, leading to frequent missed opportunities for early intervention due to limited social resources and other constraints.

When developmental delays, which affect 5–10% of all children,5,6 are not detected and intervened in early, such delays can accelerate and potentially lead to permanent impairment.7,8,9 The developmental period, which includes infancy and childhood and extends through adolescence, involves rapid neurological development, including brain development.

According to the “Research on Medical Expense Support for Children with Disabilities,” 62.5% of the 627 individuals diagnosed with developmental disorders at three university hospitals were between the ages of 0 and 2. Additionally, 32% of children aged 0–1 years, who are not eligible for a disability diagnosis in South Korea, were found to be in this category. Neurodevelopmental defects can lead to worsening issues in areas such as learning, language, self-management, and independent living in adulthood if they are not detected early.10,11 Therefore, early detection and treatment are crucial. Early diagnosis and treatment during the critical age range of 1–2 years can enhance effectiveness.12 However, in South Korea, treatment typically begins in children aged 3–4 years.13

National early intervention support systems for individuals with developmental disorders include the “National Health Screening Program for Infants and Children,” “Support for Developmental Disability Diagnostic Testing,” and “Developmental Rehabilitation Services.” The health screening program is not mandatory, as it is in Japan. The “Support for Developmental Disability Diagnostic Testing” program offers up to 400,000 KRW for medical aid recipients and lower-income households and up to 200,000 KRW for those in the lower 50% of health insurance premium contributions.14

The “Developmental Rehabilitation Services” program is a representative voucher program aimed at improving functions such as cognition, communication, adaptation, behavior, and sensory and motor skills in children with disabilities. Support is provided to households with children under 18 years of age and with a standard median income of 180% or less; those under 9 years of age can access services without disability registration. The services cover areas such as speech therapy, auditory training, art, music, behavioral therapy, play therapy, psychotherapy, sensory therapy, and motor therapy. This can be selected in parts and used twice per week for 50 minutes per session.15 As of January 2023, the service unit cost per session was 30,000 KRW, 8 times a month (twice a week), with a maximum voucher amount of 250,000 KRW per month.16

A similar initiative is the “Infant and Toddler Development Support Service” program, which is part of the “Community Service Investment” program. This initiative provides intervention services for infants at risk of developmental issues, supporting normal development in children aged 0–6 years. Households with an income of less than 140% of the median income receive monthly support of 160,000 to 180,000 KRW.17 Additionally, the “Special Education Treatment Support Program” administered by the Ministry of Education provides special education and therapeutic services to children aged 3–18 years with special educational needs. It offers voucher support of 120,000 to 170,000 KRW per month.18 These similar programs overlap with the “Developmental Rehabilitation Services” program with respect to the services that they provide and thus cannot be used concurrently with that program.

All of these programs have limitations within the target group, as they are provided to medical aid recipients, second-lowest-income households and those whose median income is below certain thresholds. As a result, these support programs are insufficient to fully cover treatment costs.

This study investigates how extreme the household burden is because the cost of treating developmental disorders is not covered by national health insurance. Additionally, it aims to alleviate the severe socioeconomic burden due to difficulty in early diagnosis and failure to receive adequate treatment due to restrictions on treatment costs.

METHODS

Participants

We conducted an online survey using a structured questionnaire from August 17 to October 3, 2023. We divided the survey subjects into three groups, and the questionnaires were developed and independently conducted according to each group. The total number of survey subjects was 208: 105 caregivers of infants and young children (under 6 years old) with developmental disorders who had experience using developmental disorder treatment services, 52 medical doctors (child and adolescent psychiatrists, pediatricians, and rehabilitation physicians), and 51 treatment specialists (speech-language pathologists, researchers, behavioral therapists, art therapists, clinical psychologists, occupational therapists, counselors, and the director of a developmental center). ABA professionals in ABA centers were mostly qualified as a Board Certified Behavior Analyst (BCBA), as certified by the Behavior Analyst Certification Board, which is an international nonprofit organization dedicated to training professionals in behavior analysis, and as a Qualified Behavior Analyst (QBA), as certified by the Qualified ABA Credentialing Board, which is internationally accredited through the American National Standards Institute.

Research method

After the research team developed the draft questionnaire, it held an advisory meeting with four external experts to review the validity of the questionnaire items. The final questionnaire, which reflected the opinions of the experts, was developed into three web-based structured questionnaires by a specialized survey company for convenience. A survey access link was distributed along with an official document of cooperation for participation in the survey to institutions, societies, and associations related to developmental disorders (Korean Parents’ Network for People with Disabilities, Korean Society of Pediatric Rehabilitation and Developmental Medicine, Seoul National University Hospital Autism and Developmental Disorder Center, etc.). The survey was conducted in a remote online survey format.

Survey

The survey content structure was different for each subject. The survey targeting caregivers of infants and toddlers with developmental disorders included items such as basic information on the respondents, perinatal information, medical history, the intervention route, experience with national health screening programs for infants and children, treatment service experience, burdens related to medical expenses, and secondary difficulties related to infants and toddlers with developmental disorders. The survey targeting medical doctors included items including basic information on the respondents, the evaluation of developmental disorders, treatment status, etc. The main findings of the survey targeting caregivers are described in Supplementary Table 1.

Ethics statement

The survey was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the National Evidence-Based Healthcare Collaborating Agency (NECA) before it was conducted (IRB No. NECA IRB23-012). Informed consent was submitted by all subjects when they were enrolled.

Sex and age distributions of the infants and children

An analysis of a total of 105 infants and children in the survey of caregivers of children with developmental disorders was conducted. The participants’ majority were male (n = 72, 68.6%) and female children consisted proportion of n = 33, 31.4%. In terms of the age distribution, the highest proportion observed consisted of children aged “5 to under 6 years” (n = 45, 42.9%), followed by those aged “4 to under 5 years” (n = 29, 27.6%) and “3 to under 4 years” (21.9%). Utilization status by treatment area

Therapy session usage patterns indicated that most therapies are conducted for 30 to 60 minutes per session, with a frequency of one to three times per week. Notably, ABA had an average session duration of 74.5 minutes and to be administered an average of 2.5 times per week, making it longer and more frequent than other therapy types.

The cost per therapy session for most services ranged from approximately 45,000 KRW to approximately 65,000 KRW. However, the average cost per session for ABA was 140,878 KRW, which was more than twice as high as the average cost for other therapies.

We examined the proportion of users applying vouchers by treatment area. Speech therapy was found to have the highest voucher application rate at 44.7%, followed by psychotherapy, including play therapy (36.7%), physical therapy (34.8%), and sensory therapy (30.9%). Despite the high treatment cost of ABA, the voucher application rate was very low at only 2.4%. Similarly, cognitive (learning) therapy had a low voucher application rate of 5.3%. In the medical doctor survey, 71.2% of the respondents answered that there was a difference between the treatment recommended as effective and the treatment that was actually used. They answered in few lines, and 20 participants cited cost as the reason, whereas 20 cited accessibility as the reason. Separate from individualized medical evaluations for each child, treatment is often determined on the basis of the types and durations of therapies available at private centers that accept indemnity insurance. In addition, treatment facilities are frequently concentrated in major metropolitan areas. Also, a shortage of experienced and skilled personnel often results in long waiting periods before therapy can begin.

In the treatment specialists’ survey, 29.4% of the respondents (n = 15) reported that there were treatment programs or techniques that they wished to implement but were unable to do so. The programs/techniques that could not be applied (multiple responses allowed) included the Early Start Denver Model and floor time, among others. The most commonly cited reason for not being able to implement these approaches was the “lack of domestic application cases” (59.1%), followed by “incompatibility with treatment duration or service fees” (54.5%) and the “absence of training opportunities such as workshops or educational programs” (45.5%) (Fig. 4).

Voucher application

Fig. 5 shows that 65.1% of the treatment service users reported applying vouchers. Regarding the hourly service rates supported by vouchers, the majority of voucher recipients expressed dissatisfaction (80.4%), and all of them responded that the support amount should be increased (100.0%).

The hourly service rate for voucher-supported services.

When asked about the appropriate hourly service rate for increased support, the most common response was 50,000–60,000 KRW (44.4%), followed by 60,000–70,000 KRW (20.0%) and 40,000–50,000 KRW (17.8%). The average hourly service rate considered appropriate for voucher support was 56,333 KRW.

In the medical doctor survey, 15.0% of the respondents indicated that their operating developmental centers provide treatments covered by vouchers. Among those who do not offer voucher-eligible services (multiple responses allowed), the reasons included the complexity of the voucher designation/registration process (5 responses), inadequate reimbursement rates, ineligibility for voucher application (e.g., medical institutions or clinics), administrative burdens related to documentation and audit preparation (4 responses each), the reluctance of therapists (2 responses), and the intention to hire highly qualified personnel (1 response).

In the treatment specialist survey (Fig. 6), only 9.8% of the respondents agreed (including “strongly agree” and “agree”) that the service fee after voucher application was reasonable. When converted to a 5-point Likert scale, the perceived reasonableness of the service fee following voucher application yielded an average score of 1.98.

With respect to the experience of using the developmental screening tests in the “National Health Screening Program for Infants and Children,” more than half of the respondents (69.5%) reported having used the service, whereas 30.5% indicated that they had not. DISCUSSION

According to the findings of this study, 83.8% of caregivers of young children with developmental disorders reported experiencing financial burdens due to developmental issues, and 72.4% of caregivers indicated that these costs are significantly burdensome. Dissatisfaction with the current “Developmental Rehabilitation Voucher Program,” which provides 30,000 KRW per session for a maximum of eight sessions per month (twice per week), was reported by all respondents (100%), indicating a strong demand for program expansion. The most preferred service model includes a per-session cost of 50,000–60,000 KRW and an increased frequency of 12 sessions per month (three times per week), highlighting the urgent need to expand early intervention services.

Currently, most treatments for developmental disorders fall outside the coverage of the national health insurance system, placing the financial burden primarily on caregivers. One major contributor to the high financial burden is the cost of ABA, which is a treatment recognized as one of the most effective interventions for ASD. In South Korea, the monthly cost of ABA therapy ranges from 3 million to 6 million KRW, the entirety of which must be covered by caregivers. Accordingly, children with developmental disorders in South Korea receive treatment according to cost rather than the most appropriate treatment considered by doctors because of the limitation of treatment costs.

In the case of the United States, a 2013 study on the expenditures of individuals with ASD and intellectual disabilities revealed that younger age groups incurred higher costs. Most of these expenses were attributed to rehabilitation and education, including early intervention, as well as challenges related to parental employment and productivity loss.19,20,21 Parents and families of individuals with disabilities covered the majority of these costs. In response, state governments across the U.S. implemented policy changes to mandate insurance coverage for ASD-related services and reduce the financial burden. From 2001 to 2017, 46 states passed legislation mandating insurance coverage for evidence-based ASD treatments such as ABA therapy.20 In Massachusetts, An Act Relative to Insurance Coverage for Autism, signed into law in 2010, mandated that private insurance cover essential services for ASD diagnosis and treatment, including ABA therapy, mental health treatments, and social skills groups. Quantitative studies have shown that families residing in states with autism insurance mandates experienced lower out-of-pocket expenses than did those in states without such mandates.22

Unlike in the U.S., in South Korea, families of individuals with developmental disorders must bear the full treatment costs. Low-income families and families with children under 18 can access rehabilitation voucher support. However, they often encounter limitations, including strict eligibility criteria, a lack of treatment institutions, limited government funding, and reduced treatment quality. As a result, the financial and socioeconomic burdens on families remain substantial. Furthermore, one of the evidence-based treatments for developmental disorders, namely, ABA, which has previously been shown to be effective, must be conducted for at least 20 hours per week over a minimum of 2 years to yield meaningful results.23

However, specialized ABA treatment centers are limited in South Korea. In South Korea, approximately 300 individuals hold U.S.-certified behavior analyst credentials, including BCBA-D, BCBA, and BCaBA, with an even smaller number actively providing therapy. Until two to three years ago, the certification exam could be taken domestically. However, it is now available only in the U.S. and is administered in English, making it significantly more difficult to obtain BCBA certification in South Korea. There are alternative credentials, such as QBA and the locally recognized Korean Behavior Analyst, but their availability remains insufficient compared with the number of ASD patients requiring treatment. Moreover, most of these therapies are not covered by insurance, and in the case of ABA therapy, the combination of high demand and limited supply makes it nearly impossible for most families to afford the necessary number of treatment sessions.24 ABA treatment costs range from 6,000 to 15,000 KRW per session, leading to monthly treatment costs of 3 to 6 million KRW.25

In contrast, in the U.S., most states have implemented mandates requiring insurance companies to cover ASD-related services. Under the U.S. system, therapeutic services for ASD, including speech therapy, occupational therapy, behavioral therapy (including ABA), and play therapy, are provided intensively, i.e., five days a week for a minimum of 25 hours per week, with each session lasting 90 minutes. This amount translates to approximately 16.6 sessions per week or 66.4 sessions per month. Drawing from the U.S. experience, South Korea must urgently implement effective early intervention strategies and establish national financial support mechanisms, including insurance coverage for ASD treatment.

This study also shows that in South Korea, early childhood screenings are not mandatory, resulting in a significant proportion of caregivers opting not to participate in these assessments. Furthermore, even when expert recommendations for follow-up evaluations are made during these screenings, there is often a delay in obtaining further diagnostic tests, evaluations, and subsequent treatment, leading to gaps in care of over one year. This research suggests that as it becomes easier to identify ASD, adherence to follow-up screenings in early childhood tends to decrease.

In Japan, under the Maternal and Child Health Act enacted in 1965, local governments administer two mandatory health checkups at 18 months and 3 years of age. These screenings are conducted free of charge and involve professional evaluations rather than simple questionnaires, with a participation rate of approximately 92–95%. If a pediatrician determines that a child requires further examination, a medical referral is issued, allowing the child to receive free in-depth diagnostic assessments at designated medical institutions as many times as necessary.

In Japan, children suspected of having developmental disorders can access support services without requiring a formal medical diagnosis. While medical diagnoses are conducted in hospitals, evaluations for service eligibility are performed at public health centers and child counseling centers, with decisions made by evaluation committees. All children with disabilities are eligible for special education support, provided that they submit a medical certificate or official documentation verifying the need for additional support, regardless of the issuing institution.

In the U.S., children suspected of having developmental disorders, including ASD, are identified through Well-Child Visits, which serve a function similar to South Korea’s “National Health Screening Program for Infant and Children.” ASD-specific screening tests, such as the Modified Checklist for Autism in Toddlers, Revised, are administered twice, at 18 and 24 months. If a child is considered at risk for ASD, he or she is referred to an Individuals with Disabilities Education Act (IDEA) early intervention center, where the child can undergo comprehensive diagnostic assessments. For children under 3 years of age, early intervention centers provide free diagnostic and evaluation services funded by the IDEA. Additionally, some states offer financial assistance for diagnostic evaluations. For example, in Colorado, children under 5 years of age may receive state-funded support covering diagnostic costs of $2,000–$5,000 (2.6–6.5 million KRW). Under Part C of IDEA, once a child is identified as having a potential developmental delay or disability, the referral process must be completed within seven days.

In the United States, once a child is confirmed to be eligible for early intervention services, an individualized family service plan is developed in collaboration with caregivers. This plan outlines the medical, therapeutic, and social services that the child and his or her family will receive. Post-diagnosis treatment services are also publicly funded, allowing children under 3 years of age to receive a minimum of 25 hours per week of intensive therapy, with each session lasting 90 minutes, either for free or at a reduced cost.

For children aged 3 to 5 years, continued special education and therapy services are provided under Part B of IDEA. These services are often delivered in developmental preschools, which are specialized early education programs for children with disabilities. All diagnosed children are eligible for free enrollment, with a maximum service provision of 60 hours per month. The range of services available is consistent with those provided under Part C early intervention programs.

The significance of this study lies in its comprehensive investigation, which involved a multifaceted survey incorporating the perspectives of experts, caregivers of patients, and treatment facility institutions. By examining both beneficiaries and service providers from a policy perspective, the current study obtains findings that can serve as foundational data for developing policy measures.

However, this study has the following limitations. First, while government financial support is proposed as a key policy measure, there are limitations in accurately estimating the financial burden of the target population. The survey in this study relied on self-reported responses, thus making it difficult to derive precise financial estimates. The literature suggested that if data from private centers were included in the analysis, the estimated costs would be significantly higher. Therefore, it is essential to conduct comprehensive field research to accurately assess the financial burden on patients and caregivers and to utilize these data to establish reimbursement rates and policy support measures.

Another limitation of this study is that caregiver reports were based on self-reports. In addition, the small sample size and recruitment through treatment centers may indicate that the participants were more economically advantaged, thus limiting the representativeness of the sample. A large proportion of participants in this study were recruited through treatment institutions, indicating that they were already receiving therapy. This suggests that participants may have had relatively higher income levels. In fact, 40.0% of caregivers in our study reported a monthly household income of over 6 million KRW, which is significantly higher than the national average monthly income of 2.73 million KRW for households with individuals with disabilities. Since respondents were recruited primarily from treatment sites, they were concentrated in the high-income group. Those who can receive treatment are relatively high-income groups, while those who do not are relatively excluded from treatment itself. Despite belonging to the high-income group, the fact that 72.4% of all caregivers complained of household burden suggests a strong need for generalizing estimates of national-aid costs in treatment and should be supplemented through in-depth and diverse national studies.

Government statistical data on the economic burden of individuals with developmental disorders, including the distribution of treatment costs by funding source, are lacking. Considering the substantial socioeconomic burden per individual with developmental disorders and the immense pain imposed on their family, it is imperative to conduct extensive follow-up research to collect comprehensive statistical data and assess the current landscape. Evidence-based policy development, grounded in objective data, is urgently needed to effectively address these challenges.
