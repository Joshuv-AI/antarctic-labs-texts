Can a New, Satellite Inpatient Hospital Maintain the Efficiency of an Ambulatory Surgery Center with No Difference in Patient-reported Outcomes? A Prospective Study☆
Authors: Vineet M Desai, Christopher J DeFrancesco, Morgan E Swanson, Divya Talwar, Alexandra Stevens, Nathan Chaclas, David P VanEenenaam, Kevin M Landrum, Akbar N Syed, Brendan A Williams, Theodore J Ganley
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPrevious research has found that anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) reconstructions performed at ambulatory surgery centers (ASCs) are more time-efficient. However, recent literature investigating this phenomenon within the pediatric population is limited and primarily retrospective. The goal of this study was to prospectively compare the operating room (OR) efficiency of pediatric ACL reconstructions performed across different surgical settings.MethodsAdolescent patients (12–18 years) undergoing ACL reconstruction by a single surgeon were prospectively enrolled from 2020 to 2024. Patients were cohorted based on the location of their surgery at one of three sites: 1) a traditional, tertiary care academic hospital, 2) hospital-owned ASCs, or 3) a satellite inpatient hospital that was built and connected to an ASC in January 2022. Patient preoperative, surgical, and postoperative data were collected, and surgical efficiency was compared across surgical sites using a variety of metrics. Patient-reported outcomes (PROMs) were collected using the Pedi-FABS and Pediatric International Knee Documentation Committee questionnaires.ResultsA total of 115 patients (56.5% female, mean age: 15.4 ​± ​1.7 years) met inclusion criteria and were enrolled. Thirty-one patients (27.0%) had ACL reconstructions at the main inpatient hospital, 36 (31.3%) at hospital-owned ASCs, and 48 (41.7%) at the new satellite inpatient hospital. When controlling for concomitant procedures, regional anesthesia type, and staff, there was a significant difference in total OR in-room, anesthesia induction, surgery preparation, surgery duration, and dressing application/wake-up times among the three cohorts (P ​< ​.001). Both the ASCs and the satellite inpatient hospital had shorter total OR in-room and surgery duration times than the main inpatient hospital (P ​< ​.001). There were no differences in PROMs among the three cohorts (P ​> ​.05).ConclusionsAdolescent ACL reconstructions performed at either an ASC or a satellite inpatient hospital were more time-efficient than those performed at a traditional, main inpatient hospital, with no differences in PROMs. Institutions should consider implementing strategies used at ASCs, such as standardized surgical teams, at main hospitals to increase efficiency.Key Concepts(1)A satellite inpatient hospital and ambulatory surgery centers are more time-efficient for adolescent ACL reconstruction than a main, inpatient hospital.(2)There were no differences in patient-reported functional outcomes after adolescent ACL reconstruction among ASCs, a main inpatient hospital, and a satellite inpatient hospital.(3)A satellite inpatient hospital can be as efficient as an ASC, with no significant difference in quality.Level of EvidenceII (Prospective Cohort Study)Keywords: Ambulatory surgery center, Anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction, Adolescent, Efficiency

Introduction

The incidence of anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) injuries among the pediatric and adolescent population has been increasing in the last few decades, with an incidence of almost seven injuries per every 1000 ​h of sports participation [1]. In lieu of traditional, large inpatient hospitals, these surgeries are increasingly performed at ambulatory surgical centers (ASCs), a trend likely due to lower costs, increased patient convenience, and faster surgical times associated with ASCs [2,3]. Several studies have also found that ASCs provide similar quality care to inpatient hospitals [4,5].

Although many studies have analyzed the financial advantages of ASCs, fewer have investigated what many physicians and other healthcare workers claim are a large advantage of ASCs—increased surgical and operational efficiency [6]. While much of the previous research has investigated physician-owned ASCs, where physician financial incentives may influence utilization and operational efficiency, research by Kadhim et al. [7] showed that hospital-owned ASCs were also more efficient than an affiliated main university hospital.

The goal of this study was to prospectively compare whether ACL reconstructions differed in time efficiency among a traditional university-affiliated pediatric hospital, its hospital-owned ASCs, and a new satellite hospital built and connected to an ASC. We hypothesized that both the ASCs and the satellite inpatient hospital would be more time-efficient than the main inpatient hospital. We also secondarily compared the functional outcomes after ACL reconstruction among these three cohorts.

Materials and methods

A prospective, comparative cohort study was conducted at a tertiary referral children's health system from 2020 to 2024, including its flagship academic hospital, three hospital-owned ASCs, and a new, satellite inpatient hospital that was built and connected to a hospital-owned ASC in 2022. Institutional review board approval was obtained.

Subject recruitment

Patients aged 12–18 years (age: <19 years) at the time of ACL reconstruction surgery were eligible to be enrolled. All ACL reconstructions were performed by a single pediatric sports medicine surgeon with fellowship training in both pediatric orthopaedics and sports medicine. Only English-speaking patients were enrolled due to the feasibility of translating surveys. Informed consent from the patient or legal guardian (depending on patient age) was obtained preoperatively at the time of enrollment. Patients who had a history of previous ipsilateral knee surgery or required multiple ligament reconstructions other than lateral extra-articular tenodesis (LET) were excluded.

Study setting

This study was initially designed to be conducted at both a traditional, inpatient university hospital and its three hospital-owned ASCs. However, on January 1st, 2022, a new satellite inpatient hospital was built and connected to one of the initial ASCs. Thus, patients undergoing ACL reconstruction at this ASC site before January 1st, 2022, were included in the ASC cohort, and those enrolled after January 1st, 2022 were included in a third cohort labeled satellite inpatient hospital. Both the main inpatient hospital and satellite inpatient hospital did not have body mass index (BMI) restrictions for admission. The ASCs had a BMI limit of 40 for admission; however, no patient enrolled met this BMI limit. Physicians had no ownership stake in any of the surgical centers included. Of note, patients were scheduled at each of the sites for their ACL reconstruction based on both family preference and convenience, as well as surgeon availability.

Data collection

Preoperative data were collected using both the electronic medical record (EMR) and baseline patient questionnaires recorded in Research Electronic Data Capture (Vanderbilt University). EMR variables collected included demographics (sex, BMI, race, age at injury, insurance status), injury characteristics (mechanism, laterality, pain, and/or instability), physical exam findings (observation, palpation, range of motion, Lachman test, etc.), and imaging findings (concomitant injuries, physeal status). Patient questionnaires yielded baseline patient reported outcomes measurements (PROMs) using both the Pediatric Functional Above Baseline Scale (Pedi-FABS) score [8] and Pediatric International Knee Documentation Committee (Pedi-IKDC) score [9]. Baseline pain scores were recorded using the numeric rating scale (NRS) [10].

Operative data collected included type of fixation, type and source of graft, concomitant procedures, type of regional anesthesia, and the type of operative assistant (resident versus fellow/physician assistant [PA]). Fellows and surgical PAs were grouped together because at our institution, both have significant experience in assistant with the ACL reconstruction procedure. These assistants, in our experience, are able to work in parallel with the attending surgeon with a heightened level of autonomy. In our institution, both the fellow and the same surgical PAs work consistently with the single pediatric orthopaedic sports surgeon to perform ACL reconstructions. Perioperative time segments were also recorded from the perioperative record in the EMR as follows: 1) total in-room time was the difference between in-room time and out-of-room time, 2) anesthesia induction time was the difference between in-room time and "anesthesia-ready” time, 3) surgery prep time was the difference between "anesthesia-ready” time and incision time, 4) surgery duration was the difference between incision time and “procedure-closed” time, 5) dressing/wake-up time was the difference between “procedure-closed” time and out-of-room time, and 6) operating room (OR) turnover time was the time between out-of-room time for the case at hand and in-room time for any following scheduled sports surgery.

Postoperative variables collected included pain scores (NRS) at post-op days one, three, and seven, as well as Pedi-FABS and Pedi-IKDC scores at three, six, nine, and 12 months postoperatively. Follow-up visits were scheduled at approximately 1 week, 6 weeks, 3 months, 6 months, 9 months, 1 year, and 2 years postoperatively. Beginning at the 3-month postoperative visit, physical exam findings, and PROMs (Pedi-FABS and Pedi-IKDC) were recorded during each follow-up visit. Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics and perioperative variables were compared among the cohorts. Pearson's Chi-squared tests for independence and Fisher's exact tests were used to compare categorical variables, whereas two-sample independent t-tests and Mann–Whitney U tests were used for pairwise comparison of continuous variables, and analysis of variance and Kruskal–Willis tests were used to compare continuous variables across >2 groups. Multiple linear regression was used to investigate the impact of various independent variables on the total operative time while controlling for potential confounding variables. Statistical significance was set at P ​< ​.05. Analyses were performed using JMP Pro v17.0.0 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC).

Results

Baseline patient and injury characteristics

From January 2020 until March 2024, 137 patients were enrolled. Twenty-two patients were excluded due to surgery cancellation, insufficient survey response, and age (Fig. 2).

Flow diagram of study enrollment.

Thus, the final cohort included 115 patients: 31 patients (27.0%) at the main inpatient hospital, 36 (31.3%) at ASCs, and 48 (41.7%) at the new satellite inpatient hospital. A majority of patients were female (65 patients, 56.5%), with a mean age of 15.5 years (range: 12.0–18.9). The mean BMI was 23.2 (standard deviation: 4.2). The racial distribution of patients was different between the cohorts (P ​= ​.02), with the main hospital having the highest proportion of African American patients. The main hospital cohort also had the highest proportion of patients with public insurance (P ​= ​.047). No other differences among the three cohorts were found in regard to baseline sex, age, race, mechanism of injury, skeletal maturity, preoperative symptoms, and additional injuries identified on preoperative imaging (Table 1).

Operative characteristics

The median time from injury to surgery was 1.6 months (interquartile range: 1.1–2.5). Forty-four patients (38.3%) underwent concurrent meniscal repair, 13 patients (11.3%) underwent concurrent partial meniscectomy, and 17 patients (14.7%) had concomitant LET. Quadriceps tendon was the most common graft source (93.9%). There were no differences among the three cohorts regarding time from injury to surgery and concurrent LET surgery performed (Table 2). While all the cases at the ASCs and satellite inpatient hospital used fellows/surgical PAs as the operative assistant, 48% of cases at the main hospital used a resident. The satellite inpatient hospital had more concurrent meniscal repairs and partial meniscectomies than either the main inpatient hospital or the ASC cohort (P ​< ​.05). Although the main and satellite inpatient hospitals did not differ in the type of regional anesthesia used, both utilized more continuous peripheral nerve catheters and fewer single-shot nerve blocks than the ASC cohort (P ​< ​.05).

Time efficiency

The total in-room time, anesthesia induction time, surgery prep time, surgery duration, dressing/wake-up time, and OR turnover time were different across the three sites (P ​< ​.01) (Table 3). The main inpatient hospital had longer total OR in-room and surgery duration times than both the ASC cohort and the satellite inpatient hospital (P ​< ​.001). There was no difference in total in-room time (P ​= ​.54) and surgery duration (P ​= ​.76) between the ASC and satellite inpatient hospital cohorts. However, when controlling for BMI, operative assistant, graft source, concurrent meniscal/LET surgeries, and regional anesthesia type (for total in-room time only), the satellite inpatient hospital had a shorter total in-room time (P ​= ​.03) and surgery duration (P ​= ​.03) than the ASC cohort.

Discussion

Given the rising cost of health care, orthopaedic surgeons are increasingly utilizing ASCs for common outpatient procedures [11]. ASCs can not only increase cost savings but can also reduce hospital stay and postoperative complications [11]. In a previous retrospective analysis, Kadhim et al. [7] found that performing ACL reconstructions at hospital-owned ASCs could improve both surgical and workday efficiency. The primary goal of this study was to prospectively compare the OR time efficiency of ACL reconstructions at three sites: a main, university inpatient hospital, hospital-owned ASCs, and a satellite inpatient hospital. The secondary goal of this study was to prospectively compare the functional outcomes after ACL reconstruction across the three cohorts. Our findings confirmed our initial hypothesis that performing adolescent ACL reconstruction at either an ASC or a satellite inpatient hospital is more time-efficient than at a traditional, main inpatient hospital with no difference in PROMs via the Pedi-FABS and Pedi-IKDC validated questionnaires.

Our study found that the total in-room, surgery duration, and anesthesia induction times were longer at the main inpatient hospital than in both the satellite inpatient hospital and ASCs. OR turnover time between cases was also higher at the main hospital. Previous research has similarly investigated the potential benefits of performing common, outpatient surgeries at ASCs in lieu of traditional large, inpatient hospitals. Rider et al. [12] conducted a retrospective analysis of supracondylar humeral fractures performed in a traditional hospital, its outpatient department, and a freestanding ASC. They found that both surgical and total in-facility times decreased significantly at the ASC, with no differences in complication rates [12]. While the results of Rider et al. [12] are similar to ours, it is important to recognize the limitation that the ASC used in their study was freestanding with physician ownership. Patrick et al. [13] performed a retrospective study comparing the efficiency of pediatric ACL reconstructions between a hospital-owned ASC and the main, inpatient hospital. They found that ACL reconstructions performed at an ASC were more efficient, saving over 30 ​min of OR time with less staff [13]. The mean age of patients in their study was 15.9 years, similar to our study's cohort [13].

Our study secondarily found that there were no differences in subjective patient-reported functional outcomes among patients receiving their surgery at the three sites. De Beule et al. [14] found that performing ACL reconstructions in an outpatient setting is safe, with no differences in complication or readmission rates in comparison to patients receiving ACL reconstruction in traditional, inpatient settings. Our study's findings possibly suggest that ACL reconstructions at ASCs or satellite sites can have overall greater OR time efficiency without significant differences in functional outcomes postoperatively than at a traditional, inpatient hospital.

One unique aspect of the current study was the inclusion of the third cohort, the satellite inpatient hospital. This site was built as a satellite inpatient hospital amidst prospective study enrollment. Interestingly, even though the third cohort is an inpatient hospital, its time efficiency for ACL reconstruction was closer to that of the ASC cohort. The satellite inpatient hospital had shorter total in-room time, anesthesia induction time, surgery preparatory time, surgery duration, and dressing/wake-up time than the main inpatient hospital. In multivariable analysis, the satellite inpatient hospital had shorter total in-room, surgery preparatory, and surgical duration times than the ASC cohort, and there was no difference in dressing/wake-up and OR turnover times. Although almost half the cases in the main inpatient hospital used residents, when controlling for operative assistant, the satellite inpatient hospital remained more time efficient.

One possible explanation for this satellite inpatient hospital displaying time efficiency closer to that of an ASC is its utilization of consistent surgical teams with little turnover. While the ASC and satellite inpatient hospital cohorts typically use standardized surgical teams, the main hospital does not often regularly use them. For example, the standardized surgical teams involve the same scrub techs, nursing teams, and operative assistants that accompany the pediatric sports surgeon for the same case—the ACL reconstruction. Standardized teams, which likely have more familiarity and more clearly established roles, coupled with an increased rate of surgical PAs/fellows as operative assistants, likely led to the satellite inpatient hospital and ASC cohorts having a lower ACL reconstruction surgical duration than in the main inpatient hospital. Stepaniak et al. [15] found that when similar cases are booked consecutively and the same surgical teams are utilized—which is common in ASCs—there are significant decreases in OR preparation and turnover times. In both the ASCs and the satellite inpatient hospital in our study, ACL reconstructions are often booked consecutively with the utilization of the same surgical team with the goal of maximizing efficiency when completing each day's case list. Merrill et al. [16] found not only that ASCs use standardization of both resources and staff to increase efficiency and decrease costs but also that staff at ASCs are internally driven to deliver excellent patient care in a time-efficient manner. Often, there are salary or time-off benefits tied to operational efficiency [16]. Thus, in the satellite inpatient hospital in our study that began as an ASC, it is possible that the staff and resource standardization practices and internal motivation carried over when the hospital reopened as an inpatient hospital. Another factor that could help explain the difference in time efficiency, especially with anesthesia induction time and in-room time, is the presence of anesthesia trainees and/or nursing/scrub tech trainees. Although we did not prospectively collect data on the presence of these trainees, they could have potentially contributed to the lower time efficiency at the main hospital.

Interestingly, our study found a significant difference in the rate of concomitant meniscal repairs across the three sites, with the satellite inpatient hospital have a significantly greater rate of meniscal repair performed. We believe that given the limited sample size of this study, this finding was most likely a result of statistical chance, given that the satellite inpatient hospital also had the greatest cohort size. Additionally, our study found that the insurance status of patients differed significantly across the three sites. There was a greater proportion of private insurance patients at both the satellite inpatient hospital and ASC sites than in the main hospital. This is likely due to the underlying locations of the sites, where private insurance patients may more likely prefer to have their surgeries done in suburban locations with increased location convenience. The difference in insurance status may have also indirectly influenced the efficiency of the ACL reconstruction. For example, it is possible that insurance status is correlated with degree of medical literacy, which could influence anesthesia induction time due to patient questions and other factors.

Our study also compared the time efficiency of residents versus fellows/PAs at the main hospital. This particular comparison was done because residents generally do not cover cases at the ASCs or satellite hospital inside our health system. We found that cases with fellow/PA operative assistants had lower total in-room and surgical times; however, this difference was not statistically significant. This was potentially due to the comparison being underpowered to detect a difference. Parameswaran et al. [17] found that while the trainee level affected ACL reconstruction time at ASCs, cases with fellows had the longest surgical times. Recognizing that surgical education is appropriately prioritized at teaching institutions, our study can help continue to inform conversations regarding staffing and efficiency. It is important to acknowledge that we chose to include the surgical PAs/fellows in one assistant group, as at our institution, the surgical PAs consistently operate with the pediatric sports surgeon at a level of autonomy and overall role that parallels the clinical fellows. However, it is also important to recognize that not all community practices or institutions will have surgical PAs who work with pediatric surgeons in a similar role and level of autonomy. Thus, it is important to recognize this limitation in generalizability when utilizing the results of this study in the context of other institutions.

There are several limitations to our study. First, the cohorts were limited in sample size, limiting the power of the analyses and findings in the current study. Second, the satellite inpatient hospital was built and connected to one of the ASCs in January 2022. In order to continue enrollment and mitigate bias, we included patients enrolled at this site after this date in a separate cohort. Third, all ACL reconstructions were performed by a single surgeon, potentially limiting generalizability. However, this also standardized the surgical protocol and eliminated other potential confounding factors influencing time efficiency. Additionally, patients were not randomized to each of the three sites and were rather scheduled based on a combination of family convenience and preference as well as surgeon availability. This likely contributed to the differences in meniscal procedures, race, and insurance status among the cohorts. Fourth, our prospective study did not collect data on the anesthesia assists present (residents, CRNA, etc.). This could have influenced the wake-up time measure of ACL efficiency. Future studies on this area should furthermore analyze how not only orthopaedic trainees but also anesthesiology trainees could influence time efficiency. Lastly, the variables included in our prospective study do not capture the full spectrum of operational efficiency and patient-reported outcomes involved. Thus, other unknown variables may have influenced the results of our study.

Nevertheless, this study prospectively compared both the time efficiency of adolescent ACL reconstruction and functional outcomes via the Pedi-FABS and Pedi-IKDC questionnaires. Furthermore, to our knowledge this is the first study to investigate time efficiency at a new satellite inpatient hospital that was initially an ASC.

Overall, this study found that ACL reconstructions performed at either ASCs or a satellite inpatient hospital can be more time efficient than those performed at traditional, inpatient hospitals with no differences in patient reported functional outcomes. These results shed light on how some of the advantages of ASCs, such as team standardization, can potentially be implemented in the inpatient setting in order to make inpatient surgery more efficient with minimal differences in quality.
