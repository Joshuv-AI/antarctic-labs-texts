CXCL10 Regulates Pressure Overload–Induced Heart Failure
Authors: Fernando Souza-Neto, Preston Le, Mobeen Abdrabbo, Ashab Alamgir, Haiguang Wang, Natalia Ferreira de Araujo, Jennifer L Mikkila, Bruno Sanches, Preethy Parthiban, Ryan Moon, Adam Herman, Andrew B Adams, Kurt W Prins, Silvia Guatimosim, Nathan Schuldt, Xavier S Revelo, Jop H van Berlo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Visual AbstractKey Words: cardiac dysfunction, cardiac remodeling, fibrosis, hypertrophy, inflammation, macrophages, T cells

Heart failure (HF) is one of the leading causes of morbidity and mortality in the United States as well globally.1, 2, 3, 4 It is now well established that immune cells contribute to the development, progression, and exacerbation of HF.5, 6, 7 Although the involvement of cardiac leukocytes has been studied,8, 9, 10 there are many unknowns regarding their exact role. Specifically, the role of macrophages and T cells have both been established independently,11 but the exact mechanisms of their involvement in the development of HF are not known.

Recently, it has been demonstrated that the chemokine receptor C-X-C motif chemokine receptor 3 (CXCR3), which is expressed by T-cells,12, 13, 14 is involved in the development of hypertrophy, fibrosis, and cardiac dysfunction in a pressure overload–induced HF model following transverse aortic constriction (TAC) surgery in mice.15 Genetic deletion of CXCR3 resulted in attenuated hypertrophy, fibrosis, and cardiac dysfunction with reduced T-cell infiltration in cardiac tissue in response to cardiac pressure overload. Separately, it was shown that cluster of differentiation 4–positive (CD4+) T cells, but not CD8+ T cells, are crucial for the transition from compensated hypertrophy to HF after TAC.16 Although the involvement of the CXCR3 receptor in the development of HF is clear from these results, it is still unknown which of the receptor's ligands are effectively causing this response in vivo. The CXCR3 receptor is a 7-transmembrane domain metabotropic receptor that can be activated by the ligands C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 9 (CXCL9), CXCL10, and CXCL11, although only CXCL9 and CXCL10 are functional in C57Bl/6 mice.17, 18, 19, 20

In the present study, we analyzed the contribution of CXCL10 to the development of HF induced by TAC in mice. Our study shows several key findings: wild-type (WT) animals show an increased cardiac expression of CXCL10 in the early phase of TAC-induced cardiac remodeling; and genetic deletion of CXCL10 is associated with improved early cardiac remodeling and delayed onset of HF. Finally, we observed that animals receiving bone marrow cells from CXCL10 knockout (KO) animals present attenuation in the pathologic changes in response to pressure overload.

Methods

Ethical approval

Animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the institutional guidelines for research and animal ethics at the University of Minnesota. All experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at the University of Minnesota. All data underlying the results presented here are available upon reasonable request to the corresponding author.

Mice

WT mice (C57BL/6), CXCL10–/–, CCR2–/–, and Lutz.6 (CD45.1), 8 to 12 weeks of age and of both sexes were obtained from the Jackson Laboratory. The animals were fed a standard rodent diet and maintained under standard housing conditions in individually ventilated cages with in-line water supply.

TAC surgery

TAC surgery was performed in mice as described previously.21 The animals were anesthetized with isoflurane (2% to 3%) by inhalation and received long-acting buprenorphine (1 mg/kg) subcutaneously for analgesia. When a sufficient anesthetic plan was confirmed, the mice were positioned in supine position and the thorax was disinfected. After this, a longitudinal skin incision was made, exposing the trachea to facilitate intubation. When intubation was confirmed, the mouse was connected to a ventilator, and the mediastinum was opened via left parasternal incision. The transverse aorta was identified, and a blunt 27-gauge needle was positioned on top of the aorta between the left carotid artery and the innominate artery. A 6-0 silk suture passed around the needle and aorta and tied tightly, promoting the constriction. The needle was removed and the mediastinum was closed with suture and the skin with suture and tissue glue. Mice were transferred to clean cages and monitored until recovery from anesthesia, with additional analgesia administered as needed. The control (sham) groups underwent the same procedure, but without aortic constriction.

Echocardiography

Echocardiography was performed in mice to assess cardiac function and dimensions using M-mode imaging for quantification. Mice were anesthetized with isoflurane (1% to 2%) and placed in supine position on a platform heated with their paws restrained. The chest area was shaved using depilatory cream and ultrasound gel was applied. Using a 30-MHz transducer on a VEVO2100 ultrasound system, B-mode imaging captured short-axis parasternal views to identify cardiac structures. M-mode imaging was then performed with the cursor positioned perpendicular to the interventricular septum and the posterior wall of the left ventricle at the level of the papillary muscle. All measurements were analyzed with VEVO software.

Proteome profiler analysis

Cytokine levels were assessed using the Proteome Profiler Mouse Cytokine Array Kit (R&D Systems, #ARY006), following the manufacturer’s instructions. Membranes were blocked and incubated with prepared samples on a rocking platform shaker. After incubation with the cytokine detection antibody cocktail and streptavidin with horseradish peroxidase, membranes were treated with chemiluminescent reagents and developed using a Bio-Rad ChemiDoc MP imaging system. Pixel densities were quantified using image analysis software. Signals were averaged from duplicate spots for CXCL10.

Cross-sectional area analysis

Hearts were arrested in diastole by perfusion with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) containing KCl (60 mM) and embedded in optimal cutting temperature (Fabric-tek). Cryosections were mounted on Fisherbrand Superfrost Plus glass slides (Fisher Scientific). Slides were initially fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) and blocked with 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in PBS and stained with wheat germ agglutinin (WGA). The slides were photographed with a Keyence or Zeiss Axio Imager M1. The cross-sectional area of cardiomyocytes was quantified from images obtained with a 40× objective, where each heart was represented by 12 images of the left ventricle from all areas of the cross-sectioned ventricle. At least 100 cardiomyocytes were traced from each animal using ImageJ. Representative images were selected based on average results per group.

Tissue immunohistochemistry

Immunohistochemistry was performed on frozen cardiac tissue sections. Slides were fixed in 4% PFA and washed with PBS. Sections were then blocked with 3% BSA containing 0.3% Triton X-100 and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies diluted in blocking solution. The following antibodies were used: CD163 (ThermoFisher, #14-1631-82) and CD68 (Cell Signaling, #97778S) to identify macrophages, and Vimentin (Abcam, #ab8978) and α–smooth muscle actin (α-SMA) (Abcam, #ab21027) to label fibroblasts and myofibroblasts. In macrophage staining, WGA was used to visualize areas of extracellular matrix accumulation, and 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) was used for nuclear staining. After washing, slides were incubated for 1 hour at room temperature with secondary antibodies, washed again with PBS, and mounted with fluorescence mounting medium. For mediastinal lymph node sections, frozen samples were stained for CD3 (eBioscience, #14-0032-82) and Ki-67 (Cell Signaling, #9129S). Images were acquired using a Zeiss epifluorescent microscope or a Zeiss LSM900 confocal microscope.

Total collagen deposition staining

Cardiac tissue sections from the mid-cardiac area were stained for total collagen deposition using Picrosirius red solution. Frozen tissue sections were incubated for 15 minutes in Picrosirius red solution, followed by a 1-minute immersion in 0.1% HCl solution. The slides were quickly rinsed in 100% ethanol 3 times. Afterward, the sections were sealed using Permount Mounting Medium (Fisher Scientific, SP15) and photographed with a Keyence microscope. Images were acquired across multiple areas covering nearly the entire left ventricular cross-section to ensure representative sampling. Total collagen quantification was performed using CellProfiler software.

Cardiac fibroblast isolation and culture

We isolated the cardiac fibroblasts following a previously published protocol.21 Briefly, isolated cardiac noncardiomyocytes from all groups were plated on noncoated 96-well plates for 4 hours in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium and Ham’s F-12 supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin/streptomycin, followed by washing off nonadherent cells using Dulbecco’s PBS. Twenty-four hours later, fibroblasts (adherent cells) were washed twice with Dulbecco’s PBS and fixed using 4% PFA for 15 minutes, permeabilized and blocked using 3% BSA with 0.3% Triton X-100 in PBS for 60 minutes. Then, the cells were stained for α-SMA (1:250; Sigma #A2547-2ML) and Periostin (1:250; Abcam #ab14041) using the BSA 3% in PBS solution overnight. The next day, the cells were incubated with the secondary antibodies and DAPI (1:1000) for 30 minutes. Cells were imaged using a Cytation 3 (Biotek) and images were quantified using CellProfiler software.

Bone marrow-derived macrophage culture

Bone marrow–derived macrophages (BMDMs) were generated using an established protocol.22 After euthanasia, the femur and tibia were collected, and bone marrow was isolated by centrifugation. Cells were resuspended in culture medium, counted, and plated at 500,000 cells per well in 6-well plates with the addition of macrophage colony-stimulating factor (50 ng/mL). Cultures were maintained at 37 °C with 5% CO2 for 7 days, with additional medium added on day 4. On day 7, nonadherent cells were removed, and adherent macrophages were detached using a non-enzymatic dissociation buffer and gentle pipetting. Cells were collected, centrifuged, and reseeded in 96-well plates at 50,000 cells per well with 1% macrophage colony-stimulating factor, then incubated for 24 hours with vehicle or lipopolysaccharide (LPS) before fixation and staining for CXCL10 (1:100; Thermo Fisher Scientific, #701225).

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay protocol for CXCL10 concentration analysis in cardiac tissue

Heart tissue samples were collected, lysed, and the isolated proteins were used to assess CXCL10 concentration using a commercially available enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kit (R&D Systems, cat. no. DY466), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. In brief, the ELISA microplate was coated overnight with the capture antibody at 4 °C. The plate was then blocked and incubated with respective standards and protein samples. Afterward, the plate was incubated with the detection antibody, followed by streptavidin with horseradish peroxidase and substrate solution. Finally, the reaction was stopped by the addition of stop solution. Optical density was measured at 450 nm with wavelength correction at 570 nm, using a Cytation 3 microplate reader.

Isolation of immune cells and flow cytometry

Mediastinal lymph nodes (LNs) were harvested and placed in Eppendorf tubes containing fluorescent-activated cell sorter (FACS) buffer. The LNs were crushed through cell strainers in 6-well plates containing FACS buffer. The resulting cell suspensions were transferred to conical tubes through filters, and the filters were rinsed with additional FACS buffer. The cell suspensions were centrifuged at 300 g for 5 minutes at 4 °C, and the cell pellets were resuspended in FACS buffer. Cells were counted, and 1 million cells from each sample were used for the subsequent staining steps. The cells were incubated in the dark at 4 °C for 30 minutes with antibodies for surface marker staining, followed by a wash with FACS buffer and centrifugation. The cells were then fixed with fixative solution and incubated at 4 °C. After fixation, the cells were washed with permeabilization buffer and centrifuged. The cells were resuspended in permeabilization buffer for intracellular staining, which was performed by incubating the cells in the dark at 4 °C. The cells were then washed with permeabilization buffer and centrifuged. Final cell suspensions were resuspended in FACS buffer and transferred to FACS tubes through a filter. The samples were analyzed using a flow cytometer (BD LSR Fortessa, 5 lasers). The list of antibodies used is provided in Table 1.

Bone marrow chimeras

Recipient mice (C57BL/6J-Smsem6Lutzy/J(B6) or CXCL10–/–) underwent lethal irradiation using an x-ray irradiator, receiving 2 doses of 300 rads, with a 4-hour interval between doses. Two hours after the second dose, the recipient mice were injected intravenously via the tail vein with 5 × 106 donor bone marrow cells (WT or CXCL10–/–) in a volume of 200 μL. Following the bone marrow transplantation, mice were treated with antibiotics for 4 weeks to prevent infection and allowed an 8-week period for hematopoietic reconstitution.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism version 10.2 (GraphPad Software). The normality of the data was evaluated using the Shapiro-Wilk test. For data that were normally distributed with 2 experimental groups, unpaired 2-tailed Student's t-tests were used. When comparing 3 or more groups with normally distributed data, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by post hoc Tukey multiple comparison tests was performed. To analyze the impact of 2 categorical independent variables on a single continuous dependent variable, two-way ANOVA with post hoc Tukey multiple comparison tests was used. Unless otherwise indicated, all pairwise comparisons were made, and adjusted P values were used to determine statistical significance. Data are presented using individual data points with the mean ± standard error of the mean. For data that were not normally distributed, nonparametric tests were used. The Kruskal-Wallis test with post hoc Dunn multiple comparison tests was used for 3 or more groups. The sample size indicates the number of independent experimental units per group. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

CXCL10 increases in response to TAC and is expressed by macrophages

To identify the inflammatory cytokines altered in the early phase of cardiac remodeling in response to TAC, we initially performed a Proteome Profiler assay to survey for cytokines that might be altered in the heart tissue lysate in response to TAC (Figure 1A, left). Among the detected molecules, we observed an increase in CXCL10 levels (Figure 1A, right). We reanalyzed our published single-cell RNA-sequence (RNA-seq) results,21 where we studied differentially expressed markers in immune cells within cardiac tissue 1 week after TAC, we found that CXCL10 is one of the markers upregulated in macrophages in response to TAC (Figure 1B). To further explore the significance of the upregulated CXCL10, we cultured BMDMs and stimulated them with LPS to activate the macrophages (Figure 1C). Activated BMDMs exhibited increased CXCL10 expression. Because CXCL10 is known to attract T cells, we next determined if we observed T-cell activation after cardiac pressure overload. We reclustered CD3+ T cells and excluded NK cells (Figure 1D, Supplemental Figure S1) from our previously published single-cell RNA-seq dataset and analyzed expression of specific markers within each T-cell cluster 1 week after TAC. Our analysis revealed a significant expansion of Cd3g+T cells in TAC hearts (3,935 cells from 6 hearts) compared to sham (209 cells from 4 hearts), as visualized by uniform manifold approximation and projection (Figure 1E), suggesting that pressure overload induces T-cell accumulation in the heart. Our analysis identified 8 distinct T-cell clusters (Figure 1F). We observed expression of Cd44 and Sell (CD62L), which are commonly associated with T-cell activation. Additionally, we observed expression of well-known markers of T-cell subsets, including Foxp3 (Treg), Tbx21 (Th1), and Gata3 (Th2) (Figure 1G). Overall, there is a clear increase in CXCL10 after pressure overload, which could result in T-cell recruitment and activation.

Genetic deletion of CXCL10 alters the initial cardiac remodeling induced by pressure overload

To determine the impact of CXCL10 in the initial phase of cardiac remodeling in the setting of pressure overload, we subjected WT and CXCL10 KO (CXCL10–/–) animals to sham or TAC surgery and assessed their phenotype 1 week later (Figure 2A). Echocardiography assessment of cardiac morphology and function did not show differences between WT and CXCL10–/– animals at 1 week after TAC (Figures 2B to 2F). Both WT and KO animals show the development of cardiac hypertrophy (Figure 2G), but deletion of CXCL10 attenuated the extent of developed hypertrophy at this timepoint. At the cellular level, WGA staining of histologic sections was used to quantify cardiomyocyte cross-sectional area. WT animals exhibited a significant increase in hypertrophy 1 week after TAC (Figures 2H and 2I), whereas CXCL10–/– animals showed a milder hypertrophic response (P = 0.0506). Next, we analyzed the development of cardiac fibrosis using Picrosirius red staining. Our data show that total collagen deposition is increased in WT animals after TAC, whereas CXCL10–/– animals show virtually no fibrosis at this timepoint (Figures 2J and 2K). To further assess if CXCL10 deletion influenced fibroblast activation 1 week after TAC, we performed immunofluorescence staining which revealed a marked increase in vimentin+α-SMA+ fibroblasts in WT hearts after TAC, which was significantly attenuated in CXCL10–/– mice (Figures 2L and 2M). These findings indicate reduced fibroblast activation in the absence of CXCL10 in vivo. To determine whether this reduction reflected a direct effect of CXCL10 on fibroblasts or was secondary to changes in the inflammatory milieu, we next performed in vitro assays. Primary cardiac fibroblasts (WT) were either co-cultured with BMDMs from WT or CXCL10–/– mice or treated with conditioned medium from these macrophages. In both settings, the proportion of α-SMA+ fibroblasts remained unchanged between WT and CXCL10–/– conditions (Supplemental Figures S2A and S2B), suggesting that CXCL10 does not directly affect fibroblast activation. We next measured the increase in microvascular density in response to TAC, which was only observed in WT mice (Figures 2N and 2O). Overall, these findings indicate that CXCL10 does not affect cardiac function early in the cardiac remodeling process, but deletion attenuates pathologic remodeling.

CXCL10 deficiency attenuates macrophage expansion after pressure overload

To determine whether the protective phenotype observed in CXCL10–/– mice could be attributed, at least in part, to changes in cardiac macrophage populations, we analyzed resident and monocyte-derived macrophage (MoMΦ) subsets 1 week after TAC. Flow cytometry revealed that WT mice exhibited a marked expansion of resident macrophages (Res MΦs) and a smaller increase in monocyte-derived macrophages following TAC (Figures 3A to 3C). In contrast, CXCL10–/– mice showed significantly reduced expansion of resident macrophages compared with WT animals, whereas the number of monocyte-derived macrophages remained low and not significantly different from sham controls.

Immunofluorescence staining confirmed these findings, showing abundant CD68+ macrophages in WT hearts after TAC, which were markedly reduced in CXCL10–/– hearts (Figures 3D and 3E). CD163+ macrophages, typically associated with tissue remodeling and repair, were increased in WT TAC hearts but were not significantly altered in CXCL10–/– mice (Figure 3F). Because CXCL10 is a well-known interferon-stimulated gene, and because natural killer (NK) cells can both produce and respond to interferon- γ (IFN-γ) in cardiac injury, we assessed the abundance of NK cells in the heart 1 week after TAC. TAC resulted in an equivalent increase in NK1.1+ cells in both WT and CXCL10–/– mice, suggesting that the observed effects are likely not due to diminished IFN signals, although we did not assess IFN levels directly (Supplemental Figure S3). Together, these data indicate that CXCL10 deficiency limits macrophage expansion after pressure overload, particularly the resident macrophages.

Genetic deletion of CXCL10 protects against long-term cardiac remodeling effects induced by pressure overload

To assess the role of CXCL10 in the development of long-term remodeling and HF, we again performed sham or TAC and analyzed the hearts of WT and CXCL10–/– animals 6 weeks later (Figure 4A). Unlike the 1-week time point, echocardiographic assessment of cardiac structure and function now showed substantial differences between the groups. WT animals after TAC displayed a reduction in ejection fraction and fractional shortening, accompanied by an increase in both the left ventricular diastolic volume and diameter (Figures 4B to 4E). In contrast, CXCL10–/– animals were protected from these changes, as no significant alterations in cardiac function or dimensions were observed (Figures 4B to 4E). Moreover, an increase in lung weight was observed only in WT animals after cardiac pressure overload, indicating that CXCL10–/– animals were protected from the development of pulmonary congestion (Figure 4F). Furthermore, whereas WT animals after TAC showed a substantial level of cardiac hypertrophy, CXCL10–/– animals were protected from this hypertrophic response (Figure 4G). At the cellular level, CXCL10–/– animals also showed a reduced hypertrophic response as measured by cardiomyocyte cross-sectional area (Figures 4H and 4I). Moreover, they were protected from the development of cardiac fibrosis, as shown by Picrosirius red staining (Figures 4J and 4K). To further test the hypothesis that the absence of fibrosis in CXCL10–/– animals was due to a reduction in fibroblast differentiation into myofibroblasts, we harvested hearts from WT and CXCL10–/– animals 6 weeks after TAC and isolated primary cardiac fibroblasts. After plating fibroblasts and allowing them to adhere for 24 hours, we assessed the proportion of α-SMA–positive cells, indicative of myofibroblasts. Our results show that WT animals have a substantial increase in the percentage of myofibroblasts after TAC, whereas CXCL10–/– animals showed no increase in the percentage of α-SMA–positive cells compared with sham-operated animals (Figures 4L and 4M). These results indicate that the deletion of CXCL10 confers protection against the development of HF and fibrosis in response to cardiac pressure overload.

Genetic deletion of CXCL10 reduces T-cell numbers in draining lymph nodes

To determine if CXCL10 impacts T-cell activation and migration, we performed flow cytometry on T cells harvested from draining lymph nodes of mice subjected to TAC. We used a common gating strategy to identify CD4+ and CD8+ T cells and further classify subsets, including naïve, central memory, and effector memory cells based on CD44 and CD62L expression (Figure 5A).23 Quantification of T-cell abundance reveals that in WT animals subjected to TAC, there is a significant increase in the absolute number of CD3+ (Figure 5B) and CD4+ T cells (Figure 5C) when compared with sham-operated controls. In contrast, CXCL10–/– animals did not show an increase following TAC, indicating that the deletion of CXCL10 prevents T-cell expansion after pressure overload. Analysis of CD8+ T cells revealed no increase in cell numbers following TAC in either group (Supplemental Figure S4). When further analyzing CD4 T-cell subsets, we noticed no difference between groups for central memory and effector memory CD4 T cells (Figures 5E and 5F), whereas naïve and terminally differentiated effector memory CD4 T cells (Effector Memory Re-expressing CD45RA) were enhanced in WT mice after TAC, but not in CXCL10–/– mice (Figures 5D and 5G). Further analyses of T-cell subsets to measure regulatory T (Tregs), Th1, and Th2 cells (Figures 5H to 5K), show that TAC increases the number of cells in all these subsets in WT mice but not in CXCL10–/– mice. Consistent with this observation, analysis at an earlier time point (1-week post-TAC) showed that CXCL10–/– mice did not develop LN enlargement despite comparable levels of T-cell proliferation at this timepoint (Supplemental Figure S5). Because cardiac pressure overload did not lead to an increase in the numbers of Tregs, Th1, and Th2 cells in CXCL10–/– mice, it is indicated that CXCL10 contributes to the T-cell recruitment and expansion associated with cardiac stress and remodeling, and its genetic deletion protects against these effects, particularly by preserving activation and expansion in population size of specific T-cell subsets.

CXCL10 from recruited macrophages alters cardiac remodeling in response to TAC

We showed that macrophages express Cxcl10 by single-cell RNA-seq (Figure 1B), and we also showed enhanced expression of CXCL10 in BMDMs upon LPS activation (Figure 1C). Moreover, a previous publication showed that mainly leukocytes show enhanced CXCL10 expression after cardiac pressure overload.15 To further test the hypothesis that recruited macrophages are largely responsible for the increase in CXCL10 levels after pressure overload, we performed TAC on CCR2–/– animals, which exhibit a deficiency in the recruitment of BMDMs (Figure 6A). Although CCR2–/– animals exhibited an equivalent degree of cardiac hypertrophy in response to TAC (Figure 6B), an increase in tissue CXCL10 levels was only observed in WT animals (Figure 6C). These data indicate that recruited macrophages are the main cause of the increased CXCL10 expression observed after TAC. To further understand how CXCL10 secretion by recruited macrophages contributes to the development of cardiac dysfunction post-TAC, we generated chimeras of CXCL10–/– mice that received bone marrow cells from WT mice, and WT mice that received cells from CXCL10–/– mice (Figure 6D). Engraftment was measured by flow cytometry on peripheral blood leukocytes and was >90% for both groups (Figure 6E). We then performed TAC and assessed cardiac remodeling 8 weeks later. Cardiac function was equivalent between groups, but WT mice that received CXCL10–/– bone marrow cells displayed reduced dilation and reduced cardiac volumes (Figures 6F to 6H). Furthermore, WT animals receiving CXCL10–/– bone marrow cells exhibited a reduced degree of cardiac hypertrophy in response to TAC, compared with KO animals that received WT bone marrow cells (Figure 6I). At the cellular level, WT animals with transplanted CXCL10–/– bone marrow also showed reduced cardiomyocyte hypertrophy (Figures 6J and 6K). The WT mice also displayed lower lung weights (Figure 6L). Interestingly, when analyzing cardiac fibrosis, no differences were observed between the groups (Figures 6M and 6N). Collectively, these results reinforce the pivotal role of CXCL10 secreted by recruited macrophages to accelerate cardiac remodeling and hypertrophy, ultimately leading to the development of HF.

Discussion

T cells are central mediators in the development and progression of HF10,24, 25, 26, 27, 28 with CXCR3 as the key receptor driving their recruitment and activation in cardiac tissue.29, 30, 31 In this study, we show that CXCL10 plays a key role in pressure overload–induced cardiac remodeling by regulating cardiomyocyte hypertrophy, fibrosis, and cardiac dysfunction. We found that the genetic deletion of CXCL10 alters the number and activation level of T cells in the mediastinal LNs. These findings are consistent with previous work showing that the deletion of the CXCR3 receptor improves cardiac remodeling after TAC.15 However, this is the first time that the central role of one of its ligands, specifically CXCL10, in the development of HF has been shown. Given the evidence we found in vivo using CXCL10 KO mice and chimeras that received bone marrow from CXCL10–/– animals, our results firmly establish that CXCL10 regulates cardiac hypertrophy, fibrosis, and dysfunction after pressure overload. We believe that CXCL10 likely exerts a dominant effect in this model because the absence promotes a remarkably strong protective phenotype, supporting the idea that CXCL9 and CXCL11 would only exert a meaningful effect through cooperative action among CXCR3 ligands rather than independently. Previous studies show that, although the 3 CXCR3 ligands bind to the same receptor, they differ in affinity, timing, and site of expression, which results in distinct functions depending on the inflammatory context.32 In several models, such as viral infection33 and brain inflammation,34 CXCL10 has a dominant role, and its absence is not compensated by CXCL9 or CXCL11. In other situations, however, there is evidence that these ligands can act cooperatively, especially when produced by different cell types or at different stages of the immune response.35 Therefore, in the context of pressure overload–induced cardiac remodeling, CXCL10 is likely to have a predominant effect, whereas CXCL9 and CXCL11 may contribute only in a collaborative way, amplifying the CXCR3-mediated response. However, because C57Bl/6 mice express a mutated, nonfunctional CXCL11, it could be that CXCL11 is more important than appreciated in our study and it could play a more prominent role in humans.20

Our results show increased expression of CXCL10 in cardiac macrophages after TAC, as shown by RNA-seq analysis and by ELISA measurement of cardiac CXCL10, as well as an increase in response to activation of BMDMs induced by LPS. The increase in CXCL10 expression in cardiac tissue was also observed in heart tissue 2 and 4 weeks after TAC in a prior publication,15 as well as in the plasma of patients with cardiac dysfunction.36,37 Moreover, we previously reported the specific regulation of CXCL10 in recruited macrophages,21 as opposed to resident macrophages, suggesting a unique role for this chemokine in modulating the local heart environment in response to stress. Additionally, in the murine immune checkpoint inhibitor myocarditis model (Ctla4+/–Pdcd1–/– mice), treatment with a CCR2-neutralizing antibody (MC-21) reduced the abundance of Cxcl9+Cxcl10+ macrophages in the heart.38 Reinforcing this evidence, our data demonstrate that CCR2-deficient animals do not show the increased concentration of CXCL10 in cardiac tissue observed in WT animals 1 week after TAC. This result is consistent with previously published data by Ngwenyama et al,15 which showed an increase in CXCL10 only in myeloid cells 1 week after TAC. Moreover, inhibition of CCR2-dependent monocyte recruitment has been shown previously to slow the progression of HF, especially during the early phase of cardiac remodeling.39 Those findings are in agreement with our results that CXCL10 deletion confers strong protection against the later development of HF with no functional protection early on.

The genetic deletion of CXCL10 attenuates the hypertrophic response in the initial phase of cardiac remodeling after TAC. To our knowledge, this is the first time that CXCL10 has been directly associated with cardiac hypertrophic response. Prior work showed that mice with deletion of the CXCR3 receptor exhibited attenuation of TAC-induced cardiomyocyte hypertrophy 4 weeks after the stimulus.15 In addition to showing attenuation of cardiac hypertrophy, CXCL10–/– mice also exhibited reduced interstitial fibrosis. This profibrotic effect of CXCL10 has been observed in other tissues.40,41 Our results show an increase in the number of myofibroblasts in the hearts of WT animals. We believe this effect could result from the in vivo action of T cells, considering that, although these cells were analyzed in vitro, they originated from a microenvironment predominantly regulated by T cells. Therefore, the differentiation of fibroblasts into myofibroblasts is likely a mechanism through which T cells contribute to adverse cardiac remodeling. Previous studies also support the association between T cells and pressure overload–induced cardiac fibrosis. RAG2 KO (RAG2–/–) mice, which lack lymphocyte recruitment to cardiac tissue, were protected against TAC-induced fibrosis.16 Additionally, T-cell deficiency through deletion of TCRα KO (TCRα–/–) showed protection against cardiac fibrosis after TAC with reduced expression levels of α-SMA.42

As mice transition from a state of compensated hypertrophy towards the development of HF, the protective effects of CXCL10 deletion against remodeling and cardiac dysfunction become more evident. The absence of CXCL10 not only prevented further hypertrophic growth but also attenuated adverse changes in cardiac function and structure at the 6-week post-TAC mark. These protective effects are potentially mediated by changes in inflammatory cell recruitment and activation, as evidenced by the reduction in T-cell subsets in CXCL10–/– animals. Other studies have reported that the interaction between CXCL10+ macrophages and T cells is involved in poor outcomes in cardiac remodeling. Ma et al38 demonstrated that both IFN-γ blockade and depletion of Cxcl9+Cxcl10+ macrophages prolonged the survival of Ctl4+/–Pdcd1–/– mice. Additionally, this study showed that in silico predictions of cell-cell communication indicated that both CD4+ and CD8+ cells were the primary ligand sources for CXCL signaling, whereas macrophages were the receivers of IFN-γ signaling.

As previously mentioned, CD4+ T cells are important mediators of the transition from compensatory cardiac hypertrophy to HF.16 In this study, the authors showed that both MHCII KO mice and transgenic mice with a CD4+ T-cell receptor specific for ovalbumin (OT-II) — animal models that either lack T cells or have CD4+ T cells unable to recognize antigens — did not develop cardiac dilation and dysfunction induced by TAC when compared to their respective controls. Interestingly, our data show that the absence of CXCL10 is sufficient to recapitulate these phenotypes observed in these other models. This further supports the notion that CXCL10 is likely the key ligand orchestrating the recruitment and activation of T cells in the development and progression of HF. Although the role of CD4+ T cells in the progression of HF has been mentioned above, some studies have also reported the involvement of CD8+ T cells in cardiac remodeling induced by TAC. An increase in CD8+ T cells was demonstrated in the left ventricle43 and in the lungs of animals subjected to TAC44 after 24 weeks or 4 weeks, respectively. The same group showed that the depletion of CD8+ T cells in animals with pre-existing left ventricular dysfunction slows the progression of HF.45 In our study, we observed significant differences in both the number and activation of CD4+ T cells, whereas for CD8+ T cells, no changes in abundance were observed. Together with existing evidence, our results indicate that the effects of CXCL10 are more pronounced in CD4+ T cells, although influence of CXCL10+ macrophages on CD8+ T-cell behavior cannot be excluded.

Finally, our study highlights the critical impact of CXCL10 on immune modulation. The significant reduction in T-cell numbers in the draining LNs of CXCL10–/– animals indicates a central role for this chemokine in immune cell trafficking and activation in response to cardiac stress. This finding identifies CXCL10 as a key mediator of the inflammatory response following cardiac injury and suggests its potential as a therapeutic target to modulate immune responses in HF.

Study limitations

This study was primarily conducted in murine models, which may limit the generalizability of the results to humans, as species differences could influence the response to CXCL10 and associated therapies. Additionally, the study did not investigate other potential immune mechanisms or molecular factors that may interact with CXCL10 in the development of HF, which could limit a complete understanding of the process. Although the study focused on the role of CXCL10, other CXCR3 ligands, such as CXCL9 and CXCL11, may also play important roles, and their interaction with CXCL10 was not fully explored.

Conclusions

CXCL10 plays a multifaceted role in cardiac remodeling, impacting both the structural remodeling and dysfunction of the heart. Our study provides strong evidence for a central role of CXCL10 in exacerbating cardiac remodeling and suggests that targeting CXCL10 or downstream signaling pathways may offer new therapeutic avenues to mitigate HF progression. Future studies should test a direct therapeutic role of inhibiting the CXCL10 axis and dissect the signaling mechanisms through which CXCL10 influences macrophage function and T-cell dynamics, which may reveal new strategies to control inflammation-driven cardiac dysfunction.

COMPETENCY IN MEDICAL KNOWLEDGE: The development of HF involves changes in many cardiac cell types. One of the emerging notions is the involvement of cardiac inflammation in the initial and later phases of HF development and progression. Despite this emerging notion, there are no approved therapies that target cardiac inflammation. Therefore, additional knowledge must be generated with the goal of identifying potential therapeutic targets that can slow HF progression. Our study shows that CXCL10 is a key regulator of macrophage–T-cell interactions that initiate early cardiac remodeling and drive the transition to cardiac dysfunction and HF at later stages. We show that CXCL10 deficiency reduces fibrosis and hypertrophy without impairing cardiac performance, refining current understanding of how immune-cell communication shapes both the onset and progression of maladaptive remodeling in pressure-overload conditions. These findings highlight CXCL10 as a central immunoregulatory factor in HF pathogenesis.

TRANSLATIONAL OUTLOOK: This study highlights the critical role of CXCL10 in cardiac remodeling, where it mediates hypertrophy, fibrosis, and dysfunction through the recruitment and activation of immune cells, particularly T cells. Targeting CXCL10 could help reduce inflammation and slow HF progression, thereby improving cardiac function and patient outcomes. These findings suggest that CXCL10 inhibitors may offer a promising therapeutic approach for preventing or treating HF with the important caveat that they may also negatively impact the immune response to infections.
