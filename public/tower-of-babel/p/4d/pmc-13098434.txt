Work-active ageing or healthy worker selection? Late-life employment and happiness among older migrants in Europe
Authors: Lawrence E. Ugwu, Uzoamaka F. Ugwoke, Botho Nanvula Ramonkga, Anthony Ejike Okonkwo, Kedibone J. Ramadie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPolicies promoting work-active ageing assume that extending working lives improves later-life well-being. Among migrants, late-life employment is shaped by migration-related selection and labour-market stratification, which may inflate associations with work well-being. We test whether an apparent work-active immigrant paradox (older migrants appearing both more likely to remain in paid work and more likely to report higher happiness) reflects benefits of work or selection into employment and residence.MethodsWe analysed cross-sectional data from the European Social Survey Round 11 (2023/24), including 12,989 adults aged ≥65 years (847 foreign-born; 12,142 native-born). Paid work was defined as respondents’ main activity in the last 7 days; happiness was measured on a 0–10 scale. We estimated naïve linear regression models and inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) models to balance workers and non-workers with respect to pre-exposure demographic, socioeconomic, and health indicators (age, gender, education, partnership status, financial strain, self-rated health, and health limitations/conditions). Extreme weights were trimmed at the 99th percentile, and post-weight balance was assessed using standardised mean differences and a Love plot. We also tested effect modification by migration status using an interaction term (Paid work and migrant status) and ran a migrant-only sensitivity analysis.ResultsOlder migrants were more likely than native-born respondents to report paid work (11.0 % vs. 6.6 %) and reported higher mean happiness (7.54 vs. 7.18). In naïve models, paid work was associated with higher happiness (β = 0.33, 95 % CI 0.21–0.45). After IPTW adjustment, the work–happiness association attenuated to a small, non-significant estimate (β = 0.06, 95 % CI: −0.09–0.22). The migrant advantage in happiness persisted in IPTW models (β = 0.45, 95 % CI 0.21–0.69).ConclusionsIn later life, the apparent well-being benefits of paid work largely reflect selection on measured health and demographic factors, consistent with healthy-worker selection. The migrant happiness advantage is not explained by employment participation. Population health strategies should prioritise health protection and the quality and voluntariness of later-life work, alongside broader supports for inclusion and security.

Introduction

Europe is experiencing a dual demographic shift: populations are ageing while becoming more diverse through migration. As earlier labour-migration cohorts reach later life and as family reunification and intra-European mobility continue, the number of older residents with migration histories is increasing across many European societies (OECD/European Commission, 2023). These trends have intensified debates about social inclusion, pension adequacy, and the conditions that support health and well-being in later life.

Within this policy landscape, active ageing has become a central framework, frequently emphasising continued participation as a pathway to healthier ageing and reduced dependency (World Health Organization [WHO], 2002; Walker and Maltby, 2012). Because the WHO concept is broader than paid work, we use the term work-active ageing to refer specifically to later-life participation in paid work. From the perspective of Activity Theory, maintaining meaningful roles can sustain identity, purpose, and subjective well-being in old age (Havighurst, 1961). In practice, work-centred interpretations have often been extended to older migrants, for whom paid work is framed as supporting financial security, social integration, and psychological stability.

However, this interpretation sits uneasily alongside life-course theories that highlight structural inequality. Cumulative advantage/disadvantage and cumulative inequality perspectives argue that constrained opportunities and exposures to adversity accumulate over time, shaping later-life health and well-being through material and psychosocial pathways (Dannefer, 2003; Ferraro and Shippee, 2009; Jackson and Engelman, 2022). Given migrants’ higher likelihood of fragmented careers, occupational segregation, discrimination, and uneven access to social protection in many contexts, these frameworks would often predict lower well-being in later life rather than an advantage (Benavente et al., 2023).

Migration status is not treated here as a convenient subgroup, but as a theoretically relevant axis of stratification in migration and health research (WHO, 2022). Older migrants often occupy distinct employment trajectories shaped by earlier-life labour-market segmentation, discrimination, and differential access to pensions and health care (OECD/EU, 2018). These pathways can jointly influence both the likelihood of remaining in paid work after age 65 and later-life well-being (Palloni and Arias, 2004; WHO, 2022). Consequently, the work–well-being association may differ in magnitude or meaning between foreign-born and native-born older adults, making migration status central to the study’s migration–health contribution.

Against this theoretical background, some datasets show an empirical co-occurrence that warrants careful interpretation: the migrant–native gap in subjective well-being may narrow at older ages, and older migrants can appear more likely to remain economically active (Sand and Gruber, 2016). We refer to this co-occurrence as an apparent work-active immigrant paradox. Importantly, the term "paradox" is descriptive rather than logical: it signals a pattern that seems surprising under expectations of cumulative disadvantage, not a theoretical contradiction. A key question is whether the pattern reflects the benefits of paid work (consistent with activity-based accounts) or primarily reflects selection processes that shape who remains in the destination country and who remains employed at older ages (Sand and Gruber, 2018).

Two selection mechanisms are particularly salient. First, the healthy migrant effect suggests that migrants may be positively selected on health relative to host populations, and observed later-life outcomes can be further shaped by selective return migration (often discussed as salmon bias), whereby those in poorer health may be more likely to leave the host country (Razum, Zeeb, and Rohrmann, 2000). Second, the healthy worker effect implies that continued employment (especially in later life) is itself selective: individuals with poorer health are more likely to exit the labour force through retirement, disability, or unemployment (McMichael, 1976). Together, these processes can create a spurious association in which working older migrants appear happier not because employment improves well-being, but because healthier individuals are more likely to both remain in the host country and remain employed.

Despite the importance of these mechanisms, research linking migration, late-life employment, and well-being remains vulnerable to bias. Much of the literature relies on correlational models that “adjust for health” using standard covariate controls, without explicitly accounting for the probability of employment at older ages (Ndjaboue et al., 2025). However, in this setting, health is not merely a confounder to be controlled linearly; it is structurally implicated in labour-force participation and closely tied to subjective well-being. Without a design that directly reduces selection bias in employment, evidence may be over-interpreted as supporting extended work as a pathway to better well-being among older migrants.

The present study addresses this gap by testing the apparent work-active immigrant paradox using Round 11 of the European Social Survey (ESS 2023/24) and focusing on adults aged 65 years and above. Drawing on migration and health scholarship linking later-life well-being to work, health, and socioeconomic security, we examine whether any observed happiness advantage among older migrants is attributable to paid work or reflects selection into work. Moving beyond conventional regression adjustment, we use inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) to balance observed differences between those who are working and those who are not, thereby reducing bias from measured selection processes. Our objectives are to: (1) describe whether a work-active immigrant profile is observable in a representative European sample of adults aged 65+; (2) estimate the association between paid work and happiness after accounting for observed demographic, socioeconomic, and health selection into employment; (3) assess whether any migrant happiness advantage diminishes once late-life employment and selection are accounted for; and (4) test whether the paid work–happiness association differs by migration status using a Paid work and migrant status interaction, supplemented by a migrant-only sensitivity analysis.

Methods

Data source and study population

Data were drawn from Round 11 of the European Social Survey (ESS), a cross-national, probability-based survey conducted in 2023. The ESS utilizes strict random sampling procedures to ensure representativeness of the residential populations aged 15 and older within participating countries.

The analytic sample for this study was restricted to older adults aged 65 to 120 years. Respondents were excluded if they had missing data on country of birth (n = 86), main activity (n = 12), or key covariates used in the propensity score model (n = 353). The final analytic sample consisted of 12,989 individuals.

Measures

Dependent variable

Subjective well-being (happiness). Happiness was assessed using the standard ESS single-item question: “Taking all things together, how happy would you say you are?” Responses were recorded on an 11-point scale ranging from 0 (“Extremely unhappy”) to 10 (“Extremely happy”). Single-item subjective well-being measures are widely used in population surveys and show evidence of validity and temporal stability in prior research; we therefore interpret happiness as a standard hedonic well-being indicator while acknowledging potential cross-cultural reporting differences.

Migration Status. Respondents were classified as Immigrants if they were born in a country different from the one in which they were interviewed (brncntr = 2). Those born in the country of interview were classified as Native-born (brncntr = 1).

Paid work (work-active ageing). Work-active ageing was operationalised as participation in paid work, using the respondent’s main activity in the last 7 days (mnactic). The variable was coded as Paid work (1) versus Not in paid work (0), including retirees, unemployed, homemakers, and those permanently sick/disabled. This operationalisation captures current work participation, not job quality, hours, voluntariness, or informal/seasonal work.

Covariates for the propensity score model (selection into paid work). To address health-related and socioeconomic selection into paid work after age 65, the propensity score model included pre-exposure covariates that are plausibly related to both paid work and happiness: Age (continuous, years), Gender (male/female), Educational attainment (harmonised ISCED categories), Partnership status (living with a spouse/partner vs not), Perceived household income adequacy (financial strain; ordinal categories), Self-rated health (SRH; ordinal categories), and health limitation/condition indicators available in ESS Round 11 (e.g., longstanding illness/disability and activity limitation, and reported health conditions). To avoid imposing linearity assumptions, SRH and financial strain were entered as categorical (indicator) variables in the propensity model. The country of interview was included as a set of fixed effects to account for cross-national differences in welfare regimes and labour-market contexts.

Statistical analysis

Identification strategy: inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW)

A naive comparison of working versus non-working older adults is prone to “healthy worker” selection bias, wherein healthier individuals are more likely to remain in the workforce. To address this, we employed Inverse Probability of Treatment Weighting (IPTW) to create a pseudo-population in which employment status is independent of measured confounders.

Propensity scores (PS) were estimated using a multivariable logistic regression model predicting the probability of being in paid work. Predictors included age, gender, education, partnership status, financial strain, self-rated health, health limitation/condition indicators, and country fixed effects. Inverse probability weights were calculated as 1/PS for individuals in paid work and 1/(1-PS) for those not in paid work. Weights were stabilised by the marginal probability of paid work and trimmed at the 99th percentile to reduce the influence of extreme weights.

Covariate balance diagnostics

The efficacy of the weighting procedure was assessed using Standardised Mean Differences (SMDs) for each covariate, reported before and after weighting. An SMD < 0.10 was considered indicative of negligible imbalance. We also visualised balance using a Love plot and examined the distribution of weights (including effective sample size) to assess stability after trimming.

Estimation models

The association between migration status, paid work, and happiness was estimated using three linear regression specifications:1.Model 1 (Naïve OLS): An unweighted Ordinary Least Squares regression to describe the baseline association.2.Model 2 (IPTW): A weighted linear regression applying inverse probability weights to reduce measured selection into paid work.3.Model 3 (Effect modification): The IPTW model extended with a Paid work × migrant status interaction term to test whether the work–happiness association differs between foreign-born and native-born older adults. As an additional robustness check, we estimated a migrant-only model comparing working versus non-working migrants, noting that statistical power is limited by the small number of working migrants.

The weighting diagnostics indicated improved covariate balance, and the estimates show that the apparent work–happiness association attenuated after adjustment while the migrant advantage persisted.

In the Naïve (Unadjusted) Model, labour force participation was positively and significantly associated with happiness (β = 0.33, 95 % CI [0.21, 0.45], p < .001). This model supported the initial observation that working older adults report higher happiness.

However, in the Causal (IPTW-Adjusted) Model, which accounts for health and demographic selection, the association between work and happiness was attenuated to non-significance (β = 0.06, 95 % CI [−0.09, 0.22], p = .432). This indicates that the positive relationship observed in the naive model was primarily driven by the “healthy worker effect”, the tendency for healthier, and thus happier, individuals to remain in the workforce.

Conversely, the positive association between migration status and happiness remained robust. In the fully adjusted causal model, being an immigrant was associated with significantly higher happiness (β = 0.45, 95 % CI [0.21, 0.69], p < .001), independent of employment status or health.

Discussion

According to data from the latest European Social Survey (ESS), this study found that older immigrants in Europe were nearly twice as likely as native-born adults to report paid work as their main activity (11.0 per cent compared to 6.6 per cent), and they also reported a slightly higher average happiness score (7.54 vs. 7.18). However, those in paid work were younger and substantially healthier than non-workers, signalling strong selection into late-life employment consistent with the “healthy worker effect” (McMichael, 1976).

Consistent with this selection pattern, the substantial association between paid work and happiness in the naïve model attenuated to a small, statistically non-significant estimate after inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW). The weighting procedure meaningfully improved the measured balance between workers and non-workers, consistent with recommended diagnostics for propensity-score weighting (Austin, 2009; Stuart, 2010). In contrast, the immigrant happiness advantage persisted and was even more pronounced in the IPTW-adjusted model. Taken together, these findings indicate that the observed “work benefit” for happiness among older adults primarily reflects health and demographic selection into employment. At the same time, immigrants continue to demonstrate a happiness advantage even after controlling for socioeconomic and related factors; in our data, this advantage is not attributable to paid work or to the selection factors measured in the IPTW model. This broader pattern is consistent with prior research showing that older immigrants in some Western European contexts are not uniformly worse off in happiness and may exhibit an adjusted happiness advantage, although the mechanisms highlighted in that literature, including social capital, were not directly tested in the present study (Jiang and Renema, 2021).

Testing for heterogeneity

We additionally explored whether paid work is differentially associated with happiness for migrants versus native-born older adults. The interaction term was non-significant (p = .400), suggesting that the “healthy worker” selection mechanism operates similarly across both groups.

While the point estimate for the effect of work was numerically higher among migrants (β = 0.25) compared to the main model’s average (β = 0.06), this difference did not reach statistical significance. This implies that even for migrants (who might theoretically benefit more from the social integration of work), employment status itself is not a primary driver of well-being once health and demographic selection are rigorously controlled. Future research with larger samples of working migrants is needed to determine if the modest positive coefficient reflects a small, undetectable effect or random noise.

A central implication is that paid work among individuals aged 65+ may not, on average, function as an independent driver of happiness once selection into employment is controlled for. This pattern aligns with occupational epidemiology’s long-standing observation that those who remain employed tend to be positively selected for health (the healthy worker effect), which can generate inflated associations between employment and health-related outcomes when selection is not explicitly accounted for (McMichael, 1976). Methodologically, the present results reinforce calls to go beyond conventional “adjust for health” regression strategies in contexts where health strongly structures exposure to work; IPTW and related causal-inference approaches are specifically designed to reduce confounding/selection by balancing measured covariates between exposure groups (Rosenbaum and Rubin, 1983; Cole and Hernán, 2008; Hernán and Robins, 2024).

Substantively, these findings speak to an enduring tension in work-active ageing policy: while many frameworks emphasise continued activity as beneficial, work-centred interpretations can overstate the well-being returns of labour-force extension if observed work–well-being gradients are treated as causal. The WHO active ageing framework is broader than paid work, and this broader view is also reflected in the Active Ageing Index, which conceptualises active ageing across multiple domains, including employment, participation in society, independent, healthy and secure living, and the capacity and enabling environment for active ageing (WHO, 2002; Boudiny, 2013; Zaidi, 2015; UNECE/European Commission, 2018). From this perspective, volunteering, caregiving, and other unpaid forms of social participation should not be treated as peripheral, but as central components of active ageing. The present results, therefore, support a more conditional reading: if work matters, it may do so primarily through how older adults are engaged rather than through paid work status per se.

The null average IPTW-adjusted association is also compatible with offsetting mechanisms. Work can plausibly improve subjective well-being via income security, time structure, and social connectedness (Jahoda, 1982). However, it can also reduce well-being through strain, physical demands, or involuntary necessity, especially for those with disadvantaged work histories. When such countervailing experiences coexist within the 65+ workforce, a near-zero average effect is plausible even if meaningful heterogeneity exists across subgroups.

Why does the migrant happiness advantage persist?

The more striking substantive result is the robustness of the immigrant advantage in happiness after adjusting for paid work and measured selection into work. This pattern is consistent with research suggesting that migrant–native differences in well-being are shaped by multiple selection processes across the life course and by post-migration adaptation, with later-life patterns varying across contexts and cohorts (e.g., Sand and Gruber, 2018). Several mechanisms (non-mutually exclusive) may explain persistence.

The healthy migrant effect posits that migrants are often positively selected on health at migration; later-life observed outcomes can be further shaped by selective survival and selective return migration (“salmon bias”), in which those with poorer health are more likely to leave the destination country (Razum, Zeeb, and Rohrmann, 2000). If such processes operate, older immigrants captured in household surveys may constitute a resilient “survivor” group with unmeasured health robustness, coping capacities, or psychological resources not fully proxied by a single self-rated health item. In this sense, the persistence of the happiness advantage after controlling for self-rated health is not inconsistent with selection; it may indicate that key selection dimensions remain unmeasured.

Migration histories can entail substantial demands and adaptation. For some, successfully navigating migration, settlement, and identity renegotiation may cultivate coping skills and a sense of mastery that supports later-life subjective well-being. While this interpretation remains speculative in the present data, it provides a theoretically plausible pathway that is not reducible to paid work participation.

Subjective well-being depends partly on comparisons, expectations, and meaning-making (Diener, 1984; OECD, 2013). Older migrants may evaluate their life circumstances relative to conditions in their country of origin or earlier hardships, potentially leading to higher reported happiness despite objective disadvantage. This does not imply “bias” in any pejorative sense; instead, it highlights that well-being measures capture both circumstances and appraisal.

Cumulative advantage/disadvantage perspectives emphasise that later-life outcomes reflect accumulated exposures and stratified opportunity structures (Dannefer, 2003; Ferraro and Shippee, 2009). For immigrants, cumulative processes may differ by cohort, origin, and destination context, producing later-life well-being patterns that are not easily predicted from contemporaneous employment alone. The persistence of the happiness advantage, therefore, motivates more fine-grained theory and measurement, rather than simple attribution to “being active” through work.

Policy implications

First, the findings caution against interpreting correlational evidence as support for extending working lives among older adults (particularly older migrants) on the assumption that work itself improves happiness. In this study, the observed work–happiness association was primarily explained by selection into employment. Policies that incentivise longer working lives without addressing health inequalities and workplace conditions may do little to improve subjective well-being and may exacerbate inequities by implicitly rewarding those who are already the healthiest. This is consistent with critiques of narrowly productivist interpretations of work-active ageing (Boudiny, 2013).

Second, the persistence of a migrant happiness advantage independent of paid work may overlook the influence of negative regional attitudes towards immigrants, which have been found to significantly decrease migrant well-being, suggesting that policy approaches should also address local social climates alongside measures such as healthcare access, social inclusion, and economic security (Šedovič, 2024). This aligns with integrative approaches to work-active ageing and with broader evidence syntheses on immigrant integration outcomes that emphasise multi-domain inclusion rather than labour-market outcomes alone.

Strengths, limitations and directions for future studies

This study contributes to the literature in three main ways. First, it poses a straightforward conceptual question: whether the “work-active immigrant” profile is primarily an outcome of employment pathways or of immigrant selection processes. It examines this using a large, cross-national, probability-based dataset. Second, it focuses explicitly on selection into late-life employment through the use of inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW), as opposed to relying solely on traditional regression methods, which aligns with established recommendations for causal inference in observational study settings (Cole and Hernán, 2008; Lai et al., 2024; Rosenbaum and Rubin, 1983). Third, the analysis reports balance diagnostics and weight trimming (99th percentile) to enhance stability and transparency, consistent with best practices in propensity-score analyses (Austin, 2009).

Employment and happiness are measured contemporaneously; reverse causation (e.g., happier individuals remaining active) cannot be ruled out. Longitudinal analyses of transitions into and out of work would provide a stronger test of whether work changes well-being beyond selection.

IPTW balances observed covariates, but causal interpretation depends on no important unmeasured confounders of paid work and happiness. We strengthened the propensity model by including education, partnership status, and multiple health indicators (beyond self-rated health), alongside age, gender, financial strain, and country fixed effects. Nevertheless, unmeasured factors (for example, detailed wealth and pension entitlements, mental health, functional capacity, job quality and voluntariness, social support, discrimination, and neighbourhood or policy contexts) may still influence both work participation and well-being. Future research could incorporate doubly robust estimators (e.g., IPTW combined with outcome regression) and sensitivity analyses to assess robustness to residual confounding.

ESS has a complex sampling design and provides survey weights. The current analysis should explicitly state whether and how survey weights were combined with propensity weights, as ignoring survey weights can affect generalizability to target populations (DuGoff, Schuler, and Stuart, 2014).

Paid work is derived from “main activity in the last 7 days” and dichotomised, which may misclassify intermittent, informal, or seasonal work and does not capture job quality, hours, or voluntariness. Health indicators in ESS are self-reported; although we included multiple health measures to reduce selection bias, residual measurement error and the lack of clinical verification remain limitations. Happiness is assessed via a single item, widely used but potentially sensitive to cross-cultural reporting differences (OECD, 2013). Future work should incorporate richer measures of work quality and voluntariness, and assess the measurement equivalence of well-being indicators across migrant and native-born groups.

Defining migrants as foreign-born is standard but broad. Without distinguishing region of origin, duration of residence, citizenship, language, or reason for migration, heterogeneity and mechanisms may be obscured. Stratified analyses by origin and duration would be particularly informative for evaluating explanations of selection and adaptation.

Finally, defining migrants as foreign-born is standard but broad. We acknowledge that without distinguishing region of origin, duration of residence, or reason for migration, important heterogeneity may be obscured. However, given the small sample size of older migrants currently in the workforce (n ≈ 93), stratifying propensity score models by origin would have yielded unstable weights and insufficient statistical power. Therefore, our estimates should be interpreted as reflecting the average experience of the migrant survivor cohort. Future research utilising larger administrative datasets would be particularly informative for evaluating how selection and adaptation mechanisms vary by migration corridor.

Conclusion

In a cross-national European sample of adults aged 65 and older, older migrants were more likely than native-born adults to report paid work as their main activity and reported higher average happiness. In naïve models, paid work was positively associated with happiness, but this association attenuated after accounting for measured selection into paid work via IPTW, consistent with healthy-worker selection. By contrast, the migrant advantage in happiness persisted after adjustment, suggesting that it is not explained by employment participation alone. Overall, the apparent work-active immigrant paradox is better understood as a product of selection processes (into both continued residence and continued work) alongside other unmeasured protective mechanisms that warrant targeted investigation.
