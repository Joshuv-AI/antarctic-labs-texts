Relief of Refractory Angina With Vericiguat
Authors: Ashen L. Vidanage, Yugang Dong, Jun Liu, Chen Liu, Jing Jing Zhao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRefractory angina represents a challenging clinical symptom, with limited therapeutic options beyond conventional antianginal therapy and revascularization.Case SummaryA 43-year-old man with a history of hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and coronary atherosclerotic heart disease presented with persistent angina pectoris, classified as Canadian Cardiovascular Society class IV, despite being on optimized antianginal therapy including calcium-channel blockers, nitrates, and other adjunctive medications. His Seattle Angina Questionnaire score was 12.61. Introduction of vericiguat 10 mg daily resulted in marked symptom improvement, with Seattle Angina Questionnaire score increasing to 57.35 and angina classification improving to class II.DiscussionThis represents to our knowledge the first documented use of vericiguat for refractory angina. Vericiguat stimulates soluble guanylate cyclase and enhances cyclic GMP production, improving endothelial dysfunction and impaired vasodilation; this may be the mechanism by which it relieves refractory angina.Take-Home MessagesVericiguat may offer therapeutic benefit in refractory angina patients unresponsive to conventional therapy. Further investigation is warranted.

History of Presentation

A 43-year-old man with established coronary disease presented with progressive chest pain over 4 years, with severe worsening during the preceding 2 months. Initial episodes were brief and infrequent, but symptoms evolved to prolonged attacks lasting 8 to 10 minutes, with a characteristic circadian pattern (5-9 am and 4-6 pm). Despite sublingual nitroglycerin, rest pain persisted for 5 to 10 minutes per episode. The patient underwent coronary angiography at the local hospital. Coronary angiography revealed complete proximal occlusion of the left anterior descending artery (LAD) at baseline, which resolved to 60% residual stenosis after administration of intracoronary nitroglycerin, confirming severe coronary vasospasm. A coexistent myocardial bridge in the mid-LAD caused additional 30% to 50% systolic compression. The left circumflex artery showed apparent 60% to 70% stenosis that completely resolved with nitroglycerin, again demonstrating vasospastic disease. The right coronary artery had mild 20% to 30% fixed stenosis with preserved TIMI flow grade 3. Initial antianginal optimization at the referring hospital included comprehensive vasospastic therapy with calcium-channel blockers (diltiazem 30 mg 3 times daily), long-acting nitrates (isosorbide mononitrate 30 mg daily), metoprolol sustained-release tablets 47.5 mg once daily, trimetazidine 20 mg 3 times a day, and standard coronary disease management. Despite this regimen, the patient experienced persistent angina at rest and with exertion. Functional capacity was severely limited, with Canadian Cardiovascular Society (CCS) class IV angina, indicating profound impairment in physical activity and angina frequency domains.

Past Medical History

The patient's medical history included class 2 hypertension (very high risk), hyperuricemia, and chronic urticaria. He had been taking irbesartan 150 mg once daily and felodipine 5 mg once daily for a long time to control blood pressure, and his blood pressure was usually stable. Type 2 diabetes mellitus was recently diagnosed, and he had been prescribed empagliflozin 10 mg once daily.

Differential Diagnosis

Initially, coronary artery spasm was considered as a potential cause of the patient's severe chest pain. However, despite daily administration of 90 mg diltiazem and nitrates, the symptoms remained incompletely relieved. This may indicate an insufficient dosage of diltiazem or a possible exacerbation of spasm owing to metoprolol use. Additionally, the patient had a history of urticaria, raising the possibility of Kounis syndrome. Although coronary angiography revealed a myocardial bridge, the degree of systolic compression appeared to be mild, making it less likely to be the primary cause of the chest pain. Given the patient's comorbidities, including hypertension and diabetes, coronary microvascular dysfunction was also considered as a potential contributor to the persistent symptoms. In addition, it was necessary to pay attention to whether the patient's chest pain was related to arrhythmia or asthma.

Investigations

Exercise stress testing demonstrated exercise-induced ischemia with limiting chest pain after 13 minutes (Figure 2). Electrocardiographic changes included significant ST-segment depression (1.0-1.7 mV) in the inferior leads and hyperacute T waves in precordial leads V2 to V6, preventing achievement of target heart rate. Additional investigations systematically excluded alternative etiologies. Echocardiography demonstrated preserved left ventricular ejection fraction with normal regional wall motion, ruling out structural heart disease or prior myocardial infarction. A 24-hour ambulatory electrocardiogram showed no significant arrhythmias, excluding rhythm disorders as a cause of chest pain. Allergy tests indicated an allergy to mites. Pulmonary function testing was normal, eliminating respiratory causes.

Management

Hospital admission permitted systematic medication optimization targeting the confirmed vasospastic pathophysiology. Diltiazem was escalated to sustained-release 270 mg daily, dosage of long-acting nitrates was increased to 60 mg daily, and nicorandil 5 mg was administered 3 times daily for enhanced antispastic efficacy. To prevent potential exacerbation of coronary artery spasm by metoprolol, ivabradine was administered as an alternative agent for heart rate control. This intensive approach achieved modest improvement in rest symptoms, permitting patient discharge.

Outpatient management focused on maximizing antispastic therapy, with nicorandil increased to 10 mg 3 times daily and diltiazem escalated to the maximum tolerated dose of 360 mg daily. External counterpulsation therapy was trialed per refractory angina guidelines but provided no clinical benefit. Despite optimized conventional therapy, the patient's Seattle Angina Questionnaire score remained severely impaired at 12.61.

Given refractory symptoms despite guideline-directed maximum therapy, vericiguat was initiated as novel off-label treatment after receiving informed consent. The medication was started at 5 mg daily, with subsequent escalation to 10 mg daily based on tolerance and clinical response.

Outcome and Follow-Up

Vericiguat demonstrated dose-dependent clinical improvement. At 5 mg daily, Seattle Angina Questionnaire score increased from 12.61 to 37.68, with notable improvements in physical activity limitation (28.89) and angina frequency (10). Escalation to 10 mg daily produced marked symptomatic relief, with Seattle Angina Questionnaire score reaching 57.35 and improvement in CCS class from IV to II.

Repeat exercise stress testing confirmed objective improvement (Figure 3). Although exercise-induced chest pain persisted, electrocardiographic changes showed less severe ischemia, with ST-segment depression of 0.1 to 1.7 mV in leads V2 to V6, replacing the previous hyperacute T-wave pattern. Discussion

This case demonstrates to our knowledge the first successful use of vericiguat for refractory angina, offering a novel therapeutic approach for patients unresponsive to conventional therapy. Refractory angina affects a clinically important subset of patients with coronary disease who remain symptomatic despite optimal medical therapy and appropriate revascularization.1 Current guidelines recommend beta-blockers, calcium-channel blockers, long-acting nitrates, ivabradine, nicorandil, and trimetazidine as evidence-based therapy, with external counterpulsation for refractory cases.1 However, some patients remain symptomatic despite maximal conventional treatment, as demonstrated in the current case.

The pathophysiology of refractory angina involves epicardial coronary obstruction, coronary spasm, and endothelial dysfunction. Comorbid diabetes and hypertension further compound endothelial dysfunction, creating a challenging therapeutic scenario. The presence of diabetes is particularly relevant, as vericiguat was reported to reduce the risk of cardiovascular death/heart failure hospitalization independent of glycemic status in the VICTORIA trial (HR: 0.92 for diabetics), suggesting broader potential in coronary disease with endothelial dysfunction.2

Vericiguat directly targets impaired NO-sGC-cGMP (nitric oxide–soluble guanylate cyclase–cyclic guanosine monophosphate) signaling by stimulating sGC, enhancing both enzyme activity and NO sensitivity under oxidative stress conditions.3 This dual mechanism restores cGMP production, improving endothelial dysfunction, vascular tone, and myocardial stiffness—key pathophysiological contributors to coronary microcirculatory abnormalities.3 These mechanistic benefits translate into improved clinical outcomes, including a significant reduction in the composite risk of cardiovascular death or heart failure hospitalization, as demonstrated in the VICTORIA trial for patients with reduced ejection fraction who remained symptomatic despite standard treatments.4

Preclinical studies have demonstrated vericiguat's ability to improve myocardial microcirculation, increase adenosine triphosphate activity, and enhance endothelial antioxidant response in ischemia-reperfusion models.5 Clinical evidence includes microcirculation improvement in Fabry disease,6 providing translational support for its coronary microcirculatory benefits in refractory angina.

The marked clinical improvement seen in our patient, as evidenced by the increase in Seattle Angina Questionnaire score from 12.61 to 57.35 and improvement in CCS class from IV to II, provides compelling evidence that vericiguat's restoration of impaired NO-sGC-cGMP signaling may be particularly beneficial in refractory angina with coronary spasm and endothelial dysfunction. This magnitude of improvement exceeds typical responses to conventional antianginal therapy and represents clinically meaningful enhancement in quality of life. The patient's clinical improvement was supported by improvement in exercise tolerance, as demonstrated by modified stress testing, as well as by substantial reduction in daily symptoms.

Real-world vericiguat use in heart failure demonstrates feasibility in reaching target doses alongside other guideline-directed medical therapy,7 and meta-analyses of randomized controlled trials confirm its safety and tolerability, with selective benefits in physical functioning,8 providing reassurance for off-label use in this challenging patient population. This is to our knowledge the first documented application in refractory angina, and it provides compelling preliminary evidence that warrants systematic investigation through randomized controlled trials.

Conclusions

This case demonstrates the significant therapeutic benefit of vericiguat in refractory angina unresponsive to maximal conventional therapy. The objective improvement in validated outcome measures provides compelling evidence for this novel approach, particularly in patients with coronary spasm and endothelial dysfunction. Limitations include the single-case design and need for long-term safety and efficacy data. Randomized controlled trials are essential to validate these promising findings and establish evidence-based recommendations for vericiguat in refractory angina management.
