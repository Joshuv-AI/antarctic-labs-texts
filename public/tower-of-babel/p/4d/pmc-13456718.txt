Male breast carcinoma in a 45-year-old without known risk factors: case report and literature review
Authors: Salina Subedi, Sushan Ghimire, Sudeep Regmi, Rishi Sherchan, Lavika Lakhanpal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction:Male breast carcinoma is an uncommon malignancy, accounting for approximately 1% of all breast neoplasms. Most cases usually occur after the age of 60 and often present in advanced stages.Case presentation:This is a case report of a 45-year-old male who presented with a lump in his left breast along with left axillary lymphadenopathy, without any known risk factors. Fine needle aspiration cytology of the lesion showed malignant epithelial cells consistent with ductal carcinoma. Core needle biopsy was advised; however, the patient underwent a left modified radical mastectomy at another center. Histopathological examination confirmed invasive ductal carcinoma. He received adjuvant chemotherapy and is currently undergoing adjuvant endocrine therapy. He is now clinically stable, with no evidence of local recurrence or distant metastasis 11 months after chemotherapy, and continues regular oncological follow-up.Clinical discussion:Male breast carcinoma usually occurs in the elderly population and is frequently associated with genetic and hormonal risk factors. Diagnosis relies on clinical assessment, imaging, and histopathological confirmation. A brief review of previously reported cases demonstrates invasive ductal carcinoma as the most common histological subtype.Conclusion:Breast carcinoma should always be considered as one of the differential diagnoses for a male breast lump. Prompt evaluation with appropriate imaging and tissue diagnosis is essential for timely management and improved outcomes.

Introduction

Male breast carcinoma accounts for less than 0.6% of all breast carcinoma and less than 1% of all carcinoma in men[1]. It is typically diagnosed in older individuals after the age of sixty[2]. Some studies have reported a modest increase in its incidence over recent decades[3]. It is often diagnosed at an advanced stage compared to women because of its rarity and poor awareness[4,5]. Bilateral forms of male breast carcinoma are much rarer than unilateral cases[6]. Male breast carcinoma in young individuals without any identifiable risk factors is uncommon and, therefore, may pose a diagnostic challenge[7].

Here, we present a case of invasive breast carcinoma in a 45-year-old man without known risk factors, highlighting the clinical presentation, diagnostic evaluation, and comparison with existing literature.

This case report has been presented in accordance with the SCARE checklist[8].

Case details

A 45-year-old male presented to the general surgery outpatient clinic with a gradually progressive, painless lump in the left breast, self-discovered 4 months ago while bathing. The lesion began as a small, discrete nodule and progressively increased in size. There was no associated pain, nipple discharge, nipple inversion, or overlying skin changes such as ulceration, erythema, or peau d’orange. There was no fever, anorexia, unintentional weight loss, bone pain, respiratory symptoms, or history of trauma. He had a history of type 2 diabetes mellitus and hypertension for 5 years, for which he was on regular medication. He had no history of smoking, alcohol consumption, anabolic steroid use, or prior radiation exposure. He worked as a farmer and had a body mass index of 19 kg/m2.

Examination revealed a hard, immobile left breast lump approximately 2 cm × 3 cm around the peri-areolar region at the 12 o’clock position, with an irregular border and uneven surface, without any nipple discharge or retraction. Left axillary lymphadenopathy was present. The contralateral breast and axilla were normal.

Ultrasonography of the bilateral breasts, axilla, and chest wall showed an ill-defined, taller-than-wide heterogeneous lesion with an irregular margin in the fibroglandular plane of the left breast at the 12 o’clock position in the retro-areolar region, suspicious for malignancy. No enlarged lymph nodes were identified.

Fine needle aspiration cytology (FNAC) of the left breast lump was positive for malignancy, showing features consistent with ductal carcinoma, and was categorized as category 5 – malignant according to the IAC Yokohama System for Reporting Breast FNAC (Fig. 1). In view of these findings, a core needle biopsy was advised for tissue-based diagnosis, grading, and receptor status evaluation. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography of the chest and abdomen showed a heterogeneously enhancing soft tissue density lesion involving the left breast along with spiculated margins (Fig. 2) without evidence of distant metastasis. The differential diagnosis included gynecomastia, lipoma, breast abscess, epidermal inclusion cyst, and metastatic lesions. Gynecomastia was unlikely due to the presence of a discrete, firm, unilateral mass rather than diffuse glandular enlargement. Lipoma and epidermal inclusion cyst were excluded based on their solid, irregular appearance on ultrasonography. Breast abscess and metastatic disease were considered unlikely due to the absence of any signs of inflammation and the lack of a known primary malignancy.

Although a core needle biopsy was advised, the patient sought to obtain a second opinion at an external surgical facility, where he underwent definitive management. After appropriate pre-operative evaluation and counseling, a modified radical mastectomy of the left breast with left axillary lymph node dissection of levels I–III was performed. Intraoperatively, a 3 cm × 2 cm hard mass was identified within the breast parenchyma, which was then excised and sent for histopathological examination. The patient tolerated the procedure well and had an uneventful post-operative recovery.

Histopathological examination showed invasive breast carcinoma of no special type, grade 2, involving the left breast. All 17 examined axillary lymph nodes were negative for metastatic involvement (0/17), corresponding to a pathological stage of pT2N0. Immunohistochemistry demonstrated estrogen receptor (ER) positivity in 90% of tumor cells and progesterone receptor (PR) positivity in 20%, with HER2/neu expression scored as 1+ (negative) and a Ki-67 proliferation index of 20% (Fig. 3). Based on the American Joint Committee on Cancer TNM Staging System, Eighth Edition, the tumor was classified as an anatomic stage IIA; pathological stage IIA with a favorable hormone receptor profile (ER/PR-positive, HER2/neu-negative), corresponding to prognostic stage group IIA. Patient was then started on adjuvant chemotherapy with a doxorubicin, cyclophosphamide, and paclitaxel-based regimen for a total of eight cycles. After the completion of the first cycle, he continued the remaining cycles of chemotherapy at our center.

The patient is clinically stable, with no evidence of locoregional recurrence or distant metastasis at 11 months of follow-up. He is currently on adjuvant endocrine therapy with Tamoxifen 20 mg. Continuous surveillance has been planned with periodic clinical evaluation and imaging, in accordance with American Society of Clinical Oncology (ASCO) breast cancer follow-up guidelines.

Discussion

Male breast carcinoma remains an uncommon malignancy that typically presents in older individuals[2]. Several risk factors for developing male breast carcinoma have been identified. Genetic predisposition, particularly germline mutations in BRCA2 and, less commonly, BRCA1, represents one of the most significant risk factors. Additional risk factors include a family history of breast cancer, advancing age, chronic liver disease, Klinefelter syndrome, obesity, and exposure to ionizing radiation[7,9–11].

The pathogenesis of male breast carcinoma is believed to involve conditions that alter the estrogen-to-androgen ratio, as seen in established risk factors. When estrogenic activity exceeds that of androgen, the rudimentary ductal epithelium is rendered susceptible to malignant transformation as it undergoes proliferation. Germline mutations in DNA base repair genes like BRCA2, BRCA1, PALB2, CHEK2, and ATM also increase susceptibility to breast cancer. At the molecular level, the majority of cases are positive for the estrogen receptor, directly implicating estrogen-driven transcription in tumor initiation and progression[12–14].

Patients with male breast carcinoma usually present with a painless subareolar mass, often discovered incidentally. In more advanced stages, patients typically present with nipple discharge, which can be either bloody or serous[6,7]. The chest wall is more frequently involved due to the smaller volume of breast tissue in men[4]. Histologically, invasive ductal carcinoma is the predominant subtype, as lobular structures are limited in development in the male breast[5]. Male breast carcinoma patients tend to be positive for estrogen and progesterone receptor expression[15].

Diagnosis of male breast carcinoma follows a structured multimodal pathway and is similar to female breast carcinoma. Initial evaluation begins with clinical examination, followed by breast ultrasonography and mammography wherever appropriate. Fine needle aspiration cytology could be used as an initial assessment tool[16]. However, tissue diagnosis with core needle biopsy is the gold standard, as it allows histological classification and receptor status evaluation essential for planning a treatment strategy[17]. Staging with cross-sectional imaging, such as computed tomography, is performed to assess locoregional and distant metastasis[7]. ASCO recommends offering genetic testing to all males presenting with breast carcinoma, regardless of their family history[18].

Management is mainly extrapolated from knowledge about female breast carcinoma[19]. It remains guided by staging and tumor biology, and surgery is still the gold standard[20]. Modified radical mastectomy is the most common surgical procedure performed in male breast carcinoma[5] with or without neoadjuvant or adjuvant systemic therapy[21]. Increased use of gene expression testing has allowed targeted therapy, such as trastuzumab for HER2/neu-positive patients, and tamoxifen and aromatase inhibitors like anastrozole and letrozole for ER/PR-positive patients[5,18,19,22].

A focused review of published literature on male breast carcinoma indicates that most patients present at a median age of 60–70 years, significantly older than the patient described in this case report. Several of them were also associated with a spectrum of risk factors, including hormonal and genetic influences. A minor proportion of these cases occur sporadically without known risk factors.

In our case, a 45-year-old male presented with a unilateral breast lump without known risk factors. FNAC showed malignant epithelial cells consistent with breast carcinoma. Core needle biopsy was advised for definitive histological assessment and receptor status determination. However, the diagnosis was made only after histopathological examination of the tissue sample obtained following a modified radical mastectomy of the left breast, which confirmed invasive ductal carcinoma of no special type. This case suggests that male breast carcinoma can occur sporadically outside the typical risk profile described in the literature, possibly driven by unrecognized genetic or hormonal alterations that are not clinically apparent.

Strengths of this report include the availability of imaging, FNAC cytology, and histopathology images to support the diagnosis of male breast carcinoma with early presentation in a relatively young patient without known risk factors. However, as a single case report, the findings have limited generalizability. Core needle biopsy was not performed prior to definitive surgery, which limits histopathological correlation and pre-operative receptor status assessment. Also, follow-up duration remains relatively short, and long-term outcomes beyond the current follow-up are not available. Genetic testing could not be offered in this case due to limitations of resources.

Conclusion

Male breast carcinoma, though rare, should always be considered in men presenting with breast lumps, regardless of age. Increased awareness among clinicians and patients can lead to earlier detection and improved outcomes.
