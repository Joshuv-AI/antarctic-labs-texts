Feasibility assessment of a multiple logistic regression model utilizing serum FT3, FT4, and TSH to predict prognosis of pulmonary infection in elderly patients
Authors: Jian Zhang, Luxue Zhang, Quanrui Li, Xiaokang Fu, Geng Tian
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNumerous studies have established a strong link between thyroid hormones and thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) and their prognostic significance in critical illness. This study aimed to evaluate whether serum FT3, FT4, and TSH levels are independently associated with prognosis in elderly patients with pulmonary infection and to determine whether a combined logistic regression model improves prognostic accuracy.MethodsAn observational study was conducted on 362 elderly patients hospitalized for pulmonary infection. The patients were categorized into two groups based on their prognosis: a mortality group (35 cases) and a survival group (327 cases). Serum levels of FT3, FT4, and TSH were measured, and their associations with prognosis were evaluated. Multiple logistic regression analysis was performed to determine the independent contributions of each hormone. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was utilized to establish optimal thresholds and assess the predictive power of the hormones both individually and as a combined prediction model.ResultsThe study revealed significant positive correlations between serum levels of FT3, FT4, and TSH and the prognosis of pulmonary infection. Each hormone exhibited a favorable area under the curve (AUC) value in the ROC analysis, suggesting their potential as prognostic markers. Optimal thresholds for FT3, FT4, and TSH were determined, providing cutoff values for risk assessment. Moreover, multiple logistic regression analysis demonstrated that all three hormones independently contributed to the prognostic significance. Integrating FT3, FT4, and TSH levels in a combined prediction model improved the accuracy of prognosis.ConclusionsElevated serum levels of FT3, FT4, and TSH are associated with a more favorable prognosis in elderly patients after pulmonary infection. Background

With the rapidly aging global population, optimizing health outcomes for older adults is crucial [1]. The global population is experiencing a notable surge in aging trends: in 1950, no country had more than 11% of its population aged 65 or older. However, by 2000, the highest percentage reached 18%, and projections indicate that this figure will soar to 38% by 2050 [2]. This demographic shift highlights the urgent need to address the health challenges associated with aging. The aging process often results in a loss of physical integrity, leading to a heightened risk of mortality and vulnerability to a variety of age-related diseases, including neurodegenerative disorders, cardiovascular disease, osteoporosis, sarcopenia, pulmonary infections, and cancer [3]. Respiratory infections, which are among the top causes of death globally, particularly affect individuals over 65 years due to the severity of the infections this age group experiences [4]. Age-related structural alterations in lung function reduce the capacity of older individuals to combat lung infections [5]. The escalating incidence of respiratory infections among the elderly is becoming an increasingly unaddressed healthcare concern. This demographic faces unique challenges due to a combination of factors such as multiple co-existing health conditions, diminished physiological resilience, and an aging immune system [6]. Furthermore, their non-standard symptom presentation can result in delayed diagnoses, leading to postponed treatment initiation, extended hospital stays, increased mortality rates, and potential further hospital-acquired disease transmission [7]. Thus, it is crucial to comprehend the impact of respiratory infections on the elderly population to meet the clinical demand for enhanced diagnostic methods and treatment plans [8, 9].

It’s essential to identify dependable prognostic indicators that can facilitate early interventions and refine treatment approaches to accurately predict the progression of lung infections in older individuals [10]. Recent research has highlighted the potential role of thyroid hormones in pulmonary infections, particularly in light of the COVID-19 pandemic [11, 12]. Thyroid hormones, including free triiodothyronine (FT3), free thyroxine (FT4), and thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH), are believed to exert influence on immune function and undergo changes during infections and critical illnesses [13, 14]. Notably, these hormones impact the respiratory center’s ability to clear mucus and secretions, which can exacerbate symptoms and complicate the body’s defense against infection [15]. As such, monitoring thyroid function in conjunction with other diagnostic measures could prove invaluable for healthcare professionals managing patients with lung infections.

While previous research has explored the association between individual thyroid hormones and infections, the effectiveness of a combined model using FT3, FT4, and TSH to predict outcomes in elderly patients with lung infections remains largely unknown [16, 17]. This study sough to fill this gap by utilizing a technique known as multiple logistic regression. This method enables the simultaneous analysis of all three thyroid hormones, potentially leading to a more precise prediction of their impact on prognosis. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the prognostic value of serum FT3, FT4, and TSH levels in elderly patients with pulmonary infection using a multiple logistic regression model, specifically addressing whether FT3, FT4, and TSH levels are independently associated with prognosis and whether a combined predictive model incorporating all three hormones can enhance prognostic accuracy compared to each marker alone.

Methods

Study design and patient selection

A retrospective analysis was conducted involving 362 elderly patients who were treated at our hospital between January 2020 and May 2022. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Xuanwu Hospital, Capital Medical University (Approval Number: LYS[2025] 285-001). The study employed a retrospective cohort design, enabling the comparison of clinical and biochemical indicators between distinct prognostic outcomes. The inclusion criteria for this study included patients elderly 60 years and above who presented with respiratory symptoms such as cough, sputum production, fever, and mild dyspnea. Outpatient chest X-ray examinations were performed on these patients, revealing pulmonary infection. Despite administering oral antibiotic treatment, their symptoms did not significantly improve. The identification of pulmonary infection in our study was guided by the Diagnosis and Treatment Guidelines for Community-Acquired Pneumonia, published by the Respiratory Disease Branch of the Chinese Medical Association [18]. Complete thyroid function test results were available to ensure comprehensive clinical data. To minimize confounding effects, patients with comorbidities or treatment histories known to independently affect thyroid function or pulmonary outcomes were excluded. Patients with a history of prolonged corticosteroid use, chronic renal insufficiency, and systemic diseases affecting the heart, brain, lungs, kidneys, or blood were excluded from the study. Patients with missing data or those who passed away during treatment were also excluded. Rigorous inclusion and exclusion criteria were implemented in this study to ensure the careful selection of subjects and data accuracy and reliability. To ensure the integrity of retrospective data, all clinical records were independently reviewed by two trained investigators. Any discrepancies were resolved by consensus or third-party adjudication. Furthermore, data entry was double-checked to avoid transcription errors. The timing of chest imaging and laboratory testing was standardized to occur within 24 h of hospital admission or presentation, ensuring consistency in disease stage at baseline. Moreover, clinical evaluators were blinded to patient outcomes at the time of data collection to reduce bias. Data collection occurred when patients’ conditions were stable, or their family members were willing to cooperate. This study was approved by the ethics committee of Xuanwu Hospital Capital Medical University, China (Ethics number XWLL-2020-01−05). The pulmonary infections included in this study were classified as acute community-acquired infections, based on the duration of symptoms (< 4 weeks) and clinical presentation. Sputum cultures and laboratory findings available in patients’ records indicated that the most frequently identified pathogens were Streptococcus pneumoniae, Haemophilus influenzae, and Klebsiella pneumoniae. In some cases, Pseudomonas aeruginosa and atypical organisms such as Mycoplasma pneumoniae were also detected.

Analytical techniques

A fully automated chemiluminescent immunoassay analyzer (ADVIA Centaur, Bayer HealthCare) was used with standardized reagent kits validated for clinical use. Calibration and internal quality control were performed daily in accordance with the manufacturer’s specifications and national laboratory standards. All assays were run in duplicate to reduce technical variability, and inter-assay and intra-assay coefficients of variation (CV) were maintained below 10%. Each participant provided a 5 mL fasting venous blood sample, collected between 7:00 and 9:00 AM to minimize diurnal variation. Blood samples were stored at 4 °C immediately after collection and centrifuged within 2 h (3,000 rpm for 10 min) to ensure sample stability before biochemical analysis. Internal quality control procedures were strictly followed during the laboratory analyses. All measurements were performed in a single laboratory using the same instruments and reagents to ensure consistency across cases. Assays were validated according to manufacturer guidelines and national clinical laboratory standards.

Outcome measures and categorization

Patients were monitored for 28 days from the time of initial diagnosis and treatment initiation. The primary outcome was 28-day all-cause mortality attributable to pulmonary infection. Based on their prognosis, the patients were categorized into the mortality group (35 cases) and the survival group (327 cases). In this context, mortality refers to deaths resulting from the pulmonary infection. The primary objective of this study was to assess and compare the serum levels of FT3, FT4, and TSH among different prognostic groups. Additionally, it aimed to examine the correlation between each indicator and prognosis and employ receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves to evaluate the clinical effectiveness of serum FT3, FT4, and TSH levels in predicting the prognosis of elderly patients with pulmonary infection. No external control group was used; instead, comparisons were made between prognostically defined subgroups (mortality vs. survival), which served as an internal stratification based on clinical outcome. Potential confounders, such as age, sex, baseline comorbidities (e.g., diabetes, hypertension), and infection severity (e.g., presence of sepsis or hypoxemia) were considered in multivariate regression modeling to control for their effects on outcomes. Furthermore, multiple logistic regression models were employed to enhance the predictive accuracy of clinical prognosis. All outcomes were independently verified using medical records and death certificates. To ensure consistent categorization, two investigators blinded to thyroid hormone levels independently assigned patients to the survival or mortality group. Missing outcome data were screened and addressed through complete-case analysis; no imputation was performed.

Statistical analysis

The research data underwent statistical analysis using SPSS 25.0 software. Prior to analysis, all continuous variables were assessed for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test to guide the appropriate selection of parametric or nonparametric tests. Variables with skewed distributions were log-transformed where necessary. For normally distributed numerical data, the independent t-test was used to compare groups, while the Mann–Whitney U test was applied for non-normally distributed data. Categorical variables were analyzed using the chi-square test; however, when expected cell counts were below 5, Fisher’s exact test was used instead. Quantitative variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (mean ± SD), and categorical variables were expressed as percentages. A significance level of α = 0.05 was used for all statistical tests. The relationship between serum FT3, FT4, and TSH levels and the prognosis of pulmonary infection in elderly patients was evaluated using Spearman correlation analysis. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were generated to assess the prognostic performance of FT3, FT4, and TSH levels, with the area under the curve (AUC), 95% confidence intervals, sensitivity, and specificity reported. To statistically compare the differences in AUCs among the ROC curves of FT3, FT4, and TSH, DeLong’s test was applied. Variables with p-values < 0.10 in univariate analysis, or those with established clinical relevance (e.g., age, sex, comorbidities), were included in the multivariate logistic regression model. The distribution of age and BMI was presented for descriptive purposes to provide a clearer picture of the study population’s demographic characteristics. These variables, although not included as covariates in the multivariable logistic regression model, were considered to ensure that the groups were comparable in terms of general demographic factors. Since no significant differences were found in the comparison of age and BMI between the mortality and survival groups (P > 0.05), these factors were not included in the subsequent multivariate analyses. Multicollinearity among independent variables was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF), with a VIF > 5 indicating collinearity. The Hosmer–Lemeshow test was performed to evaluate the goodness-of-fit of the final logistic regression model. Statistical significance was defined as a p-value less than 0.05.

Results

Demographic comparison between the two groups

The demographic characteristics of the two groups were compared and analyzed (Table 1). The mortality group consisted of 18 males and 17 females, with an average age of 69.54 ± 6.28 years. The body mass index (BMI) for this group had an average of 23.79 ± 2.51 kg/m2. On the other hand, the survival group included 175 males and 152 females, with an average age of 70.31 ± 7.45 years. The BMI for individuals in this group had an average of 23.68 ± 2.44 kg/m2. Statistical analysis revealed no significant differences between the two groups in terms of sex (χ2 = 0.055, P = 0.814), age (t = 0.101, P = 0.919), and BMI (t = 0.253, P = 0.801) (Figs. 1 and 2).

Analysis of serum FT3, FT4, and TSH levels

Figure 3 presents a comparison of serum levels between the two groups. The mortality group exhibited significantly lower levels of FT3 (2.17 ± 0.28 vs. 3.82 ± 0.41, t = 23.221, P < 0.001), FT4 (12.12 ± 1.68 vs. 17.68 ± 1.45, t = 21.220, P < 0.001), and TSH (1.71 ± 0.18 vs. 2.11 ± 0.25, t = 9.208, P < 0.001) compared to the survival group. These findings suggest a potential association between FT3, FT4, and TSH serum levels and patient prognosis, highlighting the clinical relevance of thyroid hormone measurements in prognostic evaluation.

Predictive value of serum FT3, FT4, and TSH levels in the prognosis of elderly patients with pulmonary infection

This study employed ROC analysis to evaluate the prognostic significance of serum levels of FT3, FT4, and TSH in elderly patients with pulmonary infection. The results indicated that FT3 had an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.742 and a Youden’s index of 0.459. The optimal threshold for FT3 was determined to be 2.43 pmol/L, corresponding to a sensitivity of 0.744 and a specificity of 0.686. Using a cut-off value below 2.43 pmol/L for FT3, there was a 74% probability of accurately identifying the mortality risk. Similarly, FT4 exhibited an AUC of 0.759, with a Youden’s index of 0.459. The optimal threshold for FT4 was calculated as 13.18 pmol/L, resulting in a sensitivity of 0.774 and a specificity of 0.686. By employing a threshold lower than 13.18 pmol/L for FT4, there was a 76% likelihood of accurately identifying the mortality risk. Additionally, TSH demonstrated an AUC of 0.716 and a Youden’s index of 0.372. The optimal threshold for TSH was determined to be 1.63 mIU/L, yielding a sensitivity of 0.829 and a specificity of 0.543. A threshold below 1.63 mIU/L for TSH conferred a 72% probability of accurately identifying the mortality risk (Table 3). DeLong’s test revealed that the AUC of FT4 was significantly higher than that of TSH (P = 0.033), while the difference between FT4 and FT3 was not statistically significant (P = 0.218). Similarly, FT3 showed a significantly greater AUC compared to TSH (P = 0.045), suggesting that FT3 and FT4 are more accurate than TSH in predicting mortality among elderly patients with pulmonary infection.

Discussion

Elderly patients hospitalized for various reasons often undergo procedures like tracheal intubation, mechanical ventilation, and suctioning, which can disrupt their natural defenses [19]. This weakens their immune response and nutritional status, increasing the risk of pulmonary infections [20, 21]. Furthermore, age-related decline in organ function can impair the cough reflex and ciliary movement in the airways, hindering the clearance of mucus and secretions [22, 23]. This can lead to bacterial colonization and accumulation of sputum, potentially allowing bacteria to enter the lung tissue and cause infection [24, 25]. These pulmonary infections can trigger complications like multi-organ dysfunction, significantly impacting patient prognosis [26]. Therefore, accurate prediction of such complications and identification of high-risk individuals are crucial for better management and intervention. This study investigates the potential of serum FT3, FT4, and TSH levels as prognostic markers for pulmonary infections in elderly patients.

T3 and T4 are vital hormones produced by the thyroid gland. Once they enter the bloodstream, they bind to proteins. However, this binding renders them biologically inactive, which means that their levels do not accurately reflect thyroid function [27]. The active portion of these hormones in circulation is represented by the unbound fractions called free T3 (FT3) and free T4 (FT4). Importantly, FT3 and FT4 maintain relative stability with T3 and T4 in the blood, allowing for the preservation of normal physiological function [28]. To effectively assess the state of thyroid function, it is necessary to monitor the serum levels of FT3 and FT4. These levels serve as reliable indicators because they are not affected by iodine-containing impurities or the concentration of thyroxine-binding globulin in the blood [29]. This makes FT3 and FT4 highly sensitive and specific markers for evaluating thyroid function. Thyroid hormones, including FT3 and FT4, exert widespread systemic effects that are critical to maintaining homeostasis. They regulate basal metabolic rate, modulate the cardiovascular and respiratory systems, and influence immune responses and thermoregulation. In particular, FT3 is known to enhance cardiac output, improve oxygen consumption, and stimulate mitochondrial activity, while FT4 serves as its prohormone. TSH, secreted by the anterior pituitary, modulates the synthesis and release of these hormones through the hypothalamic-pituitary-thyroid axis. In the context of infection and critical illness, alterations in thyroid hormone levels, especially non-thyroidal illness syndrome, can reflect the body’s adaptive or maladaptive response to stress. Therefore, measuring FT3, FT4, and TSH may provide insight into systemic physiological derangements and serve as valuable biomarkers for prognostic assessment in elderly patients with pulmonary infection. Thyroid hormone alterations in patients with pulmonary infection are not only associated with acute inflammatory responses, but are also detected in chronic diseases, such as COPD, where systemic inflammation and oxygen desaturation can influence thyroid function. This study aimed to build on previous research that has shown thyroid hormone disturbances in chronic inflammatory diseases, such as COPD, which could exacerbate infection risk and prognosis.

Lui et al. observed a negative correlation between alveolar oxygen pressure and serum thyroid hormone levels in chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) patients. Specifically, hypothyroidism was associated with an inhibited ventilation response to hypoxia. This phenomenon could lead to excessive ventilation, potentially compromising the immune system and increasing susceptibility to pulmonary infections [30, 31]. In alignment with their findings, our study also demonstrated a significant positive correlation between the levels of serum FT3, FT4, and TSH, and the prognosis of pulmonary infection in elderly patients. These thyroid hormones showed promising AUC values in the ROC analysis, suggesting their potential utility as prognostic indicators. We were able to establish optimal thresholds for FT3, FT4, and TSH, offering clinically useful cutoff values for risk assessment purposes. In addition, thyroid hormone levels have been shown to fluctuate in the acute phase of infections and chronic illnesses, further highlighting their importance as part of the body’s overall stress response mechanism. The relationship between thyroid hormones and prognosis in critical conditions, such as COPD exacerbations, provides a basis for understanding the role of thyroid function in infections like those observed in our study.

Serum total T3 and FT3 levels showed a significant decrease in stable COPD patients when compared to control subjects. Furthermore, during the exacerbation phase of COPD, there was a more decrease in serum TSH levels, in addition to the already observed decrease in total T3 and FT3 levels [32]. Additionally, the study revealed significant decreases in serum total T3, FT3 levels, and TT3/TT4 ratio in severe COPD patients when compared to those with mild-to-moderate COPD. Moreover, in the stable COPD group, there were notable positive correlations between PaO2 levels and both serum total T3 and TT3/TT4 ratio [32]. Furthermore, Guan et al. employed logistic regression analyses and reported a significant association between frailty and subclinical hypothyroidism, higher TSH levels, lower FT3 levels, and a decreased FT3/FT4 ratio among community-dwelling oldest-old individuals [33]. These findings in frail elderly populations may provide additional context for the present study, as frailty is a key risk factor for both mortality and the severity of pulmonary infections. The presence of hypothyroidism, as indicated by low FT3 or high TSH levels, could be an important marker for poor prognosis in these patients.

In our study, FT3 showed an AUC value of 0.742, indicating a reasonable predictive power. With a sensitivity of 0.744 and a specificity of 0.686, a threshold below 2.43 pmol/L for FT3 accurately identified the risk of mortality in 74% of cases. Similarly, FT4 displayed an AUC value of 0.759, with a sensitivity of 0.774 and a specificity of 0.686. Using a threshold below 13.18 pmol/L for FT4 resulted in a 76% likelihood of accurately identifying the risk of mortality. Although TSH had a slightly lower AUC value of 0.716, it exhibited a high sensitivity of 0.829 and a specificity of 0.543. Employing a threshold below 1.63 mIU/L for TSH provided a 72% probability of accurately identifying the risk of mortality. These results highlight the potential of these thyroid hormones as prognostic markers in assessing the risk of mortality. While age and BMI were initially shown in Figs. 1 and 2, they were not included in the multivariate logistic regression analysis as no significant differences were observed between the two groups. This suggests that, within the scope of this study, age and BMI do not appear to significantly influence the prognosis of elderly patients with pulmonary infection. However, future studies will explore these factors in more depth, potentially with larger sample sizes or different patient populations, to further clarify their role. Figures 1 and 2 present the distribution of age and BMI across the two groups. While these variables did not show significant differences in relation to patient outcomes, their inclusion as supplementary data highlights the importance of considering these factors in epidemiological studies. The lack of significant findings supports the notion that factors such as thyroid hormone levels, rather than demographic variables, such as age and BMI, may offer more relevant prognostic insights in this patient cohort.

Furthermore, our multiple logistic regression analysis demonstrated that serum levels of FT3, FT4, and TSH collectively contributed to the prognostic significance of pulmonary infection in elderly patients. Each hormone exhibited a significant positive association with the prognosis, emphasizing their individual contributions. Importantly, the absence of multicollinearity concerns, as indicated by the acceptable VIF values, validated the independent influence of each hormone. Additionally, the integration of FT3, FT4, and TSH levels in a combined prediction model significantly enhances the accuracy of prognosis in elderly patients with pulmonary infection, as evidenced by an AUC value of 0.890 and a Youden index of 0.74. These findings underscore the importance of considering all three thyroid hormones in improving predictive capabilities.

However, while our findings revealed statistically significant associations between lower levels of FT3, FT4, and TSH and increased mortality in elderly patients with pulmonary infection, these relationships should be interpreted with caution. The observational nature of this study did not allow for causal inference, and it remains unclear whether alterations in thyroid hormone levels are a cause or a consequence of critical illness. Furthermore, thyroid function may be influenced by multiple confounding factors, such as nutritional status, concurrent medications, non-thyroidal illness syndrome, or underlying endocrine disorders that were not fully controlled for in our analysis. Although the regression model demonstrated promising predictive performance, its application in clinical practice requires external validation in larger, prospective cohorts. The diagnostic utility of FT3, FT4, and TSH as independent prognostic markers remains limited by their modest specificity and AUC values. Hence, these biomarkers should be considered as part of a broader clinical and biochemical assessment, rather than standalone predictors. Future studies should aim to explore the temporal dynamics of thyroid hormone changes during the course of pulmonary infection, clarify underlying pathophysiological mechanisms, and determine whether therapeutic modulation of thyroid function could improve clinical outcomes.

The strengths of this study lie in its comprehensive evaluation of multiple thyroid hormones and their collective prognostic value in pulmonary infection. However, it is essential to acknowledge several limitations. Firstly, this study was observational in nature, preventing the establishment of causality. Secondly, the generalization of results to other populations necessitates further investigation beyond the scope of elderly patients. Thirdly, the relatively small sample size warrants cautious interpretation of the findings. Lastly, future studies should consider additional factors such as comorbidities and medication use to better comprehend their potential influence on the prognostic significance of these thyroid hormones.

Conclusions

Our study elucidates the correlation between elevated levels of FT3, FT4, and TSH in elderly patients and favorable prognosis subsequent to pulmonary infection. These thyroid hormones exhibit potential as valuable markers for risk assessment. The incorporation of FT3, FT4, and TSH levels within a combined prediction model substantially amplifies prognostic precision. Nevertheless, additional investigations employing larger and more diverse cohorts are imperative to validate our findings and acquire a comprehensive comprehension of the underlying mechanisms connecting thyroid hormones to outcomes of pulmonary infections.
