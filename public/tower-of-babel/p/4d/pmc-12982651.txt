Enteric nervous system in exercise physiology: a microbiota-neural interface
Authors: Hui-Ling Chen, Jia-Ting Huang, Jian-Jun Guo, Li-Shun Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractExercise responses vary widely among individuals, yet the biological basis of this variability remains poorly understood. Microbiota-derived metabolites operate on timescales of hours to days, making them insufficient to explain rapid gastrointestinal and performance changes that emerge within minutes of exercise. We propose that the enteric nervous system (ENS) fills this regulatory gap by integrating mechanical, immune, and microbial signals in real time. We review evidence that the ENS modulates gut motility, barrier function, and microbial ecology during exercise, engages in bidirectional crosstalk with the microbiota, and relays gut-derived signals to muscle and brain via neural and humoral routes. We further introduce the concept of neuro-enteric phenotypes to account for inter-individual differences in exercise tolerance and adaptation.Subject terms: Gastroenterology, Microbiology, Neuroscience, Physiology

Introduction

Regular physical activity improves metabolic health1, immune competence2, and cognitive function across the lifespan3. Nonetheless, responses to exercise are highly variable4. Individuals following similar exercise often differ substantially in cardiorespiratory gains, metabolic adaptation, gastrointestinal tolerance, and changes in gut microbial composition5,6. While such variability is well recognized, its biological underpinnings remain incompletely understood.

Recently, attention has increasingly focused on the gut microbiota as a contributor to exercise responsiveness. Both animal and human studies have linked baseline microbial composition and metabolic capacity to training outcomes7–9, and microbial metabolites such as short-chain fatty acids, bile acids, and tryptophan-derived compounds have been shown to influence host metabolism and muscle adaptation8,10–12. These findings provide a persuasive explanation for longer-term training effects. However, they are less informative with respect to the rapid physiological changes that arise during exercise bouts, when symptoms and performance limitations can emerge within minutes. This discrepancy suggests that additional regulatory layers are likely involved.

Notably, the enteric nervous system (ENS), a complex intrinsic neuronal network often referred to as the “second brain”, is both anatomically and functionally positioned to provide such a route13. By integrating mechanical, chemical and autonomic signals, the ENS regulates gut motility, secretion and local immune response. These regulatory functions directly influence the luminal environment and the microbial communities it sustains14–17. Here, we review emerging evidence that positions the ENS as a dynamic interface mediating exercise and the gut microbiota.

Limitations of metabolite-centric models in explaining rapid exercise responses

Gut microbial metabolites like short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), secondary bile acids, and tryptophan-derived compounds, drive chronic exercise adaptations (improved insulin sensitivity, enhanced lipid oxidation and skeletal muscle mitochondrial function) via systemic signaling over hours to days18–23. These effects are generally attributed to systemic metabolite signaling that influences peripheral tissues through transcriptional and metabolic reprogramming. However, such metabolite-centric models largely operate on relatively slow timescales24,25, as circulating microbial metabolites typically rise hours after exercise and exert functional effects over days to weeks of training26,27.

This temporal framework fails to account for several well-documented features of exercise responses that emerge rapidly during or immediately after exercise28. Yet high-intensity exercise triggers GI symptoms and measurable barrier disruption (including increases in zonulin and lactulose:mannitol ratio) within 30–60 min-too fast for metabolite kinetics29–31. Even fit individuals with similar microbiota profiles show divergent tolerance, pointing to rapid, non-metabolite regulators32.

This timing gap exposes a key flaw in prevailing gut-exercise frameworks. While microbial metabolites are well-positioned to shape longer-term adaptations, they are unlikely to be the sole mediators of rapid intestinal responses within minutes. The ENS possesses several properties that make it a plausible candidate for this role13. Enteric circuits respond rapidly to mechanical stretch, luminal chemical signals and immune-derived mediators, and exert immediate control over intestinal motility, epithelial barrier function and mucus secretion33–35. Unlike slow metabolite diffusion, ENS circuits offer local, real-time control of motility, permeability, and microbial niches during bouts, potentially gating signals to central fatigue pathways13,36.

The ENS is a rapid regulator during exercise

To elucidate how the ENS mediates rapid gut responses during exercise, we first clarify its core architecture and neural connectivity. Acute exercise imposes rapid and spatially heterogeneous stress on the gastrointestinal tract, characterized by reduced splanchnic perfusion, mechanical strain, and abrupt neurohumoral activation14,37,38. These perturbations unfold within minutes and precede measurable changes in microbial composition, indicating the involvement of regulatory mechanisms capable of fast, local control. The ENS is uniquely positioned to fulfill this role. Acute exercise marked reduces splanchnic perfusion within minutes, imposing mechanical strain and catecholamine surges that precede microbial shifts. Enteric sensory neurons (IPANs) detect luminal distension and shear instantly, triggering circuits responsive to norepinephrine and VIP14,39–41. Unlike systemic metabolites, ENS control stays local: Piezo1+ cholinergic neurons enhance peristaltic activity speed, while glial-VIP signaling deploys mucus and stabilizes barriers region-specifically42–44.

Notably, ENS-mediated regulation is spatially restricted to the gut microenvironment, enabling region-specific responses that cannot be achieved through circulating metabolites alone41,42. Coordinated, localized changes in epithelial permeability, mucus deployment, and transit time can therefore be deployed during a single exercise bout, shaping nutrient flux, luminal oxygen availability, and microbial niche structure29,45. Although direct experimental evidence linking ENS activity to exercise-induced microbiota remodeling remains limited, the temporal alignment between GI symptoms at 30 min and ENS reflexes favors primary ENS regulation over secondary metabolite effects, though direct exercise-ENS links need testing46.

ENS-immune crosstalk as a rapid regulatory interface during exercise

High-intensity running is known to induce measurable increases in intestinal permeability within 30–60 min, even in trained individuals, including hypoperfusion, hypoxia, mechanical strain, and barrier disruption38,47,48. These perturbations elicit rapid immune responses that require tight spatial and temporal regulation to prevent excessive inflammation while permitting tissue repair49. The ENS constitutes a fast-acting regulatory interface at this junction. Enteric neurons and glial cells are embedded within the lamina propria and are anatomically positioned to sense epithelial damage and immune-derived signals in real time50. Through the release of neuroimmune mediators such as vasoactive intestinal peptide (VIP), substance P, and calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP), the ENS modulates macrophage activation states, mast cell degranulation, and innate lymphoid cell activity50–53, which might calibrate local inflammatory tone during exercise-induced stress.

Additionally, this ENS-immune regulation does not operate in isolation but is strongly shaped by the gut microbiota54,55. Microbiota-derived metabolites, including SCFAs and tryptophan-derived indoles, influence baseline immune activation and neuronal excitability, effectively setting the threshold at which epithelial stress translates into inflammation. For instance, SCFAs, microbial metabolites that are upregulated by exercise in several contexts, activate G-protein coupled receptors GPR41 and GPR43 to promote anti-inflammatory cytokine profiles 56–60. Notably, acetate, a key SCFA, enables human gut microbiota to modulate colonic secretion in mice by regulating 5-HT₃ receptor expression61. Tryptophan-derived indoles act via the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR) to concurrently enhance mucosal immunity and epithelial barrier integrity, which indirectly shapes ENS excitability and gut reflex coordination62. Another critical metabolite, γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA), binds to GABA_B receptors on enteric neurons to dampen neuronal firing63. Briefly, the microbiota acts upstream of ENS-immune circuits, modulating their sensitivity rather than serving merely as a downstream target. During exercise, such preconditioning may determine whether transient barrier perturbations resolve rapidly or progress toward sustained inflammation, with downstream consequences for microbial niche stability and recovery dynamics.

ENS-driven modulation of the intestinal microenvironments and microbial ecology

ENS activity during exercise reshapes the intestinal microenvironment primarily through indirect ecological constraints rather than direct microbial targeting32. As mentioned above, enteric circuits dynamically alter nutrient availability, luminal oxygen gradients, and transit time, which were key parameters that govern microbial competition and spatial oxygen gradients, by regulating gut motility, mucosal perfusion, secretion, and vascular tone29,44. Exercise-induced activation of mechanosensitive enteric pathways, including Piezo1-expressing cholinergic neurons, enhances peristalsis within minutes, favoring taxa adapted to rapid turnover while limiting the expansion of slow-growing or inflammation-associated microbes41.

Beyond classical signaling pathways or enteropathogen-related mechanisms, these ENS-driven changes can be understood from a metabolic and ecological perspective, whereby neural regulation of the gut environment constrains microbial community structure and function during exercise. Changes in epithelial secretion and mucus deployment further contribute to microbial niche restructuring by modifying substrate accessibility and physical compartmentalization64,65. ENS-mediated control of barrier permeability and ion transport influences luminal hydration and redox state, indirectly shaping microbial metabolic strategies66,67. Mucus deployment and ion secretion further partition niches, while barrier control tweaks luminal redox and hydration. Direct interactions like ENS-derived ATP on biofilms exist but appear secondary to these broad ecological shifts68,69. Thus, the ENS functions as a rapid environmental regulator during exercise, beyond taxon-specific targeting.

Bidirectional microbiota-ENS signaling and exercise responsiveness

The relationship between the ENS and the gut microbiota is bidirectional and extends beyond acute environmental modulation. Colonization studies demonstrate that specific commensals, such as Bacteroides thetaiotaomicron, are required for normal enteric neural development and neurotransmitter biosynthetic capacity70, indicating that microbial signals shape ENS architecture and baseline excitability. In germ-free mice, colonization with this bacterium could restore the expression of key neurotransmitter-synthesizing enzymes in enteric neurons, including choline acetyltransferase, substance P, and nitric oxide synthase70.The gut microbiota maintains enteric nervous system integrity and regulates gastrointestinal function in a sex-independent manner via LPS and SCFAs71. Pathological changes in the ENS drive pre-neuromuscular intestinal dysfunction in hmTDP43 ALS mice, and targeting ENS-microbial homeostasis mitigates disease severity72. In parallel, microbial metabolites enriched by exercise modulate enteric neuronal activity through defined receptor pathways, fine-tuning gut reflexes and secretory responses under physical stress. Some of these signals engage enteric sensory neurons and vagal afferents, linking microbial metabolism to central autonomic and motivational circuits. As reported, fatty acid amides produced by specific commensals activate cannabinoid receptor CB1 on enteric neurons, enhancing dopaminergic signaling in reward-related brain regions and increasing exercise motivation7.

On the other hand, the ENS orchestrates gut physiology during exercise, with its crosstalk with the microbiota emerging as a central role in exercise performance. In response to heightened physiological demands, the ENS modulates gut motility, secretion, and immunity. Recent study highlighted Piezo1, which is a mechanosensitive ion channel in enteric cholinergic neurons, acts as a key mediator of exercise-induced gut motility41. By sensing abdominal mechanical stress, Piezo1 activation drives neuronal excitation and enhances peristaltic activity. Beyond regulating motility, the ENS also exerts a critical role in mucosal immunity and microbiota homeostasis through targeted signaling molecules. Enteric neuronal IL-18, but not IL-18 from immune or epithelial cells, is essential for goblet cell antimicrobial protein production, mucosal barrier immunity, maintenance of commensal microflora, and defense against Salmonella typhimurium infection. ENS also releases small molecules like adenosine triphosphate (ATP)69. This extracellular signal binds to P2 purinergic receptors on bacterial membranes, modulating biofilm formation and virulence gene expression in pathogens like E.coli73,74. These effects occur on timescales compatible with individual exercise bouts, underscoring the capacity of the microbiota-ENS axis to influence behavioral and physiological responses during physical activity, although direct evidence during exercise remains scarce.

Gut microbiota effects on muscle: ENS-dependent and ENS-independent pathways

The gut microbiota influences skeletal muscle adaptation to exercise through mechanisms that differ in both signaling routes and temporal dynamics. On one hand, microbial effects on muscle metabolism can occur independently of enteric neural activity, operating primarily through the systemic circulation and unfolding over hours to days. Exercise-responsive microbial metabolites, most notably short-chain fatty acids and tryptophan-derived compounds, have well-documented roles in regulating muscle energy utilization, insulin sensitivity, and mitochondrial function18,75–78. These systemic effects shape the metabolic milieu encountered by skeletal muscle during recovery and training adaptation and are well documented in both animal models and human studies.

In contrast, ENS-dependent pathways offer a more rapid route by which microbial signals may influence muscle performance during exercise itself. Signals arising from the intestinal lumen can activate enteric sensory neurons and vagal afferents, thereby transmitting information to central motor, autonomic, and motivational circuits. Such pathways have the potential to modulate fatigue perception, pacing behavior, and endurance capacity without requiring detectable changes in muscle mass or cardiorespiratory fitness32,79. Heightened ENS activity during exercise may amplify or attenuate these signals depending on microbial composition and metabolite availability, offering a plausible explanation for inter-individual variability in perceived exertion and performance that cannot be accounted for by muscle-intrinsic factors alone.

Although ENS-independent and ENS-dependent pathways are often treated separately, emerging data suggest substantial overlap. Notably, no study has yet measured ENS activity alongside muscle metabolism during exercise. Neural control of transit, epithelial transport, and barrier function, well established in isolation, fundamentally dictates metabolite absorption sites and rates80–82. Whether exercise amplifies this gating to reshape systemic exposure remains untested. The ENS-microbiota axis thus offers a compelling integrative framework for gut-muscle signaling, pending direct validation through targeted protocols. Neuro-enteric phenotypes as a conceptual framework

Marked inter-individual variability in gastrointestinal tolerance, metabolic adaptation, and performance is a consistent feature of exercise responses, yet it is poorly explained by cardiorespiratory fitness or baseline microbial composition alone. Part of this heterogeneity likely reflects differences in how the ENS integrates and prioritizes microbial, immune, and neurohumoral inputs under physical stress83,84. Rather than functioning as a uniform relay, ENS circuits exhibit context-dependent patterns of excitability, transmitter release, and sensory gain that bias intestinal physiology toward distinct adaptive trajectories during exercise. These dynamic ENS states might be termed neuro-enteric phenotypes, reflecting circuit excitability and microbiota interactions rather than static anatomy.

Observations are consistent with this framework71,85. Similar microbial communities can support divergent endurance adaptations depending on host aerobic capacity, training modality, or neural context, indicating that microbial composition alone is insufficient to predict exercise outcomes86. Moreover, disruption of vagal or enteric sensory signaling attenuates exercise-induced microbial remodeling and blunts performance gains, supporting a requirement for intact neurosensory feedback87. These observations challenge purely microbe-centric models, suggesting ENS activity may constrain microbial niches via nutrient fluxes, redox shifts, and resource partitioning dynamics amplified during exercise.

What remains unresolved is how these interactions differ across neuro-enteric phenotypes. One proposed axis of variation involves the balance between excitatory/adrenergic and inhibitory/non-adrenergic enteric signaling. States characterized by heightened adrenergic drive or increased intrinsic primary afferent neuron (IPAN) excitability are associated with accelerated transit, compromised barrier stability, and increased gastrointestinal symptoms during intense or prolonged exercise84. Similarly, endurance athletes exposed to cumulative physical or psychological stress often display transient barrier dysfunction, reduced microbial diversity, enrichment of stress-tolerant taxa, and elevated pro-inflammatory mediators such as TNF-α, accompanied by delayed recovery88–91. Chronic sympathetic activation has also been linked to reduced abundance of microbial taxa involved in neuroactive compound production, including GABA-producing bacteria92,93.

By contrast, greater engagement of non-adrenergic, non-cholinergic (NANC) pathways may exhibit a different adaptive profile, particularly circuits enriched in vasoactive intestinal peptide (VIP)94,95. VIP-expressing enteric neurons coordinate rhythmic motility and support epithelial barrier integrity, creating a luminal environment favorable to microbes involved in tryptophan and serotonin metabolism96. Endurance training has been associated with increased circulating levels of indole-3-propionic acid (IPA), a microbiota-derived tryptophan metabolite with antioxidant and neuroprotective properties that strengthens barrier function and modulates central mood circuits97–99. Notably, IPA, produced by taxa such as Clostridium sporogenes, has been shown to enhance intestinal barrier integrity and influence CNS signaling97,100,101. These observations suggest VIP signaling may stabilize gut-brain communication during exercise and potentially aid GI tolerance, mood, and training adherence102. At the same time, dysregulated VIP signaling has been implicated in gastrointestinal disorders such as IBS-D and IBD, indicating a narrow homeostatic window for this pathway103–105.

Importantly, neuro-enteric phenotypes should be considered dynamic rather than fixed. ENS development and plasticity are shaped by early microbial colonization, while exercise itself enhances vagal tone and enteric resilience across the lifespan106–108. Conversely, early-life stress disrupts ENS maturation and vagal regulation, with lasting consequences for gut-brain communication109. These findings indicate that ENS functional states may be shaped by lifestyle, environment, and training history, potentially offering opportunities for personalized exercise interventions.

Toward testing ENS-microbiota-exercise interactions in humans

Translating the ENS-microbiota-exercise framework to humans remains methodologically challenging, largely because enteric neural activity is difficult to access in vivo. Unlike animal models, direct electrophysiological recording or targeted manipulation of the ENS is currently not feasible in humans. However, recent advances in non-invasive physiological monitoring, neuroimaging, and multi-omics profiling allow indirect but mechanistically informative interrogation of neuro-enteric function during exercise. Measures such as gastrointestinal transit, permeability markers (e.g., lactulose–mannitol ratio or circulating zonulin), mucosal immune readouts, and stool metabolite profiles can be collected alongside autonomic indices, including heart rate variability, providing complementary proxies of ENS state and responsiveness14,110.

Acute exercise challenge paradigms may be particularly useful for probing ENS-dependent mechanisms, as they capture rapid physiological responses that occur before overt changes in microbial composition. Time-resolved sampling during and immediately after exercise, combined with targeted metabolomic profiling of short-chain fatty acids, bile acids, and tryptophan-derived metabolites, may help separate fast neurogenic regulation from slower microbiota-driven adaptations111–113. Importantly, stratifying participants based on gastrointestinal symptom susceptibility, vagal tone, or baseline transit characteristics may reveal distinct neuro-enteric response patterns that are obscured in group-averaged analyses114,115. Such approaches are consistent with the concept of neuro-enteric phenotypes and may help explain inter-individual variability in exercise tolerance and adaptation.

Interventional studies could test causality. Although direct evidence is still needed, pharmacological modulation of autonomic balance (e.g., β-adrenergic blockade), dietary manipulation of microbial substrates, or short-term probiotic or postbiotic interventions might reveal ENS-microbiota interactions.

Limitations and future directions

A graphical abstract illustrates this framework (Fig. 1, created with BioGDP.com116). Several limitations, however, temper the current ENS-microbiota-exercise framework. Much of the mechanistic evidence derives from rodent models, where direct manipulation of enteric circuits is feasible but may not fully capture the complexity of human exercise physiology. In humans, direct measurement of ENS activity remains indirect, and causal inference is often constrained by correlational designs.

The ENS is an integrative hub linking exercise-induced gut stress to muscle metabolism and performance via microbiota-immune crosstalk.

Acute exercise triggers gut stressors that engage the enteric nervous system (ENS), linking it bidirectionally to microbiota and mucosal immunity. ENS alters the luminal environment through motility, barrier function, and oxygen shifts, while microbial products (SCFAs, indoles, GABA) and neuropeptides (VIP, CGRP) fine-tune neural activity. Gut signals reach muscle via two routes: circulating metabolites (affecting metabolism) and neural pathways (shaping CNS-driven motivation/endurance). These dynamics span acute reflexes (minutes), immune/metabolite responses (hours), and chronic adaptations (weeks).

In addition, the temporal resolution of microbiota profiling is frequently insufficient to capture rapid, within-bout changes that may be most relevant to ENS-mediated regulation. Distinguishing primary ENS-driven effects from secondary immune or epithelial responses also remains challenging, particularly under conditions of systemic stress.

Ageing likely alters ENS-microbiota signaling during exercise, though few studies have tested this. Age-associated alterations in enteric neuronal integrity, neurochemical balance, vagal tone, and microbial diversity are well documented117–119; however, direct experimental evidence linking these changes to exercise tolerance, training adaptation, or microbiota-dependent muscle responses via the ENS remains limited. This gap highlights an important area for future investigation, particularly given the growing emphasis on exercise as an intervention to preserve metabolic health and neuromuscular function across the lifespan.

Future work should prioritize integrative, multimodal approaches that combine neural, microbial, immune, and metabolic readouts across acute and chronic exercise paradigms. Refining experimental tools to selectively probe enteric circuits in humans and defining reproducible ENS functional phenotypes will be critical for translating this conceptual model into actionable strategies for personalized exercise and metabolic health interventions.
