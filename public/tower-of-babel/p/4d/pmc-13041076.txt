Does the clinical complexity of impacted mandibular third molars influence postoperative recovery?
Authors: Ganesh Chaudhary, Pinky Singh, Hetal Amipara, Krishna Subedi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTransalveolar extraction of impacted mandibular third molars frequently presents formidable clinical challenges that may significantly impede postoperative convalescence. This investigation aimed to assess whether clinical diagnostic complexities influence the trajectory of patient recovery.MethodsIn this prospective study, 135 healthy adults (68 females, 67 males; mean age 27.3 ± 6.4 years) underwent mandibular third molar surgery under local anesthesia. Clinical diagnostic complexity was quantified using the Pederson Difficulty Index (PDI) derived from panoramic radiographs. Surgical duration (mean 9.4 ± 4.0 min) and complications - bleeding, swelling, trismus, pain, nerve paresthesia, infection, dry socket, TMJ disorders, and mandibular fracture—were systematically evaluated at postoperative days 3 and 7.ResultsPostoperative morbidity was most notable in pain (119 cases) and trismus (65 cases), with mild to moderate swelling in approximately 94% of patients; transient lingual nerve paresthesia occurred in two cases, with no other serious sequelae.ConclusionThis study demonstrated that cases with severe clinical complexity were associated with marginally higher complication rates and slightly longer extraction times compared with cases classified as moderate or mild by the difficulty index.Keywords: Clinical complexity, Difficulty index, Postoperative course, Surgical timing, Third molar impaction, Transalveolar extraction

Introduction

The surgical removal of impacted third molars is a routine practice in oral and maxillofacial surgery [1]. It is commonly performed for both preventive and therapeutic purposes. This procedure is successfully performed most of the time, but complications may arise, affecting patients’ postoperative course. These complications may encompass problems like bleeding, discomfort, swelling, infection, dry socket, nerve damage, Temporomandibular joint disorder (TMD), and in rare cases, fractures. These outcomes are influenced by many factors, such as the patient’s age and health status, gender, oxidative distress, pericoronitis, tooth impaction configuration, the surgeon’s experience, smoking habits, use of contraceptive medication, oral hygiene quality, and the chosen surgical or suture technique [2–8].

Surgeons frequently encounter difficulties when performing extractions of impacted mandibular third molars; evaluating the diagnostic and surgical complexity beforehand is crucial to navigate potential complications. Thus, scientifically validated information about the expected surgical difficulty is the utmost requisite. Despite advances in imaging techniques, intraoral periapical radiography (IOPA) and panoramic radiography (OPG) continue to serve as the primary diagnostic modalities for planning the transalveolar extraction of impacted mandibular third molars [9]. OPG, in particular, offers a broad field of view, cost-effectiveness, low radiation exposure, and a comprehensive overview making it suitable for initial evaluation. It assists in assessing anatomical structures, identifying associated pathologies, and determining the relationship of the tooth to the inferior alveolar nerve canal. Additionally OPG guides the need for further advanced imaging, when necessary, and facilitates the formulation of difficulty indices [10]. The commonly used indices comprise Winter’s, Pell and Gregory’s, WHARFE’s, and Pederson’s classifications, Modified Parant Scale, Pernambuco scale, etc [5]. It provides a systematic method for gauging the anticipated challenges associated with impacted third molars in an individualised manner.

Extensive research has focused on evaluating the treatment difficulty of mandibular molars, and Pederson’s difficulty index (PDI) continues to serve as the predominant assessment tool in this context [11–13]. It is based on radiographic factors and provides a structured approach to assess the clinical diagnostic complexity of impacted mandibular third molar extractions. This index considers angulations, depth, and the relationship with the ramus of the mandible [14]. By assigning difficulty scores based on these parameters, it supports surgeons in preoperative planning, enabling a more precise evaluation of the potential complexities linked to the extraction process. To the best of our knowledge, no study has explored the relation of clinical complexity of impacted mandibular third molars influencing postoperative recovery. Henceforth, this study aimed to assess if clinical diagnostic complexities influence the trajectory of patient postoperative recovery. The null hypothesis (H0) tested was no significant association between clinical complexity and postoperative recovery.

Materials and methods

This prospective, experimental study was designed in accordance with the principles of the Helsinki Declaration. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients. This study was approved by the Institutional Research Committee of Bharatpur Hospital (Ref no-078/079 − 005) and conducted in compliance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines (STROBE).

Sample size calculation

Using Master 2.0 software, an initial sample size of n = 120 was calculated based on an anticipated 26.6% prevalence of moderate pain, swelling, and trismus (± 8% precision). Accounting for a 10% non-response rate, the sample size was adjusted to 132; however, a total of 135 patients were ultimately enrolled [15].

Postoperative course assessment

Patients were followed up on day 3 and day 7 post-surgery. The follow-up schedule was structured so that the third postoperative day - typically corresponding to peak inflammation - and the seventh day - commonly the time of suture removal - could be utilised for assessment. This approach simplified evaluation for patients, minimised the number of visits, and provided a practical, scientifically grounded protocol for the clinician. Postoperative course was assessed by using different parameters like haemorrhage, swelling [16], trismus, pain [17], nerve paresthesia [18], infections, dry socket, TMD, mandibular fracture, other complications (if any), and operating time (in minutes)- time calculated from first incision to last suture and was noted accordingly. Any complications were managed according to standard protocols and recorded accordingly.

Biostatistics

Data were documented into Microsoft Excel 2013 and analysed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 20.0 (Armonk, NY: IBM Corp, USA). Descriptive statistics, frequency, percentage, mean, and standard deviation were calculated. A Chi-square test was performed to compare the difficulty index and postoperative course after surgical removal. The Fisher’s exact test was used to assess the association of postoperative courses and difficulty index. One-way ANOVA was used to compare the difficulty index with the mean extraction time. Pearson’s correlation was applied to examine the relationship between the difficulty index and extraction time. The significance level was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Of 135 (Female = 68, 50.37%; Male = 67, 49.63%) participants who completed the survey, the mean age of the participants was 27.28 ± 6.41 (range: 18–45 years). They were enrolled for the surgical procedure and followed up on the third day and seventh day, respectively. The most common impaction ascertained was mesioangular 47 (34.81%), followed by vertical 42 (31.11%), horizontal 28 (20.74%), distoangular 16 (11.85%), and lingual 2 (1.48%). PDI was used to calculate the complexity, showing that the most common type was moderate (n = 78; 57.78%), followed by severe (n = 34; 25.19%), and the least common was mild (n = 23;17.04%).

Upon the analysis of the postoperative recovery, the most common complications shown were trismus, pain, swelling, and lingual nerve paresthesia (Table 1). 65 showed grade II trismus, 44 showed grade I, and 14 were normal. Similarly, 119 patients had mild pain, 14 had no pain, and none had severe pain. Most patients showed mild (64.44%) and moderate (29.63%) postoperative swelling (Fig. 2). Only two patients developed lingual paresthesia, which resolved uneventfully.

The association between postoperative complications and the clinical complexity of mandibular third molars was evaluated, revealing that cases with severe PDI exhibited a slightly higher complication rate than those with moderate PDI (Table 2). In severe cases of PDI (25.19%), 97.06% had trismus, 100% had pain, and 100% had postoperative swelling. Similarly, in moderate cases of PDI (57.78%), 93.59% had trismus, 92.31% had pain, 97.44% had postoperative swelling, and 2.56% had lingual nerve paresthesia. In mild cases of PDI (17.04%), 91.30% had trismus, 65.22% had pain, 73.91% had postoperative swelling, and 0% had lingual nerve paresthesia. No patient showed profound trismus. The most common complications were trismus, followed by pain and postoperative swelling, respectively. None of the patients had shown postoperative haemorrhage, infection, dry socket, TMD, mandibular fracture, or any other complications.

Discussion

This study aimed to investigate the clinical complexity and its effect on postoperative courses following transalveolar extraction of mandibular third molars. The assessment encompassed parameters such as the extent of trismus, postoperative pain and swelling, the presence of lingual nerve paresthesia, infections, and the overall operative duration. Although a correlation was observed between the intricacy level and the occurrence of postoperative complications, no case of postoperative hemorrhage, infection, alveolar osteitis, temporomandibular disorders, mandibular fracture, or other complications was observed within the study cohort, contrasting other notable research [20, 21]. This disparity may be attributed to effective surgical procedures, meticulous sterilisation protocols, and the comparatively younger age of the individuals involved.

The most prevalent complication observed was trismus, a condition characterised by muscular spasms triggered by inflammation due to surgical trauma. Trismus severity often correlates with heightened pain levels but typically resolves spontaneously over time [22]. Regarding nerve injuries, while the literature reports a range of 1.3% to 5.3% for IAN injury and 0% to 23% for lingual nerve injury, our study found only two cases (2.56%) of lingual paresthesia, both of which resolved without complications [23–26]. Although anatomical variation of the lingual nerve is a primary concern, the careful design and execution of an appropriate surgical flap may have contributed to its uneventful postoperative recovery [27].

Morton H. Goldberg [28] in 1985 performed 177 extractions, amongst which 35 cases had complications. Complications discerned were infection 4.2%, dry socket 1.0%, nerve dysfunction 1.2% (0.2% permanent), and haemorrhage 0.6%. Similarly, Matte Chiapasco [29] in 1993 included 614 patients with an average age of 26.5 years, showing complications of alveolar osteitis 1.2%, secondary infection 1.5%, excessive bleeding 0.6%, dysesthesia 0.7% and trismus 0.3%. A retrospective study by Cíntia-Mussi-Milani Contar [30] in 2010 included 588 patients with complications, including pain in 26, root tip fracture in 21, paresthesia in seven, alveolar osteitis in two, temporomandibular joint (TMJ) discomfort in 2, and oroantral fistula in one. Ingibjorg et al. [31] in 2004 operated on 388 molars, depicting the complications as severe pain 15%, infection 2.8%, dry socket 5.9%, paresthesia 1.5%, and only 2 patients reported lingual nerve paresthesia. This study provides insights into the postoperative recovery course, with cases further stratified according to the level of surgical difficulty, in line with the complications reported in the aforementioned studies. Severe cases, although low in numbers, depicted a more severe recovery course than that of moderate and mild cases, respectively. Furthermore, the study evaluated the duration of the surgical procedure, with an average extraction time of 9.44 min. Paras Rahim Baloch [32] in 2019 included 92 patients with a minimum operation time of 10 min and a maximum operation time of 40 min, while Ignibjorg et al. [31] in 2004 showed contrasting results with an average operation time of 25 min. A weak yet statistically significant correlation was identified between extraction time and procedural complexity. These findings suggest that recovery courses depends upon anatomical variabilities that could not be eliminated; their incidence has been comparatively reduced over time with advancements in surgical techniques, individual patient characteristics and the implementation of effective prophylactic measures. These statistical analyses demonstrated that clinical complexity had a significant influence on postoperative recovery, resulting in rejection of the null hypothesis.

These findings highlight the importance of preoperative assessment of surgical complexity using difficulty indices to anticipate complications and optimise treatment planning. Utilisation of validated tools such as the PDI enables more accurate evaluation of impacted mandibular third molar extractions and supports tailored surgical approaches. Even though OPG has been used in this study for a detailed view, an alternative intraoral periapical radiograph with Nallan’s lines offers a robust framework for improving radiographic precision [33]. Moreover, recognition of common postoperative complications underscores the need for structured postoperative care, in constrast an integrated approach incorporating clinical judgement, complication risk, surgical timing, and monitoring facilitates realistic patient counselling and improved recovery outcomes. In addition medications and physical therapy, supplementation with vitamin C supports collagen synthesis, vitamin A enhances immune function, protein aids tissue repair, zinc promotes tissue growth and healing, and adequate hydration facilitates postoperative recovery and pain control [34].

Limitations

The assessment of the study was limited to diagnostic panoramic imaging (PDI) which may miss other anatomic details and risk factors. Second, the follow-up was limited to days 3 and 7 as described; later-developing complications were not assessed. Future research could expand the evaluation scope by including 3-dimensional imaging and a more extensive follow-up period.
